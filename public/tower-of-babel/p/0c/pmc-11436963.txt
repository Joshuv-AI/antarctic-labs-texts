Development of a decision tree model for predicting the malignancy of localized gingival enlargements based on clinical characteristics
Authors: Pawat Sripodok, Puangwan Lapthanasupkul, Tawepong Arayapisit, Nakarin Kitkumthorn, Natchalee Srimaneekarn, Vichaya Neeranadpuree, Watjanee Amornwatcharapong, Supawit Hempornwisarn, Sirikorn Amornwikaikul, Dulyapong Rungraungrayabkul
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study aimed to determine the prevalence of localized gingival enlargements (LGEs) and their clinical characteristics in a group of Thai patients, as well as utilize this information to develop a clinical diagnostic guide for predicting malignant LGEs. All LGE cases were retrospectively reviewed during a 20-year period. Clinical diagnoses, pathological diagnoses, patient demographic data, and clinical information were analyzed. The prevalence of LGEs was determined and categorized based on their nature, and concordance rates between clinical and pathological diagnoses among the groups were evaluated. Finally, a diagnostic guide was developed using clinical information through a decision tree model. Of 14,487 biopsied cases, 946 cases (6.53%) were identified as LGEs. The majority of LGEs were reactive lesions (72.62%), while a small subset was malignant tumors (7.51%). Diagnostic concordance rates were lower in malignant LGEs (54.93%) compared to non-malignant LGEs (80.69%). Size, consistency, color, duration, and patient age were identified as pivotal factors to formulate a clinical diagnostic guide for distinguishing between malignant and non-malignant LGEs. Using a decision tree model, we propose a novel diagnostic guide to assist clinicians in enhancing the accuracy of clinical differentiation between malignant and non-malignant LGEs.Keywords: Localized gingival enlargement, Gingival cancer, Cancer, Reactive lesion, Diagnostic tree, Decision tree modelSubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Oncology, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

Localized gingival overgrowths or gingival enlargements (LGEs) are frequently encountered in dental practices. These lesions are characterized as pedunculated or sessile swellings of the gingiva1. LGEs often arise from reactive factors, with common triggers being local chronic irritants such as dental plaques or trauma. However, other etiologies of LGEs could be encountered, including inflammatory, benign or even malignant causes2. In the most severe instances, these growths can pose a life-threatening condition. Previous studies have illustrated that malignant LGEs can manifest as harmless lesions3–5, emphasizing the diverse nature of LGEs and the importance of precise diagnosis.

To obtain a definitive diagnosis, histopathological examination stands as the gold standard6. However, clinical diagnosis or judgement remains a pivotal role in the preliminary assessment and management of LGEs. Since these lesions display various clinical manifestations depending on their nature2, proper clinical diagnosis requires a detailed consideration of clinical characteristics such as size, consistency, color, surface texture, and location7,8. Although the majority of LGEs are reactive or benign9, some are malignant and may be clinically underestimated by general practitioners or non-oral medicine specialists, causing delayed or neglected pathological diagnoses in certain cases. This situation is particularly critical and may impact the patient’s prognosis, leading to an increase in both morbidity and mortality rates. Therefore, achieving an accurate clinical diagnosis is of utmost significance in guiding definitive diagnosis, making effective management decisions and optimizing patient care.

To date, studies focusing on LGEs are still limited10–12. Thus, we aimed to evaluate the prevalence of LGEs in a group of Thai population. Subsequently, we conducted an analysis of diagnostic agreement across the various nature-based classifications of LGEs. Using the analyzed dataset, we have developed a practical clinical diagnostic guide through an implementation of a decision tree model. The guide may aid clinicians in enhancing diagnostic precision, particularly when confronted with the task of distinguishing between malignant and non-malignant LGEs.

Results

Nature of LGEs and their diseases

During 2002–2021, there were 946 cases of LGEs out of 14,487 biopsied cases, accounting for a prevalence of 6.53%. The majority of these cases manifested as reactive lesions (72.62%), followed by benign tumors (8.56%), inflammation and infection (8.35%), malignant tumors (7.51%), and other lesions (2.96%). Regarding LGE-related diseases, pyogenic granuloma was the most common LGEs (33.09%), followed by fibrous hyperplasia (28.12%), peripheral ossifying fibroma (7.40%), inflamed tissue (4.55%), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (3.70%). Demographic data and clinical information

Patient demographic data and clinical information of LGEs, categorized by the nature of diseases, are listed in Table 2. Significant differences were observed in all factors including age, sex, duration, site of lesion, color, consistency, and size across all LGE groups. The mean patient age was highest for malignant tumors (55.26 ± 16.71), followed by other lesions (46.50 ± 18.47), inflammation and infection (42.72 ± 17.23), reactive lesions (40.52 ± 18.32), and benign tumors (39.30 ± 19.18) (p < 0.001). All LGE groups exhibited a female predilection with the highest percentage of female patients seen in reactive lesions (68.41%), followed by other lesions (67.86%) and the infection and inflammation group (59.49%). Malignant tumors showed only a slight female predilection (50.70%) (p = 0.005). The majority of LGE groups exhibited a duration of longer than 6 months, with the notable exception of malignant tumors in which 60.56% of the cases presented a short duration (< 3 months) (p < 0.001).

In terms of location distribution, reactive lesions predominantly occurred in the anterior maxilla (30.42%), while benign tumors, and other lesions were commonly found in the posterior maxilla (30.86% and 32.14%, respectively). Malignant tumors and cases classified under the infection and inflammation group were more frequently observed in the posterior mandible (33.80% and 45.57%, respectively) (p < 0.001). Regarding color of LGEs, most of LGE groups commonly presented with a red coloration. In the case of benign tumors, and other lesions, pink coloration was predominantly observed (37.04% and 39.29%, respectively). Moreover, some malignant tumors (12.68%) exhibited red and white coloration (p < 0.001). For the consistency of lesions, firm consistency was predominantly observed in most of LGE groups, except for malignant tumors, and the infection and inflammation groups were more frequently characterized by a soft consistency (p < 0.001). Furthermore, malignant tumors exhibited the largest size among LGEs (mean size of 2.83 ± 1.89 cm), while other lesions showed relatively similar mean sizes, ranging from 1.05 ± 0.93 to 1.33 ± 1.50 cm. (p < 0.001). Finally, most LGE groups predominantly showed rough surface whereas reactive lesions commonly presented with smooth surface (p < 0.001).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

For the purpose of analysis, the five LGE groups, classified based on their nature, underwent reclassification into two distinct categories denoting malignant LGEs and non-malignant LGEs. Subsequently, all factors displaying significant differences in the Chi-squared test were integrated into the multivariate logistic regression analysis. Regarding the analysis, male patients (OR: 3.50, 95% CI 1.37–9.98), a duration of less than 3 months (OR: 10.95, 95% CI 2.54–47.19), consistency categorized as ‘others’ (OR: 21.94, 95% CI 1.76-273.71), advancing age (OR: 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.07), and an increase in lesion size (OR: 2.00, 95% CI 1.46–2.74) were identified as independent predictors for malignant LGEs, as demonstrated in Table 3.

Clinical diagnostic guide base on decision tree model

To conduct a clinical diagnostic guide, a decision tree model was formulated for the purpose of distinguishing between malignant and non-malignant LGEs through a recursive partitioning process. The differentiation process was implemented within a cohort of 946 patients, resulting in the development of a final decision tree model comprising six distinct groups, as depicted in Fig. 1. The primary discriminator for classifying LGEs was identified as lesion size, with a cutoff of 3 cm. Subsequently, cases were segmented based on the consistency and color of LGEs, the duration of the lesion with a cutoff of 3 months, and finally, the age criterion with a cutoff of 52 years old. Employing these discriminatory factors, the six final groups of the decision tree model were organized into three risk levels based on the probability of malignancy: high, moderate, and low, as outlined in Table 5. The high risk of being malignant LGEs was associated with lesions larger than 3 cm., and a soft consistency. Additionally, the moderate risk included lesions larger than 3 cm. with a firm consistency, or lesions smaller than 3 cm. with red-white or white in color. It also encompassed patients over 52 years old with lesions smaller than 3 cm., red or pink in color, and a duration of less than 3 months. Lastly, the low risk involved lesions in patients younger than 52 years old, smaller than 3 cm., red or pink in color, and a duration of less than 3 months, or lesions smaller than 3 cm. in size, red or pink in color, and lasting more than 3 months.

Provisional validation of diagnostic guide

A new dataset of 30 LGEs was recruited to validate the diagnostic guide. This dataset included 19 non-malignant LGEs and 11 malignant LGEs. The likelihood of malignancy in each case was assessed by the developed diagnostic guides and then classified into three risk categories: high risk, moderate risk, and low risk, with 9, 6, and 15 cases in each category, respectively. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was generated and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The diagnostic guide yielded an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI, 0.65–0.98), with a specificity of 0.84 and a sensitivity of 0.73.

Discussion

This retrospective study provides the largest and comprehensive analysis of LGEs in Thailand to date. During our 20-year experience, the prevalence of LGEs accounted for approximately 6.53% of cases. This finding falls in the range of previous studies that reported prevalence rates of LGEs ranging from 6.20 to 9.65% 10,11. Although, the primary nature of LGEs (72.62%) was reactive lesions, the other etiologies varied among numerous studies. In the present cohort, non-reactive LGEs, including benign tumors, inflammatory and infection, and malignant tumors, exhibited a nearly equal distribution, ranging from 7.51 to 8.56%. These observations contrast with prior studies reporting only 2.11% for gingival inflammation and 1.10–4.80% for malignant LGEs10,11. Another study by Morilla et al., analyzing LGEs from the northeast part of Thailand, demonstrated a higher proportion of malignant LGEs (32.12%)12. Such disparities in prevalence may be influenced by various factors including ethno-geographical difference, racial factor or lifestyle. Among LGEs, the most severe condition encountered was malignant tumors. Our study identified a spectrum of malignant tumors presenting with LGE features. Unsurprisingly, SCC, the most prevalent form of oral cancer, emerged as the predominant type, consistent with findings of a previous study13. Moreover, we identified approximately 11% of malignant LGEs with metastatic origins, underlining the occasional presence of metastatic cancer with LGE features in the oral cavity.

Employing both univariate and multivariate analysis approaches, a thorough analysis incorporating demographic data and clinical information related to LGEs was deemed. While, factors such as age, sex, duration, location, color, consistency, size, and surface texture exhibited significant differences among various LGE groups, distinctive variations emerged when comparing malignant LGEs with other etiologies. Our findings revealed that male patients, a duration of less than 3 months, consistency categorized as ‘others’, advancing age, and an increase in lesion size were identified as independent predictors for malignant LGEs. Despite the significant differences observed in many factors between malignant and non-malignant LGEs, the diagnostic concordance for malignant LGEs remained relatively low, with only 54.93% of cases demonstrating concordant results. In contrast, non-malignant LGEs exhibited a higher concordance rate of 80.69%. Interestingly, recent research has highlighted the gingival mucosa as having the lowest concordance between clinical and histopathologic diagnoses of biopsied oral lesions compared to other oral cavity locations14. Furthermore, a previous study demonstrated that gingival cancer is often associated with advanced stages at the time of diagnosis15. This particular situation may be attributed to diagnostic delays influenced by a low index of suspicion and a lack of experience with these tumors16. For the aforementioned reasons, a guide to assist the clinical differentiation between malignant and non-malignant LGEs is tempting in order to increase the awareness and attention of dentists in managing lesions more appropriately.

In the formulation of the clinical diagnosis guide, a conditional inference tree, was conducted by integrating tree-structured regression models within a meticulously defined framework of conditional inference procedures, as proposed by Hothorn et al.17. This method has recently been introduced to provide support for healthcare professionals in the areas of diagnosis, selection of treatment options, and prognosis18–20. With regard to the analysis of the decision tree, several certain factors, including size, color, duration, age, and consistency, assumed a pivotal role in differentiating between malignant and non-malignant LGEs. Notably, size emerged as the primary predictor for malignant LGEs. Lesions larger than 3 cm. with a soft consistency were classified as high-risk for malignancy, while lesions exceeding 3 cm. with a firm consistency were considered to pose a moderate risk of malignancy. This finding aligns with previous evidence indicating that oral malignancies often exhibit rapid progression21. Additionally, a recent multicenter study conducted in Brazil on gingival neoplasms revealed a similar trend, indicating that malignant gingival lesions are commonly associated with a size larger than 3 cm22.

Conversely, lesions smaller than 3 cm. with red and white or white coloration are associated with a moderate risk of malignancy. Regarding malignant LGEs, epithelial malignancies constitute a major proportion. The white coloration observed is often attributed to acanthosis with hyperkeratinization, a feature that may be seen on the surface of epithelial malignancies23. In cases where lesions exhibited red or pink coloration, additional consideration of duration and patient’s age became imperative. Specifically, lesions with a duration of less than 3 months in patients older than 52 years tended to carry a moderate risk of malignancy. However, when the lesion manifested in patients aged younger than 52 years or displayed a duration exceeding 3 months, the risk of malignancy is comparatively low. These results are in line with the study by Santos-Leite et al., which reported a lower median time of complaint and a higher median age in malignant gingival lesions22. Older age has been identified as a risk factor for developing oral cancer such as SCC, particularly in patients older than 40 years24. Therefore, it is not surprising that duration of lesion, and the patient’s age, especially those older than 52 years, are considered in this diagnostic guide.

Although histopathological analysis remains the gold standard for providing a definitive diagnosis, the importance of accurate clinical diagnosis cannot be understated. A precise clinical diagnosis facilitates prompt therapeutic management, preventing treatment delays that could positively impact survival probability and prognosis25. Due to its systematic framework, this diagnostic guide bears a potential to support clinicians in enhancing the accuracy of distinguishing between malignant and non-malignant LGEs.

Based on the present evidence, this study represents the first attempt to develop a diagnostic tree for predicting malignant LGEs. Despite the promise exhibited by the diagnostic tree, this investigation is not without limitations. The retrospective study rendered some clinical information unavailable. Moreover, the provisional validation of diagnostic guides showed promising results, though this cohort was limited to a small sample of Thai patients, which might limit the generalizability of the findings. Further research involving multicenter and multiethnic studies with a larger sample size is warranted to improve the validity of this clinical diagnostic guide.

To conclude, in a group of Thai population, LGEs predominantly exhibit a reactive nature, followed by a comparable distribution among benign tumors, inflammatory and infection, and malignant tumors. A notable discordance between clinical and pathological diagnoses is evident, particularly in almost half of the identified malignant LGEs. In the light of this, we propose a potential diagnostic guide to assist general practitioners in differentiating between malignant and non-malignant LGEs.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This retrospective study obtained a formal approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Dentistry and the Faculty of Pharmacy at Mahidol University (COA.No.MU-DT/PY-IRB 2022/047.2209). The ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki were followed in this study. Patient informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board for this retrospective study.

Data collection

The schematic flow diagram of this study was illustrated in Fig. 2. All LGE cases were thoroughly reviewed from both pathological request forms in the archives of the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology, and patient’s medical record from Dental Hospital, Faculty of Dentistry, Mahidol University, Bangkok, Thailand, between 2002 and 2021. Then the LGE cases were recruited into the study based on the following inclusion criteria: (a) a solitary mass affecting the gingiva; (b) cases with a definitive histopathological diagnosis; (c) sufficient demographic and clinical information; (d) Thai ethnicity. Cases of bony tissue origin and those located on the edentulous alveolar ridges were excluded. Subsequently, clinical diagnoses, pathological diagnoses, patient demographic data (e.g., age and sex), and relevant clinical information (e.g., duration, location, color, consistency, surface texture, and size) were systematically extracted for comprehensive data analysis.

Schematic flow diagram of the study.

Data analysis

LGEs were categorized into five groups according to their nature, encompassing reactive lesions, inflammation and infection, benign tumors, malignant tumors, and other lesions. Descriptive statistics were obtained to clarify the prevalence of LGEs. Chi-squared test was employed for a comparison of categorical data, while ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test was conducted to compare continuous data across the various LGE groups. Odds ratio was calculated using multivariate logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors associated with malignant LGEs. Moreover, diagnostic concordances between clinical and pathological diagnoses within LGE groups were determined, with concordance rates expressed as a percentage. To facilitate the concordance analysis, both clinical and pathological diagnoses were reclassified based on the nature of LGEs, and concordance was deemed present when diagnoses matched in terms of their nature. Furthermore, a diagnostic guide for differentiating between malignant and non-malignant LGEs was formulated using clinical characteristics through a decision tree model employing conditional inference tree methodology17. The developed diagnostic guide was then validated with a new dataset of LGEs using ROC curve analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1, Vienna, Austria). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered as statistical significance.
