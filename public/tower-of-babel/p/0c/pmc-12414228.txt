Trauma community clinical guidance needs: a mixed-methods iterative consensus-building study
Authors: Jenny M Guido, Morgan Krause, Andrew Steiner, Amanda Nicol, Katheryn T Grider, Gabriela Zavala Wong, Ashley N Moreno, Lacey N LaGrone, Pamela J Bixby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDeveloping and implementing trauma clinical guidance is integral to providing quality care to all trauma patients while maintaining a minimum standard of treatment. A mixed-methods novel consensus-building approach was used to identify the current barriers to developing and implementing trauma clinical guidance and highlight the priority areas for change to better support end users.MethodsAs part of year 1 of the Design for Implementation: The Future of Trauma Clinical Guidance and Research Conference Series, preconference participant surveys and hybrid, professionally facilitated, structured dialogue were used to define the ideal future state of trauma clinical guidance development and dissemination. Novel to this context, in-person and virtual “user stories”, a form of structured focus group, were generated, and a “minimum viable product” (MVP), a form of brokered dialogue, was developed. Descriptive statistics and thematic analysis were used to evaluate preconference survey and “user story” results.Results72 in-person and up to 35 virtual attendees participated. The majority (92%) of in-person attendees and nearly half (48%) of virtual attendees completed the preconference survey. Participants identified barriers along the continuum of clinical guidance development, dissemination, and adoption. Areas for improvement centered around the creation, storage, and use of guidance. Across the survey and user stories, participants expressed the need for clinical guidance that is comprehensive, evidence-based, coordinated, and easily accessible by all clinicians both domestically and abroad. The MVP targeted the risks and objectives to improved guidance. A prominent theme throughout this consensus-building assessment was the imperative for collaboration between professional societies for clinical guidance development and dissemination.DiscussionTrauma clinical guidance must be current, consolidated, and coordinated with patient-centered outcomes prioritized. Next steps include turning the MVP produced into a prototype and refining it to inform a national redesign of trauma clinical guidance.Level of evidenceLevel III.

Introduction

Background

Trauma results in the largest years of life lost compared with any other disease in the USA.1 As a public health issue, trauma is plagued by vast inequity in access to quality injury care.2 The current approach to the treatment of traumatic injuries, nationally and globally, results in disparities and preventable morbidity and mortality. Given that trauma is the leading cause of death in people under the age of 46,3 improving outcomes in trauma is an area of keen interest. To improve outcomes, information sharing and knowledge generation need to be addressed.4 There is national consensus that optimizing trauma clinical guidance is a priority means of decreasing preventable morbidity and mortality after injury.5

Development of trauma clinical guidance is a practical means by which regional, national, and international organizations support local evidence-based practice. For our analysis, the term “guidance” is used to reference all resources, including guidelines, protocols, algorithms, and best practice management which are intended to support the bedside practitioner in care decisions. Recognizing the diversity of resources in this regard, we elected an inclusive definition to best reflect the resources available to the end user. Excellent high-quality, publicly available, and user-friendly trauma clinical guidance resources and repositories exist, but the current state of knowledge is chaotic. These repositories are not consistently updated, and there are presently gaps and content redundancy due to minimal collaboration across professional societies. Of the five national or regional trauma professional societies representing the national leadership in trauma research and care, four develop and publish clinical guidelines.69 Three of these organizations make trauma clinical guidance publicly available, one disseminates to members only. The size of repositories ranges from 8 guidelines to 94, with some as old as 20 years listed adjacent to those from the past year.10 This undermines trauma clinical guidance effectiveness and perceived validity. Furthermore, there is great variability in the experience and format for the end user, reflecting the lack of research informing how best to disseminate clinical guidance to various user groups. Thus, the current state of trauma knowledge transfer leaves many trauma providers with inadequate access to up-to-date, resource-relevant, user-friendly, and trustworthy trauma clinical guidance.

The Design for Implementation: The Future of Trauma Clinical Guidance and Research (DFI) Conference Series was developed in response to this need for improved clinical guidance. Community-engaged codesign has been shown to result in products which more closely meet end-user needs.11 This conference series follows the principles of Designing for Dissemination and Sustainability and applies the Fit to Context Framework.12 Addressing the first phase within these principles—demonstration of the need/capacity for innovation and establishing partnerships for codesign—the conference team performed a mixed-methods needs assessment and facilitated consensus development. Through preconference participant surveys and expert in-person and synchronous facilitation, consensus was built around the ideal future state of trauma clinical guidance development and dissemination. Facilitators experienced in context inquiry and market research conducted in-person and virtual “user stories” and “minimum viable product” (MVP) sessions. The application of these two tools is novel to this context.13 14

Purpose/aim

Using this mixed-methods novel consensus-building approach, we sought to address the following questions as part of year one of the DFI conference series: (1) What are the current barriers to developing and implementing trauma clinical guidance? (2) What does the trauma community identify as priority areas for change in development and dissemination to support end users? (3) How do preconceived thoughts about the first two questions collected asynchronously (via the preconference survey) compare to those developed collaboratively (via the MVP) after an empathy-building exercise (user stories)?

Methods

Study design

The first meeting of the Design for Implementation: The Future of Trauma Clinical Guidance and Research (DFI) Conference Series was held on February 21–22, 2024, at the American College of Surgeons Headquarters in Chicago, Illinois. The first of three meetings was designed to explore stakeholders’ perspectives on barriers to the development and implementation of trauma guidance as well as priority areas for change. Subsequent meetings will explore methods for codesign and implementation. An exploratory sequential mixed-methods study was conducted with an adaptive design, whereby the results of one data collection effort informed the development of the next. First, a preconference survey including free text elements was used to better understand trauma clinical guidance end user needs. The results of this survey then informed the discussion and consensus building that occurred in-person and virtually (synchronous) during the conference.

Key partners for this project were identified and invited to attend the first conference of the series. Participants included pediatric and adult injury community partners (such as clinical providers), industry partners, healthcare administration, non-profit/development, government, and patient and family representatives. Conference participation included both in-person and remote attendees. Recruitment occurred through key informant snowball sampling and included strategic sampling of leadership from professional societies and then sampling within other organizations, starting with leadership and those known within the Coalition for National Trauma Research network to be active in the trauma clinical guidance design. Partners were identified based on a preidentified priority group matrix to ensure comprehensive representation (eg, job type, demographics, rural/urban service area, geography, education). Additionally, professional facilitators with Agile methodology experience were invited to facilitate the user stories and MVP sessions.

Preconference survey data collection

The cross-sectional, preconference survey component conforms to the Consensus-Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies checklist (see online supplemental item 1).15 This survey collected information regarding the perceived barriers to developing, implementing, and/or adopting improved trauma clinical guidance. Respondents were also asked how trauma clinical practice guidance development and dissemination could change to better support all end users. All survey responses were captured through the University of Colorado hosted REDCap.16 Data were deidentified and only accessible by the study team to preserve confidentiality. Descriptive statistics were calculated using REDCap and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, 2018). Thematic analysis of open-ended responses regarding barriers to implementation and possible solutions was conducted by a single reviewer (ANM) using Microsoft Excel. Themes from each response were identified and organized into groupings. These groupings were ranked in order of most to least frequent, and the most frequently mentioned themes (ie, items mentioned two or more times) were summarized in narrative format. The preconference survey allowed the facilitators to identify and explore particular points of tension or disparate/split answers during the conference facilitation. The expert facilitators (KTG and AN) along with the conference team members (LL and ANM) reviewed the most frequently mentioned themes prior to the meeting and coordinated the in-person and virtual small-group work during the conference around these foci.

Conference facilitation: “user stories” and “minimum viable product”

Predominant in marketing research, “user stories” are used to better understand the needs of various groups, as well as support empathy building. A user story asks participants to take the perspective of a particular population and state: “As a ___ I need ___ in order to ___.” Six user story cohorts were organized prior to the conference using survey demographics and previously known identifiers. These user story cohorts included a separate grouping for global, rural/non-level one trauma center, emergency medicine, patients, pediatrics, and high resource settings (ie, level one trauma centers). One combined cohort was used for the virtual user story session. Each group contained participants with personal experience with the specific focal area and participants without such expertise. During the first day of the conference, participants were instructed to write user stories from the perspective of the user group to which they were assigned. Participants first independently wrote as many user stories on individual notecards as desired. After pasting them on a whiteboard, the group collectively organized the statements by common themes as a group thought sorting activity. Sorting was done in two steps:

Users collectively grouped stickies on the white board thematically based on the “feature” that was described as needed in order to achieve an outcome. They discussed and debated as they moved sticky notes, and when there was a lack of clear consensus, facilitators asked those in the group who were the actual “end users” that everyone was solving for to weigh in.

Using the data created via the user stories from both in-person and virtual participants, a “minimum viable product” (MVP) was developed. Popular in market research, an MVP outlines the basic (or “minimum”) set of requirements needed by a group to establish a common goal, defining objectives, audience, risks, and key performance indicators (KPIs). It is created with the understanding that the outcome (or “product”) will be continuously evolving, must consider interdependencies and trade-offs as we prioritize, and appreciates that the entire process is iterative.14 The MVP framework encourages teams to establish what “minimum (is) required to be successful” as determined by user needs.17 Here, the MVP was created with the goal of ensuring consensus across the conference attendees by giving an opportunity for feedback and dissenting opinions to be discussed and to develop an early opportunity for a deliverable. To our knowledge, this approach has not been used to outline the minimum needs of trauma clinical guidance. Following the review of the user stories the evening of day 1, the facilitators then compiled and presented a drafted MVP to the attendees during the second day of the meeting. This drafted MVP was then modified based on group discussions and feedback to clarify objectives, audience, risks, and KPIs.

Results

Demographics of preconference survey and user story/MVP participants

72 in-person and up to 35 virtual attendees participated in the conference. The exact number of virtual attendees is unknown as participants logged on and off the virtual streaming platform sporadically. Overall, 92% of in-person attendees and 48% of virtual attendees completed the preconference survey. Most survey respondents (65%) were clinicians who practice in a verified or designated trauma center: Level I (79%), level II (11%), level III (3%), and level IV (7%). 47% were physicians and 17% were nurses. Pediatric patients were treated by 41% of survey respondents, and 15% practiced in a rural setting. A majority of respondents indicated they conduct research as part of their job (60%) and participate in clinical guidance development (65%) at various levels: institutional (80%), national (67%), regional (44%), and international (26%) (table 1).

Preconference survey results

Barriers to clinical guidance development and adoption: open-ended/free-text responses

Respondents of the preconference survey responded to an open-ended item regarding barriers to developing and implementing trauma clinical guidance before rank ordering offered barriers/priority areas for change. Barriers were identified along the continuum of clinical guidance development, dissemination, and adoption, which included lack of consensus among stakeholders; biases that exist for creators and users; bureaucracy; lack of communication; challenges associated with attempting to standardize practices across institutions with different resources (trauma center variability); and the insufficient use of tools to support the development, dissemination, and adoption of clinical guidance.

Quotes highlighting barriers to clinical guidance development and adoption and areas for improvement

Regarding barriers to guidance development, respondents indicated that the lack of appropriate research, the need to translate these findings into digestible guidance, and the immense time, effort, and funding needed to create current and timely guidance were problematic.

A strong theme emerged regarding the inadequate infrastructure to streamline the dissemination of clinical guidance. The robust reliance on journal publications and meeting presentations to share guidance and best practices, the lack of endorsements from appropriate national and local leadership, and the lack of understanding in how to implement new changes were mentioned.

Furthermore, respondents felt dissemination of clinical guidance should be targeted to reach the appropriate audiences while minimizing information overload.

Additionally, access to guidance, literature and resource disparity, and user inertia were identified as barriers to clinical guidance adoption. Respondents frequently mentioned various staffing hurdles and compliance with guidance that could be partially attributable to resistance to change and reliance on existing beliefs, local customs, or practices.

Rank ordering barriers to clinical guidance adoption

The sentiments expressed in the open-ended responses were mirrored in the rank ordering of predetermined barriers to the development, adoption, and implementation of guidance (see figure 1A). When ranked from most to least challenging, inaccessible platforms (M=2.54; SD=1.15) and non-user-friendly guidance (M=2.89; SD=1.19) were indicated as the most challenging, followed by paywalls (M=2.93; SD=1.56), irrelevancy to institutional resources (M=3.02; SD=1.42), and lack of authorship transparency (M=3.74; SD=1.31).

Areas for improvement in clinical guidance: open-ended/free-text responses

Respondents reported the priority areas for change largely within three sectors: the development, storage, and use of guidance. Overall, guidance should be rooted in evidence-based practices, with informative and clear applications. The quality of current research must improve to produce higher-quality evidence as a foundation for new resources. Respondents expressed that attempts to address this problem should include frequent updates to clinical guidance to ensure they remain current and timely. Frameworks, tools, and resources should be modified to support guidance development, as respondents indicated the current approach is rigid. Increased communication, collaboration, and consensus between the societal and organizational authors of guidance will improve the development of these materials. The right people must be present at the “authorship table,” and this is achievable through multidisciplinary and multispecialty representation, including engagement and participation from representatives at non-level I trauma centers. Sample quotes are provided in table 2.

Some respondents indicated guidance should be standardized nationwide, and others suggested guidance should have a degree of customizability for differing resource settings and unique populations. In addition, many respondents identified that effort should be undertaken to translate guidance into various languages for further dissemination and comprehension.

Overall, guidance should not only be available, but cost-effective and accessible without paywalls. Specific recommendations to increase accessibility included: the creation of a free, central repository that allows for open access of guidance; the use of computer and phone applications; friendly algorithms (that could be embedded into an electronic medical record); and visual materials.

Respondents indicated that adherence to guidance is essential, and effective monitoring and tracking techniques could improve such adherence. Developing a mechanism to capture regular feedback from stakeholders and trauma organizations is necessary to ensure the usability of guidance.

Rank ordering areas for improvement

The sentiments expressed in the open-ended responses were mirrored in the rank ordering of predetermined areas for change (see figure 1B). Respondents indicated that collaboration among professional societies for clinical guidance development (M=2.14; SD=1.15) and dissemination via comprehensive and equitable communication channels (M=2.71; SD=1.23) were the most important solutions to change the creation of guidance. Additional areas for improvement included open access (M=2.74; SD=1.57), established plans for regular updates of clinical guidance (M=2.86; SD=1.34), and diverse authorship (M=3.8; SD=1.41). Respondents proposed that ease of use at bedside (M=2.16; SD=1.24) and integrated options for adapting the clinical guidance to local needs (M=2.76; SD=1.32) were the most important priority areas of change for using guidance. An emphasis on patient-centered outcomes (M=2.79; SD=1.58), ease of discoverability (M=3.08; SD=1.37), and availability on a platform that allows for open feedback and utilization tracking (M=3.45; SD=1.36) were additionally identified as areas for targeted improvement.

Changing perspectives after structured empathy-building exercise

During facilitated group dialogue, key “objectives” to improve trauma clinical guidance were identified. Building on the preconference survey and user story groups, new solutions mentioned in the MVP include the idea that the creators of guidance should build consensus around a standard for future guidance and pilot test with one or two clinical scenarios. In addition, there should be clarity on roles and decision criteria across the continuum of care and between hospital teams. Key partners identified that guidance should facilitate multidisciplinary team functioning, including clarity on roles and patient care hand-offs to enhance understanding of provider responsibility and the optimal sequence of care. While participants want guidance used every time for every patient, an argument to include provisions for resource environment and social context was highlighted. Overall, guidance should support the optimal patient experience and healing through trauma-informed care practices.

During this group dialogue, key partners also identified multiple “risks” to improved trauma clinical guidance. Risks to the adoption of clinical guidance include insufficient funding to enact recommendations from standardized guidelines and insufficient buy-in from end-users. Identified barriers to lack of adoption and buy-in from end users included lack of customizability based on resource settings, concern that guidance will rapidly outdate, and difficulty with accessing multiple large files. While there was overlap between the risks identified in the MVP and the barriers identified both in the preconference survey and user story groups, several new challenges were identified. Elicited in the MVP, one theme centered on citation pressure and the complex logistics that limit collaboration. This results in multiple, separate, and often dissimilar guidance regarding a trauma diagnosis. In addition, transparency for patients raised concern. Establishing transparency could lead to provider liability if the establishment of ranges is inadequate and the detailed expectations of clinical outcomes are not considered. Furthermore, concern about changing the clinical guidance design and dissemination may be gratifying to the participants, but ultimately lead to no substantial patient benefit.

Discussion

A mixed-methods novel consensus-building approach was used as part of the Design for Implementation: The Future of Trauma Research and Clinical Guidance (DFI) conference to identify the current barriers to developing and implementing trauma clinical guidance and to highlight the priority areas for change to better support end users. Clinical guidance outlines recommendations for clinicians about a specific patient population. Disciplines, such as oncology, have a standard approach to clinical guidance for the care provided to their specific patient population.18 Trauma care does not have standard clinical guidance for the basic care needed to save a life, as it is based on a variety of professional organizations and their guidance. Key partners identified that they want an accessible, easy-to-use platform that houses consolidated, coordinated, and up-to-date guidance. The desired standard for future guidance would be a live repository to update guidance and increase user compliance. Overall themes identified through the preconference survey included lack of consensus, communication, and standardization across institutions as well as the insufficient use of tools and infrastructure to support the development, dissemination, and adoption of clinical guidance. Guidance is not updated in a timely fashion, and it can be difficult to access when located behind paywalls or among various organizations. Clinicians may be unable to adopt clinical guidance into their practice for multiple reasons, including inapplicability to less resourced practice settings, lack of user-friendliness (eg, easy to follow visualizations), institutional inertia, or lack of timely access.

In identifying priorities for change, keeping clinical guidance centered on the end-user and the patient was the unifying goal. Guidance should be timely and updated frequently. Guidance should support the optimal patient experience and healing through trauma-informed care practices that support both the clinicians and their patients. Through the user stories and MVP processes, conference attendees prioritized the need for collaboration of professional societies for clinical guidance development and dissemination, and for these organizations to use comprehensive and equitable communication channels for guidance dissemination.

A key theme for this group of community partners was the need for open access for information dissemination. While the perception of open access is often mixed, open access can be advantageous for collaborating through the rapid identification of similar work.19 Another theme identified was to produce clinical guidance that is consolidated, coordinated, and current. Trauma, like transplantation or cancer, needs clinical guidance to ensure proper care for this patient population is consistent regardless of location or available resources. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) has a process for developing and updating guidelines that apply to over 97% of cancers in the USA.18 Trauma guidance that mirrors the NCCN approach could address identified problems with regular updating of clinical guidance and accessibility issues. Similarly, liver transplantation utilizes an open access platform focused on the end user, with the patients in mind. This platform allows experts to collectively work on clinical guidance.20 This method guides the user and creates a sustainable flow for guidance implementation and dissemination.20 This idea of open access collaboration is not a new concept in healthcare, yet trauma is not up to speed on meeting this need. The structure of future guidance should include work on the sequence of care while addressing the need for guidance applicable to patients evaluated in all resource settings.

Limitations

Potential limitations of this study include the selected respondent population. The key partners identified are invested in this topic and work. They largely represent academic clinicians at highly resourced trauma centers, and their opinions may not reflect the needs of providers across the nation. Additional context inquiry is underway to investigate what other populations need from trauma clinical guidance.

Next steps

Planned next steps include needs assessments among more diverse end user populations and development of a guidance prototype based on the objectives defined in the MVP. Specific recommendations included the creation of a free, central repository that allows for access to guidance, the use of computer and phone applications, and friendly algorithms. Development of these prototypes for trauma clinical guidance is underway. A key theme for this group of community partners included a need for standardized guidance. However, many participants voiced concern that clinicians may be unable to adopt clinical guidance into practice due to inapplicability to their practice setting or financial burden. Future discussions will need to address the risks of standardizing guidance that does not allow for customization across resource settings.

Facilitators will engage participants at the second annual meeting of the DFI conference series to refine the developed prototypes to see if the products meet the objectives. Rapid qualitative analysis of these sessions will be performed, generating summary statements. In addition, conference participants will be asked to complete a survey to evaluate priorities for trauma clinical guidance product elements and dissemination strategies.

Conclusions

Trauma clinical guidance must be current, consolidated, and coordinated with patient-centered outcomes in mind. Recommendations from the conference attendees highlighted the need for the creation of guidance that is standardized nationwide, but that has a degree of customizability for differing resource settings and unique populations. Guidelines should not only be available, but cost-effective and accessible without paywalls and translated into multiple languages for further dissemination and comprehension. By reforming the development and dissemination of clinical guidance, more providers will have on-demand access to relevant resources, leading to improved outcomes and increased trauma survival.
