AC decoction augments chemotherapy in NSCLC by reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment: attenuating Tregs and enhancing CD8+ T cells
Authors: Wei Ye, Mei-ye Li, Zhuo-zhen Jiang, Tian-yong Hu, Han-chao Gao, Bao-hui Cheng, Ke-wang Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Astragalus membranaceus - Codonopsis pilosula decoction (ACD), is traditionally used as beverage to activate immunity. This study aimed to investigate the combined use of ACD and TP (paclitaxel and cisplatin) against non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) in a orthotopic mouse model. In this study, the anti-tumor and immunomodulation efficacies of ACD and TP were evaluated in an orthotopic lung cancer model. After treatment with ACD for one week, the luciferase-expressing Lewis cells were injected into the lungs of male C57 mice and treated with ACD and/or TP for about 2 weeks. Then, the flow cytometry, ELISA, IHC and WB were employed to assess immune cell populations, cytokine production and protein expression levels. Besides, in vitro cytotoxicity of ACD against NSCLC cells was performed using CCK-8 assays, while its immunomodulatory potential was evaluated using a co-culture system incorporating NSCLC cells and patient-derived PBMCs. Our results demonstrated that combination of ACD and TP had the most potent effects on tumor burden and immunity activation among treatment groups. ACD (1 g/kg) and TP (paclitaxel 22.5 mg/kg, cisplatin 12.5 mg/kg) treatment reduced tumor burden by 53.16%, as validated by IVIS imaging in the mouse model. Flow cytometry from mice spleens proved that the combined use of ACD and TP treatment significantly decreased Treg production and increased CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration. Besides, ACD and TP treatment resulted in downregulation of FOXP3 and EZH2 expression, and suppression of PI3K/AKT phosphorylation in mice tumor. Furthermore, in vivo results showed that ACD had no direct cytotoxicity against NSCLC cells, which significantly enhanced PBMC-mediated tumor cell killing and promoted IFN-γ and TNF-α production in patient-derived co-culture systems. In conclusion, the combination of ACD plus TP demonstrated potent anti-tumor and immunomodulation effects against NSCLC in association with reduced Treg activity and increased CD8+ T cell infiltration. Our study provides compelling preclinical evidence supporting ACD as an immunomodulatory adjunct to conventional NSCLC therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47805-y.

Introduction

The World Health Organization’s GLOBOCAN 2022 statistics indicate that lung cancer retains its status as the tumor with the highest global incidence (12.4% of total cases) and the predominant cause of cancer-related mortality (18.7% of all deaths)1. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes approximately 85% of all lung cancer diagnoses and demonstrates particularly aggressive clinical behavior, contributing disproportionately to global lung cancer mortality2. In addition to the remarkable success of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and tyrosine kinase inhibitors in improving clinical outcomes and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment3, neoadjuvant chemotherapy has achieved a landmark improvement in the 5-year overall survival rate, elevating it from 42% to 58% compared to conventional approaches4. However, the emergence of resistance and adverse effects during prolonged use of these therapies highlights the critical need to identify novel immunotherapeutic targets.

The combination chemotherapy of paclitaxel (T) and cisplatin (P) is the most basic and widely used treatment regimen in the clinical treatment of lung cancer. Cisplatin mainly induces tumor cell apoptosis by damaging DNA, while paclitaxel mainly inhibits tumor cell proliferation through cell cycle arrest5. The TP therapy regimen can effectively improve the survival rate and overall survival of advanced and metastatic NSCLC. However, the immune deficiency caused by chemotherapy, as well as factors such as acquired drug resistance in tumor cells, make the current efficacy of chemotherapy still unsatisfactory6. It is an urgent problem to enhance the patient’s immune system and improve the efficacy of chemotherapy in the clinical treatment of NSCLC.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), with its recognized therapeutic efficacy, favorable safety profile, and low incidence of adverse effects, has fueled both scientific interest and its widespread clinical adoption as a synergistic adjuvant to mainstream cancer therapies7. The fundamental theory of TCM in NSCLC involves enhancing anti-tumor immunity and modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME) to restrict tumor growth and metastasis8. Huangqi (Astragalus membranaceus), a TCM known for its properties of replenishing qi and strengthening the spleen, is widely used in various TCM formulations9. The primary applications of Astragalus currently focus on its immunomodulatory, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties, with demonstrated mechanisms including the inhibition of NSCLC cell proliferation and metastasis, suppression of angiogenesis, and induction of tumor cell apoptosis10. Additionally, studies have demonstrated its immunomodulatory effects, including the induction of M1 macrophage polarization, enhancement of natural killer (NK) cells and CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity, and suppression of regulatory T cells (Tregs) activity11,12. Dangshen (Codonopsis pilosula), a widely used Chinese medicinal herb, possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-tumor activities13. Its active components demonstrate significant anti-tumor effects by inhibiting tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion through suppression of angiogenesis14. In recent years, significant research has explored the roles of Huangqi and Dangshen, along with their bioactive anti-tumor components, in cancer treatment and immune regulation. Shenqi Fuzheng Injection (SFI), containing ACD as primary components, has demonstrated immunomodulatory effects and improved therapeutic outcomes in cancer patients15. This combination synergistically enhances vital energy (Codonopsis tonifying middle Jiao, Astragalus promoting Yang) while mitigating chemotherapy toxicity16. Thus, the aim of this study is to clarify the anti-tumor and immunomodulatory efficacies of ACD combined with TP against NSCLC and elucidate the underlying mechanisms of action. The herbal decoction was prepared with Astragalus and Codonopsis at a clinically established ratio of 2:1, a formulation grounded in the TCM “sovereign-minister” principle where Astragalus (sovereign) tonifies qi to expel pathogens and Codonopsis (minister) strengthens the middle jiao to support the host, thereby embodying a host‑supportive therapeutic strategy.

Evidence indicates that CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the TME of NSCLC patients are of pivotal significance for anti-tumor immunity17. CD8+ T cells, known for their cytotoxic capabilities, are established as a prognostic marker in NSCLC18. CD4+ T cells exhibit a dual role, supporting anti-tumor responses through the production of IFN-γ and TNF-α to enhance CD8+ T cell function, while also secreting anti-inflammatory mediators that may contribute to tumor progression19. Although the prognostic value of stromal CD4+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) is not yet confirmed, a high CD4/CD8 ratio exhibits robust correlation with adverse survival outcomes across NSCLC patients20. Furthermore, Treg infiltration in the NSCLC tumor microenvironment drives immune evasion and confers resistance to therapy through several distinct mechanisms21. These include: (1) secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10, TGF-β, and IL-35; (2) depletion of local IL-2; (3) granzyme B/perforin-mediated killing of NK cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs); and (4) CTLA-4–mediated competitive inhibition of effector T cells via B7 binding22. Given this pivotal role in sustaining an immunosuppressive TME, therapeutic strategies that target Tregs—either by reducing their infiltration or blocking their suppressive activity—hold promise for improving immunotherapy outcomes in NSCLC23. FOXP3+CD4+CD25+ regulatory T cells are critical for maintaining immune homeostasis and tumor immunosuppression. The differentiation and function of these cells are controlled by the transcription factor FOXP3 via multiple mechanisms, including TGF-β signaling, immune checkpoint modulation, and chemokine signaling24. Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) is a critical epigenetic modulator with functions ranging from the governance of cellular processes (e.g., cell survival, proliferation, stem cell self-renewal) to maintaining Treg stability and functional integrity in tumor tissues25. Notably, EZH2 deficiency destabilizes FOXP3 in activated Tregs, triggering their conversion to pro-inflammatory states26, identifying EZH2 as a viable immunotherapeutic target.

The PI3K/AKT pathway is frequently dysregulated in NSCLC, often through PIK3CA mutations27. While early PI3K inhibitors showed limited efficacy, combining them with epigenetic modulators like EZH2 inhibitors may enhance response and reduce toxicity28. Targeting the EZH2-PI3K/AKT axis in Tregs could achieve tumor-selective immunomodulation while preserving peripheral immunity29.

Building upon this mechanism, identifying natural compounds that modulate the ‘EZH2-PI3K/AKT axis’ holds significant value. Astragalus and Codonopsis, traditional immune-enhancing herbs, contain bioactive components with established immunomodulatory and antitumor properties. For example, astragaloside IV from Astragalus has been shown to regulate PI3K/AKT signaling30, while Codonopsis polysaccharides and lobetyolin can influence immune response and tumor growth via pathways including PI3K/AKT31. However, direct evidence is still lacking on whether the combined decoction (ACD) specifically modulates Treg function in the tumor microenvironment through the EZH2-PI3K/AKT axis to treat NSCLC.

The present study sought to assess the anti-tumor and immunomodulatory effects the combination of ACD and TP in the orthotopic lung cancer mouse model. Furthermore, we utilized flow cytometry, ELISA, IHC and WB to determine the combine of ACD plus TP on Tregs, CD8+ T cell ratios, cytokine secretion, and the modulation of critical signaling pathway components, offering a detailed understanding of its anti-tumor mechanisms.

Material and methods

Cells and reagents

Stable luciferase-expressing mouse lung cancer Lewis cells (LLC-LUC) were purchased from Shanghai SAGE Biotech, China, and were generated through lentiviral transfection of the Luc gene. Human A549 lung cancer cells and SW1573 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). Human A549 lung cancer cells (ATCC) and LLC-LUC cells were maintained under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). The culture medium for both cell lines was DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. LLC-LUC cells were maintained with 1 µg/mL puromycin to ensure stable luciferase expression and cell growth.

IL-2, IFN-γ, IL-10, and TGF-β ELISA kits (Dakewe Biotech, China), luciferin (Beyotime Biotechnology), and the following from ABclonal Technology (China): CCK-8, anti-CD25 (A2984), anti-CD8a (A23305PM), anti-PI3K (A19742), anti-AKT (A18675), anti-phospho-PI3K (AP1463), anti-phospho-AKT (AP1332), and anti-β-actin (AC026) were used in this study. EZH2 (5246 S) was provided by Cell Signaling Technology (USA), and Anti-FOXP3 antibody (sc-166212) was obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (USA).

Aqueous extract preparation

The dried herbs of Astragalus membranaceus and Codonopsis pilosula were sourced from Shenzhen Heshuntang Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (China). Astragalus membranaceus (voucher no. C21042001) and Codonopsis pilosula (voucher no. C23010501) were harvested in Gansu Province, China.

Dried Astragalus membranaceus (600 g) and Codonopsis pilosula (300 g) were subjected to a two-stage extraction process: (1) pre-soaking in 2 L ultrapure water (30 min), (2) programmed decoction in standardized equipment (15 min boiling at 100 °C → 30 min reflux at 80 °C). The decoction was filtered through sterile gauze, and the residue was re-extracted with 2 L of ultrapure water under identical conditions. The combined filtrates were concentrated by rotary evaporation at 60 °C to 20% of the original volume. Aliquots of the concentrated extract were flash-frozen at −80 °C for 12 h and lyophilized (−55 °C condenser, 0.220 mbar vacuum) to yield the final ACD extract powder at a yield of 25.97%. The product was stored in a desiccator at 25 ± 2 °C for subsequent applications.

Mouse orthotopic Lewis cancer model

To assess the anti-tumor and immunomodulatory properties of ACD and TP, we established an orthotopic lung cancer model using 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (Guangdong Provincial Animal Center, China). All mice were housed under a SPF environment, and animal procedures were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Longhua Shenzhen (NO. LHRY-2103004). Besides, we confirmed that all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and the study was reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Mice were randomized into five experimental groups: (1) control group: PBS, 0.2 ml per day by gavage, (2) low-dose ACD (ACD-low) group: 0.5 g/kg ACD, 0.2 ml per day by gavage, (3) high-dose ACD (ACD-high) group: 1 g/kg ACD, 0.2 ml per day by gavage, (4) positive control (TP) group: Paclitaxel (22.5 mg/kg single i.p.) + cisplatin (12.5 mg/kg two i.p. doses), and (5) combination (TP + ACD-high) group: 1 g/kg ACD, 0.2 ml per day by gavage with paclitaxel/cisplatin regimen as in group 4. Animal group sizes (n = 8 per group) were determined based on (i) previous orthotopic Lewis lung carcinoma studies in C57BL/6 mice that reported significant differences in tumor burden and immune parameters with similar group sizes32,33, and a post-hoc power analysis using the actual endpoint data (IVIS bioluminescence signal). With the observed effect size (≈ 53% reduction in tumor burden in the combination group vs. control) and measured variability, the study achieved > 90% power to detect the difference at α = 0.05 (two-sided t-test). This number is consistent with commonly used sample sizes in similar pharmacodynamic studies of lung cancer in mice, which typically range from 6 to 10 animals per group to detect significant differences with 80% power at α = 0.0534. The doses for the TP regimen were derived from the human clinical standard for NSCLC (paclitaxel: 135 mg/m²; cisplatin: 75 mg/m²) and converted to mouse equivalents using body surface area normalization via the Km factor method (conversion multiplier ≈ 12.3). Based on preliminary toxicity studies, we adopted a pharmacologically optimized and well-tolerated dose at 50% of the calculated maximum equivalent, resulting in the final protocol: a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of paclitaxel at 22.5 mg/kg one week post-inoculation, followed by cisplatin at 12.5 mg/kg administered as two i.p. doses on consecutive days, starting one day after paclitaxel. Mouse body weight was measured and recorded twice per week.

Based on the TCM principles of “preemptive treatment” and “strengthening body resistance to eliminate pathogens,” we designed a one-week ACD pretreatment regimen. The aim was to pre-modulate the host constitution and fundamental immune status, in order to observe its potential enhancing effects on subsequent tumor inoculation and chemotherapy response. One week after initiating ACD treatment, LLC-LUC cells (3 × 105 cells in 50µL PBS) were orthotopically injected into the lung parenchyma of anesthetized mice (1.25% Avertin, intraperitoneal injection) using a 29-gauge insulin syringe. A 1-cm skin incision was created along the mid-axillary line at the 4th-5th intercostal space. Subcutaneous muscles and connective tissues were carefully dissected to expose the underlying lung tissue. After tumor cell inoculation, IVIS imaging was taken on day 7, with fluorescence signals confirming successful model establishment. Treatments were continued according to the predefined group protocols, and IVIS imaging was conducted every 3–5 days to monitor tumor burden of lung. Changes were quantified based on fluorescence intensity, expressed as average radiance (p/s/cm²/sr). The experimental endpoint was set at Day 23, as determined by excessive tumor burden in the control groups. At the endpoint, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin (150 mg/kg body weight). Twenty minutes later, anesthesia was induced by an initial intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (Avertin, 1.25% w/v, 0.2 mL per mouse). The depth of anesthesia was assessed by the loss of pedal withdrawal reflex approximately 10 min later. If an adequate surgical plane was not achieved, a supplemental dose of 0.1 mL was administered. Once fully anesthetized, in vivo bioluminescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Spectrum system (PerkinElmer). Upon completion of the imaging session, mice maintained under deep anesthesia were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The lungs were immediately excised to allow for ex vivo imaging.

Flow cytometric analysis of T lymphocyte subsets in murine spleen

Following euthanasia, spleens were harvested and mechanically dissociated through a 70-µm cell strainer using 3 mL of mouse lymphocyte isolation medium. Single-cell suspensions were dispensed into EP tubes, overlaid with 1 mL of culture medium (1640), and centrifuged (800 × g, 30 min) using a horizontal rotor. The lymphocyte layer was collected, washed with 10 mL of 1640 medium, and pelleted by centrifugation (300 ×g, 5 min) to obtain splenic lymphocytes. Lymphocytes were fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min, washed with PBS, and surface-stained with CD4-FITC, CD8a-APC, and CD25-BV421 antibodies (30 min, 4 °C, dark). After permeabilization (30 min, 4 °C), cells were stained intracellularly with Foxp3-PE overnight at 4 °C, washed, and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II).

Tumor progression-related cytokines in TME

Grinding beads were added to a pre-chilled grinding tube, and a small section of lung cancer tissue was weighed and recorded. PBS (three times the tissue weight) was added, and the tissue was homogenized using a programmable grinder (60 Hz for 1 min, repeated twice). Lung tissue homogenates were subjected to centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C), and the clarified supernatants were aliquoted and preserved at −80 °C. Cytokine concentrations (IL-2, IL-10, IFN-γ, and TGF-β1) were quantified using species-specific ELISA kits (Dakewe Biotech, China) following the protocols, and differences among treatment groups were analyzed to assess changes in the TME.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

After fixation in 4% PFA (48 h, RT), lung tissues from tumor-bearing mice were processed through dehydration, paraffin embedding, and longitudinal sectioning (5 μm) on a Leica RM2235 microtome. Tissue sections stained with H&E by standard methods were examined under a Nikon TS100-F microscope for imaging. The tumor burden was quantified by determining the ratio of tumor area to total lung area per tissue section. Using ImageJ, the absolute tumor area (mm²) was measured, and the mean area was computed from three sections per animal.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Following fixation in 4% PFA (48 h) and paraffin embedding, 5 μm tissue sections were prepared on a Leica RM2235 microtome. Prior to immunostaining, sections underwent deparaffinization (xylene/ethanol), heat-induced antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (95 °C, pH 6.0), peroxidase quenching with 3% H₂O₂ (RT, 10min), and blocking with 5% BSA (30 min). Incubation with primary antibodies (1:200, 4 °C, overnight) was followed by HRP-conjugated secondaries (1 h, RT). Signal was visualized with DAB (8 min), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining, ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and mounting. Microscopy was performed on a Nikon Eclipse TS100-F system.

WB analysis

Prior to electrophoresis, protein samples were denatured in loading buffer (4:1 ratio) at 95–100 °C for 8 min and centrifuged. Aliquots of 20–40 µg of lysates were then resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels. Post-transfer to activated PVDF membranes, blots were blocked with 5% BSA and sequentially probed with primary (1:1000, 4 °C, overnight) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, 1 h, RT). Signals were detected by ECL and quantified via ImageJ relative to loading controls.

HPLC analysis of ACD

The chemical composition of the ACD was analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Agilent, USA). A binary gradient elution system was employed, consisting of mobile phase A (0.1% phosphoric acid in water) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile). The following gradient program was applied: 0–5 min, 5% B; 5–14 min, 5–14% B; 14–25 min, 14–17% B; 25–30 min, 17–20% B; 30–120 min, 20–50% B; 120–125 min, 50–95% B; 125–130 min, 95% B. Separation was performed on a Sepax Sapphire C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min, with detection carried out at 254 nm.

Cell viability assay

After plating cells in 96-well plates (5 × 10³ cells/well), cultures were treated with specified ACD concentrations. (The highest concentration of ACD used in the CCK-8 assay was 1 mg/mL, with serial dilutions prepared using a two-fold dilution method) and incubated for 24 h and 48 h. The CCK-8 assay was performed by: (1) aspirating supernatants, (2) a addition of 110 µL freshly prepared CCK-8 solution (10 µL CCK-8 [ABclonal] in 100 µL DMEM) per well, (3) 2-hour incubation under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), and (4) quantifying absorbance at 450 nm (SpectraMax Paradigm).

In vitro co-culture system

This study was conducted under the ethical approval of our hospital (Approval No. KY2023008). Peripheral blood specimens were obtained from NSCLC patients, and PBMCs were separated using density gradient separation.

PBMCs were activated per established co-culture protocol through 4-hour anti-CD3 antibody (Biolegend, 300438) stimulation under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂) After stimulation, the CD3 antibody solution was carefully removed by gentle aspiration followed by two PBS washes. Lymphocytes were plated in 24-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) and co-cultured with harvested SW1573 and A549 cells at defined ratios (tumor cells: lymphocyte = 1:0, 1:5, and 1:10). Tumor cells were plated at 2.5 × 104 cells per well in 1 mL complete culture medium. The culture system was supplemented with 100 U/mL recombinant human IL-2 to maintain lymphocyte viability, in combination with a concentration gradient of ACD extract (0, 0.25, 0.5 mg/ml).

Following a 16-hour co-culture under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), the supernatant was harvested for cytokine quantification via ELISA. Wells were then replenished with fresh medium, and the cultures were extended for an additional 2–3 days. When control wells achieved 80–90% confluency, cultures were processed by: (1) medium aspiration, (2) fixation (4% PFA, 15 min), (3) staining (0.1% crystal violet, 20 min), and (4) imaging (ChemiDoc MP, Bio-Rad) with subsequent image quantification using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD for in vitro experiments and mean ± SEM for in vivo experiments. SD reflects variability among independent in vitro replicates, whereas SEM indicates precision in estimating the population mean from in vivo samples.

For multiple-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA was used. Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied in in vivo studies to compare treatments against a single control; Tukey’s HSD was used for all pairwise comparisons in in vitro assays or relevant in vivo analyses. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test.

Exact p-values are provided in figures and text, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using [GraphPad Prism 9.0].

Results

The combined use of ACD and TP inhibited in vivo growth of Lewis tumors

To evaluate the effects of the combination of ACD and TP on tumor growth and immunomodulation, we established an orthotopic NSCLC model through intrapulmonary injection of LLC-LUC cells (3 × 10⁵ cells in 50 µL PBS) into the left lung parenchyma of C57 mice. Body weight monitoring revealed no significant differences among groups during the early and mid-treatment phases. However, all experimental groups exhibited modest weight reduction during days 20–23, with the TP group exhibiting the most pronounced reduction, though without statistical significance (p = 0.23) (Fig. 1A).

Tumor progression was monitored using IVIS at multiple time points. On day 6 post-modeling, tumor signals were detectable in all groups, with no significant intergroup differences. By day 17, IVIS signals increased across all groups, with the Control group showing the most substantial rise. Compared to the Control group, the TP and ACD-low group demonstrated a modest, non-significant reduction in tumor burden, while ACD-high, TP and TP + ACD-high groups exhibited significant tumor growth inhibition, with IVIS signals reduced to 38.60%, 20.22% and 48.02% of the Control group, respectively. The experiment was terminated on day 23 due to mortality in the Control group. At this endpoint, IVIS analysis revealed a dramatic increase in tumor signals in the Control group, while signals in the treatment groups either stabilized or decreased compared to day 17. Tumor signals in the ACD-low, ACD-high, TP and TP + ACD-high groups were reduced by 16.05%,33.80%, 16.77% and 53.16% versus Control as 100% (Fig. 1B). Ex vivo IVIS imaging of lung tissues further confirmed these findings, with the TP + ACD-high group showing the most significant reduction in tumor signals (51.13% vs. Control) (Fig. 1C-D).

These results demonstrate that ACD effectively inhibits lung tumor proliferation in vivo. When combined with TP, the treatment produces the most pronounced reduction in tumor burden among all groups (TP + ACD-high).

T lymphocyte subsets (CD4+, CD8+) and Tregs (CD4+CD25+FOXP3+) were analyzed by flow cytometry using standard immunophenotyping protocols. Lymphocytes were sequentially stained for surface CD4/CD8a and intracellular CD25/FOXP3 to identify Tregs (CD4+CD25+FOXP3+), with the gating hierarchy shown in Fig. 2.

ACD and TP treatments significantly altered the immune cell profile in tumor-bearing mice. Both ACD and TP treatments significantly enhanced anti-tumor immunity by increasing CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration while suppressing immunosuppressive Tregs, ultimately improving the tumor immune microenvironment. Treg populations in the ACD-low, ACD-high, and TP groups were reduced by 48.69%, 63.68%, and 77.82%, relative to the Control group. These results indicate that ACD and TP treatments augment the immune response against lung cancer in mice through the regulation of Tregs. Notably, the combination of TP and ACD-high group exhibited the most pronounced immunomodulatory effects: Tregs decreased by 88.20% (from 8.39% to 0.99%), CD4+ T cells increased from 7.24% to 22.39%, and CD8+ T cells rose from 4.63% to 18.47%. Additionally, the ratio of CD4/CD8 declined from 1.76 in the Control group to 1.14 in the TP + ACD-high group, representing a 35.23% reduction. ACD extract significantly improved anti-tumor immunity by increasing CD4+/CD8+ T cell infiltration and concurrently attenuating Treg-mediated immunosuppression, with optimal effects achieved through combination with standard chemotherapy.

The combination of ACD and TP influenced the cytokines production in TME in tumor-bearing mice

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of ACD aqueous extracts in a murine model, lung tissue homogenates were prepared from tumor-bearing Lewis mice through mechanical grinding. Subsequent ELISA analysis of the tumor microenvironment revealed that, all four treatment groups showed elevated concentrations of IL-2 and IFN-γ, accompanied by a marked reduction in IL-10 and TGF-β1 levels, as compared to the untreated controls (Fig. 3). The TP + ACD-high group showed the most robust effects, with a 2-fold increase in IL-2 and IFN-γ levels, and the greatest reduction in IL-10 and TGF-β1. These results highlight the ability of ACD aqueous extracts to modulate the TME by upregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibiting immunosuppressive factors, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for enhancing anti-tumor immunity.

The combination of ACD plus TP downregulated FOXP3 and upregulated CD8a expression in mice tumor

Lung tissues were collected from each tumor-bearing mouse for histological and IHC analysis. As shown in Fig. 4, tumor burdens were significantly reduced in all treatment groups relative to the untreated controls. The results revealed that ACD aqueous extract effectively inhibited lung tumor growth, with the ACD-low, ACD-high, and TP + ACD-high combination groups all showing reduced tumor areas. Quantitative analysis demonstrated that the ACD-high group exhibited tumor areas comprising 28.22% of total lung area (representing a 44.06% reduction versus control, set at 100%), while the TP + ACD-high combination group showed even greater efficacy with tumor areas occupying only 15.05% of lung area (a 70.17% reduction versus control; Fig. 7A). These findings indicate that ACD treatment significantly suppresses lung tumor progression, with the TP + ACD-high combination demonstrating optimal therapeutic efficacy Fig. 5.

HPLC analysis of ACD aqueous extract

The chemical composition of ACD was analyzed by HPLC. The chromatographic profile revealed that the major constituents of ACD remained stable throughout the experimental process. As depicted in Fig. 6A, the major chemical constituents quantified in ACD were as follows: formononetin (0.0214 mg/g), ononin (0.2551 mg/g), calycosin (0.0533 mg/g), and lobetyolin (0.8002 mg/g).

ACD extract exhibited no obvious cytotoxicity on LLC-LUC and A549 cells

In vitro cytotoxicity assessment revealed that ACD (0.0625–1.0625 mg/mL) exhibited no significant cytotoxic effects on either murine (LLC-LUC) or human (A549) NSCLC cell lines following 24–48 h, as quantified by CCK-8 assay. Neither cell line showed proliferation inhibition across the tested concentration range. After 24 h of ACD treatment, both cell lines displayed varying levels of proliferative activity, peaking at 1 mg/mL. Furthermore, after 48 h, all ACD-treated groups maintained significantly higher proliferation rates compared to untreated controls. The experimental results reveal no significant cytotoxic response to ACD treatment in the studied NSCLC cell lines (Fig. 6B-C).

ACD enhances lymphocyte-mediated tumor cell killing in vitro

In the co-culture system of A549 and NSCLC patient derived PBMCs, ACD treatment significantly elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels: IFN-γ increased from 35.3 pg/mL to 723.28 pg/mL, while TNF-α rose from 3.76 pg/mL to 38.2 pg/mL. Dose-response analysis confirmed that 0.25–0.5 mg/mL ACD optimally enhanced cytokine secretion (Fig. 7A). When the ratio of tumor cell to PBMC reached at 1:5 and 1:10, ACD treatment significantly suppressed A549 cell proliferation (Fig. 7B). Notably, this inhibitory effect was absent in PBMC-free cultures, indicating that ACD’s anti-tumor activity against NSCLC is mediated through lymphocyte activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly IFN-γ and TNF-α.

Discussion

TCM aims to reestablish systemic homeostasis and augment host defense mechanisms, particularly through immunomodulation. Clinical studies suggest that TCM as an adjuvant therapy can lead to improved efficacy, alleviation of cancer-related symptoms, and better quality of life for NSCLC patients35. Our previous research demonstrated that HSJZ suppresses Tregs and tumor growth via the PI3K/AKT pathway36. Astragalus membranaceus and Codonopsis pilosula represent key herbal components of HSJZ, which also revealed proliferation inhibition and apoptosis induction in hepatocellular carcinoma cells, with no significant cytotoxicity toward normal human cells37. Based on our previous findings and the reported roles of ACD in immunomodulation, we sought to evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy and immunomodulatory properties of ACD plus TP using a luciferase-labeled orthotopic Lewis lung carcinoma model with high metastatic potential.

To assess the anti-tumor efficacy and immunomodulatory properties of ACD and TP in vivo, we employed an orthotopic NSCLC model in C57 mice. This model was selected based on its superior clinical relevance, which recapitulates key pathological features of human NSCLC, thereby providing a biologically relevant platform for assessing treatment therapeutic potential. All groups exhibited progressive weight loss during the terminal phase (days 20–23), with TP treatment showing the greatest reduction (Fig. 1A). Although no statistically significant difference was shown, this enhanced chemotherapy-associated weight loss parallels the cachexia commonly seen in advanced NSCLC patients. These results imply that conventional chemotherapy may intensify cachexia development, highlighting the need for adjunct therapies to improve patient resilience during cancer treatment. ACD treatment significantly suppressed tumor progression, as quantified by reduced IVIS signals in individual mice and lung tissues (Fig. 1B,D). Our study demonstrates that the combination of ACD plus TP produces greater tumor inhibition (53.16% reduction) than either treatment alone. ACD monotherapy achieved 33.80% tumor inhibition, while the combination further increased this effect. We propose that ACD remodels the tumor immune microenvironment, while TP directly kills tumor cells; together they yield more pronounced anti-tumor activity than monotherapy. This enhanced efficacy, rather than formal pharmacological synergy, is supported by the observed reductions in tumor burden, Treg suppression, and CD8 + T-cell enhancement. This strategy establishes a more effective anti-tumor response and offers a promising approach to enhance efficacy while reducing chemotherapy toxicity. Our findings corroborate previous reports on the anticancer properties of aqueous extracts from related herbs, such as Astragalus and Turmeric, in inhibiting colorectal cancer progression38. Preliminary experiments indicated that the human-equivalent TP dose caused excessive toxicity in some frail mice, leading to severe weight loss and compromised health. Therefore, the formal study utilized only 50% of this dose. This likely explains why the TP-alone group did not exhibit a more pronounced therapeutic effect in our findings. This preclinical evidence is further bolstered by clinical studies confirming that Shenqi Fuzheng Injection (main component is ACD), when adjunct to chemotherapy, not only enhances patient well-being and mitigates treatment-related adverse effects but also exerts a synergistic anti-tumor efficacy in NSCLC15. These data support the potential therapeutic benefits of ACD decoction as an adjunctive treatment in lung cancer.

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects, we analyzed the percentage of Tregs and T cell populations in spleens of mice upon treatment completion. Flow cytometry results showed that mice bearing Lewis tumor treated with ACD and TP showed an increased proportion of CD8+ T cells and a decreased proportion of Tregs, along with a reduced CD4/CD8 ratio. The most dramatic decrease in CD25+FOXP3+ Treg cells was observed in the combination of TP and ACD-high group (Fig. 2), accompanied by substantial increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a noticeable decline in the CD4/CD8 ratio. While our previous research on HSJZ only observed a reduction in the proportion of splenic Tregs in Lewis-bearing mice, this study extended the analysis to include CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets and their ratio. It is demonstrated that tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells function as the principal executors of anti-tumor immunity by recognizing cancer cells through TCR and mediating tumor cell killing by the granzyme or perforin pathway39. Tregs, an immunosuppressive CD4+ T cell subset characterized by FOXP3 and CD25 expression, is crucial in TME regulation by maintaining immune homeostasis and suppressing excessive immune activation22. These findings revealed that the improved efficacy in combination group (TP + ACD-high) partly from the immunotherapeutic by dampening Treg activity and potentiating CD8+ T cell-mediated immunity.

ELISA results showed that high-dose ACD treatment markedly elevated secretory levels of IL-2 and IFN-γ cytokines secreted by lymphocytes in the TME, while significantly decreasing the levels of IL-10 and TGF-β1 (Fig. 3). Notably, the most profound changes were observed in the TP + ACD-high combination group. These results quantitatively correspond to our in vitro studies, in which ACD-treated PBMCs from NSCLC patients elicited a robust increase in the secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α as well as enhanced cytotoxicity to NSCLC cells when co-cultured with A549 cells (Fig. 7). These results indicate the potential ability of ACD to modulate immune function and suppress tumor growth, promoted the efficacy of TP in anti-tumor against NSCLC. Our in vivo study revealed that ACD markedly lowered the percentage of Tregs and enhanced the secretion of cytokines that disrupt the normal functioning of tumor cells, resulting in tumor suppression in lung cancer mice. The in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that ACD exerts adjuvant anti-tumor effects when combined with TP may through mechanisms as follows: (1) reducing Treg-mediated immunosuppression, (2) activating immune effector CD8+ T cells, and (3) modulating cytokine release profiles.

At the experimental endpoint, lung tissues from the tumor-bearing mice in each group were harvested, followed by H&E staining and IHC analysis. H&E results demonstrated significant reduction in tumor areas in both the high dose ACD and the combination of TP + ACD-high group (Fig. 4A). These histopathological findings correlated well with the results of IVIS in lung tumors (Fig. 3B,C,D). IHC results demonstrated that high-dose ACD and chemotherapy effectively downregulated FOXP3 expression while upregulating CD8a in tumor tissues. Notably, this immunomodulatory effect was synergistically enhanced in the combination group (Fig. 4B,C). The IHC results validate our flow cytometry findings, confirming that the combination treatment (TP + ACD-high) markedly decreases CD25+FOXP3+ Treg infiltration in the TME of lung cancer mice. Consistent with our prior findings for HSJZ, and the combined use of ACD and TP achieved to an even greater extent in enhancing the expression of CD8a within the TME.

To elucidate mechanism of action, WB analysis of protein expression in lung tissues was performed. The treatment groups significantly downregulated EZH2, FOXP3, CD25, p-PI3K, and p-AKT expression in Lewis lung carcinoma models (Fig. 5A). The most pronounced inhibitory effects were observed in the TP + ACD-high dose combination group. Studies have shown that herbal formulations containing Codonopsis pilosulae and Astragalus membranaceus exert anti-tumor effects through PI3K/AKT pathway inhibition40,41. The dual targeting of the PI3K/AKT pathway and EZH2 has been previously identified in our studies as a key mechanism underlying the anti-tumor efficacy of TCM36. Meanwhile, Chen et al. demonstrated EZH2 inhibition sensitizes PIK3CA-mutant lung tumors to PI3K inhibitors in vivo42. Geng et al. proved that EZH2 promotes lung cancer progression via the AKT signaling pathway43, suggesting that inhibition of AKT pathway may induce the suppression of EZH2. In our study, ACD treatment, especially when combined with TP, strongly blocks the PI3K/AKT pathway and lowered the protein levels of EZH2, FOXP3, CD25 in lung cancer models. EZH2 is known to faciliaate immune escape by stabilizing FOXP3 in Tregs, suppressign recruitment and activity of natural killer and CD8+ T cells44. Consistent with this, EZH2 inhibitors have shown to reduce regulatory T cells, promote memory chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) CD8 phenotypes, and reduce exhaustion, resulting in a decreased tumor burden45. Our findings indicate that the observed downregulation of PI3K/AKT phosphorylation and EZH2 protein levels was associated with reduced Treg markers and increased CD8 + T cell infiltration, consistent with previously reported roles of the EZH2–PI3K/AKT axis in Treg stability. These correlative findings suggest that modulation of this pathway may contribute to the immunomodulatory effects of ACD, although direct causal relationships require further validation. These results suggest that ACD holds promise as a potential adjuvant therapy for NSCLC by affecting immune-suppressing Treg cells and blocking cancer signals.

Besides, our in vitro results indicate that the ACD, even at a high concentration of 1 mg/mL, did not induce direct cytotoxicity in either murine LLC-LUC or human A549 NSCLC cell lines (Fig. 6B,C). The data aligns with our previous study showing that HSJZ aqueous extracts within certain concentration ranges did not exert direct toxic effects on cancer cells36. To evaluate the lethality potential of ACD, we established a co-culture system using PBMCs isolated from NSCLC patients and A549 cells. ELISA quantification of co-culture supernatants demonstrated significant elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α, which correlated with marked suppression of A549 cell proliferation (Fig. 7). This pattern of immune-mediated cytotoxicity, indicate that ACD induces NSCLC cell cytotoxicity indirectly via PBMCs and their secreted cytokines, revealing an immunomodulatory rather than direct cytotoxic mode of action. These findings parallel the effects of Codonopsis pilosula polysaccharides, which inhibit tumor growth and upregulate cytokines (IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF-α), enhancing anti-tumor immunity46. These collective findings emphasize a shared mechanism among traditional herbal medicines - enhancing anti-tumor immunity - and demonstrate that ACD can activate PBMCs to augment cytokine-mediated cytotoxicity against NSCLC cells.

This study collectively provides systematic preclinical evidence that ACD enhanced the efficacy of TP by immunomodulatory and anti-tumor activities. Our findings demonstrate that the combination of ACD and TP significantly reduces tumor burden and remodels the immunosuppressive TME in a murine NSCLC model, highlighting its therapeutic potential. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the findings are derived from a syngeneic mouse model whose tumor microenvironment differs from human NSCLC. Second, the study focused on Treg cells and did not examine ACD effects on other immune components such as macrophages or NK cells. Third, our data show clear associations between ACD treatment, downregulation of the EZH2–PI3K/AKT axis, reduced Treg activity, and enhanced CD8+ T cell responses, these remain correlative. Future studies employing pharmacological inhibitors, siRNA knockdown, or CRISPR-based approaches will be necessary to establish causality. Although the combination of ACD and TP clearly produced greater efficacy than either agent alone, we did not perform formal synergy analysis; therefore, we describe the effect only as enhanced combination efficacy. Future studies using isobologram or combination index methods would be required to determine whether true pharmacological synergy exists. Furthermore, the molecular mechanisms underlying Treg suppression by TP + ACD, particularly at genomic and proteomic levels, warrant further investigation, and clinical trials remain essential to establish the therapeutic efficacy and safety of this combination in NSCLC patients.
