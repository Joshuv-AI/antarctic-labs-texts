In silico analysis of stress distribution in ultra-translucent multilayered zirconia veneers of varying thickness and yttria content using Finite Element Analysis (FEA)
Authors: Shivani Khandelwal, Devansh Verma, Anuj Bhardwaj, Surekha Puri Bhat, Shivani Bhardwaj, Suraj Arora, Dian Agustin Wahjuningrum, Ajinkya M Pawar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical success of laminate veneer restorations largely relies on the mechanical properties of the materials used. With the rise of ultra-translucent multilayered zirconia, it is crucial to evaluate the impact of facial thickness and yttria content on fracture resistance. This study aimed to computationally assess and compare the fracture resistance of zirconia veneers with varying facial thicknesses and yttria levels using Finite Element Analysis (FEA).Materials and methodsA standardized 3D model of a maxillary central incisor featuring a butt-joint incisal design was created using ANSYS R18.1. Veneers with thicknesses of 0.75 and 1.00 mm were modeled using 3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP zirconia. Supporting structures, such as the periodontal ligament and bone, were simulated. Occlusal forces of 15 N at 90° (protrusive) and 50 N at 60° (oblique) were applied to the palatal surface, and the stress distributions were analyzed using the von Mises criteria. Mesh convergence was verified, and the material properties were considered to be isotropic and elastic.ResultsThe highest von Mises stress was 687.4 MPa in 5Y-YZP veneers at 0.75 mm thickness under oblique loading, while 3Y-YZP veneers showed significantly lower stress (407.2 MPa). Under a 50 N oblique load, 3Y-YZP with a thickness of 1.00 mm had a strength of 418.3 MPa compared to 561.3 MPa at 0.75 mm (25.5% reduction), while 5Y-YZP decreased from 687.4 MPa to 601.7 MPa (12.5% reduction). Overall, the 3Y-YZP veneers consistently exhibited superior mechanical performance compared to the 5Y-YZP veneers under all loading scenarios.Clinical significanceThis FEA-based simulation emphasizes the importance of selecting the appropriate zirconia type and veneer thickness based on load conditions. The 3Y-YZP is preferable for high-stress cases such as bruxism, and 5Y-YZP is preferable for aesthetic areas with adequate thickness.Keywords: Dental aesthetics. facial thickness, Finite element analysis (FEA), In silico simulation, Stress distribution, Zirconia veneers

Introduction

A well-aligned smile, determined by the maxillary central incisors, is crucial for facial beauty and self-confidence [1, 2]. Dental restorations enhance smiles and positively impact mental health, highlighting the link between aesthetics and psychological well-being [3]. Veneers and implants improve appearance and oral function, leading to better health outcomes across all age groups [4].

Ceramic veneers are thin porcelain shells bonded to the front teeth to improve their appearance by addressing issues such as spacing, discoloration, and misalignment while preserving tooth integrity [5]. They have a survival rate of 89.3% over 9–10 years, with 79.4% showing minimal problems such as discoloration [6]. Success factors include fracture resistance, adhesive strength, tooth reduction, proper bonding, and care [7–10].

Veneer thickness is crucial in veneer restorations, especially for minimally invasive preparations or worn dentition, where enamel preservation is essential [11]. Thinner veneers help avoid overpreparation but must still provide adequate fracture resistance [12]. Research indicates that decreasing veneer thickness can lead to higher stress concentrations, increasing the risk of mechanical failure if the appropriate material is not selected [11].

CAD/CAM technology has transformed dentistry by allowing the precise and efficient production of dental restorations, especially veneers [13–15]. Studies have reported a 100% survival rate for CAD/CAM veneers over four years and 99.83% over 30.8 months [16–18]. Chairside systems utilize materials such as lithium disilicate, porcelain, and zirconia, emphasizing the aesthetics of lithium disilicate and porcelain [19, 20].

Zirconia veneers are gaining popularity in dentistry because of their superior strength and aesthetics [21]. Initially, the high opacity of zirconia limited its aesthetic appeal, leading to the use of glass ceramics on top for improvement [22]. The transformation toughening mechanism provides high strength and fracture toughness, making it more durable than other dental ceramics [23]. Recent advancements in high-yttria-content zirconia, specifically 3Y-YZP (3 mol% yttria) and 5Y-YZP (5 mol% yttria), have significantly enhanced aesthetic outcomes and clinical applications. The 3Y-YZP features a predominantly tetragonal phase that offers excellent fracture resistance. In contrast, 5Y-YZP had a higher cubic phase content, providing better translucency but lower fracture toughness. These differences are crucial in clinical decisions, particularly for veneers, where reduced facial thickness can affect material integrity.

Although lithium disilicate is the gold standard for anterior veneers because of its optical qualities, modern ultra-translucent multilayer zirconia has increased translucency and fracture strength [18]. This study focused on examining the biomechanical variations within zirconia systems rather than comparing other ceramic classes.

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) has been used in dental biomechanics since 1973 to analyze stress and strain. It improves fracture mechanics and failure analysis, aiding in the prediction of material behavior under stress [24]. However, independent research on the fracture resistance of zirconia veneer restorations, particularly for maxillary central incisors, is limited. This is concerning, given the increasing use of zirconia veneers in aesthetic dentistry, as their longevity and effectiveness are vital for patient satisfaction and success.

Previous FEA investigations on 3Y- and 5Y-zirconia focused mostly on crowns or bulk specimens, with no comparisons of multilayer veneers at clinically relevant thicknesses using a conventional butt-joint preparation. The present study provides new insights into how yttria content and thickness affect the anterior loading behavior of veneers. This study is novel in that it uses multilayered ultra-translucent zirconia, clinically realistic oblique loading (50 N at 60°), and comprehensive periodontal support modeling to mimic anterior functioning conditions precisely.

This study aimed to use FEA to assess the fracture risk of ultra-translucent multilayered zirconia veneers with yttria content and two thicknesses (0.75 and 1.00 mm). The null hypothesis was that no difference in stress distribution existed between the veneer types and thicknesses.

Materials and methods

This study aimed to evaluate the fracture resistance of ultra-translucent multilayered zirconia veneers, specifically 3 mol% (3Y-YZP) and 5 mol% yttria-stabilized zirconia (5Y-YZP). The objective was to assess the effect of different facial thicknesses on the durability of veneers under mechanical stress, focusing on a recently extracted maxillary central incisor. Figure 1 depicts a Finite Element Mesh (FEM) model of the tooth, which helps analyze the stress and strain responses to occlusal forces by dividing the tooth into smaller segments for a detailed mechanical response evaluation during biting and chewing. A healthy, caries-free, crack-free, and restoration-free maxillary central incisor was selected for modeling. The tooth was extracted owing to periodontal issues, despite being verified as intact. Teeth with cavities, restorations, or structural problems were excluded to create an accurate anatomical model of the tooth. The tooth was scanned using a laboratory extraoral scanner (Medit T710, Medit Corp., Seoul, South Korea) with an accuracy of 4 μm. The scan was performed under regular lighting conditions, and the tooth was digitized into a high-resolution STL file. This dataset was used in CAD software to generate the 3D geometry of the crown, preparation design, and veneer models. The STL file of the scanner was imported into CAD modeling software (Autodesk Meshmixer 3.5 and Fusion 360; Autodesk Inc., USA) to clean the mesh and generate a closed solid body for FEA. The solid model was exported to the STEP format and then loaded into ANSYS R18.1 for meshing and simulation. The model was constructed using an optical STL scan rather than micro-CT imaging; therefore, no DICOM data were employed.

Finite Element Mesh (FEM) representation of the maxillary central incisor tooth model

The finite element mesh consisted of linear tetrahedral solid elements with minimum and maximum element sizes of 0.1 and 0.6 mm, respectively. Mesh convergence was confirmed with a tolerance error margin of less than 5% in the peak von Mises stress values across the tested models. Following convergence, the final mesh had 471,917 elements and 111,655 nodes for the sound-incisor model. The butt joint preparation model had 507, 590 elements and 125, 333 nodes, whereas the palatal chamfer preparation model had 504, 351 elements and 124, 462 nodes. The model was stabilized by completely limiting the nodes at the base of the cortical bone, leaving all other bone surfaces free to undergo deformation. The tooth-PDL-bone and veneer-tooth interfaces were modeled as bonded contacts to simulate physiological support and prevent interfacial sliding of the restoration.

The tooth was prepared using a butt joint incisal veneer design with a 0.40 mm chamfer margin and 1.00 mm incisal reduction to ensure secure and aesthetic adhesion of the veneer. To standardize the preparation and isolate the impact of the material type and veneer thickness, we employed the same butt-joint design for both materials without palatal wrapping. Two precise digital models were created by scanning the prepared teeth using advanced 3D imaging technology (ANSYS R18.1 software). Each model featured two facial reductions: one at 0.75 mm and the other at 1.00 mm. These thicknesses (0.75 and 1.00 mm) represent the clinically recommended range for multilayered zirconia veneers. Unlike etchable glass ceramics, zirconia cannot be manufactured in ultrathin designs (< 0.3–0.5 mm) because of its low etchability and higher cracking risk. Thus, 0.75–1.00 mm is the optimal thickness for zirconia restorations. Figure 2a and b show the FEM of the 0.75 mm thickness, illustrating the stress distribution, whereas Fig. 2c and d depict the FEM of the 1.00 mm thickness for a comparative analysis of the mechanical properties.

Finite Element Models of zirconia veneers with two facial thicknesses. a Tooth prepared at 0.75 mm; (b) veneer for 0.75 mm preparation; (c) tooth prepared at 1.00 mm; (d) veneer for 1.00 mm preparation

Supporting tissues were also modeled to maintain anatomical accuracy and reproduce realistic biomechanical behavior. The periodontal ligaments (PDL), spongious bone, and cortical bone structures were designed and included in the finite element models using the root-form geometry of the central incisor. The elastic modulus of the PDL was 0.0689 GPa, which is consistent with the accepted literature ranges, although it is acknowledged that reported values vary significantly in the literature [25]. These structures played an important role in simulating in vivo boundary conditions, allowing for a more accurate stress analysis.

The performance of the two zirconia veneer types could be thoroughly compared owing to this design distinction. A framework for evaluating the fracture resistance of each veneer type under controlled conditions was successfully created by restoring each model using multilayered zirconia with two distinct yttria concentrations (3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP) (Table 1). Following the normal FEA methodology, all materials, including the enamel, dentin, PDL, bone, and zirconia, were considered homogeneous, linearly elastic, and isotropic during the simulation. The cement layer was intentionally omitted from the model to reduce the geometric complexity and avoid variability in the cement thickness, which could introduce additional simulation errors. This simplification is consistent with previously published FEA studies that aimed to isolate the influence of restorative material properties.

Each model’s palatal surface was subjected to mechanical loads as part of the fracture resistance evaluation. Loads were applied 2 mm below the incisal edge along the palatal surface. A force of 15 N was applied vertically (Z-axis) at a 90° angle, and a 50 N load was applied obliquely at a 60° angle in the vector direction of X: 0.5, Y: 0.0, Z: −0.866. Load steps were applied in a single-step static simulation (Fig. 3). The loads of 15 N (vertical) and 50 N (oblique) were chosen to reflect light functional and moderate oblique loading conditions commonly applied to anterior teeth in FEA models These values are within the established anterior force ranges, where protrusive/incisal contact pressures are typically 10–20 N and oblique functional/parafunctional loads range from 40 to 90 N, according to previous investigations [24, 25]. The purpose of these simulations was to replicate the functional and parafunctional occlusal forces. Validation of the results was performed by comparing the stress patterns and values with those previously published in vitro studies on 3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP zirconia, confirming consistency with the fracture load behavior reported by Alraheam et al. [27] and Zhang et al. [28].

Applied boundary conditions and loading vectors in the FEA model. Vertical (15 N at 90°) and oblique (50 N at 60°) loads were applied 2 mm below the incisal edge

The quantitative evaluation revealed that the peak stresses in our simulations were consistent with the reported fracture stress ranges for both materials. The 3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP values produced under oblique loading fell within the experimental thresholds reported by Alraheam et al., and the locations of the peak stress matched their observed fracture sites, demonstrating the accuracy of the model [27].

This strategy prevented an unwarranted concentration of stress at any location by promoting an even distribution of stress throughout the palatal surface. This approach provided a more realistic depiction of the zirconia veneer’s ability to withstand fractures in scenarios that closely mirrored the functional demands found in everyday life. The goal of this extensive experimental design was to provide useful information on the dependability and performance of ultra-translucent multilayered zirconia veneers, ultimately advancing restorative dentistry.

Results

The stress distributions of the dental components were carefully analysed using the von Mises stress criterion, a widely recognised approach for determining material yield under complex loading situations. Figure 4 presents a comprehensive depiction of the von Mises stresses calculated for the 0.75 mm and 1.00 mm facial thickness tooth models, with a particular emphasis on the effects of protrusive movements, such as those encountered during biting or grinding, and mechanical forces associated with the tearing function. This extensive analysis not only shows the stress concentrations at various sites inside the tooth structure, but also helps us understand how these dynamic interactions affect the integrity and performance of dental components in real-world circumstances. All FEA images were standardized with the same color legend and von Mises stress scale (60–1100 MPa) to allow for direct visual comparison across veneer types and thicknesses.

von Mises stress distribution for zirconia veneer models under loading. a Stress distribution for 0.75 mm veneer; (b) stress distribution for 1.00 mm veneer

The color gradient in Fig. 4 depicts the von Mises stress levels, with a scale on the left ranging from deep blue at 60 MPa to bright red at 1100 MPa. The brilliant red zones at the crown of the tooth indicate areas of high stress, showing regions subjected to the highest load or strain, particularly on the incisal surface. In contrast, the lower areas and base of the tooth are portrayed in dark blue, signifying minimal stress. This low-stress area is most likely due to the boundary conditions or fixation, indicating good support and stability in those places.

The Von Mises stress distributions in two yttria-stabilized zirconia types, 3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP, under a 15 N vertical load at 90° are presented in Table 2. Thicknesses between 0.75 mm and 1.00 mm were analyzed at four locations: mesial, distal, cervical, and incisal. The results demonstrate that 5 Y-YZP had greater stress values than 3Y-YZP in most locations, which can be attributed to the higher yttrium content, which promotes translucency but reduces fracture toughness. The maximum stress was recorded in the incisal region of 5Y-YZP at a facial thickness of 0.75 mm, reaching 407.19 MPa. The increase in thickness from 0.75 to 1 mm often reduced the stress values, indicating improved stress distribution. While both materials handle vertical masticatory stresses, 3Y-YZP may provide higher mechanical resistance at high-stress locations.

Under 50 N oblique loading, the highest von Mises stress was 687.4 MPa in 5Y-YZP veneers (0.75 mm thickness), nearing the material’s tensile strength of ~ 600 MPa, indicating a risk of failure (Table 2). In contrast, 3Y-YZP veneers of the same thickness exhibited a maximum stress of 561.3 MPa, well within their tensile strength range of 900–1200 MPa. 5Y-YZP exhibited consistently higher stress levels than 3Y-YZP. Increasing the veneer thickness from 0.75 mm to 1.00 mm significantly reduced stress for both materials, with 3Y-YZP stresses decreasing by 25.5% from 561.3 MPa to 418.3 MPa, and 5Y-YZP reducing by 12.5% from 687.4 MPa to 601.7 MPa, demonstrating improved stress distribution with greater material bulk.

Only the peak von Mises stress values are presented, which enable a clearer comparison across materials and conditions than the broad min-max ranges.

Discussion

The growing need for aesthetic and long-lasting dental restorations has fueled the development of advanced ceramic materials, such as zirconia. Ultra-translucent multilayered zirconia is a remarkable improvement in this discipline, as it combines mechanical strength with optical qualities suited for anterior esthetics [29]. This study aimed to use Finite Element Analysis (FEA) to determine how facial thickness (0.75 and 1.00 mm) and yttria content (3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP) affect fracture resistance under simulated masticatory forces. This study rejected the null hypothesis that there is no difference in fracture resistance between zirconia veneers with 0.75 and 1.00 mm facial thickness and yttria content (3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP).

Finite Element Analysis (FEA) results highlighted a significant difference between 3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP veneers, with 3Y-YZP displaying lower stress concentrations at all facial thicknesses. This advantage stems from its tetragonal phase, which enables transformation toughening to halt crack propagation and enhance its durability [30]. In contrast, although 5Y-YZP offers appealing translucency, its higher cubic phase content reduces the fracture toughness [30]. Alraheam et al. found that increasing yttria concentration from 3Y to 5Y diminishes fracture toughness and flexural strength [27]. Thus, for high-load-bearing restorations, especially in patients with bruxism, 3Y-YZP remains the superior choice for durability and reliability. These findings uniquely address veneer-specific behavior, which earlier studies by Alraheam and Zhang did not evaluate in relation to combined thickness and yttria content [27, 28].

Lithium disilicate is well-known for its esthetics and adherence; however, this study focused on zirconia, specifically ultra-translucent multilayered zirconia, which is preferred for anterior veneers owing to its strength, crack resistance, and optical performance. This study aimed to determine the differences between 3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP zirconia for minimally invasive veneer designs. Future research should compare zirconia with lithium disilicate or resin composite veneers to improve clinical guidance.

Lithium disilicate veneers can be made ultrathin (< 0.5 mm) and bonded strongly to resin-enamel, allowing them to function as reinforced monoblocks [18]. In contrast, 5Y-YZP, with its high cubic content, has a lower fracture toughness and lacks micromechanical etching, making it rely more on thickness for strength [31]. The increased thickness required for 5Y-YZP reflects its material properties and is not a contradiction of glass-ceramic behavior.

The thickness of the dental veneer significantly affects stress reduction during use [32]. In our study, 1.00 mm-thick veneers consistently demonstrated lower von Mises stress values than 0.75 mm veneers in both material types. Thicker veneers improve load distribution and structural integrity, which helps mitigate localized stress from clenching and bruxism [14, 31, 32]. At a 50 N oblique force, the highest stress (687.4 MPa) occurred in the incisal area of the 0.75 mm 5Y-YZP veneer, indicating that thinner veneers made from low-toughness materials are more susceptible to fractures.

The 25.5% stress reduction observed in 3Y-YZP as the thickness increased to 1.00 mm is most likely because of its greater elastic modulus and transformation-toughening tendency, which promote fracture deflection and energy dissipation under oblique loading. This supports the clinical guidelines for crafting thinner veneers from stronger materials [33]. Reducing peak stress below the tensile strength threshold of ~ 600 MPa can help prevent mechanical failures, while 3Y-YZP’s tensile strength of 900 to 1200 MPa provides a greater safety margin [34]. These findings are crucial for enhancing the durability of dental restorations for individuals with high-stress dental habits.

The application of von Mises stress in FEA effectively predicts failure under complex loads, highlighting mechanical vulnerabilities in incisal and cervical regions, particularly with low-toughness ceramics like 5Y-YZP [35]. High stress concentrations at the incisal margin and cervical transitions are associated with common clinical failures. Incisal tensile peaks cause veneer chipping and edge fractures, whereas cervical stress adds to debonding. Thus, von Mises stress patterns represent difficulties such as chipping, marginal cracks, and early delamination. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that incisal coverage and veneer geometry significantly impact fracture resistance, with sharper transitions being more prone to cracks [36]. Essam et al., further emphasized that smooth internal designs and adequate incisal reduction are crucial for stress distribution in dental systems [37]. This study incorporated these design features to enhance stress management.

The introduction of multilayer zirconia veneers has provided a significant step forward in aesthetic dentistry by replicating a tooth structure with graduations found in nature in terms of the thin enamel-type highly translucent outer layers and the thick dentin-type robust inner core [38]. The gradient structure of materials like 5 mol% yttria-stabilised tetragonal zirconia polycrystals (5Y-PSZ) produces improved optical properties, which is most important for anterior restorations [39]. The internal differences in microstructure and mechanical properties in each layer may lead to stress concentrations under functional loading and may not be structurally reliable [40]. Recently, it has been reported that even when translucency is optimised, 5Y-PSZ has worse fracture toughness than thin-walled, 3Y-PSZ, which was the veneer material mentioned above [31].

In our study, a 0.75 mm, multilayer 5Y-PSZ veneer showed significantly high stress concentration at the incisal margin under a simulated load of only 15 N. If high stress is present with such a low simulated load of 15 N, we see very real benefits to optimise the thickness and architecture of the veneer to achieve an outcome that is both durable and aesthetic. Although palatal wrapping can improve fracture resistance, the preparation was standardised as a non-wrapped butt-joint design in order to isolate the independent influence of yttria content and face thickness. Including palatal coverage would have introduced another geometric reinforcing variable, limiting a direct comparison of material-driven stress differences.

Lithium disilicate has been the standard material for anterior veneers due to its excellent cosmetic qualities and good fracture resistance [41]. However, advancements in ultra-translucent multi-layer zirconia, such as 5Y-YZP zirconia, now offer comparable translucency and superior strength [42]. The 3Y-YZP veneer can match or exceed the mechanical strength of lithium disilicate. Yet, for veneers thinner than 0.7 mm, lithium disilicate remains preferable due to its flexibility and bonding capabilities [43]. While zirconia may excel in strength, a slight increase in thickness could be necessary for optimal aesthetics. Therefore, it is crucial to balance aesthetic needs with functional considerations when selecting materials for restorative dentistry.

The use of FEA in our study is an effective non-invasive method for simulating mechanical behavior in dental biomechanics. Since the 1970 s, it has been essential for evaluating restoration performance without destructive testing. Our findings highlight the significance of material selection and veneer thickness. The 3Y-YZP offers superior strength for anterior restorations, allowing minimal preparation at 0.75 mm. In contrast, the 5Y-YZP requires a minimum facial thickness of 1.00 mm to prevent stress-related failures. These results support previous research, such as Panayong et al., [6] which reported over 89% survival for ceramic veneers over a decade, and Imburgia et al., [18] confirming 99.83% survival for CAD/CAM veneers over four years with appropriate case selection.

While stress reduction is generally regarded as positive, it is crucial to determine whether a decrease of ~ 100 MPa is clinically significant. In 5Y-YZP, a 100 + MPa decrease (from 687.4 to 542.3 MPa) lowers peak stress below its lower tensile limit (~ 600 MPa), potentially preventing failure in high-stress locations. Thus, the reduction is not only statistically significant but also clinically substantial, particularly for thin anterior restorations. Furthermore, only von Mises stress was utilized to forecast failures. While suitable for isotropic, ductile materials, zirconia is brittle and breaks under tensile or shear stresses, which von Mises does not directly capture. Future research should use maximal primary stress and Rankine criteria to better predict crack onset.

One significant limitation is the lack of a resin cement layer, which may affect stress distribution at the veneer-tooth interface. The elimination of the cement layer can modify interfacial stress transmission because resin cements absorb and redistribute occlusal loads. Previous FEA studies have demonstrated that removing this layer may move peak stresses toward the veneer or enamel [44, 45].

This study investigated the intrinsic mechanical response of zirconia veneers, specifically the impact of yttria content and veneer thickness on stress distribution. It removed the cement layer and tooth structure from primary stress reporting because effective prediction of resin cement behavior requires complicated modeling, which generates significant uncertainty. Future studies should look into interfacial cohesive-zone modeling or multilayered assemblies to properly assess debonding concerns [46].

Ceramics are brittle and typically fail under tensile stress. Von Mises stress was chosen for its stable scalar representation of multi-axial stress states, which is widely used in dental FEA research. It enables reliable comparisons between models of changing thicknesses and materials, while minimizing the false tensile peaks inherent in first principle stress estimates. Thus, von Mises is appropriate for determining stress distributions in 3Y- and 5Y-zirconia veneers under standardized loading conditions [35].

An additional challenge is the assumption that lower von Mises stress always translates into better results. While it serves as a valuable guideline, clinical success is also determined by aspects such as bonding quality, occlusal scheme, and parafunction [47]. A thin veneer with little internal stress may nonetheless fail if bonding is inadequate or loading is repeated at unfavourable angles. These factors are critical and require extensive investigation to better understand their effects [48]. These results have significant implications for material selection and preparation protocols: 3Y-YZP should be used in circumstances when minimal preparation is required or in patients with parafunctional behaviors (e.g., bruxism). 5Y-YZP is acceptable if aesthetics are important, but only if a minimum facial thickness of 1.00 mm can be obtained, ideally in low-load anterior regions.

Despite its benefits, the FEA model assumes isotropic and linear material behavior, which may not accurately represent multilayered ceramics. Because multilayered zirconia’s microstructure varies with layer, it behaves partially anisotropically. Modeling it as isotropic may slightly distort stress transmission across layers; hence, stated results should be considered as approximations rather than layer-specific.

Further in vitro validation through cyclic fatigue testing is needed, as the model is based on idealized static loads and does not consider real-world dynamic fatigue, thermal aging, or occlusal variability. It excludes cyclic fatigue, a primary failure mode in ceramics, and simplifies factors like resin cement layer thickness, which affects load distribution. Additionally, while forces of 15 N and 50 N simulate certain functional movements, they do not reflect the complex dynamics of actual occlusal loads influenced by temperature, chemical, and cyclic factors in the oral cavity.

Future studies should simulate the influence of the cement layer, thermomechanical loading, aging, and patient-specific anatomies. Upcoming FEA models should also include multilayer interface behavior and cyclic fatigue simulation, as these have a major impact on crack initiation, veneer chipping, and long-term durability. Incorporating fatigue models and dynamic occlusal forces would enhance clinical relevance. Finally, in vivo clinical trials are required to validate the FEA’s findings. These studies should compare the long-term survival rates of 3Y-YZP and 5Y-YZP veneers in different thicknesses to ensure thorough validation in a real-world scenario.

Conclusion

This FEA analysis shows that material composition and veneer thickness significantly affect stress distribution in zirconia restorations. The 3Y-YZP veneers exhibit lower stress values and better mechanical performance, making them ideal for patients with bruxism or less invasive situations. Within the limits of this FEA simulation, 5Y-YZP revealed significantly higher stress concentration at 0.75 mm, implying that additional thickness (about 1.00 mm) may benefit this material when employed in higher-stress anterior locations. However, this finding is material-specific and does not imply a universal clinical minimum thickness, especially when compared to etched glass-ceramic veneers like lithium disilicate. Clinically, 3Y-YZP is preferred for minimally prepared veneers and parafunctional patients, while 5Y-YZP should be reserved for low-load aesthetic areas with a thickness of ≥ 1.0 mm to minimize chipping and delamination risk.
