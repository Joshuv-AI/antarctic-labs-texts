Association between indoor air pollution and risk of oesophageal cancer: a Chinese population-based case–control study
Authors: Kuangyu Liu, Zi-Yi Jin, Fang Fang, Jin-Yi Zhou, Mia Hashibe, Jianyu Rao, Hua Zhou, Xing Liu, Liming Li, Lina Mu, Ming Wu, Jin-Kou Zhao, Zuo-Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOesophageal cancer is a highly lethal malignancy with geographic disparity and a heavy burden in developing countries. While smoking and alcohol are established risk factors, emerging evidence suggests that indoor air pollution (IAP) may also play a role through the inhalation of aerosols along the aerodigestive tract, but its relationship with oesophageal cancer remains poorly understood. Previous studies have focused on how IAP is associated with lung cancer, and evidence on oesophageal cancer is sparse, underpowered and inconclusive.MethodsThis population-based case–control study was conducted in the Jiangsu Province, China, from 2003 to 2010. We analysed 2969 cases and 8019 controls using unconditional logistic regression, adjusting for potential confounders. In addition to the six individual household air pollution sources, we also studied their combined effect by calculating the unweighted and weighted sum of these IAP sources to improve comparability. Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses by sex and smoking status, as well as by sex exclusively among non-smokers.ResultsIn both the overall sample and subgroups, higher exposure to various individual or combined IAP sources was associated with a dose-response increase in oesophageal cancer risk (weighted risk score (WRS) semi-Bayesian (SB)-adjusted OR (aOR): 2.70; 95% CI 2.36 to 3.07). Solid fuels appeared to have the strongest association (SB-aOR: 1.76; 95% CI 1.56 to 1.98), followed by coal used for cooking, passive smoking and poor ventilation in the kitchen and bedrooms. The vulnerability to different sources varied by sex and smoking status, and females among non-smokers consistently experienced a more profound impact from combined IAP exposure using WRS.ConclusionsExposure to common IAP is associated with an increased risk of oesophageal cancer among the Chinese population, with variations in susceptibility observed across sex and depending on smoking status.Keywords: Public Health, Air Pollution, Environmental Exposure, Primary Prevention, Epidemiology

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Indoor air pollution (IAP) is an established carcinogen for lung cancer, yet its role in oesophageal cancer remains unclear. Existing studies on IAP and oesophageal cancer have shown inconsistent and sometimes contradictory findings: some reported null associations, while others identified elevated risks associated with specific sources such as solid fuels, biomass use or passive smoking. However, these studies often relied on heterogeneous exposure definitions and assessed different pollution sources, making their findings difficult to compare or synthesise. As a result, the overall relationship between household air pollution and oesophageal cancer risk is still undetermined.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Using data from a large population-based case–control study in China, this study demonstrates the risks associated with multiple indoor pollution sources and their combined burden, quantified using unweighted and weighted risk scores, showing clear and monotonic dose–response relationships with oesophageal cancer incidence. The findings further show that females and non-smokers may be at a higher risk for certain indoor pollution exposures.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings underscore the importance of public health strategies to reduce IAP at the population level, particularly in relation to oesophageal cancer burden. Policies promoting cleaner household fuels and improved cooking or heating technologies are particularly relevant where solid fuels remain common. Strengthening household ventilation and enforcing smoke-free indoor environments may further reduce exposure to harmful combustion by-products.

Introduction

Oesophageal cancer is an aggressive upper gastrointestinal malignancy with high mortality and low survival. It ranked 11th in terms of incidence and 7th in terms of mortality in 2022 worldwide.1 Moreover, the number of new cases continues to increase, due to rising cases in males with oesophageal adenocarcinoma. However, the global burden is not evenly distributed—over half of the oesophageal cancer cases are clustered within the region known as the ‘Asian Esophageal Cancer Belt’.2 Despite improvement in surgery and treatment, the prognosis remains poor because patients are often diagnosed at advanced stages and oesophagectomy is a complex surgical procedure associated with substantial morbidity and postoperative complications.3 4 Therefore, it is crucial to elucidate the risk factors and potential carcinogens for oesophageal cancer to facilitate primary prevention.

Besides several established lifestyle risk factors (eg, tobacco, alcohol consumption, hot beverages), many putative environmental factors have also been investigated, among which indoor air pollution (IAP) has become an emerging concern because people spend as much as 90% of their time indoors,5 especially during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. IAP remains a major global environmental health challenge, with an estimated 2.4 billion people worldwide relying on solid fuels or biomass for cooking and heating.6 China, despite rapid economic development, still has substantial rural populations exposed to these pollution sources, placing it among the countries with the largest number of individuals at risk. Recent estimates indicate that household air pollution accounts for nearly one million premature deaths annually in rural China, representing over one-fifth of all mortality in these communities.7 Emerging epidemiologic evidence from low-income and middle-income settings further suggests that chronic exposure to IAP is associated with a range of adverse systemic health conditions, including frailty and anaemia, underscoring the broader health relevance of household air pollution exposure. This substantial health burden underscores the national importance of understanding the carcinogenic potential of common indoor pollution sources.8,10 In addition, indoor air contains an array of contaminants common to outdoor air (eg, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter (PM), volatile organic compounds (VOCs)), but is often overlooked despite the fact that indoor concentrations can be up to twice as high as those outdoors.11 12 Currently, typical sources of indoor exposure include fuel/tobacco combustion, cooking, furnishing, paints and exchanges from outdoor air, among others.13

These indoor pollution sources have been postulated as risk factors for various cancers along the aerodigestive tract. Nevertheless, current evidence primarily shows their carcinogenicity in lung cancer, while evidence on oesophageal cancer remains inconclusive. A meta-analysis in 2015 suggested that household air pollution may exhibit carcinogenic effects on upper aerodigestive tract cancers, but not oesophageal cancer, with limited sample sizes from only two studies.14 Similarly, Watkins et al did not detect an obvious association with solid fuel.15 On the other hand, an increased risk was found with IAP (HR=1.57; 95% CI 1.08 to 2.29).16 When examining specific sources, a meta-analysis of 16 case–control studies reported a substantially increased risk among biomass fuel users compared with non-users (pooled OR=3.02; 95% CI 2.22 to 4.11), with considerable heterogeneity across studies and settings, and elevated risks also shown for wood fuel exposure.17 Consistent with these findings, several individual studies illustrated positive associations between oesophageal cancer and domestic biomass smoke exposure (OR=2.1; 95% CI 1.1 to 3.8),18 passive smoking,19 as well as solid cooking fuels (HR=1.42; 95% CI 1.20 to 1.68).13 In contrast, evidence on passive smoking and upper digestive cancers has been mixed, often characterised by wide CIs and limited statistical precision.20,22 Overall, risk estimates for IAP sources remain heterogeneous, and population-level evidence evaluating combined IAP exposures is sparse. Given the inconsistent findings and sparse data, we aimed to further strengthen the quantitative evidence using a large population-based case–control study in the Chinese population. In particular, we examined six individual sources that are commonly used in developing countries. Furthermore, we investigated the combined effect of these sources. This study aims to foster an understanding of the link between IAP and oesophageal cancer and shed light on effective prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

The Jiangsu Four Cancer study is a population-based case–control study in Jiangsu Province, China. Data were collected in four counties—Chuzhou, Dafeng, Ganyu and Tongshan, from 1 January 2003 to 31 December 2010. Details of this study have been described elsewhere.23 Briefly, newly diagnosed cases of oesophageal, liver, lung and stomach cancers were identified within a year of diagnosis from local tumour registries, with diagnoses confirmed by pathology or imaging or clinical examinations at hospitals before reporting to tumour registry. They were eligible if they had lived in that same county for at least 5 years. Secondary and recurrent cases of malignant tumours were excluded. Controls were healthy individuals identified from the demographic database of each county and matched in a 1:1 ratio for each cancer case by county, sex and age (±5 years). Oesophageal cancer cases were coded as C15.0 to C15.9 according to the 10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases. In the subsequent analysis, we pooled the control groups for all four cancer types to enhance the statistical power. Regional hospitals were required to notify the local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention of every newly diagnosed case of oesophageal cancer. Study recruitment reached a participation rate of 30% among affected patients and 87% among the control group.23 A total of 2969 cases of oesophageal cancer and 8019 controls were included in the final dataset.

Exposure assessment

IAP exposure was assessed at the individual level from six potential factors/sources: solid fuels used for heating (yes/no), coal used for cooking (yes/no), high-temperature cooking oil (yes/no), ventilation in the kitchen (poor/good), ventilation in the bedroom (poor/good), passive smoking (ever/never). Information on these IAP sources was collected through a standardised, in-person questionnaire. Cases were interviewed as soon as new diagnoses were reported in the cancer registries, while controls were interviewed twice a year. Proxy respondents (ie, family members) were allowed to answer on behalf of the cases if the cases were too ill.

In addition to assessing exposure to individual sources, we developed two summary measures of IAP: unweighted and weighted. The unweighted IAP score summed all individual sources:

,where IAPi represents each IAP source, including solid fuel used for heating, coal used for cooking, high-temperature cooking oil, poor kitchen ventilation, poor bedroom ventilation and passive smoking. A score of 1 was assigned for an answer of yes and 0 for no. The total IAP score ranged from 0 to 6 and was categorised into four levels (0=no; 1 to 2=light; 3 to 4=moderate; 5 to 6=heavy).

Beyond simple summation, the weighted risk score (WRS) of IAP accounts for the effect size of each individual source:

,where βi is the coefficient from the overall logistic regression model including all IAP sources and adjusted for potential confounders.24 The WRS approach is a two-stage strategy for risk assessment. In the first stage, each IAP factor was evaluated individually to estimate its association with oesophageal cancer in fully adjusted logistic regression models. In the second stage, these effect estimates (βᵢ) were incorporated into a joint model to construct a weighted coefficient that summarises overall exposure burden. This weighting approach improves on simple exposure counts by allowing more influential IAP sources to contribute proportionally more to the combined exposure index than sources with weaker associations, while mitigating issues like multicollinearity that arise when highly correlated predictors are entered simultaneously into a model. The WRS therefore provides a more nuanced summary of cumulative household exposure, particularly in settings where only questionnaire-based exposure data are available and where different IAP sources exhibit varying effect sizes. We modelled the WRS both as a continuous variable and in categorical form: (1) into quartiles based on its distribution among controls and (2) as a binary variable dichotomised at the control‐group median. Collinearity was assessed, with values of variance inflation factors all below four.

Statistical analysis

In the primary analysis, we fitted multivariate unconditional logistic regression models to estimate the adjusted ORs (aORs) and corresponding 95% CIs for the association between each IAP source and oesophageal cancer risk. In addition, the semi-Bayesian (SB) approach was used to reduce false positives by combining observed data with prior information (Prior OR=1.00, 95% CI 0.25 to 4.00) to calculate the posterior estimates.25 Logistic regression and SB yielded similar results in our analyses. We reported both in tables and only SB-aORs in text and figures. The relationships between oesophageal cancer risk and both unweighted and weighted IAP scores were examined using both the fully adjusted logistic regression models and the SB approach.

Covariates were selected based on a priori knowledge of established risk factors and potential confounding factors, including age, sex (male/female), education (illiteracy/primary/middle/high school or college), place of residence, income 10 years prior (Yuan/year), body mass index, family history of oesophageal cancer (yes/no), pack-years of tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption. The temperature for tea drinking (cold/warm/hot) was only controlled in the sensitivity analysis. Missing covariates (<10%) were imputed with the county- and gender-specific median value of controls of the specific variable.

In secondary analyses, we examined effect modification by sex through subgroup analyses. In the models with only males and females, all covariates were adjusted except for sex. We also fitted separate regression models among non-smokers to minimise confounding by smoking. All covariates were adjusted except for the pack-years of smoking.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using complete-case analysis and additionally adjusted for tea consumption temperature for comparison with the primary results on summary measures. We evaluated the validity of proxy-reported data by conducting analyses that excluded all proxy respondents.

All analyses were performed using R V.4.0.2.

Results

The population characteristics are summarised in table 1. As observed in previous studies based on this dataset, over 70% of participants were male and less than 30% were female. Controls generally had higher income 10 years prior than cases. Specifically, 32.4% of controls had income over 2500 yuan per year compared with 21.8% of cases. We also observed a higher proportion of underweight individuals among cases and a greater proportion of overweight/obese individuals among controls. Cases were more likely to have a family history of oesophageal cancer (18.0%), and tended to be ever-drinkers (53.8%) and ever-smokers (61.0%).

Comparing the exposure characteristics between cases (n=2969) and controls (n=8019), we observed a higher percentage of cases than controls in all IAP sources, except for the use of solid fuels for heating (60.6% vs 64.8%) (figure 1). Overall, cases experienced greater exposures to poor air quality, including inadequate ventilation in both the kitchen (cases vs controls: 62.4% vs 53.6%) and the bedroom (60.5% vs 52.7%). Primary analysis of IAP among all participants

We considered both individual sources of IAP and summary measures of IAP (ie, unweighted and WRS) across all study participants. After adjusting for all covariates, all six IAP sources were positively associated with an increased risk of oesophageal cancer (figure 1). Among the six sources, solid fuel use for heating had the strongest association, with a semi-Bayesian-based odds ratio (SB-aOR) of 1.76 (95% CI 1.56 to 1.98). This was followed by coal use for cooking (SB-aOR:1.52; 95% CI 1.36 to 1.69) and passive smoking (SB-aOR:1.44; 95% CI 1.31 to 1.58). The effect of poor kitchen ventilation (SB-aOR:1.30; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.43) was comparable to that of poor bedroom ventilation (SB-aOR:1.27; 95% CI 1.15 to 1.40).

Since these sources were prevalent among the Chinese population and were associated with oesophageal cancer, we calculated summary IAP measures to illustrate the aggregated risk (table 2). For the unweighted measures, the risk of oesophageal cancer associated with a 1-unit increase in IAP was 1.17 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.20). Individuals exposed to 3–4 sources had twice the odds (SB-aOR: 2.06 (95% CI 1.64 to 2.57) and exposed to 5–6 sources had nearly three times the odds (SB-aOR: 2.98; 95% CI 2.32 to 3.83) compared with unexposed individuals. After adjusting for the effect size, the risk of oesophageal cancer associated with a 1-unit increase in the WRS was 2.70 (95% CI 2.36 to 3.07). A significant dose-response relationship was observed (Ptrend<0.001). Individuals in the high WRS group had an 84% increase in odds compared with the low WRS group, adjusting for potential confounders. Across quartiles, SB-aOR progressively increased from 1.39 (95% CI 1.21 to 1.59) in Q2, to 1.74 (95%CI 1.50 to 2.01) in Q3, and to 2.58 (95% CI 2.25 to 2.97) in Q4. Of the 2969 oesophageal cancer cases, 822 (27.7%) had information provided by a proxy respondent and 991 (33.3%) were pathologically diagnosed while the remaining cases were diagnosed by clinical, radiological and laboratory testing only. Sensitivity analyses yielded results consistent with the main conclusion (online supplemental Tables S1-S4).

Stratified analysis by sex, smoker status and among non-smokers

To better understand the effect of sex, we performed stratified analyses. All examined measures were found to be pronounced risk factors for oesophageal cancer, except for poor bedroom ventilation, poor kitchen ventilation, high-temperature cooking oil and an unweighted summary of IAP levels 1–2 among females (table 3; online supplemental Figure S1). Considering the sex-based difference in exposure sources, we found that females tended to show higher point estimates for several IAP sources, including solid fuels, coal cooking, high-temperature oil fumes and ETS. However, the CIs between males and females overlapped; therefore, we would interpret these subgroup findings as suggestive of differential susceptibility rather than evidence of statistically significant effect modification. The unweighted summary IAP showed more pronounced effects among males than females, but the WRS had less pronounced effects among males. The largest difference between sexes was observed in the highest exposure to unweighted IAP, where the SB-aORs were 3.16 (95% CI 2.32 to 4.30) and 2.44 (95% CI 1.59 to 3.75) among males and females, respectively.

The smoke burden-adjusted analysis also examined the link between oesophageal cancer incidence and various IAP exposures stratified by smoker status (table 4; online supplemental Figure S2). For individuals using solid fuels, the SB-aOR for oesophageal cancer was 1.86 (95% CI 1.59 to 2.18) among smokers, which was higher than that for non-smokers (SB-aOR: 1.64, 95% CI 1.35 to 1.98). This pattern, where smokers were more affected, was also observed in cooking with coal and poor kitchen ventilation. Nevertheless, non-smokers appeared to have a substantially heavier burden of passive smoking. In addition, the use of high-temperature cooking oil showed a modest association among non-smokers, with an SB-aOR of 1.25 (95% CI 1.08 to 1.44), whereas among smokers, this association was not obvious (SB-aOR: 1.08, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.23). Regarding unweighted IAP, the strongest association was observed in the highest categorical exposure (5–6) among smokers (SB-aOR: 3.04, 95% CI 2.12 to 4.35), which was markedly higher than that among non-smokers at the same exposure level (SB-aOR: 2.84, 95% CI 2.00 to 4.04). Continuous measures of weighted and unweighted IAP also showed increased SB-aORs across both smoking groups.

We further stratified the data by sex among non-smokers (online supplemental Table S5; online supplemental Figure S3), revealing patterns consistent with those observed in the sex-based stratification of the entire study population. In other words, females were more adversely affected by solid fuels, cooking with coal and passive smoking. Nonetheless, the oesophageal cancer risk due to the use of high-temperature cooking oil was slightly more pronounced in males (SB-aOR: 1.27, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.58) than in females (SB-aOR: 1.23, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.50), with overlapping CIs. Furthermore, the between-sex pattern for summary IAP closely mirrored the previous finding. When categorising unweighted IAP, female non-smokers exposed to the highest level (5–6) had an SB-aOR of 2.71 (95% CI 1.70 to 4.32). Similarly, WRS also revealed a stronger association with oesophageal cancer among females in the high-exposure category (SB-aOR: 2.08, 95% CI 1.67 to 2.59).

Discussion

In this population-based case–control study, poor indoor air quality was associated with a higher risk of oesophageal cancer across different measures examined in this study (eg, WRS, individual sources). While the impact of various IAP sources varied across sexes and smoking status—with certain sources exerting a more pronounced effect on one group over another—the binary WRS conveyed a clear message that females and non-smokers bore more substantial adverse effects. This finding is particularly striking given that smokers and males accounted for the majority of diagnoses. We further identified a distinct dose-response relationship using WRS as an integrated index, evidenced by the point estimates for higher exposure levels barely overlapping with the 95% CIs of the lower exposure levels. For instance, the CI for Q4 was entirely above the CI for Q3.

When examining by individual sources, the harmful associations were consistent in the SB-aORs for most pollution sources. The only exception was solid fuel for heating, where an apparent protective association in the crude model was no longer observed after full adjustment. This suggests that the crude association was likely confounded, leading to an underestimated risk. Over 40% of the Chinese population,26 27 especially in the rural areas, depend on solid fuels for cooking and heating. Although the usage has been declining, given the significant impact and the number of people involved, it is pivotal to reduce its use and increase access to clean alternatives (eg, electricity, natural gas). Other positive associations remained strong in subgroup analysis stratified by sex and smoking status. For instance, the impacts of solid fuel combustion, coal utilisation, high-temperature cooking oil and involuntary smoking were more pronounced in females. This disparity may stem from women’s greater involvement in household cooking and related domestic activities.28

Beyond sex-based difference, smokers were more affected by poor household ventilation whereas non-smokers were more affected by involuntary smoking. This discrepancy could be attributed to variations in baseline exposure to harmful substances. Smokers, who already have direct exposure to tobacco-associated pollutants, may experience compounded effects when poor household ventilation fails to disperse additional indoor pollutants effectively. On the other hand, non-smokers, who lack direct exposure to tobacco smoke, are more susceptible to the effects of secondhand smoke, as it serves as their primary source of tobacco-related pollutants. Among non-smokers, all summary measures showed stronger effects in females. Similar patterns have been observed in lung cancer, where the adverse effects of air pollution were significant only in females with EGFR wild-type lung adenocarcinoma who never smoked, suggesting a greater susceptibility among females than males.29 Considering that IAP is a widespread problem in low-resource countries and areas, it is crucial to allocate resources strategically, prioritising high-risk populations. Expanding access to clean cooking fuels and improved stove technologies can reduce chronic smoke exposure for females, who often shoulder a disproportionate health burden from traditional solid fuel use. Similarly, enforcing smoke-free indoor environments is critical to shield non-smokers from secondhand tobacco smoke, as heavy domestic exposure to environmental tobacco smoke has been associated with a nearly doubled lung cancer risk in never-smoking females.

Comparison with previous studies

The current epidemiological evidence on how IAP is associated with cancers other than the lung is not only limited, but also inconsistent. Most studies had small sample sizes, which may have yielded unstable estimates. Our findings generally align with a cohort study in Iran, where IAP was associated with oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), with a dose-dependent increase (HR: 1.57; 95% CI 1.08 to 2.29).16 Focusing on specific sources, solid fuel—specifically wood—was identified as a risk factor in hospital-based case–control studies in Zimbabwe (OR: 4.22; 95% CI 1.22 to 14.59)30 and Central/Eastern Europe (OR: 2.71; 95% CI 1.21 to 6.10).31 Similarly, a systematic review documented several African studies that linked biomass fuel use to oesophageal cancer.18

In examining the effects of passive smoking, the 95% CI of our study overlapped with another Chinese case–control study conducted in the same province, which investigated squamous cell carcinoma of the oesophagus (OR: 2.42; 95% CI 1.36 to 4.32).21 Conversely, a meta-analysis failed to detect an association between household air pollution and oesophageal cancer (OR: 1.92; 95% CI 0.82 to 4.49);14 however, the point estimate was comparable to the binary WRS in our study. In terms of specific chemicals, Mwachiro et al examined polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) metabolite levels in urine, but these levels were not significantly associated with advanced oesophageal dysplasia.32

Biological plausibility

The International Agency for Research on Cancer has identified household coal use and environmental tobacco smoke as group 1 carcinogens, while biomass use and high-temperature frying are classified as group 2A carcinogens, primarily based on evidence for lung cancer.33 However, evidence remains limited for other cancers along the upper aerodigestive tract. The biological plausibility of these associations is supported by several mechanistic pathways.

First, IAP contains a complex aerosol mixture of genotoxic and carcinogenic agents, and tissues like the oesophagus are directly exposed to inhaled substances such as PM, VOCs and PAHs. Various studies have identified these compounds as potent inducers of DNA damage, contributing to increased mutagenesis and DNA adduct levels.34 Specifically, PAH-induced p53 mutations have been linked to ESCC.35 Moreover, chronic exposure to these harmful agents can lead to persistent airway inflammation (eg, in the oesophagus and throat), resulting in increased cell turnover, oxidative stress and the release of cytokines and growth factors that drive cellular proliferation and suppress apoptosis.36 37

Strengths and limitations

First, the reliance on self-reported data for exposures and covariates represents a limitation of this study. Participants may not have accurately recalled past exposure histories, and direct measurements of indoor air pollutant concentrations in earlier life periods were unavailable. However, IAP is not widely recognised as a risk factor for oesophageal cancer in China, and exposure variables were derived from a standardised questionnaire using simple binary categories, making differential reporting by case–control status unlikely. In addition, all cancer registries used rapid case ascertainment, so the recall period is relatively short. Proxy respondents accounted for approximately one quarter of case interviews; nevertheless, most IAP variables reflect household-level characteristics, which proxy respondents generally report reliably. Given the limited public awareness of IAP as an oesophageal cancer risk factor, exposure misclassification is therefore expected to be non-differential and would likely bias effect estimates toward the null, yielding conservative associations. Second, our questionnaire did not capture additional pollution sources, such as paints and furnishings, which could have led to an underestimation of WRS. Nevertheless, these six sources represent the most significant contributors to household air quality issues. By focusing on these, we adopted a targeted approach to mitigating the most harmful effects on health, even though it does not capture the full spectrum of potential pollutants. Thirdly, our study was unable to distinguish between ESCC and oesophageal adenocarcinoma, which have similar and different risk factors. However, in China, ESCC remains the most common (>90%) type of oesophageal cancer,38 and thus our study provides insights into ESCC prevention and control. Lastly, non-participation among eligible cases raises the possibility of selection bias, particularly if individuals who declined differed systematically in their exposures. Nonetheless, the distributions of key sociodemographic and lifestyle indicators among the enrolled participants were consistent with known patterns in the base population, reducing concerns of representativeness.23

Strengths of the study include examination of specific IAP sources and integrated statistics (ie, WRS) to analyse both individual pollution sources and their combined impact. Using a WRS approach provides a more nuanced assessment of exposure, especially in studies that rely solely on questionnaire data without direct environmental measurements.24 WRS condenses information from separate risk factor analyses into a single summary measure of risk and provides a straightforward way to quantify combined risk. The use of WRS enhances the clarity of our main conclusion and comparability of our findings with other studies by accounting for the effect size of pollution sources rather than relying solely on simple counting. It can further improve consistency and accuracy in risk prediction models by accounting for the fact that some pollution sources pose greater risk than others. Conversely, simply counting exposures without weighting would have misrepresented such differences. An unweighted sum of exposures might not translate well between settings (due to varying prevalence or impact of specific sources), while a weighted score standardises the contribution of each exposure according to its risk. In addition to the use of WRS, we conducted this large population-based case–control study, which provided sufficient power for our analysis. The large sample size also allowed us to investigate associations among non-smokers, thereby minimising residual confounding by active smoking.

Future research and public health policy implications

Further research is warranted to refine and extend these findings in several ways. First, studies should examine the association between IAP and specific histology of oesophageal cancer in different regions with different dominant subtypes of ESCC and adenocarcinoma as they differ in aetiology and geographic distribution. Second, prospective cohort studies incorporating objective exposure measurements, such as direct measurement (eg, instrument-based measurements, biomarker-based assessments), would help validate questionnaire-based assessments. Third, mechanistic studies investigating inflammatory and genomic pathways, including the roles of PAHs and oxidative stress, could provide biological insights into different susceptibilities.

The observed associations underscore an urgent need for public health strategies aimed at reducing indoor air pollution exposure at the population level. Policies promoting access to cleaner household fuels, such as electricity or gas, and the adoption of improved cookstoves or heating technologies are critical in regions where solid fuels remain prevalent. Strengthening ventilation infrastructure in homes, especially in rural areas, could substantially reduce cumulative exposure to combustion by-products. Additionally, enforcing smoke-free environments and expanding community education on the hazards of passive smoking may lessen the burden among non-smokers. Given the widespread reliance on indoor fuel sources not only in China, but also globally, these interventions have the potential to generate meaningful reductions in oesophageal cancer risk and broader improvements in respiratory and cardiovascular health.

In summary, our study provided evidence for a strong association between IAP and an increased risk of oesophageal cancer. Among various sources of household air pollution, the burning of solid fuels was identified as the most substantial contributor. Although oesophageal cancer predominantly affects males and smokers, our findings underscore the urgent need to mitigate IAP in order to prevent oesophageal cancer in females and non-smokers. Future research should further explore the relationship between IAP and different histological subtypes. A consistent and comparable framework should also be adopted to interpret findings from heterogeneous sources of IAP.
