Development of a risk score for colorectal cancer screening
Authors: Elham Maserat, Amir Sadeghi, Pardis Ketabi Moghadam, Mohamad Amin Pourhoseingholi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim:The aim of this study was to develop a simple and reliable scoring system for colorectal cancer risk assessment considering the effectiveness of different screening options.Background:Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide. As a preventable disease, accurate risk assessment plays a critical role in early detection and prevention strategies.Methods:This study analyzed data from 3,465 individuals, including patients, first-degree, second-degree, and third-degree relatives of patients, and volunteers participating in a national CRC screening program. The dataset reflects nine years of prevention efforts targeting at-risk populations. Machine learning algorithms—Logistic Regression, Naïve Bayes, Neural Networks, Random Forest, and Support Vector Machines (SVM)—were applied to the data. Model performance was evaluated using cross-validation, ROC curves, F1 score, classification accuracy (CA), and overall accuracy metrics.Results:Of the 3,465 participants, 2,240 were diagnosed with colorectal cancer, 60 were relatives of affected patients, and 1,086 were non-patient relatives. Among CRC patients, 1,979 were over the age of 40. Logistic Regression and Naïve Bayes achieved the highest AUC values (0.910 and 0.907, respectively), with corresponding accuracies of 84.5% and 82.8%. Logistic Regression demonstrated the highest overall accuracy. The proposed scoring system incorporated variables such as age, sex, diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), smoking, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, BMI, family history, and five key symptoms: rectal bleeding, abdominal pain, unintended weight loss, changes in bowel habits, and anemia. The highest score was assigned to individuals over 60 years of age, while a score of 2 was allocated for a family history involving more than two affected relatives.Conclusion:A scoring system based on easily measurable variables offers a practical and efficient tool for widespread clinical use. Such systems can assist healthcare professionals in streamlining risk assessment and improving early identification of high-risk individuals.Key Words: Risk score, Colorectal cancer, Screening

Introduction

By 2040, there will be an estimated 3.2 million new cases of colon cancer annually, a 63% increase. In terms of deaths, the world will face 1.6 million deaths annually, which shows a 73% increase (1). Primary non-modifiable risk factors for colorectal adenoma are age, gender and family history. Modifiable risk factors include alcohol, obesity, physical activity, and diet. Ambiguous risk factors for colorectal adenoma include acromegaly, hereditary hemochromatosis, and patients who have had a ureterosigmoidostomy (2). According to the clinical guidelines of the American College of Gastroenterology, the development of cost-effective, highly accurate and non-invasive methods associated with improving the overall adherence to the screening process is also a desirable goal (3). Appropriate risk-based screening can translate into improved efficiency and optimal use of services. Reductions in overuse or disproportionate resources, combined with increased capacity for appropriate procedures with reduced wait times, ultimately lead to greater savings in health care costs. In addition, the expansion of CRC screening for people between 45 and 49 years of age and the reduction of the burden on the health care system (4).

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most important causes of cancer-related deaths with age-adjusted rates of 8.2 and 7.0 per 100,000 population for Iranian men and women, respectively. There is also a significant increase in the incidence of colorectal cancer among young Iranians (5). It is expected that the number of new cancer cases will increase from 112,000 in 2016 to 160,000 new cases in 2025. In other words, this shows an increase of 42.6%. Colorectal cancer is expected to increase by 54.1% during this time period (6). Considering the economic burden and high mortality rate of colorectal cancer, policies should be adopted to reduce morbidity, prevent and early detection of CRC (7). Colorectal cancer is preventable (8) and Risk assessment plays a key role in colorectal cancer prevention (7). One of the risk assessment tools is the clinical guidelines for colorectal cancer screening. Guidelines play a vital role in improving the quality of the CRC screening program. National guidelines are revised based on environmental and genetic criteria for colorectal cancer in Iran. These guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations for risk groups. National screening guidelines as a risk assessment tool can evaluate and improve cancer screening processes and outcomes in practice (9).

Several colorectal cancer risk prediction models have been developed, some of which are available as risk assessment tools or risk calculators. These tools combine multiple risk factors to display risk estimates and are used to facilitate decision-making for colorectal cancer screening, but their widespread use in the clinical setting has so far been limited(10). Various risk calculator tools have been designed and compiled. National Cancer Institute's (NCI) Colorectal Cancer Risk Assessment Tool, Cleveland Clinic's (CC) Colon Cancer Risk Assessment, Colorectal Cancer Predicted Risk Online calculator (CRC-PRO), QCancer, and MY CancerIQ are examples of colorectal cancer risk calculator tools (10).

The Asia-Pacific Colorectal Screening Score (APCS) is used optimize and improve the efficiency of resources in the field of prevention. This scoring system aims to classify risk based on risk factors such as gender, age, family history, smoking habits, obesity, diabetes and other possible risk factors. Modified APCS scoring system, Korean Colorectal Screening Score (KCS), Colorectal Neoplasia Prediction Score (CNP) and 8-point risk score are used in predicting advanced colorectal neoplasia (CAN) (11).

The aim of the study is to create a straightforward, reliable scoring system for early detection of colorectal cancer, considering the effectiveness of various screening options. This system aims to enhance early diagnosis and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

In this study, data from patients, their families, and volunteers participating in the colorectal cancer screening program were considered. A total of 3,465 individuals were analyzed. We conducted a cross-sectional study as part of the colorectal cancer screening initiative at the Gastroenterology and Liver Research Institute of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Iran. The data reflect nine years of colorectal cancer prevention efforts targeting individuals at risk. A cancer prevention center was established in Tehran, along with additional centers in various provinces, which provided training through workshops and courses for physicians and the general public. The Colorectal Cancer Prevention Center comprised several specialized units, including education, consultation, genealogy drawing, data registry, informatics, genetic testing, and colonoscopy. These units collaborated to identify individuals at risk based on specific protocols. After consultation and obtaining ethical consent from participants, genetic screening procedures were carried out in accordance with established protocols. To optimize limited resources, colonoscopy was recommended only for individuals identified as high-risk, and examinations were conducted at Taleghani Hospital according to a predetermined schedule. The data warehouse used for this study was a dynamic and continuously evolving classified repository, serving as the foundation for exploratory analysis. For the machine learning workflow, the selection of quantitative and qualitative variables was reviewed and approved by domain experts to ensure methodological rigor.

Quantitative variables in the study included age, age at diagnosis, BMI, and tumor size (grades 1 and 2). Qualitative variables included: sex, education level, ethnicity, place of residence, marital status, history of exposure to chemicals and pollutants, history of hypertension, history of diabetes, tobacco use, alcohol consumption, use of sedative narcotics, use of injectable narcotics, use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, family history of cancer, abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, anemia, unexplained weight loss, weakness, changes in stool patterns, symptoms of intestinal perforation, symptoms of intestinal obstruction, fever of unknown origin, life status, quality of life, personal history of colorectal cancer, history of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC) status, familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) status, initial treatment modality (surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, biopsy), progression grading, pathological grading, PM grading, PN grading, PT grading, tumor differentiation grade, tumor morphology, and tumor topography.

Selection of variables—both quantitative and qualitative—was approved by domain experts to ensure relevance and consistency. In this study, missing data were either omitted or handled through standard methods compatible with machine learning (e.g., mean imputation, deletion, or flagging). In this study, preprocessing was conducted through a rigorous and structured workflow. Experts first validated and selected clinically relevant quantitative and qualitative variables. Byte-format and categorical data were transformed using encoding and standardization methods to ensure compatibility with machine learning algorithms. New features—such as risk scores and composite indicators—were engineered to enhance model predictive power. Missing data were addressed through imputation, exclusion, or binary flagging, depending on the variable’s nature and importance. Continuous variables underwent normalization, and statistical techniques were applied to detect and treat outliers, followed by clinical validation to maintain data integrity.

Following initial data analysis, several transformations were applied, including the conversion of byte-format variables to integers and the creation of new attributes to enhance feature representation. Subsequently, machine learning algorithms were implemented, including Logistic Regression, Naïve Bayes, Neural Networks, Random Forest, and Support Vector Machines (SVM). Model evaluation was conducted using cross-validation, with 80% of the data allocated for training and 20% for testing in each iteration. Comparative performance across algorithms was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Key metrics reported included the area under the ROC curve (AUC), area under the precision–recall curve (PR-AUC), F1 score, and classification accuracy (CA) (Figure 1).

Steps of machine learning method

Modeling was implemented using Orange and R Studio software. At the end, the scores of different effective variables in the model were calculated and reported through the scoring system.

Results

In this study, data from patients, their families, and volunteers were analyzed. A total of 3,465 individuals participated in the screening program, including 1,625 women and 1,840 men. Among them, 2,240 were patients diagnosed with colorectal cancer, 60 were relatives of colorectal cancer patients, and 1,086 were non-patients. The number of colorectal cancer patients over the age of 40 was 1,979. The geographic distribution of age, sex, and disease status among affected and non-affected individuals, as well as their relatives, was illustrated using maps and diagrams (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6). Individuals covered by the screening program included patients with colorectal cancer and their first, second, and third degree relatives with colorectal cancer and non-patient relatives. The distribution of the covered population was presented by province (Figures 2). Colorectal patients who are overweight are shown in Figure 3 by province. The age group 65 to 70 had the highest number of male colorectal cancer patients, and the age group 55 to 60 had the highest number of female colorectal cancer patients (Figures 4). The age group of 30 to 35 had the highest number of female relatives with colorectal cancer, and the age group of 60 to 65 had the highest number of male relatives with colorectal cancer (Figure 5).

A risk scoring system was developed based on demographic and clinical variables, including age, sex, diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), smoking, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, body mass index (BMI), family history, and five key symptoms: rectal bleeding, abdominal pain, unintended weight loss, altered bowel habits, and anemia. The highest risk score was assigned to individuals aged over 60 years. A score of 2 was allocated for a family history involving two or more affected relatives.

Each variable was assigned a score based on its association strength, with confidence intervals (CI) provided for statistical interpretation. This table presents risk scores ranging from 1 to 4, assigned according to each variable’s association strength with colorectal cancer. Age ≥60 carries the highest score of 4, supported by a notably high odds ratio of 4.11 (95% CI: 6.70–10.14), indicating strong predictive value. Other variables such as sex (male), diabetes, smoking, and anemia received scores of 1, reflecting moderate associations, while stomach pain showed the lowest odds ratio of 0.08 but was still scored 1. Intermediate-risk variables—including inflammatory bowel disease, family history (≥2 members), and rectal bleeding—were assigned scores of 2 or higher, highlighting their clinical relevance in the stratification model.

Discussion

In this study, the population covered by the colorectal cancer screening program was analyzed. The covered people included healthy people and people with colorectal cancer. This study was conducted with the aim of scoring the risk of colorectal cancer. In this study, data cleaning was done in the first stages, and the data was processed with five machine learning algorithms, and finally, the scores of the 10 main influential variables for risk assessment were determined.

The cross-sectional study evaluated China's national colorectal cancer screening program (CanSPUC), highlighting its structured implementation by trained staff. It demonstrated that the APCS and KCS scoring systems effectively predict advanced neoplasia risk and support resource allocation. The study recommended integrating additional risk factors to enhance the model’s predictive accuracy (11).

The BMI variable is not included in the APCS scoring system (12). In the present study, variable BMI was included. In the CNP scoring system, the diabetes variable was added to the risk factors. In the present study, the diabetes variable was included. In the KCS scoring system, 70 higher points were considered for the older age (13). In the present study, 1 points was considered for age 40-45, 2 points for age 45-50, 3 points for age 50-60, and 4 point for age over 60. In the modified APCS, a lower BMI range has been included for evaluation (from 25 to 23) (14). In the present study, more than 30 points have been assigned to BMI. The 8-point risk score for family history and smoking was associated with more precise scoring criteria (15). Both in the 8-point scoring system and the scoring system of the present study, a score of 2 was included for ≥2 first degree relatives with colorectal cancer. Based on the Turkish scoring system, colorectal cancer screening may start at the age of 45 in Turkish patients. The Colorectal Cancer and Polyp Study Group (TCS) score is more than 4. In the scoring system in the score range of 4 or higher, doctors should more carefully screen for polyps and colon cancer for any age group of patients (16). Both the Turkish and the current study scoring systems assigned equal weights to BMI >30, male gender, age groups, smoking, and family history of colorectal cancer as risk factors. Age 40–45 was scored as 1 and 45–50 as 2 in both models. The UK scoring system additionally considered a wide range of symptomatic presentations including bowel habit changes and systemic symptoms (17). The study assessed clinical symptoms such as rectal bleeding, abdominal pain, weight loss, bowel changes, and anemia for risk scoring. It analyzed data from 4,500 U.S. individuals aged 50–89 undergoing initial screening colonoscopy between 2004–2011. A risk scoring algorithm was derived using five key factors: age, sex, family history, waist circumference, and smoking status (18). The study included all risk factors except waist circumference, aligning partially with existing tools. Several international colorectal cancer risk models—such as NCI, QCancer, MY CancerIQ, and CRC-PRO—provide time-specific assessments for diverse populations. Many use clinical algorithms to categorize individuals into high, moderate, and low-risk groups based on key variables (10).

Conclusion

This study introduced a colorectal cancer risk scoring system using nine years of data and machine learning techniques. It incorporated accessible variables like age, diabetes, BMI, smoking, and alcohol use to enhance practicality in routine care. The system helps clinicians efficiently identify high-risk individuals and improve colonoscopy selection. It also supports preventive strategies such as lifestyle modifications. Risk stratification may further guide personalized post-polypectomy surveillance intervals.

The risk score model lacks external validation across diverse populations and relies partly on self-reported data, risking bias. It excludes molecular features like genetics and microbiome, limiting predictive power. Real-world clinical impact has not yet been assessed. Ethical, legal, and implementation challenges remain, particularly regarding algorithmic decision-making in healthcare settings.

The study on developing a colorectal cancer risk score indicates its potential to optimize clinical decision-making in primary care. By leveraging basic demographic and clinical data, high-risk individuals can be prioritized, enabling more efficient allocation of limited healthcare resources. Integrating this score into electronic health systems may support intelligent alerts and improve screening effectiveness. General practitioners would also be empowered to implement timely and targeted screening interventions.

Future research should focus on external validation across diverse populations to assess generalizability. Incorporating genetic and microbiome data could enhance predictive accuracy and support personalized screening. Cost-effectiveness analyses and long-term clinical impact evaluations will help determine the utility of the tool in shaping health policy. Ethical and legal considerations surrounding algorithm-based decision-making must also be carefully examined.
