Bilateral choroidal metastases as the presenting sign of disseminated endometrial adenocarcinoma: a case report
Authors: Tara Srinivas, Elizabeth Ciociola Kelly, Rohan Shah, Mustafa Iftikhar, Colleen McCormick, Emily Claire MacArthur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChoroidal metastases are the most common intraocular malignancy, typically resulting from breast and lung primary cancers. Ocular metastases of gynecologic origin are extremely rare. Reported cases involving endometrial adenocarcinoma are especially limited, with few documented instances globally of ocular involvement at initial presentation.Case presentationWe report the case of a 60-year-old woman with no known pertinent past gynecologic history who presented with two weeks of dyspnea, postmenopausal bleeding, and five days of progressively blurry vision with floaters in the left eye. Imaging revealed a markedly enlarged uterus, diffuse lymphadenopathy with lesions involving the lungs, liver, and bones, and bilateral choroidal lesions with overlying exudative retinal detachments, concerning for ocular carcinomatosis. Ophthalmologic examination confirmed bilateral choroidal metastases. Cytopathologic analyses of samples obtained from Pap smear and thoracentesis were consistent with high-grade adenocarcinoma of endometrial origin. Shortly following admission, the patient developed progressive multisystem organ failure requiring intubation and was not a candidate for chemotherapy. She passed away 17 days after presentation.ConclusionTo our knowledge, this case represents the first report of bilateral choroidal metastases as the presenting manifestation of previously undiagnosed stage IV endometrial adenocarcinoma, underscoring the complex phenotypes seen with high-grade histologic manifestations of this increasingly prevalent disease. As the rate of high-grade endometrial cancer continues to rise, clinicians should remain aware that atypical presentations may arise. Early recognition and comprehensive systemic evaluation are essential, as unusual presentations of endometrial malignancy, as was the case with this patient, may signal advanced disease with limited treatment options.Keywords: Choroidal metastases, Endometrial adenocarcinoma, Ocular metastases, Uveal metastases

Introduction

Endometrial cancer is the most common gynecologic malignancy in the United States, with an estimated 66,200 new cases and 13,030 deaths in 2023. (Siegel et al., 2023) It is the fourth most common cancer in women, and incidence has risen by 132% over the past 30 years. (Crosbie et al., 2022) The median age at diagnosis is 61 years with a lifetime risk of approximately 3%. (Crosbie et al., 2022) While a majority of patients present with FIGO stage I disease confined to the uterine corpus, (Lu and Broaddus, 2020) the incidence of aggressive, nonendometrioid histologies is rising, with rates increasing most sharply among Black women. (Lu and Broaddus, 2020) Unfortunately, the etiologies of this disparity are not yet fully understood.

Endometrial cancer typically spreads via direct extension through the myometrium and cervix, with metastatic dissemination most commonly via the pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes. (Tangjitgamol et al., 2009) Distant hematogenous metastases to the lungs, liver, bones, and brain occur less frequently. (Mao et al., 2020) The prognosis for patients with stage IV disease is poor, with median overall survival of approximately 12–29 months and 5-year survival rates of 15–31%. (Haddad et al., 2025; Cook et al., 1999)

Ocular metastases are an exceptionally rare manifestation of disseminated malignancy. They are generally hematogenous in origin, typically occurring in the choroid due to its high vascularity, which accounts for approximately 90% of intraocular metastases. (Shields et al., 2023) Choroidal metastases typically present as ill-defined, elevated, yellow-white, lesions, often associated with subretinal fluid. Patients report blurred vision, floaters, and visual field defects. Choroidal metastases are most commonly due to breast (37%) and lung (27%) primaries, (Shields et al., 2023) while other reported primary sites include kidney (4%), gastrointestinal tract (4%), cutaneous melanoma (2%), prostate (2%), thyroid (1%), and pancreas (1%). (Shields et al., 2023) The prognosis for patients with choroidal metastases regardless of underlying malignancy is generally poor, with 32% survival at 3 years and 24% at 5 years. (Shields et al., 2023)

Only a handful of cases of choroidal metastases from endometrial adenocarcinoma have been described in the literature. Al Nabhani et al. reported a 58-year-old woman with a 20-year history of irregular menstrual bleeding in the setting of uterine fibroids who presented with visual disturbance and was found to have a large choroidal peri-papillary tumor. Enucleation revealed metastatic adenocarcinoma, and further workup identified an endometrial mesonephric-like adenocarcinoma as the primary tumor. (Al Nabhani et al., 2022) Other reported cases of choroidal metastases from endometrial cancer have occurred in patients with known and previously treated disease who developed ocular involvement during the course of recurrence, rather than as the presenting sign of undiagnosed cancer. (Tsang et al., 2025; Smith et al., 2018; Cormio et al., 2006; Saxena et al., 2012)

To our knowledge, bilateral choroidal metastases as the presenting manifestation of previously undiagnosed endometrial adenocarcinoma has not been previously described. We present a case of a 60-year-old woman with no past gynecologic or ophthalmologic history who presented with visual symptoms and was found to have bilateral choroidal metastases leading to the diagnosis of widely metastatic endometrial adenocarcinoma.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old G6P2123 female of Black race with no known past medical history presented to the emergency department with two weeks of dyspnea, generalized weakness, nonproductive cough, and headache. She also reported new intermittent postmenopausal vaginal bleeding for the past two weeks. Five days prior to presentation, she developed progressively worsening blurry vision, loss of temporal visual field, and floaters in the left eye with an associated feeling of pressure behind the eye.

Chest X-ray demonstrated diffuse patchy airspace opacities. Computed tomography angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed a markedly enlarged uterus with heterogeneous enhancement, extensive abdominopelvic lymphadenopathy, consolidative nodules throughout the lung parenchyma and pleural surfaces, bulky mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy, multiple hypodense liver lesions, and irregular femoral and rib lytic lesions highly suspicious for metastatic carcinomatosis. Computed tomography of the head demonstrated hyperdensity in the posterior left globe suspicious for retinal detachment versus a choroidal lesion. Magnetic resonance imaging of the globe revealed plaque-like enhancing lesions of the posterior left greater than right choroid.

Metastatic cancer was suspected based on imaging findings and diffuse lymphadenopathy. Ophthalmologic consultation revealed bilateral choroidal lesions with overlying exudative retinal detachments further concerning for metastatic disease. Visual acuity was 20/20 in the right eye and 20/300 in the left eye with normal intraocular pressures. Slit lamp examination demonstrated normal anterior segments with no anterior chamber or vitreous cell. Dilated fundus examination of the right eye showed an inferior elevated lesion along the inferior arcade with normal disc and periphery (Fig. 1A). The left eye showed two elevated lesions, one smaller lesion superior to the disc and a larger lesion in the macula extending from the superior arcade to slightly above the inferior arcade with an overlying serous retinal detachment and extension of subretinal fluid beyond the inferior arcade, and a chorioretinal scar superotemporally (Fig. 1B). B-scan ultrasonography of the right eye showed a small dome-shaped lesion with medium to high reflectivity inferotemporally (thickness 1.6 mm with diameter 4.9 mm laterally by 7.8 mm radially). B scan ultrasonography of the left eye revealed moderately dense dispersed vitreous opacities with membrane formation, an area of focal fundus thickening with medium irregular internal reflectivity located superotemporally and temporally (largest thickness 3.1 mm with diameter 10.1 mm laterally by 9.0 mm radially), and an overlying exudative retinal detachment. Spectral domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) (Heidelberg Spectralis) over the tumors revealed dome-shaped retinal elevation due to underlying choroidal masses. Detachment of the neurosensory retina was noted in some areas overlying the lesion with splitting of the retinal layers at the level of the outer plexiform layer (Fig. 2). Additionally, OCT imaging showed punctate hyperreflective opacities in the outer retina with granularity of the photoreceptor layer, possibly due to photoreceptor damage and phagocytosis of photoreceptor cells. (Saxena et al., 2012) Fluorescein angiography demonstrated late phase staining of the elevated lesions bilaterally (Fig. 1C & D and Fig. 3).

Given the patient's new onset vaginal bleeding and enlarged uterus on CT, gynecologic consultation was obtained. Further history revealed that the patient's last menstrual period was eight years prior; she had not experienced vaginal bleeding since that time. Two weeks before presentation she began having vaginal bleeding with clots every 3–4 days. Per the patient, her last Pap smear was estimated at eight to nine years prior, and she had no history of abnormal findings. Pelvic examination revealed a visible 3 cm friable mass protruding into the vagina. On bimanual examination, a palpable cervical mass and a nontender bulky uterus were found. Transvaginal ultrasound showed an endometrial stripe of 1.4 cm. Endometrial biopsy was attempted but could not be completed due to patient discomfort. Pap smear cytology resulted in tumor cells positive for PAX8, claudin 4, and p16, focally positive for TTF-1, and negative for calretinin, thyroglobulin, napsin-A, and P40, with a final diagnosis of high grade adenocarcinoma of likely endometrial origin. Thoracentesis of right-sided pleural effusion was performed with cytopathologic analysis consistent with adenocarcinoma of endometrial origin. Hepatic biopsy including FNA and cores also supported this diagnosis. Multiplex immunostain (lung triple stain) was performed on all samples and the case was presented at the institutional cytopathology quality assurance conference and all findings supported an endometrial primary malignancy.

While histologic confirmation was not possible via direct endometrial biopsy, our diagnosis of endometrial primary in this patient was based on several factors. In the context of postmenopausal bleeding with cervical mass, cytopathologic and immunohistochemical analysis of both pap smear tissue and pleural fluid (PAX8+, claudin 4+, p16+, TTF-1+) was most consistent with a Müllerian primary. We acknowledge the possibility of alternative primary or dual primaries, including thyroid and lung carcinomas, however expert cytopathologic review did not support these diagnoses. Additionally, the patient's clinical context and cytopathologic findings – including thyroglobulin negativity, PAX8 positivity, and napsin A negativity – make these alternatives less likely.

The decision to pursue empiric treatment with carboplatin and paclitaxel was made. However, in the time prior to initiation of chemotherapy, the patient developed progressive hypoxic respiratory failure requiring intubation. On day 12 of hospitalization, she was transferred to the intensive care unit, where she developed worsening shock and recurrent fevers unresponsive to empiric antibiotics and critical care management. Due to continued decline in respiratory function likely secondary to superinfection, critical illness, and multisystem organ failure, the patient was determined to no longer be a candidate for chemotherapy. After discussions with family, comfort care was pursued. On day 17 of hospitalization, the patient was palliatively extubated and passed away shortly thereafter.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of bilateral choroidal metastasis in a stage IV endometrial adenocarcinoma discovered in a previously asymptomatic patient with no known gynecologic history whose presenting symptoms included unilateral blurry vision, dyspnea, and postmenopausal bleeding for two weeks. While choroidal metastases are the most common intraocular malignancy, gynecologic primaries are extremely rare. (Salcedo-Villanueva et al., 2021)

Endometrial cancer is increasing in both incidence and mortality globally and is now the most lethal gynecologic malignancy. (Corr et al., 2025) The disproportionate increase in the rate of aggressive, high grade histologic subtypes, particularly among Black women, is especially concerning. (Lu and Broaddus, 2020) In this context, it is important to recognize that clinicians may encounter extra-pelvic and atypical symptoms of advanced disease, including but not limited to ocular manifestations of metastatic involvement, as in our patient.

The prognosis for patients with choroidal metastases is generally poor. Kaplan-Meier analysis in a series of 1111 patients with uveal metastasis revealed 32% survival at 3 years and 24% at 5 years. (Salcedo-Villanueva et al., 2021) Our patient's rapid clinical deterioration and death within 17 days of hospitalization highlights the aggressive nature of metastatic endometrial cancer with ocular involvement. Metastatic disease should not be overlooked in a patient presenting with new onset vision changes and choroidal lesions, particularly in the setting of concurrent symptoms suggestive of malignancy. The most common symptoms of ocular metastases include blurry vision, decreased visual acuity, floaters, and pain; however, some patients may be asymptomatic. (Shields et al., 2023) As such, routine annual gynecologic exams and regular ophthalmologic evaluation for the detection of common comorbidities should be continued to be emphasized for all patients. While routine eye exams are not designed to screen for cancer metastases generally associated with advanced disseminated disease, providers should be aware of metastatic disease in cases of new visual changes with known or suspected malignancy, as early recognition can optimize visual function and quality of life.

As the incidence and lethality of endometrial cancer continues to rise, timely gynecologic assessment, especially in cases of postmenopausal bleeding, is essential for early detection and improved outcomes. (Corr et al., 2025) Similarly, regular eye exams in older patients can facilitate earlier detection of ocular pathology, including metastatic disease. Current recommendations from the American Academy of Ophthalmology include a baseline comprehensive eye examination at age 40 for all adults, which precedes the median age at diagnosis of endometrial cancer. (Smith et al., 2018) Beyond encouraging routine gynecologic and ophthalmologic care, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for advanced disease in postmenopausal patients with new onset visual symptoms and vaginal bleeding. Advanced presentations of malignancy may include ocular metastases, and ophthalmologic consultation in such cases can aid staging. Treatment of endometrial cancer depends on extent of disease, life expectancy, primary tumor characteristics, and symptom constellation, with external beam radiation therapy and systemic chemotherapy providing options.

Conclusion

This case underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential, which may include gynecologic malignancies, in the evaluation of choroidal metastases. This consideration may be particularly pertinent to ophthalmologists and primary care providers, who are sometimes the first to encounter such patients presenting with ocular lesions of unknown origin and who warrant comprehensive systemic workup. As the rate of high-grade endometrial cancer continues to rise, clinicians should remain aware that atypical presentations may arise. Early recognition, a thorough review of systems, comprehensive workup, and interdisciplinary are essential, as unusual presentations of endometrial malignancy, as was the case with this patient, may signal advanced disease with limited treatment options.
