Prevalence and associated factors of acute diarrhea among under-five children in community-led total sanitation and hygiene implemented and non-implemented kebeles in Chiro Woreda, Eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Wondimagegn Wolde Eba, Getachew Gashaw, Kaleab Terefe, Abel Desalegn Demeke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiarrheal diseases, particularly acute diarrhea, remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among low-income children aged five years. Diarrheal diseases are characterized by the passage of three or more loose or watery stools within a 24-hour period and typically last for 14 days.ObjectivesThis study aimed to determine the prevalence of acute diarrhea and identify associated factors among under-five-year-old children in Community-Led Total Sanitation and Hygiene implemented and non-implemented kebeles in Chiro Woreda, Eastern Ethiopia.MethodologyA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March to May 2023 among 606 households selected using a multistage systematic sampling technique. Acute diarrhea was defined as the occurrence of three or more loose or watery stools within 24 h during the two weeks preceding the survey. Data were collected using a structured interviewer-administered questionnaire and observational checklist and analyzed using binary logistic regression.ResultsThe two-week prevalence of diarrhea was 26.6% (95% CI: 23.1–30.1), with a lower prevalence in Community-Led-Total Sanitation and Hygiene implemented kebeles (19.8%) compared to non-implemented kebeles (30.0%). In multivariate analysis, factors significantly associated with acute diarrhea include an unimproved water source (AOR = 5.84; 95% CI: 2.66–12.70), taking more than 30 min to collect water (AOR = 3.18; 95% CI: 1.46–6.85), improper use of latrines (AOR = 7.45; 95% CI: 4.20–13.21), poor hand washing practices (AOR = 2.87; 95% CI: 1.62–5.10), lack of rotavirus vaccination (AOR = 96.30; 95% CI: 30.21–305.42), male sex, and household sizes greater than 5.Conclusion and recommendationAcute diarrhea remains a public health problem among under-five children in Chiro Woreda. The prevalence was lower in community-led total sanitation and hygiene implemented kebeles. Water source, water collection time, latrine utilization, hand washing practices, rotavirus vaccination, child sex, and household size were significantly associated with diarrhea. Strengthening sanitation and hygiene practices, improving access to safe water, and increasing vaccination coverage could help reduce the burden of childhood diarrhea.Keywords: Acute diarrhea, Community led-total sanitation and hygiene, Households, Sanitation, Under-five children

Introduction

Diarrhea is defined as the passage of loose or watery stools three or more times within a 24-hour period in a child [15]. It is mainly caused by the ingestion of pathogens through unsafe drinking water, contaminated food, or unclean hands [35]. diarrhea, regardless of its underlying cause or classification, usually results in the loss of water and essential salts, particularly sodium and potassium, potentially leading to metabolic disorders and, in severe cases, fatalities [5].

Globally, under-five-year-old children experience an average of 3.2 episodes of diarrhea each year [17]. diarrhea remains the second leading cause of death among under-five-year-old children, accounting for approximately 1.7 million cases of morbidity and 760,000 deaths annually [7]. While global mortality from diarrhea has declined considerably over recent decades, morbidity in Africa has not followed the same trend. Childhood diarrhea in sub-Saharan Africa results in morbidity of 25%–75% and mortality of approximately 50% [14]. In Ethiopia, diarrheal disease remains a major public health concern, with an incidence rate of 8.5% to 54% [13],a situation influenced by socioeconomic, environmental, and behavioral factors [3], [39]. Approximately 60%–80% of cases in Ethiopia can be attributed to unsafe drinking water, poor sanitation, and unhygienic waste management, which are directly linked to open defecation practices [36]. These conditions can be effectively addressed through improved total community-led sanitation and hygiene practices [36].

The United Nations aims to ensure that everyone has access to satisfactory and equitable sanitation and hygiene by 2030 to achieve Sustainable Development Goal six, Target two, with a specific focus on women, girls, and vulnerable groups [36]. Community-Led Total Sanitation and Hygiene is a promising alternative to traditional top-down sanitation methods, focusing on community empowerment and collective behavior change [16], [9], [23].

Ethiopia aims to achieve universal access to safe water services by 2030 and 82% improved sanitation coverage by 2020. However, by 2015, coverage had reached only 57.2% for water and 28% for sanitation [21]. This disparity—42.8% for water and 54% for sanitation—highlights the need for further investigation into sanitation and hygiene interventions [33].

Previous studies conducted in Ethiopia have reported notable differences in diarrhea prevalence between CLTSH-implemented and CLTSH non-implemented areas. Studies in Kersa District, Jimma Zone, found 18% and 22% prevalence rates in areas where CLTSH was implemented, and in CLTSH non-implemented areas, respectively, while studies in Yaya Gulele reported 13% and 36% rates, as cited in Eshete, Beyene et al., (2015) and Degebasa et al., [9]. These discrepancies indicate persistent challenges and underscore the need for further localized research.

Diarrhea remains a significant public health issue in Ethiopia, even though cost-effective prevention and treatment options like CLTSH are available through the country’s health extension system, with unacceptably high mortality rates among children under-five years old. This necessitates locality-based studies to assess the magnitude, patterns, and trends of diarrhea and its associated risk factors and to evaluate the effectiveness of the CLTSH strategy [14, 18] Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence and associated factors of acute diarrhea among under-five-year-old children in CLTSH-implemented and CLTSH non-implemented kebeles of Chiro Woreda.

Methodology

Study area and period

The study was conducted in ChiroWoreda, West Hararghe Zone, Oromiya Region, and eastern Ethiopia. The district has an estimated population of 251,864, including approximately 41,300 children aged five years. Within Woreda’s 40 rural kebeles, 15 (37.5%) adopted the CLTSH approach, while 25 (62.5%) not adopted it, as stated in unpublished records from the Chiro Woreda Health Office (Office., 2018) [24]. The 2018 fiscal year was used as the reference period due to the availability of complete CLTSH implementation and population data. The study was conducted from March 01 to May 30, 2023.

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted.

Source population

The source population was all mothers and caregivers who had at least one under-five children in Chiro Woreda.

Study population

The study population was all mothers and caregivers who had at least one under-five children living in the selected CLTSH implemented and CLTSH non-implemented kebeles in Chiro Woreda.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

All households in the CLTSH implemented and CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles that had at least one under-five children and who had resided there for more than 6 months.

Exclusion criteria

Children with bloody diarrhea and persistent diarrhea were excluded.

Children with bloody diarrhea and persistent diarrhea were excluded.

Mothers or caregivers who are mentally ill, seriously ill, had hearing and speaking problem were excluded from the study.

Mothers or caregivers who are mentally ill, seriously ill, had hearing and speaking problem were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Sample size determination

For the first objective, the sample size was determined using the double population proportion formula. A 95% confidence interval, 5% margin of error, a design effect of 1.5, and a 10% non-response rate were assumed. The two-week prevalence of diarrhea among under-five children in CLTSH-implemented kebeles (P₁) was 18.9%, based on a study conducted in the Gamo Gofa District [6], whereas the prevalence in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles (P₂) was 36.3%, as reported from the Yaya Gulale District [9].

After calculating for both objectives one and two, the total sample size, which was the largest of all, was 606 under-five-year children (202 in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 404 CLTSH non-implemented kebeles).

Sampling technique

A multistage sampling technique was employed to select the study participants. In the first stage, the district was stratified into CLTSH-implemented and CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. Six kebeles (three from each category) were randomly selected from the total kebeles in each stratum using a simple random sampling technique, specifically the lottery method. For each chosen Kebele, a sampling frame of households containing at least one child under-five years of age was obtained from the Community Health Information System (CHIS) accessible at the health posts of individual kebeles.

The sample size was proportionally allocated between the selected kebeles. In the second stage, a systematic random sampling technique was applied to select specific households. The sampling interval (K) was calculated as 52 for the CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 37 for the CLTSH non-implemented kebeles.

Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire adapted from previously published and validated studies conducted in Ethiopia and comparable settings and pretested on 5% of the total sample size in kebeles of Oda Bultum Woreda. Based on the pretest, minor modificationsvwere made to improve the wording and sequencing of some questions, clarify ambiguous terms, and ensure that response options were understandable to respondents. Although formal psychometric validation was not performed, the tool demonstrated acceptable clarity and consistency during piloting. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and then translated into Amharic and Afan Oromo to ensure clarity and ease of understanding for respondents.

The questionnaire consisted of two sections. The first section captured socio-demographic variables, including the relationship of the respondent to the child, sex of the respondent, marital status, educational status of the mother or caregiver, occupation of the respondent, sex of the child, total number of under-five children, age of the child, family size, birth order, and family income. The second section focused on potential risk factors associated with the prevalence of diarrhea among under-five-year-old children and was developed based on relevant literature ([6]; [9, 20]). In addition to the questionnaire, an observational checklist was used to assess environmental factors, providing complementary data for the study.

Data collectors

Data were collected by six trained female nurses who held diplomas and had ample experience in data collection. The activities of the data collectors were supervised by two Bachelor’s degree-qualified environmental health professionals. Before the commencement of data collection, the principal investigator provided a three-day training session for both data collectors and supervisors. The training included two days of theoretical instruction and one day of practical training, focusing on data a collection tools and procedures.

In households with more than one child under-five years of age, one child was randomly selected using the lottery method. For households where the study participants were not present during the initial visit, a second visit was made. If participants were still unavailable during the second visit, the next household in the sampling frame was included in the study to ensure data completeness.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The outcome variable was acute diarrhea (yes/no) among under-five children, assessed separately in CLTSH-implemented and CLTSH non-implemented kebeles.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic factors

relationship of the respondent to the child, sex of respondent, marital status, educational status of mother or caregivers, occupation of the respondent, sex of child, total number of under-five children, age of the child, family size, birth order and family income.

Environmental factors

source of drinking water, time taken in a minute to fetch water, daily water consumption, water interruption, availability of latrine, types of the latrine, latrine seat hole cover, child feces disposal, frequency of cleaning latrine, hand washing facility near the latrine, refuse disposal, waste water disposal and livestock in the house.

Behavioral factors

current breast feeding, started supplementary food, Rota vaccine, water collection container, cleanness of water storage, method of drawing water from storage, method of drinking water treatment, hand washing material, faces seen around the pit, feces saw around the compound, mothers or caregivers history of diarrhea.

Operational definition

Acute diarrhea is defined as a diarrhea disease that causes three or more loose or watery stools per 24 h during the past two weeks before data collection as reported by the mother or caregiver, in accordance to WHO guidelines (WHO, 2005).

CLTSH is an approach to draw attention to changing sanitation and hygiene behavior rather than making physical changes in the community [1].

CLTSH-implemented kebeles

Kebeles where Community-Led Total Sanitation and Hygiene programs had been officially implemented and certified by local health authorities at least six months prior to data collection.

CLTSH non-implemented kebeles

Kebeles where CLTSH programs had not been implemented or officially certified during the study period.

Caregiver

Any person who provides care for the child other than the mother (Agegnehu, Bewket Zeleke, Goshu, Ortibo, & Mehretie Adinew, 2019) [2].

Drinking water treatment: refers to methods used for treating water for drinking in the home using any of the following affordable water treatment techniques: boiling, filtration, and chlorination [37].

Unimproved water sources

if the mother or caregiver who fetched water from an Unprotected dug well, unprotected spring, surface water (e.g., river, dam, lake, pond, and stream) [32].

Hand washing at critical times

if a mother/caregiver reported practiced hand washing practices with soap mainly after visiting latrines, cleaning bottoms of children, before preparing food, before eating, and before feeding children (Fantay Gebru, Mekonnen Haileselassie, Haftom Temesgen, Oumer Seid, & Afework Mulugeta, 2019) [12].

Proper refuse disposal

A way of disposing of refuses that which included burning, burying in a pit or storing in a container, and disposing of in the designed site [12].

Safe child feces disposal

if the mother or caregivers dispose of child feces in the latrine [10].

Data quality control

The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample size to assess its relevance, clarity, and appropriateness in addressing the study objectives. The pretest was conducted in communities within Oda Bultum Woreda, allowing for necessary modifications before the actual study started. Data collectors received comprehensive training, and investigators supervised the data collection process.

Study participants were oriented about the purpose and significance of the survey to ensure their understanding and cooperation. To maintain data quality, a random selection of completed questionnaires was rechecked for accuracy. Additionally, double data entry was performed using Epi-Data software to minimize errors during the data entry process. A multicollinearity test was conducted to evaluate the correlations among the independent variables. All variance inflation factors (VIF) were less than 10, showing there was no substantial correlation between the variables. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = 0.72) indicated that the model had an adequate fit to the data.

Data processing and analysis

Data were entered into Epi-Data (version 3.1) to check completeness and accuracy before being transferred to SPSS (version 20) for analysis. The characteristics of the study population were summarized using descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages.

Internal comparisons were performed using binary logistic regression to determine the independent effects of variables. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values were used to assess the associations between dependent and independent variables. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate logistic regression analysis were included in the final multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounders.

A multicollinearity test was conducted to evaluate correlations among independent variables. (In the final multivariate logistic regression analysis, variables with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 606 respondents participated in the study, including 202 households with CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 404 households from CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. In CLTSH-implemented kebeles, mothers comprised 185 (91.5%) of caretakers, whereas in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles, they made up 382 (94.5%) of caretakers. Regarding household income, 156 (77.2%) households in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 234 (57.9%) households in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles reported a monthly income above 5000 ETB. Most households had two or more under-five children, reported by 130 (64.4%) in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 321 (79.5%) in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. The majority of caretakers were married, accounting for 102 (50.5%) in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 280 (69.3%) in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. Farming was the predominant occupation among caretakers, reported by 125 (61.9%) in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 218 (54.0%) in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. Most households in CLTSH-implemented kebeles had a family size of fewer than five members (117 [57.9%]), compared to 102 (25.2%) in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. In both kebele types, most caretakers were aged 25–34 years, and child sex distribution was comparable across group (Table 1).

Prevalence of diarrhea

The study’s findings revealed that the two-week prevalence of diarrheal disease among children under-five was 26.6% (95% CI: 23.1%, 30.1%), which was lower at 19.8% (95% CI: 14.3%, 25.3%) in areas where the CLTSH program was implemented, as opposed to 30% (95% CI: 25.5, 34.5) in areas without the CLTSH program. The status of diarrhea has a significant difference between the two CLTSH-implemented and CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles with P value of 0.001.

Behavioral characteristics

Proper latrine utilization was reported by 141 (69.8%) households in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 289 (71.5%) households in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. Handwashing with soap or ash was widely practiced, reported by 181 (89.6%) participants in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 300 (74.3%) participants in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. Regarding critical handwashing times, washing hands at least three times per day was reported by 190 (94.1%) participants in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 341 (84.4%) participants in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles, whereas 14 (6.9%) and 63 (15.6%) participants, respectively, reported washing hands fewer than three times daily.

Household water treatment practices were reported by 152 (75.2%) participants in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 386 (95.5%) participants in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. Initiation of complementary feeding at the recommended age of six months was reported by 150 (74.3%) participants in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 201 (49.8%) participants in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. In the CLTSH-implemented kebeles, 32 (15.8%) participants said they started complementary feeding before six months, whereas in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles, 163 (40.3%) participants reported the same, and delayed initiation (after six months) was reported by 20 (9.9%) and 40 (9.9%) participants in the respective Kebele groups.

Most children had received the rotavirus vaccine, reported by 198 (98.0%) participants in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 380 (94.1%) participants in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. Breastfeeding practices were reported by 123 (60.9%) participants in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 200 (49.5%) participants in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles (Table 2).

Environmental characteristics

Improved water sources were used by 170 (84.2%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 182 (45.0%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. The majority of households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles reported spending more than 30 min to obtain drinking water (305 [75.5%]), compared with 92 (45.5%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles. Consumption of more than 25 L of water per capita per day was reported by 197 (97.5%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 304 (75.2%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles.

Most households stored drinking water in jerry cans, reported by 183 (90.6%) in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 279 (69.1%) in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. Pouring was the most common method of drawing water, practiced by 184 (91.1%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 279 (69.1%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. Water storage containers were covered in 195 (96.5%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 273 (67.6%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles.

Latrines were available in 149 (73.8%) households with CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 239 (59.2%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. Among households with latrines, shared facilities were more common, reported by 135 (67.3%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 203 (50.2%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. Proper waste disposal practices were reported by 109 (54.0%) households in CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 88 (21.8%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles.

Hand washing facilities were present in 92 (45.5%) households with CLTSH-implemented kebeles and 74 (18.3%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. Disposal of infant feces in toilets was reported by 120 (59.4%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 299 (74.0%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles. Domestic animals were kept in the same house by 91 (45.0%) households in CLTSH-implemented Kebeles and 113 (28.0%) households in CLTSH non-implemented Kebeles (Table 3).

Factors associated with the prevalence of diarrhea

Factors associated with the prevalence of diarrhea

Socio-demographic characteristics

In the multivariable analysis, several socio-demographic factors showed meaningful associations with the occurrence of acute diarrhea among under-five children. Children who were not cared for by their mothers had a higher chance of developing diarrhea compared to those cared for by their mothers; nevertheless, this relationship did not attain statistical significance. Similarly, caretaker age was not significantly associated with diarrhea status, as children of younger caretakers did not differ substantially in diarrhea occurrence compared to those whose caretakers were aged 25–34 years.

Household composition also showed limited influence. Children from households with two or more under-five children exhibited increased odds of diarrhea compared to households with only one child, but this difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, marital status demonstrated a strong protective effect. Children from married households had substantially lower odds of diarrhea than those from unmarried households, and this association was statistically significant.

Child sex was significantly associated with diarrhea prevalence. Male children were markedly more likely to experience diarrhea than female children, indicating a clear difference in vulnerability by sex. Additionally, household size emerged as an important determinant. Children living in households with more than five members had significantly higher odds of developing diarrhea compared to those residing in smaller households, suggesting increased exposure risks in more crowded living conditions.

Overall, marital status, child sex, and household size were identified as significant socio-demographic factors associated with acute diarrhea, whereas caretaker characteristics and the number of under-five children in the household did not show statistically significant associations (Table 4).

Environmental characteristics

Environmental factors played an important role in the occurrence of acute diarrhea among under-five-year-old children. Children from households that relied on unimproved sources of drinking water were significantly more likely to experience diarrhea than those using improved water sources. Similarly, prolonged time required to obtain drinking water, particularly when water collection took more than 30 min, was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of diarrhea.

In contrast, several environmental sanitation and hygiene-related variables did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with diarrhea occurrence. These included the type of latrine used, methods of solid waste disposal, household hand-washing practices, disposal practices of infant feces, and the presence of domestic animals within the household. The lack of statistically significant differences for these variables suggests that, within this study population, water source and accessibility were more influential environmental determinants of diarrhea than sanitation or hygiene practices (Table 5).

Behavioral characteristics

Behavioral factors showed strong associations with the prevalence of acute diarrhea among under-five children. Proper latrine utilization was significantly protective, as children from households with improper latrine use were markedly more likely to experience diarrhea than those from households practicing proper latrine utilization. This finding indicates that the effective use of sanitation facilities plays a critical role in reducing diarrhea risk.

Hand washing behavior was also a significant determinant. Children whose mothers did not practice hand washing with soap or ash were more likely to develop diarrhea than those whose mothers practiced appropriate hand hygiene, underscoring the importance of basic hygiene behaviors in preventing enteric infections.

Rotavirus vaccination demonstrated a strong protective association. Children who had not received rotavirus vaccination were substantially more likely to experience diarrhea than vaccinated children. However, the wide confidence interval observed for this association suggests considerable statistical uncertainty, likely reflecting the small number of unvaccinated children in the study population. Therefore, this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a clear difference in the two-week prevalence of diarrhea among under-five children between CLTSH-implemented and CLTSH non-implemented kebeles. The lower prevalence in the kebeles implemented with CLTSH indicates that community-led initiatives for sanitation and hygiene significantly contribute to reducing childhood cases of diarrheal illness. Consistent with this finding, previous comparative studies have reported substantial reductions in diarrhea prevalence in areas where CLTSH programs were actively implemented compared to non-implemented areas [6]. Similarly, evidence from a cluster-randomized trial indicated a significant decline in childhood diarrhea incidence in intervention villages following CLTSH implementation [8]. In addition, studies focusing on integrated water, sanitation, and hygiene interventions have reported lower diarrhea prevalence in communities with improved sanitation coverage [26].

The observed reduction in diarrhea prevalence is likely attributable to improved sanitation behaviors, including increased latrine utilization, reduced open defecation, and enhanced hygiene practices promoted through CLTSH activities. These behavioral changes interrupt fecal–oral transmission pathways and contribute to improved child health outcomes. However, not all studies have reported consistent effects. For example, research conducted in the Shebedino district of the Sidama Zone did not find a statistically significant difference in diarrhea prevalence between CLTSH and non-CLTSH areas [29]. Such variation may reflect differences in implementation quality, community participation, or contextual environmental factors.

Although the overall prevalence of diarrhea in this study was higher than the national estimate reported in previous national surveys [3], this discrepancy may be explained by local conditions such as water source quality, seasonal variation during data collection, population density, and sociodemographic characteristics. Moreover, differences in program maturity and consistency of CLTSH enforcement across kebeles may have also influenced the observed prevalence.

In the present study, male children were significantly more likely to experience acute diarrhea compared with female children. This finding is consistent with several studies conducted in Ethiopia, which have reported a higher prevalence of diarrheal diseases among male children [4, 25, 28]. Possible explanations include behavioral differences, as male children are often more physically active, spend more time outdoors, and may have greater exposure to contaminated environments. In addition, caregiving practices may differ by sex in some settings, potentially influencing hygiene supervision and exposure to enteric pathogens. Biological susceptibility has also been suggested, although evidence remains inconclusive. The consistency of this finding across multiple studies suggests that child sex may be an important consideration in designing targeted diarrhea prevention and control strategies.

Environmental factors were also found to play a critical role in childhood diarrhea. In particular, reliance on unimproved drinking water sources was associated with a higher likelihood of diarrhea, which is consistent with existing evidence identifying unsafe water as a major contributor to enteric infections among under-five-year-old children in Ethiopia [22]. Similarly, longer time spent collecting drinking water was associated with increased diarrhea prevalence. This may be due to the increased risk of water contamination during transport and storage, as well as reduced water availability for hygiene practices, as highlighted in previous studies [19].

Adequate water availability appeared to have a protective effect against diarrhea, likely by enabling regular handwashing, cleaning of utensils, and maintenance of household hygiene. In contrast, unsafe water storage practices were associated with a higher occurrence of diarrhea. Specifically, storing water in open or inadequately protected containers may increase the risk of contamination, thereby facilitating pathogen transmission. Consistent with this, prior studies have emphasized the importance of safe water storage practices in preventing diarrheal diseases [31]. Similarly, evidence from Ethiopia and Nigeria has demonstrated that the use of uncovered or improperly designed water storage containers significantly increases the risk of childhood diarrhea [11, 34].

Furthermore, access to sanitation facilities emerged as an important protective factor. Households with access to latrines exhibited lower diarrhea prevalence, underscoring the role of improved sanitation in reducing environmental contamination and interrupting disease transmission. Previous research, including meta-analyses by [27] and [30], has found that children living in households without latrine facilities face a higher risk of diarrhea. Collectively, these findings highlight the synergistic importance of sanitation infrastructure, safe water access, and hygiene practices in reducing childhood diarrheal disease.

Conclusion

Acute diarrhea remains a significant public health problem among under-five children in Chiro Woreda. The prevalence of diarrhea was notably lower in CLTSH-implemented kebeles compared to CLTSH non-implemented kebeles, indicating the comparative advantage of CLTSH in reducing childhood diarrheal disease. Key determinants significantly associated with diarrhea included source of drinking water, time spent collecting water, latrine utilization, handwashing practices, rotavirus vaccination status, child sex, and household size. These findings underscore the multifactorial nature of childhood diarrhea and highlight the importance of integrated water, sanitation, hygiene, and immunization interventions.

Recommendation

The study supports scaling up CLTSH interventions, particularly in CLTSH non-implemented kebeles, to reduce under-five diarrheal disease. Priority should be given to improving access to safe water, reducing water collection time, and strengthening latrine utilization and handwashing practices through routine hygiene promotion. In addition, increasing rotavirus vaccination coverage via strengthened routine immunization services is essential. Integrating these actions into existing WASH and child health programs will support sustainable reductions in childhood diarrhea.

Strength and limitation of the study

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between CLTSH implementation and diarrhea occurrence. Second, diarrhea status was self-reported, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Third, the questionnaire was not formally validated, although it was adapted from prior studies and pretested. Additionally, the five-year gap between CLTSH implementation and assessment may have influenced observed associations, as sanitation behaviors may change over time.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable community-level evidence by comparing CLTSH-implemented and non-implemented kebeles using a relatively large sample size.
