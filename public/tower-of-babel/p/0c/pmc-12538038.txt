A case of chronic bilharzia endocervicitis, mimicking cervix dysplasia, in sub-Saharan Africa
Authors: Yacouba Cissoko, Amavi Essenam Akakpo Alle, Mamadou Sima, Aliou Bina Diarra, Ezéchiel Djokdelna Gandaye, Sounkalo Dao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Unknown prevalence.•Misdiagnosed and underestimated disease.•Efficient medical treatment.Keyword: Female genital schistosomiasis, Mali

Introduction

Schistosomiasis is the second most endemic parasitic disease after malaria. In 2022, it affected 264.3 million people in 52 countries, with 80% to 90% of them residing in Africa [1]. Schistosomiasis can cause severe morbidity and mortality by generating granulomas, organ disease, malignancies, and an increased chance of HIV infection, resulting in 800,000 fatalities per year. The pathogen is a trematode of the Schistosoma genus that is transmitted to people by agricultural, residential, professional, or recreational activities in freshwater environments where gastropod mollusks live [2]. Schistosomes infect people in five different species, all of which have similar life cycles that include freshwater mollusks. Schistosoma haematobium has a tropism for the urogenital tract, while the other species—mansoni, japonicum, mekongi, and intercalatum—cause digestive illness [2]. Female genital schistosomiasis (FGS) is one of the most underdiagnosed diseases, defined as a “sexual and reproductive health problem that you probably have never heard of” [3] and a “hidden scourge of young girls and women” [4]. FGS is thought to be one of the most prevalent conditions in many sub-Saharan African societies. In Mali, a previous study showed a frequency of 14.3% of FGS among all gynecological surgy and 19% of chronic cervicitis among all FGS cases [5]. Women with FGS may exhibit signs and symptoms similar to those associated with sexually transmitted infections, such as vulvar burning, light bleeding (spotting), atypical and foul-smelling vaginal discharge, lower abdomen pain, hematuria, and genital ulcers [6]. Long-term effects of FGS include genital organ damage, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, low birth weight, and premature birth. Increasingly, evidence suggests that FGS may be a risk factor for HIV infection [[7], [8], [9]]. It is estimated that 40 million women and girls in Sub-Saharan Africa are affected by FGS [4], with many of them not receiving an accurate diagnosis due to health care workers’ lack of understanding about the disorder or limited access to health services [10]. The best methods for diagnosing BGF include using a colposcope to observe lesions similar to grains of sand and abnormal blood vessels that are diagnostic of the infection, as well as identifying distinctive eggs on biopsy specimens. The treatment is praziquantel-based. This treatable disease is frequent [11], but it is often misdiagnosed because of its unusual location in the cervix, where other pathologies, including cervical dysplasia and cancer, are most expected and searched for. The present case illustrates the identification of chronic bilharzia cervicitis when investigating cervical dysplasia.

Case presentation

The patient is a 23-year-old female from Lambidou Soma Niakaté, a village in the Kayes area of Mali known for its many temporary swamps. She is married and has one child. She has been living in Bamako, the capital city, for three years. She has no known medical or surgical history. The obstetrics department at Point-G Teaching Hospital referred her to the infectious and tropical diseases department for genital schistosomiasis therapy. The patient's history goes back roughly three months, when she went to a community health center for her child’s vaccination and was offered a voluntary cervical cancer screening. The visual inspection approach was used after applying acetic acid and Lugol’s iodine to the cervix (Figure 1). With the positive results from these tests, she was referred to an upper-level health care facility, where a cervical biopsy was performed. Histological investigation of the biopsy revealed low-grade dysplasia, which was categorized as CIN1 (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia). These lesions were treated with two sessions of thermoablation but showed no improvement. She was then prescribed an antibiotic (Azithromycin 500 mg for 9 days) and an antiparasitic (Albendazole 400 mg for 3 days). Despite all of these therapies, the lesions remained. The patient was therefore referred to the gynecology and obstetrics department of Point G Teaching Hospital for another cervical biopsy. Pathology analysis of the second biopsy revealed inflammatory infiltration of the stroma composed of lymphocytes and neutrophils, as well as fibrosis, congestion, and schistosome eggs, in addition to typical endocervical glands (Figure 2). The conclusion was chronic bilharzia endocervicitis. The patient was then sent to the infectious and tropical diseases department for definitive treatment. During questioning, she reported frequent contact with freshwater during household and agricultural duties. Clinical examination revealed a good general condition, with normal vital signs. Organ examination showed no abnormalities, except on gynecological examination with speculum, which revealed lesions similar to grains of sand. A pelvic ultrasound revealed calcification located at the cervix, and she tested negative for HIV. The patient was prescribed praziquantel at a dose of 60 mg/kg in a single dose, repeated 15 days later. Follow-up appointments were arranged after the second dose of praziquantel, at months 1, 2, and 3. The cervical lesion progressively regressed and disappeared completely (Figure 1).

Discussion

This clinical case highlights the diagnostic challenges that many health care professionals may confront while identifying FGS. Before the final diagnosis, this patient visited three different health care facilities. FGS, on the other hand, is diagnosed visually by inspecting the cervix and vaginal wall for typical lesions. A digital camera or colposcope can help with visualization [10]. According to studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa, female genital schistosomiasis is poorly understood by health care professionals and the community, which explains the diagnostic delay [12,13]. The discovery was serendipitous, as it occurred during a volunteer screening for cervical cancer in our patient; despite experiencing post-coital pain, which could indicate this pathology, she had never sought medical care. This demonstrates that, despite government and non-governmental organizations' efforts to promote women's sexual health, the subject remains taboo in some areas. The cervical cancer screening test was positive, and the first biopsy revealed low-grade dysplasia (CIN 1). Several investigations have shown that the clinical characteristics of cervical schistosomiasis are similar to those of a malignant tumor, which could explain these findings [[12], [13], [14]]. Schistosomiasis can have other uncommon locations such as the skin, brain, and thorax [[15], [16], [17]]. The involvement of such locations by schistosomiasis is likely due to ectopic deposition of the parasitic eggs within a definitive host during vascular migration. These ectopic locations are underestimated. Thus, suspicion for schistosomiasis should be heightened based on patient demographics and travel to endemic areas. The evolution of these ectopic locations, as well as the usual ones, can lead to complications such as cancer [18]. For instance, our patient had previously been treated with thermoablation for precancerous lesions as well as antibiotics because the lesions persisted despite thermoablation. The chance that FGS will be confused with cervical cancer exposes women suffering from FGS to ineffective therapy. In some case series, surgical treatments such as hysterectomy are reported on erroneous clinical bases, yet the postoperative diagnosis is persistent cervical bilharziasis [12]. Nonetheless, this patient requires regular follow-up because some studies have concluded that S. haematobium infection appears to promote persistent genital infection by human papillomavirus, either by traumatizing the general epithelium or through local immunosuppression, which is associated with an increased risk of cervical cancer [19,20].
