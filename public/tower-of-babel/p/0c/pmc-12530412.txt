Retrospective study on the ‘sex ratio at birth’ in Western Sydney Local Health District between 2011 and 2020
Authors: Charmi Perera, Gyan Ainkaran, Beata Gidaszwski, Marjan Khajehei, James Elhindi, Seng Chai Chua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveSex selection is not permitted in Australia; however, there are indirect means for such selection to occur, for example, through sex identification and the early termination of pregnancy. Indirect evidence for such selection, if any, may be inferred from variations in the ‘sex ratio at birth’ (SRB) among different populations. This study aims to characterise the SRBs over 11 years in spontaneous and assisted births in the largely migrant population of Western Sydney.DesignThis is a retrospective cohort study.SettingWestern Sydney Local Health District over three maternity hospitals.ParticipantsA total of 109 931 births between 2011 and 2020, where gender was assigned at birth, were included in the study.Primary and secondary outcomes measuredThe primary outcome measured was the SRB between ethnicities and mode of conception.ResultsThere was a higher male to female SRB for mothers born in China (SRB=1.12) compared with mothers born in Australia (SRB=1.05). Mothers from China were 1.1 times more likely to have a son. Otherwise, there was no significant difference with the SRB according to year, parity and mode of conception.ConclusionAmong the Western Sydney population, mothers born in China show a higher male SRB, reflecting a possible sex preference. However, this study is not without some limitations, for which purpose further study is warranted.Keywords: NEONATOLOGY, REPRODUCTIVE MEDICINE, OBSTETRICS

Introduction

There are ethnicities that have a preference for a son.1 Son preference may stem from cultural, religious, socioeconomic and societal reasons. These include (but are not limited to) sons performing manual labour, ceremonial rituals, the costs of a dowry, carrying on the family name, the traditional responsibility of the son to care for the parents in old age and the possibility of a higher earning capacity (in some environments).2

To the extent that preferences may exist, sex selection is indirectly possible through sex identification and the early termination of pregnancy. Sex identification can be performed through genetic testing of the embryo after in vitro fertilisation (IVF), non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT, introduced in Australia in 2012, and becoming the primary method of aneuploidy screening since 2015),3 ultrasound technology and/or prenatal diagnosis using invasive methods (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis). These modalities of sex identification can be performed prior to IVF implantation, or from 10 weeks (NIPT) or thereafter for prenatal invasive investigations. In New South Wales, Australia, access to termination of pregnancy for non-medical reasons is available for gestations up until 22 weeks and 6 days,4 for which purpose, although not expressly permitted, sex selection is nevertheless possible. Notably, though there is no direct way of knowing if sex selection is occurring based on pregnancy termination, we can, however, infer the possibility from the sex ratio at birth (SRB) in different ethnic groups compared with the general population.5 SRB is the ratio of male sex:female sex neonates at birth, assigned based on their external anatomy. The Australian male-to-female ratio (SRB herein) was 1.05 in 2021.6 While there are natural variations, the range is expected to be between 1.02 and 1.06.7

International studies which have explored the SRB suggest a male preference in Indian and Chinese ethnicities. Urquia et al8 showed that the SRB for mothers born in India was 1.38 (for their third birth) and 1.66 (for their fourth or higher births) in a Canadian migrant population. A study from Huang et al1 showed that China’s SRB was 1.1 in 2015 and 1.09 in 2014, favouring a male preference. A study by Howell et al9 showed similar findings, with an increased male SRB for mothers born in China from their second pregnancy and women born in India from the third pregnancy.

The Western Sydney Local Health District (WSLHD) in New South Wales, Australia, is the most populous region of Australia, with a population of over 940 000 covering an area of 780 km2 (WSLHD 2024). Over 65% of the population consists of individuals from immigrant and culturally diverse backgrounds—those from the Indian subcontinent and China being the two biggest migrant groups.10 The primary aim of this study is to characterise the SRBs of the WSLHD population according to mother’s country of birth, wherein we hypothesise an increased SRB for mothers born in China and/or India. Secondary aims include characterising the SRBs according to year (covering the period NIPT was introduced and became popular), parity and mode of conception, wherein we hypothesise an increased SRB according to parity and with IVF pregnancies. Finally, we hypothesise that if there were any sex ratio differences between groups, they would be accentuated with the introduction of NIPT testing.

Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study conducted in WSLHD. The data included all deliveries at Auburn, Blacktown and Westmead hospitals, for singleton fetuses between January 2010 and December 2020. The maternal country of birth, parity, gravidity, mode of conception and sex of neonate determined at birth was extracted from the existing electronic database (ObstetriX prior to 2017 and eMaternity from 2017). The database is monitored daily by an appointed data custodian wherein errors and/or omissions are updated continuously. Babies with ambiguous genitalia or ‘unidentified’ gender were excluded from the analysis—this left a total of 109 931 births collected over the 11-year period. As a retrospective cohort study, patients were not involved in the design or recruited for this study.

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was given by Western Sydney Local Health Human Research Ethics Committee as 2201–04 QA. The requirement for consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the minimal risks to participants and use of deidentified data. There was no patient and public involvement in this study due to privacy and confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

Data preparation and analysis was performed using R, V.4 (R Core Team, 2023, Vienna, Austria). SRBs (male to female) were calculated and compared for each exposure of interest—year, parity, mother’s country of birth, financial class and conception mode—by conducting χ2 tests. We reported the sample counts, SRB and 95% CIs. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were then performed to determine whether the SRB differed by mother’s country of birth and whether that difference was further differentiated by subgroups and over time. A likelihood ratio test assessed whether including an interaction term between mother’s country of birth and another factor improved the overall fit of the model. ORs, 95% CIs and p values were used to report the odds of a male-born son, and marginal values and 95% CIs were used to quantify the effect of any additional interaction term.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate the ‘SRB’ for all singleton births within the WSLHD between 2010 and 2020 according to several variables. To the extent that we hypothesised, guided by previous studies,1 8 9 an increased male-sex ratio for (1) women born in China and India, (2) women with increasing parity and (3) children born via assisted conception, the results of the present study were largely inconclusive for want of sample size. To that end, it is difficult to make any substantiated comments in respect of any possible cultural predisposition towards sex selection; further study is certainly warranted.

Despite these general findings—wherein there was no significant difference with the SRB according to year, parity and mode of conception (table 1)—we nevertheless found a borderline significant result in respect of the SRB according to maternal country of birth (table 1). That is, mothers born in China had a greater tendency towards male births than mothers born in Australia, India and ‘other’. These findings are consistent with a number of previous studies suggesting a cultural predilection for male children1 8 9 and the possible use of certain sex ‘selection’ techniques, before and after conception, so as to achieve such family planning outcomes.11 What is reassuring, however, is that the SRB difference was not increased among the Chinese mothers with the advent of non invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), pointing to a minimal impact, if any, of this potential bias, if it is in fact true.

Our data demonstrate a SRB of 1.05 for Australian-born mothers and 1.07 for Indian-born mothers. These figures are consistent with international findings, including an SRB of 1.08 among Indian-born mothers in the USA.9 Similarly, UK government data from 2017 to 2021 reported SRBs of 1.052 for Chinese-born mothers and 1.056 for Indian-born mothers.12 Notably, our study identified a higher SRB of 1.12 among Chinese-born mothers, exceeding both international benchmarks and the expected biological range, suggesting a possible underlying sex preference within this subgroup.

There are a few considerations in respect of this result. First, the assessment was according to the maternal country of birth, not origin. For example, a mother of Australian descent, nevertheless born in China herself, was taken as ‘Chinese’ for the purpose of this assessment. This was done according to how data were collected in the relevant maternity databases; however, it leads to a realistic difficulty in assessing the likely cultural inclinations of any said mother. Australia is a net immigration country, with low rates of emigration overseas, meaning most migrant births will represent the Australian population.13 As such, reliance on country of birth as a cultural proxy must be interpreted with caution, and future research would benefit from considering language spoken at home or cultural integration.

Second, the assessment clearly left out the paternal country of birth/origin. To the extent that family planning, and any possible sex selection foresought, is usually a joint decision, the absence of any data in respect of the father (paternal or otherwise) is clearly a deficiency making it difficult to make any further substantiated claims in respect of any posited Chinese cultural predilection towards males.

Thirdly, to the extent that any sex ‘selection’ can only realistically be carried out via IVF, or via the early termination of pregnancy up until 22 weeks—with the former only being allowed in Australia for ‘medical reasons’14 and the latter being a substantial claim in the absence of any evidence—we hypothesised that assisted births would indeed yield a higher male-sex ratio. Generally speaking, this was not the case (table 1, p=0.76). However, via secondary analysis, inclusion of ‘mode of conception’ to a ‘country of birth’ based model yielded demonstrable effect sizes in respect of mothers born in China whose births were conceived via assisted conception (online supplemental table 1 and figure 2). Although this might provide borderline evidence to suggest a Chinese cultural predilection towards male embryo implantation when using IVF, the result itself was insignificant (p=0.19) possibly for want of sample size—it is also worth noting the large, overlapping confidence intervals (online supplemental table 1 and figure 2). Further investigation with a larger sample size is certainly warranted.

Further, subgroup analyses are according to ‘maternal age’ and ‘country born’. Our results suggest a significantly elevated SRB among Chinese-born mothers aged 25–29. The age-specific pattern has not been widely reported in the international literature. This may be attributable to intergenerational influence. Studies show that Chinese immigrant mothers may be influenced by elders in their families.15 As such, intergenerational dynamics may influence reproductive preferences. If so, this highlights the importance of culturally appropriate education to target this cohort.

Socioeconomic status was assessed via financial model of obstetric care (overseas visitor, free Medicare or private fee) as education/income data were not available. Medicare service in Australia is a publicly funded, free healthcare that covers maternity care, while some patients may elect to pay privately for obstetric care. There were no significant associations in SRB seen among financial classes (table 4), suggesting limited influence of these factors on SRB in our cohort.

In respect of ‘parity’, we were only able to collect data on the total number of children born to our mothers of interest up to a category of ‘≥2’ (tables1 2). This resulted in two limitations: (1) we could not accurately assess the SRB according to specific parities greater than 2, and (2) we could not accurately assess the SRB according to the last child in family planning—this is a deficiency of a number of previous studies.1 8 11 We hypothesise, in the context of future research, that it is indeed the ‘final’ child which is most prone to sex selection, particularly if the previous children were female.

The most obvious limitation is that the results of this study, if any, merely provide indirect evidence of any sex selection tendencies that may be occurring in migrant populations in Australia. Not only are these results specific to a local health district which is vastly different from other health districts demographically, socioeconomically and culturally—the results themselves (in the form of an SRB), in the absence of corroborated testimony by mothers, merely provide an inference of possible sex selection.

Conclusions

Our findings provide borderline evidence to suggest that in Western Sydney, between 2010 and 2020, mothers themselves born in China have an increased male SRB compared with other countries of maternal birth (including Australia). This may suggest a cultural predisposition to male sex selection. To the extent that any selection may exist, this can have profound impacts on society and population health, including gender imbalances and the reinforcement of gender stereotypes, especially within certain ethnicities. However, without additional statistically significant findings and in the presence of a number of discussed limitations, any further substantiated conclusions cannot be drawn.
