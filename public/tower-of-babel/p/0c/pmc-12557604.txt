The association of steatotic liver disease with cardiometabolic criteria and cardiovascular disease risk in a large cohort of people living with human immunodeficiency virus
Authors: Richard K. Sterling, Alice L. Sternberg, Jordan E. Lake, Susanna Naggie, Sonya Heath, Jennifer C. Price, Laura Wilson, Holly Crandall, Samer Gawrieh, Naga Chalasani, Rohit Loomba, Mark Sulkowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsSteatotic liver disease (SLD), both metabolic dysfunction-associated liver disease (MASLD) without or with alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD), is common in people with HIV (PWH) and associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD).MethodsClinical and laboratory data were collected prospectively. MASLD was defined as SLD with ≥1 cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRF) and non-significant alcohol use. CVD risk was assessed by five 10-year risk scores (Framingham CVD score, 2013 ASCVD score, and PREVENT scores for total CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure) and one 5-year CVD score (D:A:D). Those without SLD were compared to MASLD and MetALD.ResultsAmong 991 PWH without history of CVD, MASLD was present in 40% and MetALD in 9%. At least 4 CMRF were present in 55% of those with MASLD, 61% MetALD compared to 29% without SLD. Those with MASLD had higher PREVENT total CVD score than those without SLD and similarly for mean PREVENT ASCVD and heart failure scores. Those with MASLD also had higher mean risk than those without SLD by Framingham CVD score, 2013 ASCVD score, and D:A:D score. Conversely, those with MetALD and those without SLD had similar CVD risk scores. 10-year CVD risk level by PREVENT score was generally least.ConclusionsCMRF are very common in PWH, with MASLD or MetALD frequently having multiple CMRF. The PREVENT scores showed higher 10-year CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure risk in those with MASLD versus those without SLD.

Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has prolonged the lives of people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Consequently, chronic co-morbidities, including liver and cardiovascular disease are common in people with HIV (PWH) [1,2] and result in significant morbidity and mortality [3]. With effective therapies for viral hepatitis, steatotic liver disease (SLD), including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [NAFLD]), has become a major comorbidity in PWH [4]. MASLD is a broad term that encompasses simple hepatic steatosis to steatohepatitis that can result in fibrosis leading to cirrhosis and is defined by SLD in the presence of at least 1 cardiometabolic disease risk factor (impaired glucose function or type 2 diabetes mellitus [T2DM], increased waist circumference [WC], overweight or obesity, dyslipidemia, or hypertension; CMRF). While there has been a recent nomenclature change [5], most PWH with NAFLD have MASLD. If the MASLD occurs in the presence of moderate alcohol use (140–350 g/week in females and 210–420 g/week in males), it is considered MASLD with co-existing alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD) and termed MetALD [6].

The burden of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in PWH has also increased significantly [7]. PWH have nearly double the risk of CVD compared to those without HIV[7]. While traditional risk factors for CVD include smoking, atherogenic dyslipidemia, hypertension, and weight gain (all features of the metabolic syndrome), PWH may have unique factors including CD4+ T-cell depletion, systemic inflammation, and increased intestinal permeability [8]. MASLD is also associated with the development of CVD [[9], [10], [11], [12]], and MASLD in PWH may further modulate CVD risk [13]

To minimize the potential limitations and over-estimates of prior CVD scores, the American Heart Association (AHA) recently developed a new CVD risk calculator, the Predicting Risk of CVD EVENTs (PREVENT) equations [14], to account for additional factors that are associated with metabolic health not included in the 2013 atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk score [15,16] or Framingham risk score [17,18]. The base PREVENT equations include terms for body mass index (BMI) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in addition to age, total cholesterol (XOL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, systolic blood pressure (SBP), and presence of diabetes, current smoking, and use of anti-hypertensive and lipid-lowering medications; extended equations include terms for glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and/or zip code of residence that produce a more comprehensive assessment of CVD risk [14,19] . The PREVENT equations provide estimates of total CVD risk, ASCVD risk, and heart failure risk for adults in the United States (US) 30 to 79 years of age without CVD at baseline.

While several studies have examined the relationship of CVD risk in PWH and MASLD [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]], the cardiometabolic risks and the impact of their interaction with alcohol use in MetALD compared to MASLD on CVD/ASCVD risk using the newer PREVENT risk scores or the data-collection on adverse effects of anti-HIV drugs (D:A:D) score develop in PWH are not known. While differences in cardiometabolic risks between MASLD and MetALD without HIV have been studied, differences in PWH are unknown [28]. The aim of this analysis was to compare cardiometabolic features and CVD risk scores in a large, US-based, adult cohort of PWH stratified by MASLD, MetALD, or no SLD.

Methods

Study design and population

Adult PWH at eight centers across the US (Duke University, Indiana University, Johns Hopkins University, University of Alabama Birmingham, University of California, San Diego, University of California, San Francisco, UTHealth Houston, and Virginia Commonwealth University) were invited to enroll in this prospective observational study (R01DK121378; HIV NASH CRN, ClinialTrials.gov NCT05023044). Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, documented HIV defined by a history of positive HIV antibody assay and on an ART regimen for a minimum of 6 months prior to enrollment with HIV-1 RNA <200 copies/mL Exclusion criteria included evidence of current or prior chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV). This was assessed by the presence of HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) in serum at any time prior to enrollment (patients with isolated antibody to hepatitis B core [HBc] antigen, anti-HBc total antibody, were not excluded), evidence of recent or current HCV as marked by the presence of anti-HCV antibody with detectable HCV RNA in serum within 3 years prior to enrollment. We excluded known other chronic liver disease, including but not limited to alpha-1-antitrypsindeficiency, Wilson’s disease, hemochromatosis, polycystic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, and primary biliary cholangitis; disseminated or advanced malignancy; pregnancy; concomitant severe underlying systemic illness that, in the opinion of the investigator, would interfere with completion of study procedures. We also excluded those unable or unwilling to complete a FibroScan® vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) scan, or any other condition that, in the opinion of the investigator, would impede compliance or hinder completion of study procedures; inability to complete the informed consent process or comply with study procedures, and unwillingness to sign the informed consent statement. Participants with prior diagnosis of CVD (defined as report of angina, cerebrovascular disease or stroke, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, aortic dissection, acute coronary disease, blockage in heart, carotid artery stenosis, ischemic cardiomyopathy, quadruple bypass, left hemiparesis and dysarthria) were also excluded. The study protocol was approved by a central institutional review board (Advarra) and each site’s institutional review board.

Clinical and laboratory parameters

Clinical and laboratory parameters were collected by chart review supplemented by participant interview and self-administered questionnaires, physical examination, and laboratory testing. Transient elastography (TE) by Fibroscan® was performed by trained study staff for liver stiffness measurement (LSM) for fibrosis and controlled attenuated parameter (CAP) for steatosis using the M or XL probe, as appropriate. Unreliable LSM was defined as Equivalent Liver Stiffness (LSM IQR/median) >30 %. Data collected included demographics, anthropometrics, medical history for comorbidities and AIDS-defining illness, medication use, smoking and alcohol history, and laboratory tests obtained within 90 days of the visit, including HIV-1 RNA copies, CD4+ T-cells, CD8+ T-cells, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), platelet count (PLT), fasting lipid panel (XOL, HDL, LDL, triglycerides [TG]), and HbA1c. Current ART medications were categorized by class (nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor [NRTI], non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor [NNRTI], protease inhibitor [PI], integrase strand transfer inhibitor [INSTI]). Alcohol use information was collected via self-completion of the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) which provides an assessment of current drinking amount and habits [29] and is scored by summing the responses to the 10 items comprising the questionnaire. Alcohol use was categorized as non-significant (<3 drinks/day with AUDIT score<8), moderate (<3 drinks/day with AUDIT score ≥8 or 3–4 drinks/day), or other.

Assessment of steatotic liver disease

Presence of SLD and classification of SLD type were determined using TE data, alcohol use data, and assessment of CMRF per the AASLD criteria [6,30]. CAP ≥263 dB/m defined presence of SLD [31]. Alcohol use was categorized as above (non-significant, moderate, or other). The 5 CMRF defined by AASLD were: BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (≥23 kg/m2 in Asians) or WC >94 cm in males (>80 cm in females); fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥5.7 %, T2DM, or use of antidiabetic medications and not T1DM; blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or use of anti-hypertensive medication; serum TG ≥150 mg/dL or use of fibrate, nicotinic acid or omega-3 medication; HDL cholesterol ≤40 mg/dL in males (≤50 mg/dL in females) or use of statin or lipid-lowering medication.

Participants classified as having no SLD (CAP<263 dB/m), MASLD (CAP ≥263 dB/m, ≥1 CMRF, non-significant alcohol use), or MetALD (CAP ≥263 dB/m, ≥1 CMRF, moderate alcohol use) were included; those who were classified as having ALD (CAP ≥263 dB/m and greater than moderate alcohol use regardless of CMRF), cryptogenic SLD (CAP ≥263 dB/m, no CMRF, non-significant or moderate alcohol use), or other etiology SLD, or whose status could not be assessed due to missing data were excluded.

Other measures of categorizing steatotic liver injury obtained included Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) [32,33], LSM level [34], and Fibroscan-AST (FAST) score [35,36]. A FIB-4 > 1.3 defined any fibrosis and FAST score at least 0.36 defined at risk metabolic dysfunction associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Significant (F2–4) and advanced (F3–4) fibrosis were defined as LSM of ≥ 8 kPa and 12 kPa, respectively.

Outcome measures

Ten measures of CVD risk were calculated: total points score and 10-year risk of first CVD event using the Framingham equations [17,18] 10-year risk of hard CVD event using the 2013 ASCVD score [16]; 10-year risk of total CVD mortality, 10-year ASCVD risk, and 10-year risk of heart failure using the base PREVENT equations and using the PREVENT equations including the HbA1c term [14]; and 5-year risk of CVD using the reduced D:A:D model which is recommended for those highly exposed to antiretroviral treatment [37]. The Framingham risk scores were categorized as low (<10 %), intermediate (10–19.9 %), or high (≥20 %). The 2013 ASCVD risk scores were categorized as low (<5 %), borderline (5–7.4 %), intermediate (7.5–19.9 %), or high (≥20 %). The PREVENT total CVD scores were categorized as low (<7.5 %) or high (≥7.5 %).

All CVD risk measures were calculated using 2 strategies: including participants with parameters outside the ranges specified for the measure reset to the appropriate extreme allowed values (age 20–79 for Framingham; age 40–79 for 2013 ASCVD; age 30–79 years, SBP 90–200 mmHg, XOL 130–320 mg/dL, HDL 20–200 mg/dL, BMI 18.5–39.9 kg/m2, eGFR 15–150 ml/min/1.73m2, and HbA1c 3–15 % for PREVENT; no range limits were discerned for the D:A:D) and then redoing the analysis excluding participants whose parameters were outside the specified acceptable ranges.

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics and CVD risk measures were summarized with means or frequency distributions. Difference in means overall was assessed using analysis of variance; pairwise differences in means (MASLD v. No SLD; MetALD v. No SLD; MetALD v. MASLD) were assessed using t tests. Difference in frequency distribution was assessed using the chi-squared test. The pairwise P-values presented in the tables are unadjusted for multiple comparisons; a Bonferroni correction for 3 pairwise comparisons on each characteristic or outcome of interest results in a P value of 0.05/3 or 0.017 being considered statistically significant. Univariate logistic regression was used to assess differences between groups in risk of a 10-point increase in each 10-year risk score. All analyses were conducted in SAS version 9.4 (Cary, NC) or STATA version 15 (College Station, TX).

Compared to participants without SLD (Table 1), those participants with MASLD were older, more likely Hispanic, more likely to have a history of an AIDS defining illness and LSM ≥8 kPa, and had higher mean CD8+ T-cell count, ALT, MCV and FAST score. Participants with MetALD were more likely than those without SLD to be male, Hispanic, less likely Black, and have higher ALT, AST, FAST score and LSM. Comparing MASLD and MetALD, those with MASLD were older, female, more likely to have an AIDS-defining illness, and more likely on a PI, while those with MetALD had lower mean CD8+ T-cell count and higher mean ALT, AST, MCV, and FAST scores. We did not observe differences between MASLD and MetALD in race, LSM, CAP or mean CD4+ T-cell count.

Cardiometabolic features

Cardiometabolic features are shown in Table 2 for all and by SLD group. Mean (SD) XOL in the entire population was 175.5 (39.6) mg/dL and did not differ by group. HDL was lower in the MASLD group compared to the no SLD group (45.1 (15.9) v. 52.4 (17.3); P<0.001). Triglycerides were higher in the MASLD group compared to the no SLD group (169.1 (169.9) v. 112.7 (65.3); P<0.001). Participants with MASLD and MetALD tended to have greater BMI compared to those with no SLD. Discussion

In our large, multicenter, multiethnic cohort of PWH with and without SLD in the US, we observed that CMRF were common. While increased BMI, waist circumference, dyslipidemia, glucose intolerance and multiple cardiometabolic factors were more frequently observed in those with SLD, many without SLD also had CMRF. While all five cardiac risk scores were higher on average in the MASLD group compared to the no SLD group, the PREVENT risk scores were lower than the Framingham and ASCVD scores with lower percentages in the high risk (≥20 %) group, whereas scores in the MetALD group were similar to scores in the no SLD group. Risk scores for those with MASLD were generally higher than scores for those with MetALD.

With significant improvements in HIV-related outcomes, comorbidities such as MASLD and CVD are increasingly recognized in PWH and an important area of research focus [38,39]. PWH have an excess risk of CVD, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke, compared to those without HIV, and this risk is related to both direct and indirect HIV-associated mechanisms [8]. While use of INSTI as part of ART has been associated with MASLD, we did not observe a difference between MASLD and no SLD [40] in people on INSTI, likely due to the pervasive use of these agents in the current ART era. Due to shared pathogenesis, MASLD is often associated with cardiometabolic risks and those with MASLD are at increased risk for CVD, including PWH [23,41,42]. In addition, PWH and SLD have higher incidence of developing diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia [27,43]. In a study by Kaplan et al. those with MASLD had 3-fold greater odds of CVD. This was related to a greater prevalence of metabolic risks (BMI, hypertension dyslipidemia, and diabetes) in those without MASLD [24]. In another study of 941 PWH, of which 20 % had NAFLD and using the ASCVD risk score [16], Cervo et al observed that those with NAFLD had a 2-fold increased risk for CVD regardless of BMI [22]. In a recent study of US Veterans, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) were higher in PWH with MASLD compared to matched non-HIV MASLD controls (5.18 v. 4.48 per 100 person-years; P=0.03) [44]. Similar results on increased CVD outcomes were seen using a large non-Veteran cohort [25,45]. However, it may be the cardiometabolic risks in those with MASLD and not the SLD itself driving CVD outcomes [20,46].

While several studies have used coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores as a direct measure of CVD risk in PWH [19,21], other non-invasive scores are more widely applicable in clinical practice. Traditional CVD risk scores, such as the Framingham risk score and 2013 ASCVD equation are recommended to assess CVD risk in PWH [48] and often used [47,48]. Numerous studies have reported an underestimation, and some an overestimation, of observed risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) and CVD in PWH using equations commonly used in the general population, such as the Framingham and ASCVD risk algorithms [49,50]. Using the ASCVD risk estimator, Cervo et al. observed a higher score in those with NAFLD (aOR 2.11; 95 % CI 1.40–3.18; p<0.0001) and in those with NAFLD and BMI < 30 kg/m2 [22]. Similarly, Lake et al. observed higher ASCVD scores in those with NAFLD (5.6 v. 4.3; p=0.002) finding that those with SLD had higher lipoprotein-associated phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2) (a marker of vascular health), high sensitivity C-Reactive Protein (systemic inflammation), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 [51]. The D:A:D 5-year risk score, developed in PWH was recently evaluated in 3893 subjects participating in the REPRIEVE trial with moderate c-statistic of 0.71 (95 % CI 0.65–0.77) [52] In our cohort, the 2013 ASCVD and D:A:D scores were higher in those with MASLD compared to no SLD, while we observed no differences between those with MetALD v. no SLD nor MetALD v. MASLD, suggesting these findings may not be applicable to all PWH with SLD.

Newer scores, such as the PREVENT scores, may be superior to other scores but are impacted by renal function [41,53,54]. There are limited data on the use of the PREVENT scores in PWH [55]. One advantage of the PREVENT scores over ASCVD and Framingham scores is that they provide 10-year risks for CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure. In our cohort of PWH, those with MASLD had higher 10-year CVD risk, ASCVD risk, and heart failure risk compared to those without SLD. Interestingly, those with MetALD had lower 10-year CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure risk scores compared to MASLD. The lower risks in MetALD compared to MASLD may be related to higher HDL, as we observed no differences on other cardiometabolic risks. Our observation is similar to a Spanish cohort of 2227 PWH (76 had MetALD) that showed higher HDL in those with MetALD [26]. While we did observe statistical differences in PREVENT scores (base and with HgbA1c) (Table 3), the numerical differences were small. Therefore, the clinical significance is unknown and will require further study to translate into clinical decision making. However, our data do support the use of CVD risk scores in PWH and MASLD to identify those who might benefit from additional testing, such as CAC score.

Differentiating MASLD from MetALD can be challenging. In our cohort, AUDIT was used to identify moderate drinking and those with heavy drinking were excluded. The AUDIT is specifically designed to assess alcohol consumption patterns and identify potential alcohol use disorders, making it a valuable tool in evaluating patients where alcohol intake is a concern. Therefore, if the goal is to differentiate MetALD from MASLD, AUDIT is the preferred tool due to its direct assessment of alcohol consumption. Other tools, such as ALD/NAFLD Index (ANI)[56] is less effective in this specific context and is more useful for distinguishing severe ALD from MASLD rather than MetALD. While mild-moderate alcohol use has not been associated with lower CVD risk in NAFLD without HIV [57] and in heavy alcohol use in PWH [58], we observed lower risks by all three PREVENT scores in MetALD compared to MASLD; results for Framingham and 2013 ASCVD scores were inconsistent or showed no difference. Studies with CVD events in PWH differentiating MASLD from MetALD are needed to determine if mild-moderate alcohol use in PWH and SLD might have a protective effect.

The strengths of our study include the large, multi-center cohort from diverse clinics in the US, the inclusion of a high proportion of racial/ethnic minority participants that have not been included in many studies of SLD in PWH, and the prospective study design including AUDIT scores to define moderate alcohol use in a contemporaneous population of PWH on current ART. While we did exclude active HCV or HBV, we did not require an elevated ALT in those with SLD, which should increase the generalizability of our observations. We also utilized the PREVENT risk score recommended by the AHA and expert panels [14,59,60]. However, our study was not without limitations. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference. And future longitudinal studies in this cohort are planned using CAC scores as the reference standard to better assess if the PREVENT scores translate into clinically meaningful decision-making. Because phosphatidylethanol (PeTH) tests were not used in all subjects, we relied on self-reported alcohol use, which may have resulted in mis-classification of some participants. We also defined SLD with a CAP score of at least 263 dB/m [31]. Therefore, our results may have changed if a higher threshold was used [33]. We also had fewer subjects with MetALD limiting the power to detect differences between MASLD and MetALD. Finally, we did not compare the CVD risk scores to MASLD patients without HIV.

In conclusion, while cardiometabolic risk factors are very common in PWH with MASLD or mild-moderate MetALD, they are also present in those without SLD, highlighting the importance of both MASLD and CVD in this population. The PREVENT scores showed higher 10-year CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure risk in those with MASLD versus those without SLD, and fewer with high risk than Framingham and 2013 ASCVD scores suggesting the PREVENT scores may have broader generalizability and lower overestimation in assessing CVD risk in PWH.
