A low-cost approach to high quality responsible conduct of research education
Authors: Sharmella Roopchand Martin, Bernardo García Camino, Paul J Cummins, Cheryl C Macpherson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe need for high-quality, accessible training in responsible conduct of research (RCR) globally is evident in the annual increase in paper retractions for scientific misconduct. This paper describes an RCR course that we developed as part of a master’s in bioethics degree (MScB) program available to a culturally and linguistically diverse group of scientists, clinicians, and other research stakeholders from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The course has been delivered fully online simultaneously in English and Spanish using instructional materials informed by the perspectives of two culturally diverse co‑teaching faculty members. In addition to aiming to educate learners and inspire them to conduct research responsibly and motivate others to do so, the pedagogical and technical design of the course is intentionally structured to strengthen fellows’ critical thinking, deepen their understanding of regional and international research ethics guidance, and promote collaboration between Spanish‑ and English‑speaking participants. The RCR course has earned Quality Matters certification, reflecting its commitment to high‑quality online pedagogy, whilst containing cost. In this paper, we outline the course’s structure and core design elements to offer a model that can be adapted for other RCR programs across diverse countries and cultural contexts.Keywords: Scientific misconduct, Research ethics, Distance education, Caribbean region, Low-middle-income countries, Capacity building

Introduction

Retraction rates of scientific publications have trebled in the past ten years with most of these being associated with scientific misconduct (Van Noorden 2023). During its 2022 fiscal year, the United States Department of Health and Human Services Office of Research Integrity (ORI) received 269 allegations of research misconduct (United States Department of Health and Human Services Office of Research Integrity 2022). ORI carried over 33 cases from the previous year, opened 38 new cases, closed 10, and issued 9 findings of research misconduct (United States Department of Health and Human Services Office of Research Integrity 2022). These cases and the sensational retraction of a Lancet article promoting the use of hydroxychloroquine to treat COVID-19 reaffirm the ongoing need for programs to instil research integrity among scientific researchers and foster public confidence in science (Mehra et al. 2020 [retracted]; Watson 2020).

Scholars have conducted empirical research to understand the prevalence of research misconduct (Fanelli 2009; John et al. 2012; Stricker and Günther 2019; Kaiser et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2021; Gopalakrishna et al. 2022; Phogat et al. 2023; Herrera-Añazco et al. 2024). Phogat et al. (2023) reported prevalence values ranging from 4.2% for self-reported studies to 33.4% in nonself-reported studies. Herrera-Añazco et al. (2024) reviewed 147 articles with authors from Latin America and the Caribbean that were published between 2003 and 2022 and subsequently retracted due to misconduct. The primary reasons for retraction included errors in procedures or data collection (n = 39), inconsistency in results or conclusions (n = 37), plagiarism (n = 21), and suspected scientific fraud (n = 19).

Research also reveals global deficits in knowledge of and attitudes toward research integrity (Azakir et al. 2020; Armond et al. 2021; Armond and Kakuk 2022; Bain et al. 2022; Boesz and Lloyd 2008; Heitman and Litewka 2011; Kim and Park 2013; Mwaka 2017; Rohwer et al. 2017; Were et al. 2023; Yi et al. 2019; Zeng and Resnik 2010; Felaefel et al., 2018) with some indicating that a higher percentage of scientists in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are not aware of many aspects of misconduct and a significant need for training (Azakir et al. 2020; Bain et al. 2022; Rohwer et al. 2017; Armond and Kakuk 2022). Even among those deeply knowledgeable about research ethics there is limited clarity and consensus about how to educate and motivate responsible conduct of research (RCR), for example, in what research benefits should be budgeted and provided by any given study (Macpherson 2019). International funders of research like the United States National Institutes of Health (NIH) recommend that all science training programs include RCR instruction and that scientists engage in ongoing RCR training throughout their careers (NIH Office of Intramural Research, 2024). Although the NIH and its Office of Intramural Research (OIR) supply guidance on goals and content for RCR (NIH OIR, 2024) there is little standardization in how this guidance is implemented with respect to time, format, assignments, or content.

The wholesale adoption of United States of America (USA) or European RCR frameworks is not appropriate for all LMICs (Bain et al. 2022). RCR curricula cannot be standardized across settings, as variations in customs, norms, and implicit biases demand culturally sensitive approaches to both structure and content (Davis 2003; Bosch and Titus 2009; Steele et al. 2016). Although “research is global, integrity is local” (Kleinert 2010), culturally responsive RCR education must still uphold rigorous standards of design, content, and delivery. Yet, despite a substantial body of RCR literature, there remains a lack of standardized or detailed guidance on how to achieve this. Few publications address the importance of adapting RCR education to national or institutional contexts, and existing efforts have not curbed the persistent problem of scientific misconduct.

Al‑Khatib and Kalichman (2019) describe a National Institute of Health Fogarty International Center (NIH–FIC) initiative in Jordan that developed a culturally responsive RCR curriculum focused primarily on human subjects’ research. However, research misconduct can arise in any domain and carries potential harms for humans, non‑human species, and entire ecosystems. Although several East Asian countries have reportedly strengthened their RCR education systems, Chou et al., (2024) identified persistent weaknesses in instructor quality and overall educational effectiveness. In Uganda, Mwaka (2017) notes that national efforts to promote RCR remain heavily dependent on NIH support, and numerous authors highlight the broader need for more robust and contextually grounded instruction (Plemmons and Kalichman 2018; Kretser et al. 2019; Silverman et al. 2013; Vasconcelos et al. 2015; Zeng and Resnik 2010; Labib et al., 2022; Bain et al. 2022).

The Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) program represents one of the most widely used sources of RCR education (Braunschweiger and Goodman 2007). Between its launch in 2000 and 2007, CITI delivered RCR training to more than 600,000 learners across 715 institutions in multiple countries (Braunschweiger and Goodman 2007). While CITI remains a valuable resource and offers single‑language tracks in English, Spanish, and Korean, its content is designed primarily to meet USA federal funding requirements, and the associated costs place it out of reach for many potential users.

In this paper, we describe and discuss an online RCR course developed as a required component of a master’s degree in bioethics for a culturally and internationally diverse pool of health scientists, professionals, and other research stakeholders from low‑ and middle‑income countries (LMICs). We present it as a scalable, low-cost, high-quality model for online RCR education that can support a wide range of stakeholders engaged in research and research ethics.

Pedagogical approach

The pedagogical approach for this bilingual online RCR course was grounded in the ethical commitments of cultural responsiveness, equity, and accessibility for learners from diverse LMIC contexts. These commitments shaped both the design philosophy and the instructional strategies used throughout the course. Recognizing that research integrity education cannot be meaningfully delivered through a one‑size‑fits‑all model, the course was intentionally structured to respect linguistic diversity, cultural variation in ethical reasoning, and differences in educational norms across regions.

The pedagogical and technical approaches were intentionally crafted to enrich critical thinking, expand understanding of regional and international research ethics guidance, and strengthen communication and collaborative skills among and between Spanish‑ and English‑speaking students.

Course design

To ensure a rigorous and learner‑centred design, we partnered with Clarkson University’s Teaching and Learning Corner (TLC) and adopted a backward‑design framework (Wiggins and McTighe 2005). This approach began with the identification of desired learning outcomes, followed by the development of assessments aligned with those outcomes, and finally the selection of content and activities to support them. The Quality Matters (QM) Higher Education Course Design Rubric Standards further guided the development process, prompting systematic reflection on clarity, accessibility, alignment, and learner engagement. The QM framework was especially valuable given the complexity of creating a unified course experience across two languages and multiple cultural contexts.

Cultural and linguistic responsiveness were central pedagogical commitments in course design. To this end the course was collaboratively designed by multiple partners, including the instructors, two LMIC‑based faculty members—a native English speaker from Jamaica and a native Spanish speaker from Mexico who brought deep familiarity with the cultural, linguistic, and professional landscapes of their regions. This collaboration ensured that concepts, examples, and instructional language were carefully negotiated to avoid cultural bias, maintain conceptual clarity, and promote acceptability among students. Weekly topics, for example were modelled on North American RCR curricula but were deliberately curated to incorporate regional and international guidelines. Assignments and activities encouraged discussion of regionally relevant issues, and word limits as well as grading criteria were adapted to account for linguistic, cultural, and contextual nuances in both languages. The full course content including the syllabus specifying the learning outcomes, activities and instructions, and grading rubric, are open access through the CREEi Centre for Global Bioethics https://www.windref.gd/center-for-global-bioethics/ and can be used by those designing similar courses or teaching similar content (CREEi 2025).

All course materials—including instructions, assignments, presentations, and grading rubrics—were concurrently provided in both English and Spanish. Artificial intelligence (AI)‑assisted translation tools were used to support accessibility, with all translations reviewed and refined by the co‑faculty to ensure accuracy and cultural sensitivity. A small subset of readings received professional human translation to maintain fidelity of meaning.

To ensure that scientific integrity was reinforced throughout the program of study, the course was offered during the early portion of the first year of a two‑year MScB program and ran for ten weeks each year for three years with annual cohorts of approximately fifteen students. Bilingual proficiency was not required, and fewer than 5% of students were bilingual.

Instructional strategies

The asynchronous online format was selected to accommodate students’ varied linguistic backgrounds, time zones, and professional obligations. This structure allowed learners sufficient time to process materials, engage with AI translation tools, and participate meaningfully in cross‑language interactions.

Pedagogical strategies were designed to foster critical thinking, ethical reasoning, and collaborative learning. Discussion forums required students to engage with peers across languages, encouraging reflection on regional similarities and differences in ethical challenges. Case studies, peer‑review assignments, and regionally relevant examples supported the development of analytical skills and contextual understanding. Oral presentation assignments using Flipgrid, narrated PowerPoint, and podcasts incorporated AI‑generated opposite‑language subtitles, ensuring accessibility for all students regardless of the language of delivery.

Building a learning community was also an intentional component of the pedagogical design. The course began with an icebreaker activity in which students shared photos, backgrounds, and interests, helping to establish trust and familiarity across cultural and linguistic boundaries. Faculty moderated discussion forums were used to deepen ethical reflection, support respectful dialogue, and model inclusive communication. Optional synchronous meetings were offered in students’ preferred languages to provide additional support.

Together, these pedagogical choices created a culturally sensitive, linguistically inclusive, and pedagogically rigorous learning environment. The approach not only addressed the challenges identified in regional evaluations of research ethics education but also demonstrated how bilingual, LMIC‑responsive RCR instruction can be effectively delivered online.

Quality assurance

Quality assurance was central to the course’s development. Online education has evolved significantly since its emergence in 1982 (Hamilton and Feenberg 2012), with online bioethics instruction pioneered at Loyola University in 2000 and Union College in 2001. The Clarkson Bioethics Program is the legacy successor to the Union College initiative. Prior to the COVID‑19 pandemic, online education had limited support among full‑time faculty (Ruth 2018), but the pandemic accelerated its adoption and the refinement of best practices across higher education (Dennis 2021). Clarkson University actively supports faculty in developing high‑quality online instruction (Cummins et al. 2022) through its Teaching and Learning Corner (TLC), and at the onset of the pandemic required all instructors to complete Reframing Instruction for Success Everywhere (RISE), a workshop focused on effective online pedagogy.

As described earlier, we partnered with TLC to implement best practices in online instructional design and to prepare the course for Quality Matters (QM) certification. QM is a nationally recognized, faculty‑centred peer‑review process for evaluating online course design, grounded in research‑supported standards and widely adopted both nationally and internationally (Quality and Matters 2025). After the course had been taught twice, QM‑certified reviewers evaluated it, confirmed that it met QM standards, and advanced it for formal peer review. In 2023, the course was officially certified as meeting the QM Higher Education Course Design Rubric Standards. Although QM certifies courses in multiple languages, to our knowledge, ours is the only simultaneous bilingual course to have earned this distinction.

Resources

The primary resources for delivery of this course and program were within the scope of those available within most or all tertiary level academic institutions. These include access to a learning management system (LMS) that can host course pages, readings and multimedia, quizzes, assignments, and discussion forums. Moodle, the LMS for our program, is open-source software that provides options for free access. Social media options and free AI translation services are available in internet browsers. Our program, but not the RCR course, designated course time to learning to use these to enable students to communicate with each other and with faculty at their convenience. More specialized resources essential to successfully designing and simultaneously delivering our RCR course in two languages include first, the appointment of two qualified faculty (in terms of content, credentials, and languages) with dedicated time and remuneration, and second, the academic freedom and motivation with which to collaboratively identify readings and course activities together. Motivation grew out of their openness to develop mutual trust and respect with each other as co-faculty and having a shared vision for the course and project. This led to a high standard of academic integrity.

Access to professional development and accreditation through QM was provided by the host university which had a long-standing agreement with QM to advance the quality of its online courses. Many universities and institutions choose not to invest financially in this or related services limiting access to this resource. Free resources that provide guidance for online teaching and courses have become widely available since the COVID-19 pandemic. Others can use the RCR course materials and approach that reflect QM standards without the QM designation. Finally, a significant resource was program directors and colleagues who were invested in the project, supported the RCR course faculty, and valued what they accomplished.

Discussion

In global health ethics, epistemic justice has been framed around three central questions: who produces ethics knowledge, which theories and concepts are used to generate that knowledge, and whose voices are sought and valued in the process (Pratt and de Vries 2023). Strengthening epistemic justice therefore requires intentionally inviting and nurturing voices from the global South through curricular content that introduces philosophical and ethical traditions rooted in the region, as well as “different epistemologies and ways of being that impact on” ethics in global health (Pratt and de Vries 2023). As Atuire (2023) argues, ethics curricula should empower students in the global South to identify and “find solutions to their problems,” rather than entrench epistemic injustice by simply transferring global‑North knowledge systems and epistemologies into LMIC contexts.

Our QM‑certified RCR course was designed and delivered in alignment with these recommendations. It actively engaged global South partners in curricular design and delivery, and in recruiting students, faculty, and bioethics research supervisors. Using our dual‑language model, 45 students from English‑and Spanish‑speaking LMIC countries completed the RCR course, with most completing and graduating from the entire MScB program. The RCR course, along with other courses in the full program, would have contributed to equipping graduates with skills to lead research ethics committees, teach RCR and scientific integrity, conduct research to the highest ethical standards, and integrate research ethics into healthcare and policy within their institutions.

Few, if any, RCR courses have attempted to measure the impact of their courses on subsequent scientific integrity or misconduct because the challenges of doing so are multiple. It is equally challenging to evaluate the impacts of any ethics teaching on subsequent behavior or actions because this involves moral education, whose methods and instructional techniques are disputed (Raho et al. 2024). Despite this, countless efforts to document and share experiences of designing and teaching RCR have been published.

The integrated bilingual model successfully fostered student engagement and collaboration across languages. Students interacted widely in discussion forums, often responding to peers in their non‑native language. One student noted that the course “motivated me to begin to study Spanish… this interaction has given me the inspiration to seriously pursue learning the language.” A native Spanish speaker similarly chose to complete some assignments in English to strengthen their academic writing skills.

Two years after the first delivery of the course, an onsite gathering revealed that several cross‑cultural relationships had endured. Students reported gaining insight into both the differences and similarities in research ethics needs and challenges across their countries, and expressed a deeper appreciation for the cultural diversity represented in the cohort. As one participant reflected, “it really gave me a much better appreciation for issues and challenges beyond the English‑speaking Caribbean.” A Spanish‑speaking student echoed this sentiment in a discussion forum, writing, “I like your point of view, and it allows me to better understand the situation in Grenada when it comes to ethical review matters.”

Ensuring that students received equivalent content in both languages posed ongoing challenges, particularly given the limited availability of bioethics literature published in both English and Spanish. While AI‑assisted translation helped bridge some linguistic gaps, cultural nuances were sometimes lost, underscoring the need for traditional human translation for key materials such as instructions, objectives, and assignments. Linguistic capacity to review translations is therefore critically important to dual-language courses. Without language proficient faculty, budgeting for translation is therefore essential for dual‑language course development. Having at least one bilingual lecturer significantly reduced translation costs and helped navigate cultural assumptions and expectations that shaped student interactions. For example, word limits required careful adjustment, as conveying a concept in Spanish often requires nearly twice as many words as in English; without this awareness, Spanish‑speaking students could have been inadvertently disadvantaged.

Cultural and linguistic differences also shaped patterns of communication. Spanish‑speaking students were notably more reluctant than English‑speaking students to initiate email communication with faculty, regardless of language. This reinforced the importance of clear, culturally sensitive instructions and the need to avoid relying solely on AI translation for critical course information. To this end the two faculty members delivering the course systematically vetted all instruction to ensure clarity. Students were also encouraged to develop informal groups using social media, and peer support through these channels improved communication.

Delivering a program across cultures and languages is inherently complex. Differences in educational systems, academic norms, and ways of thinking must be anticipated and addressed. Saenz et al. (2014), in their review of Fogarty‑funded programs in Latin America and the Caribbean, described tensions arising when programs were perceived as promoting an “American agenda.” A similar assessment ten years later found that many of the same concerns remain and highlighted how linguistic barriers, structural inequities, and limited regionally relevant materials continue to hinder equitable participation (Daly et al. 2025). Against this backdrop, the co‑teaching model was essential to the success of our course. Two lecturers—each deeply familiar with the cultural, linguistic, and professional contexts of their regions—collaborated on every aspect of the course, jointly agreeing on wording, concepts, examples, and presentation approaches to ensure acceptability and comprehension for all participants. Their partnership fostered mutual respect and helped balance how core RCR concepts were conveyed across languages and cultures.

Finally, the competitive landscape of global education and the scarcity of grant funding made high‑quality execution essential. The Quality Matters Higher Education Course Design Rubric Standards guided the development of the course, with support from Clarkson University’s Teaching and Learning Corner (TLC). Preparing the course to meet QM standards was meticulous, particularly in designing an online environment that maintained a unified look and feel across two languages. The QM rubric encouraged careful reflection on every aspect of course development and ensured that the course remained learner‑centred, with ample opportunities for human interaction and straightforward access to relevant information.

It is important to note that this course is designed as a facilitated online course for delivery to class sizes of no more than sixteen students. Student’s active engagement in all classroom activities is required as is guidance and grading by qualified and enthusiastic course faculty. Other online courses like CITI, and those offered by the World Health Organization and NIH are mostly asynchronous, lack personalized feedback, may not facilitate regular student-to-student communication, and may enroll far more than sixteen students in each iteration. NIH courses and resources are freely available, but CITI courses and resources are prohibitively expensive to most potential students unless their university or institution pays for and provides access.

Conclusions

The global need to strengthen research ethics capacity remains urgent, particularly in LMICs where structural, linguistic, and resource constraints continue to limit access to high‑quality training. Our bilingual, culturally responsive RCR course represents one model for addressing these challenges through intentional pedagogical design, cross‑cultural collaboration, and the strategic use of technology. By integrating international research ethics standards with regionally relevant examples and facilitating meaningful engagement across languages, the course provided learners from 17 LMICs of the Latin American and Caribbean region with opportunities to deepen their understanding of RCR in ways that were contextually grounded and practically applicable.

The co‑faculty model, supported by AI‑enabled translation tools and guided by QM standards, enabled the development of a unified course experience that was accessible, rigorous, and sensitive to cultural and linguistic diversity. Through asynchronous discussions, case analyses, peer review, and cross‑language presentations, students strengthened their ethical reasoning, communication skills, and capacity to navigate the complexities of applying research ethics principles in diverse settings. These experiences not only expanded their knowledge but also enhanced their ability to recognize, respond to, and help prevent scientific misconduct within their institutions and professional communities.

As global research collaborations continue to grow, the demand for research ethics education that is inclusive, adaptable, and culturally sensitive will only increase. We believe that the design principles and pedagogical strategies described here can be adapted for use in other international, intercultural, and inter‑university RCR programs, as well as in multi‑site research consortia. Our experience demonstrates that high‑quality, bilingual online instruction is both feasible and impactful, and that such approaches can contribute meaningfully to the development of ethically responsible research cultures across LMICs and beyond.
