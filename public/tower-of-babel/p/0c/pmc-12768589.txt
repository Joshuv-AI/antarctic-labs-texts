Prognostic Role of Preoperative Axillary Ultrasound for Lymph Node Metastasis and Recurrence in Early Stage Breast Cancers
Authors: Han Song Mun, Eun Young Ko, Boo-Kyung Han, Eun Sook Ko, Ji Soo Choi, Sang Hee Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEvaluating the role of preoperative axillary ultrasound (US) in early-stage, clinically node-negative breast cancer, focusing on its ability to predict nodal metastasis and long-term recurrence.MethodsThis retrospective study included patients with T1–T2 clinically node-negative breast cancer who underwent preoperative axillary US and surgery between January and December 2009. Based on US findings, patients were classified as US-positive (presence of suspicious nodes, such as cortical thickening or absent fatty hilum) or US-negative. Clinicopathological features and recurrence outcomes were analyzed using the χ2 test, Cox proportional hazards regression, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis.ResultsAmong 878 women (mean age, 49 ± 9 years), 234 were US-positive and 644 were US-negative; 283 patients were pathologic node-positive (pN ≥ 1) and 595 were node-negative (pN0). Preoperative axillary US demonstrated a sensitivity of 42.4% (95% confidence interval [CI], 36.8–48.2); specificity, 80.8% (95% CI, 77.5–83.8); positive predictive value, 51.3% (95% CI, 44.9–57.6); and negative predictive value, 74.7% (95% CI, 71.2–77.9). The US-positive group had a higher rate of axillary lymph node dissection (62.8% vs. 32.8%), greater mean number of metastatic nodes (2.6 vs. 0.5), and higher proportion of macrometastasis (94.2% vs. 71.8%) compared with the US-negative group (all p < 0.001). The 10-year recurrence-free survival was lowest in the pN-positive/US-positive group (90.3%; 95% CI, 82.7–94.7), intermediate in the pN-positive/US-negative group (92.4%; 95% CI, 86.7–95.7), and highest in the pN-negative group (97.4%; 95% CI, 95.4–98.5) (log-rank p < 0.001).ConclusionPreoperative axillary US might help assess lymph node metastasis in clinically node-negative patients. Moreover, US positivity was associated with an increased risk of long-term recurrence.Keywords: Axilla, Breast Neoplasms, Lymph Nodes, Recurrence, Ultrasonography

INTRODUCTION

Lymph node (LN) metastasis is the primary prognostic factor for recurrence and overall survival in breast cancer [1,2]. Nodal status often determines the need for systemic therapy, extent of surgical intervention, available reconstruction options, and requirement for postmastectomy radiation therapy [3].

The clinical approach to axillary management has evolved after pivotal trials such as the American College of Surgeons Oncology Group (ACOSOG) Z0011 [4] and the International Breast Cancer Study Group (IBCSG) 23–01 [5] trials. These studies demonstrated that patients with early-stage breast cancer and up to two positive sentinel lymph nodes (SLNs) had comparable outcomes, regardless of whether they underwent axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) following sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB). More recently, the 8th edition of the AJCC staging system incorporated tumor biology, including hormone receptor (HR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, into the traditional anatomic staging, further reducing the emphasis on nodal staging alone [6,7,8,9,10]. In this evolving context, the clinical utility of SLNB has shifted from a therapeutic procedure to a staging tool, with growing interest in omitting SLNB in patients at low risk of axillary LN metastasis [8,10,11].

Axillary ultrasound (US) is a simple, widely available, and routinely used preoperative tool [12,13,14]. However, its performance is operator-dependent and can be limited in deep or technically difficult regions, such as in patients with obesity. Previous studies have reported a wide range of sensitivity for axillary US (49%–87%) [15], similar to that of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (33%–97%) [16,17]. While accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity are commonly used to evaluate diagnostic tools, negative predictive value (NPV) is especially important in preoperative triage, as it directly reflects the proportion of patients with negative US findings who truly have no metastasis—an essential consideration when selecting candidates for omission of axillary surgery. Despite this relevance, few studies have examined the association between preoperative axillary US findings and long-term recurrence.

Therefore, in this study, we aimed to evaluate the role of preoperative axillary US in early-stage, clinically node-negative breast cancer, with an emphasis on its ability to predict nodal metastasis and long-term recurrence.

METHODS

Patient selection

This single-center retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Samsung Medical Center (IRB No. 2024-02-051-001), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Between January and December 2009, 943 patients diagnosed with T1 or T2 primary breast cancer and clinically node-negative status who underwent preoperative US and subsequent surgery were consecutively identified. Among them, 65 were excluded because of re-operation or recurrence (n = 20), bilateral breast cancer (n = 19), missing or incomplete data (n = 11), receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (n = 9), male sex (n = 5), or ongoing pregnancy (n = 1).

Clinically node-negative breast cancer was defined as the absence of clinically palpable LNs on physical examination at the time of diagnosis, as documented in electronic medical records. Finally, a total of 878 patients (mean age, 49 ± 9 years) with T1 or T2 primary breast cancer and clinically node-negative status were included in the study (Figure 1A).

US examination and analysis

Preoperative US was performed in all patients diagnosed with breast cancer as part of the initial staging workup, in accordance with the institutional standard clinical protocol at the time. The examination included bilateral breast and axillary evaluation, with axillary scanning covering levels I through III. One of five breast radiologists, each with 13–23 years of experience in breast imaging, conducted the US examinations. US findings were considered positive if at least one suspicious node was detected (US-positive), and negative if no suspicious nodes were identified (US-negative). In cases with borderline features, the classification was based on the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) category assigned in the original report: LNs assessed as BI-RADS 4 or higher were classified as positive, and those assessed as BI-RADS 3 were classified as negative. Suspicious findings suggestive of metastatic LN included focal nodular or eccentric cortical thickening (cortical thickness > 3 mm), rounded hypoechoic LNs (long-to-short axis ratio < 1.5), complete or partial effacement of the fatty hilum, and complete or partial replacement of LNs with an ill-defined or irregular mass [15,18,19,20]. At the time of US examination, MRI images were not available, and all axillary US assessments were performed solely based on real-time US findings without reference to MRI.

US data were collected and analyzed without retrospective reassessment by different readers. To promote consistency in the assessment of suspicious LNs, all breast radiologists regularly participated in weekly internal conferences where cases with axillary US findings suggestive of metastasis were reviewed and discussed. As part of ongoing quality assurance efforts, these sessions included educational feedback by correlating the initial US impressions with final pathology results, when available. This process was intended to refine interpretation skills and ensure uniform application of the diagnostic criteria, without influencing the classification used in this study.

Clinicopathologic data collection

Clinicopathologic data, including age, menopause status, family history, type of breast surgery (breast-conserving surgery vs. mastectomy), tumor histology (ductal vs. others), tumor stage (pathologic T1 vs. T2), tumor subtype, type of axillary surgery, number of metastatic LNs, size of metastasis (macrometastases, > 2 mm; micrometastases, ≤ 2 mm), pathologic lymph node (pN) stage (pN0, 1, 2, or 3), adjuvant chemotherapy, adjuvant radiotherapy, and endocrine therapy, were extracted from electronic medical records.

Tumor subtype was categorized as hormone receptor-positive (HR+)/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2−), HR+/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+), hormone receptor-negative (HR−)/HER2+, or HR−/HER2− (triple-negative). HR status was defined as positive if tumor cells showed estrogen receptor and/or progesterone receptor expression, with a cut-off of an Allred score ≥ 3 [21]. HER2 positivity was defined according to the 2007 American Society of Clinical Oncology/College of American Pathologists guidelines as immunohistochemistry (IHC) 3+ (uniform, intense membrane staining in > 30% of invasive tumor cells) or IHC 2+ with a HER2/chromosome enumeration probe 17 ratio > 2.2 by fluorescence in situ hybridization [22]. Axillary surgery was classified as SLNB or ALND. Patients who underwent both SLNB and ALND (i.e., ALND following SLNB) were categorized into the ALND group. The pN stage was classified based on the number of metastatic axillary LNs as follows: pN0 (no metastatic LNs), pN1 (1–3 nodes), pN2 (4–9 nodes), and pN3 (10 or more nodes).

The final axillary status was determined as positive when one or more metastatic axillary LNs were present based on pathological reports after surgery. Cases were divided into pN-positive (pN ≥ 1) and pN-negative (pN0) groups.

Recurrence surveillance

The follow-up period was assessed from the date of surgery. Follow-up data and recurrence status were extracted from electronic medical records. If there were no medical records of visits to any department at our institution for over one year, the date of the last visit was recorded as the end of follow-up, and the follow-up duration was calculated accordingly. Recurrence was defined as locoregional (including in-breast and regional involvement of the axilla, chest wall, internal mammary, or supraclavicular fossa LNs), contralateral cancer, or distant metastasis [23,24,25]. Combined recurrence was defined as the simultaneous occurrence of two or more of these categories (i.e., locoregional ± contralateral ± distant events). When recurrence was suspected during examination, a biopsy was performed whenever possible. However, in cases where tissue sampling was difficult or clinically challenging for the patient, the determination of recurrence status was based on a combination of various test results, including PET-CT by the attending physician. We last accessed the patients’ medical records on September 30, 2022.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics of the US-positive and US-negative groups were compared using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the independent t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. The diagnostic performance of preoperative axillary US in determining the final axillary status, based on pathological reports, was calculated using Wilson’s method [26]. The DeLong test was used to compare the area under the curve (AUC) among different tumor subtypes based on the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve [27]. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with recurrence, with the variables showing p < 0.05 in the univariable analysis being included in the multivariable model. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with recurrence, in conjunction with US findings.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, USA) and SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). Two-tailed p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

According to the preoperative US findings of the axilla, 234 patients (age, 47 ± 9 years) were classified into the US-positive group and 644 (age, 49 ± 9 years) into the US-negative group (Table 1). The median interval between preoperative US and surgery was 13 days (interquartile range [IQR], 8–22 days). Based on final pathology, 283 patients were pN-positive (pN ≥ 1) and 595 were pN-negative (pN0).

In the US-positive group (n = 234), a significantly higher incidence of mastectomy (31.2% [73/234] vs. 19.9% [128/644]), non-HR+/HER2− subtypes (49.6% [116/234] vs. 27.4% [177/644]), and pT2 stage (50.8% [119/234] vs. 25.3% [163/644]) were observed compared with the US-negative group (all p < 0.001; Table 1). For the axillary region, ALND was more frequently performed (62.8% [147/234] vs. 32.8% [211/644], p < 0.001). The mean number of metastatic axillary LNs (2.6 ± 4.8 vs. 0.5 ± 1.4), proportion of macrometastasis (94.2% [113/120] vs. 71.8% [117/163]), and proportion of patients with pN-positive disease (51.3% [120/234] vs. 25.3% [163/644]) were also significantly higher in the US-positive group (all p < 0.001). Notably, 25.3% (163/644) of patients in the US-negative group had nodal metastasis on final pathology, despite negative axillary US findings. This result indicated that preoperative US may not reliably exclude occult axillary metastasis. However, the mean number of metastatic LNs was significantly lower in the US-negative group than in the US-positive group (p < 0.001), suggesting a lower overall nodal burden.

In the pN-positive group (n = 283), the number of metastatic axillary LNs was significantly higher in the US-positive group (n = 120) compared to the US-negative group (n = 163) (5.0 ± 5.8 vs. 1.9 ± 2.2, p < 0.001; Table 3). Subgroup analysis by tumor subtype showed that this difference remained significant in all groups except for the triple-negative subtype (23 [3.8 ± 4.3] vs. 20 [3.1 ± 5.2], p = 0.08). Specifically, within the HR+/HER2− group (70 vs. 124), patients with US-positive findings had significantly more metastatic axillary LNs than those with US-negative findings (5.0 ± 5.6 vs. 1.8 ± 1.2, p < 0.001). Among other subtypes, the HR−/HER2+ subgroup did not show a statistically significant difference (p = 0.121); however, given the limited sample size (7 vs. 10), this result should be interpreted with caution.

In the diagnostic performance of preoperative axillary US, subgroup analysis revealed that sensitivity was highest in the HR+/HER2+ subtype (69.0%, 20/29), whereas specificity was highest in the HR+/HER2− subtype (87.7%, 343/391). The HR−/HER2+ subtype showed the highest NPV (82.1%, 46/56; 95% CI, 70.2–90.0) (Figure 1B). However, given the small sample size of this group, these results should be interpreted with caution. Diagnostic accuracy was also the highest in HR+ cases (HR+/HER2+, 70.7% [58/82]; HR+/HER2−, 70.6% [413/585]). In contrast, triple-negative cases demonstrated the lowest accuracy (59.2%, 77/130) and PPV (41.1%, 23/56). These diagnostic performance metrics were further confirmed by ROC analysis, with the AUC averaging 0.62 and reaching up to 0.70 (Figure 2A).

The median follow-up duration for the entire cohort from the date of surgery was 131.3 months (IQR, 82.3–153.6). The median follow-up durations were 143.9 months (IQR, 74.3–157.5) in patients with recurrence and 130.9 months (IQR, 83.2–152.7) in those without recurrence. During follow-up, 79 cases of recurrence (9.0%, 79/878) occurred, including 27 locoregional, 8 contralateral, 32 distant, and 12 combined recurrences (involving two or more categories). Cox regression analysis showed that pN stage was the only variable independently associated with recurrence in the multivariable model (p < 0.001). Tumor histology, subtype, T stage, adjuvant radiation therapy, adjuvant chemotherapy, and endocrine therapy were not significant factors (Table 4). The median time to recurrence was 54.2 months (IQR, 23.9–99.0). The 5-year recurrence-free survival (RFS) rate for the entire cohort was 98.4% (864/878; 95% CI, 97.3–99.1), and the 10-year rate was 95.4% (838/878; 95% CI, 93.6–96.7). When stratified by pN status and US findings, RFS differed significantly between the pN-positive/US-positive, pN-positive/US-negative, and pN-negative groups (log-rank p < 0.001; Figure 2B). The 5-year and 10-year RFS rates were 95.6% (115/120; 95% CI, 89.7–98.1) and 90.3% (108/120; 95% CI, 82.7–94.7) for pN-positive/US-positive, 96.8% (158/163; 95% CI, 92.6–98.7) and 92.4% (151/163; 95% CI, 86.7–95.7) for pN-positive/US-negative, and 99.5% (592/595; 95% CI, 98.4–99.8) and 97.4% (580/595; 95% CI, 95.4–98.5) for pN-negative, respectively.

DISCUSSION

In our study, the number and size of metastatic axillary LNs were significantly higher in the axillary US-positive group, particularly in the HR+/HER2− subtype. Previous studies have explored whether axillary US or MRI can predict the final axillary nodal disease burden [28,29,30,31,32,33,34]. Although we did not quantify the number of suspicious LNs on axillary US, our results qualitatively align with those of previous studies, indicating that a higher number of suspicious findings is associated with higher nodal disease burden. Specifically, the presence of at least two highly suspicious LNs, or a total of three nodes with high and intermediate suspicion, has been suggested to indicate pN2 or higher disease [35].

In patients with clinical T1–2 N0 breast cancer, axillary US findings were strongly associated with RFS. RFS was the poorest in patients with both axillary US and pathologic positivity, while those with axillary US-negative but pathologically positive nodes showed intermediate outcomes. These findings indicated that axillary US positivity may identify a subset of patients with more aggressive disease, even within the same nodal stage. These results are consistent with those of previous studies, suggesting that axillary US may offer prognostic information beyond its conventional role in staging. Notably, a 2016 pilot randomized controlled trial by Cyr et al. [36] demonstrated that axillary US had a negative predictive value of 96.9% for detecting clinically significant axillary metastases (> 2 mm). No axillary recurrences were observed in patients who did not undergo further axillary surgery after a negative axillary US, supporting its clinical utility in early-stage breast cancer. Our study also indicated that axillary US positivity may not merely reflect nodal involvement, but also the underlying tumor biology associated with higher recurrence risk.

Further supporting this interpretation, our results showed that patients with pathological nodal metastases but negative axillary US findings had a significantly better RFS than those with both axillary US positivity and pathological metastases. This finding suggests that axillary US positivity may reflect not only the presence of nodal involvement, but also more aggressive tumor biology, thereby providing additional prognostic information beyond conventional N staging. In line with our findings, Keelan et al. [37] reported that among axillary US-negative patients, 4.2% had high nodal burden (≥ 3 positive LNs), which was significantly associated with T2 tumors and lymphovascular invasion. Although axillary US cannot completely exclude substantial nodal disease, our study further suggested that US positivity is prognostically meaningful for identifying patients at a higher risk of recurrence.

Our findings can also be contextualized alongside those of the SOUND trial, a multicenter randomized controlled study that demonstrated the non-inferiority of omitting SLNB in patients with early-stage breast cancer and negative axillary US findings. In that study, the 5-year distant disease-free survival rate was 95.3% in the no-SLNB group and 96.3% in the SLNB group, with no significant difference in axillary recurrence rates [38]. Although our study included a broader real-world population and did not randomize patients for axillary surgery, we found that patients with negative axillary US had low recurrence rates, supporting the prognostic utility of axillary US. Importantly, even among patients who were ultimately found to have SLN metastases, those with negative axillary US findings showed significantly better RFS than those with axillary US positivity. This reinforces the notion that axillary US may not only serve as a staging tool but also help stratify recurrence risk.

Although 25.3% (163/644) of the axillary US-negative patients in our study were ultimately found to have nodal metastases (pN-positive), they had significantly lower nodal burden and better RFS than those with both positive axillary US and pathological findings. These results aligned with those of previous studies indicating that false-negative axillary US cases tend to involve small-volume disease and should not diminish the clinical value of axillary US as a non-invasive and prognostically meaningful tool [15,18,39,40].

Our findings were also in line with long-term population-based data showing that nodal positivity at diagnosis remains a strong predictor of recurrence, even after 10 years. In a cohort of over 20,000 disease-free breast cancer survivors followed for 32 years, LN-positive disease was independently associated with a significantly higher risk of late recurrence [41]. This reinforces the importance of accurately identifying nodal involvement and supports the notion that axillary US findings may reflect the underlying tumor biology associated with long-term recurrence risk.

There are a few possible explanations for why axillary US-positive pN0 patients have worse outcomes despite the absence of pathologically confirmed LN metastasis. Some axillary US-positive cases might have had undetected non-SLN metastases that were not removed during SLNB. However, this hypothesis does not fully align with the ACOSOG Z0011 criteria [4], in which disease-free survival was not significantly different between patients who underwent ALND and those treated with SLND alone without further axillary surgery. Another possible explanation is that axillary US-positive LN characteristics, such as cortical thickening and loss of fatty hilum, may indicate more aggressive tumor behavior, independent of the actual nodal burden. These factors collectively suggest that preoperative axillary US provides more than just a staging tool—it may also offer prognostic insights that could refine risk stratification and guide individualized management strategies. However, our dataset did not include detailed information on the extent of axillary dissection performed in the US-positive/pN0 subgroup or specific sonographic features (e.g., nodal morphology or size) associated with recurrence in this group. Therefore, we were unable to directly test these hypotheses, and readers should consider this limitation when interpreting our findings. Nonetheless, some supportive insights can be drawn from our data. In our cohort, although ALND was more frequently performed in the axillary US-positive group than in the US-negative group, our multivariable analysis did not identify ALND as an independent predictor of recurrence. This finding suggests that the higher recurrence rate observed in the axillary US-positive group is unlikely to be explained solely by the type of axillary surgery. Nevertheless, the potential for confounding factors must be considered when interpreting these findings.

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, retrospective study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. To address this limitation, prospective validation in a larger, multi-institutional cohort would be beneficial, strengthening our conclusions. Second, the axillary US assessment was performed by multiple radiologists, which may have introduced inter-reader variability. Although we used standardized criteria for interpretation, variations in experience and subjective judgment may have influenced the classification of LNs. A future study incorporating blinded image review or consensus reading could help clarify the robustness of axillary US interpretations and improve standardization. Third, some tumor subtype subgroups, particularly HR−/HER2+ and triple-negative breast cancer, included relatively small numbers of patients. While significant differences in nodal burden were observed in the HR+/HER2− subgroup, the statistical power may have been insufficient in the smaller subgroups to detect meaningful associations. Therefore, subgroup-specific results should be interpreted with caution within the context of our sample size. Finally, our survival analysis categorized patients as either pN-positive or pN-negative, without further stratification by N stage. Although a higher N stage is known to correlate with worse clinical outcomes, our cohort included a substantial proportion of pN-negative cases (68%), limiting a more detailed analysis of nodal burden and prognosis.

In conclusion, axillary US findings were significantly associated with RFS, even after accounting for pathological nodal status. US-positive patients had a higher nodal burden and worse outcomes, suggesting that axillary US may reflect underlying tumor biology. Although US-negative patients had lower burden, a notable proportion had occult metastases, highlighting the limitations of imaging alone. When combined with pathology, axillary US can provide complementary prognostic information and contribute to recurrence risk assessment in early-stage breast cancer.
