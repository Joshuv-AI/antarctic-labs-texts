Effect of intraoperative hypothermia on postoperative pulmonary complications in elderly off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Guang Feng, Chenfei Li, Yitong Jia, Guanxu Zhao, Tianlong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIntraoperative hypothermia has significant effects on physiological functions such as anti-infection and coagulation, but its association with specific postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) still remains unclear. The objective of this study is to explore the association between intraoperative hypothermia and PPCs.MethodsThis study retrospectively analyzed 311 consecutive elderly patients who received off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (OPCABG) at Xuanwu Hospital between January 2019 and April 2024. Baseline characteristics, intraoperative data, anesthesia records, perioperative laboratory parameters and post-surgery outcomes were gathered. Univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression were performed to assess the association between intraoperative hypothermia and the occurrence of PPCs.ResultsAmong the 311 patients, 134 experienced intraoperative hypothermia, defined as the intraoperative core temperature < 36.0 °C lasting for more than 5 min, with the incidence rate of 43.1%. Compared with the non-hypothermia group, the hypothermia group showed significantly higher incidence of postoperative pleural effusion and respiratory infection. Further multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that intraoperative hypothermia was an independent risk factor for postoperative pleural effusion (OR: 2.725, 95% CI: 1.656 ~ 4.484, P < 0.001) and respiratory infection (OR: 2.342, 95% CI: 1.336 ~ 4.105, P = 0.003).ConclusionIntraoperative hypothermia is significantly associated with the increased risk of postoperative pleural effusion and respiratory infection in elderly patients undergoing OPCABG. This finding highlights the important role of strict intraoperative temperature management in reducing the incidence of these two PPCs. Multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate the causal relationship and explore optimal temperature management strategies.

Introduction

Perioperative temperature management is of vital importance in ensuring patient safety and improving postoperative outcomes [1, 2]. Although body temperature has been recognized as one of the four core vital signs, alongside pulse, respiration and blood pressure, the incidence of inadvertent intraoperative hypothermia (core body temperature < 36.0 °C) remains high in clinical practice, particularly among patients undergoing major surgeries, with rates reaching 6% − 90% [3, 4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that intraoperative hypothermia induces multi-systemic adverse effects [2, 5]. It not only suppresses myocardial contractility, prolongs clotting time leading to increased bleeding and elevates the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, but also impairs the phagocytic function of neutrophils, thereby compromising the immune defense capacity and increasing the risk of postoperative infections [5, 6].

For severe and complex cases of coronary artery disease, the established surgical treatment is coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [7, 8]. Currently, off-pump CABG (OPCABG) has emerged as the preferred choice in many centers due to its prominent advantages in safety and postoperative recovery [9, 10]. Elderly patients constitute the primary population undergoing this surgery [11, 12]. Due to age-related declines in thermoregulatory center function, reduced subcutaneous fat, inadequate heat production and rapid heat dissipation, coupled with prolonged surgical duration and extended thoracic exposure, the risk of intraoperative hypothermia in elderly OPCABG patients is further increased [13, 14].

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) constitute the most frequent complication among patients undergoing OPCABG, significantly affecting patient prognosis and representing one of the most challenging issues in postoperative management for clinicians. PPCs encompass a range of adverse respiratory events, including postoperative respiratory infections, atelectasis, pleural effusion and respiratory failure etc. Clinical evidence indicates that the incidence of PPCs in elderly patients undergoing CABG can reach 11% − 40% [15–17]. PPCs not only lead to prolonged mechanical ventilation time, extended postoperative hospitalization and elevated mortality rates, but also significantly increase medical expenses, imposing the substantial burden on patients’ families and society [18, 19].

Previous studies have indicated that intraoperative hypothermia is associated with complications such as coagulation dysfunction and surgical site infection [20, 21]. Recent study has suggested the potential association between intraoperative hypothermia and pulmonary infection in non-cardiac major surgeries [22]. However, the relationship between intraoperative hypothermia and PPCs still remains unclear, especially regarding the impact of hypothermia on PPCs in elderly patients, which has not been reported yet. Based on this, the present study aims to investigate the effects of intraoperative hypothermia on PPCs in elderly patients undergoing OPCABG, providing evidence-based support for the development of targeted temperature management strategies and the reduction of PPC risks in clinical practice.

Methods

Participants and ethics

The study retrospectively analyzed consecutive elderly patients (aged ≥ 65 years) who underwent OPCABG at Xuanwu Hospital between January 2019 and April 2024. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: concomitant OPCABG and cardiac valve surgery; use of cardiopulmonary bypass during the procedure; severe preoperative pulmonary disease meeting the diagnostic criteria for PPCs; history of thoracic surgery within 30 days before the OPCABG; presence of thoracic deformities; or lack of key clinical data required for analysis. This study was based on the retrospective database established in our previously published research, which focused on PPCs of CABG surgery in elderly patients [15].

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital (2024-277-001), guaranteeing that all study protocols comply with ethical standards and legal regulations. Due to the retrospective design of this study, the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital has waived the need for patient informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

By reviewing the electronic medical records system and the anesthesia information management system, we systematically collected clinical data. These include general patient demographics and characteristics, preoperative comorbidities and other relevant baseline data. Additionally, we collected surgical- and anesthesia-related data, encompassing surgical procedure type, anesthesia method, surgical duration, intraoperative fluid administration volume, blood transfusion volume and other related parameters. Moreover, we documented relevant vital sign measurements, laboratory findings, examination results and other perioperative clinical data. The preoperative P/F ratio is defined as PaO₂/FiO₂, obtained from preoperative arterial blood gas analysis. The operation-end albumin level was defined as the plasma albumin concentration measured shortly after surgery. Basal metabolic rate (BMR) was calculated for all patients using the 1984 revised Harris-Benedict equation [23].

Intraoperative temperature management and hypothermia definition

In this study, the operating room temperature was constantly maintained at 21–23℃. All patients received standardized active thermal insulation measures, including intraoperative application of the warming blanket to cover the lower extremities and trunk, combined with whole-body warming via the forced-air warmer when clinically indicated, for the prevention of intraoperative hypothermia. Using the temperature probe connected to the anesthesia monitor (Philips MP70), core body temperature was tracked continuously; medical staff place the probe in either the nasopharynx or oropharynx, with readings documented every 5 min intraoperatively. The operational criterion for intraoperative hypothermia was a sustained drop in core body temperature below 36.0 °C for longer than five minutes. The severity of intraoperative hypothermia is classified into three grades: mild hypothermia (34.0 °C ≤ core body temperature < 36.0 °C), moderate hypothermia (32.0 ≤ core body temperature < 34.0 °C) and severe hypothermia (core body temperature < 32.0 °C).

Outcome definition

The primary outcome measure of this study was the occurrence of PPCs within 7 days post-surgery, with their definitions and determinations adhering to the European Perioperative Clinical Outcome (EPCO) criteria—co-established by ESICM and ESA [24]. This set of criteria is the internationally recognized standard for standardizing the evaluation of PPCs. Specifically, this composite outcome includes postoperative pleural effusion, respiratory infection, atelectasis, pneumothorax, bronchospasm, respiratory failure and aspiration pneumonia. In this study, two independent reviewers assessed the presence of PPCs in all enrolled patients by carefully examining relevant diagnostic tests, laboratory results and medical documentation within the electronic medical record system, in strict accordance with the EPCO criteria. When discrepancies arose in their evaluations, the reviewers discussed to reach a consensus. If no resolution was achieved through discussion alone, they could consult the extended review team for further guidance.

The secondary outcomes were assessed, including the postoperative mortality rate (defined as any in-hospital death occurring between the day of surgery and discharge) and the length of hospital stay (LOS, calculated as the number of days from surgery completion to discharge).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was carried out using SPSS software (version 23.0). Continuous variables were expressed either as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were described in terms of frequencies and percentages. For continuous variables, differences between groups were evaluated using either Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, depending on the circumstances. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Variables that demonstrated statistical significance in univariate analyses were subsequently subjected to multivariate logistic regression analysis for further assessment.

The post-hoc power analysis was performed to confirm the adequacy of the sample size for the primary and secondary focused outcomes. For the primary focused outcome of postoperative pleural effusion, with observed incidences of 25.4% (45/177) in the non-hypothermia group and 49.3% (66/134) in the hypothermia group, a two-sided α of 0.05 and power of 80%, the minimum required total sample size was calculated to be 197 patients. Our final cohort of 311 patients exceeded this requirement, and the actual statistical power was calculated to be > 99.8%, ensuring sufficient statistical power. A similar post-hoc analysis verified that the sample size was also adequate for the secondary focused outcome of postoperative respiratory infection with the actual statistical power of > 90%.

Results

This study included 311 elderly patients undergoing OPCABG, and their ages spanned from 65 to 86 years (Fig. 1). Based on intraoperative core body temperature monitoring, 134 of these patients developed intraoperative hypothermia, yielding an incidence of 43.1% (134/311). The remaining 177 patients maintained normothermia intraoperatively without hypothermic events (Table 1).

The initial investigation was performed to clarify the association between intraoperative hypothermia and PPCs, whereby patients were categorized into either the hypothermia group or the non-hypothermia group based on whether intraoperative hypothermia occurred during their procedure. Intergroup comparisons of PPC incidence were subsequently carried out, and full results are presented in Table 3. Distinct variations in PPC occurrence were noted between the two groups. Specifically, the incidence of pleural effusion was 49.3% (66/134) in the hypothermia group and 25.4% (45/177) in the non-hypothermia group, showing a significant difference between the two groups (P < 0.001). The incidence of respiratory infection was 32.8% (44/134) in the hypothermia group and 17.5% (31/177) in the non-hypothermia group, also demonstrating the statistically significant intergroup difference (P = 0.002). For the remaining PPCs (atelectasis, pneumothorax, respiratory failure and bronchospasm), the incidence did not differ statistically significantly between the two groups.

To further clarify the relationship between intraoperative hypothermia and postoperative pleural effusion, this study divided the 311 included patients into the non-pleural effusion group (n = 200) and the pleural effusion group (n = 111) based on the presence or absence of postoperative pleural effusion. Initially, univariate analysis was performed, revealing that six variables (smoking, hypertension, P/F ratio, minimally invasive CABG surgery, operation-end albumin and intraoperative hypothermia) differed significantly between the two groups (Table 5). These six variables were subsequently incorporated into the multivariate logistic regression analysis, with corresponding results detailed in Table 6. The analysis revealed that intraoperative hypothermia constituted an independent risk factor for the development of postoperative pleural effusion (P < 0.001, OR: 2.725, 95% CI: 1.656 ~ 4.484). Additionally, operation-end albumin and P/F ratio were also identified as independent risk factors for postoperative pleural effusion (Table 6).

The same analytical approach was employed to investigate the association between intraoperative hypothermia and postoperative respiratory infection. Based on whether the 311 patients developed postoperative respiratory infection, they were categorized into the non-respiratory infection group (n = 236) and the respiratory infection group (n = 75) for univariate analysis (Table 7). The results revealed that seven variables (age, P/F ratio, NYHA classification, chronic bronchitis, ASA classification, COPD and intraoperative hypothermia) demonstrated statistically significant differences between the two groups. As Table 8 shows, after further incorporating these seven variables into the multivariate logistic regression analysis, the results showed that intraoperative hypothermia was an independent risk factor for the occurrence of postoperative respiratory infection (P = 0.003, OR: 2.342, 95% CI: 1.336 ~ 4.105). Additionally, P/F ratio was also identified as an independent risk factor for postoperative respiratory infection.

Discussion

This retrospective study included 311 elderly OPCABG patients to assess the effect of intraoperative hypothermia on PPCs. The results showed that those with intraoperative hypothermia had a notably longer postoperative LOS compared to the non-hypothermia patients. Moreover, the hypothermia group had significantly higher rates of pleural effusion and respiratory infection postoperatively. Further analysis confirmed intraoperative hypothermia as an independent risk factor for postoperative pleural effusion and respiratory infection in elderly OPCABG patients.

Despite the routine implementation of perioperative warming measures in our hospital, such as the use of warming blankets or forced-air warmers and the pre-warming of all intraoperative fluids in heating cabinets, more than 40% of patients in this study still developed intraoperative hypothermia during the surgical procedure. At the beginning of OPCABG surgery, patients need to undergo extensive cutaneous disinfection, covering areas from the neck to the chest, abdomen and lower limbs. This process involves a large exposed area and the evaporation of the disinfectant accelerates body surface heat loss, serving as the initial trigger for the decline in body temperature. Meanwhile, OPCABG involves full thoracotomy and the prolonged exposure of the thoracic cavity to room temperature directly results in rapid heat dissipation from the thoracic cavity. Furthermore, the average duration of OPCABG surgery exceeds three hours. During such a prolonged procedure, the body’s basal metabolic heat production is insufficient to offset continuous heat loss [25]. Additionally, elderly patients exhibit the lower basal metabolic rate and general anesthesia further inhibits the body’s thermoregulatory function. The combination of these factors is likely to be the underlying cause of the high incidence of intraoperative hypothermia in OPCABG surgeries. Given the results of this study, the current intraoperative warming measures for large surgeries such as OPCABG are still insufficient. It is highly necessary to adopt more effective perioperative warming measures.

Previous studies have investigated how intraoperative hypothermia affects postoperative complications and clinical outcomes, specifically encompassing its correlation with surgical site infections and postoperative deep vein thrombosis, as well as its impacts on anesthetic recovery, postoperative intestinal function and postoperative cognitive function etc. [26–28]. However, systematic research focusing on the relationship between intraoperative hypothermia and PPCs has not been reported. PPCs refer to a series of postoperative pulmonary complications in general, and their pathogenesis varies significantly. The etiologies and pathophysiological mechanisms vary among different types of PPCs, and there are complex interactions and associations among various types of PPCs. Many previous studies have treated PPCs as a single entity, exploring their common risk factors and intervention strategies, making it difficult to accurately reveal the specific impact of individual risk factors on different types of PPCs. This study focuses on intraoperative hypothermia, a critical perioperative factor, and conducts the detailed analysis of its independent effects on six common types of PPCs, aiming to more precisely elucidate the association mechanisms between intraoperative hypothermia and various types of PPCs.

This study found that intraoperative hypothermia significantly increases the risk of postoperative pleural effusion in elderly patients undergoing OPCABG surgery. Previous studies have confirmed that intraoperative hypothermia impairs multiple physiological functions [29, 30]. Core temperature < 36.0 °C impairs coagulation by reducing platelet activity and coagulation factor efficacy [31], a risk amplified in elderly patients with limited coagulation reserve [13]. Hypothermia also disrupts pulmonary homeostasis through sympathetic activation-induced arteriole constriction and reduced alveolar surfactant secretion [31–33], while OPCABG-related pleural-pulmonary trauma elevates capillary permeability. Moreover, hypothermia slows tissue metabolism and impairs pleural mesothelial cell function, reducing lymphatic exudate clearance [34]. Collectively, these factors exert a combined effect and increase the risk of postoperative pleural effusion in the elderly vulnerable population undergoing OPCABG surgery through multiple synergistic mechanisms.

In this study, the incidence of postoperative pleural effusion in elderly patients reached 35.7% (111/311), which was the highest among all PPCs. Patients undergoing OPCABG in this study underwent routine chest X-ray examination on the first postoperative day, with dynamic re-evaluation or escalation to chest CT scans based on clinical conditions. This screening protocol enables the identification of early asymptomatic pleural effusion that is prone to be missed by symptom-driven screening. some patients with effusion required only observation or symptomatic treatment, while others with larger effusions necessitated interventions such as closed thoracic drainage. Although the clinical symptoms of some pleural effusion cases are not prominent, they still merit the attention of clinicians when managing vulnerable elderly patients, to avoid adverse effects on the recovery of patients’ postoperative cardiopulmonary function.

The prior study showed the association between intraoperative hypothermia and the increased risk of surgical site infection [22]. However, recent meta-analyses have indicated that uncertainty remains regarding the relationship between intraoperative hypothermia and postoperative surgical site infection [35]. This study found that intraoperative hypothermia significantly increases the risk of postoperative respiratory infection in elderly patients receiving OPCABG. From the perspective of immune mechanisms, when core body temperature decreases, the phagocytic activity of neutrophils declines, and the efficiency of macrophages in clearing bacteria is reduced, making it easier for colonizing bacteria in the respiratory tract to breach the immune barrier and trigger infection [36]. Meanwhile, hypothermia markedly reduces the ciliary beat frequency of the airway mucosa, weakening the ability to expectorate sputum. Sputum retention in the alveoli and bronchi provides the favorable environment for bacterial proliferation [34]. Additionally, elderly patients inherently have physiological functional degenerative changes such as decreased pulmonary functional reserve and impaired immune function [37]. Hypothermia further exacerbates lung tissue damage by inhibiting the synthesis of pulmonary surfactant and interfering with the ventilation/perfusion ratio, ultimately leading to the significantly elevated risk of postoperative respiratory infection.

Previous studies have indicated that the selection of different anesthesia methods may potentially affect the risk of PPCs, but the conclusions remain controversial. Zhou et al. found that sevoflurane inhalation anesthesia reduced the incidence of PPCs compared with propofol-based total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) in patients undergoing head and neck cancer surgery [38]. Conversely, Chang et al. demonstrated that propofol-based TIVA was more effective than inhalation anesthesia in reducing PPCs during head and neck cancer microvascular reconstruction [39]. In the present study, TIVA and combined intravenous-inhalation anesthesia were included in univariate analysis, and the results showed that different anesthesia methods were not significantly associated with the risk of postoperative pleural effusion and pulmonary infection, which was inconsistent with the findings of the two aforementioned studies. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in surgical types and patient population characteristics among the study cohorts. Furthermore, as this was a retrospective study, there were individual variations in the types, doses and administration routes of anesthetics used for each patient, which may also have contributed to the lack of statistical significance in the effect of anesthesia methods on PPCs. Therefore, the specific impact of anesthetic selection and anesthesia protocol optimization on PPCs still needs to be further verified by large-sample, multi-center prospective controlled studies.

Moreover, the results also demonstrated that the preoperative P/F ratio < 350 mmHg was an independent risk factor for both postoperative pleural effusion and respiratory infections. As an evaluative parameter derived from arterial blood gas analysis, a decrease in the preoperative P/F ratio can serve as an early warning sign of potential lung injury and may exhibit favorable predictive performance for PPCs based on the findings of this study. This finding helps clinicians identify high-risk patients for PPCs at an early stage and implement effective preventive and interventional measures in a timely manner.

This study still has some limitations. Firstly, as a single-center retrospective study, potential selection bias in patient enrollment may exist, and the demographic characteristics and perioperative management strategies specific to a single center could limit the generalizability of the findings. Secondly, retrospective data collection from medical records may introduce information bias due to inconsistencies in the completeness and accuracy of medical documentation, and some potential confounding factors (e.g., preoperative nutritional status, details of postoperative temperature management, postoperative ventilation duration) have not been fully captured or included in the analysis due to incomplete medical records. Thirdly, while this study confirmed the association of intraoperative hypothermia with postoperative pleural effusion and respiratory infection as well as its role as an independent risk factor, the specific mechanisms underlying this association were not explored in depth. Additionally, the sample size of this study remains relatively small. In subsequent research, multicenter prospective investigations are warranted to further confirm the causal relationship and evaluate the efficacy of interventions.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that intraoperative hypothermia is an independent risk factor for postoperative pleural effusion and respiratory infection in elderly patients undergoing OPCABG and is associated with the increased risk of these two PPCs. These findings provide important evidence for further optimizing temperature management strategies in elderly patients to reduce the risk of PPCs. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to further validate the causal relationship between intraoperative hypothermia and PPCs and to explore effective temperature management strategies to improve patient outcomes.
