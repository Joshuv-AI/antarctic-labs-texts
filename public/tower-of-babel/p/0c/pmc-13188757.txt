Interpretative diagnostic model for neuroblastoma metastases using bone marrow cytology
Authors: Juan Ma, Qiang Yao, Xiaoying Fu, Zhouqi Xia, Yaoting Yue, Dongqing Xu, Xiaojun Yuan, Liebin Zhao, Jinhu Wang, Ao Dong, Limei Gao, Junyao Yang, Lisong Shen, Yingxia Zheng, Shaoqing Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone marrow (BM) is the most common site of metastatic disease at diagnosis and a frequent site of relapse in neuroblastoma. Digital cytology images of BM smears offer a rich data source for artificial intelligence models, which may potentially facilitate more cost-effective risk stratification within the diagnostic workflow. This study aims to develop an interpretable cytology model for detecting BM metastasis in pediatric neuroblastoma.MethodsThis retrospective diagnostic study used Wright-Giemsa–stained BM cytology images from 359 neuroblastoma patients who underwent BM screening between January 2019 and June 2024 across multiple centers in China. After the quality evaluation, we generated 1384,007 patches from BM digital cytology to develop and validate the cytology model. In the model construction, we integrated a multiple-instance learning framework with convolutional neural networks to extract cytology features, referred to as cMIL. The cytology model was trained for BM metastasis detection and risk stratification with interpretability.ResultsFor metastasis detection, the cytology model achieved an AUC of 0.924 (95% CI, 0.775–1.000) in the training cohort. Performance remained strong in external validation, with AUCs of 0.826 (95% CI, 0.741–0.911) in Cohort A and 0.795 (95% CI, 0.684–0.906) in Cohort B, indicating consistent performance across independent multicenter cohorts. The cMIL score also successfully stratified patients in terms of survival outcomes (log-rank p < 0.05). Interpretability analyses further demonstrated that the model’s predictions were associated with clinically relevant cytological features.ConclusionsIn this retrospective diagnostic study, the developed cytology model demonstrated high discriminative performance in detecting BM metastasis and captured the underlying complexity and heterogeneity of BM. These findings suggest that the cytology model could serve as a promising tool for improving metastasis detection and risk stratification in patients with neuroblastoma, potentially contributing to personalized treatment strategies and enhanced disease monitoring.Trial registrationThis retrospective study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06703944) on November 21, 2024. Introduction

Neuroblastoma is the most common extracranial solid tumor in children [1], characterized by heterogeneous disease onset and distant metastases at the time of diagnosis [2, 3], accounting for approximately 15% of all pediatric cancer-related mortality [4]. The prognosis for individuals with neuroblastoma varies significantly based on their risk categorization with reported 5-year overall survival (OS) rate ranging from 20% to 70% [5, 6]. There remains a significant challenge for those with high-risk neuroblastoma in developing countries, with survival rate of 20–50% [7]. As a vital niche for hematopoietic and immune cell development, the bone marrow (BM) facilitates the dissemination of neuroblastoma and recognized as the most frequent metastatic site at diagnosis and/or relapse, especially for high-risk patient groups [7–9]. Consequently, the BM evaluation is essential for the early detection, dynamic monitoring, and prognosis prediction of neuroblastoma. BM aspirate consists of particles of BM tissue smeared onto a glass slide, allowing individual cell classification in a process known as cytology [10]. The characteristic neuroblastoma cells, which resemble small round cells arranged in nests or rosettes can often be identified in BM cytology.

In patients with clinical suspicion of advanced cancer, BM smears are valuable resources that have the potential to help rule out metastasis, thereby potentially reducing the need for additional diagnostic procedures. This is particularly important when obtaining a pathological biopsy is difficult in pediatric patients. However, identifying metastatic neuroblastoma cells on BM smears is technically challenging using conventional microscopic screening, which often exhibit inter- and intra-observer variability among specialists [11]. Digital BM cytology images, providing a rich data source for deep learning (DL) models, which can be utilized to identify tumor cells or tumor clusters and generate precise risk stratification criteria.

In medical practice of neuroblastoma, the integration of AI technologies including pathology diagnostics, cancer staging, gene profiling, and diagnostic prediction, almost employed to analyze hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained histopathological whole-slide images (WSIs) [12–18], could streamline workflows, improve diagnostic efficiency, and support medical decision-making in a cost-effective manner [19–21]. More recently, a DL model using attention-based multiple instance learning (aMIL) and self-supervised learning was developed to perform pathologic classification of neuroblastoma and assess MYCN amplification status [13]. While these computational pathology workflow support tools have been developed for histopathology analysis on H&E-stained samples, the inherent complexity of BM aspirate specimens has limited the application from a computer vision perspective [22–24]. Additionally, the persistent challenge of deep learning in clinical practice focuses on the model interpretability.

To address these challenges and improve the efficiency of metastasis detection in BM cytology, we adopted a deep learning-based image analysis to conduct cytology model for neuroblastoma with BM metastases. In this retrospective study, we integrated a weakly-supervised MIL model with CNNs architecture to extract features from BM cytological WSIs, referred to as cMIL, incorporates a crucial quality supervision process that selects qualified patches from BM cytological images before feature extraction, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the downstream analysis. To evaluate interpretability and clinical utility, we assessed cMIL performance in both metastasis detection and the identification of clinically relevant cytological features. Validation on multicenter retrospective datasets demonstrated promising performance of the cytology model in detecting BM metastasis and supporting risk stratification in neuroblastoma.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital (XHEC-C-2024-023-2). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This multicenter study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06703944) on November 21, 2024. This study adhered to the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) reporting guideline.

The flowchart of patient enrollment is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Inclusion criteria were newly diagnosed neuroblastoma completed with BM cytology evaluation.

BM metastasis was defined using a composite clinical reference standard, whereby a positive result from any diagnostic modality—BM biopsy, BM cytology, flow cytometry, or positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT)—was sufficient for classification. This approach reflects routine clinical practice and was intentionally adopted to prioritize sensitivity. BM cytology was the primary source of whole-slide images for model training due to its cell-level morphological resolution. BM biopsy and flow cytometry complemented cytology by detecting focal or low-burden metastatic disease, while PET/CT provided supportive whole-body evidence at the patient level. In cases of discordant findings, a sensitivity-first rule was used, with any single positive modality determining metastatic status. This strategy was applied uniformly across datasets to reduce false-negative labeling.

Exclusion criteria included previously received chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy, presence of other malignant neoplasms, absence information of BM metastasis status, and without representative cytology image. Finally, a total of 359 pediatric patients with neuroblastoma from 3 independent hospitals were included in this retrospective study. The internal cohort consisted of participants from Xinhua Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (n = 169) were randomly divided into a training set, comprising 80% (n = 135) of the dataset, and the remaining 20% (n = 34) as internal validation set. The independent cohorts included participants from Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (cohort A, n = 115) and Shenzhen Children’s Hospital (cohort B, n = 75) as external testing cohorts.

We collected clinical data for enrolled participants at the time of their initial hospital admission before they received any treatment. The clinical information collected included demographic characteristics (age, sex), disease characteristics (primary tumor location, tumor grade, BM metastasis status, MYCN amplification status), laboratory variables (hematological parameters of peripheral blood and BM, and serum concentrations of lactate dehydrogenase [LDH] and neuron-specific enolase [NSE]) and survival time. The BM evaluation via Wright-Giemsa–stained BM smears met the requirements set by International Committee for Standardization in Hematology (ICSH). We scanned the BM cytological image at 40× magnification using a digital slide scanner (UNIC PRECICE 500, Beijing Unci Technology Co., Ltd., China) and segment whole slide images (WSI) into non-overlapping 512 × 512 pixel patches. The Vahadane method was applied to normalize the color of these patches. During cell classification and metastasis detection, all cytology slides were independently reviewed by two experienced hematopathologists using a double-blind protocol to minimize observer-related variability. Typically, metastatic neuroblastoma clusters in the BM were characterized by aggregated, round, atypical cells with a high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio. Clinical data were collected between January 2019 and June 2024, with the follow-up period ending on June 30, 2024.

Construction and validation of diagnostic cytology model for BM metastasis in neuroblastoma

In the diagnostic cytology model, we employed a multi-task learning strategy that combined the filtered patches from the quality supervision module with features extracted by the signature extraction module to perform WSI-level diagnosis of BM metastasis. The workflow of artificial intelligence-based cytology analysis is shown in Fig. 1.

The workflow of artificial intelligence-based cytology analysis. (a) Data preprocessing workflow for model training: Wright-Giemsa-stained BM smears were collected and digital whole slide scanning. The WSI were divided into non-overlapping 512 × 512-pixel patches and color-normalized patches. (b) The architecture of our designed cytological-based multiple instances learning models (cMIL). This model employed a multi-task learning strategy that integrated the filtered patches from the quality supervision module with features extracted by the signature extraction module. For patch-level predictions, a convolutional neural network (CNN) with a ResNet50 architecture was employed to extract BM cytological features and generate label predictions. For WSI-level predictions, a weakly-supervised multiple instance learning (MIL) algorithm to aggregate dispersed patch-level features by combining Patch Likelihood Histogram (PLH) pipeline with the Bag of Words (BoW) pipeline to represented the collective BM cytological information of patient. The final WSI-level prediction served as the representations of the participant for subsequent analytical operations. (c) Model construction and evaluation. For the BM cytology diagnosis, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) algorithm to predict the metastasis state of BM. For the prognosis prediction, a prognostic model to make individualized predictions of survival outcomes based on BM cytological features. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with a log-rank test was performed to compare OS. The performance and effectiveness of models were assessed on independent external cohorts

Quality supervision: we proposed a quality supervision module to evaluate the quality of BM digital cytological images before feature extraction, which was pre-trained on both high-quality and low-quality patches of BM cytological images, resulting in a dataset of 10,000 patches (Supplementary Fig. 2). Based on the evaluation results, we generated 1384,007 patches from three cohorts through image process to construct model.

Feature extraction: we applied a weakly-supervised MIL model with features extracted by CNN algorithms, which aggregate dispersed patch-level features to WSI- level features for predicted labels and probabilities. For patch-level predictions, we employed a residual network 50 layers (ResNet50) architecture to extract BM cytological features and generate label predictions along with their respective probabilities. For WSI-level predictions, we combined the Patch Likelihood Histogram (PLH) and Bag of Words (BoW) pipelines to represent the collective BM cytological information from a patient’s BM aspirate specimen.

Model evaluation: During the feature selection process, we employed the LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) method to screen cytology features and identify the final WSI-level features for subsequent analytical operations. The selected cytology features were then used to develop cytology model using multilayer perceptron (MLP) algorithms. We employed the area under the curve (AUC) as the primary performance metric and evaluated the model’s sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, F1 score, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) across various classification thresholds. Furthermore, confusion metrics and decision curve analysis (DCA) were performed to evaluate the reliability and clinical utility of the model.

The prognostic value of cytology model in neuroblastoma

To evaluate the prognostic value of the cytology model, patients from the internal cohort were used as the training dataset (n = 163) and cohorts A served as an independent validation dataset (n = 114). The optimal cutoff value of the predicted cMIL score for risk stratification was determined using X-tile. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed to assess the prognostic significance of the cMIL score and to compare overall survival (OS) between patients stratified into high- and low-risk groups. OS was defined as the time from neuroblastoma diagnosis to death from any cause or to the last follow-up for censored patients. To assess the independent prognostic value of the cMIL score, univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed. The multivariable model incorporated established prognostic covariates, including age at diagnosis, MYCN amplification status, tumor grade, and BM metastasis status.

Model interpretability

Model interpretability was assessed using Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) to visualize prediction heatmaps and probability distributions. Furthermore, correlation analyses were conducted between cytological features generated by cMIL and morphological indicators of hematopoietic and immune cells observed on BM slides to elucidate the biological basis of model predictions.

Statistics

Clinical characteristics of participants were compared using independent sample t-tests for continuous variables and chi-squared (χ²) test for categorical variables. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For WSI-level diagnosis of BM metastasis, data preprocessing and model development were conducted using Python (version 3.7.12) and the deep learning platform Anaconda (version 3). Python packages used in the analysis included Pandas v.1.2.4, NumPy v.1.20.2, PyTorch v.1.8.0, Onekey v.2.2.3, Seaborn v.0.11.1, Matplotlib v.3.4.2. Prognostic analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2).

The experiments were conducted on a Windows server, equipped with Intel® Xeon® Core i9-13900k Central Processing Units (CPUs), and the models were trained on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 Graphics Processing Units (GPUs). The ResNet50 model was trained using the stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimizer, with 40 epochs and an initial learning rate of 0.01, and a batch size of 64.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the participants

The median age at diagnosis of neuroblastoma patients in the internal dataset was 3.0 years (range: 2.0 to 5.0 years). The median age in the external test datasets was 2.7 years (range: 0.8 to 4.2 years, cohort A) and 3.0 years (range: 1.1 to 5.1 years, cohort B). For BM metastasis status, the proportions of patients with BM involvement were 37.9% (n = 64) in the internal cohort, 39.1% (n = 45) in external cohort A, and 37.3% (n = 28) in external cohort B. No statistically significant differences in BM metastasis rates were observed across the three cohorts. MYCN amplification was detected in 21.9% (n = 37) of patients in the internal cohort, 24.3% (n = 28) in external cohort A, and 9.3% (n = 7) in external cohort B. MYCN amplification rates did not differ significantly between the internal cohort and external cohort A. Compared with the internal cohort, external cohort B exhibited a lower frequency of MYCN amplification and a higher proportion of low-grade tumors. Model diagnostic performance in neuroblastoma with BM metastases

In the validation set, the diagnostic AUC for BM metastasis was 0.924 (0.775–1.000), with an accuracy of 0.941, sensitivity of 0.833, specificity of 1, PPV of 1, NPV of 0.917, and an F1 score of 0.909. In the external testing datasets, the AUC for BM metastasis detection was 0.826 (95% CI: 0.741–0.911) in cohort A and 0.795 (95% CI: 0.684–0.906) in cohort B. The AUC values across cohorts are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, and detailed performance metrics are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

A whole-slide attention heatmap corresponding to the probabilities for qualified patches was generated by computing the attention scores for the predicted status of metastasis in BM, as shown in Fig. 2. The visualization of cytological features in the diagnostic model indicates that specific cellular arrangements that were particularly informative in the model’s decision-making process.

The prognosis value of cytology model in risk stratification

The median OS of neuroblastoma patients in the internal dataset was 18.0 months (range: 7.0 to 34.0 months, n = 163), while in the external datasets, it was 25.9 months (range: 15.5 to 42.7 months, cohort A, n = 114) and 20.0 months (range: 10.0 to 31.0 months, cohort B, n = 75). The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients in the low-risk subgroup had a significantly better prognosis compared to those in the high-risk subgroup (p < 0.005). This statistically significant difference was observed in both the training and validation cohorts, the result presented in Fig. 3.

The model interpretability and clinically relevant cytological features

Grad-CAM analysis demonstrated that regions prioritized by the cytology model overlapped with established cytomorphological features of neuroblastoma. High-attention regions frequently contained atypical cells with increased nucleus-to-cytoplasm (N/C) ratios and hyperchromatic or irregular chromatin, distinguishing them from surrounding hematopoietic cells. In addition to single-cell features, the model highlighted spatial patterns, including dense cellular clusters with nest-like or rosette-like configurations (arrows). These patterns correspond to recognized morphological characteristics of neuroblastoma involvement in BM.

Correlation analysis between cMIL-derived cytological features and hematological parameters showed that the feature “His-0.01” was associated with lymphocytes in bone marrow (BML). The feature “His-1.0” and “Bow-10” were associated with both BML and granulocytes in bone marrow (BMG). The feature “His-0.02”, “His-0.03” and “Bow-001” were associated with BML, lymphocyte in peripheral blood (PBL) and monocytes in peripheral blood (PBM). The feature “His-0.04” and “His-0.05” were associated with PBL and PBM, while “His-0.02” and “His-0.14” were associated with PBM and granulocytes in peripheral blood (PBG), as shown is Fig. 4.

We also performed correlation analysis of cytological features related to red blood cell and platelet, and found that red blood cell counts, hemoglobin concentration-related features highly overlapped, and crossed over with platelet-related features (Supplementary Fig. 4). Differential analysis of prognosis-related hematological parameters revealed a significantly higher proportion of peripheral blood neutrophils and a significantly lower lymphocyte count in the high-risk group (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

The cytology evaluation of BM is a crucial procedure for detecting metastatic tumor cells and assessing hematopoietic capacity in neuroblastoma [24–27]. In this multicenter study, we developed a cytology-based AI framework for BM detection and prognostic stratification in pediatric neuroblastoma. The cytology model identifies diagnostically relevant cytological features and demonstrated strong performance across independent cohorts, highlighting the potential for AI-assisted diagnosis based on BM cytology images. Multicenter validation constituted a rigorous stress test for model generalizability, as the cohorts differed substantially in overall survival, primary lesion distribution, and key laboratory parameters, including granulocyte and lymphocyte proportions (p < 0.05). Although a modest performance decrease was observed in external Cohort B (AUC = 0.795, 95% CI: 0.741–0.911), this difference is likely attributable to inter-cohort biological and clinicopathological heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the cytology model maintained clinically acceptable performance, supporting the generalizability of the learned cytological features across diverse patient populations.

The previous studies suggesting the potential for AI-assisted workflow in clinical practice [28–30]. However, to date, limited studies has focused on the BM cytology in diagnostic workflow of neuroblastoma [20, 21, 27]. Some studies have applied AI-based architecture for automated BM cytology in cell detection and classification, mostly with high diagnostic relevance to hematological diseases [31–33]. A single institutional retrospective study employed an automated system to identify metastatic cancer cell clusters in BM smears, which highlighted the challenge of distinguishing tumor clusters from hematopoietic islands and megakaryocytes clumps [23]. We propose a novel computer-aided quality supervision module designed to assess the quality of BM digital cytological images before feature extraction, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the downstream analysis. To investigate the prediction process, we utilized class activation mapping to generate visual representations that highlight the crucial regions of the cytology model. Interestingly, consistent with key findings in histopathological model [13], our cytology model emphasized complex cellular arrangements as significant predictors, aligning with the morphological features currently recognized by experienced hemopathologists.

To explore the potential prognostic relevance of cytology-based modeling in neuroblastoma, we applied the cMIL framework to estimate patient risk and observed an association between higher cMIL scores and poorer survival. Across independent cohorts, the model enabled stratification based on BM cytological features. After adjustment for established clinical and genetic risk factors, including MYCN amplification, the cMIL score remained statistically associated with OS, suggesting that image-derived cytological patterns may capture prognostic information not fully represented by conventional variables. These findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and may reflect underlying BM cellular heterogeneity. Further prospective validation and biological correlation studies are required to clarify the clinical utility and mechanistic basis of this approach.

Correlations between cMIL-derived features and immune cell profiles suggest a biologically plausible association with BM tumor microenvironment heterogeneity. The model captures variations in cytological patterns reflecting differences in hematopoietic cell distributions, including lymphoid and myeloid populations, which were descriptively associated with prognostic groups. Observed differences in peripheral blood neutrophil proportions and lymphocyte levels between risk groups are descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and may reflect broader immune alterations accompanying disease progression. Notably, neutrophils are short-lived myeloid cells that primarily reside in the BM, are continuously released into the peripheral circulation, and undergo expansion under pathological conditions [10]. Together, these findings suggest that image-based analysis of BM cytology may provide complementary and exploratory insights for future biological and translational studies, without implying causal or mechanistic relationships.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias in data collection. Prognostic analyses were necessarily restricted to patients with available follow-up data, excluding those treated elsewhere or discharged without therapy, which may bias survival estimates. Prospective, multi-center studies are therefore required to further evaluate clinical generalizability. Second, detection of low–tumor-burden or subtly infiltrative disease remains an inherent limitation of routine BM cytology and is influenced by inter-observer variability. To mitigate false-negative classification due to focal and patchy marrow involvement, we adopted a composite clinical reference standard using an any-positive rule integrating BM biopsy, cytology, flow cytometry, and PET/CT, consistent with standard diagnostic practice. While this sensitivity-oriented approach may introduce limited label noise, it reflects real-world clinical decision-making. The cytology model captures both cellular morphological features and microenvironment-related patterns; however, its performance in settings of minimal or occult disease warrants further investigation. Future studies integrating complementary data modalities, such as histopathology, radiologic imaging, and molecular profiling, may further enhance diagnostic precision and improve the translational applicability of AI-assisted cytological analysis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study presents a cytology-based artificial intelligence framework with strong performance validated across three independent cohorts. While further prospective validation and economic evaluation are required, the model may support risk stratification within cytology-based diagnostic workflows. The integration of visualization and correlation analyses enhances interpretability by linking model outputs to recognizable cytological features. Collectively, these findings suggest that image-based cytology modeling represents a feasible and adaptable direction for future translational research in neuroblastoma and other rare malignancies.
