Experimental investigation of mechanical properties of structural interlayers for rock masses during drilling process
Authors: Xinxing Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the continuous development of underground engineering construction in China, it is particularly important to study the identification of structural plane characteristics of rock masses. In this study, three types of pseudo-rock specimens with structural plane interlayers were fabricated to analyze the patterns of drilling parameter changes in rock bodies with structural planes during the drilling process and to explore the characterization and identification methods of rock body structural planes. Gneiss, granite, and sandstone were used as rock materials, with gypsum mortar as the interlayer material for the structural planes in these three types of specimens. The indoor digital drilling equipment was utilized for conducting indoor digital drilling experiments. The variation patterns of drilling parameters in rock bodies with structural surfaces under different interlayer inclinations and thicknesses were analyzed. The relationship between the ratio of the change in rotational speed and drilling speed during the stable phase of the upper rock mass and the peak torque and peak drilling pressure has been established. The relationship between the structural plane inclination angle and the ratio of change in rotational speed and drilling speed has been determined. By utilizing the variation in these ratios, the impacts of the structural plane inclination angle and the thickness of the structural plane on the peak torque and peak drilling pressure have been elucidated. The research results provide a theoretical basis for the stability evaluation of rock masses with structural planes under drilling action.Keywords: Structural planes, Drilling speed, Rotational speed, Drilling pressure, TorqueSubject terms: Natural hazards, Engineering

Introduction

With the rapid development of underground engineering such as drilling and tunneling in our country, research on digital drilling experiments of rock masses containing structural planes has become a focal point in the field of rock engineering1. The primary task for safe construction is to quickly and accurately obtain the structural characteristics of engineering rock2. Difficulties in engineering construction are caused by the complexity and variability of rock masses containing structural planes in practical engineering construction3. Over half of engineering construction projects are related to the structural characteristics and deformations of rock masses containing structural planes in practical situations4. It is important to study the structural characteristics of rock masses containing structural planes through digital drilling experiments5. The stability and distribution of rock masses containing structural planes are important guarantees for construction safety and also important bases for evaluating geological conditions.

With the advancement of technology, digital drilling technology is used in rock engineering research6. Domestic and international scholars have achieved a wealth of results in drilling experiments in recent years. Digital drilling technology was employed in the field of petroleum exploration. The objective is to reflect the deep geological conditions through drilling parameters7,8. Digital drilling technology has gradually been applied in hydraulic engineering and rock engineering since the 1970s. The heterogeneity of the rock mass and the complexity of the geological conditions are the main reasons for the large errors and dispersion in drilling parameters. Enpasol, Papero and Kajima et al.9,10 have also developed drilling systems for geological exploration. This allows for the real-time acquisition of relevant borehole data. These systems can only provide rough judgments of rock mass characteristics.

He et al.11 investigated the effect of anisotropy on the specific energy of six types of rocks. A special energy index f was proposed to characterize and evaluate the anisotropy of different rocks. Jiang et al.12 established a formula for the ultimate cutting force based on the principle of limit equilibrium. She derived the relationship between digital drilling parameters (DDP) and c-cp parameters (where c denotes cohesion and cp. denotes the internal friction angle) through the DDP-CCP formula. The response of drilling parameters and cutting ratio to strength parameters are analyzed. Wang et al.13 has developed a rotary cutting test system for rock mass (RCT system) for research on the relationships between drilling parameters and rock mechanics parameters. Yu et al.14 revealed the relationship between the mechanical parameters of rocks and drilling parameters; evaluated the relationship model between drilling parameters and strength, and derived a prediction method for compressive strength values based on drilling experiments. Through comparative analysis with parameters obtained from triaxial tests, the reliability of the rock c-φ values obtained from digital drilling experiments was verified. Wang et al.15 analyzed the effects of changes in shale structural plane dip angles on drilling pressure, torque, and described the rock fragmentation situation. Furthermore, the fluctuation curves of drilling pressure and torque and the displacement variation of the drill bit during angle changes were elucidated based on the vibration of the drilling column. He et al.16 conducted research on the relationship between drilling parameters and rock mechanics parameters during the rotary cutting process based on a self-developed rock mechanics parameter rotary cutting probe. Theoretical formulas have been established for determining rock mechanics parameters based on rotary cutting probe parameters through these studies. This rotary cutting technique was applied in engineering practice. The reliability of the rock mechanics parameters obtained through rotary cutting probing technology has been verified. Babaei et al.17 derived the relationship between drilling speed and rock mechanics parameters by installing data monitoring instruments on a hydraulic rock drill to organize and analyze the statistical data. Yasar et al.18 explored the relationship between drilling parameters and uniaxial compressive strength of rocks during the drilling process. Karasawa et al.19 defined parameters for effective rotational energy per revolution and effective axial force energy to determine rock drillability index. Additionally, they established the relationship between the rock drillability index and rock compressive strength. Aalizad et al.20 based on the developed drilling monitoring device suitable for construction sites, conducted analysis and research on various drilling data using neural network methods, and discovered that drilling speed, borehole diameter, and the integrity of the rock mass are highly related. Domestic and foreign scholars have also explored the relationship between drilling parameters and energy loss during the drilling process from an energy perspective. Zeng et al.21 analyzed the correlation between the change in specific energy of drilling during the drilling process and rock quality, using specific energy of drilling as an indicator to assess the quality of the rock mass. Lu et al.22, focusing on different formation rock masses in the southern margin of the Junggar Basin, deduced the relationship between drilling-specific energy and drilling speed through drilling experiments, and selected different drilling methods based on variations in drilling parameters in different formations. Tan et al.23,24 obtained drilling data of granite with different degrees of weathering, proposing the use of drilling specific work as a new index for rock mass classification, and concluded that drilling specific work of granite is negatively correlated with the degree of rock weathering. Liu et al.25 compared the prediction effects of two parameters, rotational power ratio and drilling specific work, on the location of soft-hard interfaces in rock layers, and found that the prediction of lithological properties by rotational power ratio is more accurate. Liu et al.26 proposed that the gas drilling mechanism improves the efficiency of rock fragmentation. Hou et al.27 analyzed the impact of parameter changes of insert tooth roller cutter on rock-breaking specific energy, providing reference for selecting appropriate insert tooth bits, drilling speed, and drilling pressure to improve rock-breaking efficiency. Kristopher et al.28 analyzed the impact of changing the drilling angle on the impact energy of the drill bit and its effect on rock fragmentation, and conducted research on rock-breaking mechanisms. Shanker et al.29 found that the uniaxial compressive strength of rocks is approximately equal to the drilling specific energy, and the obtained values are sufficient for judging drilling performance through a large number of drilling experiments. Kalantari et al.30 established a drilling resistance limit equilibrium analysis model based on drilling specific energy, and employed obtained drilling parameters to estimate mechanical parameters such as uniaxial compressive strength, cohesion, and internal friction angle of limestone. Munoz et al.31 considered the influence of the interaction between drilling tools and rocks on drilling performance, and proposed a new energy-based rock brittleness index to evaluate drilling performance. In summary, there is currently limited research on determining the relationship between drilling parameters and the response of different strata in rock masses with structural surfaces through digital drilling experiments. Therefore, analyzing the pattern of drilling parameter changes in structural rock masses is important. Identifying the exact locations of structural planes within the rock is crucial. This research is key for studying rock stability and providing theoretical support for engineering construction.

This study selected gneiss, granite, and sandstone as rock materials and used gypsum mortar as interlayer material to prepare three types of rock-like specimens with structural interlayers. This study conducted indoor digital drilling experiments using self-developed equipment. The variations in drilling parameters of rock masses with structural interlayers at different interlayer dip angles and thicknesses were analyzed. This provides a theoretical basis for evaluating the stability of rock masses with structural interlayers under drilling conditions.

Materials and methods

Test equipment

The small indoor digital drilling equipment employed in this drilling experiment mainly consists of five components: a drilling unit, dynamic torque sensor, pressure sensor, confining pressure loading device, and data acquisition system. Figure 1 shows a schematic diagram of this digital drilling equipment. The drilling rig device is the main equipment for conducting digital drilling tests. The maximum power of the drilling machine is 1050 watts, with six adjustable speed levels, corresponding to a maximum spindle speed of 800 revolutions per minute (rpm). The length and width of the specimens that can be loaded do not exceed 50 mm. The data acquisition system can realize real-time recording and processing of test data, and obtain the trend waveform graph of the drilling parameters. With this device, relevant drilling parameters during the rock drilling process can be obtained in real-time, including drilling pressure (F) in Newtons (N), torque (M) in Newton-meters (N·m), rotational speed (w) in rpm, drilling speed (v) in millimeters per second (mm/s), and confining pressure magnitude.

Rock mass sample preparation

The rock materials used in this indoor digital drilling experiment are gneiss (metamorphic rock), granite (igneous rock), and sandstone (sedimentary rock). The lithological and structural properties of these three rock types are distinct. The mineral compositions and their contents of gneiss, granite, and sandstone are shown in Table 1. It can be observed that quartz and feldspar are the predominant minerals in gneiss and granite, accounting for about 60% of the total content. In sandstone, feldspar accounts for about 50% of the total mineral content, while quartz constitutes about 10%. The gneiss, granite, and sandstone were obtained from materials at a construction project. The intact rock specimens of gneiss, granite, and sandstone exhibit no visible macroscopic cracks or damage on the surface. They are all taken from the same in-situ sample. The structural plane material of the rock mass is gypsum mortar. Using gypsum mortar as a simulant material to mimic the structural planes of a rock mass can produce rock bodies with structural planes that have relatively distinct mechanical property differences.

Results and discussion

Research on the variation patterns of drilling parameters of rock mass structural planes

Impact of different inclination angles on the drilling parameters of rock mass structural planes with a thickness of 5 mm

The variation curves of torque and drilling pressure with drilling depth for the three types of rock mass at a thickness of 5 mm with different structural plane inclinations are shown in Figs. 5, 6 and 7. It is apparent that the trends in torque and drill pressure with drilling depth are similar across the three types of rock with structural planes as shown in Figs. 5, 6 and 7. Generally, the internal structure of gneiss and granite minerals is more complex. The pores between mineral particles are very small. The inherent strength is relatively high. The difference in drilling parameters between gneiss and the structural plane is the most significant, followed by granite. The difference in drilling parameters between sandstone and the structural plane is the smallest. The torque and drill pressure of rock masses containing structural planes can be roughly divided into three stages with the change of drilling depth. The first stage is when the curve gradually increases upon entering the upper rock mass until it reaches a stable phase. The torque and drilling pressure of the drill bit will sharply increase when it first comes into contact with the upper rock mass. This increase is due to the contact area between the drill bit and the rock drilling surface continually growing as the drill bit penetrates the rock. When drilling, the drill bit needs to overcome the frictional resistance generated with the borehole surface. Therefore, the torque and drilling pressure will gradually increase. The torque and drilling pressure of the upper rock mass remain stable as the drilling depth continues to increase. The distance of its stable stage is relatively large because the thickness of the structural plane is relatively small, and the thickness of the upper and lower rock layers is larger. The second stage occurs as the drill enters the intermediate structural plane, where the curve momentarily decreases before stabilizing. When the drill bit contacts the intermediate structural plane, the torque and drill pressure curves gradually decrease due to the significantly reduced strength at the structural face. The final stage is when the drill penetrates the lower rock mass, and the curve ascends to a stable phase.

The relationship between the torque and thrust force of granite under the inclination of different structural plane and the drilling depth (d1 = 5 mm): (a) α = 0°; (b) α = 15°; (c) α = 30°; (d) α = 45°.

The relationship between the torque and thrust force of sandstone under the inclination of different structural plane and the drilling depth (d1 = 5 mm): (a) α = 0°; (b) α = 15°; (c) α = 30°; (d) α = 45°.

From Figs. 5, 6 and 7, when the structural face thickness is 5 mm, the torque and drill pressure for all three types of rock instantly decrease as the drill bit enters the structural face. As the inclination angle of the structural plane increases, the decreasing trend of torque and drilling pressure exhibits varying degrees of curvature. The arc of the decline curve gradually increases. It is observed that when the structural face inclination angle is 0°, the curves of torque and drill pressure nearly drop vertically. This is because when the inclination angle of the structural plane is 0°, the surface of the drill bit instantaneously enters the structural plane completely from the upper rock mass. The strength of the structural plane is relatively smaller compared to the three types of rock masses. The structural plane does not produce excessive resistance to the drill bit. The torque and drill pressure curves will exhibit a vertical decline. When the structural face inclination angles are 15°, 30°, and 45°, the transition of the drill bit surface from the upper rock layer to the structural face does not occur all at once. A part of the drill bit surface enters first, followed by the entire surface. Therefore, at this stage, the drill bit surface will contact both the rock layer and the structural face simultaneously. There will be a noticeable difference between the torque and drilling pressure as they decrease. The image exhibits a certain degree of curvature. Moreover, the larger the structural plane inclination angle, the greater the difference in contact area between the drill bit surface and both the rock layer and the structural plane during operation. The curvature of the declining trend of torque and drill pressure also becomes greater.

As shown in Figs. 8, 9 and 10, the drilling parameters of torque and drill pressure of the three types of rock bodies containing structural planes clearly decline upon encountering structural planes. For the same type of rock body, the curvature of the declining curve becomes greater as the angle of the structural plane increases.

The torque and drill pressure trends of the three types of rock bodies containing structural planes during drilling process are similar as indicated in Figs. 8, 9 and 10. The downward trend of torque and drill pressure at the structural plane is not affected by the thickness of the structural plane. When the structural face inclination angle is 0°, the stratification difference between the rock layer and the structural plane is pronounced. When the drill bit contacts the intermediate structural plane, the curve is not influenced by the rock mass, and the structural plane does not exert significant resistance on the torque and drill pressure, resulting in a vertical decline in the torque and drill pressure curves at the structural plane. The angle of the structural plane is inversely proportional to the rate of curve decline consistent with the conclusions in Sect “Impact of different inclination angles on the drilling parameters of rock mass structural planes with a thickness of 5 mm”.

The overall strength of gneiss and granite is relatively high. During the drilling process, the resistance exerted by the rock mass on the drill bit is noticeably significant. The overall torque and drill pressure values are relatively high, and there is no significant damage observed in the rock mass. For instance, in gneiss, at a structural face inclination angle of 0° and a drilling depth of 16 mm, and at a structural face inclination angle of 30° and a drilling depth of 14 mm, minor damage occurs. Similarly, in granite, at a structural face inclination angle of 0° and a drilling depth of 12 mm, minor damage occurs. The strength of sandstone is relatively lower, leading to significant damage in parts of the rock mass during drilling. Significant damage to the sandstone was observed at a structural plane angle of 0° and a drilling depth of 11 mm, as well as at structural plane angles of 45° and drilling depths of 8 mm and 14 mm. At these points, both the torque and drilling pressure curves exhibited varying degrees of decline.

For the slate samples with a structural plane thickness of 5 mm, when the structural plane inclination angle is 0°, the peak torque and peak drilling pressure are 0.65 N·m and 82 N, respectively. When the structural plane inclination angle is 45°, the peak torque and peak drilling pressure are 1.27 N·m and 157 N, respectively, an increase of 0.62 N·m and 75 N compared to 0°. Similarly, for a structural plane thickness of 10 mm, the peak torque and peak drilling pressure increased by 0.49 N·m and 78 N, respectively. For a structural plane thickness of 15 mm, the peak torque and peak drilling pressure increased by 0.46 N·m and 74 N, respectively. This indicates that as the structural plane thickness increases, the change in peak torque of slate decreases with the increase of the structural plane inclination angle, while the change in peak drilling pressure initially increases and then decreases. The average peak torque and peak drilling pressure for different structural plane thicknesses under the inclination angles of 0°, 15°, 30°, and 45° are calculated to be 0.51 N·m, 0.65 N·m, 0.84 N·m, 1.03 N·m, and 56 N, 75 N, 92 N, 132 N, respectively. The peak drilling pressure at 45° is 58% higher than at 0°. This indicates that the angle of the structural plane has a greater impact on the peak drill pressure at the gneiss structural plane than on the peak torque.

For granite, when the structural plane thickness is 5 mm, 10 mm, and 15 mm, the peak torque increases by 0.56 N·m, 0.46 N·m, and 0.31 N·m, respectively, and the peak drilling pressure increases by 45 N, 68 N, and 48 N, respectively, as the structural plane inclination angle increases from 0° to 45°. At this point, the greater the thickness of the structural plane, the smaller the change in peak torque of granite with the increase of the structural plane inclination angle, the variation of peak drill pressure increases initially and then decreases as the angle of the structural plane increases. The average peak torque and peak drilling pressure for different structural plane thicknesses of granite under inclination angles of 0° to 45° are 0.37 N·m, 0.43 N·m, 0.59 N·m, 0.82 N·m, and 46.7 N, 62 N, 81 N, 100.3 N, respectively. The changes are 55% and 53%, respectively. This indicates that the structural plane inclination angle has a greater effect on the peak torque of granite than on the peak drilling pressure.

Similarly, an analysis of the peak torque and peak drill pressure at the sandstone structural planes reveals that as the thickness of the structural plane increases, the variation in peak torque of sandstone first decreases and then increases with the increase in the angle of the structural plane. The variation in peak drill pressure shows a decreasing trend as the angle of the structural plane increases. The average values of peak torque and peak drill pressure at different thicknesses of the structural planes for the same inclination angle of sandstone were taken. It can be observed that when the angle of the structural plane increases from 0° to 45°, the variations in peak torque and peak drill pressure are 32% and 45% respectively. At this point, the influence of the angle of the structural plane on the peak drill pressure at the sandstone structural plane is greater than that on the peak torque.

Impact of thickness on peak torque and bit pressure at rock mass structural plane

The impact of thickness on peak torque and bit pressure at rock mass structural plane is shown in Figs. 17, 18 and 19. For gneiss, as the thickness of the structural plane increases from 5 mm to 15 mm, when the inclination angle of the structural plane is 0°, the peak torque at the gneiss structural plane decreases from 0.65 N·m to 0.39 N·m, a reduction of 0.26 N·m; the peak drill pressure decreases from 82 N to 36 N, a reduction of 46 N. Similarly, at an inclination angle of 15°, the reductions in peak torque and peak drill pressure are 0.22 N·m and 37 N, respectively. At an inclination angle of 30°, the increases in peak torque and peak drill pressure are 0.39 N·m and 34 N, respectively. At an inclination angle of 45°, the increases in peak torque and peak drill pressure are 0.42 N·m and 47 N, respectively. It can be observed that as the inclination angle increases, the increments in peak torque and peak drill pressure at the gneiss structural plane first decrease and then increase with the increase in structural plane thickness. When the thickness of the structural plane is the same, the average values of peak torque and peak drill pressure at the structural plane of schist under different structural plane angles are calculated. When the thickness of the structural plane is 5 mm, the average values of peak torque and peak drill pressure are 0.93 N·m and 110 N, respectively. When the thickness of the structural plane is 10 mm, the average values of peak torque and peak drill pressure are 0.73 N·m and 87.5 N, respectively. When the thickness of the structural plane is 15 mm, the average values of peak torque and peak drill pressure are 0.61 N·m and 69 N, respectively. From a thickness of 5 mm to 15 mm, the peak torque and peak drill pressure decrease by 0.32 N·m and 41 N, respectively, with the change percentages being 34% and 37%, respectively. This indicates that as the thickness of the structural plane increases, its impact on the peak drill pressure at the gneiss structural plane is greater than on the peak torque.

For granite, when the structural plane inclination angles range from 0 to 45 degrees, the decrease in peak torque at the structural plane is 0.32 N·m, 0.37 N·m, 0.45 N·m, and 0.57 N·m respectively, while the decrease in peak drilling pressure is 51 N, 40 N, 41 N, and 48 N respectively. Here, as the structural plane inclination angle increases, the decrease in peak torque at the granite structural plane gradually increases, while the decrease in peak drilling pressure shows a trend of first decreasing and then increasing. Similarly, for sandstone, as the structural plane inclination angle increases, the change in peak torque at the sandstone structural plane exhibits a trend of initially decreasing and then increasing, while the increment of peak drilling pressure shows a decreasing trend. When the structural plane thickness increases from 5 mm to 15 mm, the changes in these two values are 32% and 17% respectively. This indicates that the impact on the peak torque at the sandstone structural plane is greater than that on the peak drilling pressure.

The impact of the relationship between drilling parameters and structural planes on the peak of these parameters at structural surfaces in the rock mass

Analysis of the relationship between structural plane inclination angle and drilling parameters

Experimental results indicate that there is an instantaneous increase in the torque and drilling pressure values when the drill bit penetrates the upper rock mass and starts to enter the middle structural plane, and a peak value point appears as shown in Fig. 20. It is found that the peak torque and peak drilling pressure at different rock mass structural surfaces increase with the increase of the structural plane inclination angle when the structural plane thickness is the same. It is also found that the rotation speed of the drill bit and the drilling speed can affect the magnitude of the peak values. Therefore, the reasons for the changes in peak torque and peak drilling pressure at the rock mass structural surfaces can be analyzed by studying the relationship between drilling speed, rotation speed, and structural plane inclination angle. During the drilling process, the trend of change in drilling speed is similar to that of torque and drill pressure, indicating a positive correlation, as shown in Fig. 20. However, the trend of rotational speed change is negatively correlated with torque, drill pressure, and drilling speed. This suggests that an increase in drilling speed leads to a deeper penetration of the drill bit into the rock per unit of time. On the other hand, an increase in rotational speed enhances the cutting action of the drill bit on the rock. If the change in drilling speed is minimal, the “squeezing action” between the drill bit end and the rock does not intensify significantly with an increase in rotational speed, resulting in reduced resistance and friction from the rock to the drill bit, thus causing a decrease in torque and drill pressure. However, when the drill bit enters the upper rock layer, the stability phase and the range of variation of these four drilling parameters when the drill bit enters the middle structural plane are roughly the same. Moreover, when the drill bit starts to contact the structural plane, its torque, drill pressure, and drilling speed all reach a maximum value, while the rotational speed reaches a minimum value. Therefore, by studying the relationship between the changes in rotational speed, drilling speed at the stable phase and peak points, and the inclination angle of the structural plane, the variation patterns of torque and drill pressure at the rock structural plane can be analyzed.

Analysis of the relationship between structural plane thickness and drilling parameters

The above research results indicate that when the structural plane inclination remains constant, the peak torque and peak drilling pressure at the structural plane of different rock masses decrease with increasing structural plane thickness. At the same time, the structural plane thickness also affects the rotational speed and drilling speed during the drilling process. When the structural plane inclination angle remains the same, the ratio of changes in rotational speed and drilling speed from the stable stage to the peak point at the structural plane during the drilling process gradually increases for different rock masses with the increase in structural plane thickness. Moreover, the ratio for sandstone is the highest, followed by granite, with the ratio for gneiss being the lowest, as shown in Fig. 22. Additionally, as the change in drill bit rotational speed increases, the change in drilling speed decreases, leading to a gradual decrease in torque and drilling pressure values. Therefore, as the structural plane thickness increases, the peak torque and peak drilling pressure at the structural plane gradually decrease.

Conclusion and outlook

This paper analyzes the variation patterns of drilling parameters in rock masses containing structural planes and explores methods for characterizing and identifying these planes. Three types of artificial rock specimens with structural planes were prepared using gypsum mortar as the interlayer material. Indoor digital drilling experiments were conducted to analyze the changes in drilling parameters and energy evolution trends in rock masses with different angles and thicknesses of structural planes. Based on this, a method for identifying structural planes was proposed and validated. This provides a theoretical basis for evaluating the stability of rock masses containing structural planes under drilling. This research offers a more convenient and efficient method for identifying structural planes in the preliminary investigation and design stages of rock engineering. The main conclusions drawn from the study are as follows:

(1) Gneiss has the highest overall torque and drill pressure, followed by granite and sandstone. With a constant structural plane thickness, torque and drill pressure decrease significantly as the structural plane angle increases. The stable phase length increases with structural plane thickness when the inclination angle is constant.

(2) As the drill bit penetrates the upper rock mass and encounters the structural plane, the peak torque and drilling pressure of all three rock types show non-linear trends with changes in structural plane angle and thickness. An increase in structural plane angle generally increases peak torque and drilling pressure while an increase in thickness decreases them.

(3) For different rock types with structural planes, an increase in structural plane angle affects peak drilling pressure more for schist and sandstone and peak torque more for granite. As structural plane thickness increases, the impact on peak drilling pressure is greater for schist and granite, the impact on peak torque is greater for sandstone. Relationships between rotational speed, drilling speed, structural plane inclination angle and thickness have been established to elucidate these impacts.

This study still has some limitations. The small size of the rock samples used in the laboratory tests may have overlooked the impact of the rock size effect on the experimental results. The size effect of rocks mainly affects their mechanical properties and deformation characteristics. However, this research is based on the dynamic response of drilling parameters to structural plane interlayers, these changes in drilling parameters are not mitigated by the size effect. This research provides a convenient and efficient method for identifying structural planes in engineering. Although laboratory tests have verified the method, the limitations of experimental conditions hinder its in-situ validation. The study did not account for the inherent properties of the drilling machine which may affect the accuracy of identification. These limitations could introduce some deviations in the results. The sample size should be increased, the impact of the drilling rig should be considered, on-site testing should be conducted for validation, and the interlayer identification mechanism should be further investigated to reduce the errors and shortcomings of the current method.
