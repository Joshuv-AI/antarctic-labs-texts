A deep embedded clustering algorithm in conjunction with an ensemble technique for mineral prospectivity mapping
Authors: Mobin Saremi, Zohre Hoseinzade, Mahyar Yousefi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional clustering algorithms are popular unsupervised methods and have been widely applied in mineral prospectivity mapping (MPM). Despite the advantages of these algorithms in terms of simplicity and popularity, they are not strong enough to struggle with high-dimensional, complex, and non-linear geospatial data. Consequently, they may lead to suboptimal clustering performance, a reason for not being able to precisely recognize and discriminate complex mineralization-related anomaly patterns in mineral exploration datasets. To improve the clustering performance, we propose a deep embedded clustering (DEC) approach for MPM. DEC is an unsupervised method that uses deep neural networks to learn from the feature representations and optimize cluster assignments simultaneously. In this study, evidence layers, representing porphyry copper mineralization, were first generated. Then, four clustering techniques were applied to generate prospectivity models. The prediction rate of the models was evaluated using the prediction-area (P-A) plot. The results showed that the prediction rates of K-means, Gaussian mixture model (GMM), DEC-K-means, and DEC-GMM prospectivity models were 66, 68, 69, and 72%, respectively. This demonstrates that DEC-based clustering outperforms conventional clustering algorithms and that DEC-GMM effectively recognizes mineralization-related patterns. Finally, to benefit from the advantages of all the applied clustering methods, we calculated a confidence index, as an ensemble technique, to recognize exploration targets, those that support further mineral exploration programs in terms of low uncertainty.Keywords: Mineral prospectivity mapping; Clustering; Deep learning; Porphyry copper mineralization, Shahr-e-Babak regionSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Mineral prospectivity mapping (MPM) integrates layers of multiple geo-exploration data, each of which represents a subsystem of ore-forming processes, to recognize and delimit potential targets, one step towards undiscovered deposit sites1–6. MPM and exploration of mineral deposits in areas with complex geological settings is a challenging undertaking in that, in such areas, conventional methods are inept at recognizing anomalies and revealing patterns representing mineralization over and around ore deposition sites7–11. Due to the complex nature, nonlinearity, and high dimensionality of geospatial data, exploration geologists have attempted to adapt and develop strong statistical and mathematical approaches, aiming at including them in machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) MPM algorithms12–17. Additionally, to struggle with the inevitable challenges of mineral exploration of the future, conceptualizing and adapting concepts such as “survival bias”18 and “confidence index”19 have been prompted.

ML and DL algorithms are generally classified into supervised and unsupervised models. Supervised learning models analyze and quantify the intricate correlation and structures between ground truth samples and geospatial data to extract hidden geo-patterns linked to mineralization20,21. The negative side of the supervised methods is their dependence on labeled (i.e., positive and negative) datasets to achieve high classification accuracy20,22. Therefore, unsupervised approaches have been proposed in that they don’t require labeled samples and can reveal hidden patterns in complex datasets23,24. These methods are capable of capturing complex, nonlinear relationships and discovering new anomalies linked to mineralization that may present exploration evidence not entirely similar to that observed in known mineral occurrences19. Unsupervised methods applied in the field of geochemical exploration and MPM for the identification of mineralization-related anomalies can broadly be classified into several groups. These include, but are not limited to, reconstruction-based methods (e.g., deep autoencoders and their variants)6,25–27, one-class classification-based approaches (e.g., one-class support vector machines)16,28–30, clustering-based algorithms (e.g., K-means)31,32, tree-based methods (e.g., isolation forest and its extensions)24,33,34, and dimensionality reduction-based techniques (e.g., PCA)35,36. Recently, nonlinear dimensionality reduction approaches, such as the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), have also been introduced as an effective tool for recognizing mineralization-related anomalies from high-dimensional datasets37.

Cluster analysis is a fundamental technique in data processing38 that groups similar data samples into distinct clusters based on their inherent similarities without requiring prior knowledge or labeled data39,40. Prevalent clustering algorithms are generally classified into several main categories41, comprising K-means-based42–44, graph-based45, kernel-based46, spectral clustering47,48, and hybrid methods.

In the past decades, classical clustering algorithms such as K-means49, density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN)50, and hierarchical clustering51, have been adopted as statistical tools for data analysis, of which K-means has been widely applied in mineral exploration tasks due to its capacity to identify inherent cluster structures in geo-exploration data, its relatively low computational cost, and ease of implementation32,52,53. Traditional clustering methods cannot well handle the large and high-dimensional datasets due to the curse of dimensionality, which adversely affects their practical applications54–56. For instance, the performance of K-means is dependent on the initial clustering centroids38,57. Besides, K-means is a hard method41 that suffers from poor non-linear representation ability and cannot perform well on complex, large-scale, and high-dimensional datasets38. To mitigate these issues, several studies have proposed the use of dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis58 and feature selection59 before applying traditional clustering methods. However, such approaches are linear and shallow models with limited representational capacity that ignore the interconnection between feature learning and clustering54,60. Thus, the use of dimensionality reduction techniques is not an impeccable solution for struggling with the challenge of detecting hidden patterns and subtle anomalies among the complex nonlinear relationships in mineral exploration datasets through traditional clustering methods.

To address the limitations of traditional clustering approaches, deep clustering methods, such as deep embedded clustering (DEC)61, have been proposed. The DEC combines deep neural networks with clustering algorithms to reveal complex patterns in mineral exploration datasets. Unlike conventional methods, it simultaneously optimizes feature learning and clustering, leading to more meaningful representation and improved clustering performance. Hoseinzade and Bazobandi62 demonstrated the ability of DEC to face nonlinear geochemical datasets by automatic data clustering. Thus, it effectively identifies patterns and anomalies linked to mineralization.

This study aims to adapt the DEC algorithm for MPM and to illustrate how it improves the success rate of mineral exploration targeting. In addition, this study develops and demonstrates an optimized variant of the DEC algorithm that shows improved performance compared to traditional clustering methods.

To illustrate the proposed procedure, a dataset of hydrothermal copper mineralization in the Shahr-e-Babak region, Iran, was selected. Then, four clustering algorithms were used to analyze and cluster the dataset, aiming at generating prospectivity models. Finally, to benefit from the advantages of all the applied modeling methods, we calculated a confidence index, as an ensemble technique, to recognize exploration targets, those that support further mineral exploration programs in terms of low uncertainty.

Regional geological setting

From the structural perspective, the study area, namely the Shahr-e-Babak region, is a significant part of the UDMB63. This metallogenic province (i.e., UDMB) is a long tectono-magmatic belt that is composed of different types of intrusive, volcanic, and volcano-sedimentary rocks. Also, it is recognized as one of the most important metallogenic belts in Iran. Many hydrothermal copper deposits, such as porphyry copper systems, are found in this belt64,65. The intrusive rocks in the UDMB are the main source of heat and metal-bearing fluid. These rocks play a critical role in the mineralization process. They include diorite, granodiorite, granite, monzonite, monzodiorite, quartz diorite, quartz monzonite, and so on64,66. Also, the volcanic units include andesite, basalt, rhyolite, and others. Several well-known porphyry copper deposits, such as Sungun, Dali, Sarcgeshmeh, Kahang, Masjed Daghi, and Meydouk, are located along the UDMB65.

The Meydouk deposit is located in the Shah-e-Babak geological map. Outcrops of Eocene andesite and basalt are observed in the northern part of the area. Granodiorite rocks are present and may be related to these volcanic rocks. Also, Neogene conglomerates are present in the northern parts of the study area. Quaternary terraces are observed in the north and around the Babak plain. Intrusive rocks such as diorite are exposed in the Masahim volcanic caldera. Altered rocks cover a significant portion of the region. Several studies have shown that hydrothermal alterations are widespread in the region and represent the surface footprint of porphyry copper mineralization. The altered rocks in the Meydouk area may be related to Neogene volcanic activity. Hydrothermal copper occurrences are common across the region. The most important deposit is the Meydouk deposit. Also, vein-type copper mineralization is reported in the Chah-Masi area, and includes pyrite, chalcopyrite, and galena. In the Chehel Dokhtaran area, quartz veins with magnetite and malachite are present. Also, weak copper distribution has been observed in this zone. Overall, the Shah-e-Babak geological map shows favorable conditions for the formation of porphyry copper deposits. Figure 1 shows the geological map of the study area.

General workflow

This study aims to improve exploration targeting and identify new exploration targets related to porphyry copper mineralization in the Shahre-babak region by introducing a novel clustering method. To this end, several evidence layers were generated based on the conceptual model of porphyry copper mineralization, and continuous weighting was applied to these layers. Then, clustering methods were applied for the detection of promising areas for follow-up mineral exploration programs. Specifically, the study investigated how clustering methods, particularly deep-based models, can improve the prediction rate of prospectivity mapping. The performance of the generated models was quantitatively evaluated using the prediction-area (P-A) plot67. In the final step, the confidence index (CI) ensemble method and percentile method were applied to benefit from the strength of different clustering algorithms and to delineate new targets for follow-up mineral exploration efforts. Clustering-based methods

K-means

Clustering algorithms are an important tool in the unsupervised learning fields56, which focus on grouping similar samples into cohesive clusters39 without prior labels. In this regard, different types of clustering algorithms have been developed and applied for real-world applications that one of the important algorithms is K-means, that have garnered substantial attention from researchers in many disciplines, such as those working in mineral exploration, thanks to the exceptional capacity of K-means to capture hidden structures in datasets and its low computational costs32. The K-means clustering algorithm, originally introduced independently by several researchers, such as Steinhaus68, Jancey69, MacQueen49, and Lloyd70, from different disciplines. In the standard K-means clustering method, clusters are formed based on the mean values of the objects within each cluster41. This leads to the creation of a membership matrix for the original data, consisting of binary values represented as 0s and 1s. As a result, this classic version is classified as a form of hard clustering41.

The K-means technique starts by randomly choosing k initial centroids from the dataset. Each data point is then assigned to the closest centroid, usually based on Euclidean distance41. After assigning all points, the cluster centroids are recalculated as the mean of all points assigned to each cluster. This process is iteratively performed until the cluster memberships are stable. The K-means procedure can be summarized following key stages40:

Gaussian mixture model (GMM)

Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) clustering71 is a flexible, probabilistic approach to unsupervised learning that models a dataset as a mixture of several Gaussian distributions, each representing a different cluster. Unlike traditional clustering methods like K-means, which assign each data point to a single cluster (hard clustering), GMM performs soft clustering by estimating the probability that each data point belongs to each Gaussian component. This is particularly useful for capturing uncertainty in cluster assignment and for identifying overlapping clusters. The model is trained using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, which iteratively updates the parameters of the Gaussian distribution, such as mean, covariance, and mixing coefficient, to maximize the likelihood of the observed data. GMM is capable of modeling more complex cluster shapes, such as elliptical distributions, and can handle clusters with varying sizes and orientations, making it more powerful and versatile than distance-based clustering methods. Due to its probabilistic foundation, GMM also provides a natural way to model the underlying data distribution, which can be beneficial in tasks such as density estimation, anomaly detection, and pattern recognition.

DEC-based clustering

The Deep Embedded Clustering (DEC)61 is an unsupervised learning technique that leverages the capabilities of deep neural networks (DNNs) along with clustering algorithms to uncover meaningful patterns in the dataset. The core idea behind this method is to first learn feature reorientations and then perform clustering on those learned features. This approach enables DEC to capture complex relationships and structures in data that traditional clustering methods might overlook. Broadly, the process is carried out in two stages and four main steps, which are described in detail below. The first stage is referred to as the pretraining phase. In this stage, a deep autoencoder (typically a neural network with stacked layers) is employed to initially learn the embedding space. The autoencoder is trained layer by layer to encode the data with minimal reconstruction loss. After training, the decoder is discarded, and only the encoder is retained to project the data into the embedding space. The second stage involves five steps. In the first step, the data is passed through the encoder, and a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is applied to the embedding space to initialize the cluster centers (µj). The second step, known as soft assignment, computes the probability of each data point (zi) belonging to each cluster (j) using the Student’s t-distribution. Here, qij denotes the probability of assigning sample i to cluster j(Fig. 3, number 1). The third step focuses on minimizing the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. In this step, using an auxiliary target distribution, the deep mapping function fθ and cluster centers are updated by learning from high-confidence assignments. This is achieved by aligning the soft assignment distribution (qi) with the target distribution (pi), with the objective function being the KL divergence between qi and pi(Fig. 3, number 2). In the fourth step, the target distribution pi must be defined in a way that satisfies three key properties: (1) it sharpens predictions to increase cluster purity, (2) it places more emphasis on data points with high assignment confidence, and (3) it normalizes the contribution of each cluster center to mitigate the distortion of the latent feature space by disproportionately large clusters(Fig. 3, number 3). Finally, in the fifth step, both the cluster centers and the parameters θ are optimized simultaneously. To update the cluster centers and the parameters of the DNNs, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum is used. The gradients ∂L/∂zi are backpropagated through the DNN and used in standard backpropagation to compute ∂L/∂θ (Fig. 3, number 4). Figure 4 presents a schematic diagram of the algorithm.

Confidence index

In recent years, within the framework of an exploration information system19, proposed the confidence index as an ensemble method (Eq. 1). This method aims to identify exploration targets with minimal uncertainty when several prospectivity modeling approaches are applied. In the resulting CI map, cells with higher CI values represent areas of greater confidence and more reliable exploration targets (i.e., lower uncertainty) for follow-up mineral exploration programs. In contrast, lower CI values indicate areas with a higher degree of uncertainty and may be excluded from future investigation.

Generation of evidence layers

Mineral system components can be conceptualized as a series of ore-forming subsystems72–74. According to7, the ore-formation process can be divided into three main sub-systems, namely pre, syn, and post-mineralization. This classification reflects the different geological processes influencing mineralization and facilitates the generation of effective and scale-appropriate evidence layers, and overall generates more reliable prospectivity models. This division is based on the available data and the scope of the study. For example, in regional-scale geochemical exploration, stream sediment spatial data are used for generating a geochemical evidence layer. Stream sediment data are related to post-mineralization. Other examples are litho-geochemical data that are related to syn-mineralization and are suitable for deposit-scale mineral exploration targeting11. For other data and evidence, this classification can be used. Accordingly, this study generated the following evidence layers. The evidence layers and final models were generated using QGIS and Python 3.

Metal deposition evidence layers

Geochemistry is a fundamental science in the field of mineral exploration and is widely used as a critical tool in the detection of ore deposits in a study area75–78. The primary goal of exploration geochemistry is to provide a strong framework for distinguishing true geochemical anomalies from background variations79,80. Among various geospatial datasets used in mineral exploration targeting, geochemical evidence layers are important81, particularly at the regional scale. At this scale, stream sediment sampling is recognized as a widely adopted and cost-effective technique for identifying regional geochemical patterns and guiding follow-up exploration efforts82–85.

Stream sediments collected at specific locations reflect the footprints of the upstream catchment basin, which makes them suitable proxies for the identification of mineralized zones86. Mapping the distribution of elements in stream sediments enables exploration geologists to identify univariate and multivariate geochemical anomalies, which often are indicators of ore-forming processes87. These anomalies provide valuable insights into the occurrence, spatial distribution, and potential enrichment of mineralization-related elements. In this study, directed by metallogenic features and considering the classification of mineral system components and the conceptual model of porphyry copper mineralization, geochemical maps for As, Cu, Pb, Zn, Sb, and Mo were generated (Fig. 5).

Evidence layers of hydrothermal alterations

In regional-scale mineral exploration, geospatial data and information are often acquired through low-cost (i.e., cost-effective), time-efficient, and rapid methods such as satellite remote sensing88,89. Satellite-based remote sensing data analysis is one of the most effective approaches to identify several surface footprints (e.g., hydrothermal alteration zones) that indicate the presence of mineral deposits90,91. These zones result from chemical reactions between host rocks and ore-bearing hydrothermal fluids. Specific alteration types such as argillic (presence of clay minerals such as kaolinite, illite, and montmorillonite), phyllic (presence of minerals such as sericite (muscovite), quartz, and often pyrite), propylitic (presence of minerals such as chlorite, epidote, and calcite), iron oxide-rich zones, silicic alteration zones are the key indicators of hydrothermal mineral systems. These can be mapped using multispectral and hyperspectral satellite data. In this study, ASTER and Landsat remote sensing data were processed using the band ratio method to map hydrothermal alteration zones. Following this, five remote-sensing evidence layers, namely proximity to argillic alteration, phyllic alteration, propylitic alteration, iron oxides, and silicic alteration, were generated (Fig. 6). Based on this figure, some alteration zones display circular patterns, particularly around KMOs, which may be indicative of porphyry copper systems in the region.

Proximity map of to (a) argillic alteration, (b) phyllic alteration, (c) propylitic alteration, (d) iron oxides, and (e) silicic alteration, generated using Python 3.

Pathway evidence layer

Hydrothermal ore-forming systems (e.g., porphyry copper deposits) are an example of attractive mineral systems for exploration geologists due to the variety of evidence layers that can be used to support prospectivity mapping frameworks92. Hydrothermal mineralization is a complex natural process that includes the transport of ore-bearing hydrothermal fluids, their reaction with surrounding rocks (i.e., wall rocks), mineral precipitation, and the final concentration of ore deposits rich in specific elements. Many studies have shown that modeling structural features (e.g., faults, fractures, and so on) is important for the prediction of porphyry copper mineralization, as they provide pathways (i.e., conduits) for the movement of ore-forming materials92–95. From a structural geology perspective, faults often have strong spatial relationships with ore deposits and often control their distribution. Faults provide conduits for fluid flow, facilitate mineral precipitation, and create favorable sites and structural traps for ore accumulation. Hence, modeling faults can help identify the optimal location where mineralization is likely to occur. In this study, we generated a continuous evidence map that shows proximity to faults (Fig. 7).

Clustering-based prospectivity modeling

Today, there is a consensus among exploration geologists that the majority of the surface mineral deposits with strong surface geochemical and geological signatures have already been found and exploited. As a result, the next generation of exploration activity needs to focus on near-surface or deep-seated mineral deposits, where the mineralization-related signals may be weak or subtle due to several factors such as depth and geological complexity. However, discovering and delimiting new mineral deposits at depth and under cover in each study area has become a difficult and uncertain task, due to the inherent complexity of ore-forming processes and our limited understanding of the subsurface geological features linked to mineralization. Therefore, according to the above-mentioned contents, given the geological complexity of the study area and the nature of the available geospatial datasets, exploration geologists must adopt strong hybrid approaches that consider uncertainty and recognize nonlinear and hidden patterns in complex geospatial datasets to support the discovery of new mineral deposits. Accordingly, this study proposed a hybrid approach that combines geological knowledge derived from the mineral system model of porphyry copper deposits, the fusion capabilities of unsupervised ML and DL clustering algorithms, the confidence index (CI) ensemble method, and a percentile-based technique.

At present, there is a strong trend among geoscientists to use ML and DL techniques in mineral exploration targeting. In this study, four ML and DL clustering algorithms were applied to generate porphyry copper prospectivity models aimed at the identification of potential mineralized zones. First, the K-means algorithm, a basic and widely used unsupervised clustering technique, was used. The optimal number of clusters was determined to be five using the elbow method. The clustering result (Fig. 8a) shows that several of the generated clusters exhibited weak to moderate spatial correlation with known mineral occurrences. This indicates that the model cannot fully capture meaningful geological patterns. Next, the GMM, as another classical clustering method capable of modeling data distributions more flexibly, was applied for the generation of other prospectivity models (Fig. 8b). Similar to K-means, this method clusters the study area based on input evidence layers. The prospectivity model generated by GMM also showed a relatively moderate spatial correlation with geological evidence, such as known mineral occurrences. However, the inherent limitations of these shallow clustering methods prevent them from effectively handling and integrating complex exploration data. Therefore, the use of stronger approaches, such as DL-based clustering algorithms, is needed to generate more accurate prospectivity maps and to identify nonlinear patterns linked to mineralization.

The models generated using (a) K-means, (b) GMM, (c) DEC-K-means, and (d) DEC-GMM (generated using Python 3).

Among DL-based clustering methods, DEC-based approaches are considered among the most effective. These algorithms have strong fusion capabilities, as they integrate a deep autoencoder, a customized clustering mechanism, a loss function, and a training process. In the custom clustering procedure within this architecture, initial cluster centers are determined using the K-means algorithm (DEC-K-means). An important consideration at this stage is the use of a hard clustering method, in which each data point is assigned exclusively to one cluster. To improve this process, this study introduces a modified version of DEC that incorporates soft clustering with GMM (DEC-GMM). In this enhanced framework, each point is probabilistically associated with multiple clusters. Learning rates for pretraining and fine-tuning, as well as the number of epochs and the size of each hidden layer, need to be determined through experimentation on input layers in order to achieve an optimal architecture. Several parameters, such as encoding dim, learning rate, and epochs, must be defined before training. The encoding dim determines the dimensionality of the compressed space in the encoder. This value should be selected based on the data complexity and the number of intended clusters. In this study, it was set to half the number of input features. Another key parameter is the learning rate, which affects the model’s convergence speed; it was set at 0.003. The number of epochs is also determined based on data volume and model complexity. The final encoder architecture was selected as 64-64-32-32. After training the DEC-K-means and DEC-GMM algorithms, two prospectivity models were generated (Fig. 8c and d). Due to their ability to learn feature representations and optimal cluster assignments, these methods well-integrate and analyze input evidence layers and have high fusion capability. As a result, they produced some clusters that showed stronger spatial correlations with known mineral occurrences compared to those generated by K-means and GMM.

To quantitatively assess the relationship between each cluster and known mineral occurrences, the normalized density index was computed. Based on this index, the weight of each cluster in each prospectivity was quantified (Fig. 9), which allows distinguished anomalous clusters from background ones. For example, in the DEC-GMM model, the anomalous cluster is shown in red (Fig. 8d), shows a strong spatial correlation with known mineral occurrences. Also, in terms of lithological correspondence, the anomalous cluster is associated with some units, such as volcanic and intrusive rock units. Compared to traditional methods like K-means and GMM, and the DEC-K-means model, the DEC-GMM approach better identifies mineralization-related zones and captures zonal patterns and cyclic structures.

The weight of each cluster in (a) K-means model, (b) GMM model, (c) DEC-K-means model, and (d) DEC-GMM model.

To analyze how DEC-based methods improve the success rate of mineral exploration targeting, the prediction-area (P-A) plot67,96 was used (Fig. 10). The P-A plot consists of two curves, and the intersection point of these two curves can be used as a useful criterion for assessing the result. The higher intersection point demonstrated the better performance of the model in predicting known mineral occurrences. The results demonstrated that the prediction rate of 66%, 68%, 69%, and 72%, for K-means, GMM, DEC-K-means, and DEC-GMM, respectively. Based on these results, it is observed that DEC-based clustering models improve the success rate of mineral exploration targeting. Among all models, the DEC-GMM model achieved the highest prediction rate of 72%, meaning it was able to predict 72% of known mineral occurrences in 28% of the study area.

The P-A plot of (a) K-means model, (b) GMM model, (c) DEC-K-means model, and (d) DEC-GMM model.

After applying four clustering models to integrate porphyry copper datasets in this study area, CI which is a new ensemble technique, was employed to benefits the advantages of four clustering methods while considering point that the main goal of mineral exploration targeting is to recognize regions with highest potential and lowest uncertainty for the detection of new mineral deposits. For this end, the maps of mean and standard deviation of four prospectivity models were generated (Fig. 11a and b). Then, based on Eq. 1, the CI map was generated.

Maps were generated using Python 3 (a) the mean of the applied models, (b) the standard deviation of the applied models, (c) the confidence index prospectivity model, and (d) the final targets after applying the percentile method.

In the raster map obtained (Fig. 11c), cells with higher CI values delineate more reliable target areas, as these cells show high prospectivity values with low variation, thus leading to greater confidence in mineralization prediction. Although such areas can be considered as exploration targets, the task of delimiting them remains complex, which poses one question: What threshold value is suitable to discriminate the targets for follow-up mineral exploration efforts? To address this question, a commonly accepted statistical practice, the namely percentile threshold (90th percentile), was applied to generate a binary prospectivity model (Fig. 11d). As shown in Fig. 9d, several new targets were identified which these targets have highly prospective zones with increased confidence and reduced uncertainty. The majority of identified targets show a strong positive correlation with volcanic units, suggesting that these areas have high potential for the discovery of new porphyry copper deposits, which may be overlain and concealed beneath volcanic rocks, consistent with the classic model. Additionally, some targets located near the boundaries of the intrusive rocks also indicate promising potential for porphyry copper exploration. As a result, the integration of clustering methods, the CI, and the percentile threshold effectively reduced the search space areas under exploration activities and identified new exploration targets with low uncertainty for follow-up mineral exploration programs.

Discussion

Mineralization-controlling geological processes, such as hydrothermal, magmatic, and metamorphic events, are complex and operate across a range of geological scales and times7,74,87. Given that direct observation of the entire subsurface geological features is not possible for exploration geologists and that there are poorly understood and/or unknown mineralization-related processes, modeling all the underlying ore formation subsystems is an uphill struggle. Furthermore, the complexity of the processes and the difficulty in understanding the subsystems make the mineral exploration targeting task a complex process and challenging practice. This may be one of the most important reasons why geoscientists have pointed out declining success rates in mineral exploration7,97, especially where the search space for new mineral deposits moves from the surface to the greater depths18,98,99.

Exploration efforts in new search spaces require innovative strategies struggling with the detect of the subtle and reliable footprints linked to the targeted mineral deposit. In this regard, integrating the data fusion capabilities of ML and DL algorithms13,100 in conjunction with novel concepts such as CI19 and survival bias18 provides an effective approach to reveal patterns among geospatial datasets and to recognize more reliable targets with lower uncertainty for follow-up exploration. Accordingly, the hybrid framework, a deep clustering method called DEC-GMM, was proposed in this study.

This method, which requires no prior assumptions about the data distribution, is designed to improve the integration of evidence layers representing ore-forming subsystems and to support the discovery of new mineral deposits. Comparison of the prediction rate of the DEC-GMM prospectivity model with that of other traditional clustering models (i.e., K-means, GMM, and DEC-K-means) demonstrated that K-means and GMM, which are widely used in MPM, fail to effectively model high-dimensional and complex geospatial data. DEC-K-means, through incorporating feature learning, improves the modeling performance, but it still inherits some limitations from the K-means algorithm. In contrast, the DEC-GMM method combines deep feature representation with probabilistic clustering, which offers more flexibility and a better ability to capture subtle mineralization-related patterns. According to the result of the P-A plot, the DEC-GMM achieved a prediction rate of 72%, which is better than classical clustering approaches.

Despite these improvements, no single model can fully handle and capture the complexity of ore-forming subsystems or effectively integrate all relevant evidence layers5,101. As a result, the outputs of different clustering methods often vary due to the different behavior of each algorithm in processing evidence layers and in the detection of mineralization-related anomalies/patterns. To address this issue, we applied the CI19 as an ensemble strategy that combines the strengths of various clustering models to mitigate the uncertainty in the MPM results. The CI provides an interpretable measure of model agreement, helping to identify targets with lower uncertainty. This ensemble approach delimits the search spaces and increases the likelihood of discovering new mineral deposits, especially those hidden beneath cover or associated with weak signals.

Since MPM is essentially an area-reduction process aiming at identifying new (rather than previously known) deposits, we applied a percentile-based method to convert the CI model into a binary prospectivity map. Areas above the 90th percentile were classified as high-confidence targets, reflecting zones with low uncertainty for the presence of mineralization. In the final prospectivity model, there are recognized targets with few mineralization features and no known mineral deposits. According to the survival bias concept18, such areas may represent opportunities for new discoveries at greater depth. Thus, the targets identified in this study should not be ignored due to the absence of obvious mineralization signals and lack of known deposits. Instead, based on the survival bias concept, it is reasonable to further analyze these targets. Overall, the proposed hybrid framework offers a decision-making tool supporting the follow-up mineral exploration practices in regions where the ore-forming signals are weak and subtle.

The framework developed in this study has the potential to be applied in other regions and for different types of mineralization, and it can also be employed to generate exploration targets for follow-up mineral exploration programs. However, it should be noted that the hyperparameters adopted for clustering algorithms in this study may not necessarily be optimal for other regions or deposit types. Therefore, these hyperparameters, which play a critical role in the performance of the algorithms, should be carefully tuned and adapted to the specific geological setting and data characteristics of each new study area.

Conclusion remarks

In this study, a hybrid framework was developed to leverage the advantages of four machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) clustering methods in capturing nonlinear geo patterns and integrating evidence layers representing the key process of ore-forming subsystems, with the aim of identifying porphyry copper mineralization patterns in the Shahr-e-Babak region, Iran. The methods that were applied and adapted are K-means, Gaussian mixture model (GMM), deep embedded clustering with K-means (DEC-K-means), and deep embedded clustering with Gaussian mixture model (DEC-GMM).
