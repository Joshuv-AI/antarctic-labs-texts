Effects of Monocular Perceptual Learning on Eye-Specific Cortical Activity in Residual Anisometropic Amblyopia: Evidence From EEG Asymmetry
Authors: Zixuan Xu, Yusong Zhou, Yangfei Pang, Yunsi He, Wentong Yu, Yudan Zhong, Qiuying Li, Qingqing Ye, Junpeng Yuan, Xuan Qiu, Yun Wen, Jinrong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to investigate whether a 30-day monocular perceptual learning (PL) program improves visual function and cortical activity in children with residual anisometropic amblyopia.MethodsIn this prospective interventional study, 38 patients (8–15 years old) with monocular residual anisometropic amblyopia completed a 30-day home-based monocular PL program using the lateral masking paradigm. Visual function included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), area under the log contrast sensitivity function (AULCSF), and stereopsis. Five-minute resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) recordings were obtained at baseline and post-training. Alpha-band (8–13 hertz [Hz]) power spectral density (PSD) was computed for four cortical regions of interest (frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital) in hemispheres ipsilateral to the amblyopic eye (AE) and the fellow eye (FE). Interocular differences (IODs) in visual function and interhemispheric differences (IHDs) in neural metrics were calculated, and correlations were assessed. Participants were stratified into responders (BCVA improvement > 1 line) and nonresponders to explore baseline characteristics associated with treatment response.ResultsAt baseline, AE visual function and alpha-band PSD in the ipsilateral parietal and temporal cortices were significantly lower than those of the FE. PL improved AE BCVA and AULCSF, accompanied by PSD reductions in the parietal, frontal, and occipital regions, and shifted parietal and temporal IHD-PSD toward interocular balance. Visual gains correlated with FE-ipsilateral parietal PSD changes, whereas interocular differences in BCVA and AULCSF correlated with parietal IHD-PSD. Baseline parietal IHD-PSD distinguished responders from nonresponders.ConclusionsResidual anisometropic amblyopia is characterized by both visual and cortical interocular asymmetry. Monocular PL enhances visual function and rebalances parietal PSD. Notably, baseline parietal IHD-PSD is associated with responder status at the group level, serving as a candidate neural correlate of training responsiveness. Electrophysiological measures may complement conventional visual tests and inform future work aimed at individualized rehabilitation.

Amblyopia is one of the most common developmental visual disorders in childhood, with a prevalence ranging from 1% to 6% in population-based studies.1–3 Despite the effectiveness of conventional therapies, such as optical correction and occlusion, only approximately 30% of patients achieve complete resolution with occlusion therapy,4 largely due to delayed detection, poor response to initial treatment, recurrence of acuity loss after cessation of therapy, or only partial remediation of the deficit.5 As a result, a substantial proportion of individuals continue to suffer from amblyopia later in life, a condition referred to as residual amblyopia, which may persist into adulthood and negatively affect quality of life.2,6

Perceptual learning (PL), which is based on plasticity in the visual system, refers to permanent and consistent improvements in sensory tasks’ performance through repeated experience or practice.7 Neural plasticity in the visual system of adults with amblyopia, including those with anisometropia and/or strabismus, has also been clearly demonstrated.8 Recent studies further showed that PL can improve a variety of visual functions in adults with amblyopia,9–11 supporting its potential as a therapeutic strategy for treatment-resistant cases.

Electrophysiological studies and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have demonstrated that PL-induced visual improvements are associated with top-down attentional control,12,13 reweighted responses of neurons activated by the trained stimulus14 and engaged high-level cognitive compensation.15 In addition, evidence has suggested that the degree of PL transfer may depend on an individual's brain state during stimulus processing,16 indicating that baseline neural activity may influence the extent of PL transfer. Electroencephalography (EEG) is a noninvasive technique for recording brain electrical activity and has been widely applied to the study of visual developmental disorders such as amblyopia.17 In particular, oscillatory activity in the alpha frequency band (8–13 hertz [Hz]) has been closely linked to visual attention.16 Lower alpha power has been associated with enhanced discrimination of brief visual stimuli,18 modulation by attended stimulus features,19 and improved performance in high-contrast detection tasks.20 Collectively, these findings indicated that alpha spectral power is generally reduced with greater effort and attention to visual tasks, making it a useful neural marker for evaluating cortical plasticity in response to PL. Hou et al. used EEG source imaging and demonstrated that dichoptic attention training in adults with strabismic or anisometropic amblyopia enhanced attentional modulation in both the primary visual cortex (V1) and the intraparietal sulcus.21 Building on these findings, EEG provides a practical tool for investigating the neural mechanisms of amblyopia. Yet, rarely have studies systematically examined how PL influences both visual function and neural activity in children with residual anisometropic amblyopia.

Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of a 30-day monocular PL program on both visual function and neural activity in participants with residual anisometropic amblyopia following a previous course of therapy, with a particular focus on alpha-band EEG as a neural marker. By linking behavioral improvements with electrophysiological changes, this study seeks to provide new insights into the neural mechanisms of residual anisometropic amblyopia and support the development of more effective rehabilitation strategies.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective, interventional study was conducted at the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center, Guangzhou, between March 8, 2021, and March 8, 2023. All participants underwent a 30-day home-based monocular PL therapy. Visual function assessments, including best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), contrast sensitivity function (CSF), refraction and stereopsis assessment, and a 5-minute resting-state EEG assessment were recorded for all subjects at baseline and for those in the treatment groups after treatment. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Zhongshan Ophthalmic Center Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, including guardians of participants younger than 18 years of age.

Participants

Inclusion criteria for all participants were: (1) aged 8 to 18 years, (2) only right-handed participants were enrolled to minimize variability in hemispheric lateralization that may influence EEG measures,22 (3) enrolled in age-appropriate kindergarten or primary education, (4) raised in a Mandarin-speaking environment, (5) prior diagnosis of monocular anisometropic amblyopia, (6) completion of occlusion therapy at least 6 months prior to the intervention, with no further improvement of visual acuity (VA) during a minimum 6-month post-occlusion observation period under full refractive correction (spectacles or rigid gas-permeable contact lenses), and (7) present residual anisometropic amblyopia at enrollment, with BCVA worse than logMAR 0.3 (20/40) in one eye with an interocular difference ≥0.10 logMAR.6 Exclusion criteria included: (1) combined with other organic ophthalmic diseases, (2) neurological, psychiatric, or systemic disorders, (3) mental or developmental delay, and (4) inability to cooperate with required assessments.

Monocular Perceptual Learning Therapy

Monocular PL training was implemented using a commercial perceptual learning software (RevitalVision, Talshir, Israel), which is based on the lateral masking paradigm. Visual stimuli were presented on an LCD monitor with a refresh rate of 60 Hz and an 8-bit luminance resolution. The core stimulus was a Gabor patch, modeled as a cosine wave with edges blurred by a stationary Gaussian filter (Fig. 1A).

Perceptual learning stimuli and lateral masking paradigm. (A) Gabor patch, an effective stimulus for the visual system that can mimic the receptive field structure of the primary visual cortex (area V1). (B) Schematic diagram of lateral masking: the stimuli consist of a Gabor patch target, whereas below and above the Gabor there are two high-contrast collinear Gabor patches as the flankers.

Each stimulus set consisted of a low-contrast central Gabor patch presented at the fixation point, flanked by two relatively high-contrast collinear Gabor patches positioned above and below the central target (Fig. 1B). Stimulus parameters, including spatial frequency, contrast level, orientation, and spatial arrangement, were presented in individualized and adaptive configurations. These parameters were dynamically adjusted by the RevitalVision algorithm according to each participant's responses, allowing task difficulty to be continuously updated throughout the training session. The algorithm operates on a central server, which calculates participant performance and transmits updated training parameters to the local workstation in real time.

Contrast threshold tracking during training was achieved using a three-down/one-up staircase procedure embedded within the RevitalVision software, combined with a temporal two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) paradigm. For the 2AFC task, participants were presented with two intervals: one containing a central Gabor patch with low contrast, flanked by two collinear Gabors with high contrast, and the other containing only the high-contrast collinear Gabors. Participants were required to identify which interval included the low-contrast central Gabor patch. Stimulus presentation duration initially began at 320 ms and was adaptively reduced by the software, within a range of 80 to 320 ms, based on participant performance to maintain task difficulty near perceptual threshold, providing an adaptive challenge to enhance perceptual learning outcomes.

Participants were seated at a viewing distance of 150 cm from the monitor in a darkened room and responded using mouse buttons. The training protocol consisted of daily home-based sessions conducted over a 30-day period. Each session comprised 9 blocks totaling 900 trials and lasted approximately 35 to 45 minutes. Training was performed on a personal computer connected to the central RevitalVision database server.

At enrollment, all participants underwent cycloplegic refraction to confirm refractive status. Full refractive correction was ensured and, when necessary, updated, and was stable for at least 3 months before baseline visual testing and the initiation of perceptual learning. Spectacles were used for correction, and rigid gas-permeable (RGP) contact lenses were prescribed when clinically indicated for moderate-to-high astigmatism to optimize optical correction and minimize meridional blur. The treatment course lasted for 1 month, with daily training sessions divided into 9 blocks, totaling 900 trials per session, each lasting approximately 35 to 45 minutes. The 30-day training was conducted daily at home on a personal computer connected to the central database server.

Physicians assessed patient adherence based on their training performance. Specifically, the RevitalVision software generates a training score after each session, which is weighted across three dimensions: accuracy, training quality, and stability. Accuracy refers to the proportion of correct responses. Training quality is determined by the system's capture of the participant's threshold values (e.g., spatial frequency, contrast, stimulation duration, etc.) during the training, which are then compared against large-scale data to derive a performance score relative to others within the same demographic group. Stability is assessed through an algorithm that evaluates the participant’s rate of “misoperations.” Misoperations refer to incorrect responses on subthreshold catch trials, that is, trials in which the stimuli should not be visible under normal viewing conditions. Such responses indicate lapses of attention or inconsistent task engagement and are used by the software to monitor response reliability. Participants with fewer misoperations and a more consistent response pattern receive a higher stability score. A higher score in this dimension is awarded when the participant demonstrates fewer misoperations and maintains a stable response pattern. The training score ranges from 0 to 100. If the patient’s training score falls below 80, the physician will contact the participant via phone or other communication methods to remind them to complete the training tasks.

Refraction and Visual Acuity

The refractive error was determined using cycloplegic refraction. BCVA was assessed using a Tumbling-E Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart (WEHEN Vision, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) from a distance of 4 meters under a luminance of 200 cd/m² and expressed in logMAR units. The charts consist of 5 optotypes per line for a total of 12 lines, with optotype size increasing from −0.3 logMAR to 1.0 logMAR in steps of 0.1 logMAR. Each falsely identified optotype will add 0.02 logMAR in BCVA. All participants were first measured on the right eye, followed by the left eye.

Contrast Sensitivity Function

CSF was measured using the quick CSF method (qCSF) using the Manifold Contrast Vision Meter (Adaptive Sensory Technology [AST], San Diego, CA, USA), a computerized system that includes a small-form factor Intel PC and a large-format NEC LCD P463 monitor (46-inch LED screen, luminance: 95.4 cd/m2, 60 Hz), with a gamma-corrected resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. The viewing distance was set at 400 cm, allowing for the display of optotypes that range in spatial frequency from 1.4 to 36.2 cycles per degree (cpd). During testing, three filtered Sloan letters of the same spatial frequency are simultaneously displayed in a horizontal line on the LED screen with decreasing contrast. The contrast of the rightmost letter, selected by the qCSF algorithm, was near threshold contrast, with the middle and left letters displayed at two and four times the contrast of the right letter, respectively. Patients verbally reported the letters displayed to the examiner, who operated the test using a handheld tablet to record responses as “correct,” “incorrect,” or “no response.”

The qCSF uses an adaptive Bayesian active learning algorithm with a one-step-ahead search to identify the next optimal stimulus, maximizing expected information gain and enabling data collected at single spatial frequencies to improve sensitivity estimates across the entire frequency range. The algorithm dynamically adjusts the contrast and spatial frequency of the stimuli based on each subject’s feedback, providing a personalized assessment. The full test comprises 25 trials and typically lasts between 3 and 5 minutes, efficiently estimating the global shape of the contrast sensitivity function rather than local estimations at individual frequencies.

The primary outcome measure of the qCSF provides a comprehensive measure of spatial vision, represented by the area under the logarithm of the contrast sensitivity function (AULCSF), which represents CSF estimates integrated across spatial frequencies ranging from 1.5 cpd to 18 cpd.23 All participants were measured on each eye individually. To minimize the potential impact of learning effects on the results, all participants completed three practice qCSF tests using the fellow eye before formal assessment, and qCSF measurements were performed in a fixed order, with the fellow eye tested first followed by the amblyopic eye.

Stereopsis

Near stereoacuity was measured at 40 cm with the Random Dot Stereo Acuity Test (Vision Assessment Corporation, Elk Grove Village, IL, USA) with the circle and symbol targets (test plates with monocular cues) spanning disparities from 12.5 to 400 arc-seconds. For results where near stereoacuity exceeded 160 arc-seconds, we recorded them as nil, because the patterns could be recognized under monocular conditions.24 Distance stereoacuity was measured using the Randot Stereoacuity Tests (Stereo Optical Co., Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) at 4 m, including disparities ranging from 60 to 400 arc-seconds with no monocular cues. Each measurement was repeated twice. Nil stereopsis was recorded as 10,000 arc-seconds. A logarithmic transformation with a base of 10 was applied to the stereopsis measurement results.

Electroencephalography Recording and Preprocessing

During the EEG recording, participants were instructed to sit comfortably in a sound-shielded room, remain relaxed and awake, and to minimize eye-movement throughout the recording. Resting-state EEGs were recorded for 5 minutes using a BioSemi ActiveTwo system (BioSemi B.V., Amsterdam, The Netherlands) in an eyes-closed paradigm. This setup included a 64-channel BioSemi headcap, a BioSemi ActiveTwo amplifier, and an ActiveTwo analog-to-digital conversion box, ensuring comprehensive data acquisition and recording. Electrode gel was applied to keep electrode impedances below 50 kΩ. Data acquisition was managed using the ActiView 8.06 software system.

EEG signals were preprocessed using MATLAB R2021a software (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), with in-house scripts and some functions from the EEGlab toolbox version 2023 (https://sccn.ucsd.edu/eeglab/index.php). Raw data were downsampled offline to 1000 Hz, and filtered with a 0.05 to 100 Hz digital bandpass. Bad channels were topographically interpolated, and data were segmented into 2-second segments, and re-referenced to bilateral mastoid. Epochs containing EEG data with amplitudes outside the 30 standard deviation range were removed. Eye blinks, saccades, and muscular and cardiac artifacts were removed using the independent component analysis tool from EEGlab.

In addition, brain EEG spectral content was measured by power spectral density (PSD), calculated via Welch's method using Fast Fourier Transform. PSD was calculated for each epoch and electrode in the alpha frequency band (8–13 Hz). Last, the PSD from all participants was subject-wise normalized. Subsequently, electrodes were grouped into four regions of interest (ROIs): frontal (left: Fp1, AF7, AF3, F7, F5, and F3, and right: Fp2, AF4, AF8, F4, F6, and F8), parietal (left: CP3, CP5, P3, P5, and P7, and right: CP4, CP6, P4, P6, and P8), temporal (left: FT7, T7, and TP7 and right: FT8, T8, and TP8), and occipital (left: PO7, PO3, and O1 and right: PO4, PO8, and O2; Fig. 2).

Electrode grouping by cortical regions of interest. Electrodes were grouped into four cortical regions of interest: red box = frontal region, orange box = parietal region, green box = temporal region, and blue box = occipital region.

Because participants in this study had either left- or right-eye amblyopia, brain regions were defined relative to the amblyopic eye rather than by absolute left or right hemisphere. Specifically, if the left eye was the amblyopic eye, the left hemisphere was designated as the amblyopic eye–ipsilateral region and the right hemisphere as the fellow eye–ipsilateral region; conversely, if the right eye was the amblyopic eye, the right hemisphere was designated as the amblyopic eye–ipsilateral region and the left hemisphere as the fellow eye–ipsilateral region.

Interocular and Interhemispheric Asymmetry Measures

Interocular differences (IODs) in visual function were calculated as the difference between fellow-eye and amblyopic-eye measures (FE–AE). For EEG metrics, cortical hemispheres were defined relative to the amblyopic eye. Interhemispheric differences (IHDs) in PSD were calculated as the difference between PSD values in cortical regions ipsilateral to the fellow eye and those ipsilateral to the amblyopic eye (FE-ipsilateral–AE-ipsilateral).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Shapiro–Wilks normality tests were performed for continuous variables. Depending on their distribution, continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range). Demographic and clinical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s Exact test for frequency distributions, whereas the Mann-Whitney or independent Student's t-test was applied for comparisons of median and mean values, respectively. All metrics measured before and after training were compared with paired t-tests or paired nonparametric tests based on the data distribution. Pearson’s test was applied to assess correlations. All participants were divided into two groups based on a one-line BCVA improvement. Baseline demographic, visual, and neural measures, including age, sex, amblyopic-eye BCVA, and AULCSF, as well as IOD-BCVA, IOD-AULCSF, and IHD-PSD across frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions, were compared between groups using independent t-tests. A confidence interval (CI) of 95% was applied, with the significance threshold set at α = 0.05.

Results

Baseline Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

A total of 38 patients with monocular residual anisometropic amblyopia (22 male patients and 16 female patients; mean age = 9.95 ± 2.09 years, range = 8–15 years) were enrolled in this study. Among them, 19 patients had right-eye residual anisometropic amblyopia, and 19 had left-eye residual anisometropic amblyopia. According to the training records generated by the RevitalVision system, all participants maintained adequate adherence throughout the home-based training period, with training scores consistently meeting the predefined compliance threshold, ranging from 80 to 100. No participants were excluded from the analysis due to poor compliance. At baseline, mean BCVA was 0.86 ± 0.16 logMAR in the amblyopic eye and −0.01 ± 0.06 logMAR in the fellow eye, and mean AULCSF was 0.22 ± 0.13 in the amblyopic eye and 1.32 ± 0.14 in the fellow eye. At baseline, none of the participants demonstrated measurable distance stereoacuity, resulting in a mean distance stereoacuity of 4.00 ± 0.00 log arc-seconds. Only one participant showed measurable near stereoacuity, resulting in a mean near stereoacuity of 3.94 ± 0.36 log arc-seconds. Detailed baseline clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Effects of Monocular Perceptual Learning

Visual Function

Following 30 days of monocular perceptual learning, BCVA of amblyopic eye improved significantly from 0.86 ± 0.16 to 0.76 ± 0.18 logMAR (mean difference = −0.10, 95% CI = −0.13 to −0.06, P < 0.001), and AULCSF of amblyopic eye increased from 0.22 ± 0.13 to 0.35 ± 0.19 (mean difference = 0.14, 95% CI = 0.10 to 0.18, P < 0.001; Table 3; Fig. 4). No significant changes were observed in BCVA or AULCSF of fellow eye (all P > 0.05). In contrast, stereoacuity remained unchanged following training, with no improvement detected in either near or distance stereoacuity. Furthermore, none of the participants who exhibited no stereopsis prior to training regained stereoacuity after training.

Cortical Activity

Cortical activity showed significant post-training PSD reductions in multiple ROIs in the frontal and parietal regions ipsilateral to both AE and FE, the AE-ipsilateral occipital region, and the FE-ipsilateral temporal and occipital regions (all P < 0.05). IHD-PSD in the parietal region decreased from 9.41 ± 8.73 to 3.61 ± 13.51 (mean difference = −5.80, 95% CI = −10.13 to −1.47, P = 0.010), and in the temporal region from 5.48 ± 8.12 to 0.82 ± 9.05 (mean difference = −4.67, 95% CI = −7.16 to −2.18, P = 0.001), indicating a shift from imbalance toward greater hemispheric symmetry (see Table 3; Fig. 4).

Improvements in BCVA and AULCSF of the AE were significantly correlated with PSD reductions in the FE-ipsilateral parietal region (BCVA: r = 0.34, 95% CI = 0.02 to 0.59, P = 0.038; AULCSF: r = −0.39, 95% CI = −0.63 to −0.08, P = 0.016; Figs. 5A, 5B). Similarly, the changes in interocular differences in BCVA and AULCSF were also correlated with the changes in IHD-PSD in parietal region (IOD-BCVA: r = −0.57, 95% CI = −0.75 to −0.30, P < 0.001; IOD-AULCSF: r = 0.36, 95% CI = 0.05 to 0.61, P = 0.026; Figs. 5C, 5D).

Difference Between the Groups

Participants were classified into a group with a BCVA improvement of >1 line (n = 17) and a group with an improvement of ≤1 line (n = 21). Baseline demographic, visual, and neural measures by treatment outcome are summarized in Table 4. A significant between-group difference was observed only in the parietal IHD-PSD, which was greater in the >1-line improvement group compared with the ≤1-line improvement group (mean difference = 9.03, 95% CI = 4.44 to 13.62, P < 0.001). All other baseline characteristics—including age, sex, amblyopic eye BCVA, and AULCSF, as well as IOD-BCVA, IOD-AULCSF, and IHD-PSD in the frontal, temporal, and occipital regions—showed no significant differences between the two groups (all P > 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, children with monocular residual anisometropic amblyopia showed significant baseline interocular differences in both visual and cortical function. A 30-day monocular perceptual learning improved BCVA and AULCSF, accompanied by PSD reductions in multiple cortical regions and a shift of interhemispheric PSD differences toward balance. Improvements in amblyopic-eye visual function were correlated with PSD changes in the parietal cortex contralateral to the trained eye, and baseline PSD asymmetry in this region differed between treatment responders and nonresponders at the population level.

In the present study, monocular PL improved BCVA and contrast sensitivity in children with residual anisometropic amblyopia, supporting the notion that visual function can still be enhanced beyond the critical period. The mean BCVA improvement after approximately 30 days of training was 0.1 logMAR, which is consistent with prior PL studies in similarly aged children with anisometropic amblyopia following conventional therapy.25–28 The concurrent increase in AULCSF indicates functional enhancement in spatial vision beyond letter acuity alone. Previous studies have shown that PL can improve visual function in patients with amblyopia who are beyond the critical period of visual development,29–31 In our study, participants with residual amblyopia achieved visual gains through PL training, suggesting that patients who are unresponsive to traditional treatments still retain a degree of visual plasticity.

For binocular function, stereoacuity did not show detectable change following 30 days of monocular perceptual learning. This likely reflects the persistence of binocular deficits despite improvements in monocular measures even after conventional treatment, as gains in VA or contrast sensitivity do not necessarily translate into recovery of stereopsis. Notably, behavioral interocular suppression was not explicitly quantified in the current study. Assessing suppression using graded tools, such as neutral density filters or a Bagolini filter bar, serves as a critical measure for evaluating interocular inhibition and the re-establishment of binocular balance. Future investigations into residual amblyopia should incorporate quantitative suppression assessments to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of both visual function loss and the extent of functional recovery.

Training-induced cortical changes followed two patterns. Parietal and temporal cortices exhibited baseline interhemispheric asymmetry, with greater post-training changes in the hemisphere contralateral to the amblyopic eye; this imbalance tended toward symmetry after training, suggesting rebalancing of lateralized regions involved in spatial attention and higher-order integration. In contrast, frontal and occipital cortices showed no baseline asymmetry and underwent symmetrical PSD reductions, reflecting their bilateral involvement in early visual and executive processes. These findings align with prior work suggesting that functional improvements in amblyopia are not confined to primary visual cortex but instead involve distributed cortical networks,32 supporting a network-level account of residual visual plasticity.

In line with prior studies, we observed a post-training decrease in alpha-band PSD, an index of cortical desynchronization and reduced inhibitory gating that supports more efficient visual processing and attention allocation.16 Alpha suppression has been linked to attentional deployment and increased cortical excitability,33,34 and similar reductions in alpha power have been reported in amblyopia following push–pull/dichoptic training.35 In the present study, improvements in BCVA and AULCSF of AE were significantly correlated with PSD changes in the FE-ipsilateral parietal cortex, but not the amblyopic-eye side, suggesting that functional recovery depends on bilateral network-level rebalancing rather than modulation restricted to the amblyopic hemisphere.

After perceptual learning training, the PSD imbalance between the AE- and FE-ipsilateral parietal and temporal regions was reduced, putatively reflecting more balanced cortical activation across hemispheres. This finding suggests that PL not only enhances the function of the trained eye but also promotes broader interhemispheric normalization of cortical processing. Furthermore, reductions in parietal IHD-PSD were correlated with improvements in IOD-BCVA and IOD-AULCSF, suggesting that monocular training can modulate bilateral visual processing and that rebalancing neural activity between hemispheres may be a key mechanism underlying visual recovery.36,37

Animal model studies of naturally strabismic and amblyopic monkeys have revealed anatomic asymmetries related to the visual pathways.38 However, whether patients with residual anisometropic amblyopia exhibit interhemispheric asymmetry in neural electrical activity has remained unclear. By defining anatomically eye-specific PSD (AE-ipsilateral versus FE-ipsilateral), the present study provides electrophysiological evidence consistent with fMRI findings, showing that brain function asymmetry captures aspects of impairment beyond standard visual metrics. At baseline, AE-ipsilateral parietal and temporal cortices showed lower alpha PSD than FE-ipsilateral counterparts, paralleling visual function asymmetry. However, these baseline visual and neural asymmetries were not significantly correlated, suggesting that neural asymmetry reflects additional dimensions of impairment not fully captured by standard visual metrics. This implies that latent neural plasticity may underlie differential treatment responsiveness even in patients with similar visual deficits.

Baseline parietal PSD asymmetry differed significantly between responders and nonresponders, indicating a group-level neural marker associated with training responsiveness. In contrast, baseline visual metrics did not distinguish these groups. In a cohort with relatively homogeneous baseline VA, parietal PSD asymmetry may therefore provide complementary information for understanding variability in training effects in research settings. These observations support the notion that the extent of PL transfer may depend on an individual's baseline neural state during stimulus processing. This finding may provide useful reference for future studies aimed at outcome stratification and the development of individualized treatment strategies for residual amblyopia.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size was modest and derived from a single center, which may limit generalizability, including the ability to detect subtle age-related effects. The relatively short training duration and follow-up period may also constrain the magnitude and durability of observed visual gains. Second, the electrophysiological assessment consisted solely of resting-state EEG. Whereas this offered insights into intrinsic network activity, it provides limited topographical spatial resolution compared to fMRI. Furthermore, pattern visual evoked potentials (pVEP), which serve as a direct objective metric for functional improvements in primary visual cortical (V1/V2) processing, enabling us to directly correlate primary visual processing with higher-level, more networked brain activity, should be considered in the further research. Third, although RGP lenses were prescribed to maximally mitigate the impact of high astigmatism on visual function and perceptual learning, the potential influence of astigmatism on study outcomes should still be considered in future investigations. Future studies with larger cohorts using age-stratified designs, longer training protocols, binocularly targeted interventions, and multimodal neurophysiological assessments (combining pVEP and resting-state EEG) will be important for further characterizing visual and neural plasticity in residual anisometropic amblyopia.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that monocular PL based on the lateral masking paradigm improves visual function and modulates cortical activity in children with residual anisometropic amblyopia. The findings underscore the importance of interhemispheric PSD differences in the parietal cortex and support the view that the brain's functional state may play a decisive role in determining plasticity potential. Electrophysiological metrics may complement conventional visual assessments by providing group-level markers of training responsiveness. These findings provide a rationale and preliminary evidence to inform future studies designed specifically for robust individual-level prediction.
