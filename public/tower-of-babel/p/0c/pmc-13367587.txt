Comparative analysis of demographic, behavioral, and nutritional correlates of thyroid disorders in the central region of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Abdulrhman A. Almazrou, Abdulelah F. Alshehri, Mohammad A. Majrashi, Meshal Alzakari, Rana A. Alhazzani, Yazeed I. Alfarhan, Faysal K. Bindayel, Fahad Mohammed Alomairini, Zyad Azzam Almishaal, Saeed Bin Ayed Al-Sadain, Turki Ahmed Aljuhani, Saud A. Alarfaj, Bandar M Alotaibi, Osama Ibrahim Alhammad, Abdulmohsen S. Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Thyroid disorders are a significant public health concern in Saudi Arabia, yet data on public awareness and associated behavioral factors remain limited. This study aims to assess and compare the demographic, behavioral, and nutritional awareness of thyroid disorders between healthy individuals and those with a diagnosed condition in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted in September 2025, involving 697 participants aged 18 and older. A validated, self-administered questionnaire was distributed online to assess demographics, medical history, and awareness of thyroid disorders and related nutritional factors. Data were analyzed using RStudio, with Chi-square tests used to examine associations between disease status and awareness variables.Results:The study included 581 healthy participants and 116 with thyroid disorders. Females predominated in the thyroid disease group (88% vs. 50%). Participants with thyroid disorders demonstrated significantly higher awareness of risk factors (P < 0.001), symptoms (P < 0.001), and the role of iodine (P = 0.040). Those with a diagnosis were more likely to cite healthcare professionals as their primary information source (58%), whereas healthy individuals relied on informal sources. Notably, nearly half of all respondents (46.5%) had never been tested for thyroid function.Conclusion:Although individuals with a thyroid disorder have greater knowledge, public awareness among the general population in Riyadh is insufficient, with a heavy reliance on non-professional sources. Targeted educational initiatives are required to improve health literacy, encourage proactive screening, and address misconceptions, particularly among younger adults and males, to reduce the burden of thyroid disease.

Introduction

Thyroid disorders impose a major global health burden as the most prevalent endocrine abnormalities, arising from impaired synthesis or regulation of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), hormones essential for metabolic balance and neurocognitive function.[1] Iodine, a critical trace element, constitutes a structural component of thyroid hormones, and remains indispensable for their biosynthesis and physiological activity.[2] Both deficiency and excess of iodine disturb thyroid homeostasis, predisposing individuals to hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, or goiter, each associated with considerable morbidity and economic costs.[3]

Despite major advances in universal salt iodization, nearly two billion people remain vulnerable to iodine deficiency due to variable fortification standards, reduced seafood intake, and evolving dietary behaviors.[4] Iodine imbalance has also re-emerged in high-income nations, reflecting inconsistent food policies and shifting consumer habits.[5] Across the Middle East and North Africa, thyroid disease prevalence exhibits striking heterogeneity, influenced by nutritional status, healthcare access, and public awareness.[6] These global and regional disparities highlight the need for context-specific investigations that integrate biological and behavioral determinants.

In Saudi Arabia, thyroid disorders represent the most frequently reported endocrine condition, yet their distribution and underlying correlates differ markedly across provinces. Subclinical hypothyroidism is the dominant form, but evidence regarding behavioral and nutritional risk patterns remains limited.[7] Rapid urbanization, declining reliance on iodized salt, and lifestyle transitions may have altered iodine consumption and disease trends in the Central Region. Behavioral determinants such as health-seeking attitudes, dietary preferences, and knowledge of iodized salt use are insufficiently characterized, despite their potential modifiability.

To date, few population-based studies in Saudi Arabia have concurrently examined demographic, behavioral, and nutritional factors associated with thyroid dysfunction within a unified analytic framework. Addressing these gaps is vital for designing preventive strategies and guiding health education under the national Vision 2030 agenda. Therefore, this study aims to identify and compare the demographic, behavioral, and nutritional correlates of thyroid disorders among adults in the Central Region of Saudi Arabia, providing evidence to support targeted iodine sufficiency programs and public awareness interventions.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This analytical cross-sectional study was conducted among adults residing in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, between September 12 and September 29, 2025. The objective was to identify and compare demographic, behavioral, and nutritional factors associated with thyroid disorders by contrasting participants with a prior diagnosis and healthy individuals. The study design and reporting followed the STROBE recommendations for observational studies.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Imam Mohammad ibn Saud Islamic University (approval no. 855/2025; reference HAPO-01-R-061, dated September 10, 2025). Participation was voluntary, and all respondents provided informed electronic consent after reading a clear explanation of the study purpose, data privacy, and withdrawal rights. No identifying information was collected, and all procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population and sampling

The study targeted Saudi adults aged 18 years and older of both genders residing in the Central Region. Individuals who had formal education or employment in medical, pharmaceutical, or health-related fields were excluded to avoid professional knowledge bias. Pregnant women and incomplete responses were also excluded. Participants were recruited through non-probability convenience sampling using open online invitations distributed across social-media platforms such as Twitter, WhatsApp, and university community groups. Although convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, the broad distribution encouraged participation from various demographic backgrounds within Riyadh.

Sample size estimation

The minimum required sample size was calculated using the Raosoft online calculator (Raosoft Inc., Seattle, USA) at a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error, resulting in 384 participants. To increase statistical precision, 697 valid responses were included in the final analysis after excluding incomplete entries.

Study instrument

Data were collected through a structured bilingual (Arabic/English) self-administered questionnaire developed after reviewing previous literature and established thyroid-awareness tools. Two endocrinologists and one internal-medicine consultant evaluated the instrument to ensure content validity, yielding a content validity index (CVI) of 0.89. A pilot test involving 30 participants confirmed clarity and internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α of 0.81. The final 45-item questionnaire comprised three main sections covering sociodemographic characteristics, medical and family history of thyroid disease, and awareness of thyroid function, risk factors, symptoms, and nutrition-related aspects. Awareness was assessed using 11 knowledge-based questions, each scored 1 for a correct response and 0 for an incorrect or “I don’t know” answer. The total score ranged from 0 to 11, representing the overall level of knowledge regarding thyroid disorders.

Data management and quality control

Survey responses were downloaded from the online platform, reviewed for completeness, and coded before analysis. Incomplete or duplicate submissions were removed. Data were manually verified for accuracy and internal consistency prior to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using RStudio version 2024.09.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics, expressed as means ± standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Associations between thyroid-disease status and categorical variables were evaluated using Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests. For significant associations, adjusted standardized residuals were examined to identify those subgroups contributed most to the observed differences. All tests were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Bias control and reproducibility

Selection bias was mitigated through wide dissemination of the survey link across multiple community channels. Recall bias was minimized by employing concise and specific closed-ended questions. The anonymized dataset and questionnaire are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request to support transparency and reproducibility.

Results

A total of 697 participants were analyzed, including 581 healthy individuals and 116 with thyroid disorders. Females predominated among participants with thyroid disease (88%) compared with 50% in the healthy group. Age distribution differed markedly: Most patients with thyroid disorders were 50–59 years (34%), whereas younger adults aged 18–29 years formed the majority among healthy participants (58%). Educational attainment also varied between groups. Participants with thyroid disease mainly held secondary (28%) or bachelor’s degrees (68%), while most healthy individuals possessed a bachelor’s degree (83%). Regarding socioeconomic profile, thyroid disorders were more frequent among participants earning 10,000–20,000 Saudi RIyal (SAR) (38%), whereas almost half of healthy individuals reported monthly incomes below 5,000 SAR (46%). Marital status showed a clear distinction. Most participants with thyroid disorders were married (71%), while healthy participants were predominantly single (61%). Collectively, these findings indicate that thyroid disorders were more prevalent among older, married females with moderate-to-high income and intermediate educational levels [Table 1].

Knowledge of thyroid gland function was higher among respondents with thyroid disease, 72% of whom correctly identified its role in muscle contraction, cardiac regulation, and metabolism, compared with 60% of healthy individuals. However, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.11). Symptom awareness showed a significant association with disease status (P < 0.001). Nearly half of respondents with thyroid disease (48%) correctly identified symptoms of both hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism, compared with only one-third (33%) of healthy participants.

Information sources varied notably between groups (P < 0.001). Participants with thyroid disease were more likely to obtain information from healthcare professionals (58%), whereas healthy individuals relied mainly on family, friends, or online sources (23% each).

Awareness of the thyroid as an endocrine gland and its physiological functions was significantly higher among respondents with thyroid disease (84% vs. 63%, P < 0.001). Similarly, knowledge regarding the association between thyroid dysfunction and nervousness or memory loss, the role of iodine deficiency, and the impact of maternal thyroid disorder on fetal health was significantly greater among respondents with thyroid disease (all P ≤ 0.031). Awareness of hereditary risk and general importance of the thyroid gland showed no significant differences between groups (P = 0.11).

Collectively, these findings indicate that individuals with thyroid disorders demonstrate higher awareness of thyroid function, related symptoms, and biomedical risk factors, yet public knowledge among healthy individuals remains limited, and primarily dependent on informal information sources.

Table 3 compares respondents’ dietary and nutritional awareness according to their thyroid status. Most participants in both groups correctly acknowledged that diet could influence thyroid disorders, with nearly identical proportions among healthy (67%) and affected respondents (68%), indicating no significant difference (P > 0.9). Knowledge of iodine-rich foods differed significantly (P = 0.040). A higher proportion of participants with thyroid disease (64%) correctly identified seafood and seaweeds as the main dietary sources of iodine, compared with 46% of healthy respondents. Despite this awareness, seafood consumption was generally low in both groups, with most participants reporting infrequent or monthly intake. Preferred meat type showed a statistically significant association with disease status (P = 0.029). Participants with thyroid disorders more frequently preferred chicken (52%) and seafood (17%), whereas healthy respondents commonly consumed chicken and red meat (44% and 42%, respectively).

Awareness of symptoms demonstrated distinct patterns. Participants with thyroid disease showed stronger knowledge of hypothyroidism (5.44) and both thyroid conditions (2.29), whereas healthy respondents were more likely to report no symptom knowledge (2.19). Regarding information sources, respondents with thyroid disease were more likely to obtain information from healthcare professionals (6.48), whereas healthy participants more often relied on family and friends (1.58) or reported no prior information (1.63).

Recognition of the thyroid as an endocrine gland was significantly higher among individuals with thyroid disease (2.36), while those without the condition were less likely to identify it correctly (–3.34). For nervousness or memory loss, participants with thyroid disease were more uncertain (2.95) and less likely to associate these symptoms with thyroid dysfunction (–3.67).

Nutritional awareness also differed between groups. Participants with thyroid disease more frequently recognized iodine deficiency as a health problem (2.23) and understood its link to fetal development (1.70). They were also more likely to identify seafood and seaweeds as the primary dietary iodine sources (2.26) and to prefer seafood (1.61) or chicken (1.02) compared with healthy respondents.

Collectively, these residual patterns reinforce that individuals with thyroid disease demonstrated greater biomedical and nutritional awareness, while healthy participants displayed more uncertainty and reliance on informal information sources.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the demographic, behavioral, and nutritional correlates of thyroid disorders among adults in the Central Region of Saudi Arabia. As shown in Table 1, females comprised 88% of participants with thyroid disease, particularly within the 50–59-year age group, whereas healthy individuals were mainly 18–29 years old. Despite a generally moderate level of awareness, only one-third of respondents reported undergoing thyroid-function testing, and nearly half had never been screened [Figure 1]. Participants with thyroid disease exhibited greater knowledge of gland function, symptoms, and iodine-rich foods, while healthy individuals more often relied on informal sources such as family, friends, and social media [Table 2]. Although most respondents agreed that diet could influence thyroid disorders, this association was not statistically significant (P > 0.9). These results demonstrate that awareness, behavior, and disease prevalence are shaped by demographic and informational disparities rather than by knowledge alone.

Our findings align with national and regional literature. A Saudi survey revealed that only 57.3% of respondents had adequate knowledge of thyroid disorders, irrespective of sex or educational level, confirming persistent public gaps in thyroid-health literacy.[8] A study from the Al-Baha region further highlighted environmental and dietary contributors, reporting a higher female prevalence and linking thyroid dysfunction to low seafood consumption and inconsistent iodized-salt use in mountainous areas with naturally low iodine content.[9] In western Libya, 84% of respondents reported using non-iodized salt and demonstrated limited understanding of iodine’s physiological importance, reflecting similar behavioral deficiencies among our healthy participants.[10] Evidence from Ethiopia supports this mechanism, showing that adolescent girls with low dietary diversity and open salt storage had a total goiter rate exceeding 50%, underscoring how preventable nutritional practices can influence thyroid health.[11] Collectively, these comparisons indicate that modifiable dietary and behavioral factors remain central drivers of thyroid dysfunction across Middle Eastern and African populations.

The predominance of females in our cohort likely reflects the interplay of hormonal and autoimmune susceptibilities with cultural patterns influencing healthcare-seeking behavior. Although women reported higher awareness scores, this knowledge did not consistently translate into preventive testing [Figure 1], suggesting motivational or logistical barriers. The higher knowledge observed among affected individuals [Table 2] indicates reactive learning following diagnosis rather than proactive prevention, a pattern consistent with regional studies.[8910] Younger adults and unmarried participants showed less engagement with thyroid-related education, suggesting that current awareness initiatives are not adequately reaching these populations. Furthermore, dietary habits across both groups showed low seafood consumption, demonstrating that knowledge does not necessarily lead to behavioral adherence. This gap highlights the need for community-level interventions that convert awareness into sustained health practices.

From a clinical and public-health perspective, these results emphasize the importance of integrating structured thyroid-screening programs within primary care, particularly for women aged 40 years and above and for individuals with a family history or nonspecific symptoms such as fatigue or weight fluctuation. Clinicians should incorporate concise counseling on iodized-salt use, proper storage, and balanced seafood intake into routine visits. National authorities are encouraged to strengthen salt-iodization policies, ensure compliance monitoring, and promote labeling transparency. Educational programs targeting schools and universities should further emphasize iodine’s critical role in metabolism, fetal development, and neurocognitive function, which remained underrecognized in Table 2 responses.

Strengths and limitations

This study’s strengths include a large and demographically diverse community sample, integration of behavioral, nutritional, and cognitive parameters within a unified analytical framework, and use of validated instruments with adjusted standardized residuals that clarified subgroup differences beyond statistical significance alone. Including both affected and unaffected participants allowed direct comparison within a single population context. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and reliance on self-reported diagnoses may introduce recall bias or misclassification. Online recruitment could have led to selection bias toward younger and more educated respondents, potentially limiting generalizability to less-connected populations. Furthermore, biochemical verification of thyroid function (TSH, FT4), and laboratory assessment of iodine intake were not performed for all participants. Despite these limitations, the internal consistency of findings with previous regional evidence[891011] strengthens their validity and provides a credible foundation for future interventional and longitudinal studies aimed at reducing the thyroid-disease burden in Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

This study provides the first region-specific comparative assessment using adjusted standardized residuals to identify behavioral and nutritional correlates of thyroid disorders in Saudi Arabia. Fewer than one-third of participants had ever undergone thyroid testing, and nearly 90% of affected individuals were women, revealing a critical gender and behavior linked prevention gap. The results emphasize that awareness alone is insufficient unless translated into sustained screening and dietary adherence. Integrating routine TSH screening into primary care and strengthening national iodized-salt monitoring should be prioritized. These findings offer an empirical foundation for developing evidence-based endocrine prevention frameworks and advancing longitudinal research that measures the long-term effectiveness of behavioral and nutritional interventions.
