Reversibility in plethysmographic airway and specific airway resistance in children
Authors: Nicole Beydon, Cécile Du Boisbaudry, Christophe Delclaux, Rola Abou Taam, Iulia Ioan, Stefan Matecki, Jessica Taytard, Marie-Agnès Giroux-Metges, Muriel Le Bourgeois, Maxime Beydon, Plamen Bokov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlethysmographic airway resistance (Raw) and specific airway resistance (sRaw) are widely used to assess baseline lung function and bronchodilator reversibility in children unable to perform spirometry. However, no consensus exists on cut-offs for significant reversibility.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study on a large cohort of asthmatic children tested during panting without electronic thermal compensation, to determine optimal Raw and sRaw cut-offs for detecting significant forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) reversibility. A smaller cohort undergoing tidal breathing plethysmography with electronic thermal compensation was also analysed.ResultsFrom 2436 tests without thermal compensation, receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified cut-offs of −34.4% (Rawtot) and −36.0% (sRawtot) from baseline to detect significant FEV1 reversibility, with sensitivities of 70% (95% CI 65–74%) and 72% (95% CI 67–76%) and specificities of 70% (95% CI 68–72%) and 73% (95% CI 71–75%), respectively. Expressing reversibility as a percentage of predicted did not improve accuracy, but required larger cut-offs (−42.6% for Rawtot, −56.1% for sRawtot) and increased the influence of baseline value. In the 106 tests performed using electronic thermal compensation, Rawtot/eff and sRawtot/eff cut-offs were lower than that without electronic thermal compensation (−25% of baseline) with sensitivities and specificities ranging from 63% to 70%.ConclusionRawtot and sRawtot reversibility effectively identify FEV1 reversibility with cut-offs around −35% of the baseline value, showing no added benefit when using larger cut-offs expressed as a percentage of the predicted value. When electronic thermal compensation is applied, it may necessitate lower cut-offs of −25% of the baseline, compared to those determined without it.

Introduction

Plethysmography measurements of airway resistance (Raw) and specific airway resistance (defined as the product of Raw by lung volume, sRaw) are pulmonary function indices particularly used in paediatrics because children may not produce or may incorrectly perform forced expiration manoeuvres during spirometry [1, 2]. In addition to its greater feasibility compared to spirometry, the plethysmography technique measures airway patency differently than spirometry. Deep inspiration is not required for plethysmography measurements, thereby eliminating the risk of bronchoconstriction induced by deep inspiration [3]. Furthermore, the resistance encountered by airflow directly reflects the airway diameter, making it a highly sensitive index to assess bronchodilation [4, 5].

Asthma is the most prevalent chronic respiratory disease in children. To diagnose and monitor children with asthma, physicians need information about the patient's clinical status and pulmonary function, including baseline bronchial obstruction and significant reversibility after bronchodilator administration [6, 7]. Guidelines typically include only spirometry to define bronchial obstruction (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) to forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio lower than the lower limit of normal), and significant reversibility (post-bronchodilator increase in FVC and/or FEV1 >10 % predicted) [8].

Due to the large interindividual variability of resistance values in healthy subjects, this index is more useful for assessing reversibility than baseline obstruction. However, there is no consensus on the clinically relevant cut-off to use for Raw and sRaw to assess a significant reversibility. Previous studies have used either change in sRaw related to the baseline value (%B) or to the predicted value (%P) with different cut-offs identified (from −22% to −42%) [4, 9, 10]. Unlike FEV1 reversibility, the impact of using %B or %P to express reversibility for Raw and sRaw has not yet been studied. Another challenge emerged with the use of electronic thermal compensation in calculating sRaw, as it adjusts for the volume change between dry, cold inspired air and cool, vapour-saturated expired gases. This digital adjustment significantly affects sRaw, which is dependent on lung volume and highly sensitive to the respiratory rate during the measurement (panting versus tidal breathing) [11]. Consequently, the variability in values when comparing sRaw obtained during panting without electronic thermal compensation complicates the establishment of a unified cut-off applicable to all techniques. Finally, there are different methods to calculate sRaw, two of them (sRawtot and sRaweff) are recommended, and this may also influence the cut-off value of bronchodilator response [1, 12].

We hypothesised that Raw and sRaw reversibility expressed as %B or %P might differ in accuracy for predicting significant FEV1 reversibility and could require different cut-offs due to potential variations in their dependence on clinical factors or baseline values. Additionally, we speculated that plethysmography reversibility measured during panting without electronic thermal compensation could predict significant FEV1 reversibility differently from measurements obtained during tidal breathing with electronic thermal compensation, given that laryngeal closure decreases during panting, but increases during tidal breathing, in parallel with bronchial obstruction [13–15]. In this study, our primary goal was to evaluate the optimal cut-offs for Rawtot and sRawtot, measured without thermal compensation, to predict a significant FEV1 reversibility in a large single-centre paediatric cohort, analysing whether %B or %P provided a more relevant expression. Secondly, we examined the consistency of these established cut-offs for Rawtot and Raweff, and sRawtot and sRaweff when measured with thermal compensation in a multicentre study [12].

Methods

Additional information is provided in the supplementary material.

This retrospective study, which was not pre-planned, utilised the lung function database of all participating centres to identify children with asthma or suspected asthma who had performed baseline and post-bronchodilator sRaw and FEV1 measurements. In all participating centres, children referred for asthma or suspected asthma were coached by a technician who was only aware of the reason for the test, to perform sRaw measurements, attempted plethysmography for static lung volumes, and forced spirometry during the same visit. Children and their parents were informed about the potential retrospective use of anonymised lung function results, and gave oral consent. The study received approval from the institutional review board of the French learned society for respiratory medicine (Société de Pneumologie de Langue Française; identifier number CEPRO 2024-002).

Children refrained from using short-acting bronchodilators for ≥6 h before the test. Trained technicians conducted the tests following the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society recommendations, using a plethysmograph calibrated daily: BodyBox (Médisoft, Serinnes, Belgium) without electronic thermal compensation, or Vyntus Body (E. Jaeger, Würzburg, Germany) with electronic thermal compensation. Plethysmography, including sRaw and Raw measurements, was systematically conducted prior to forced spirometry at baseline and post-bronchodilator in asthmatic children. Measurements obtained without thermal compensation were performed during panting (1–2 Hz), whereas these with electronic thermal compensation were recorded at a targeted respiratory rate of 30–45 breaths·min−1 [12, 16]. Technicians visually inspected each sRaw loops and deleted incorrect loops. The mean of the median of at least three series of ≥10 sRaw loops was recorded. After each series of sRaw, the airway was occluded to measure the thoracic gas volume (TGV), and the device calculated Raw as the ratio of sRaw to TGV. If the child failed to breathe correctly against the closed shutter, only sRaw was available, as described by Klug and Bisgaard [17]. The BodyBox device provided a measurement of sRawtot (calculated using the line connecting the flow points at the maximum change in plethysmography volume), which are hereafter referred to as sRaw, to avoid confusion with the Vyntus Body results, which included also sRawtot and sRaweff (calculated from multiple points throughout the breathing cycle using an integration method) [1, 18].

The spirometry reported was the best curve out of two or three reproducible manoeuvres according to recommendations [19]. Measurements were taken at baseline and 15 min after bronchodilation (400 µg salbutamol).

Analysis

Results are presented as median (interquartile range (IQR)) and mean±sd when normally distributed, or number and percentage. We calculated body mass index (BMI) z-scores using World Health Organization equations, defining overweight as BMI z-scores >1 and ≤2, and obesity for BMI z-score >2, because of the known effect of overweight and obesity on lung function [20, 21].

The reversibility of sRaw or Raw was expressed as %B or %P, and FEV1 reversibility was determined as a >10% predicted improvement using the Global Lung Initiative (GLI) multiethnic (or race-neutral equation) [22–24]. Comparisons between baseline and post-bronchodilator respiratory rates were performed using Mann–Whitney testing. Proportions were compared using the Chi-squared test. Sensitivity and specificity of sRaw reversibility to detect significant FEV1 reversibility were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses. The optimal cut-off was identified as the point on the ROC curve where the sensitivity and specificity were approximately equal, but additional cut-offs favouring sensitivity are also reported. To assess the effect of the magnitude of Raw or sRaw reversibility on the difference between results expressed as %B and %P, we constructed Bland–Altman plots. Associations between the reversibility of Raw or sRaw and factors such as age, sex, height, BMI, inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) treatment, baseline Raw or sRaw values (% predicted), and reversibility of FEV1 were analysed using univariable and multivariable linear models. Standardised β-coefficients were used, with independent continuous variables (all except sex and ICS treatment), being scaled. All these clinically relevant variables were included in the multivariable analysis regardless of their associations in the univariable analysis, as the objective was aetiological rather than predictive. We conducted three sensitivity analyses. First, because of nonlinearity issues between Raw and sRaw reversibility and their baseline values, we repeated the multivariable analysis after natural spline transformation of baseline values. Second, we conducted a mixed-effect linear regression including a subject-level random intercept because of repeated measures in some individuals. Third, we specifically evaluated whether ICS were associated with plethysmography reversibility using a propensity score analysis (supplementary material).

Limits of normal were set at −1.645 z-score for FEV1, FEV1/FVC or specific airway conductance (the inverse of sRaw) and +1.645 z-score for Raw. Statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 6.07; San Diego, CA, USA) and R (version 4.4.1). p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A total of 1471 (60%) lung function tests were performed in children treated with ICS. Discussion

In this retrospective study, we identified the optimal cut-off values for significant reversibility in Raw and sRaw (Rawtot and sRawtot) in asthmatic children to be approximately −35% of the baseline without electronic thermal compensation, and −25% with it (Rawtot, sRawtot and Raweff, sRaweff). In the results obtained without electronic thermal compensation, we found no indication that expressing Raw or sRaw reversibility as %B or %P impacted the accuracy of plethysmography in detecting significant FEV1 reversibility. However, the cut-offs were substantially higher when expressed as %P, and this measure was more affected by the baseline value, supporting the preference for using cut-offs expressed as %B.

Assessing lung function in asthmatic subjects is crucial for the detection of airway obstruction and/or significant post-bronchodilator reversibility; the latter defining asthma in presence of compatible symptoms. In young children unable to perform forced spirometry, non-cooperative techniques, such as plethysmography, oscillometry or the interrupter technique are commonly employed to measure respiratory mechanics. Due to the strong inverse relationship between airway resistance and airway calibre, and the absence of potential bronchoconstriction induced by deep inspiration during measurement, these techniques were found superior to spirometry for assessing post-bronchodilator changes [5, 10].

Among the three techniques for measuring respiratory mechanics, plethysmography has the advantage of specifically measuring airway resistance, whereas oscillometry and the interrupter technique include additional thoracopulmonary components [25]. Therefore, plethysmography is the most relevant technique (best sensitivity/specificity) to discriminate asthmatic from healthy young children, justifying its systematic use in this disease [4]. Despite its widespread use in routine practice, particularly for assessing sRaw in paediatric asthma follow-up, plethysmography remains poorly standardised, with investigators employing varying cut-offs to define significant reversibility [26]. Moreover, the development of electronic thermal compensation has established new reference values for sRaw measured during tidal breathing in children aged 3–10 years, which are nearly twice as high as the previously published values, which should impact the cut-offs used for significant reversibility [12, 24]. This advancement may also affect the evaluation of airway calibre due to variations in glottis aperture associated with differences in respiratory rate during the measurement; specifically, a greater decrease in glottis area during expiration in tidal breathing compared to that observed during panting [13, 27]. The impact of upper airway resistance during tidal breathing, as measured by forced oscillation, has been studied in children in whom expiratory measurements were found higher than inspiratory measurements, consistent with results observed in adult subjects [27]. Moreover, baseline inspiratory and expiratory resistance were significantly higher, and the difference between them was significantly larger in asthmatic children compared to controls, consistent with evidence indicating that bronchoconstriction reduces the area of the glottis [15]. In agreement with these results, we measured higher Raw or sRaw during tidal breathing (i.e. with electronic thermal compensation) and showed a lower cut-off for predicting FEV1 reversibility compared to measurements performed during panting.

Various cut-offs for Raw and sRaw have been reported, influenced by factors such as the age and health status of the study population, as well as the methodology employed to calculate sRaw or to assess significant post-bronchodilator changes. Importantly, we established that using %B or %P did not impact the sensitivity or specificity of Raw and sRaw in detecting FEV1 reversibility, provided that different cut-offs are applied, as previously suggested by Nielsen and Bisgaard [4]. Unlike FEV1 reversibility [8], both Raw and sRaw reversibility, expressed as %B or %P, were significantly associated with the baseline values of Raw or sRaw (table 3). However, similarly to FEV1, the greater the abnormality in the baseline values of Raw or sRaw, the larger the observed reversibility (figure 1) [28]. The stronger relationship between Raw and sRaw baseline values and their reversibility expressed as %P, compared to reversibility expressed as %B (figure 1), is also reflected in the greater dependence of Raw and sRaw reversibility %P on baseline values (table 3). The greater dependence of reversibility expressed as %P on the initial level of obstruction is further illustrated by the higher reversibility cut-off (table 2), which mitigates the risk of false positives. Consequently, the baseline value has a more significant impact on reversibility expressed as %P than on %B, supporting the recommendation to use %B as the preferred choice. The other results of the multivariate analyses revealed that neither age nor height were significant determinants of Raw or sRaw reversibility, while the other variables had little to no effect except for ICS treatment, which was associated with reduced reversibility. This is consistent with previous findings showing less frequent significant FEV1 reversibility in children using ICS [28].

Our study has some limitations. The largest set of measurements was provided from a single centre where six technicians routinely used lung function devices of the same brand, without electronic thermal compensation, to perform the measurements according to international guidelines and under the supervision of paediatric pulmonologists extensively trained in paediatric lung function testing. However, there was no selection of the asthmatic children for undertaking plethysmography before forced spirometry was performed. Consequently, using the same methodology should enable the application of the provided cut-offs. We did not limit our study to a single pulmonary function test per child, as the relationship between the reversibility of FEV1 and Raw or sRaw is not expected to be subject-specific. To support this assumption, we conducted a sensitivity analysis with subject-level random effect in our multivariable linear regression model, which did not alter overall results. In contrast, our multicentre study included a more limited population. However, all centres followed the methodology described by Kirkby et al. [12] to measure Raw and sRaw. Additionally, we ensured consistency in respiratory rate between baseline and post-bronchodilator measurements, as modifications in respiratory rate have been shown to significantly affect sRaw values measured with electronic thermal compensation [11, 17]. We cannot exclude the possibility that the reference values we used for Raw and sRaw measured without electronic thermal compensation were inaccurate, as they were derived from a small single-centre study [24]. Moreover, we were unable to calculate %P values for the measurements obtained using electronic thermal compensation [12]. The former may explain the different relationship observed between baseline Raw and sRaw values and their reversibility when expressed as %P compared to %B. However, the latter may be of limited importance, since in the population tested without electronic thermal compensation, the sensitivity and specificity of Raw and sRaw reversibility were similar whether expressed as %B or %P, with reversibility %B being less dependent on baseline values than reversibility %P. Lastly, our sample was not limited to young children, in whom Raw and sRaw measurements may be particularly valuable when spirometry technique is not yet mastered. Although in young children, FEV1 reflects airway physiology differently (FEV1 being closer to FVC) and volume after a shorter forced expiratory time may be more relevant, there are currently no recommendations to apply different cut-off for FEV1 reversibility in this age group [8, 29]. Moreover, the sRaw cut-off we found with electronic thermal compensation was close to 5th percentile of change after placebo ( −22 % of baseline, −26 % predicted) found in preschool children using the same method [4].

The strengths of this study are the large number of lung function tests used to calculate the cut-offs of Raw and sRaw; the control that results remain unchanged using GLI race-neutral spirometry reference values [30]; the highest sensitivity of our cut-offs compared to previous publication (70–75% versus 55–66%) [4, 9].

In conclusion, this study determined the cut-offs for Rawtot and sRawtot reversibility that best predict FEV1 reversibility in children with asthma. We did not find that expressing reversibility as %P was more relevant than using %B in detecting FEV1 reversibility, possibly due to the different influence of baseline values. However, in all cases, appropriate cut-offs must be used. The clinical utility of these cut-offs for diagnostic purposes should be evaluated in relation to clinical symptoms and the final diagnosis.
