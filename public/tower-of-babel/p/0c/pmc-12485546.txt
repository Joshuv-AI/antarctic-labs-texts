The Destination, Not the Journey: Optimal Vessel Preparation to Prevent Recurrent In-Stent Restenosis After Brachytherapy
Authors: Samantha Espinosa, Musa Sharkawi, Brian Bergmark
Published: 2025

Devices and techniques for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) have continued to improve in efficacy and durability, with average annual rates of target lesion revascularization (TLR) estimated at 3% with current generation drug-eluting stents (DES). This is a drastic improvement compared with 40% and 20% annual TLR rates in the plain old balloon angioplasty (POBA) and bare metal stent eras, respectively.1 However, in-stent restenosis (ISR) remains the Achilles heel of DES therapy, with approximately 10% of PCIs in the United States each year performed for ISR.2 In-stent restenosis is also independently associated with worse clinical outcomes including mortality.3 Biological (eg, neointimal hyperplasia and neoatherosclerosis) and mechanical (eg, underexpansion and stent fracture) factors are known to contribute to ISR development, with multiple morphologic and imaging-based classifications in common use.4 The most recent Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) classification of ISR further describes the timing of ISR development (early, late, and very late) and associated common mechanisms of stent failure.5

Intravascular imaging (IVI) is known to improve clinical outcomes of PCI in de novo complex lesions and is requisite in ISR PCI, now appropriately garnering a class 1A recommendation in American guidelines for complex disease in acute coronary syndromes with anticipated future upgrades in recommendations for ISR.4,6,7 IVI assessment allows for intentional ISR lesion modification with therapies such as POBA, cutting/scoring balloons, laser atherectomy, rotational or orbital atherectomy, and lithotripsy.8 After adequate lesion preparation is performed, definitive antiproliferative therapy is needed, with options including repeat DES, drug-coated balloon, and/or brachytherapy. Brachytherapy continues to provide an effective option, with previously reported TLR rates of 24% at 1 year and 42% at 3 years.9

Regardless of the antiproliferative therapy ultimately applied, the first critical step of ISR PCI is to achieve adequate lesion preparation. As suggested by SCAI Consensus Statement, early (<30 days) and late (30 days to 1 year) stent failure rates are typically related to PCI technique failures, including undersized stents, underexpansion, and uncovered stent struts with fewer cases of delayed healing and neointimal hyperplasia. The very late (>1 year) stent failures tend to be related to biologic causes that are more likely to respond to optimization of pharmacology and modifiable patient risk factors, which may be less predictable in compliance and response.5 Thus, it is imperative that excellent PCI practices are used and studied to reduce the risk of both early and late stent failure and to further define optimal technique. Unfortunately, randomized trial data are lacking to guide lesion preparation strategy.

In this issue of JSCAI, Sella et al10 tackle this challenge by reporting 1- and 3-year outcomes in a single-center, retrospective study of 219 patients undergoing brachytherapy for ISR. After removing patients who underwent atherectomy or intravascular lithotripsy, the remaining patients were categorized by lesion preparation with either POBA (140 patients) or cutting/scoring balloons (79 patients). Clinical events following PCI were then documented for these 2 groups.

Patients treated with POBA versus cutting/scoring balloons were overall similar, as were lesion characteristics, with an average vessel diameter of 3.5 mm and lesion length of 26.0 to 28.0 mm in both groups. No information was provided regarding the ISR mechanisms or IVI findings. Successful lesion modification before proceeding with brachytherapy was defined as a <20% visual residual stenosis as documented by the operator. The primary finding was that there was no statistically significant difference in 1-year TLR rates between the POBA and cutting/scoring balloon groups (18% vs 27%; P = .18). Rates of cardiac death were 1.3% and 1.4%, respectively (P > .99), and similarly, there was no significant difference in all-cause mortality between the groups (6% vs 4%; P = .76). Myocardial infarction was common in both groups (9% vs 9%; P > .99), 1 in 4 had a cardiac hospitalization (27% vs 27%; P > .99), and 1 in 3 suffered a major adverse cardiovascular event within the year (35% vs 33%; P = .87).

So what have we learned from these data? First, it is helpful to see clinical outcomes from a large brachytherapy center. Most strikingly, these findings emphasize that this is a high-risk population with rates of major adverse cardiovascular event and all-cause mortality of approximately 33% and 5%, respectively, in the year following ISR PCI. With regard to the treatment modality, these data emphasize that the critical issue is likely to be achieving good lesion preparation, not the means by which preparation was achieved. The observational nature of this analysis does not allow us to infer a causal relationship between treatment modality and clinical outcomes, but it does stand to reason that if a similar final percent stenosis is achieved, it is probably not of major significance which type of balloon was used. Unfortunately, there are no IVI data from the analysis to provide a more detailed understanding of the degree of lesion preparation success.

The current era in PCI is marked by a paradox—despite an expanding set of tools for lesion preparation, PCI optimization, and antiproliferative therapy, ISR remains common and vexing. In this context, the article by Sella et al10 is an important reminder that suboptimal index PCI can be a nidus for later ISR, that patients undergoing ISR PCI and brachytherapy are a high-risk population, and that there is an urgent need for randomized trials to inform ISR PCI optimization. At present, there are confusingly numerous ways to combine the available lesion preparation techniques, IVI modalities, and antiproliferative therapies, with little robust comparative data to guide decision making. The analysis by Sella et al is a welcome addition to this clinically important and rapidly evolving field and likely serves as a reminder that in ISR PCI, it is the destination that matters most, not the journey.
