Utilizing functional neuroimaging to study early language development
Authors: Halie A. Olson, Trevor K.M. Day, Kelly J. Hiersche, Brittany L. Manning, Holly Bradley, Soujin Choi, Gavkhar Abdurokhmonova, Sarah A. McCormick, Amy L. Conrad, Dustin Scheinost, Kelly A. Vaughn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Language develops rapidly over the infant and toddler period and has been a key area of research within the field of developmental cognitive neuroscience. Understanding the neural basis of early language development may help us predict delays or disorders, recommend early interventions, and provide a deeper mechanistic understanding of how the brain supports language learning. While the ontogeny of many cognitive functions can be studied in animal models, language development can only be studied in human children. Thus, functional neuroimaging is critical for uncovering the neural basis of language in early development. The purpose of this review is to take stock of some examples of what we have learned so far, and to explore some of the biggest open questions for the next phase of fetal, infant, and toddler neuroimaging research of language development.

Why study language development with functional neuroimaging methods?

To study the neural underpinnings of language is to probe the circuitry of one of the most remarkable, impactful, and human aspects of development. Language rapidly develops in the first few years of life, setting the stage for later social and academic development (e.g., Rautakoski et al., 2021; Reikerås and Dahle, 2022). It is thus unsurprising that the behavioral trajectory of language development has been (and continues to be increasingly) well-characterized (e.g., Bloom and Lahey, 1978; Gleason and Ratner, 2022; Hoff, 2013): from early sensitivity to speech in fetuses and neonates (Kisilevsky et al., 2009, Vouloumanos et al., 2010, Vouloumanos and Werker, 2007), to comprehension of familiar words around six months of age in infancy (Bergelson and Swingley, 2012), to word production a few months later (Ferguson and Farwell, 1975), and to progressively complex sentence production in subsequent years (Brown and Fraser, 1963). Our understanding of the brain bases of language development in the fetal, infant, and toddler (FIT) period, however, is in a more nascent stage.

Before delving into what we have learned about the neurodevelopment of language in the FIT period thus far, and where we want to go next as a field, it is worth stepping back and considering why we want to use neuroimaging in the first place. What insights can functional neuroimaging provide? First, from a basic science standpoint, studying language development can elucidate neural mechanisms that support a uniquely human cognitive function. Why is it that only humans learn language – which features of the developing human brain enable this complex cognitive feat? While studying the brains of other animals can provide some insights (Berwick et al., 2013, Doupe and Kuhl, 1999, Rilling, 2014), functional neuroimaging in humans is crucial for studying language development. The brain bases of language have already been extensively studied in adults, revealing both dedicated brain regions for language as well as broader networks involved in processing language in context (for some reviews, see Eyre et al., 2021; Fedorenko et al., 2024; Friederici, 2011; Hagoort, 2019). Since humans are not born comprehending and producing language, the ontogenetic origins of this complex system are not clear. Spatially precise neuroimaging tools can uncover the topographic organization of the language system and its relation to other cognitive functions in early development, while temporally precise tools are ideal for studying real-time language processing.

Language is also a perfect system to study the effects of experience on the developing brain. While some neurobiological predispositions and constraints set the stage for language learning, experience with language is necessary, providing an opportunity to investigate the mechanisms by which such experiences impact developing neural circuitry (e.g., sensitive periods in speech perception, Werker and Hensch, 2015). Finally, neuroimaging tools complement behavioral measures in the study of language development, as the behavioral repertoires of infants and toddlers are limited. While the field has greatly benefited from infant looking paradigms, neonatal sucking paradigms, and other methods that minimize behavioral demands on young children, neuroimaging approaches allow for a deeper understanding of the brain development that supports language acquisition. Furthermore, fetuses are almost inaccessible without neuroimaging, severely limiting investigation of the foundations for language before birth. Neuroimaging tools provide additional dimensions of measurement, enabling more detailed study of language development.

In addition to these and other basic science questions, neuroimaging studies of early language development are also poised to have profound translational impacts. Early language difficulties are associated with poorer academic, social, and developmental outcomes (Aram et al., 1984, Catts et al., 2002; Johnson et al., 2010; Young et al., 2002). Such difficulties are not rare – across studies in different countries, an estimated 3–10 % of individuals have a developmental language disorder (Calder et al., 2022, Norbury et al., 2016, Nudel et al., 2023, Scharff Rethfeldt et al., 2023, Tomblin et al., 1997, Wu et al., 2023). Early intervention can improve language outcomes (Fricke et al., 2013), but this in turn requires early identification of potential language difficulties.1 Neuroimaging in the FIT period is a potential tool for predicting future language challenges (see Section 2.5 Neural Predictors of Early Language).2 Furthermore, neural markers may help identify which intervention approach is most likely to be effective for a given child (i.e., predictive modeling; Gabrieli et al., 2015; Scheinost et al., 2023; Woo et al., 2017). Outside the clinical domain, functional neuroimaging might also help us understand the role of specific types of early language experiences – like multilingualism (Ferjan Ramírez et al., 2017, Garcia-Sierra et al., 2016, Jasinska and Petitto, 2013), interactive language with caregivers (Hutton et al., 2017, Romeo et al., 2018, Romeo et al., 2021), or delayed access to language input (MacSweeney et al., 2008, Mayberry et al., 2011, Twomey et al., 2020) – on the developing language system.

Taken together, the promise of functional neuroimaging studies of language in the FIT period is clear. In this review, we will first discuss how functional neuroimaging has already contributed to our understanding of the neural bases of language development. We will then turn to exciting open questions and future directions that we expect to shape the next couple decades of research into the neural underpinnings of language in FIT development.

Early-emerging sensitivity to speech

To understand language acquisition, the first key step is to understand how infants process incoming speech (for review, see Kuhl, 2004). Behavioral research has shown that humans are sensitive to speech at and even before birth (Kisilevsky et al., 2009, Mehler et al., 1988). Complementing these methods, functional neuroimaging tools can measure speech responses in the absence of overt behavior, adding other dimensions of information – not only showing that there is a response to speech, but also when and where it happens. These additional dimensions are critical for identifying both similarities and differences between early speech processing in fetuses and infants, and later language processing in older children and adults.

EEG is particularly well-suited to examining temporal features of the neural response to speech across early development. One method is to measure event-related potentials (ERPs) evoked by speech. In adults, the mismatch negativity (MMN) component is a negative peak in voltage that is reliably observed ∼150–250 ms after a deviant auditory stimulus (Duncan et al., 2009). In early infancy (newborns to 3 months), an ERP component is also observed ∼150–250 ms after a deviant auditory stimulus; however, this component is sometimes negative (like the adult MMN) and sometimes positive (Leppänen et al., 2004). This infant mismatch response (MMR) appears as a brain signal to both speech and non-speech stimuli (Cheour et al., 1998, Leppänen et al., 1997), and transitions to the more typical adult-like negative MMN pattern around 4–9 months of age (Y.-Y. Cheng et al., 2015; He et al., 2009; Jing and Benasich, 2006). MMN responses also become more prevalent in a native language contrast than in non-native languages by the end of the first year (Cheour et al., 1998, Friederici et al., 2007, Kuhl et al., 2006) and continue to mature throughout the toddlerhood and preschool years.

With its superior spatial resolution, MEG studies have provided additional insight into the mismatch response. In a cross-sectional study with newborns, 6-month-olds, and 12-month-olds, all age groups had a mismatch response to tones, harmonic chords, and speech syllables in superior temporal cortex; however, only the 6- and 12-month-olds also showed a response in inferior temporal cortex (Imada et al., 2006). When comparing mismatch responses for native and non-native phonetic contrasts, 7-month-olds responded similarly to both contrasts in auditory and motor regions, whereas 11- and 12-month-olds had larger responses to native than non-native contrasts in auditory areas, and larger responses to non-native than native contrasts in motor areas (Kuhl et al., 2014). Ultimately, the MMR/MNN provides a time-resolved index of early auditory discrimination and category formation in the brain, revealing when infants begin to neurally differentiate between sounds and phonemes, even before they can produce or behaviorally report them.

Another approach with EEG has been to examine neural tracking as a means of studying how infants and young children process speech as a continuous stream. Neural tracking studies using naturalistic speech stimuli, such as nursery rhymes, have demonstrated that even newborns can track speech rhythm, although tracking strength increases with age (Jessen et al., 2021). Infants as young as 4 months exhibit cortical tracking of the amplitude envelope of speech (Attaheri et al., 2022). By 7 months, infants show stronger neural tracking for infant-directed speech than adult-directed speech (Kalashnikova et al., 2018). Notably, stronger tracking of speech is thought to aid in the language acquisition process (Menn et al., 2022). Neural tracking has been observed across different languages, including English, Dutch, German, and French. Some evidence suggests that by 6–12 months, infants show language-specific tuning, with stronger neural tracking for the rhythm and prosodic patterns of their native language compared to unfamiliar languages (Ortiz Barajas et al., 2021). More recently, decoding-based neural tracking methods have been successfully applied to EEG data, demonstrating the utility of forward, backward, and mutual-information approaches in measuring phonetic and acoustic encoding (Keshavarzi et al., 2024). Beyond ERPs and envelope tracking, time-frequency approaches and EEG encoding models extend this work by linking continuous speech features to brain responses (Desai et al., 2024). Such models have revealed the emergence of cortical phonetic feature encoding in infants (Di Liberto et al., 2023), complementing traditional ERP and tracking results. In contrast, decoding models predict language features from EEG signals. Recent studies show that this can capture orthogonal neural codes in infants (Gennari et al., 2021) and the temporal dynamics of word recognition in adults (McMurray et al., 2022). Together, these findings suggest that neural tracking of speech is present from early infancy and continues to develop in parallel with language acquisition (for review, see Bradley et al., 2025).

Complementing the temporal information provided by EEG, fNIRS is a flexible tool for examining the location of early speech responses in cortex. While its spatial resolution is limited by the density and placement of optodes, and only reflects hemodynamic changes close to the surface of cortex, fNIRS research in young infants has revealed early sensitivity to speech in temporal areas of the brain. Newborns show greater activation to speech than an auditory control (backward speech) in left temporal regions (Peña et al., 2003, Sato et al., 2012), particularly for native speech (Sato et al., 2012, Vannasing et al., 2016). Neonates also show higher activation in left frontotemporal regions when listening to turn-taking exchanges compared to a single speaker or two speakers repeating the same pseudowords (Forgács et al., 2022). Older infants (1–6 months) become highly attuned to speech versus other sounds, also exhibiting greater activation in temporal regions (Homae et al., 2014, Shultz et al., 2014, Taga and Asakawa, 2007). In particular, infants ranging from 3 to 13 months show sensitivity to infant-directed speech in bilateral temporal regions (Bortfeld et al., 2007, Bortfeld et al., 2009, McDonald et al., 2019, Naoi et al., 2012).

In addition to overall sensitivity to native speech sounds, fNIRS has also demonstrated early sensitivity to speech structure in the human brain. Neonates exhibit higher activation in temporal and left frontal areas to repeated syllable sequences than to random syllable sequences (Gervain et al., 2008). By 6 months, when infants first show knowledge of word meanings (Bergelson and Swingley, 2012), frontal and parietal regions show sensitivity to both syllable repetition as well as sequences of different syllables, and these regions distinguish between the two structures (de la Cruz-Pavía and Gervain, 2023). Critically, this sensitivity to linguistic structure is not restricted to speech – 6-month-old infants also show greater responses in frontotemporal areas for repeated visual signs than non-repeated signs, but do not show this pattern for a visual control condition (Berent et al., 2021).

Early topography of speech responses

For more detailed characterization of the topography of the hemodynamic response to speech in the developing human brain, researchers have turned to fMRI. Prior fMRI studies have shown that the language network is strikingly domain-specific and left-lateralized in the vast majority of healthy adults, and there is evidence from outside the FIT period that this specificity is present in early childhood (Hiersche et al., 2024) and that laterality may increase with age after 5 years (Olulade et al., 2020, Szaflarski et al., 2006; c.f. Ozernov-Palchik et al., 2024). These findings raise questions about the origins of such specificity and left-laterality for language that fMRI in the FIT period is uniquely suited to answer.

Researchers are in the early stages of using task-based fMRI to study how the fetal brain processes speech. During fetal development, auditory function exists by the third trimester (Birnholz and Benacerraf, 1983), and there is preliminary evidence that task-based fMRI can capture auditory processing of both externally (guitar music, R. J. Moore et al., 2001) and maternally generated sounds (singing, Goldberg et al., 2020) in the bilateral temporal lobe. While these studies are limited by their use of a single task condition, they set the stage for more complex designs to examine how different auditory (linguistic vs. non-linguistic) sounds are processed in the fetal brain.

More work has been done examining speech processing in infancy using task-based fMRI, providing compelling evidence that the infant brain processes language stimuli in similar regions as adults, though the specificity of this processing is unclear. Speech, compared to rest, activates frontotemporal and parietal language regions (Altman and Bernal, 2001), perhaps with initial right-lateralization at 2 days old (Perani et al., 2011), followed by left-lateralization at 3 months (Dehaene-Lambertz, 2002, Dehaene-Lambertz et al., 2010). Infants are also similar to adults in their temporal organization of cortical responses to language, with the fastest responses in primary auditory regions and increasingly slower responses in posterior temporal regions, the temporal pole, and inferior frontal regions (Dehaene-Lambertz et al., 2006). However, under more stringent experimental conditions, when language processing is contrasted with conditions other than rest, our understanding of the specificity of language processing is less clear. Some evidence suggests 2–3 month old infants differentiate forward and backwards speech in the angular gyrus, as well as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex when they are awake (Dehaene-Lambertz, 2002), and that task-based connectivity of primary auditory cortex differs between instrumental music and music with language (Loukas et al., 2024), but others have failed to find a language-specific response when contrasting language with a spectro-temporally matched auditory control or music (Kosakowski et al., 2023).

Toddlers, like infants, show speech specificity in the angular gyrus, as well as in the cingulate and precuneus, all of which respond more to forward than backward speech during sleep by two years of age, and by three years there is activation across traditional temporal language regions (Redcay et al., 2008). Further, speech processing in sleeping toddlers appears bilateral during typical development, but is right-lateralized in toddlers with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and this atypical lateralization correlates with language skills and ASD severity (Redcay and Courchesne, 2008), which aligns with similar findings in adults with ASD (Lindell and Hudry, 2013).

Functional connectivity within the emerging language system

Adult neuroimaging studies have examined functional connectivity within the brain’s language system (Braga et al., 2020, Tomasi and Volkow, 2012), using approaches that have also been applied to examining the emerging language system in FIT populations. Resting-state fMRI, which captures correlations between regions, is well-suited to studying the emerging language system in infancy as it does not require infants to be awake or to attend to stimuli. To examine seed-to-seed connectivity in the emerging language system, researchers have consistently placed seeds in left and right inferior frontal cortex and superior temporal lobe (Day, 2024, Emerson et al., 2016, Scheinost et al., 2021, Schuler et al., 2018). Homologous connectivity tends to increase between the prenatal period and about one year, with lateralization seeming to increase after the one year mark (Emerson et al., 2016, Scheinost et al., 2021). The two pairs of regions show dissociable timing, with changes in temporal lobe preceding changes in frontal lobe by a few months in some studies (Day, 2024, Scheinost et al., 2021) but not others (Emerson et al., 2016).

Using a resting state network (RSN) approach, a bilateral temporal auditory network has been identified in fetuses (Turk et al., 2019, van den Heuvel et al., 2018), infants (Eyre et al., 2021; L. A. Moore et al., 2024; Rajasilta et al., 2020), and toddlers (B. Chen et al., 2021). An RSN that is similar to the adult language RSN – though more variable in its detection and spatial characteristics – can be found in infants (Eyre et al., 2021, Rajasilta et al., 2020) and toddlers, by which point it is lateralized (B. Chen et al., 2021). Researchers have examined the effect of specific experiences, such as preterm birth and congenital deafness, on the development of these resting state networks. Preterm birth, for instance, seems to not broadly affect network topography, but does reduce connectivity especially in language regions (Eyre et al., 2021), potentially due to specific effects of preterm birth on cross-collosal connectivity (Thomason et al., 2017). In deaf infants, amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations and regional homogeneity was reduced in language areas and increased in visual areas compared to hearing infants (Xia et al., 2017). RSN properties have also been associated with language behavior. Greater network variability has been associated with better language outcomes for neonates (Y. Xu et al., 2024), but by the toddler period, lower variability is associated with greater simultaneous language scores (B. Chen et al., 2021), suggesting a shift from early-life high plasticity to specialization. An increase in left-laterality may also be associated with higher language scores (Emerson et al., 2016; c.f. Day, 2024).

Neural indications of word learning

Not only have neuroimaging tools provided insights into speech processing generally, but also into early comprehension and word learning. Another well-studied ERP component in language acquisition research is the N400. It serves as an early neural marker of vocabulary development, reflecting infants’ ability to link words to referents. One of the first N400-like effects in the FIT period was found in 19-month-olds, who exhibited a more negative ERP response to incongruent picture-word pairs, though at a delayed latency (800–1400 ms) compared to adults (Friedrich and Friederici, 2004). Later research demonstrated that the N400 emerges as early as 6 months, with 600–900 ms negativity observed for consistently paired word-object associations (Friedrich and Friederici, 2011). These findings align with behavioral studies showing that infants recognize words by this age (Bergelson and Swingley, 2012, Tincoff and Jusczyk, 1999).

Beyond word-object associations, the N400 has also been linked to speech segmentation. For instance, Dutch-learning 10-month-olds familiarized with isolated words exhibited an N400 response to familiar words in continuous speech (Kooijman et al., 2005). Similar findings have been reported in French-, German-, and English-learning infants (Goyet et al., 2010, Kidd et al., 2018). While N400 research provides valuable insights into infant word learning, when compared with adult studies, infants demonstrate differences in N400 latency and distribution, highlighting developmental changes in semantic processing (Junge et al., 2021). Despite methodological challenges such as limited infant attention and trial requirements, the N400 remains a key tool for understanding early language acquisition (Junge et al., 2021).

Neural predictors of early language

Multiple neural metrics collected during the FIT period have been associated with concurrent or later language behavior. For instance, researchers have examined the relation between the power of various EEG frequency bands and language skills. Some studies have found a relation between having higher alpha power in infancy and higher concurrent and later expressive language abilities (Huberty et al., 2023; A. R. Levin et al., 2017). In older children, there appears to be a relation between increased alpha power (a more developmentally immature pattern) and lower language skills (Kwok et al., 2019, Lum et al., 2022, Schneider and Maguire, 2019), but some have suggested this may be related to overall development rather than language skills in particular (Lum et al., 2022). Although theta power has been predominantly investigated in relation to other cognitive processes, emerging evidence suggests it has relevance for language development. Recent studies indicate that development in theta power is associated with language proficiency and may reflect processes related to syntactic and semantic information processing (Hald et al., 2006, Schneider et al., 2016). In school-aged children, decreases in resting-state theta power have been associated with sentence repetition (Lum et al., 2022) and shown to predict vocabulary skills (Meng et al., 2022). Another potentially useful frequency band to examine in relation to language development is gamma. In one study, higher gamma power in toddlers predicted better language skills in four-year-olds (Gou et al., 2011). Gamma power may also be associated with language disorder risk; however, the directionality of gamma power across these studies has varied (Heim et al., 2011, Nikolaeva et al., 2024).

Another feature of frequency bands is EEG coherence. EEG coherence has been posited to be a marker of hemisphere maturation (Thatcher, 1994), and one longitudinal study found that left hemisphere maturation at 14 months and right hemisphere maturation at 18 months were related to language development at 24 months (Mundy et al., 2003). Another study with younger infants (1–7 months) found negative correlations between frontoparietal coherence in alpha and theta bands and early language, suggesting that decreased connectivity might be associated with more advanced development at this stage (Bradley et al., 2024). Both studies highlight the potential of EEG coherence as an indicator of early language development, suggesting its utility in understanding typical development and identifying infants at risk for neurodevelopmental disabilities.

Other EEG and MEG measures have been linked to language outcomes as well. By 10 months, neural tracking has been linked to vocabulary size, with higher coherence at syllabic frequencies predicting larger expressive vocabularies in later months (Çetinçelik et al., 2023). MEG studies have also predicted later vocabulary: higher right frontal cortex activation in 14-month-olds was associated with later vocabulary growth (Bosseler et al., 2021), and individual differences in the mismatch response to non-native speech contrasts in left inferior frontal and superior temporal regions at 11 months were associated with later vocabulary (Zhao and Kuhl, 2022).

MMN/MMR components in infants and toddlers may be especially useful for predicting difficulties and monitoring intervention progress (Y. Chen et al., 2016; Kujala and Näätänen, 2001), as features of the MMN responses in older children and adults have been associated with developmental language disorders (Kujala and Leminen, 2017), reading difficulties (Gu and Bi, 2020, Norton et al., 2021, Volkmer and Schulte-Körne, 2018), and autism (T.-C. Chen et al., 2020). Indeed, multiple EEG studies have examined the association between infant mismatch responses (MMN/MMR) and later language and pre-reading outcomes. In longitudinal studies, MMN in infancy relates to school-age reading ability (Lohvansuu et al., 2021). Newborn MMN/MMR can predict later receptive language in toddlerhood, pre-reading abilities (such as phonological skills, rapid naming, and letter knowledge) at preschool age, and reading speed and spelling accuracy at second-grade (Guttorm et al., 2005, Guttorm et al., 2010, Leppänen et al., 2010). MMN/MMR at as early as 2 months and at 17 months was absent in infants at familial risk for dyslexia compared to control infants (van Leeuwen et al., 2006, van Zuijen et al., 2012), and larger and left-lateralized MMRs at 28 months were associated with better phonological processing skills in preschoolers (Navarrete-Arroyo et al., 2024). Together, this evidence suggests that MMN/MMR is a particularly promising neural predictor of language development.

Summary

Together, the existing body of functional neuroimaging research on early language development presents compelling evidence for a human brain primed to acquire language. With their complementary strengths, EEG, fMRI, MEG, and fNIRS have demonstrated early signs of the emerging language system across both temporal and spatial features. What are some of the open questions in the field?

In this final section, we turn from looking back to looking ahead. From theoretical to methodological advancements, there are many opportunities to apply emerging approaches to deep basic and applied questions in language development. Here, we focus on a few key open questions that are guiding ongoing research.

How can functional neuroimaging complement behavioral approaches?

Behavioral studies will always be fundamental to understanding the development of language. As functional neuroimaging tools continue to advance, however, they are poised to lend complementary insight into trajectories of language development. One strength of neuroimaging in the FIT period is the ability to detect brain changes that precede behavioral development. Language development comprises multiple, non-discrete stages, some of which are more difficult than others to measure. For example, comprehension precedes production during development, yet the former is more difficult to measure with behavioral approaches. Functional neuroimaging may reveal if changes to the brain happen before, during, or after behavioral developments in language acquisition.

In particular, neuroimaging tools may lend insight into the neural underpinnings of sensitive periods (Gabard-Durnam and McLaughlin, 2020, Hensch, 2018) in language development. Behavioral research has shown that the timing of language input matters for language learning and development (Kuhl, 2010). For example, infants typically lose sensitivity to phonetic distinctions that are not meaningful in their native languages by the end of the first year of life, but these windows can be “extended” with exposure to additional languages (Byers-Heinlein and Fennell, 2014, Petitto et al., 2012, Tees and Werker, 1984). Functional neuroimaging can complement insights from model organisms in studying the timing of changes in speech perception and the neurobiological mechanisms driving such effects (Werker and Hensch, 2015). Functional neuroimaging has also been used to understand the effects of delayed access to language, such as when a deaf child is born to hearing parents who do not yet know sign language, on brain function (Q. Cheng et al., 2020; Mayberry et al., 2011; Mayberry and Kluender, 2018). Because a common goal of research on language development in the FIT period is to support early language learning, understanding the brain mechanisms underlying sensitive periods may help optimize interventions and other early language experiences for children at-risk for language delays and disorders.

Recent efforts to collect large functional neuroimaging datasets are particularly promising from this standpoint. These joint efforts across research groups allow for large enough sample sizes to examine individual differences linking brain measures to behavior or experiences (Olson, Camacho, et al., 2025). On a smaller scale, for instance, researchers examined features of the home-language environment and resting-state functional connectivity in regions associated with language comprehension, finding that infants who experienced more conversational turns had lower connectivity in a posterior temporal network (King et al., 2020). On a larger scale, the Baby Connectome Project (Howell et al., 2019) aims to collect resting-state data from thousands of infants, which will enable higher-powered examinations of individual differences.

Functional neuroimaging measures may also facilitate comparisons of language development across diverse language families. Because the same system underlies language processing in the brain across languages (Malik-Moraleda et al., 2022) – and modalities (Emmorey, 2021, MacSweeney et al., 2008) – its properties can be examined and compared across children in diverse linguistic environments, potentially more easily than behavioral measures.3

Finally, functional neuroimaging can contextualize similarities – and differences – in behavioral measures, including in non-human development. For example, human infants and tamarins share some artificial language-learning skills, but from there our abilities diverge (Christiansen and Kirby, 2003). Neuroimaging has already shown how human brains differ from other primates in ways important for language (Rilling, 2014), and FIT neuroimaging can establish which similarities and differences are present at birth, and which emerge over time. Longitudinal functional neuroimaging can allow researchers to examine regions of the brain that will later specialize for language, which may inform behavioral studies of early language acquisition.

How can different neuroimaging methods complement each other in the study of language development?

In addition to complementing behavioral measures, neuroimaging methods can also complement each other when studying language development. For example, EEG source localization has been used as early as in neonates to determine the spatial location of the neuronal activity that drives changes in EEG signal at the surface of the scalp (Michel and He, 2019, Reynolds and Richards, 2009, Roche-Labarbe et al., 2008), including to study auditory processes in infancy (Hämäläinen et al., 2011, Ortiz-Mantilla et al., 2012). While one approach to source localization is to use age-appropriate atlases, structural MRI scans can also be used in conjunction with EEG for source localization, and including more detail can improve results (such as modeling cerebral spinal fluid; Conte and Richards, 2021). Advances in source localization methodology combining EEG and MRI is particularly promising for studying the emerging language system, as EEG studies are typically much easier to conduct with infants and toddlers than fMRI.

In general, functional neuroimaging tools vary in their spatial and temporal sensitivities, thus providing different information about underlying neural activity. Particularly because language is processed across multiple timescales, a multimodal neuroimaging approach is well-suited for examining language acquisition and development during the FIT period. For example, previous research suggests that the MMR/MMN, RSN functional connectivity, and neural coupling each predict later language skills (B. Chen et al., 2021; Y. Chen et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2023; Y. Xu et al., 2024), but it is still unknown whether these predictors reflect the same or different neural mechanism(s). Multimodal research can, for instance, identify whether children with a diagnosis such as developmental language disorder show consistent deficits in each neural metric, or whether specific patterns of symptoms are associated with specific neural markers.

How can the neural correlates of language development be assessed in more naturalistic ways?

Another key direction for functional neuroimaging of language development is to measure the brain in real-world contexts (Dopierala and Emberson, 2023). Language is acquired and used in social, interactive contexts, but this is not how language development has traditionally been studied with neuroimaging tools due to practical constraints, such as equipment portability, and analysis constraints, such as tolerance for noise and motion. Despite these challenges, advances in technology have made data collection in more naturalistic contexts easier. Mobile EEG and fNIRS systems now allow for the collection of data in real-world settings like at home or in the classroom (Dikker et al., 2017, Gervain et al., 2023, Janssen et al., 2021, Pinti et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2022). Lab-based studies using EEG and fNIRS have also made strides towards more naturalistic data collection. This includes designing experiments where children observe humans (rather than viewing video clips), interact with a caregiver (rather than an experimenter), and play with toys or read books (rather than just observing) (Jones et al., 2015, Liao et al., 2015, St. John et al., 2016). Other studies have coded naturally occurring behaviors like eye gaze and other markers of communication and engagement (Leong et al., 2017, Norton et al., 2022, Wass et al., 2018). Technological advancements in neuroimaging tools also promise to increase the feasibility of naturalistic data collection. A particularly exciting development in MEG is optically pumped magnetometry (OPM) (Brookes et al., 2022). Unlike SQUID-MEG, which requires a large machine, OPM-MEG sensors can be embedded in a cap, making it more tolerant of motion and thus enabling data collection in more naturalistic settings (Turk-Browne and Aslin, 2024). Though it is more challenging to incorporate highly naturalistic approaches in fMRI, movie watching is another popular approach to increase ecological validity (Cantlon, 2020, Vanderwal et al., 2019). Overall, understanding how young children process language in naturalistic environments, including how this is similar to or different from more closely controlled experimental contexts, is an important goal of future neuroscience research.

An emerging approach with fNIRS and EEG that holds promise for studying language development is dual-brain simultaneous recording, or “hyperscanning,” in which neural recordings are collected from multiple people simultaneously (such as an infant and a caregiver) (Alonso et al., 2024, Hoyniak et al., 2021, Leong et al., 2017, Nguyen et al., 2020, Nguyen et al., 2021, Piazza et al., 2020, Quiñones-Camacho et al., 2020, Rosen et al., 2025). In one study, 9–15-month-old infants showed neural coupling (i.e., synchronized brain activity) in prefrontal and parietal channels during social and narrative processing while interacting with an adult (Piazza et al., 2020). In another study, increased neural coupling between 4 and 6-month-old infants and their caregivers was observed when they engaged in more frequent turn-taking during a proto-conversation (Nguyen et al., 2023). This neural coupling positively predicted vocabulary size when the infants were 12 months old. Examining communicative interactions with dual-brain fNIRS or EEG is a relatively new approach, which has the potential to provide key insights into the dynamics of language learning and processing in context.

How can neuroimaging help us better understand language development in multilingual children?

Over half the global population acquires more than one language (Grosjean, 2019), and behavioral research has highlighted the importance of neuroplasticity in multilingual language development, such that young children are often better able to acquire native-like skills in two languages than adults (Birdsong, 2018, Flege et al., 1999; Johnson and Newport, 1989). However, most functional neuroimaging studies of FIT language development have focused on monolingual language development (Byers-Heinlein et al., 2019). While monolingual language development typically follows a set of experience-expectant milestones during the FIT period, multilingual development is more variable across individuals, reflecting experience-dependent differences in the age of exposure and amount of exposure to each language (Birdsong, 2018, Vaughn et al., 2015). As such, FIT neuroimaging research is needed to understand how the brain adapts to acquire multiple languages in early childhood, and how this may be different from acquiring multiple languages later in life.

For instance, how are multiple languages integrated – and segregated – in the brain during language acquisition? Research from multilingual adults suggests that there are shared and unique brain networks for each language (Rüschemeyer et al., 2005; Zhang et al., 2020), and these language networks integrate with cognitive networks to enable a bilingual adult to select the context-appropriate language (Green and Abutalebi, 2013, Seo et al., 2018). To date, there are no studies using fMRI to examine multilingual brain network development during the FIT period.

Existing EEG and fNIRS research suggest that the process of perceptual narrowing, in which the brain exhibits unique neural responses to their native language compared to their non-native language, may occur later for multilingual infants than for monolingual infants (Garcia-Sierra et al., 2011, Petitto et al., 2012). This may reflect the fact that multilingual infants have less exposure to each of their native languages than monolingual infants (Garcia-Sierra et al., 2011), but researchers have also interpreted this finding as an extended window of neuroplasticity for language among multilinguals (Petitto et al., 2012).

Research thus far has only scratched the surface in terms of understanding how the brain processes multiple languages during the FIT period. Many questions remain, particularly focused on individual differences in multilingual learning. For example, neural processes for multilingualism may differ between unimodal multilinguals (i.e., children exposed to two spoken languages) and multimodal multilinguals (i.e., children exposed to at least one signed language and at least one spoken language), and between multilinguals whose languages are quite similar (e.g., two or more Romance languages, such as Spanish and Portuguese) and multilinguals whose languages are quite different (e.g., tonal and non-tonal languages, such as Mandarin and English). Neuroscience research in the FIT period can also uncover when and how multilingual children recognize that their two (or more) languages are distinct, which may help to dispel a common misconception: that multilingual exposure may confuse young children (Guiberson, 2013, Hoff and Core, 2015), particularly those at-risk for language difficulties, such as children with ASD (Beauchamp and MacLeod, 2017, Kay-Raining Bird et al., 2012). Findings from this emerging area of research have the potential to inform educational policies and targeted interventions for multilingual children.

How can neuroimaging contribute to diagnosis and/or prediction of language difficulties?

Neuroimaging has the potential to act as a unique contributor for predicting developmental disorders affecting language. Thus far, FIT neuroimaging work examining language development has primarily focused on healthy development (Gaudet et al., 2020), setting the stage for more translational work by first elucidating typical trajectories of language development. For example, because we know that the mature language system is characterized by left lateralization, this feature can be examined in atypical language trajectories. FIT neuroimaging has demonstrated that in infancy, reduced left lateralization during speech processing is a marker for later language impairment (Cantiani et al., 2019) and is associated with future ASD diagnosis (Seery et al., 2013). Similarly, reduced left lateralization for speech in sleeping toddlers has also been found to be associated with autism (Redcay and Courchesne, 2008). Lateralization of speech processing in infants has also been shown to predict future language skills, above and beyond early behavioral measures (Wang et al., 2024), suggesting that lateralization may be a key target for predicting and understanding language impairment. Other risk factors for language difficulties may include decreased neural specificity to a native language (Wagner et al., 2025) and altered functional connectivity in putative language regions in utero (Thomason et al., 2017). Overall, a better understanding of the neural computations in putative language regions prior to behavioral language acquisition promises to inform future treatment and intervention.

Concluding remarks

The past few decades of FIT neuroimaging research have led to remarkable insights, particularly in the domain of language development. With its superior temporal resolution, EEG research has identified multiple ERP components that tap into aspects of language processing, associations between properties of frequency bands and language behavior, and evidence for neural tracking of speech. MEG’s blend of temporal and spatial resolution has revealed the involvement of distinct regions in speech processing across early development, which can predict future language outcomes. FMRI has enabled key discoveries about the spatial layout of the developing language system, through both task-based approaches showing frontotemporal brain responses to speech, as well as resting-state approaches uncovering networks with similar structures to those for audition and language in the adult brain. Particularly due to its ease of use with infants and toddlers, fNIRS has been a valuable tool for measuring early brain sensitivity to speech structure and communicative function, including through simultaneous dual-brain recordings.

Lucky for us researchers, there are still more open questions than answers about the neural bases of language development. Emerging research directions promise to combine insights from multiple neuroimaging tools with behavior, assess language acquisition in increasingly naturalistic ways, characterize the neural basis of language development across more diverse contexts, and seek translational applications. It is no surprise that some of the most interesting and influential findings in FIT neuroimaging have focused on speech and language – language development is a hallmark of the FIT period, and it provides the ideal testbed for understanding the interplay between experience and the developing brain. As we look forward to the next few decades of functional neuroimaging research, we are inspired to integrate technological and theoretical advancements to uncover the neural underpinnings of language development.
