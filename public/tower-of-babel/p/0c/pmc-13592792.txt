Safety and Efficacy of Efbemalenograstim Alfa for Patients at Risk for Chemotherapy-Induced Neutropenia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Prasanjit Das, Sreejoi Dutta, Shampa Maji, Bisweswar Ojha, Alapan Das, Bhairav Kumar Pathak, Arkapal Bandyopadhyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChemotherapy-induced neutropenia (CIN) and its severe complication, febrile neutropenia, remain major challenges in oncology. Although granulocyte colony-stimulating factors (G-CSFs) have reduced the incidence of these complications, limitations related to dosing schedules, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-associated concerns, and pharmacokinetic variability persist. Efbemalenograstim alfa (F-627) is a novel non-PEGylated Fc fusion G-CSF designed for once-per-cycle administration. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy and safety of efbemalenograstim alfa compared with placebo or standard G-CSFs in patients at risk of CIN.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024628001). MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and ClinicalTrials.gov were searched from inception to October 22, 2025. Randomized controlled trials evaluating efbemalenograstim alfa in patients receiving myelotoxic chemotherapy were included. Efficacy outcomes included duration of severe neutropenia (DSN), incidence of severe neutropenia (ISN), and depth of absolute neutrophil count (ANC) nadir. Safety outcomes included adverse events, serious adverse events, and treatment discontinuations. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed.ResultsFive randomized controlled trials involving approximately 800 patients with breast cancer receiving myelotoxic chemotherapy were included. Compared with placebo or standard G-CSFs, efbemalenograstim alfa demonstrated comparable efficacy in reducing DSN during the first chemotherapy cycle (MD −0.32 days; 95% CI: −0.79 to 0.14) and ISN (RR 0.86; 95% CI: 0.73–1.03). A significant improvement in ANC nadir was observed (MD + 0.40 ×109/L; p = 0.0002). Rates of serious adverse events (RR 0.71; 95% CI: 0.29–1.70), adverse events, and treatment discontinuations were comparable between groups. Heterogeneity was primarily attributable to the inclusion of a placebo-controlled study.ConclusionEfbemalenograstim alfa demonstrated efficacy and safety comparable to currently available long-acting G-CSFs for the prevention of CIN. Its Fc fusion structure enables once-per-cycle administration without PEGylation, supporting its potential as an alternative long-acting G-CSF. Further studies are needed to evaluate long-term outcomes and real-world effectiveness.

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced neutropenia (CIN) is among the common hematological toxicities linked with cytotoxic chemotherapy. It is defined as an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of less than 1,500 cells/µL. Severe neutropenia which is defined as ANC less than 500 cells/µL increases the risk of acquiring infection and thereby making the body susceptible to developing febrile neutropenia (FN). FN is defined by either a single oral temperature of 38.3®C or higher, or a sustained temperature above 38®C and associated with severe neutropenia [1, 2]. CIN and FN are the leading causes of dose-limiting toxicities which affects the decision-making in terms of either delay or reduction in chemotherapy dosage. This leads to a reduction in effectiveness of the treatment and eventually the survival outcome.

CIN and FN impose a notable clinical burden in patients undergoing chemotherapy where FN is known to be associated with major inpatient mortality which ranges from 5% to 11% and it can go up to 15% in high-risk patients [2, 3]. Complications linked to CIN are an increase in rate of hospitalization which may go beyond 60% and two to four times raise in infection-related morbidity [3]. The maximum chances of these complications are seen during the initial cycles of chemotherapy with a notable and progressive decline as we go further in the subsequent chemotherapy cycles.

In current practice, International Oncology Societies advise for use of granulocyte colony-stimulating factors (G-CSFs) for primary prophylaxis. G-CSFs have been recommended for patients undergoing chemotherapy cycles where the risk of FN is 20% or higher, or when there are additional patient-related specific risk factors involved [2]. Traditionally, G-CSFs fall into two categories.1.Short-acting agents. Filgrastim, which demands daily dosing.2.Long-acting PEGylated agents. Pegfilgrastim, given once per chemotherapy cycle.

Despite their proven efficacy, these agents have certain limitations such as fluctuations in drug levels in the body due to varying pharmacokinetics, likelihood of an immune reaction, chances of developing antibodies against the polyethylene glycol (PEG) component (anti-PEG antibody formation), and variation in timing of administration relative to chemotherapy [4–6]. Efbemalenograstim alfa (F-627) is a novel long-acting recombinant G-CSF. Unlike the PEGylated agents, efbemalenograstim alfa is known to achieve once-per-cycle dosing without the need for PEGylation because its non-PEGylated homo dimeric Fc fusion protein structure enables prolonged systemic exposure. This design links two G-CSF molecules to the Fc fragment of human immunoglobulin G2, utilizing the neonatal Fc receptor for recycling which reduces the renal clearance and overcomes limitations of previous G-CSF treatments [7, 8]. Efbemalenograstim alfa demonstrates comparable or potentially improved receptor binding and neutrophil recovery kinetics without the immunogenicity associated with PEGylation. Efbemalenograstim alfa has a dimeric structure which contributes to sustained receptor engagement causing rapid neutrophil recovery when compared to the traditional G-CSFs [9].

Randomized controlled trials furnish clinical evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of efbemalenograstim alfa. A phase II RCT demonstrated that efbemalenograstim alfa was comparable to pegfilgrastim in its efficacy for reducing the duration of severe neutropenia (DSN) maintaining a similar safety profile [10]. Another phase III multicenter RCT among breast cancer patients undergoing myelotoxic chemotherapy demonstrated noninferiority to pegfilgrastim. It was measured by the DSN and the incidence of FN [11]. In yet another RCT use of efbemalenograstim alfa, once per cycle was just as effective for supporting neutrophil count as standard therapy without increasing any adverse events (AEs) [12]. These results suggest that efbemalenograstim alfa is a viable and effective alternative to the currently available long-acting G-CSFs.

Efbemalenograstim alfa has received the initial regulatory approval in China on May 06, 2023, specifically for managing CIN. This was followed by a second approval on November 16, 2023, expanding its use to reduce the incidence of FN in adults who have nonmyeloid malignancies. These regulatory approvals were granted following promising results from clinical studies [13, 14].

There is presence of a significant literature gap concerning that the overall safety and efficacy of efbemalenograstim alfa are due to lack of a comprehensive and high-level evidence required for a clear understanding of its clinical utility. Currently available studies suffer from critical heterogeneity in several areas such as variations in study design (from single arm to randomized controlled trials), limited and specific patient population characteristics that limit generalizability, and non-standardized parameters for key outcome reporting, for example, incidence/severity of neutropenia and quality of life. These variations limit the ability to reach a robust conclusion about efbemalenograstim alfa’s comparative efficacy against other existing treatments. This underscores the need for a systematic approach to synthesize comprehensive evidence-based data. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to address the safety and efficacy of efbemalenograstim alfa for patient at risk of CIN.

Methods

This study has been registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024628001), can be accessed from the database, and is performed according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and Cochrane Collaboration Handbook [15–17]. The completed PRISMA checklist is provided in the online supplementary file (for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553270).

Data Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was carried out up to October 22, 2025, by using electronic databases like Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Clinicaltrials.gov.in using MeSH terms like “Efbemalenograstim alfa” and “Chemotherapy induced neutropenia.” Hand-searching of the articles was also conducted. Articles published in English were included, with no restrictions on publication date or sample size. No additional systematic search of gray literature source was performed, but one additional record was included after manual searching of other literature sources. The comprehensive search strategies employed for these databases are outlined in online supplementary Table S1.

Abstract Screening

All studies identified through the systematic search were exported to reference management software Zotero for duplicate removal and to smooth the screening process. To warrant relevance according to the inclusion criteria, four reviewers independently assessed the articles, initially based on their titles and abstracts using Rayyan [18] as the screening tool, followed by a detailed full-text review. Consistency of the selection of studies was assessed by inter-rater agreement among the four reviewers during the title and abstract screening phase. Inclusion and exclusion decisions were recorded independently prior to conflict resolution using Rayyan software. Agreement was quantified using Fleiss’ kappa across the entire set of screened records (n = 7,881). Conflicts were discussed and resolved with a fifth reviewer.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Strict criteria were followed to select the studies eligible for the review. Randomized clinical trials assessing the efficacy and safety of efbemalenograstim alfa in patients at risk for CIN were selected after title and abstract screening, followed by a thorough full-text screening. Details of inclusion and exclusion criteria in PICO format are mentioned in online supplementary Table S2.

Data Extraction

Four independent investigators extracted data from the included articles into an MS Excel sheet using a structured questionnaire, which included the relevant details to be obtained from the selected studies. The efficacy outcomes of this systematic review were DSN, incidence of severe neutropenia (ISN), incidence of FN, depth of the ANC nadir, and time for the recovery of the neutrophil counts after the cancer chemotherapy. Safety endpoints included AEs that occurred during the study period. Additionally, secondary endpoints, such as the formation of antidrug antibodies, were noted. Baseline characteristics were also obtained, like the mean age of the included participants, the type of cancer the patients were being treated for, and the respective chemotherapy regimens.

Data Synthesis

After extracting all efficacy, safety, and baseline data into an Excel workbook, four independent reviewers cross-checked the information. Continuous outcomes were documented as group means ± SD or least-square means, often with 95% confidence interval (CI), while dichotomous outcomes were recorded as event counts and risks. Key study-level variables, such as population and dosing, were tabulated for easy comparison. Results were presented in detailed summary tables for each trial, showcasing point estimates for all key endpoints without pooling. A narrative synthesis highlighted consistencies and divergences across studies. A formal meta-analysis was conducted for the key efficacy parameters using the Cochrane Program Review Manager (version 5.4) [19]. We also used the Meta-Essentials software to conduct meta-regression and analyze publication bias [20].

Risk of Bias in Included Studies

Using the risk of bias 2 (RoB 2) assessment tool (developed by the Cochrane Collaboration), four review authors independently evaluated the risk of bias in each study [21, 22].

Unit-of-Analysis Issue

The term “study” has been used in the current meta-analysis as a “unit of design.” The studies with different doses of efbemalenograstim alfa were considered separate units of analysis.

Measures of Treatment Effect

The primary outcome measures included DSN in the first cycle of the chemotherapy which is a continuous variable for which the effect size was presented as the mean difference (MD) with a 95% CI (as outcomes were reported on a consistent scale [days]) and ISN in the first cycle of the chemotherapy which is a categorical variable for which the effect size was presented as risk ratio (RR) with a 95% CI. Outcome measurements were restricted to the 1st chemotherapy cycle as the incidence and severity of CIN are highest during this time and from clinical perspective it was most relevant. The effect sizes of different outcome measures from included studies were analyzed in Cochrane Program Review Manager (version 5.4) [19]. The random-effects model was used for overall between-group analyses and to get the pooled effect. The prediction interval (PI) for the co-primary outcome measures was also calculated using the Meta-Essentials software [20]. Apart from the co-primary outcomes, certain other efficacy and safety parameters were also considered.

Assessment of Heterogeneity

The chi-square test and I2 statistics were used to quantify the heterogeneity. In cases of high heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis was performed to investigate the source of the high heterogeneity.

Sensitivity Analysis

In cases of significant heterogeneity, forest plots were recreated after the removal of each study individually to assess the impact of each exclusion on the I2 value.

Meta-Regression

Meta-regression was performed to estimate how the co-primary outcome parameters (ISN and DSN in the first cycle of chemotherapy) changed with the independent variable (dose of efbemalenograstim alfa). The statistical significance of the regression coefficient was determined to assess any linear relationship between the dependent and independent variables.

Assessment of Publication Bias

Publication bias was qualitatively assessed across randomized controlled trials using the funnel plot and quantitatively using the Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test and Egger’s test [23]. The trim and fill method was used to adjust for funnel plot asymmetry.

Results

After a thorough search across multiple databases (PubMed [4,940], Embase [4,728], Web of Science [3,134], CENTRAL Cochrane [1,417], and ClinicalTrials.gov [14]), 14,233 records were identified. After removing duplicates, 7,881 articles were screened by title and abstract. Among them, 7,860 articles were excluded because they did not meet the screening criteria. Inter-rater agreement during title and abstract screening was high (Fleiss’ κ = 0.87). Subsequently, 21 full-text articles were evaluated for eligibility, along with one more record obtained from other resources (gray literature). Out of these 22 articles, 17 were excluded for specific reasons: one was a duplicate of the finally selected articles, 3 were deemed inadequate due to data limitations, and 13 were subparts of the finally selected studies. Finally, five (5) studies that met all the inclusion criteria were included in the systematic review and followed by a meta-analysis [10–12, 24, 25]. Study Characteristics

Five clinical trials evaluated the safety and efficacy of efbemalenograstim alfa in patients with breast cancer who are on different chemotherapy regimens. All the studies were randomized controlled trials comparing efbemalenograstim with a control. Among them, the study by Glaspy et al. [24] compared the study drug to a placebo. Duration and ISN, incidence of FN, ANC nadir, time to recover ANC count, and other safety parameters were assessed by the majority of them.

Several dose-ranging and dose-finding studies have identified 20 mg as the optimum fixed dose per cycle for preventing neutropenia [10, 26]. Among the five included studies, the study done by Glaspy et al. [12] administered either 240 or 320 µg/kg efbemalenograstim and compared with 6 mg pegfilgrastim. To convert the weight-dependent dose to a fixed dose, the average weight of an adult female was considered to be 77.97 kg, or approximately 171.8 pounds (lb). Subsequently, the doses of 240 and 320 micrograms per kg were calculated as 18.6 mg and 24.8 mg [27] which were close to the dose selected by other studies. Details of each study with the design, patient characteristics, and baseline findings have been summarized (Table 1).

Efficacy Outcomes

Co-Primary Outcomes

DSN in cycle 1. Five studies evaluated mean DSN and reported diverse results. A placebo-controlled study by Glaspy et al. [24] demonstrated a significant reduction of 2.6 days (1.3 vs. 3.9 days, p < 0.001). In contrast, four active-controlled trials reported therapeutic equivalence to standard G-CSFs, where differences were consistently below 0.5 days. Glaspy et al. [12] reported comparable durations between efbemalenograstim and pegfilgrastim, with differences of 0.3–0.4 days across doses. Zhang et al. [11] and Glaspy et al. [25] similarly demonstrated therapeutic equivalence to the comparators (filgrastim and pegfilgrastim, respectively) for both overall and moderate-to-severe neutropenia. These findings established the therapeutic efficacy of efbemalenograstim.

ISN in Cycle 1. Glaspy et al. [24] showed significant therapeutic benefit by decreasing the incidence from 94.9% to 69.9%. In contrast, the other four studies showed comparable efficacy against standard G-CSFs. Glaspy et al. [12] reported similar incidence rates between efbemalenograstim (66.7–72.4% across doses) and pegfilgrastim (63.3%). Zhang et al. [11] also found comparable results between the study drug and filgrastim (34.2% vs. 42.9%). Comparing with pegfilgrastim, both Ji et al. [10] and Glaspy et al. [25] similarly show equivalence (10.8% vs. 10.9% and 11.7% vs. 11.7%), respectively.

Secondary Outcomes

DSN in Cycle 2–4. Zhang et al. [11] and Glaspy et al. [25] reported low and consistent DSN (mean ranged from 0.1 to 0.2 days) across the groups in later cycles. Two studies did not report cycle 2–4 duration data.

ISN in Cycle 2–4. DSN in later cycles was reported by three studies. Gla spy et al. [11], Zhang et al. [12], and Glaspy et al. [25] demonstrated similarly low comparable incidence rates between treatment groups across subsequent cycles.

Incidence of FN. Glaspy et al. [24] showed a significant reduction in FN with placebo (4.8% versus 25.6%). Other studies by the same team (Glaspy et al. [12] and Glaspy et al. [25]) reported very low and comparable incidences (0.8–3%) in both treatment and control arms.

Antibiotic Usage. Glaspy et al. [24] found that 10.8% in the treatment group used antibiotics, and 3.6% used IV antibiotics. Compared to 33.3% and 17.9% in the control group, respectively, Glaspy et al. [25] showed 10.2% in the treatment arm used antibiotics (oral or IV), and in the control arm 8.7% used antibiotics (oral or IV).

Depth of ANC Nadir. Across studies, mean ANC nadirs (×109/L) showed varied result between efbemalenograstim and control. Glaspy et al. [24] reported the mean depth of ANC nadir to be 0.7× 109/L vs. 0.2 × 109/L in study vs. control groups. All other studies observed comparable nadir depths across study groups.

Time to Neutrophil Recovery. In all studies, efbemalenograstim groups showed comparable time to recovery. Glaspy et al. [24] found mean recovery time of 2.1 vs. 4.1 days (control). Studies by Glaspy et al. [12] and Glaspy et al. [25] also reported recovery within 1.7–2.4 days for efbemalenograstim and 1.9–4.1 days for controls. Ji et al. [10] reported time to recovery for efbemalenograstim 1.7 days (10 mg) or 0.8 days (20 mg) compared to 1.2 days (filgrastim arm). Details of efficacy parameters have been compared in Table 2.

Safety Profile

Therapy with efbemalenograstim alfa was found consistently safe across studies (Table 3). Most of the participants in both study and control groups across the trials experienced at least one AE (90–100%), which are mostly consistent with known adverse effects of chemotherapy. Alopecia was the most frequent (≈65–80%), followed by bone and back pain (≈2–12%), nausea, and leukopenia, which had no significant difference across efbemalenograstim and control groups. Incidence of serious adverse event (SAE) was low (≤6–9%) across studies, with most of them having no causal relation with the study drugs. Glaspy et al. [24] reported an overall 12.3% SAEs; among them, one was possibly control-related. Glaspy et al. [12] observed one event of SAE in 2 of the treatment arms versus four events in the control. Zhang et al. [11] noted SAEs in 5% vs. 6.7% of subjects (study vs. control); Ji et al. [10] reported SAEs in 5.1% (10 mg), 4% (20 mg) in the study groups, and 9.8% in the control. Only 1 case each on both the study and control arm was causally related. Glaspy et al. [25] found 6.1% SAEs in efbemalenograstim and 1.5% in controls. Glaspy et al. [24] and Glaspy et al. [25] reported the presence of non-neutralizing antidrug antibodies with incidence rates of 9.1% and 8.6%, respectively.

Result from Meta-Analysis

Co-Primary Outcomes

DSN in Cycle 1. All the five included studies had reported DSN in chemotherapy cycle 1. Tests for heterogeneity were significant (chi2 = 99.36; p < 0.00001; I2 = 94%). Random-effect model analysis showed a pooled MD of −0.32 (95% CI: −0.79 to 0.14, Z = 1.36; p = 0.17, PI = −2.02 to 1.38), favoring efbemalenograstim alfa group.

Sensitivity Analysis

To investigate the high heterogeneity, a sensitivity analysis was performed by removing one study at a time and by exclusion of Glaspy et al. [24], the heterogeneity became statistically nonsignificant (chi2 = 5.62; p = 0.34; I2 = 11%), without affecting the pooled result significantly. Pooled MD was found to be −0.01 (95% CI: −0.12 to 0.10, Z = 0.19; p = 0.85) after performing sensitivity analysis.

ISN in Cycle 1. All the studies that were included mentioned this outcome. Tests for heterogeneity were not significant (chi2 = 8.55; p = 0.20; I2 = 30%). Random-effect model analysis showed a pooled RR of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.73 to 1.03, Z = 1.65; p = 0.10, PI = 0.61 to 1.20), favoring efbemalenograstim alfa group.

Discussion

CIN is commonly defined as a reduction of ANC <2,000 cells/mm3 in circulation in patients receiving chemotherapy. Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) by the National Cancer Institute, version 5.0, has further stratified the severity of neutropenia into four grades. A >20% risk of CIN with chemotherapy is considered high risk, which is often seen in the management of several carcinomas. For breast cancer, the risk is highest with TAC, TCH AC-docetaxel, docetaxel-AC, doxorubicin/docetaxel, doxorubicin/paclitaxel regimens due to high myelosuppression by anthracyclines and taxanes [3, 28]. High-risk regimens for other carcinomas include ACE, ICE, DHAP, ESHAP, BEACOPP, and IGEV regimens. G-CSF is often advised in patients with a high risk of FN (>20%) after assessment of several patient and treatment factors [2].

G-CSFs, since 1991, have been extensively used in the management of CIN with notable success, which was often restricted by the need for daily dosing, low tolerance, etc. Pegylated G-CSFs have improved patient compliance, pharmacokinetics, and side effect profile. Still, there are concerns, including accumulation of PEG in vivo with potential risk of toxicity in the long term, reduced biologic activity due to steric hindrance of protein interactions, accelerated clearance from circulation, immunogenic reactions due to development of anti-PEG antibodies, increased production cost, and manufacturing complexities [4, 5, 29]. ADR associated included mild to moderate myalgia, arthralgia, bone pain, drug-induced hypersensitivity, anaphylaxis, capillary leak syndrome, and aggravation of chemotherapy-induced pulmonary toxicities [6].

Efbemalenograstim alfa, a long-acting drug (G-CSF and human IgG2-Fc fragment fusion protein), has shown promising results in preclinical and clinical studies [9, 30]. Absence of PEGylation excludes all related adverse effects, and Fc fusion leads to biological prolongation of activity in efbemalenograstim, which might improve patient-related outcomes in therapeutics. There is limited evidence regarding comparative analysis between efbemalenograstim alfa and pegylated G-CSFs with respect to pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters. This systematic review and meta-analysis represent the most methodologically rigorous synthesis of evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of efbemalenograstim alfa for the management of CIN.

Efficacy and Safety Outcome Analysis

The pooled analysis evaluated the effect of efbemalenograstim as compared to the control on the DSN but did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference. Differences in DSN were found to be statistically nonsignificant (MD −0.32 days). A similar trend was found in the ISN. Incidence of SAE among participants was lower in patients with the investigational drug compared to the control (RR = 0.71). Other safety outcomes of AEs were comparable. Similar findings after inclusion of placebo and active comparator might further imply that the majority of AEs might be related to chemotherapy and unrelated to the study or control drugs, as also indicated by the authors of the studies by Glaspy et al. [24] and Ji et al. [10].

Time to ANC recovery initially showed a trend toward increased time with study drug (MD −0.26 days) with high heterogeneity (I2 = 89%). Sensitivity analysis adjusted the heterogeneity and altered the trend of the result (MD 0.37 days).

Overall analysis also revealed modest but statistically significant improvement of ANC nadir with efbemalenograstim therapy (MD = +0.40 ×109/L, p = 0.0002). This can be attributed to specific pharmacokinetic properties, specially Fc fusion-mediated recycling via neonatal Fc receptor, resulting in prolonged exposure and sustained granulopoiesis [31]. Still due to modest effect size, the clinical significance remains doubtful in the absence of clinically relevant outcomes like DSN and FN.

Formal subgroup analysis was limited due to fewer number of included studies; however, differences between placebo-controlled and active comparator studies were explored. All these analyses highlighted the noninferior safety and efficacy profiles of efbemalenograstim, compared to placebo and standard drug. But in most cases, high heterogeneity was encountered in the preliminary forest plots reporting the outcome.

Heterogeneity Management

Heterogeneity due to the presence of a placebo-controlled study (Glaspy et al. [24]) among studies with active control is expected due to differences in effect size. Administration of various chemotherapy regimens (TA, TC, TAC, epirubicin/cyclophosphamide) across the studies also might influence the heterogeneity. Multiple geographical regions with differences in race and genomics can also have some effect. Incidence of SAE, the study by Glaspy et al. [25], showed higher cases of SAE compared to the control. The authors opined that, as SAEs due to chemotherapy were also included in the total SAE count, most of the reported SAEs described were consistent with the chemotherapy and not related to the study drug. To explore potential factors impacting the heterogeneity across the included studies, meta-regression was performed for both duration and ISN. The moderator variable identified was the dose of the study drug, and its association with effect size was tested. For both DSN and ISN, meta-regression revealed that variation in the dosage of efbemalenograstim did not significantly impact the pooled effect estimate (p > 0.05), which suggests that the treatment effect of efbemalenograstim was largely consistent across all doses. Publication bias was low in reported studies.

Strengths and Limitations

Five randomized controlled trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of the efbemalenograstim conducted across European regions, the USA, and China were compared in this review. This is the first study to evaluate the safety and efficacy parameters through advanced statistical analysis, meta-regression, and multiple approaches to identify publication bias. Studies across geographical regions and diverse population patterns will advocate for the generalizability of the result. Use of strict inclusion criteria, extensive systematic search of databases, methodical review of the studies, and analysis of multiple safety and efficacy endpoints using advanced statistical methods have further contributed to the strength of our study. Despite these strengths, the limitations in the study should also be highlighted. A limited number of studies met the inclusion criteria, restricting the strength of statistical assessment. A systematic search of all gray literature was not conducted, which may introduce potential publication bias due to the exclusion of unpublished or non-indexed studies, despite the inclusion of one additional record identified through manual searching. Studies with contrast in comparators, drug dose, and outcomes measured further introduced heterogeneity in the pooled effect. However, meta-regression was performed to mitigate the challenge. All patients included were receiving chemotherapy for breast carcinoma; therefore, its efficacy in other neoplasms needs to be established. But the limitations were expected considering the novelty of the drug and its current approved indications.

Clinical Implication

From a clinical perspective, efbemalenograstim alfa might be considered as a practical alternative to currently available G-CSFs due to its once-per-cycle dosing and long-lasting effects, and improved safety profile due to the absence of PEG-related immunogenicity. Therefore, due to largely comparable efficacy outcomes and modest improvement in ANC nadir, efbemalenograstim should be considered as an alternative therapy specifically in patients with simplified dosing preference and PEG-related adverse effects.

Conclusion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated comparable safety and efficacy of efbemalenograstim alfa compared to existing G-CSFs. In future, larger studies with active comparators and long-term follow-up should be undertaken to explore other outcomes, including long-term safety, feasibility, cost-effectiveness, real-world effectiveness, and its efficacy against both pegylated and non-pegylated controls in diverse tumor types and regimens to validate these results.
