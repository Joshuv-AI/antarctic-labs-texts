Rectal metastasis as the initial presentation of primary lung adenocarcinoma with an EGFR L858R mutation: a case report
Authors: Rui Nian, Mengzhu Wang, Juanyi Wang, Xiang Li, Jianjun Li, Ling Lin, Shushen Ji, Huihui Jiang, Wanjun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 80% of all Lung cancers, and 40% of patients with lung cancer present with distant metastases at diagnosis. Symptomatic rectal metastasis from primary lung adenocarcinoma before the onset of lung symptoms is exceedingly rare. This is the first report to describe an uncommon case of rectal metastasis from an EGFR L858R-mutant primary lung adenocarcinoma.Case presentationA 79-year-old male presented to the hospital for worsening constipation. Colonoscopy revealed a rectal mass, but initial biopsies revealed only chronic rectal mucosal inflammation. The patient received symptomatic treatment to stimulate defecation, but his symptoms of constipation worsened. As symptoms progressed to dyspnoea, cough, expectoration, bilateral lower limb weakness, and poor appetite, imaging revealed a mixed ground-glass nodule with poorly defined margins in the apical segment of the right upper lobe. Imaging also revealed thickening of the rectal wall in the mid-to-lower segments with multiple small perirectal lymph nodes. Immunohistochemical analysis of both the rectal puncture biopsy specimen and the white nodules resected during the laparoscopic peritoneal biopsy consistently revealed CK7 (+), TTF-1 (+), Napsin A (+), CK20 (−), and CDX2 (-) staining, which confirmed rectal metastasis from primary lung adenocarcinoma. After the laparoscopic transverse colostomy was performed, the patient began treatment with aumolertinib mesilate tablets that target the EGFR L858R mutation and received palliative radiotherapy for the rectal metastatic lesion.ConclusionsAlthough rectal metastasis harbouring EGFR L858R mutations from primary lung adenocarcinoma is extremely rare, clinicians should have a low threshold for pulmonary low-dose spiral CT. Background

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 80% of all Lung cancer cases, and nearly 40% of patients present with distant metastases at initial diagnosis. The brain, bones, liver, and adrenal glands are the most frequently involved metastatic sites [1, 2]. Although gastrointestinal (GI) metastases are identified in up to 11.9% of cases during autopsy [3], they are clinically evident in only 0.19–0.33% of patients [4, 5], as rectal involvement is relatively rare [6]. The presence of distant metastases generally indicates a poor prognosis. However, the advent of targeted therapies has significantly enhanced outcomes in advanced-stage NSCLC by enabling treatment decisions according to tumour molecular profiling. A landmark discovery in this context was the identification of somatic, activating epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, which confer high sensitivity to epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) [7–10]. Among these, the most prevalent are exon 19 deletions (ex19del) and the exon 21 L858R point mutation, which account for 45% and 44%, respectively [11]. Here, we report a rare clinical case in which rectal metastasis originated from primary lung adenocarcinoma with an EGFR L858R mutation, and was accompanied by intestinal symptoms.

Case presentation

In late October 2024, a 79-year-old Han Chinese male without a history of smoking or alcohol consumption began experiencing constipation, which he initially disregarded. The patient had a history of hypertension for more than 20 years, which was managed with amlodipine besylate. He was also diagnosed with hyperlipidaemia and received treatment with rosuvastatin calcium and fenofibrate for lipid control. In 2013, he experienced a cerebral infarction and subsequently underwent long-term secondary prevention therapy with simvastatin, clopidogrel, and enteric-coated aspirin. By the end of December 2024, due to worsening constipation, he presented to a nearby hospital. Colonoscopy revealed a circumferential, irregular, protruding lesion located above the dentate line in the rectum, with features including intraluminal extension, a moss-like covering on the surface, contact bleeding, and significant Luminal narrowing that did not allow for deeper insertion of the endoscope, leading to a clinical suspicion of rectal malignancy. However, histopathological examination of biopsy specimens from the lesion indicated only chronic rectal mucosal inflammation. The patient received treatment to stimulate defecation, but his symptoms of constipation worsened. At the beginning of January 2025, the patient developed additional symptoms, including dyspnoea, cough, expectoration, bilateral lower limb weakness, and poor appetite, and presented to our hospital for treatment. On January 8th, 2025, a chest CT scan revealed a mixed ground-glass nodule approximately 17 × 13 × 11 mm in size located in the apical segment of the right upper lobe. The lesion also exhibited poorly defined margins (Fig. 1). Obstruction of the adjacent bronchus was noted, and band-like dense shadows were observed in the distal bronchus. The mediastinum was centrally located, and multiple enlarged lymph nodes were observed, and of these, the largest measured approximately 23 × 13 mm and demonstrated mild enhancement after contrast agent administration. Upon radiological evaluation, the mixed ground-glass nodule in the right upper lobe was considered morphologically suspicious for malignancy, and percutaneous biopsy was recommended to obtain histopathological confirmation. However, due to the patient’s advanced age, his family refused the percutaneous biopsy.

On January 6th, 2025, a colonoscopy revealed a circumferential irregular mass located 3.0 cm from the anal verge that was characterized by an uneven surface, erosion, and contact bleeding, with severe luminal narrowing such that the endoscope could not be inserted beyond the lesion (Fig. 2A). However, although the repeated rectal biopsy still revealed nonspecific chronic inflammation, endoscopic ultrasound demonstrated that at the site of the lesion, the bowel wall was markedly thickened, as represented by heterogeneous echogenicity; the bowel wall also demonstrated disruption of the normal layer architecture, and the serosal layer was not clearly visualized (Fig. 2B). On January 7th, 2025, with the patient in the left lateral decubitus position, a digital rectal examination revealed marked anal canal stenosis, precluding finger insertion. Approximately 1 cm from the anal verge, a circumferentially distributed hard mass was palpated and was characterized by an irregular surface, limited mobility, and pronounced tenderness. No blood was observed upon glove withdrawal. On January 9th, 2025, high-resolution rectal MRI revealed a mid-to-distal rectal mass that measured approximately 7.3 cm in length and extended across the peritoneal reflection, with its inferior margin located approximately 2.1 cm from the anal verge. However, pelvic diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) demonstrated irregular thickening of the rectal wall with Luminal narrowing and a mildly hyperintense signal, accompanied by several adjacent lymph nodes that measured less than 5 mm in diameter (Fig. 3). On January 10, 2025, a rectal puncture biopsy was subsequently performed through the rectum under ultrasound guidance. The results revealed poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma that had infiltrated the muscular and fibrous tissue. The immunohistochemical staining results were as follows: CK7 (+), CK20 (−), CDX2 (-), Villin (−), SATB2 (−), SMARCA4 (+, intact), HER-2/4B5 (0), Ki-67/MX002 (+, approximately 10%), MSH2/MX061 (+), MSH6/MX056 (+), MLH1/ES05 (+), PMS2/EP51 (+), P53 (−, mutation pattern), CKpan (+), TTF-1 (+), Napsin A (+), and SOX10 (−) (Table I). On January 18, 2025, following a multidisciplinary team (MDT) assessment, the surgical team initially confirmed a diagnosis of rectal metastasis from lung adenocarcinoma.

According to the MDT discussion, radiotherapy directed at the rectal lesion carried a significant risk of radiation-induced proctitis, which could have resulted in complete intestinal obstruction. Therefore, a staged treatment strategy was recommended, which involved transverse colostomy to relieve bowel dysfunction followed by localized radiotherapy aimed at alleviating rectal Luminal stenosis. On January 22, 2025, the patient underwent laparoscopic transverse colostomy in conjunction with laparoscopic peritoneal biopsy. Intraoperative exploration revealed numerous scattered white nodules (2–5 mm in diameter) over the colonic wall, peritoneum, pelvic wall, omentum, and mesentery. Adhesiolysis between the transverse colon and small intestine was performed, and part of the gastrocolic ligament was dissected. A transverse colostomy was performed through a 2 cm incision in the upper mid-abdomen, with 4 cm of the transverse colon fixed to the abdominal wall. Using an ultrasonic scalpel, several nodules were removed for pathological evaluation. Histopathological examination confirmed metastatic poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma infiltrating fibro-adipose tissue, which was consistent with a lung origin. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated CK7 (+), TTF-1 (+), Napsin A (+), CK20 (−), CDX2 (-), Villin (−), and Ki-67 (+, approximately 5%) (Table 1). Therefore, the rectal metastatic tissues were subjected to genetic testing, and EGFR L858R and TP53 G245D and R213X were identified in these tumour tissues, which indicated a potential benefit from targeted therapy (Fig. 4). Postoperatively, the patient recovered well and was treated with aumolertinib mesilate tablets as a first-line targeted therapy on February 4th, 2025, on the basis of the presence of the EGFR L858R mutation [12, 13]. However, he subsequently experienced Lung embolism, internal jugular vein thrombosis, and carotid atherosclerosis with plaque formation. Notably, given his long-standing history of hypertension and hyperlipidaemia, carotid atherosclerosis was likely present before admission but was undiagnosed. On March 24, 2025, he received palliative radiotherapy for the rectal metastatic lesion.

Discussion and conclusions

Lung adenocarcinoma typically metastasizes to the brain, bones, liver, and adrenal glands, whereas GI involvement is rare, and rectal metastasis is exceedingly uncommon. In a study of 366 cases of primary lung cancer with GI metastases, only five involved the rectum [14]. Notably, in 1995, Johnson et al. reported the first documented case of rectal metastasis arising from pulmonary small cell carcinoma, which developed 2 years after curative resection of a mixed small and large cell bronchial tumour [15]. In contrast, the patient in our report initially presented with constipation and lacked respiratory symptoms, and thus this patient differed markedly from the previously reported case in which rectal metastases developed following treatment for known lung cancer [15, 16]. Histopathological evaluation remains a cornerstone in the differentiation of primary and secondary colorectal malignancies. Among the immunohistochemical markers, CK7 and CK20 are widely used to distinguish between tumours of lung and colorectal origin. Primary lung adenocarcinomas typically exhibit CK7 positivity and CK20 negativity, whereas primary colorectal carcinomas demonstrate the opposite profile-CK20 positivity and CK7 negativity [17, 18]. Additional lineage-specific markers, such as thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1) and CDX2, further aid in differential diagnosis. Approximately 80% of lung adenocarcinomas express both TTF-1 and napsin A, and nearly 90% express at least one of these two markers. Importantly, these markers are rarely coexpressed in adenocarcinomas of non-lung origin [19–23].

In our case, although percutaneous lung biopsy was refused because of the patient’s advanced age, consistent CK7(+), TTF-1(+), Napsin A(+), and CK20(−) staining in rectal and peritoneal lesions supported a diagnosis of rectal metastasis from primary lung adenocarcinoma.

EGFR-TKIs are the standard first-line therapy for patients with advanced NSCLC harbouring EGFR mutations. A comprehensive pooled analysis of 151 global studies involving 33,162 patients with NSCLC/adenocarcinoma revealed an overall EGFR mutation frequency of approximately 29% [24]. Notably, the prevalence of EGFR mutations exhibits marked geographic variation, ranging from 40 to 60% in Asian cohorts and from 10 to 15% in Western cohorts [25, 26]. These mutations are predominantly localized in exons 18 through 21 of the EGFR gene, which encode the tyrosine kinase domain critical for receptor signalling [27, 28]. The most prevalent EGFR-TKI-sensitive activating mutations are exon 19 deletions (ex19del), which account for 45%, and the exon 21 L858R point mutation, which is present in approximately 44%, while exon 20 insertions constitute a minority (2%) [11]. In our patient, the EGFR L858R mutation was identified in rectal metastatic tissue. Although the patient demonstrated clinical stabilization following the administration of aumolertinib mesilate tablets (Fig. 1), the long-term safety and efficacy of EGFR-TKI therapy in the context of rectal metastases from EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma remain uncertain because of the rarity of such lesions. Consequently, further large-scale clinical studies are warranted to determine the optimal treatment strategies for this subset of patients. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of rectal metastasis from an EGFR L858R-mutant lung adenocarcinoma.

Both G245D and R213X mutations occur within the DNA-binding domain of TP53 and are among the most frequently observed pathogenic mutations in lung cancer [29–31]. G245D is a missense mutation in a highly conserved residue that is critical for DNA binding, whereas R213X is a nonsense (truncating) mutation that results in premature termination and loss of functional p53 protein. These mutations are associated with loss-of-function phenotypes and may contribute to tumour progression and resistance to therapy [32]. In NSCLC, TP53 mutations, especially those within DNA-binding domains such as G245 and R213, have been consistently linked to poor prognosis, particularly when these mutations are concomitant with other driver alterations. For example, TP53 mutations are correlated with shorter progression-free survival and overall survival in patients treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors [33–35], and they may also indicate a reduced response to immune checkpoint inhibitors [36, 37]. Collectively, while TP53 is not currently a directly targetable gene, it holds potential utility as a prognostic marker and may influence future therapeutic strategies aimed at reactivating p53 function or exploiting synthetic lethality pathways.

In conclusion, the EGFR L858R mutation in a rectal metastasis from primary lung adenocarcinoma is relatively rare. Immunohistochemistry combined with molecular diagnostics is essential to accurately distinguish rectal metastases from primary rectal malignancies. Early diagnosis and precise intervention can significantly increase the quality of life and potentially prolong the survival of patients with this rare malignancy. Further clinical studies are needed to better define the therapeutic benefits and associated risks of targeted therapy in patients with EGFR-mutant lung adenocarcinoma with rectal metastasis.
