Surgical treatment of chronic pancreatitis with an inflammatory pancreatic head mass: a retrospective study
Authors: Michael Rousek, Pavel Záruba, Jiří Pudil, Eliška Kšírová, Radek Pohnán
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConservative treatment of chronic pancreatitis has only a limited effect in most patients. Surgery offers very good long-term results, even in the early stages of the disease. Unfortunately, only a minority of patients undergo surgical treatment. The aim of this work was to summarise the current treatment options for patients with an inflammatory mass of the pancreatic head. Data from patients in our study demonstrates that the surgery is a safe method, and here we compare the perioperative and early postoperative outcomes of patients who underwent a pancreatoduodenectomy and duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection for chronic pancreatitis.MethodsAll patients who underwent a pancreaticoduodenectomy or a duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection in our department between 2014 and 2022 were included in this study. Perioperative and early postoperative results were statistically analysed and compared.ResultsThirty-eight pancreaticoduodenectomies and 23 duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resections were performed. The overall mortality was 3%, whereas the in-hospital mortality after pancreaticoduodenectomy was 5%. The mortality after duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection was 0%. No statistically significant differences in the hospital stay, blood loss, and serious morbidity were found in either surgery. Operative time was significantly shorter in the duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection group.ConclusionsBoth pancreatoduodenectomy and duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection are safe treatment options. Duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection showed a statistically significant superiority in the operative time compared to pancreaticoduodenectomy. Although other monitored parameters did not show a statistically significant difference, the high risk of complications after pancreaticoduodenectomy with a mortality of 5%; maintenance of the duodenum and upper loop of jejunum, and lower risk of metabolic dysfunctions after duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection may favour duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection in recommended diagnoses. Attending physicians should be more encouraged to use a multidisciplinary approach to assess the suitability of surgical treatment in patients with chronic pancreatitis.

Background

Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is defined as a pathologic fibro-inflammatory syndrome of the pancreas in individuals with genetic, environmental, and/or other risk factors who develop persistent pathologic responses to parenchymal injury or stress [1]. Although multiple distinct molecular mechanisms initiate the pathologic process, the reaction of the pancreas to insult creates a histologically-similar result. The organ response to repeated injury or prolonged stress may become pathologic and drive cellular loss and fibrotic replacement [2]. CP typically affects the entire parenchyma of the gland. However, a subgroup of patients suffers from a local form of CP that is predominantly localised in the head or tail of the pancreas, especially in the early phase of the disease [3]. An inflammatory mass of the pancreatic head (IMPH) is almost exclusively seen in patients with alcoholic CP [4]. Radiological criterion of head enlargement is a pancreatic head diameter above 4 cm, and approximately one-third to one-half of all patients hospitalised for the treatment of CP exhibit a pancreatic head enlargement [5]. IMPH is also the most common indication for surgery [4].

Pain is the most common symptom in patients with CP and occurs in 80–90% of cases [6]. In patients with IMPH, the pain is typically located in the epigastric area, with possible irradiation to the flanks or back. The pain can be contiguous or episodic, with sudden exacerbations of variable frequency ranging from daily to once every several months. In many cases, the pain is triggered by alcohol or food intake [7]. The pain mechanism is multimodal, and the understanding of it remains challenging. The three main proposed mechanisms consist of peripheral nociception, pancreatic neuropathy and neuroplasticity, and central neuropathy and neuroplasticity [8]. Conservative management is always the first step of treatment, even in patients with clear morphological changes of the gland [9]. The conservative approach to treatment consists of alcohol abstinence, quitting cigarette smoking, and the pharmacological management of pain. This should follow the pain relief ladder, starting with paracetamol rather than non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. In cases where generalised hyperalgesia is present, low-dose opioids are to be considered [10]. Severe pain lasting at least three months is an indication for intervention [11]. Other less commonly seen indications for intervention are stenosis of the common bile duct, duodenal stenosis, or compression of the portomesenteric watershed. About 40% of patients suffer from a single pancreatic main duct stenosis in the pre-papillary duct of the pancreatic head [5]. Endoscopy and surgery are both possible treatment modalities for patients with obstructive CP; however, surgery has been shown to be superior to endoscopy in terms of pain relief while maintaining similar morbidity and mortality levels [12].

In patients with CP localised predominantly in the head of the pancreas, a surgical resection is a treatment option [9]. Pancreaticoduodenectomy (PDE) and duodenum-preserving pancreatic head resection (DPPHR) are both commonly performed in these situations. The ESCAPE Randomised Clinical Trial found surgery to be superior to endoscopic treatment, even in the early phase of the disease [13]. Despite this, the number of surgical procedures is decreasing year-to-year [14].

The aim of our study was to provide a clear review of the current treatment options for CP localised in the head of the pancreas. A comparison of the perioperative and postoperative outcomes of the two most widely-used pancreatic head resections is presented. Furthermore, the safety of the surgical resection is demonstrated in the patient data set from our study. These results may encourage physicians to adopt a more multi-modal approach to treatment, and early referral to a pancreatic surgeon may help increase the number of operations in patients, thus improving the quality of life.

Methods

A retrospective analysis of the data of patients who underwent a surgery at the Department of Surgery at the Military University Hospital in Prague and The Second Faculty of Medicine of Charles University in Prague was performed. All consecutive patients who underwent a PDE or a DPPHR for CP between 2014 and 2022 were included in the study. The monitored parameters were operative time, blood loss, hospital stay, overall morbidity, and 90-day mortality. The patients were divided into two groups according to the type of surgery performed, and the perioperative and early postoperative results were statistically analysed. The results were compared between both groups. Moreover, to minimise the risk of selection bias, patients were subdivided into two groups after PDE. Patients with a strong indication for PDE, which included tumour suspicion and groove pancreatitis were separated from other PDE indications. The only potential source of differences in the outcomes between those two subgroups was selection effects. The magnitude of differences between the two subgroups can then be treated as an indirect estimate of the magnitude of selection effects for the two main groups, PDE and DPPHR.

Statistical analysis

The between-group comparisons of continuous outcomes were performed using Welch’s t-test for the logarithm of the values to accommodate the properties of the data (lower bound at zero, positive skew). In this formulation, the null hypothesis is that the median is identical in both groups. Non-parametric comparisons with the Wilcoxon test were performed as a robustness check and qualitatively agreed. The differences in binary outcomes were analysed using logistic regression with Firth correction and were summarised with odds ratios.

Results

Basic characteristics

Between 2014 and 2022, a total of 61 resections for CP localised in the pancreatic head were performed at the Department of Surgery, Military University Hospital in Prague and The Second Faculty of Medicine of Charles University. Thirty-eight patients after PDE and 23 patients after DPPHR were included into the study. From the PDE group, the standard Whipple procedure was performed in 20 patients, whereas 18 patients underwent a pylorus-preserving PDE. In the DPPHR group, 20 patients underwent the Beger procedure, and three patients had the Frey procedure. The median age of the patients was 52 years, with a clear preponderance of males over females. Severe chronic pain was present in 63% of patients. The most common local complication was common bile duct stenosis present in 38% patients, followed by duodenal stenosis present in 18% of cases. 28% of patients had diabetes prior to the surgery. Moreover, the PDE was performed in five patients with CP due to tumour suspicion. Basic demographic data about the patients are summarised in Table 1.

Perioperative and early postoperative monitored parameters

Operative time

The median operative time for PDE was 315 min (180–600). The median operative time in DPPHR was 260 min (180–380). DPPHR showed a significantly shorter operative time compared to PDE (p = 0.004, 95% CI for ratio of medians 0.72–0.94). There was no significant difference in operative time in patients with and without a strong indication for PDE, indicating a minimal role of selection bias (p = 0.87, 95% CI for ratio of medians 0.84–1.22) (Fig. 1).

Blood loss

The median blood loss in the PDE group was 525 mL (200–4,000). The median blood loss in the DPPHR group was 500 mL (150–1,500). There was no statistical difference in comparison between the PDE and DPPHR groups, and we can exclude substantially larger blood loss from the DPPHR condition (p = 0.13, 95% CI for ratio of medians 0.51–1.1). In addition, no significant difference was found when comparing patients with and without a strong indication for PDE (p = 0.2, 95% CI for ratio of medians 0.41–1.22) (Fig. 1).

Hospital stay

No statistical difference was found when comparing hospital stay in the PDE and DPPHR groups (p = 0.39, 95% CI for ratio of medians 0.87–1.4). The median hospital stay was 12 days in patients after PDE, and 13 days in patients after DPPHR. Furthermore, no difference was found in patients with and without a strong indication for PDE (p = 0.58, 95% CI for ratio of medians 0.84–1.37) (Fig. 1). The two subgroups of patients who underwent PDE present low differences, indicating that selection effects did not play a large role.

Postoperative complications

Seven patients in the PDE group underwent a relaparotomy (18%), and five relaparotomies occurred (22%) in the DPPHR group. In the PDE group, two patients underwent relaparotomy for POPF (one postoperative pancreatic necrosis of the remnant with POPF; one dehiscence of pancreaticojejunostomy with enteral leak). One patient underwent acute portomesenteric vein reconstruction because of thrombosis. One patient underwent relaparotomy for massive bleeding from the common hepatic artery pseudoaneurysm. Two patients underwent relaparotomy for deep surgical site infection, and one patient for dehiscent laparotomy. In the DPPHR group, three patients underwent relaparotomy for insufficiency of the pancreaticojejunostomy (two patients had massive bile leak; one had POPF related sepsis). One patient underwent relaparotomy for a large postoperative intraabdominal hematoma, one for deep surgical site infection.

Overall occurrence of postoperative complications according to Clavien-Dindo grade III and higher were present in 32% of the PDE group (12/38) and 35% of the DPPHR group (8/23) (p = 0.78, 95% CI for odds ratio: 0.29–2.57). No statistical difference was found; however, due to the relatively low incidence of events in individual areas, the level of statistical uncertainty remains relatively high. In the PDE group, seven patients underwent relaparotomy, three patients developed POPF Grade B (two patients underwent CT-guided drainage, one underwent endoscopic-guided transgastric drainage). One patient underwent CT-guided liver abscess drainage, one patient underwent ultrasound-guided pleural effusion drainage, and one patient had severe central-venous-catheter-related bloodstream sepsis. In the DPPPH group, five patients underwent relaparotomy. One patient underwent a CT-guided intraabdominal abscess drainage, one underwent surgical intervention for a wound infection and one patient suffered from acute pneumonia with respiratory failure. The incidence of the clinically-relevant postoperative pancreatic fistula was 13% (5/35) in the PDE group, and 17% (4/23) in the DPPHR group. The 90-day readmission rate was 13,2% (5/38) in the PDE group and 17% (4/23) in the DPPHR group respectively. The list of selected postoperative complications can be found in Table 3.

Mortality

The overall mortality was 3% (2/61). Both patients died in the PDE group (5%; 2/38). The morality in the DPPHR group was 0% (0/23). In the first scenario, a massive bleeding from the common hepatic artery occurred, and in the second case, a postoperative pancreatic fistula grade C was present. No statistical analysis was applicable due to the low number of events in both groups.

Discussion

The true incidence of CP is unknown, but the prevalence is higher in men than in women. The general prevalence is reported as 12 cases in males versus 6 cases in women per 100,000 inhabitants [10]. In the Czech Republic, the most recent study dealing with the prevalence of CP was published more than 20 years ago. The results correspond to generally-accepted numbers, indicating the prevalence of 7.9% [15]; however, in the last 10 years, just over 100 surgeries for CP have been performed annually in the Czech Republic [14]. Considering the above-mentioned overall prevalence, this means that roughly only 13% of patients with CP undergo surgery.

Although there are many approaches to the treatment of CP, conservative management is rarely consistently effective. Often, it is necessary to proceed with an intervention. In the traditional manner, surgery is offered in the late phase of the disease after a long period of conservative management and multiple endoscopic interventions [9]. In the current reassessment of the view, early surgical treatment should be considered. A relationship between the duration of pain and pain relief after surgery was observed [12]. The delay in surgical intervention may lead to neuropathic pain, which is difficult to solve [13]. An inflammatory mass in the pancreatic head acts as a pacemaker of CP [5], and early surgery may be a way to prevent loss of residual pancreatic function in the future [16]. Therefore, the endoscopy should play a bridging role prior to the definitive surgery [13]. Endoscopies could also be reserved for patients whose condition does not allow for the surgery to be performed.

Surgical treatment of CP offers a wide range of surgical procedures. The resection procedures are suitable for the IMPH. In 1972, Beger proposed a DPPHR in an effort to find a less burdensome performance than the conventional PDE [17], and DPPHR became generally-adopted in Europe. The use of DPPHR in North America is not nearly as common; however, there are large differences between individual workplaces [4, 18]. In 1987 Frey described a new procedure consisting of drainage of the dilated pancreatic duct and limited extirpation of the pancreatic head [19]. Although the operation was based on the modification of a Partington-Rochelle procedure, it is included among the DPPHR by the group of authors [20].

A comparison of perioperative, early postoperative, and long-term postoperative results of both types of resections has already been the goal of a long list of studies. The Cochrane meta-analysis of the randomised trials showed no evidence of any difference in the mortality, adverse events, or quality of life between DPPHR and PDE [21]. However, individual studies more or less favour DPPHR [22]. This is also why the international consensus guidelines of 2020 recommend DPPHR as a preferable method in patients with enlarged pancreatic head due to CP; PDE seems to be the most-suitable surgical option for patients with groove pancreatitis and patients with suspicion of neoplasia [9]. According to the prospective randomised study, PDE and DPPHR are to be equally effective in pain relief and eventual quality of life after long-term follow-up without differences in endocrine or exocrine function [23]. However, some studies showed lower rates of exocrine insufficiency after the DPPHR procedure when compared to the PDE in the long-term follow-up [24]. A randomised controlled trial comparing DPPHR and PDE showed that long-term survival is higher in patients after the Frey procedure. This is explained by a higher preservation of the pancreatic remnant, which leads to a lower risk of diabetes and its associated complications [25]. In addition, DPPHR showed slight superiority over PDE in our set of patients. DPPHR showed a significantly shorter operation time, though other monitored parameters did not show significant differences. Furthermore, overall mortality in the DPHHR group was lower in comparison with PDE (0% vs. 5%); however, no statistical analysis was applicable in this parameter. The overall morbidity was 3%, and this generally corresponds with the low mortality reported in other studies, showing a high level of safety if the surgical approach is chosen [23].

Certain limitations of our study must be acknowledged. This is a retrospective, single pancreatic centre study. Only perioperative and early postoperative results were evaluated.

The heterogeneity of the included patients is relatively high; however, the study was intentionally designed to minimise the risk of bias. All patients were operated on by a single pancreatic team. An auxiliary analytical method to detect selection bias in the operation was used, and large selection pressures were not detected.

To our belief, a study conducted in this way has non-negligible clinical impact. It is important to repeatedly emphasise the benefits and high level of the safety of surgical resection, especially when considering previously-shown epidemiologic data. There is a large pool of patients with CP who might benefit from surgery, but have not yet been considered for the operation. Demonstrating the high degree of safety of the surgical procedure in our group of patients could encourage the attending physicians to more-frequently consider referring the patients to a pancreatic surgeon. Further discussion on a multi-modal approach to the treatment of CP could be stimulated.

Conclusions

PDE and DPPHR are both safe methods in the treatment of CP localised in the head of the pancreas. The overall mortality in our pool of patients was 3% In-hospital mortality in the PDE group was 5%. The mortality after DPPHR was 0%. DPPHR was also superior in the operative time. Other monitored parameters did not show a statistically significant difference. The high risk of complications after PDE with a mortality of 5%, maintenance of the duodenum and upper loop of jejunum, and lower risk of metabolic dysfunctions after DPPHR may encourage the preference DPPHR in recommended diagnoses. However, considering the present study in isolation, substantial uncertainty remains, though our conclusions are consistent with other sources of evidence. Recently published epidemiologic data showed that there is a large pool of patients with CP who might benefit from surgery, but have not yet been considered for the operation.
