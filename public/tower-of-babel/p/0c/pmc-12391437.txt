FOXO4-AR crosstalk in PCOS: A pivotal mechanism of anovulation
Authors: Shuangning Qian, Guojun Pan, Mingjie Fan
Published: 2025

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common reproductive endocrine and metabolic disorder in women characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory disorder, and polycystic ovary, affecting approximately 11%–13% women worldwide.1 However, the underlying mechanisms of the ovulatory dysfunction in PCOS remain incompletely understood, hindering the development of effective treatment strategies.

PCOS is a multifactorial disorder influenced by both genetic predisposition and environmental factors. While its exact etiology remains unclear, insulin resistance (IR) and subsequent compensatory hyperinsulinemia are central to its pathogenesis. Obesity and IR are frequently associated with PCOS, exacerbating its clinical manifestations.1

While hyperandrogenism is a hallmark of PCOS and sustained androgen receptor (AR) activation drives ovarian fibrosis, the mechanistic link between androgen signaling and ovulatory dysfunction remains elusive.2 The FOXO family transcription factors (FOXO1, FOXO3, and FOXO4) are critically involved in diverse cellular processes and have been widely implicated in metabolic disorders and PCOS.3 Zhou et al. firstly discovered that the FOXO4-AR negative feedback loop regulates LH surge-induced granulosa cells (GCs) proliferation, providing a partial explanation for PCOS-associated anovulation and revealing a potential novel therapeutic target for PCOS treatment.4

FOXO4-AR negative feedback loop controls granulosa cell proliferation and ovulatory dysfunction in PCOS

In this study, Zhou and colleagues identified a dysregulated molecular axis in PCOS GCs, marked by elevated AR and CCND1 expression coupled with reduced FOXO4 and CDKN1A levels. Intriguingly, both AR and FOXO4 functions as upstream regulators of LH surge signaling, coordinately modulating CDKN1A expression and GCs proliferation. Furthermore, FOXO4 knockdown in vitro promoted GCs proliferation and CDKN1A expression while suppressing CCND1. Conversely, FOXO4 overexpression elicited the opposite effects, demonstrating its regulatory role in these processes.

By mimicking the LH surge with forskolin (FSK), the researchers established FOXO4-dependent suppression of GC proliferation and cell cycle progression. After FSK treatment, the FOXO4 and CDKN1A expression levels increased, while AR and CCND1 expression levels decreased. Clinical correlations in patients with PCOS further supported these findings; the researchers identified an inverse correlation between FOXO4 expression and follicular testosterone concentrations. These findings suggest a potential link between hyperandrogenism and FOXO4 dysregulation. Mechanistically, dihydrotestosterone (DHT) treatment confirmed that AR-mediated androgenic signaling suppresses FOXO4 expression, providing mechanistic insight into PCOS-associated ovulatory dysfunction. In addition, FOXO4 may reciprocally regulate AR protein stability via proteasomal degradation.

Using a DHEA-induced PCOS rat model, the researchers observed consistent in vivo findings. Lentiviral short hairpin RNA-mediated FOXO4 knockdown exacerbated PCOS-like phenotypes, including elevated AR expression and hyperandrogenism-associated anovulation, further supporting a protective role of FOXO4 in ovulation regulation. In conclusion, these results uncover a previously unrecognized FOXO4/CDKN1A-AR/CCND1 regulatory axis driving ovulatory defects in PCOS, bridging hyperandrogenism and GCs cell cycle dysregulation while revealing actionable therapeutic opportunities.

Perspective and future directions

While the AR signaling pathway has been most extensively characterized in prostate cancer, emerging evidence highlights its functional significance in other malignancies. Recent studies have documented AR overexpression and potential oncogenic roles in non-prostate cancers, including hepatocellular and gastric carcinomas,5 suggesting that AR-dependent interorgan crosstalk may play a critical role in disease progression. As a nuclear receptor, AR is activated by its natural ligands—androgens. Androgen biosynthesis initiates with cholesterol side-chain cleavage mediated by cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1 (CYP11A1), followed by conversion of testosterone to the more potent metabolite DHT via 5α-reductase. Experimental evidence shows DHT-induced FOXO4 downregulation is AR-dependent, as flutamide blockade restores FOXO4 levels, confirming androgenic regulation of this pathway in PCOS pathogenesis.4 Notably, artemisinins have recently been shown to ameliorate PCOS by directly binding to mitochondrial Lon peptidase 1, a key regulator of CYP11A1 protein stability, thereby suppressing excessive androgen synthesis.6 Beyond their canonical synthesis in gonadal and adrenal tissues, androgens are also synthesized in peripheral tissues such as the liver, where they exert systemic effects on fat distribution, muscle mass, and bone density.5 A groundbreaking study has identified a previously uncharacterized microbiota-derived bile acid (BA) that functions as an AR antagonist, suppressing tumor progression through CD8+ T cell-intrinsic AR signaling. This reveals a previously unrecognized microbiota-BA-AR regulatory axis in cancer immunology.7 Parallel research demonstrates that the Bacteroides vulgatus-glycodeoxycholic acid-interleukin-22 axis modulates both IR and ovarian dysfunction in PCOS.8 Intriguingly, the same team discovered an additional BA-independent pathway involving the microbial metabolite agmatine, which acts as a farnesoid X receptor agonist to exacerbate PCOS phenotypes.9 Collectively, these findings position microbiota-derived metabolites (including BAs) as upstream molecular regulators of AR signaling.

While Zhou et al. have established GC AR signaling as a critical node in PCOS pathogenesis, the multifactorial etiology of PCOS warrants future investigation into its systemic crosstalk with other organ systems. However, certain study limitations must be addressed to advance this research field. Primarily, the reliance on KGN human ovarian granulosa cell lines in the current in vitro studies could be improved by employing more physiologically relevant models, such as organoids or iPSC-derived GCs, which better recapitulate human ovarian microenvironments. Second, although this study focused on FOXO4’s role in GCs, emerging evidence suggests broader FOXO family involvement in PCOS pathogenesis. For instance, TOX3 (a PCOS-susceptibility gene) regulates FOXO1 in glucose metabolism, implying FOXO1 may contribute to IR in PCOS. This warrants further investigation to elucidate potential crosstalk between FOXO isoforms in PCOS progression.10 Third, the identification of the FOXO4-AR regulatory loop in GCs proliferation provides a compelling rationale for accelerated screening of therapeutic compounds targeting this pathway for PCOS treatment.

In summary, Zhou et al. establish a novel paradigm in PCOS pathophysiology by elucidating the FOXO4-AR negative feedback loop that critically regulates GC proliferation and subsequent ovulatory dysfunction (Figure 1).

Schematic of the proposed AR-FOXO4 feedback loop in PCOS pathophysiology

Gut microbiota dysbiosis, obesity, and insulin resistance contribute to the pathogenesis of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). PCOS is characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology. Elevated androgens activate the androgen receptor (AR) signaling pathway, which disrupts granulosa cell (GC) proliferation and impairs ovulation. Concurrently, hyperandrogenism suppresses FOXO4, a key upstream regulator of luteinizing hormone (LH) surge, leading to decreased CDKN1A and increased CCND1 expression, further inhibiting GC proliferation and exacerbating anovulation. Additionally, FOXO1, another member of the FOXO family, plays a critical role in obesity and insulin resistance, suggesting its potential involvement in PCOS progression. Notably, gut microbiota-derived bile acids can also activate AR signaling, reinforcing the hyperandrogenic milieu. These findings highlight a central AR-FOXO regulatory network, integrating endocrine, metabolic, and microbial factors, and provide novel insights into the mechanistic underpinnings of PCOS.
