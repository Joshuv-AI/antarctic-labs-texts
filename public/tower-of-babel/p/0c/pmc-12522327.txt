Emotion regulation, coping strategies, and burnout among Chinese competitive athletes: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Jianling Zuo, Yibing Bai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis mixed-methods study explored the relationships between emotion regulation, coping strategies, and burnout among 286 Chinese competitive athletes (aged 18–28, 54% female) from diverse sports and competition levels across China. Quantitative data, collected via the Trait Meta-Mood Scale, Brief COPE Inventory, and Reduced Exhaustion Depression and Reduced Accomplishment Scale, revealed that emotional clarity and repair, alongside adaptive coping strategies (e.g., positive reframing, emotional support), were negatively associated with burnout, while maladaptive strategies (e.g., denial, self-blame) were positively linked to burnout. Mediation analyses indicated that coping strategies, such as positive reframing and denial, partially mediated the emotion regulation-burnout relationship. Qualitative interviews with 23 athletes identified four themes: (1) Emotion Regulation as a Dynamic Process, (2) The Interplay of Coping Strategies, (3) The Lived Experience of Burnout, and (4) The Social Construction of Resilience and Vulnerability. These findings highlight the complex interplay of emotion regulation, coping, and social support in mitigating burnout, suggesting that interventions should enhance adaptive strategies and foster supportive environments to promote athlete well-being.Keywords: Athlete burnout, Emotion regulation, Coping strategies, Mixed-methods, Chinese athletes, Resilience, Vulnerability

Introduction

Burnout in competitive sports—defined by emotional and physical exhaustion, a diminished sense of accomplishment, and detachment from training and competition [1, 2, 3]—poses a widespread challenge for athletes at all levels. This condition jeopardizes mental health, undermines performance, and reduces engagement [4, 5]. In China, the issue is especially critical. Intense athletic demands, driven by policies aimed at global success, significantly heighten the risk of burnout. This context calls for a deeper exploration of its causes and preventive measures tailored to these unique pressures [6, 7].

Research consistently ties burnout to chronic stressors, including rigorous training schedules, unrelenting performance expectations, and limited personal control [8, 9]. These pressures often reflect a persistent imbalance between the demands placed on athletes and the resources available to meet them [10, 11, 12]. Yet, not all athletes respond the same way. Individual differences in managing stress—particularly through emotion regulation and coping strategies—play a pivotal role in determining who thrives and who falters under such strain [13, 14, 15, 16].

Emotion regulation, the ability to manage how emotions are experienced and expressed [17], is a key factor in reducing distress and fostering resilience [18, 19, 20]. Athletes skilled in maintaining emotional clarity and repairing their mood tend to experience less burnout; these abilities help sustain motivation and well-being [21, 22]. However, simply being aware of one’s emotions without the tools to manage them can backfire. This may lead to increased rumination and anxiety, potentially worsening burnout [23, 24].

Coping strategies—the thoughts and behaviors athletes use to handle stress [25]—also shape burnout outcomes. Adaptive approaches, such as problem-focused coping (e.g., planning) or positive reframing, are linked to a lower risk of burnout [26, 27]. In contrast, maladaptive strategies like denial or self-blame often amplify exhaustion and negativity [13, 28]. While these findings are compelling, few studies have examined how emotion regulation and coping strategies work together to influence burnout, especially among Chinese athletes. Cultural and social dynamics may uniquely affect stress responses in this population.

This gap underscores the need to investigate how these two factors interact to influence burnout in Chinese competitive athletes. Understanding their interplay could pinpoint specific processes to target in prevention and intervention efforts. While surveys and standardized scales have been valuable for identifying broad patterns, a mixed-methods approach promises deeper, more contextualized insights. By adding qualitative data, researchers can uncover personal narratives that reveal how individual experiences, training environments, and cultural norms intersect with statistical trends.

With this in mind, the present study adopts a mixed-methods design to explore the relationships among emotion regulation, coping strategies, and burnout in a sample of Chinese competitive athletes. The quantitative phase measures various aspects of emotion regulation and coping to assess their contributions to burnout, adjusting for demographic variables. The qualitative phase then enriches these findings by examining how athletes articulate their emotional experiences, develop coping strategies, and perceive the onset and progression of burnout. By blending these approaches, this study seeks to offer a nuanced perspective on the factors that either shield athletes from burnout or heighten its impact. Ultimately, these insights aim to guide tailored, context-specific interventions and advance the broader conversation on athlete well-being in China.

Literature review

Theoretical framework: self-determination theory (SDT)

This study is grounded in Self-Determination Theory (SDT [29]), which posits that individuals have three basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—central to motivation and personality development [30, 31]. Autonomy refers to feeling in control of one’s actions, competence to feeling effective, and relatedness to feeling connected to others. When these needs are satisfied, individuals experience intrinsic motivation, characterized by enjoyment and volition, which enhances performance, well-being, and persistence—particularly relevant in competitive sports [32, 33, 29]. However, unmet needs can lead to stress and motivational depletion, increasing burnout risk [34].

Athletes with satisfied needs are more likely to be intrinsically motivated, buffering against stress and reducing burnout [35, 32, 36], whereas those feeling pressured or unsupported may face diminished motivation and greater burnout risk [14]. SDT also distinguishes between intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, with autonomous extrinsic forms (e.g., identified regulation, where activities are valued) linked to better well-being than controlled forms (e.g., external regulation, driven by rewards [30, 29, 31]).

Building on this, our study examines how emotion regulation and coping strategies interact with motivation and psychological needs to influence burnout. Effective emotion regulation supports autonomy and competence by sustaining motivation under pressure [37]. Adaptive coping, such as positive reframing and seeking support, fosters need satisfaction and reduces burnout [36]. We hypothesize that athletes with strong emotion regulation and adaptive coping will better satisfy their needs, enhancing intrinsic motivation and lowering burnout [14, 34]. Those with poor regulation or maladaptive coping, however, may face unmet needs and increased burnout risk [28]. Through SDT, this study aims to deepen understanding of burnout factors and inform interventions for promoting athlete well-being and sustainable sporting experiences.

Athlete burnout

Athlete burnout has become a critical concern in sports psychology, manifesting as a multifaceted syndrome of emotional and physical exhaustion, reduced sense of accomplishment, and sport devaluation [1, 5]. This condition threatens athletes’ mental health, performance quality, and career longevity, driven by chronic stressors inherent in competitive sports, such as intense training loads, performance pressures, and limited control over one’s athletic path [38, 3]. Personal factors, like perfectionism, amplify susceptibility, as athletes with perfectionist tendencies often face heightened self-imposed pressure and an unyielding drive for excellence [13].

Smith’s (1986) Cognitive-Affective Stress Model provides a foundational lens, suggesting burnout stems from a persistent mismatch between perceived demands and resources, triggering stress appraisal and subsequent emotional and physical responses [12]. Raedeke and Smith (2001) reinforced this, finding that athletes with elevated perceived stress reported more burnout symptoms, emphasizing the pivotal role of cognitive appraisal [11]. Research identifies key predictors, including overtraining, competitive anxiety, and insufficient recovery [39], while social support serves as a buffer, lowering burnout risk [40, 14]. Conversely, a lack of support consistently heightens burnout symptoms, highlighting the need for a supportive athletic environment.

Beyond performance deficits, burnout’s impact extends to well-being. Affected athletes often experience depression, anxiety, and diminished quality of life [4]. This sometimes leads to sport dropout—a significant loss for both individuals and organizations [9]. Interventions typically target stress management, recovery, and balanced lifestyles. Mindfulness-based approaches reduce symptoms by fostering present-moment focus [41]. Autonomy-supportive coaching also aligns sport with personal values to curb burnout risk [34]. Glandorf et al.’s (2023) systematic review links burnout to mental health challenges like depression, anxiety, and insomnia, alongside reduced life satisfaction [8]. Crowell and Madigan (2022 [42]) and Olsson et al. (2021 [43]) pinpoint perfectionism—particularly fear of negative evaluation—as a key driver, amplifying stress and negative self-perceptions.

The connection to physical health is less straightforward. Glandorf et al. (2023 [8]) noted mixed evidence for direct effects. However, burnout may indirectly impair health by decreasing motivation for sleep, nutrition, or recovery practices, potentially weakening immunity and raising injury risk. Further longitudinal research is needed to clarify these links, focusing on markers like immune function and sleep quality. Protective factors include emotional intelligence (EQ). EQ bolsters stress management and resilience through self-awareness and social skills [44]. Supportive coaching that emphasizes mastery goals and open communication is also protective [45]. Intrinsic motivation and engagement also mitigate burnout. They foster persistence and flow states—optimal experiences of immersion—that sustain a positive outlook [46].

In conclusion, athlete burnout, with its profound implications for mental and potentially physical health, demands a multifaceted response. Addressing perfectionism, enhancing emotional intelligence, cultivating supportive coaching, and nurturing intrinsic motivation are essential strategies to build resilience and prevent burnout, ensuring athletes maintain well-being and a sustainable relationship with sport.

Emotion regulation

Emotion regulation, the ability to influence how we experience and express emotions [17, 23], stands as a key factor in mental fitness. It goes beyond suppressing negative emotions to strategically managing them for well-being and performance. Research consistently underscores cognitive reappraisal—reframing situations to adjust their emotional impact—as an effective strategy. It is linked to reduced negative affect and greater resilience [18, 47]. In contrast, suppression, often used to push emotions aside, tends to increase physiological stress and emotional strain [48]. This skill extends its impact to performance. Athletes adept at regulating emotions under pressure perform better and recover faster from setbacks [19]. Similarly, students with adaptive regulation strategies face less academic stress and achieve more [49], demonstrating its value across challenging contexts. Emotion regulation also shapes interpersonal relationships. Effective regulators enjoy more satisfying connections [50]. Reappraisal enhances conflict resolution [51], while chronic suppression correlates with relational difficulties [52].

The neural basis of emotion regulation involves the prefrontal cortex (PFC), which modulates amygdala activity during reappraisal to temper emotional reactivity [53, 54]. Interventions like mindfulness-based programs and cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) bolster these skills, fostering resilience through present-moment awareness and healthier reappraisal habits [55, 56]. In sport psychology, emotion regulation is pivotal in preventing burnout—a syndrome of exhaustion, reduced accomplishment, and sport devaluation [1]. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) frames this link, showing that athletes with intrinsic motivation and autonomy, supported by strong regulation, experience lower burnout [35, 32, 22]. Conversely, those driven by extrinsic pressures struggle to manage emotions amid setbacks, increasing burnout risk [32].

Coping strategies further influence this relationship. Adaptive methods like problem-solving and social support mitigate stress and burnout [57, 58]. Maladaptive tactics such as avoidance or rumination intensify emotional exhaustion [58]. Interpersonal emotion regulation highlights team dynamics. Supportive climates with emotionally adept teammates boost enjoyment and commitment [22], creating a ripple effect of resilience. Mindfulness training offers additional promise. It enhances regulation and reduces burnout through emotional acceptance [59, 60], especially in high-pressure situations where maintaining focus is critical.

In conclusion, emotion regulation equips athletes to navigate the emotional demands of competitive sport. By fostering intrinsic motivation, adaptive coping, supportive team environments, and mindfulness, coaches and psychologists can strengthen these skills, enhancing performance and safeguarding mental well-being against burnout.

Coping strategies

Coping strategies, fundamental tools for managing stress and navigating life’s challenges [61, 25], are central to psychological well-being. Research identifies two primary types: problem-focused and emotion-focused. Problem-focused coping directly addresses stressors through actions like planning, seeking information, or developing skills [26, 62], consistently linked to reduced stress and stronger problem-solving abilities [63, 27, 64]. For controllable stressors, such as an overwhelming workload, this proactive approach is often most effective [65]. Conversely, emotion-focused coping manages emotional responses via strategies like social support, relaxation techniques, or cognitive reframing [66, 25, 67]. While less optimal for addressable stressors [68], it proves valuable when stressors are unchangeable, with social support notably enhancing resilience and reducing distress [69].

Effective stress management relies on flexibility. It integrates both coping types to match specific stressors [70]. Studies demonstrate that combining problem-focused efforts, such as creating schedules, with emotion-focused tactics, like deep breathing, yields better outcomes [71, 72, 73]. Beyond stress, coping influences health outcomes. Adaptive strategies improve quality of life and lower distress in chronic illness [74, 75, 76, 77, 78]. In workplace settings, problem-focused coping, including time management and colleague support, boosts satisfaction and curbs burnout [79, 80]. Maladaptive approaches like avoidance or denial, however, increase stress and impair performance [81, 82, 83]. Cultural factors also shape coping preferences. Collectivist cultures favor social support, while individualist ones emphasize problem-solving [84, 86], highlighting the need for culturally sensitive interventions.

For athletes, coping strategies are critical in preventing burnout—a state of emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced accomplishment [1]. High stress strongly predicts burnout [15, 85], underscoring the importance of effective coping. Emotion regulation enhances this process, with strategies like reappraisal and acceptance enabling athletes to stay composed under pressure [87, 88]. Problem-focused coping, such as seeking solutions, reduces burnout risk [89, 28], whereas avoidance heightens it [28]. Perfectionism adds complexity: self-oriented perfectionism can motivate adaptive coping or, through self-criticism, exacerbate burnout, while socially prescribed perfectionism, tied to external pressures, consistently increases risk via anxiety and avoidance [89].

Motivation and team dynamics further influence this interplay, with intrinsic motivation and hope buffering stress through adaptive coping [90, 91, 92]. Supportive team climates, encouraging communication and collaboration, also bolster well-being [88]. In conclusion, coping strategies are indispensable in combating athlete burnout. Promoting problem-focused coping, a growth mindset, supportive teams, and intrinsic motivation equips athletes to manage stress and thrive in competitive sports.

Methods

Participants

To ensure a representative sample of China’s diverse athletic population, this study recruited 286 competitive athletes through collaborations with various sporting organizations across different provinces, including Guangdong, Jiangsu, and Sichuan. We employed a stratified sampling strategy to ensure adequate representation of athletes from each province. Specifically, we collaborated with provincial sporting organizations to identify eligible athletes and invite them to participate. To achieve a balanced gender distribution, we set recruitment quotas for male and female athletes in each province.

These athletes, with an average age of 21.0 years (SD = 3.2) and ranging from 18 to 28 years old, hailed from a balanced mix of genders (54% female, 46% male). To capture the full spectrum of training demands and competitive pressures, participants were recruited from a variety of individual and team sports. Basketball (25%), Track and Field (18%), Swimming (15%), Gymnastics (12%), and Badminton (10%) were the most prominent, with other sports such as Volleyball, Table Tennis, and Soccer making up the remaining 20%. Competition level also varied, with 15% competing at the national level (championships or provincial teams), 30% at the regional level (regional competitions or provincial leagues), and 55% at the city/provincial level within organized training programs. All participants met the following inclusion criteria: (a) aged between 18 and 28 years; (b) a minimum of one year’s experience in competitive training (average 5.2 years, SD = 2.1, range 2–10 years); (c) currently injury-free; (d) no history of diagnosed mental health disorders; and (e) actively participating in organized training programs and competitions at the city/provincial, regional, or national level. Athletes who were unable to provide informed consent or who did not complete the full battery of questionnaires were excluded from the study.

In addition to the 286 athletes who participated in the quantitative phase of the study, a subset of 23 athletes was purposefully selected for semi-structured interviews. These athletes were chosen to represent a range of emotion regulation, coping strategies, and burnout profiles, as identified in the quantitative data. This purposive sampling strategy aimed to maximize the diversity of perspectives and experiences captured in the qualitative data.

The study protocol, adhering to ethical guidelines outlined by the Chinese Psychological Society, was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hebei North University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study procedures ensured participant confidentiality and anonymity throughout.

Instruments

Quantitative measures

To assess emotion regulation, the Trait Meta-Mood Scale (TMMS) developed by Salovey et al. [93] was used in its standard three-subscale form, consisting of Emotional Attention, Clarity, and Repair. This 30-item self-report questionnaire measures individuals’ typical ways of attending to, clarifying, and repairing their emotions. Each subscale reflects a distinct aspect of emotion regulation, with higher scores indicating greater use of that dimension. The TMMS has demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.80) and construct validity in various populations, including athletes [94]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for Emotional Attention, Clarity, and Repair ranged from 0.82 to 0.86.

Coping strategies were assessed using the Brief COPE Inventory [95]. This 28-item measure comprises 14 subscales, capturing different coping approaches: active coping, planning, positive reframing, acceptance, humor, religion, using emotional support, using instrumental support, self-distraction, denial, venting, substance use, behavioral disengagement, and self-blame. Participants rated the frequency with which they employed each strategy on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (I haven’t been doing this at all) to 4 (I’ve been doing this a lot). The Brief COPE is a widely used and psychometrically sound instrument with established reliability and validity across diverse samples [95, 96]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha ranged from 0.71 to 0.84 for the Brief COPE subscales. While some values in the lower end of this range are moderate, they are consistent with prior research using this instrument. This is often attributed to the inherent brevity of its subscales, many of which contain only two items; internal consistency estimates like Cronbach’s alpha are known to be lower for scales with fewer items [95].

Athlete burnout was assessed using the Reduced Exhaustion Depression and Reduced Accomplishment Scale (RED-A) by Raedeke and Smith [11]. This 10-item measure specifically targets the three core dimensions of athlete burnout: emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced sense of accomplishment. Scores on each subscale range from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater levels of burnout. The RED-A has been validated for use with athletes and demonstrates good internal consistency and convergent validity with other burnout measures [97]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha for the RED-A subscales were 0.85 for emotional exhaustion, 0.78 for cynicism, and 0.81 for reduced sense of accomplishment.

Procedures

Participants in the quantitative phase of the study completed a battery of questionnaires online. A secure online platform was used to ensure data privacy and confidentiality. Participants were provided with a unique link to access the questionnaires, which they could complete at their own convenience, either at their training facilities or at home. Clear instructions were provided at the beginning of each questionnaire, and participants were able to contact the research team via email or phone if they had any questions or concerns during the completion process. The questionnaires were self-administered, allowing athletes to respond at their own pace and in a comfortable environment.

Following the completion of the quantitative phase, the 23 athletes selected for the qualitative phase were contacted by a member of the research team to schedule an interview. Interviews were conducted in-person at the athletes’ training facilities, in a private room to ensure confidentiality and minimize distractions. The interviews were conducted by trained research staff who followed a semi-structured interview guide. The interviewer began by establishing rapport with the athlete and explaining the purpose of the interview. The interviewer then proceeded with the interview questions, allowing for flexibility to probe further into interesting or relevant responses. All interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ permission and later transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Data analysis

Data analysis for the quantitative phase of the study was conducted using SPSS (version 28). Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables, including means, standard deviations, and frequencies. To examine the relationships between emotion regulation, coping strategies, and athlete burnout, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was employed to assess the predictive power of emotion regulation and coping strategies on athlete burnout, controlling for demographic variables such as age, gender, and type of sport.

Following the recommendations of Hayes [98], PROCESS macro (version 4.0) was used to test the mediating role of coping strategies in the relationship between emotion regulation and athlete burnout. Specifically, we tested a parallel mediation model with multiple mediators to examine the indirect effects of emotion regulation on athlete burnout through various coping strategies. Bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was used to generate 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects, as recommended by Hayes and Preacher [99].

The qualitative data from the semi-structured interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis [100]. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and then systematically coded to identify recurring themes and patterns related to emotion regulation, coping strategies, and athlete burnout. The coding process involved an iterative approach, with two researchers independently coding the transcripts and then comparing and discussing their codes to ensure consistency and reliability. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Data collection for the qualitative phase continued until theoretical saturation was achieved. This was determined when no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews, indicating that additional data collection would likely yield redundant information regarding the core constructs of emotion regulation, coping strategies, and burnout experiences among the athletes. For instance, after 18 interviews, the rate of new theme emergence slowed considerably, and by the 23rd interview, no entirely new conceptual categories were identified. The identified themes were then organized into a coherent narrative that captured the key findings from the qualitative data.

Indirect effects of emotion regulation on burnout through coping strategies

Findings indicated that Positive Reframing partially mediated the inverse relationship between Clarity and burnout, suggesting that athletes who clearly identify their emotions may be more likely to reinterpret stressful experiences in constructive ways. Meanwhile, Denial and Self-Blame emerged as mediators of the negative association between Repair and burnout; in other words, athletes who struggle to recover from negative emotional states may rely more on maladaptive coping, escalating their burnout risk. Qualitative section

Thematic analysis of the semi-structured interviews, following the six-phase approach outlined by Braun and Clarke (2006), yielded four overarching themes that provide deeper insights into the quantitative findings: (1) Emotion Regulation as a Dynamic Process, (2) The Interplay of Coping Strategies, (3) The Lived Experience of Burnout, and (4) The Social Construction of Resilience and Vulnerability.

Emotion regulation as a dynamic process

The interviews revealed that emotion regulation is not a static trait but rather a dynamic process that unfolds in response to the ongoing demands and challenges of competitive sports. Athletes described actively engaging with their emotions, utilizing a combination of cognitive and behavioral strategies to monitor, evaluate, and modify their emotional responses. This dynamic process was characterized by both proactive and reactive strategies.

Proactive emotion regulation involved anticipating and preparing for emotionally challenging situations. Athletes described using techniques like mental imagery, goal setting, and self-talk to manage their emotions before and during competitions. For instance, a national-level badminton player shared, “Before a big match, I always take some time to visualize myself on the court. I picture myself staying calm and focused, even if I’m facing a tough opponent or the crowd is loud. This helps me to control my nerves and play my best.” (Athlete 21). This athlete’s proactive use of mental imagery highlights how athletes can prepare themselves emotionally for challenging situations.

Reactive emotion regulation involved responding to emotions as they arose. A regional-level basketball player explained how he uses reappraisal to manage his frustration after a missed shot: “When I miss, I used to get really down on myself. But now, I try to reframe the situation. I tell myself that missing a shot is part of the game, and that it’s more important to focus on the next play and what I can learn from the mistake.” (Athlete 8). This athlete’s ability to reappraise setbacks demonstrates the adaptive nature of reactive emotion regulation. Another athlete, a national-level swimmer, spoke about the importance of accepting difficult emotions: “Sometimes I feel overwhelmed by the pressure to perform. In the past, I would try to push those feelings away, but I’ve learned that it’s better to acknowledge them and accept that it’s okay to feel that way. It actually helps me to move forward.” (Athlete 12).

However, the regulatory process was not always a clear choice between a successful adaptive strategy and a maladaptive one. Many athletes described being in ambiguous, transitional states where they struggled to apply coping skills effectively. This “messy middle ground” highlights the difficulty of managing persistent stress. For example, a regional-level volleyball player who was struggling with confidence after being benched explained:

“My coach told me to ‘focus on the process’ and stay positive, and I tried. I really did. I’d do the self-talk, you know, ‘You’ve got this, you’ll get back out there.’ Some days it would help me get through practice. But other days, it just felt hollow, like I was lying to myself. So I’d end up just putting on my headphones and blasting music on the way home, trying to not think about it at all. It wasn’t really denial, but it wasn’t exactly facing it either. I was just… stuck in the middle, trying to function.” (Athlete 15). This narrative captures an ambiguous regulatory state, where an athlete attempts an adaptive strategy (self-talk) but, finding it only partially effective, shifts to a more neutral, numbing strategy (distraction) without fully committing to avoidance or denial.

Nevertheless, not all athletes with strong Repair skills consistently managed their emotions effectively. A national-level gymnast described struggling after a significant setback: “I’m usually good at getting over things quickly, but after bombing that routine at nationals, I couldn’t let it go. I kept pushing it out of my mind, pretending it didn’t happen, and that just left me feeling more drained and distant from the sport.” (Athlete 14). This athlete’s avoidance of emotional processing reflects a reliance on Denial, which may heighten burnout risk despite generally effective Repair abilities.

The effectiveness of these strategies varied depending on the individual athlete, the specific situation, and the nature of the emotions involved. However, athletes who demonstrated greater emotional awareness and flexibility in their emotion regulation approaches tended to report lower levels of burnout and greater well-being. This finding aligns with the quantitative results, which showed a significant negative association between emotion regulation and burnout.

The interplay of coping strategies

Athletes described utilizing a diverse repertoire of coping strategies to navigate the stressors and challenges of competitive sports. The interviews highlighted the interplay between different coping strategies, with athletes often employing a combination of problem-focused, emotion-focused, and avoidance-focused coping.

Problem-focused coping involved taking active steps to address the source of stress. A city/provincial-level track and field athlete explained how she uses planning to manage her demanding training schedule: “When I’m facing a difficult training regimen, it can feel overwhelming. So I break it down into smaller, more manageable goals. This helps me stay motivated and focused, and it feels less daunting.” (Athlete 3). This athlete’s use of planning demonstrates how problem-focused coping can help athletes manage challenges effectively.

Emotion-focused coping involved regulating emotional responses to stress. Athletes described using strategies like seeking social support, positive reframing, and emotional expression to manage their feelings. A regional-level gymnast emphasized the importance of social support: “When I’m feeling stressed or anxious before a competition, I talk to my teammates or my coach. They always listen and offer support, which helps me feel better and more prepared.” (Athlete 17). This athlete’s reliance on her social support network highlights the role of emotion-focused coping in managing stress.

Avoidance-focused coping involved disengaging from the stressor. A national-level swimmer admitted to occasionally using avoidance to cope with pressure: “Sometimes, when the pressure gets really intense, I just try to ignore it and pretend it’s not there. It’s like I’m trying to escape from it all for a little while. But I know that’s not always the best way to deal with things in the long run.” (Athlete 12). This athlete’s awareness of the limitations of avoidance-focused coping underscores the complexities of coping in high-pressure environments.

Athletes also revealed how maladaptive coping could emerge even among those with adaptive tendencies. A regional-level track athlete noted: “I’m usually pretty good at staying positive, but when I don’t hit my times, I start thinking it’s all my fault—like I didn’t train hard enough or I’m just not cut out for this. That self-criticism wears me down and makes it tougher to keep going.” (Athlete 7). This experience shows how Self-Blame can creep in despite generally strong emotion regulation, potentially linking Repair efforts to increased burnout.

The qualitative data revealed that the effectiveness of coping strategies depended not only on the type of strategy used but also on the context and the individual’s needs. While problem-focused and emotion-focused coping were generally associated with positive outcomes, avoidance-focused coping tended to be less effective and, in some cases, even detrimental. This finding aligns with the quantitative results, which showed that changes in coping strategies moderated the relationship between emotion regulation and burnout.

The lived experience of burnout

The interviews provided rich insights into the lived experience of burnout among athletes. Burnout was described as a multifaceted phenomenon encompassing physical, emotional, and cognitive exhaustion, as well as feelings of cynicism, detachment, and reduced accomplishment. Athletes’ narratives revealed the pervasive impact of burnout on various aspects of their lives, including their athletic performance, relationships, and overall sense of self.

A regional-level swimmer described the emotional toll of burnout: “It’s like a feeling of emptiness and disillusionment. It’s like I’ve lost my passion for the sport, and nothing seems to matter anymore. I used to love competing, but now it just feels like a chore.” (Athlete 5). This athlete’s experience highlights the emotional exhaustion and loss of motivation that often accompany burnout.

Another athlete, a national-level badminton player, described the cognitive impact of burnout: “When I’m burned out, I feel like I’m on autopilot. I’m just going through the motions in training, and I don’t have the same energy or enthusiasm. It’s hard to focus, and I make careless mistakes that I wouldn’t normally make.” (Athlete 21). This athlete’s experience demonstrates how burnout can affect cognitive functioning and performance.

A city/provincial-level gymnast shared how burnout affected her relationships: “When I’m feeling burned out, I tend to withdraw from my friends and family. I don’t feel like socializing, and I just want to be alone. It’s like I’m putting up a wall between myself and the people I care about.” (Athlete 17). This athlete’s experience illustrates the social isolation that can result from burnout.

The qualitative data also highlighted the individual variability in the experience of burnout. A national-level track and field athlete described a sudden onset of burnout: “It was like flipping a switch. One day I was feeling fine, and the next day I was completely drained. I had no energy, no motivation, and I just wanted to quit.” (Athlete 19). In contrast, a regional-level basketball player described a more gradual decline: “It was a slow burn. I started to feel more and more tired, and my performance started to slip. It took me a while to realize that I was burned out.” (Athlete 10). This variability underscores the importance of individualized approaches to prevention and intervention.

The social construction of resilience and vulnerability

The interviews revealed the significant role of the social environment in shaping athletes’ experiences of emotion regulation, coping, and burnout. The qualitative data highlighted the social construction of resilience and vulnerability, with athletes’ interactions with coaches, teammates, family, and friends influencing their ability to cope with stress and adversity.

Supportive relationships were described as a crucial protective factor against burnout. Athletes who felt supported by their social network reported feeling more confident, motivated, and resilient in the face of challenges. A city/provincial-level basketball player spoke about the positive influence of his coach: “My coach is like a mentor to me. He pushes me to be my best, but he also understands when I need support. He creates a safe space for us to talk about our struggles, and he always has our backs. I know I can always count on him.” (Athlete 10). This athlete’s experience demonstrates how a supportive coach can foster resilience and well-being.

Conversely, unsupportive or negative relationships were identified as contributing factors to burnout. Athletes who felt pressured, criticized, or neglected by their social network reported feeling more vulnerable to stress and burnout. A national-level track and field athlete confided in the interviewer about the pressure she felt from her parents: “I feel like my parents put a lot of pressure on me to succeed. They mean well, but they don’t always understand the challenges I’m facing, and it makes me feel isolated and alone.” (Athlete 19). This athlete’s experience highlights how unsupportive relationships can contribute to feelings of vulnerability and burnout.

Another athlete, a regional-level swimmer, spoke about the negative impact of comparing herself to others on social media: “Sometimes I scroll through social media and see other swimmers who seem to be doing so much better than me. It makes me feel inadequate and like I’m not good enough. It’s a constant reminder of my shortcomings.” (Athlete 5). This athlete’s experience illustrates how social comparison can contribute to negative self-evaluation and vulnerability.

These findings underscore the importance of creating positive and supportive social environments for athletes. Coaches, teammates, family members, and friends can play a vital role in fostering resilience and protecting athletes from burnout. By providing encouragement, understanding, and a safe space for athletes to express themselves, they can help athletes navigate the challenges of competitive sports and maintain their well-being.

Overall, the qualitative findings enrich the quantitative mediation results by showing how athletes with strong Repair skills may still experience burnout through maladaptive coping. For example, Athlete 14’s use of Denial after a competition failure and Athlete 7’s tendency toward Self-Blame despite adaptive efforts mirror the indirect effects identified in the mediation analysis (Table 3), where Denial and Self-Blame link Repair to higher burnout. These narratives highlight the complex, context-dependent nature of emotion regulation and coping among competitive athletes, reinforcing the need for interventions that target both adaptive and maladaptive strategies.

Discussion

The current study employed a mixed-methods approach to explore the intricate relationships between emotion regulation, coping strategies, and burnout among Chinese competitive athletes. Quantitative findings revealed that specific dimensions of emotion regulation, particularly emotional clarity and repair, alongside adaptive coping strategies like positive reframing and emotional support, were strongly linked to reduced burnout levels. In contrast, maladaptive strategies such as denial and self-blame showed a significant association with increased burnout symptoms, including emotional exhaustion and cynicism. Complementing these results, qualitative insights provided a deeper understanding by illuminating the dynamic nature of emotion regulation, the interplay of various coping methods, and the lived experiences of athletes facing burnout. This cohesive narrative integrates these findings with existing literature, clarifies their theoretical implications, and proposes practical strategies to mitigate athlete burnout, ensuring a smooth flow of ideas with effective transitions.

Emotional clarity, defined as the ability to recognize, label, and understand one’s feelings [93], emerged as a key protective factor against burnout. This capacity underpins adaptive self-regulation, enabling athletes to quickly detect distress signals and respond appropriately [17, 47]. When athletes accurately interpret their emotional states, they can address performance setbacks or interpersonal conflicts constructively. This reduces confusion and prevents burnout escalation [101]. Similarly, emotional repair—the ability to recover from negative affective states—proved equally vital. Techniques such as reframing adverse events or engaging in positive self-talk [19] empower athletes to shift away from spiraling negativity. By focusing on strengths or controllable solutions, they effectively counteract emotional exhaustion and cynicism [54], mirroring findings from resilience studies in workplace settings where high emotion repair correlates with fewer burnout symptoms [79].

Interestingly, the role of mood awareness, or Emotional Attention, presented a more complex picture than expected. Our finding that it did not directly predict burnout (β = 0.03, p > .05) seems to contradict literature suggesting awareness is a foundational component of regulation. However, we hypothesize that emotional attention operates as a “double-edged sword,” where its effect on burnout is conditional on an athlete’s other regulatory skills—a possibility that a simple linear model may not capture. Specifically, high emotional attention in the absence of strong emotional clarity and repair skills may be counterproductive. Without the ability to understand and manage emotions, paying close attention to them can devolve into maladaptive rumination, where an athlete gets “stuck” on negative feelings of failure or anxiety without a clear path forward [24, 102]. This obsessive focus can amplify distress and deplete the very mental resources needed to prevent burnout. Conversely, when high emotional attention is paired with strong clarity and repair abilities, it likely facilitates adaptive emotional processing, allowing an athlete to accurately diagnose and respond to their feelings. This suggests that emotional attention is not beneficial in and of itself, but only when it serves as the first step in a successful regulatory sequence. Future research should therefore explicitly test for an interaction effect, examining whether emotional repair and clarity moderate the relationship between emotional attention and burnout.

Transitioning to coping strategies, the study highlighted the protective power of adaptive approaches. Positive reframing stood out as a critical mechanism, enabling athletes with strong emotional clarity to reinterpret stressors constructively, thereby preserving psychological resources for performance [49] and shielding against persistent stress [26]. Likewise, social and emotional support served as a vital buffer, offering athletes avenues to vent concerns, gain perspective, and feel validated [27]. The Brief COPE subscale ‘Using Emotional Support’ demonstrated a significant negative association with burnout (β = − 0.14, p < .05), underscoring its standalone protective effect. Although not tested as a mediator, this direct influence suggests potential for future studies to explore whether social support mediates or moderates the emotion regulation-burnout link, enriching theoretical models.

In contrast, maladaptive coping strategies like denial and self-blame intensified burnout symptoms. Denial might provide temporary respite but ultimately hinders proactive problem-solving [68]. Self-blame, meanwhile, fuels self-criticism that undermines confidence and heightens negative affect [13]. These patterns align with prior research linking such strategies to increased dropout rates, mental distress, and reduced performance [9]. Athletes trapped in cycles of self-criticism after poor performances often fail to seek support or develop solutions, exacerbating their challenges [103].

The mixed-methods framework enriched this analysis by offering a holistic view of emotion regulation and coping in daily athletic life. Qualitative data revealed athletes employing proactive strategies—such as visualizing success or using self-talk to boost confidence—that echo cognitive-behavioral techniques [41, 55]. These preemptive measures buffer emotional disruptions, preparing athletes to tackle competitive pressures with clarity. Conversely, reactive strategies like mindfulness-based acceptance proved effective during acute stress [104, 56], allowing athletes to reframe setbacks as growth opportunities. Crucially, the interviews also captured the messier reality of this process, illustrating the transitional or ambiguous states where athletes attempted adaptive strategies that felt only partially successful, leaving them feeling ‘stuck in the middle’ (Athlete 15). Interviews further showcased the fluidity of coping, with athletes blending problem-focused, emotion-focused, and avoidance-based tactics to suit specific contexts [70, 71]. This highlights the value of a versatile coping “toolkit” over rigid approaches [28].

Social environments played a pivotal role in shaping burnout trajectories. Supportive coaches, teammates, and families acted as critical buffers [40, 22], reinforcing athletes’ competence and worth [14]. However, to fully understand these dynamics, it is essential to interpret our findings through a specific Chinese cultural lens, as suggested by cultural-contextual models of coping [84]. Key cultural values, such as collectivism, the concept of “face” (面子, miànzi), and high familial expectations, likely influence the psychological processes we observed. For example, the strong link between self-blame and burnout may be amplified in a collectivist context where individual failure can be perceived as letting down the team or family, thereby causing a collective loss of face. This pressure can lead athletes to harshly internalize setbacks rather than attribute them to external factors. Similarly, the role of social support in collectivistic cultures can be complex; while support is highly valued, explicitly seeking help for emotional struggles may be avoided to maintain group harmony or to not appear weak [86]. This cultural script could explain why some athletes in our interviews struggled in silence, and why those who did find a safe space to share concerns found it so profoundly protective. Furthermore, our qualitative theme of pressure from parents and peers reflects the immense emphasis on collective success within the high-performance Chinese sporting system [6, 7]. Success is often viewed not just as a personal achievement but as a reflection of family honor, repaying parental sacrifice. While this can be a powerful motivator, it transforms performance into a heavy obligation, increasing the risk of emotional exhaustion when expectations are not met [60]. These cultural interpretations do not change our core findings but enrich them, suggesting that the pathways between emotion regulation, coping, and burnout are not universal but are shaped by deep-seated cultural values and social structures.

Notably, the final hierarchical regression model explained 45% of burnout variance, reflecting the combined influence of emotion regulation, coping, and demographics [103, 32]. This substantial figure aligns with theoretical frameworks of emotion regulation [101] and coping [26, 27], indicating that targeted interventions—like cognitive-behavioral training or psychoeducation—could markedly reduce burnout risk. These results also resonate with Self-Determination Theory [29], which posits that environments promoting autonomy, competence, and relatedness fortify athletes against chronic stress [32, 35].

Demographic differences further nuanced these findings, with older and female athletes exhibiting higher burnout rates, consistent with prior studies [39, 15]. Older athletes may grapple with cumulative training loads, injuries, or external demands [105], while female athletes face sociocultural pressures and structural disparities [35]. These patterns emphasize the need for tailored stress-management programs by age and gender [106], with future research probing interactions with competition level or traits like perfectionism [13].

Ultimately, this study advances our understanding of how emotion regulation and coping strategies converge to influence burnout in Chinese competitive athletes. By weaving together quantitative and qualitative evidence, it underscores the importance of prevention efforts that account for individual differences in regulatory skills and coping styles, as well as the wider social and cultural context. This integrated perspective clarifies how to identify at-risk athletes and equip them with psychological tools to maintain performance, well-being, and a rewarding athletic career.

Implications

A more effective approach to reducing burnout among competitive athletes involves integrating emotional regulation training, adaptive coping strategies, social support, and culturally attuned interventions into existing training programs. Rather than merely encouraging athletes to “stay positive,” interventions should prioritize developing emotional clarity and repair by equipping athletes with concrete strategies for recognizing and labeling their emotions, identifying negative thought patterns, and implementing rapid recovery techniques. Guided reappraisal exercises, for instance, can help athletes shift their perspective on stressful situations in real time, allowing them to manage performance-related anxiety and emotional setbacks more effectively. This approach is particularly beneficial for those who experience persistent negative moods or struggle to recover emotionally from poor performances.

Alongside emotional regulation training, athletes should be encouraged to cultivate a diverse and flexible repertoire of coping strategies that can be adapted to different stressors. Coaches, sport psychologists, and trainers can facilitate structured workshops that emphasize both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping. This ensures that athletes can respond effectively to anticipated challenges as well as unpredictable setbacks. By incorporating practical skills drills—such as developing contingency plans for high-stakes competitions or practicing immediate cognitive reframing of mistakes—athletes can learn to apply these strategies instinctively under pressure. Encouraging a problem-solving mindset while also promoting constructive emotional expression can enhance resilience and reduce the likelihood of athletes resorting to avoidance-based coping, which has been linked to higher burnout.

Beyond individual coping strategies, the role of social support in mitigating burnout cannot be overstated. Creating a team environment that fosters open communication and mutual encouragement may serve as a protective buffer against both acute and chronic stressors. Implementing peer-based check-in sessions, where athletes can safely share their concerns, may help normalize discussions around emotional struggles and encourage proactive stress management. Furthermore, coaches who model supportive and nonjudgmental feedback can create an atmosphere in which athletes feel comfortable seeking assistance early, rather than suppressing their struggles. Simple strategies, such as incorporating brief one-on-one check-ins during intense training cycles, can provide valuable opportunities to identify early signs of burnout, reinforce coping strategies, and ensure that athletes receive timely support.

The findings of this study also highlight the importance of considering cultural and demographic nuances when designing interventions for burnout prevention. Given that many Chinese athletes experience unique pressures related to social comparison, familial expectations, and the collectivist nature of their sporting environments, targeted educational sessions for parents and key stakeholders could help mitigate these stressors. Educating families on how to provide motivational support without excessive pressure may help athletes develop a healthier relationship with competition. Additionally, screening older athletes or those transitioning from junior to senior levels may be critical in identifying individuals at a heightened risk for chronic stress and burnout. Implementing interventions that align with cultural values, such as group-based mindfulness sessions or team-oriented coping strategies, may enhance engagement and effectiveness. By tailoring interventions to the specific psychological and social realities faced by athletes, sport organizations and practitioners can take meaningful steps toward reducing burnout while fostering long-term well-being and sustained performance.

Limitations and future directions

A key limitation of this study is the cross-sectional design of the quantitative phase. As a result, the relationships identified, particularly within the mediation model, should be interpreted as associations rather than causal pathways. While our analysis suggests that coping strategies may explain the link between emotion regulation and burnout, we cannot confirm the direction of these effects. For instance, it is plausible that heightened burnout could lead athletes to adopt more maladaptive coping strategies over time. To establish causality with greater confidence, future research should adopt stronger methodological designs.

A longitudinal study, for example, could track athletes at multiple points throughout a competitive season. Such a design would allow researchers to determine temporal precedence—that is, whether changes in emotion regulation and coping strategies consistently precede changes in burnout symptoms. Furthermore, an experimental approach, such as a randomized controlled trial (RCT), would provide the most rigorous test of causality. Researchers could design an intervention that trains one group of athletes in adaptive coping skills (e.g., positive reframing) and compare their burnout levels post-intervention to a control group that did not receive the training. This would offer direct evidence of the strategy’s protective effect.

A second methodological limitation concerns the measurement of coping strategies. In terms of measurement, we acknowledge that certain Brief COPE subscales yielded moderate internal consistency estimates in our sample. This presents a trade-off: the Brief COPE was chosen for its ability to assess a wide range of coping strategies with minimal burden on participants, but the cost of this breadth is the brevity of its subscales. Therefore, the findings related to the coping strategies measured by these specific subscales should be interpreted with appropriate caution, as they may be subject to a greater degree of measurement error. Future research could build on our findings by employing a more focused approach, using longer, more robust scales for the specific coping strategies (e.g., positive reframing, denial) that our study identified as significant mediators. This would provide a more reliable assessment and allow for a deeper analysis of their role in the burnout process.

A third methodological point is that our analytical approach focused on mediation rather than moderation. While our findings help explain how emotion regulation influences burnout through coping, we did not explore for whom or under what conditions these relationships might be stronger or weaker. The inclusion of moderation analyses, as the reviewer noted, would have allowed us to identify important boundary conditions for these effects. For example, it is plausible that sport type acts as a moderator, where the link between maladaptive coping and burnout is stronger for athletes in individual sports (e.g., swimming, gymnastics) compared to those in team sports, who may benefit from shared responsibility and built-in peer support. Similarly, variables like competition level or gender could moderate these pathways. Future research should therefore incorporate moderation analyses to test these potential interaction effects, which is a critical next step for developing interventions tailored to the specific needs of different athlete subgroups.

A further limitation involves the study’s demographic scope. Although the 23 interviews provided rich, contextual insights, a larger qualitative sample could reveal more variability. More pointedly, our focus on athletes aged 18–28, while representing a critical developmental period of peak competitive pressure, means our findings may not be generalizable to athletes at other career stages. We agree with the reviewer that younger adolescent and older professional athletes face distinct challenges that likely influence their emotional and coping responses. For instance, adolescent athletes must navigate the pressures of identity formation alongside academics and sport, and their coping repertoires are often still developing and more reliant on external support from coaches and parents. In contrast, veteran professional athletes may grapple with stressors such as performance decline, career-transition anxiety, and managing family responsibilities, which demand different coping strategies. Future research should therefore adopt a lifespan perspective, conducting comparative studies to map how the risk factors for burnout and the most effective coping strategies evolve from an athlete’s early career through to retirement.

Furthermore, while our discussion now explores the influence of the Chinese cultural context, a key limitation is that we did not formally measure cultural variables. Our interpretations of how values like collectivism or face-saving may affect our findings are therefore theoretical. Future research should address this by including validated scales of cultural values to quantitatively test their role as potential moderators in the relationship between stress, coping, and burnout. This would allow for a direct examination of how these cultural factors shape psychological resilience in elite athletes and would help in designing more culturally-attuned interventions [84].

Finally, the sample size of the study warrants consideration. For the quantitative component, while our sample of 286 athletes was sufficient to detect the significant relationships reported, a larger sample would increase statistical power and provide more stable estimates for our complex mediation model. Regarding the qualitative interviews, our sample of 23 athletes allowed us to reach thematic saturation on the primary research questions, as no new overarching themes emerged in the final interviews. However, we acknowledge that a larger sample would be required to explore thematic variations across different subgroups with confidence. For example, our current sample is likely insufficient to fully capture potential differences in the burnout experience between athletes in individual versus team sports or across China’s diverse regional training cultures. Future research using large-scale, multi-site designs is needed to verify our quantitative findings and conduct more fine-grained analyses of these important subgroup experiences.
