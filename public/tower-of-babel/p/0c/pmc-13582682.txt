Preparation, characterization and application of feline CD3-specific single-domain antibodies
Authors: Jingjing Wang, Qihuan Zhao, Bo Wang, Chengquan Du, Hui Wang, Jingsi Mei, Ruifeng Gao, Fuxiang Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFeline lymphoma, a prevalent feline malignancy, responds poorly to conventional chemotherapy—a setting that underscores the urgent need for targeted therapeutic alternatives. The stable expression of CD3 on T-cell lymphoma cells positions this molecule as an ideal immunotherapeutic target. Compared with conventional antibodies, single-domain nanobodies derived from camelid heavy-chain antibodies offer distinct advantages, including smaller size, enhanced tissue penetration, and superior stability, thereby rendering CD3-targeted nanobodies particularly promising for managing feline T-cell lymphoma. We immunized a Bactrian camel with recombinant feline CD3ε extracellular domain and constructed a phage display nanobody library (capacity 5.97 × 108). Sequencing of the constructed library revealed high diversity; among 10 randomly selected clones, 7 unique sequences were identified, and 93.33% of the clones carried intact VHH inserts. After three panning rounds, three high-affinity single-domain antibodies were selected and expressed, with clone 73 retaining stable binding at 156 ng/mL concentration. Flow cytometry showed that clone 73 specifically recognized approximately 37.95% of feline peripheral blood CD3+ cells. Western blotting detected a specific ~ 20 kDa band in feline lymphocyte lysates, and immunofluorescence confirmed precise cellsurface localization without non-specific signal. These findings demonstrate successful generation of feline CD3-specific nanobodies, which enable flow cytometric diagnosis of feline T-cell lymphoma and provide a foundation for developing immunotherapeutics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13568-026-02093-7.Keywords: Feline, CD3ε, Nanobody, Phage display, T-cell lymphoma

Introduction

Feline lymphoma represents one of the most prevalent malignancies in cats, constituting approximately 30% of all feline tumors. The alimentary form is the most common clinical presentation and primarily affects geriatric cats aged 10–12 years (Ettinger 2003). Although chemotherapy remains the standard first-line treatment for the majority of cases, its efficacy is limited by modest remission rates, short response durations, and an elevated risk of secondary neoplasia associated with prolonged alkylating agent exposure (Ettinger 2003). A multicenter retrospective analysis of 57 cats diagnosed with high-grade or large granular lymphocyte alimentary lymphoma reported an overall response rate of only 42% following COP or CHOP chemotherapy, with a median progression-free interval of 148 days and a median overall survival of merely 131 days (Bernardo Marques et al. 2024). These sobering outcomes underscore the urgent need for novel precision therapeutic approaches.

CD3, a pan–T-cell antigen, stably expressed on the surface of T-cell lymphoma cells, represents an attractive immunotherapeutic target in this context (Chetty and Gatter 1994). Nanobodies, also termed single-domain antibodies (VHHs), are derived from camelid heavy-chain-only antibodies and are characterized by their small size, superior tissue penetration, high conformational stability, and remarkable engineering versatility (Hamers-Casterman et al. 1993; Tang et al. 2023). These inherent advantages have positioned nanobodies as promising candidates for applications in tumor molecular imaging and targeted therapy (Tang et al. 2023; Hu et al. 2023). Recently, Li et al. successfully immunized a Bactrian camel with the extracellular domain peptide of canine CD20, constructed a phage display nanobody library, and isolated high-affinity anti-canine CD20 single-domain antibodies, thereby establishing a valuable tool for the diagnosis and treatment of canine B-cell lymphoma (Li et al. 2025). In a comprehensive review, Thamm highlighted recent therapeutic advances for canine and feline lymphoma and emphasized that innovative strategies integrating immunotherapy and small-molecule inhibitors are essential for improving clinical outcomes (Thamm 2024).

The CD3 molecule constitutes an integral component of the T-cell receptor complex. Among its constituent chains, the ε chain displays the most pronounced immunogenicity and is surface-exposed, rendering it a critical determinant for T-cell recognition and signal transduction (Chetty and Gatter 1994). Since the landmark description of the antihuman CD3 monoclonal antibody OKT3 in 1979 (Norman 1995), CD3-targeted antibodies have been extensively utilized in the management of transplant rejection and autoimmune disorders (Chatenoud 2006). More recently, CD3-bispecific antibodies have achieved transformative clinical success in tumor immunotherapy (Zhu et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2025; Goebeler et al. 2024), providing compelling validation of CD3-targeting as a viable therapeutic strategy. In the feline system, He et al. have previously generated an anti-feline CD3 monoclonal antibody using conventional hybridoma technology (He et al. 2023). However, conventional antibodies are limited by their relatively large size and complex engineering requirements, and a nanobody-based targeting molecule specific for feline CD3 has not been documented. The development of feline CD3-specific nanobodies could therefore bridge this gap, offering a smaller, more versatile reagent for both diagnostic and therapeutic applications.

Building upon an established nanobody phage display platform (Li et al. 2025), the present study aimed to address this gap. A Bactrian camel was immunized with recombinant feline CD3ε extracellular domain protein, and a high-capacity phage display library was subsequently constructed. High-affinity nanobodies were isolated through iterative rounds of affinity biopanning and ELISA screening, and their binding specificity and reactivity toward native feline CD3+ cells were rigorously validated using flow cytometry, Western blotting, and indirect immunofluorescence. This work seeks to provide the first feline CD3-targeted nanobody tools, to facilitate the diagnosis and immunotherapy of feline T-cell lymphoma, and to establish a foundation for the future development of nanobody-based bispecific T-cell engagers or antibody–drug conjugates.

Materials and methods

Expression and purification of recombinant feline CD3ε protein

The region of the feline CD3ε gene that codes for the extracellular domain was amplified using the GenBank sequence AB195839.1 as a reference. To enable directional cloning, forward and reverse primers were designed to include Nco I and Not I (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) restriction sites, respectively. This design allowed the amplified fragment to be inserted into the pET-22b(+) vector in frame with the vector's C-terminal 6×His tag. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. All oligonucleotides were synthesized commercially (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

The extracellular coding sequence of feline CD3ε was amplified from cDNA synthesized using a PrimeScript™ One Step RT-PCR Kit (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan) with MIR/MIF primer pairs, using total RNA extracted from feline peripheral blood lymphocytes (RNA extraction kit, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The PCR product was digested with Nco I and Not I, ligated into the pET-22b(+) vector, and transformed into Escherichia coli (E. coli) BL21(DE3) competent cells (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Positive clones were verified by sequencing. For protein expression, a confirmed colony was cultured and induced with isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The His-tagged recombinant protein was purified using a Ni–NTA affinity column (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) under denaturing conditions (Aghdam et al. 2022). The protein was then refolded via stepwise dialysis with gradually decreasing urea concentration: 6 M urea for 4 h, 4 M urea for 6 h, 2 M urea overnight, 1 M urea for 6 h, and PBS for 3 h in the final stage. The obtained protein was analyzed by SDS-PAGE.

Construction of anti-feline CD3 phage antibody library

A healthy female Bactrian camel (Camelus bactrianus) was immunized with recombinant feline CD3ε protein emulsified in Freund's complete adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for primary immunization and in Freund's incomplete adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich) for three subsequent boosts at two-week intervals. Serum antibody titers were monitored by indirect ELISA using HRP-conjugated rabbit anticamel IgG antibody (Chengdu AlpVHHs Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). Two weeks after the final immunization, peripheral blood lymphocytes were isolated using a bovine lymphocyte separation kit (TBD Science, Tianjin Haoyang Biological Manufacture Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China). Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). First-strand cDNA was generated by one‑step RT-PCR with the PrimeScript™ kit using primer pairs P1/P2 detailed in Table 1. Nested PCR with PMIR/PMIF primer pairs (Table 1) was performed to amplify VHH coding sequences. The amplicons were double-digested with Nco I and Not I, then ligated into the pMECS phagemid vector (a kind gift from Professor Serge Muyldermans, Vrije Universiteit Brussel, Brussels, Belgium). Ligation mixtures were electroporated into E. coli TG1 cells for construction of the VHH phage display library. To evaluate library diversity, 15 clones were randomly selected and subjected to colony PCR identification using primers MP57/GⅢ. Ten positive clones were then chosen for Sanger sequencing.

Enrichment and screening of anti-feline CD3 specific phage antibody library

The VHH phage display library was rescued and amplified using M13K07 helper phage (Beijing Bio-viewshine Bio-Tek Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Briefly, 1 mL of library was cultured in 100 mL 2×YT medium to an OD600 of 0.6, infected with helper phage at an MOI of 20:1, and grown overnight in 2×YT containing ampicillin and kanamycin. Phage particles were precipitated from the culture supernatant with 20% PEG-8000, resuspended in PBS, and pre-incubated in a BSA-blocked immunotube to reduce non-specific binding. The phage solution was then transferred to a tube coated with 10 μg/mL recombinant feline CD3ε protein and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing five times with PBST (PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20), bound phage were eluted by adding 1 mL of actively growing E. coli TG1 cells and incubating at 37 °C for 30 min without shaking, followed by the addition of 4 mL of 2 × YT and further incubation for 1 h with slow shaking. Three successive rounds of biopanning were performed following similar screening procedures to enrich high-affinity binders (Kazemzadeh et al. 2024; Mehdipour et al. 2020).

From the final round, 95 individual clones were randomly selected and rescued in 96-well format. Binding activity was assessed by phage ELISA using plates coated with 5 μg/mL recombinant feline CD3ε protein. M13K07 helper phage served as the negative control, and PBS was used as the blank. Bound phage were detected using HRP-conjugated anti-M13 monoclonal antibody (Chengdu AlpVHHs Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China). After adding single-component TMB chromogenic solution (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and incubating for 10 min, the reaction was terminated with stop solution. The absorbance was then measured at 405 nm. Clones exhibiting an absorbance value ≥ 2.1-fold over the negative control were scored as positive and submitted for sequencing.

Expression and purification of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibodies

Three sequence-confirmed VHH clones (clones 26, 66, and 73) were selected for subsequent protein expression. The VHH inserts were digested with Nco I and Not I restriction endonucleases, and the purified fragments were ligated into the pET-22b(+)-SBP-His dual-tag recombinant vector (laboratory prepared). This vector was modified from the original pET-22b(+) backbone by inserting an streptavidin binding peptide (SBP) tag upstream of the His tag. The resulting recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells for subsequent protein induction and expression. A single colony was cultured in 2×YT-Amp medium at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until the OD600 reached approximately 0.6. Protein expression was induced with 1 mM IPTG and continued for an additional 12 h under the same conditions.

Following induction, cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in binding buffer containing 8 M urea. The suspension was disrupted by sonication and centrifuged to remove insoluble debris. The cleared lysate was applied to a Ni–NTA affinity column for purification of the His-tagged single-domain antibodies. The bound protein was eluted and refolded by stepwise dialysis against urea-containing buffers of decreasing concentrations (6, 4, 2, 1 M) and finally PBS. Protein yield and purity were assessed by SDS-PAGE.

Binding activity and specificity of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibodies

Indirect ELISA identification

The binding activity of the purified single‑domain antibodies to feline CD3 was evaluated by indirect ELISA. Microtiter plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with 10 μg/mL total protein extracted from feline peripheral blood lymphocytes. After blocking with 3% BSA for 2 h at 37 °C, the purified antibodies were serially diluted two‑fold in PBS starting from 20 μg/mL and applied to the wells. Lysate from bacteria carrying the empty pET-22b(+) vector served as the negative control, and PBS alone served as the blank control. Following incubation and washing, an HRP‑conjugated anti‑His‑Tag mouse monoclonal antibody (Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China) diluted 1:10,000 was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. After washing, TMB substrate (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added and incubated for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Western-blot identification

Total protein (25 μg) from feline peripheral blood lymphocytes was resolved by SDS‑PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% BSA, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the purified single‑domain antibody (clone 66) (10 μg/mL). Following TBST washes, it was incubated with a Streptavidin/HRP (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) diluted 1:10,000 for 2 h at 37 °C. Because the recombinant single-domain antibody carries an SBP tag that binds streptavidin with high affinity, this step specifically detects the VHH protein. After washing, bands were visualized using ECL detection reagents (Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China) and captured with a chemiluminescence imager.

Indirect immunofluorescence identification

Coverslips coated with poly-L-lysine (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were sterilized in absolute ethanol, placed in 24-well plates, and seeded with feline peripheral blood lymphocytes. After 3 h of adhesion at 37 °C, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, Hefei, China) for 20 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Coverslips were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the purified anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibody (clone 26) (100 μg/mL). Following washing, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with a CoraLite® 488-conjugated anti-6 × His tag antibody (Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China). Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at 1 μg/mL for 10 min. After final washes, coverslips were mounted and imaged under a fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometry analysis of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibodies

The purified single-domain antibody (clone 73) (2 mg/mL) was conjugated with FITC using a commercial labeling kit (Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer's protocol. Feline peripheral blood lymphocytes were adjusted to 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL in complete RPMI 1640 medium, and 200 μL aliquots (2 × 10⁶ cells) were dispensed into microcentrifuge tubes. The FITC-conjugated antibody was added, and cells were incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Unstained cells served as the negative control. After incubation, cells were washed three times with DPBS by centrifugation, resuspended in 200 μL PBS, and analyzed on a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer.

Results

Expression and purification of recombinant feline CD3ε protein

The extracellular domain coding sequence of feline CD3ε was amplified and cloned into the pET22b(+) vector. Sequencing confirmed complete identity with the GenBank reference (AB195839.1) and correct reading frame maintenance. Following transformation into E. coli BL21 (DE3) and IPTG induction, SDS-PAGE analysis revealed a prominent band at approximately 15 kDa in the induced sample, whereas no corresponding band was observed in the uninduced control (Fig. 1a). The recombinant protein was purified by Ni–NTA affinity chromatography and refolded by stepwise dialysis. Purified protein yielded a single 15 kDa band on SDS-PAGE, indicating high purity (Fig. 1b).

Construction of an anti-feline CD3 VHH phage display library

Serum antibody response in the immunized camel

Serum antibody titers against recombinant feline CD3ε protein were determined by indirect ELISA after three booster immunizations. The blank control (PBS) yielded an absorbance of 0.086 at 450 nm, and the pre-immune serum negative control gave a value of 0.364. At a serum dilution of 1:512,000, the absorbance was 0.786, which exceeded twice the negative control value, corresponding to an antibody titer of 1:512,000 (Fig. 2a). This result confirms that the recombinant protein elicited a robust and specific antibody response in the camel.

Isolation and amplification of VHH gene sequences

Total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood lymphocytes of the immunized camel. The VHH-CH2 region (approximately 600 bp) was first amplified by one-step RT-PCR, and this product then served as the template for a nested PCR, which yielded the full-length VHH coding sequence (400 bp). Agarose gel electrophoresis revealed a single, sharp band for each amplification, indicating specific and efficient amplification (Fig. 2b, c).

Library construction and quality control

The VHH fragments were ligated into the pMECS phagemid vector, and the constructs were electroporated into E. coli TG1 cells to establish the phage display library.The total transformant library capacity was measured to be 5.97 × 10⁸. Fifteen clones were randomly selected for PCR identification; 14 out of 15 clones harbored the correct insert, resulting in 93.33% of clones carrying intact VHH inserts (Fig. 2d). Additionally, Sanger sequencing of 10 randomly chosen clones identified 7 unique VHH sequences, confirming high library diversity. These results indicate that a high-quality VHH phage display library with satisfactory diversity and a high recombinant rate was successfully generated.

Enrichment and screening of the anti-feline CD3 VHH phage display library

The VHH phage display library was subjected to three rounds of biopanning against recombinant feline CD3ε protein. Of 95 randomly selected clones screened by phage ELISA, 65 clones (68.4%) exhibited specific binding, defined as an absorbance signal ≥ 2.1-fold higher than that of the negative control (Fig. 3). Based on sequence analysis, clones 26, 66, and 73 were selected for further characterization, and the detailed sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Expression and purification of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibodies

Colony PCR confirmed the correct insertion of the VHH gene in clones 26, 66, and 73 (Fig. 4a). Following IPTG induction, SDS-PAGE revealed a prominent band at approximately 13 kDa in the soluble fraction of cell lysates, consistent with the expected size of the single-domain antibody (Fig. 4b). The protein was purified by Ni–NTA affinity chromatography and refolded by stepwise dialysis. The purified product migrated as a single 13 kDa band on SDS-PAGE, indicating high purity (Fig. 4c).

Indirect ELISA analysis of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibody binding

The antigenbinding activities of clones 26, 66, and 73 were assessed by indirect ELISA using total feline peripheral blood lymphocyte protein as the coating antigen. All three clones exhibited concentration-dependent binding, with clone 73 producing the highest signal (Fig. 5). Clone 73 retained detectable binding at a concentration as low as 156 ng/mL. These results confirm that the recombinant single-domain antibodies specifically recognize feline CD3, and clone 73 shows superior binding capacity.

Antigen-binding activity of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibodies assessed by indirect ELISA. All three clones showed concentration-dependent binding. Clone 73 displayed the highest binding activity and maintained a signal significantly above background at 156 ng/mL

Western blot analysis of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibody specificity

To evaluate the binding ability of the anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibody (clone 66) to native CD3 molecules on feline lymphocytes, Western blot analysis was performed. The membrane probed with clone 66 displayed a specific band at approximately 20 kDa, which was consistent with the theoretical molecular weight of feline CD3ε. These results confirmed that clone 66 could specifically recognize native CD3ε protein on feline lymphocytes (Fig. 6). In addition, three non-specific bands were detected near 40 kDa, which were speculated to result from endogenous biotin binding in cellular samples. The negative control western blot results of feline lymphocytes without probing with single-domain antibody also showed these non-specific bands as shown in Fig S1 in the supplementary materials. Furthermore, Western blot using purified recombinant feline CD3ε protein as antigen was also conducted to verify the binding specificity of clone 26, clone 66 and clone 73. All three antibody clones yielded distinct specific bands at around 15 kDa, and no signal was observed in the PBS negative control. The relevant results are presented in Fig S2 of the supplementary materials.

Immunofluorescence detection of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibody binding

Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that the anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibody (clone 26) specifically binds to native CD3 on the surface of feline peripheral blood lymphocytes, producing distinct membrane-localized green fluorescence. DAPI counterstaining revealed intact nuclei in all cells examined. No green fluorescence was detected in control cells incubated without the primary antibody (Fig. 7). These results validate the specificity of the antibody for identifying CD3-positive T lymphocytes.

Flow cytometry analysis of anti-feline CD3 single-domain antibodies

Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that clone 73 specifically bound to CD3-positive lymphocytes, which accounted for approximately 37.95% of feline peripheral blood mononuclear cells (Fig. 8). This confirms the utility of clone 73 for flow cytometric detection of feline T cells.

Discussion

In this study, immunization of a Bactrian camel with the recombinant feline CD3ε extracellular domain enabled the successful construction of a high-quality phage display nanobody library. The library achieved a capacity of 5.97 × 108 and 93.33% of clones harbored intact VHH inserts. Sanger sequencing of randomly selected clones confirmed high library diversity, with 7 unique sequences identified among 10 clones. Following three rounds of affinity biopanning, three nanobody clones (designated 26, 66, and 73) exhibiting specific binding to feline CD3ε were isolated. Notably, only clone 73 proved capable of recognizing the native CD3 molecule in its physiological conformation. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that clone 73 specifically labeled approximately 37.95% of peripheral blood lymphocytes. Western blotting under reducing conditions detected a specific band at approximately 20 kDa, and indirect immunofluorescence confirmed that the recognized protein was membrane localized. Collectively, these results establish that this study has generated the first feline CD3-specific nanobody with clear practical utility, thereby addressing a critical gap in T-cell immunological reagents for this species.

Phage display technology currently represents one of the principal platforms for nanobody generation. Compared with conventional hybridoma methods, phage display offers a shorter timeline, reduced cost, and obviates the need for terminal procedures (Yang 2026). The high recombination efficiency (93.33%) and positive clone rate (68.4%) attest to the potent immunogenicity of the recombinant feline CD3ε protein and its capacity to elicit a robust antigen specific humoral response in the camel.

Feline lymphoma is the most common hematopoietic malignancy in domestic cats, and a T-cell immunophenotype is generally associated with poorer therapeutic responses and less favorable outcomes (Vail et al. 1998). Accurate immunophenotyping is a prerequisite for targeted intervention, yet the scarcity of specific reagents has left targeted therapy for feline T-cell lymphoma lagging far behind advances in canine and human medicine.

CD3ε is a core signal-transducing subunit of the T-cell receptor complex and an ideal target for T-cell-directed intervention. The specific band detected by clone 66 at approximately 20 kDa is consistent with the electrophoretic mobility of feline CD3ε observed in prior studies, and the apparent discrepancy from the predicted molecular mass of 25 kDa is commonly attributed to post-translational modifications under reducing SDS-PAGE conditions (Nishimura et al. 1998).

The flow cytometric positivity rate of clone 73 (37.95%) is lower than the 54.8% pan-T-cell proportion previously reported using anti-CD5 reagents (Dean et al. 1991). This discrepancy is consistent with the observation by Nishimura et al. (2004) that a CD5⁺, CD3ε⁻ cell subset exists in feline peripheral blood, suggesting that anti-CD3ε reagents offer superior lineage specificity for defining authentic T-cell populations compared with anti-CD5 reagents.

Among the three clones, clone 73 was the only one binding native CD3 on live cells. Clones 26 and 66 reacted in ELISA and fixed-cell immunofluorescence, but not with live cells, suggesting they may recognize epitopes exposed upon protein immobilization or fixation. On the live cell surface, CD3 subunits assemble into a multimeric complex with stringent spatial constraints (Chetty and Gatter 1994); clones 26 and 66 likely target cryptic epitopes unmasked during extraction or fixation, while clone 73 binds a conformational epitope on the native complex. This highlights the importance of early selection under native conditions to avoid antibodies with solid-phase or fixed-cell reactivity but no live-cell utility.

The anti-feline CD3 nanobody clone 73 obtained in this study has a well-defined translational path. For diagnostic applications, it can serve as a highly specific reagent for flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry in feline lymphoma. For therapeutic applications, its small size and genetic tractability make it a promising module for constructing bispecific nanobodies or nanobody-drug conjugates (Yuan et al. 2021; Kijanka et al. 2015).

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, clone 73 has been validated only with peripheral blood samples from healthy cats; its performance in clinical lymphoma specimens remains to be evaluated. Second, cross-reactivity with other immune cell subsets and non-target tissues has not been systematically assessed. Third, as a monovalent nanobody lacking an Fc region, clone 73 cannot mediate antibody-dependent or complement-dependent cytotoxicity. Future studies will evaluate its clinical diagnostic utility, construct bispecific nanobodies for redirected T-cell cytotoxicity, and assess in vivo therapeutic efficacy.
