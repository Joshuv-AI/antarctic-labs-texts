Amine/ammonium buffering enables efficient anti-Markovnikov hydroamination of olefins with alkylamines
Authors: Jing-Yu Shen, Jin-Kai Cheng, Yanan Wang, Zheliang Yuan, Fei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe hydroamination of alkenes represents a highly desirable and powerful strategy in synthetic chemistry for the direct construction of alkylamines from abundant starting materials. However, excess alkenes are typically required for anti-Markovnikov hydroamination of unactivated alkenes with alkylamines, significantly limiting its general utility in synthesis, especially for complex and expensive alkenes. In addition, significant enantiopurity erosion occurs when using α-chiral alkylamines as coupling partner. Herein, we present a solution to these challenges by developing an amine/ammonium buffering system under photocatalytic conditions. Key to this strategy is mitigating deprotonation of the involved aminium radical cation intermediate, formed through single electron oxidation of the associated alkylamine substrate, ensuring its effective addition to alkenes. The reaction works efficiently at a near-stoichiometric olefin-to-amine ratio while maintaining good yields. The method exhibits remarkable functional group tolerance, allows late-stage modification of complex alkenes and hydroamination of gaseous alkene, and largely preserves stereochemical fidelity in transformation of α-chiral alkylamine.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Homogeneous catalysis

Introduction

Alkyl amines represent privileged structural motifs in bioactive molecules, such as pharmaceuticals and natural products1,2. In this regard, substantial efforts have been devoted to developing innovative methods for their synthesis3–6. Among the strategies established, hydroamination of alkenes is particularly attractive as it directly utilizes readily available olefins and amines7. However, this transformation faces many fundamental challenges, including the high kinetic barrier and unfavorable entropic factor that mandate catalytic activation8–11. While significant advances have been made in coupling alkenes and alkylamines via transition metal catalysis, the anti-Markovnikov hydroamination of unactivated alkenes remains a formidable challenge12,13. Current solutions rely on radical-mediated reaction pathway, wherein the nitrogen-centered radicals (NCRs) add preferentially to the less substituted position of alkenes to form the more stabilized β-aminoalkyl radical (Fig. 1a)14–20. Such mechanistic paradigm overcomes the inherent electronic bias of polar pathways that favor Markovnikov products, while maintaining good functional group tolerance.

anti-Markovnikov hydroamination of unactivated alkenes with alkylamines has been enabled by merging photoredox and hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) catalysis19,21–24. An aminium radical cation (ARC) was invoked as the key intermediate, generated through single electron oxidation of alkylamine substrate. However, the efficiency is limited by ARC deprotonation with the parent alkylamine and/or the higher-order amine product, leading to either an unreactive neutral aminyl radical or α-amino alkyl radical (Fig. 1b). Such unproductive pathways necessitate the use of excess alkene, especially for the non-electron-rich α-olefins, to drive the reaction forward, which significantly constrains the substrate scope. In addition, the product inhibition effect has also been observed, as a result of the possible quenching of PC* by higher-order amine product. These undesired pathways become particularly problematic when using α-chiral amines as coupling partner, in that the racemization of both the starting materials and products would take place via deprotonation of α-C–H of chiral amines (Fig. 1b). We envisioned that these challenges should be addressed by minimizing ARC deprotonation and attenuating the reductive quenching of PC* by product.

Generation of the ARC intermediate under acidic conditions would avoid its deprotonation, holding the promise to improve reactivity with α-olefins. For example, we have employed alkyl azides as ARC precursor in the presence of (TMS)Si–H under acidic conditions, enabling the use of α-olefins as limiting reagents and late-stage hydroamination of complex alkenes25–27. However, this method, along with many other examples using amine donors with higher oxidation states coupled with stoichiometric reductants, requires additional step(s) to access amine coupling partners, rather than directly utilizing readily available alkylamines28–33. With our continuous interest in radical-mediated amination reactions34–41, we sought to address the challenges in alkene hydroamination with primary alkylamines by establishing an amine/ammonium buffering system (Fig. 1c, d). Under buffered conditions, the relatively low concentration of free amine would attenuate the unproductive deprotonation of ARC, enabling an efficient radical addition to alkene and the high stereochemical fidelity for reaction of chiral alkylamine. Moreover, secondary amine product is expected to be generated as an ammonium salt due to its superior basicity than primary amine (Fig. 1c)42. Such production deactivation would also mitigate the product inhibition and the racemization of chiral alkylamines. However, the reduced concentration of free amine concurrently poses a challenge for the efficient oxidation of amine to ARC for the desired transformation. We speculated that employing a redox mediator could address this issue by facilitating the oxidation. Herein, we report an amine/ammonium buffering-facilitated anti-Markovnikov hydroamination of unactivated alkenes, operating efficiently with a near-stoichiometric olefin-to-amine ratio and demonstrating exceptional reactivity toward α-olefins. This catalytic system enables the hydroamination of styrene derivatives, gaseous alkenes and complex molecules in late-stage settings, transformations that are typically difficult with existing methods (Fig. 1d). More importantly, the reaction also demonstrates excellent functional group compatibility, and allows the large preservation of stereochemical integrity when using α-chiral alkylamine.

Results and discussion

Our study commenced with the coupling of 4-phenyl-1-butene (1) and cyclohexyl amine (2) in 1:1 stoichiometry (Fig. 2a). Employing [Ir(III)(dF(CF3)ppy)2(4,4’-d(CF3)bpy)]PF6 (PC-1) as photocatalyst and 2,4,6-triisopropylbenzenethiol (TripSH, HAT-1) as hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) catalyst afforded the desired secondary amine product in only 14% yield (Fig. 2a, entry 1)19. While increasing the alkene loading to 6.0 equivalents enhanced the yield (43%, Fig. 2a, entry 2), such excess stoichiometry is impractical for applications to more complex and expensive alkene substrates. It has been demonstrated that the inclusion of (TripS)2 (HAT-3) could facilitate the reoxidation of Ir(II) photocatalyst, which indeed increases the yield to 28% yield (Fig. 2a, entry 3)24. Notably, simply extending reaction time failed to improve conversions, revealing the fundamental limitations in existing protocols for α-olefin hydroamination (Fig. 2a, entries 1 and 3). As noted above, the low efficiency might arise from the deprotonation of ARC (2•+) by 2 and/or 3, diverting the reaction pathway away from productive alkene addition24. To address this, we implemented an amine/ammonium buffer, containing 0.1 equiv. of 2 and 0.9 equiv. of 2•HX (X = OTf, NTf2, OTFA and Cl), in order to suppressing this unproductive pathway by decreasing the concentration of basic amine in the solution (Fig. 2a, entries 4–7)43. The optimal buffer combination (2 + 2•HOTf) increased yields from 14 to 25% (Fig. 2a, entry 4), suggesting the potential of using buffering strategy to overcome limitations in radical hydroamination chemistry. The inhibition effects of HCl and HOTFA might arise from the ability of Cl⁻ and CF₃COO⁻ to intercept the PC*, which is corroborated by the Stern–Volmer fluorescence quenching experiments (see Section 4.1 of the Supplementary Information for details).

Through systematic evaluation of photocatalyst and HAT catalyst combinations, we identified [Ir(III)(dF(CF3)ppy)2(5,5′-d(CF3)bpy)]PF6 (PC-2) with 2,6-diisopropyl-4-phenylbenz-enethiol (HAT-2) in the presence of HOTf (0.8 equiv.) as an optimized system, further increasing the yield to 40% (Fig. 2b, entry 1, see Tables S1–S2 of the Supplementary Information for details). A yield of 53% was achieved by modestly increasing the alkene loading to 1.5 equivalents (entry 2). The moderate conversion might attribute to the difficulty in amine oxidation at low concentration. We proposed that this kinetic limitation could be addressed through the use of a redox mediator to catalyze the oxidation step. Screening of different additives revealed that phenyl ether A-1 provided exceptional enhancement, increasing the yield to 75% (Conditions A), while other tested additives (A-2 to A-4) exhibited either comparable or diminished activity (entries 3–6). Most significantly, this additive-enabled system maintained good efficiency (62% yield) with only 1.0 equivalent of alkene substrate (entry 7, Conditions B), and showed comparable performance when scaling the amine component to 1.5 equivalents and using alkene as limiting reagent (entry 8, 68%, Conditions C). Reaction in the absence of HOTf and A-1 afforded the product in only 15% yield (entry 9). Additional control experiments indicated that light illumination, photocatalyst and hydrogen atom transfer catalyst were all indispensable components for the reaction (see Table S3 of the Supplementary Information for details). Notably, the low yields observed in many cases (Fig. 2a, and entries 1–2 and 9 of Fig. 2b) reflect incomplete conversion of the substrates, and no tertiary alkylamine was detected by 1H-NMR or GC-MS.

Efforts were then made to evaluate the generality across diverse alkene substrates using cyclohexylamine (2) as coupling partner (Fig. 3). The transformation exhibits many remarkable characteristics. First, it displays insensitivity to steric encumbrance, achieving comparable yields for α-substituted alkenes whenever they bear primary, secondary, or tertiary alkyl groups (3-28). Second, the protocol demonstrates excellent functional group tolerance, successfully accommodating electron-rich arenes (5, 7, 19-22), alcohols (primary, secondary, and tertiary) (8, 14, 15, 22, 23, 25 and 27), esters (10, 17-19, 25-26), nitriles (6, 11 and 18), phosphates (12), amides (19 and 22), carbamate (21), aliphatic and α,β-unsaturated ketones (23 and 25), ketals (24), and various heterocycles, such as pyrazole (17 and 28), thiazole (18), β-lactam (22), and pyrimidine (26). Third, the method proves capable for late-stage functionalization, as evidenced by successful hydroamination of structurally complex alkenes derived from pharmaceuticals and natural products, such as Febuxostat (18), Bezafibrate (19), Gemfibrozil (20), Metaxalone (21), Ezetimibe (22), Glycyrrhetinic acid (23), Diacetone-D-glucose (24), Pleuromulin (25), Rosuvastatin precursor (26), Lithocholic acid (27), and Celecoxib (28). Moreover, this method is readily to scale up without deleterious effect on the efficiency (14, 78% yield in 5 mmol scale).

While the buffering system was established to address challenges in hydroamination of α-olefins, it is also applicable to cyclic, 1,1-disubstituted, and 1,2-disubstituted alkenes (29-32). Notably, the yields are generally higher than that of the existing method using much higher loadings of alkenes (13, 29)19. In addition, the present reaction overcomes the limitations of styrenic substrates in the existing protocol. A range of substituted styrenes, including those bearing electronically demanding groups, proved competent coupling partners, delivering the valuable β-aryl amine products in synthetically useful yields (42–60%, 33-38). Importantly, similar to 1, the reaction could afford the secondary alkyl amine products in good yields (57–70%) with only 1:1 ratio of 2 and alkene (4, 11, 12, 16, 17, 19, 21 and 27), and in good-to-excellent yields (65–84%) when using 1.5 equivalents of 2 and with alkene as limiting reagent.

We next investigated the generality of the hydroamination reaction with respect to amine coupling partners under standard conditions A (Fig. 4). The system demonstrated excellent compatibility with various cyclic amines, such as cyclopentylamine (39 and 44) and 4,4-difluorocyclohexanamine (46), delivering products in yields comparable to that of cyclohexylamine (2) (60–69%). Acyclic α−2° alkyl amines proved equally effective, coupling efficiently with both simple and complex alkene substrates (40, 45 and 58). Notably, the method successfully accommodated sterically demanded α−3° alkyl amines, including tert-butylamine (53), 1-aminoadamantane (59), the pharmaceutical compound memantine (57), and 2-phenylpropan-2-amine (56), all providing the alkylated products in good yields (60–80%). While 1-butanamine and 6-aminopentanenitrile showed somewhat reduced reactivities (41, 49, and 55), other α−1° alkyl amines generally maintained moderate-to-good efficiency under the standard conditions (47, 48, 50-52 and 60). Additionally, the atorvastatin calcium intermediate ATS-9 (61) and amino acid derivatives (62) are also compatible to the hydroamination reaction. Strikingly, the reaction exhibits promising reactivity with gaseous alkenes under ambient pressure conditions. While ethylene only delivered the desired product in ca. 20% yield (with 2), propene afforded the N-alkylated product in moderate to good yields (55% for 42 and 80% for 43). Reaction with 1:1 stoichiometry of alkene and primary alkyl amine was also examined, which afforded the associated product with good yield (57). Notably, excellent yields were obtained when using alkene as limiting reagent in the presence of modest excess of alkyl amine (53 and 59).

The above results suggested the effective addition of ARC to α-olefins under the buffered conditions, which also prompted us to examine the stereochemical fidelity of chiral alkylamines (Fig. 5a). When α-chiral primary amine 63 was subjected to the existing conditions with 1 (6.0 equiv.) as coupling partner, the product was afforded with low yield and substantial enantiopurity erosion (64, 20% yield, 39% ee)19. In addition, ee of the recovered 63 also decreased to 59% ee. Similar results were obtained under conditions with HAT-3 as additive, although the yield of 64 could be increased to 36%24. Notably, the enantioselectivity of 64 is significantly lower than that of the recovered 63 under both reaction conditions. These results imply that both the substrate and product undergo racemization (Fig. 1b), which is consistent with the fact that secondary amine could quench the excited state of photocatalyst under reaction conditions24. Pleasingly, under the present buffered conditions, 64 was obtained in 55% yield and 89% ee. The stereochemical fidelity could increase to 95% with 0.9 equiv. of HOTf (43% yield). Notably, the ees of 64 are comparable to that of 63, indicating the possible deactivation of 64 through protonation.

Several alkenes were then evaluated for their coupling with 63 (Fig. 5b). Pleasingly, moderate yields and excellent stereochemical fidelity were achieved for both simple alkenes and complex olefins bearing diverse functional groups (65-69). These results underscore the advantage of the buffering strategy, which not only enhances reactivity but also preserves stereochemical information in alkene hydroamination. However, chiral α‑amino carboxylic esters were proven unsuitable substrates for the present reaction.

To get insights into the reaction mechanism, fluorescence quenching experiments were performed, which suggested the feasibility of reductive quenching of Ir(III)* with both A-1 and 2 (Fig. 6a, and Table S5–S10 of the Supplementary Information). However, we did not observe appreciable decomposition of A-1 (ca. 95% recovered) under the standard conditions. Only trace amount of 70 was detected, presumably formed via a cation radical-accelerated nucleophilic aromatic substitution pathway from A-1 and 2 (Fig. 6b-i)44. The yield of 70 could increase to 4% yield when 1 was removed from the reaction mixture. In addition, H/D exchange of methyl group in A-1 was observed when the reaction was conducted in the presence of D2O (Fig. 6b-ii). These results are consistent with the generation of an arene radical cation from A-1 via single electron oxidation (see Tables S15–S16 and Fig. S21–S25 of the Supplementary Information for details). Interestingly, isomerization of 1 to internal alkenes took place in the absence of alkylamine. Control experiments indicated that light irradiation, photocatalyst and A-1 are all essential to the alkene isomerization (Table S17 and Fig. S26 of the Supplementary Information), in which case the arene radical cation might also be formed. However, no appreciable alkene isomerization was observed in the hydroamination reaction. Thus, we proposed that the arene radical cation would preferentially react with alkylamine. Given the low yield of 70, an electron transfer between arene radical cation and 2 was speculated to take place, leading to the desired ARC, which was then rapidly captured by 1. Cyclic voltammetry studies reveal that the oxidation potential (Ep/2) of A-1 and 2 are 1.13 and 0.90 V vs Fc+/Fc, respectively, suggesting the possibility of such electron transfer pathway (Fig. 6c, and Figs. S14–S18 of the Supplementary Information). Moreover, our computational studies also suggest the feasibility of such single electron pathway with a ∆G of 3.7 kcal/mol (see Section 8 of the Supplementary Information for details). Taken together, we proposed that A-1 might serve as a redox mediator between Ir(III)* and 2, ensuring the rapid quenching the short-lived Ir(III)* and effective oxidation of 2 in low concentration. Consistent with this hypothesis, the quantum yield increases from 0.81 to 1.08% by including A-1 as an additive (see Section 4.3 of the Supplementary Information for details). However, there remains inconsistence between the quenching constants observed in fluorescence quenching experiments for A-1 and 2, i.e., Ksv(A-1) > Ksv(2), and the oxidation potential measured by cyclic voltammetry, i.e., Ep/2(A-1) > Ep/2(2). The possibility of an energy transfer pathway for quenching PC-2* with A-1 has been considered, but it appears energetically unfavorable, as the triplet energy of PC-2, i.e., ET(PC-2), is 54.6 kcal/mol45, while ET(A-1) is estimated to be 76.3 kcal/mol46. Future efforts will be directed toward elucidating the detailed mechanism.

A plausible reaction pathway was illustrated in Fig. 6d according to the literature precedents19,24. The reaction is proposed to start with reductive quenching of excited photocatalyst Ir(III)* by primary alkyl amine 2 to afford ARC and reduced form of photocatalyst Ir(II). An alternative pathway for ARC generation might involve an arene radical cation-mediated amine oxidation as noted above. Then, ARC adds to alkene to afford β-aminoalkyl radical, followed by hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) from thiophenol to form product (3•HOTf). Finally, Ir(II) oxidation by the resulting ArS• radical, followed by proton transfer from 2•HOTf to ArS- anion, completes the catalytic cycle and releases free amine 2. An alternative pathway might involve proton coupled electron transfer (PCET) between Ir(II), ArS•, and 2•HOTf to directly afford Ir(III), ArSH and 2.

To conclude, we have developed an efficient photocatalytic hydroamination of unactivated alkenes with primary alkylamines through an amine/ammonium buffering strategy. The reaction exhibits extraordinary broad scope in olefins, including gaseous alkenes, styrenic substrates and complex olefins, and proceed efficiently with near equal stoichiometry of olefin to primary alkylamine. More importantly, this method shows promise to maintain excellent stereochemical fidelity for α-chiral alkylamine. We contribute the high efficiency to the buffering-enabled mitigation in ARC deprotonation and arene-mediated amine oxidation for ARC generation. Future studies will focus on expanding this buffering concept to other challenging amination reactions.

Method

General procedure for hydroamination of alkenes

Under argon atmosphere, a 10 mL Schlenk tube was charged with alkyl amine (1.0–1.5 equiv.), degassed anhydrous iPrOAc (1 mL) and a freshly prepared solution of HOTf (1 mL, 0.16 M or 0.26 M in iPrOAc, 0.8 or 1.3 equiv.), the solution was vigorously stirred for 1 min. To this solution were added HAT-2 (16.2 mg, 0.06 mmol, 30 mol%), [Ir(dF(CF3)ppy)2(5,5’-CF3-bpy)]PF6 (4.6 mg, 0.004 mmol, 2 mol%), A-2 (42 μL, 0.3 mmol, 1.5 equiv.), α-alkenes (1.0 or 1.5 equiv.) and iPrOAc (2 mL). The resulting yellow solution was irradiated by a single 75 W Kessil PR160L-440 nm LED lamp and magnetically stirred for 36 h. The reaction mixture was added with 1 M Na2CO3 solution (3 mL), then extracted with DCM (20 mL) for three times. The combined organic phase was dried over Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified through column chromatography on silica gel to afford the titled product.
