Integrating Lab‐ and Field‐Based Approaches to Decipher Individuals' Response to Anthropogenic Change
Authors: M. de Guinea, R. Landesman, J. R. Madden, T. Bugnyar, Y. Bartan, R. Nathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe global expansion of human activities increasingly exposes wild animals to novel, potentially threatening conditions. Consistent behavioural responses to perceived risk may be detrimental to wildlife, either by limiting foraging opportunities or by overexposing them to lethal hazards. We examine the relationship between risk responses, movement patterns and survival in fan‐tailed ravens ( Corvus rhipidurus ) inhabiting Israel's Dead Sea coastline. We found consistent individual differences assessed through controlled laboratory assays (novel food, objects, environment and foraging near humans) and even stronger consistencies in the wild via GPS‐derived movement metrics. While risk‐prone ravens remained near tourist sites and travelled less, risk‐averse individuals foraged farther towards the edge of their home ranges, avoiding human activity. Further, risk‐averse individuals were more likely to survive over extended periods than risk‐prone individuals. As anthropogenic change accelerates, variation in consistent risk‐taking behaviour could determine individuals' capacity to adapt and survive and consequently shape population composition and persistence.

Introduction

Within populations, individuals from a wide array of species have shown consistent differences over time in risk‐taking behaviour when exposed to potentially threatening stimuli or situations (insects: Muller et al. 2010; cetaceans: Lilley et al. 2018; mustelids: Johnstone and Garvey 2023; rodents: Best et al. 2020; ungulates: Schaffer et al. 2021; corvids: Miller et al. 2022; primates: Kalan et al. 2019). Individuals that consistently take risks may profit from efficient resource acquisition or niche expansion (Moiron et al. 2020; Laskowski et al. 2022), yet indiscriminate exploitation of resources near threats can conversely elevate mortality risks through predation and environmental hazards (Réale et al. 2010). If differences among individuals in risk‐taking behaviour extend across disparate threatening contexts, then the willingness to take risks in one context may correspond to risk‐taking in another context (Fletcher et al. 2023), thereby increasing their overall exposure to threats (Sih et al. 2023). Conversely, an animal tendency to respond consistently to risk may depend on the type of risk itself since behavioural responses might entail different trade‐offs across risk contexts (Van Oers et al. 2005). Nevertheless, both context‐consistent and ‐inconsistent risk‐taking behaviour may affect survival by means of natural selection and the selection of specific behaviours in one context could affect individual behaviour in others (Dingemanse and Réale 2005; Sih et al. 2015).

Yet, while evidence on risk‐taking behaviour within a single context or across two contexts is widespread (Laskowski et al. 2022), exploration of behavioural responses to risk across multiple contexts and their interactions with survival remains understudied (Moiron et al. 2020; but see Carter et al. 2012). Most behavioural assessments of risk‐taking are based on laboratory assays, with fewer studies evaluating behavioural responses in natural contexts (Morand‐Ferron et al. 2016), and even fewer applying both approaches to the same individuals (Spiegel et al. 2017). Research exploring consistency in risk‐taking across contexts has relied on wild animals being captured and exposed to unfamiliar or threatening conditions in laboratories (Dall and Griffith 2014). Measurements under controlled conditions can help identify specific behavioural traits while minimising confounding factors (Dingemanse et al. 2010; Sih et al. 2015). However, captivity may also lead to behavioural artefacts, for example arising due to increased stress (Butler 2016), reduced motivation (Niemelä and Dingemanse 2018), or unrealistic experimental conditions that do not reflect natural challenges (Perals et al. 2017). Likewise, behavioural assays in captivity may also preclude tests of natural behaviours in ecologically realistic settings either due to ethical constraints (exposure to genuine predation risks) or logistical constraints (exposure to out‐of‐scale settings; Clinchy et al. 2013). Comparisons of risk responses measured for the same animals both in captivity and in the wild have yielded mixed results, with some evidence for matching (e.g., Herborn et al. 2010; Yuen et al. 2016) but also evidence of disparity (e.g., Mouchet and Dingemanse 2021; Ladurée et al. 2026). Indeed, since laboratory and natural settings are notably different, it has been argued that behavioural traits measured in these two settings are likely to differ (Niemelä and Dingemanse 2014; Janmaat 2019; Horn et al. 2022). Therefore, rather than considering laboratory and wild settings as extensions of one another when assessing consistent individual differences, they should be considered complementary scenarios that together provide a more complete picture of an individual's behavioural profile (Calisi and Bentley 2009).

Although links between behavioural traits and (proxy measure of) fitness in natural populations are increasingly documented (e.g., individual survival; Laskowski et al. 2022), survival is often inferred from capture–recapture data, with few studies directly quantifying mortality events (Moiron et al. 2020). Evaluating consistent individual differences expressed in natural habitats is essential to understand their consequences for individual fitness (Dingemanse and Réale 2005), their adaptive value under rapid environmental change (Sih 2013), and their broader implications for population dynamics (Dingemanse and Wolf 2013). However, only a few studies have quantified both behavioural traits and fitness (e.g., survival) of the same free‐ranging individuals that were tracked throughout their home range with sufficient detail and over extended periods in the wild (e.g., McCormick et al. 2018; de Van Walle et al. 2024). This scarcity is largely explained by the difficulties of measuring either behavioural traits or survival in the wild, and by the high uncertainty associated with interpreting field‐based behavioural traits assessments. Closing these gaps is required to understand how behavioural responses mediate the fate of individuals, and thereby population trajectories and persistence, in human‐modified environments (Sih 2013).

High‐resolution wildlife tracking technologies provide new opportunities for inferring both risk‐taking behaviour and survival (and other fitness assays) from movement data obtained from free‐ranging individuals in natural settings (Hertel et al. 2021; Nathan et al. 2022). For example, individuals might either constrain their range to minimise their exposure to potential threats in unfamiliar areas (Eccard et al. 2022) or increase their daily movements to forage in areas that minimise such exposure to potential predators or humans (Shaw 2020). However, most studies have examined individual responses to only a single threatening stimulus and a single movement metric, limiting the ability to assess consistency in risk‐related behaviour across different contexts in both experimental and wild settings (Sih et al. 2023).

Corvids, in general, tend to nest and thrive in proximity to human activity (Benmazouz et al. 2021; Marzluff and Neatherlin 2006), exhibiting risk‐taking behaviour that varies markedly between species (Brown and Jones 2016; Miller et al. 2022). However, consistency in risk‐taking behaviour between conspecific individuals is understudied, despite potentially being a critical response to human‐induced pressures. The population of fan‐tailed ravens ( Corvus rhipidurus ; hereafter “FTs”) in the Dead Sea area in Israel has been declining over the last five decades in parallel to increasing anthropogenic pressure primarily derived from tourism (de Guinea et al. in prep). The expansion of human activities shifted the original, desert habitat from one of scarce and unpredictable resources (Lewin et al. 2024) towards one of increasing abundance and predictability of food resources at key sites (i.e., campgrounds, recreational areas, hotels). Yet, this shift came at the cost of increasing risk to the birds derived from interacting with hazardous items, ingesting poisonous food sources, being harmed by humans or colliding with unfamiliar structures at the site (Loss et al. 2015; Oro et al. 2013). Consistent individual differences in risk‐taking behaviour across contexts may influence how ravens interact with novel resources or human disturbance, and ultimately their survival. For example, risk‐averse individuals might avoid unfamiliar but profitable food sources, while risk‐prone individuals might persistently expose themselves to potential threats (Sih et al. 2023). Thus, increased mortality might be associated with either increased foraging effort in risk‐averse individuals or increased hazard exposure in risk‐prone individuals.

Here, we test whether individual‐level differences both in risk‐taking behaviour under controlled laboratory conditions, and in movement patterns under natural settings, are consistent across contexts within each setting. We then test whether these differences are associated with individual survival in the wild. First, we hypothesise that individuals are characterised by consistent individual differences in risk‐taking behaviour: (1) within and across contexts in the lab (i.e., reaction towards unfamiliar food items, unfamiliar objects, foraging in the presence of humans and unfamiliar environments); and, (2) within and among movement metrics in the wild (i.e., displacement, speed, residency time, time in proximity to tourist sites and time spent in proximity to novel food provision sites). Second, we explore the link between captivity‐ and wild‐measured differences in risk‐taking, to assess whether the more feasible captivity‐based controlled trials used to characterise variation in behavioural traits are relevant for assessing the variation in their key outcomes of movement in the wild. We tested whether risk‐prone individuals (as characterised in lab tests) either spend time in proximity to tourist sites where waste food resources are now available, minimising their time and energy invested into travelling, or instead spend time at the edge of their range, travelling further and expending time and energy to avoid these potentially risky tourist sites. Lastly, we examined the relationship between consistent individual variation in risk response, as measured in both settings, and their survival in the wild. We examined two opposing hypotheses. First, that risk‐taking brings rewards. If this is the case, then risk‐prone individuals will be more likely to survive due to improved access to human‐derived food sources. Second, that risk‐taking is truly risky. If this is the case, then risk‐averse individuals will be more likely to survive due to reduced exposure to lethal threats.

Methods

Study Site

We conducted our research in the Eastern section of the Judean Desert, the Dead Sea coastline of Israel (31°19′26″–31°47′37″ N, 35°15′28″–35°25′10″ W), a desert ecosystem characterised by high temperatures and prolonged dry periods. The Dead Sea coastline has experienced an expansion of tourist infrastructures including an intensively used highway that links several viewpoints, recreational spots, a resort compound and three National Parks (i.e., Qumran National Park, Ein Gedi Nature Reserve and Masada National Park). The mean number of daily visitors in the area increased by 60.3% between 2015 and 2023 (Wendt 2016; INPA 2025), and it is expected to continue rising in future years.

Data Collection

We trapped FTs using an Australian trap (i.e., 4 × 4 m2 and 2.5 m height, Moran 1991) located approximately 500 m from Ein Gedi Nature Reserve, the main communal roosting site of FTs in the Dead Sea coastline. During the breeding season (i.e., between February and May), individuals disperse along the Dead Sea coastline to nest at different locations, but after breeding they consistently return to the Ein Gedi roost. Trapping was limited to the period between late August and December of each year to minimise seasonal effects on behaviour, particularly those associated with the breeding season, and we only transferred individuals in optimal physical conditions. We trapped a total of 58 FTs, of which 54 FTs (males: 25; females: 29) were transferred to our facilities at The Hebrew University of Jerusalem across four consecutive years (2021: 21 individuals; 2022: 14 individuals; 2023: 10 individuals; 2024: 9 individuals; see Table S1 for details). With the exception of birds trapped in 2021, which were housed for up to 83 days, all individuals captured between 2022 and 2024 were kept in the aviary for 29–47 days.

To ensure that individuals within each trapping round experienced similar levels of habituation, we only transferred birds that were captured within two weeks of initially opening the trap. We housed FTs in an aviary (i.e., five interconnected compartments encompassing a total of 10 × 10 m2 and 3 m height; s Figure S2) in groups comprising 3–12 individuals. We determined the sex of each bird using molecular methods, collecting chest feathers and extracting DNA using a NaOH‐based lysis protocol that chemically breaks down feather tissue to release genomic DNA, followed by PCR amplification of sex‐linked markers (Turjeman et al. 2016). Birds were habituated to the site for one week and then tested for the next six weeks. Throughout their time in captivity, birds had access to the entire aviary and were maintained under standardised husbandry conditions. We provided a plain, consistent diet that included dry dog food, boiled eggs, and fresh vegetables (e.g., cucumbers, carrots, sweet potatoes). To reduce food competition and encourage natural foraging behaviours, the food was placed in 4–5 dishes dispersed across different locations in the aviary. We installed several branches and standing platforms together with wood ladders and balls hanging from branches for enrichment. Each individual was examined by a licensed veterinarian upon arrival and again prior to release, and body weight was measured at both time points to track changes in physical condition.

We assayed risk‐taking behaviour in captivity using four different assays each conducted over three trials lasting 30 min each: (1) exposure to novel food; (2) exposure to novel objects; (3) exhibition of foraging under risk; and (4) behaviour within a novel environment. We ran three trials per subject for each assay, in the same order across rounds, while continuously observing and recording bird behaviour.

Novel Food Test

We exposed FTs to food items that ravens presumably do not encounter in their natural habitat: shrimps, mussels, squids, dragon fruit, red cheese, purple chips and dog treats of three different shapes and colours. A testing area of 2 m × 1 m was demarked in the centre of the room, where items were placed at 0.5 m from each other (see s Figures S1 and S2). For each testing trial, we selected similar volumes of three unfamiliar food items each of which was placed in a different plate (e.g., generally 4–5 shrimps, 4–5 mussels and 4–5 squids). To maintain consistency within each trial and minimise potential learning effects across trials, we used a different set of three novel food items for the second and third trials. At the beginning of each trial, we isolated individual ravens in a chamber next to the testing compartments to subsequently allow them to enter the testing compartments without direct handling. Individuals in the testing compartments were visually isolated from the rest of the group. We measured latency time (i.e., time duration since starting the experiment until subjects entered the testing arena), frequency of approach (i.e., number of times subjects entered the testing arena during a trial), time in proximity (i.e., total time subjects spent within the testing arena) and manipulation frequency (i.e., total number of times that animals touched the novel items). For details on control trials please see Text S1.

Novel Object Test

We exposed FTs to a set of unfamiliar objects (i.e., recreational dog toys) that had never interacted with. We selected three objects per trial that were placed in the novel arena at 0.5 m from each other. As in the novel food test, objects were consistent within trial but varied across trials (i.e., all individuals were exposed to the same objects in a given trial but we selected different objects across trials). We followed the same protocol as in the novel food test by which we isolated each individual from the group in a blinded compartment and recorded their latency to approach novel objects, frequency to approach novel objects, time in proximity of novel objects and manipulation frequency.

Foraging Under Risk Test

We examined FTs tendency to forage in proximity to a human experimenter by displaying three white plates at 0.5 m distance from each other, with each plate containing three familiar, palatable food items (i.e., peanut butter‐flavoured puffed corn snack, hereafter referred to as “reward”). The human experimenter was sitting outside the enclosure within sight of the raven. As in the novel food test and novel object test, we isolated individual ravens from the rest of the group and we measured latency as the time to approach the reward, frequency of approach, time in proximity of the reward and manipulation frequency of the reward.

Novel Environment Test

The fourth assay was administered in another novel arena (9 m × 4 m × 2.5 m; see s Figure S2c) in which we randomly modified the arrangement of five large branches, stones and ropes for each of the three trials. We also isolated individuals from the rest of the group in an adjacent compartment of the novel environment arena and allowed them to enter the arena without direct handling (s Figure S1). We recorded latency time to enter the arena, latency time to enter in the second compartment of the arena (i.e., the entire body of the individual had entered the compartment), number of travel bouts (i.e., number of flying/walking trajectories separated by at least 1 min of inactivity), number of standing locations (i.e., number of times that individuals rested continuously for at least 1 min in locations separated by at least 1 m), number of touches on branches, time spent flying, time spent in the upper section of the compartment, time spent on the ground and number of quadrats used (i.e., we divided each chamber in 9 quadrats each). All trials were recorded using a custom‐designed synchronised multi‐camera recording system (i.e., six cameras, Recnode, Loopbio GmbH, Austria), and a commercial software for automated behavioural coding (loopy, Loopbio GmbH, Austria). Cameras (Basler ace cameras, type: acA1920‐155uc) were connected to a computer and a hardware synchronizer (Loopbio Trigger Box). All behaviours were manually coded during or after the trial using the Loopbio automated behavioural coding tool.

Tracking Raven Movement in the Wild

After completing all behavioural tests, we released birds back to the wild in the same location they had been trapped. Each bird was fitted with a 15‐g solar‐powered GPS/accelerometer (ACC) device (OrniTrack‐15, Ornitela), which was attached with a harness as a backpack. The frequency at which GPS fixes were collected depended on the battery of the tag: (1) > 90% battery: 1‐s intervals; (2) 75%–90% battery: 5‐min intervals; (3) 50%–75% battery: 10‐min intervals; (4) 25%–50% battery: 30‐min intervals; (5) < 25% battery: 2‐h intervals. Tri‐axial accelerometery was recorded at 20 Hz frequency during 4 s accompanying each GPS fix made as long as the battery of the tag was above 10%. We selected movement data spanning up to one‐year post‐testing in captivity to account for potential ontogenetic variation in behavioural responses or movement patterns. In addition, we standardised the post‐release monitoring period to span from June to December to minimise the potential confounding effects of the breeding season on movement metrics. Thus, we selected six months of tracking data per individual within the same temporal window (i.e., excluding breeding season) across four consecutive years (2021–2025).

Since we only tagged individuals that weighed > 500 g (i.e., tags cannot exceed 3% of body mass to meet wildlife tagging conventions; Geen et al. 2019), we tagged all but three birds that were brought and tested in our facilities (i.e., 51 individuals). To ensure sufficiently long tracking duration necessary for representative bird behaviour in the wild, we set a threshold of three weeks of tracking data for inclusion in the analyses. Consequently, we excluded four individuals that died post‐release, and six with tag malfunctions. Overall, 41 individuals were included in our movement analyses.

To minimise short‐term effects of capture, handling and tagging, we excluded the first five days of tracking data following release for all individuals (Stiegler et al. 2024). We then removed all GPS and ACC data collected at night (20:00–05:00) and locations interpreted as outliers due to satellite inaccuracy (i.e., speeds between consecutive points exceeding 25 m s−1). Since GPS sampling frequency varied as function of solar battery loading, days with extremely high temporal resolution (i.e., 1 s sampling) were first subsampled by retaining the first GPS fix every 30 s. Lastly, we selected days with a mean time interval between consecutive GPS fixes of 15 min, discarding days below the identified inflection point in sampling effort while retaining days with higher sampling frequency (maximum 198 GPS fixes per day, ~2 fixes min−1), based on: (1) the visual inspection of the distribution of sampling rates considering a maximum of 4 h, 2 h, 1 h, 30 min, 20 min, 15 min and 10 min, which showed that threshold of 20 and 15 min led to normal distributions of sampling frequencies (see Figures S4 and S5); and (2) segmented regressions on all collected data identifying inflection points at 42.5 daily GPS fixes in relation to daily distance travelled (mean time interval of 14.1 min), 40.7 daily GPS fixes in relation to daily displacement (mean time interval of 14.7 min), 31 daily GPS fixes in relation to daily speed (mean time interval of 19.4 min) and 32 daily GPS fixes in relation to residency time (mean time interval of 18.8 min; Figure S5). Similarly, accelerometer data were included only for the same weeks that met the GPS data inclusion criteria.

Mortality

We monitored each GPS‐tracked individual to detect prolonged periods of inactivity, and visited the last recorded location in the field when a tag remained stationary for at least one day. An individual was classified as dead if a complete or partial carcass was recovered, or when a detached tag was found at the site along with evidence for death (e.g., feathers or skeleton; i.e., 22 out 51 tagged individuals were classified as dead). Cases of detached tags with no clear evidence of mortality were classified as unknown rather than death (i.e., 9 out of 51 tagged individuals were classified as unknown; Panter et al. 2025). For each mortality event, we defined the day of death as the first stationary or motionless day (Panter et al. 2025). All retrieved carcasses were transported to the University Veterinary Institute (Beit Dagan, Israel) for post mortem analyses; however, these analyses provided only limited insights into the mortality causes and are therefore not presented in this study.

Mapping Tourist Sites and Feeding Stations

To map tourist sites across the Dead Sea area, we first consulted two widely used hiking applications in the region, the Israeli Hiking Map and Amud Anan, to identify relevant locations such as viewpoints, campgrounds, national parks and trails. We then conducted field visits to each of these sites to assess the presence of visitors and human‐derived food items, and to consult with experienced regional rangers in charge of the area. Based on this combined information, we manually delineated the perimeters of the identified tourist sites. Similarly, we visited and manually mapped the location of provisioned feeding sites provided by the Israel Nature and Parks Authority. For the purposes of our analysis, we used the centroid of each mapped polygon and applied a 500 m radius to determine time spent in proximity to each site using the “recurse” package v.1.4 (Bracis et al. 2018). We then calculated the proportion of time each individual spent within tourist sites and provisioned feeding sites by dividing the time spent in these areas by the total tracking duration for that individual on a given date.

We use publicly available spatial layers from the Ministry of the Interior of Israel (www.govmap.gov.il and israelhiking.osm.org.il) to determine the locations of hotels, campgrounds, viewpoints, parking areas, hiking sites, beaches and national parks, which we characterised as tourist sites. We considered three provisioned foraging sites at the edge of FTs' range, two of which were protected feeding stations without human presence established by the Israeli Nature and Parks Authority and one of which was an urban dumpster with low human presence located 3.2 km away from the closest urban centre (s Figure S3).

Number of daily visitors to each of the national parks present in the study area was provided by the National Parks Authority of Israel. We estimated human presence at tourist sites as the total number of visitors in the area for a given day.

Environmental Variables

Additionally, we used temperature measures from four meteorological stations (Thaller shelter model 4) collected at a resolution of 10 min belonging to the Israel Meteorological Service (Arad, Metzoke Dragot, Ein Gedi and Bet Haarava). For each recorded GPS location, we interpolated the maximum temperature (hereafter temperature) collected by the closest meteorological station at the time the GPS location was taken. Since we used weekly measurements of movement metrics, we calculated the mean number of visitors and temperature at a weekly level as well.

Data Processing and Statistical Analyses

Lab‐Based Metrics

We carried out all our analyses in R v4.4.2 (R Core Team 2024). To summarise individual risk‐taking behaviour, we applied a principal component analysis (PCA) using the FactoMineR package (Husson et al. 2023) to reduce the dimensions of our measured variables per assay and trial into a single score (PC1). Thus, we obtained three estimates of risk‐taking behaviour per assay and per individual that we incorporated in subsequent analyses (similar to Harris et al. 2020; Figure S6).

Movement‐Based Metrics

Each metric was calculated on a daily basis and averaged per week. From the cleaned tracking data (mean 14,644 ± 8105 GPS fixes per individual; mean 38,034 ± 20,770 ACC bursts per individual; see Table S1 for details), we calculated: (1) Proportion of time spent in tourist sites; (2) Proportion of time spent in provisioned feeding sites; (3) Displacement (i.e., maximum distance in metres between roosting site and most distant GPS fix recorded in the same day); (4) Speed (i.e., daily distance travelled in metres divided by tracking time in seconds); (5) Residency time (seconds; i.e., daily time spent at stopping locations divided by the number of daily stops); and (6) Overall Dynamic Body Acceleration (hereafter, “ODBA”; i.e., proxy for energy expenditure derived from tri‐axial accelerometery measures). For a detailed description on the movement metrics calculations see Text S2.

Double Hierarchical Generalised Linear Mixed‐Effect Model (DHGLM)

We ran a multivariate DHGLM implemented in a Bayesian framework with the brms package in R (Bürkner 2017; as in Hertel et al. 2021). Lab‐ and field‐based behaviours were analysed jointly using a stacked multivariate data structure. Each behavioural trait was modelled using only the subset of observations for which it was available, while sharing a common individual‐level random‐effects structure across all traits. This approach allowed information to be pooled across behaviours and contexts while accommodating uneven sampling effort and partial overlap in trait availability among individuals, a common feature of long‐term behavioural and tracking datasets (Nakagawa and Schielzeth 2010; Hertel et al. 2021). Thus, the response variables comprised four laboratory‐based behavioural measures (i.e., responses to a novel object, novel food, foraging under risk and novel environment) and six movement metrics derived from GPS and tri‐axial accelerometry data (i.e., proportion of time in proximity to tourist sites, proportion of time in proximity to edge, displacement, speed, residency time and ODBA). All response variables were modelled assuming Gaussian error distributions.

The mean model described expected behavioural values as a function of fixed and random effects. For behavioural metrics obtained in the lab, fixed effects included sex, month of testing, group housing size and days since trapping (i.e., to account for potential inter‐individual differences in habituation resulting from non‐simultaneous capture dates within experimental rounds; Table S2). For movement metrics obtained in the wild, fixed effects included ambient temperature, tourist presence and sampling intensity. All continuous predictors were centred and scaled prior to analysis to facilitate model convergence and interpretation of effect sizes (i.e., z‐transformed, Gelman and Rubin 1992). Individual identity was included as a random intercept for each behavioural trait, allowing estimation of consistent among‐individual differences in behavioural means. Individual‐level random intercepts were allowed to be correlated across all traits, yielding a multivariate among‐individual variance–covariance matrix that quantified behavioural integration across laboratory and field contexts (Dingemanse and Dochtermann 2013; Araya‐Ajoy and Dingemanse 2013). Residual correlations among traits were fixed to zero because laboratory and field behaviours were never observed simultaneously and residual covariance across contexts is not biologically meaningful.

The dispersion component of the model captured residual (within‐individual) variance around individual‐specific behavioural means. Residual variance was estimated separately for each behavioural trait and represents average within‐individual variability after accounting for fixed effects and among‐individual differences in behavioural means. In contrast to fully parameterised double hierarchical models that explicitly model individual differences in residual variance (i.e., behavioural predictability sensu Hertel et al. 2021), residual variance in our model was not allowed to vary among individuals or as a function of predictors. This modelling choice allowed decomposition of phenotypic variance into among‐individual, fixed‐effect and residual components while maintaining model identifiability and computational tractability given the number of traits and the cross‐context nature of the data (Cleasby et al. 2015; Hertel et al. 2021).

Models were fitted using sampling as implemented in Stan (Carpenter et al. 2017). We ran four Markov chains with 12,000 iterations each, including 5000 burn‐in iterations. Convergence was assessed using visual inspection of trace plots, effective sample sizes and the potential scale reduction factor (R^), with all parameters showing satisfactory convergence (R^<1.01).

Behavioural Consistency Within Contexts

We quantified consistent individual differences in behaviour by decomposing phenotypic variance into variance attributable to among‐individual differences in behavioural means, variance explained by fixed effects, and residual variance (Nakagawa and Schielzeth 2010; Dingemanse and Dochtermann 2013). Repeatability was calculated as the proportion of total variance attributable to among‐individual differences in intercepts. We obtained posterior distributions of repeatability directly from MCMC samples and summarised them using posterior means and credible intervals.

Behavioural Consistency Across Contexts

Since the individual was seen as a random effect, we determined the relationship among (i) risk‐taking measurements, (ii) movement metrics, and (iii) risk‐taking assays and movement metrics, by extracting posterior draws of individual‐specific random intercepts to build correlation matrices (Hertel et al. 2021; Gaona‐Gordillo et al. 2023). Thus, for each posterior draw, correlation matrices were computed across individuals, yielding posterior distributions for all pairwise correlations (Houslay and Wilson 2017). Because laboratory and field behaviours were not available for all individuals, the correlation between lab and movement measures was restricted to the subset of individuals measured in both contexts.

Survival

We analysed survival using Cox proportional hazards regression (Therneau and Grambsch 2000) while explicitly accounting for uncertainty in individual‐level trait estimates through an errors‐in‐variables (EIV) framework (Dingemanse et al. 2021). Survival time was defined as the number of days from GPS tag deployment to either confirmed mortality or the end of the observation period (1 January 2026), with individuals still alive or of unknown status treated as right‐censored. Mortality events were coded as 1 and censored observations as 0.

Individual behavioural and movement traits were treated as latent variables with posterior distributions obtained from multivariate hierarchical models. To propagate uncertainty in these trait estimates into survival analyses, we fitted Cox proportional hazards models repeatedly across posterior draws of individual trait values. For each posterior draw, trait values were merged with the survival dataset and a Cox model was fitted including sex as a covariate. This posterior‐draw Cox procedure yielded a distribution of regression coefficients and hazard ratios for each trait, from which we derived posterior median hazard ratios and 90% credible intervals.

Results

Behavioural Consistency Within and Across Contexts in the Lab

The first component of the four PCAs represented a behavioural continuum, with lower values corresponding to risk‐averse tendencies and higher values to risk‐prone tendencies (Text S3; Table S3). Repeatability estimates from the risk‐taking tests indicate consistent differences among individuals ranging between 0.30 [Credible Intervals (CI): 0.17–0.46] and 0.55 [CI: 0.42–0.67] (Table 1, Figure S7). There were positive correlations between risk‐taking assays particularly between the novel food test and the novel object test [posterior median: 0.76, 95% CI = 0.61–0.87], the novel food test and the foraging under risk test [posterior median: 0.44, 95% CI = 0.19–0.66], and between the novel object test and the foraging under risk test [posterior median: 0.60, 95% CI = 0.35–0.78]. Thus, ravens that scored higher in the novel food test also scored higher in the novel object test and in the foraging under risk test. Associations between the novel environment test and the other assays were generally positive but showed wider credible intervals, indicating greater uncertainty. The covariance estimates indicate positive associations, though with wider credible intervals, suggesting variability in the strength of these relationships (Table 1; Figure 1).

Across the four behavioural assays, sex, trial number, month and days since trapping showed no clear effects on performance in any of the assays (Table S4). The effect of group size (i.e., number of individuals housed together) had a positive effect on risk‐taking behaviour for the novel object test (posterior median = 0.28, 95% CI = 0.02–0.54) and the novel environment test (posterior median = 0.45, 95% CI = 0.10–0.79), while the corresponding effects for the novel food test (posterior median = 0.19, 95% CI = −0.08–0.46) and the foraging under risk test (posterior median = 0.14, 95% CI = −0.19–0.36) were positive but more uncertain, with credible intervals overlapping zero (Table S4).

FTs consistently differed among each other in the proportion of weekly time spent in tourist sites (range across individuals: min: 0.07, max: 0.51), time spent at foraging sites on the edge of their home range (min: 0.01, max: 0.47), displacement (min: 1.64 km, max: 2.75 km), speed (min: 0.44 m s−1, max: 1.57 m s−1) and ODBA (min: 1.95, max: 3.94), with repeatability estimates between 0.31 [CI: 0.22–0.43] and 0.54 [CI: 0.44–0.55] (Table 2, Figure S8). Time spent in proximity to tourist sites was negatively related to time in proximity to foraging sites on the range edge, displacement, speed and ODBA, while time in proximity to foraging sites in edge areas was positively related to displacement, speed and ODBA (Table 2; Figure 2). Estimating Behavioural Consistency Across Lab and Field

Correlations between behavioural assays in captivity and movement metrics in the wild revealed consistent but generally weaker links between individual risk‐taking tendencies and movement strategies than among risk‐taking laboratory measures and among wild movement metrics (Figure 3; Table S6). FTs that scored higher in the foraging under risk test tended to spend more time in proximity to tourist sites (posterior median = 0.55, 95% CI = 0.30–0.74) and longer residence times (posterior median = 0.52, 95% CI = 0.22–0.73), while being negatively associated with time spent at feeding stations (posterior median = −0.36, 95% CI = −0.60 to −0.07), displacement (posterior median = −0.46, 95% CI = −0.68 to −0.19) and speed (posterior median = −0.55, 95% CI = −0.76 to −0.29). We did not find a significant relationship between the foraging under risk test and ODBA (posterior median = −0.12, 95% CI = −0.37 to 0.15; Figure 3). Thus, individuals exhibiting higher risk‐taking during foraging in the presence of human observers tended to concentrate their activity spatially, spending more time near tourist sites while reducing overall movement extent and speed. Associations among the novel food test, novel object test and novel environment test with movement metrics were generally weaker and characterised by higher uncertainty (Table S6).

Survival

Survival analyses revealed that increased mortality was consistently associated with risk‐taking behaviour across behavioural assays specifically in the novel food test (posterior median HR = 1.65, 90% credible interval: 1.32–2.20), novel object test (HR = 1.74, 1.35–2.37), and the foraging under risk test (HR = 1.72, 1.27–2.48; Figure 4). The novel environment test showed a trend in the same direction but with a higher uncertainty than the other behavioural assays (HR = 1.44, 0.85–2.62). Time spent at tourist sites was also strongly associated with increased mortality risk (HR = 1.63, 1.45–1.84). In contrast, individuals with higher mean displacement (HR = 0.79, 0.69–0.92) and higher mean speed (HR = 0.74, 0.62–0.88) were more likely to survive over time. Time spent at range edge showed a negative association with mortality with high uncertainty (HR = 0.88, 0.75–1.02). Lastly, higher mean residence time (HR = 1.57, 1.11–2.21) and overall activity levels as indexed by ODBA (HR = 1.69, 1.44–2.01) were both associated with increased mortality in FTs (Figure 4).

Discussion

Fan‐tailed Ravens inhabiting the rapidly changing Dead Sea coastline not only consistently differ in their reactions towards a range of unfamiliar and potentially threatening stimuli, but also exhibit responses that are consistent across contexts (Sih et al. 2023). In the wild, individual FTs consistently differed in their movement metrics, suggesting a tendency to occupy one of two opposing ends of the movement spectrum. First, individuals that spent longer time at tourist sites travelled shorter distances and spent more time at foraging patches. Second, individuals that spent longer periods at provisioned feeding sites away from human presence travelled longer distances and reduced their time at foraging patches. Behavioural assays conducted in captivity were linked to these movement strategies in the wild, particularly for the foraging under risk test, indicating that individuals expressing higher risk‐taking tendencies when under human observation tended to spatially concentrate their activity near tourist sites. These consistent individual differences had fitness consequences. Risk‐averse individuals exhibited higher survival probabilities than risk‐prone individuals who faced increased exposure to human‐related threats at tourist sites. Overall, consistent individual variation in captivity‐measured risk‐taking is linked to movement behaviour in the wild in FTs, and is also reflected in differential fitness consequences in human‐modified landscapes.

Focusing on the lab‐based work, our findings of consistent individual variation in risk‐taking behaviour in response to different threatening contexts align with previous research on other taxa (Laskowski et al. 2022), and expand prior findings in corvids, which have primarily focused on interspecific differences (Vernouillet and Kelly 2020; Miller et al. 2022). Notably, individual variation was consistent across novel food, novel object and foraging under risk assays, suggesting the presence of an underlying behavioural mechanism that mediates risk response at individual‐level. Since these laboratory test contexts resemble situations commonly encountered by FTs in the wild at anthropogenic sites such as exposure to human‐derived food items, discarded objects and human presence, the perception of any activity associated with humans may trigger such differential response in FTs. However, even though we designed the novel environment test to understand FTs response to unexpected changes in a familiar landscape, its weaker association with the other risk‐taking assays suggests that FTs do not perceive it as a similar risk to the other contexts. Detecting behavioural responses to landscape change may require spatial scales larger than those achievable in captivity to supply ecological realism of environmental novelty. While it is also possible that stress induced by bringing animals from the wild into the lab may have supressed the FTs' behaviour, comparisons between control conditions (neutral stimuli such as empty plates) and test conditions revealed clear behavioural shifts, indicating that individuals were actively engaging with the assays (Figure S9). Ideally, future work should therefore assess responses to environmental change directly in the wild in combination with lab‐based behavioural research associated with other risks such as domestic animal predation or exposure to poisoned crops (Ritchie and Johnson 2009; Sih et al. 2023).

Focusing on field‐based work, we found consistent individual variation in movement patterns among free‐ranging FTs. Not only did individuals consistently differ in their displacement, speed, ODBA and time spent in proximity to tourist sites and feeding sites at the edge of their home range, but most of these metrics were also interconnected. Specifically, the weak association between ODBA and time spent in proximity to tourist sites or residence time may reflect the high energetic demands of fine‐scale movements within foraging patches, particularly at tourist sites, where individuals that remain locally still incur substantial energetic costs. While a growing body of literature has highlighted interindividual differences in single, specific movement metrics across a wide range of taxa (reviewed by Shaw 2020), it is only recently that multiple, individually consistent movement metrics have been integrated to explain animals' spatial behaviour at species and individual level (Abrahms et al. 2017; Forkosh 2021). By combining multiple movement measures, we revealed that consistently low‐mobility individuals (i.e., with shorter displacements, lower speed and lower ODBA) spent more time in proximity to tourist sites, whereas consistently high‐mobility individuals (i.e., larger displacements, higher speed and higher ODBA) spent more time near provisioned feeding sites with very low human presence at the edge of their home range. Importantly, these patterns should be interpreted in light of potential sampling biases arising from non‐random spatial distributions of behavioural types. Holtmann et al. (2017) found evidence that risk‐taking individuals are more likely to occupy anthropogenically disturbed environments, whereas risk‐averse individuals preferentially settle in less disturbed areas. To minimise such bias, we trapped birds in close proximity to their main communal roosting site, allowing us to target, in principle, individuals across the full spectrum of risk‐taking tendencies. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that individuals entering traps were disproportionately characterised by higher risk‐taking tendencies, biasing against the more risk‐averse segment of the population (Carter et al. 2012). This could be addressed by incorporating alternative trapping methods designed to improving our coverage of behavioural phenotypes.

While we emphasise the importance of consistent among‐individual differences in risk‐taking behaviour, our results indicate that approximately 0.15–0.66 of the observed phenotypic variance is attributable to consistent individual variation across contexts (Tables S7 and S8). This implies that a substantial proportion of behavioural variation occurs within individuals, consistent with the presence of flexible behavioural responses (i.e., behavioural plasticity; Dingemanse and Réale 2005; Dingemanse et al. 2010). Indeed, analyses of within‐individual covariance revealed context‐dependent patterns in both laboratory assays and in the wild, although their strength differed markedly between settings. In captivity, within‐individual correlations were generally weak and assay‐specific (e.g., negative covariation between novel food and foraging under risk, but positive associations between novel food and novel environment). In contrast, movement metrics in the wild exhibited stronger and more systematic within‐individual covariation, with displacement, speed and ODBA all being positively associated. All three tended to covary negatively with time spent near tourist sites. Thus, under controlled conditions, isolated individuals adjusted their behaviour in a largely context‐specific manner, whereas in natural settings, individuals moving in social and environmentally heterogeneous landscapes expressed more coordinated behavioural adjustments (Dingemanse and Wolf 2013). Moreover, environmental drivers influenced behavioural expression, with temperature and tourist presence shaping movement patterns in the wild, indicating flexible behavioural responses to context. Altogether, these results show that repeatable individual differences in risk‐taking coexist with structured behavioural plasticity, consistent with theoretical expectations that stable among‐individual variation and plastic responses jointly shape individual and population‐level responses to anthropogenic pressures (Dingemanse et al. 2010; Dingemanse and Wolf 2013).

Combining lab‐ and field‐based approaches, we found that risk‐prone individuals, identified under controlled laboratory settings, were the same low‐mobility individuals who prioritised spending time near tourist sites. Similarly, risk‐averse individuals were high‐mobility individuals who tended to avoid tourist sites (as in Found and St. Clair 2016; Uchida et al. 2019). This suggests that lab‐based risk‐taking assays, although presenting a notably different setting than natural landscapes, are still informative for assessing movement and behavioural response of free‐ranging FTs in their human‐modified environment. The finding that the laboratory assays which were related to human presence (i.e., foraging under risk) was a better predictor of the movement patterns than novel food, novel object, and novel environment tests, indicates that FTs' movement syndromes primarily reflect risk‐taking responses to human presence. This context‐dependent response, inferred from integrating both lab‐ and field‐based approaches, differs from the context‐free response inferred when either approach is used alone. This further underscores the importance of using multiple assays representing different contexts of risk, rather than a single one and highlights the valuable insights that can emerge from integrating approaches (Dochtermann and Royauté 2019; Dingemanse and Wright 2020). Nevertheless, although multivariate models provide a powerful framework to jointly examine relationships between behavioural assays conducted under controlled laboratory conditions and movement metrics quantified in the wild, our estimates for laboratory‐derived traits were characterised by higher uncertainty than those for movement traits. This likely reflects differences in sample size between data sources. Whereas behavioural assays are necessarily limited in number and repetition, especially when drawn from wild animals tested in cognitive laboratories, wild movement data collected through biologging enables continuous monitoring of consistent individual differences across ecological contexts, resulting in more precise estimates of individual‐level variation (Hertel et al. 2021; Nathan et al. 2022). Altogether, these results highlight the complementary roles of laboratory and field approaches, they also emphasise that robust inference about individual behavioural strategies emerges most clearly when both are jointly analysed within a unified modelling framework.

Consistent differences in risk‐taking essays and movement patterns were both linked to survival, with risk‐averse, long‐distance travelling individuals being more likely to persist over time than risk‐prone individuals that remained in proximity of tourist sites. This finding supports our second hypothesis indicating that consistent risk‐taking behaviour across contexts carries significant costs in ecosystems undergoing rapid environmental changes (Sih et al. 2023). The higher mortality among risk‐prone individuals is likely explained by their dependence on anthropogenic food sources at tourist sites, but this in turn might expose them to poisons (Curk et al. 2025), heavy metals (e.g., lead or mercury, Buekers et al. 2009) and/or nutritionally poor items (Murray et al. 2015) that can cause sudden death or chronic health deterioration (Oro et al. 2013). Our post‐mortem analyses examined potential infections (e.g., West Nile Virus and Avian Influenza Virus), as well as lead levels, but no clinical evidence was found. A closer examination of the diet composition and metabolic processing of nutrients will help clarify the role of human‐derived food sources in FTs survival. Risk‐averse individuals, in contrast, relied on carcasses and natural food sources, which despite being located further away from their roosting site likely represented nutritionally valuable food sources (Newsome et al. 2015; Plaza et al. 2019). Hence, a plausible scenario is that human‐induced rapid environmental changes are generating ecological pressures that select for risk‐averse individuals in the population. However, the tendency of risk‐averse individuals to forage farther away than risk‐prone individuals might imply that they expend more energy on a daily basis, with higher constraints on other key life‐history functions such as mating, breeding and parental care (Speakman 2007). Thus, a dual negative impact on the population might be taking place: risk‐prone individuals face higher mortality rates in the short term, while risk‐averse individuals survive over longer periods but expend more energy and thus may face reduced reproductive opportunities. Future research integrating diet analyses and reproductive monitoring will be essential to disentangle the causes of mortality and fitness costs in FTs and elucidate the key drivers of population viability in the Dead Sea region.

The association between behavioural phenotypes and patterns of space use in human‐modified environments suggests that anthropogenic pressure is actively driving FTs' population decline. Although corvids exhibit substantial evolutionary lability and have undergone rapid niche expansion and adaptive divergence at macroevolutionary scales (Garcia‐Porta et al. 2022), the current pace and intensity of human‐driven landscape change may exceed the capacity for adaptive responses at contemporary timescales, resulting instead in behavioural mismatches and selective loss of particular phenotypes (Sih et al. 2011; Tuomainen and Candolin 2011). These effects are likely amplified by the smaller body size of FTs, which may place them at a competitive disadvantage at key anthropogenic sites where they compete not only with conspecifics but also with larger corvid species and other scavengers. Thus, selection on specific behavioural phenotypes associated with anthropogenic disturbance has the potential to reshape interspecific interactions, with effects that may scale up to alter competitive hierarchies and the strength of trophic and non‐trophic interactions, ultimately influencing community composition and the functioning of ecological networks (Sih et al. 2011; Hunter et al. 2022). Assessments of population vulnerability and design of mitigation strategies would, therefore, benefit from explicitly accounting for individual behavioural variation even in taxa generally considered tolerant of human‐altered environments (Mackinlay et al. 2023; Tuomainen and Candolin 2011).

Conclusions

Measuring behaviour in the lab under controlled conditions has been criticised for potentially increasing the probability of detecting consistent individual differences in behaviour (Dingemanse et al. 2010). Field measurements, in turn, have been criticised for lacking control over confounding variables, complicating the interpretation of behavioural traits (Hertel et al. 2021). Here, we integrate both approaches to show that consistent variation in risk responses is linked not only to alternative movement strategies but also to differential survival in wild FTs inhabiting the rapidly changing Dead Sea desert environment. Future studies should explore any metabolic and/or reproductive consequences of consistent individual variation in risk responses across landscapes increasingly dominated by human activities.
