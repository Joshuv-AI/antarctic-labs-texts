Determinants of Poor Glycemic Control among Malaysian Women with Gestational Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Abd-Khalim Mohd-Adli, Abdul Jalil Rohana, Surianti Sukeri, Mohd Hanief Ahmad, Hafizuddin Awang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is associated with short- and long-term complications for mothers and offspring. However, information regarding poor glycemic control is still limited in Malaysia. This study aimed to determine self-efficacy, self-care, and physical activity levels, as well as factors, associated with poor glycemic control among women with GDM.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted among women with GDM in Northern Terengganu, Malaysia. Stratified sampling proportional to the population size was used to obtain 238 samples. Logistic regression was applied to determine associations between factors and poor glycemic control.Results:The mean (SD) scores for overall self-efficacy and self-care were 147.00 (23.94) and 3.55 (0.74), respectively. The median (IQR) for overall physical activity was 137.38 (111.91). Dietetic counseling (AOR = 2.95; 95% CI: 1.41, 6.17; P = 0.004) and diet self-efficacy (AOR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.61, 0.98; P = 0.040) were associated with poor glycemic control.Conclusions:Comprehensive health education focusing on managing glycemic control can enhance individuals’ self-efficacy, empowering them to effectively perform diabetes self-care activities, which subsequently reduces short- and long-term complications.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as glucose intolerance with varying degrees of severity that is first detected during pregnancy, regardless of its persistence postdelivery.[12] Globally, it affects about 14% of pregnancies,[3] but in Malaysia, 22.5%.[4] Since a high rate of GDM often indicates a high prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in a population[2] and since Malaysia has seen a consistent rise in T2DM prevalence from 11.2% in 2011 to 13.4% in 2015 and 18.3% in 2019,[5] a corresponding increase in GDM cases in Malaysia is expected in the future. The implications of GDM extend beyond pregnancy, as epidemiological studies show that women with GDM are 10 times more likely to develop T2DM,[6] while their offspring have seven times greater odds of developing T2DM or prediabetes.[7]

Previous studies have identified several factors contributing to poor glycemic control among women with GDM, including advanced maternal age,[8] low educational levels,[9] multiparity,[810] prepregnancy body mass index (BMI),[811] and receiving of insulin therapy.[8] Adherence to self-care practices, such as dietary modification and regular physical activity, is crucial for achieving optimal glycemic control.[12] However, possessing knowledge and skills alone may not be sufficient to prompt individuals to adopt healthier behaviors; they also need to have good self-efficacy.[13] Self-efficacy can be defined as individuals’ belief in their ability to carry out actions necessary to achieve desired outcomes.[13] Bandura[14] introduced this concept and proposed that individuals with high self-efficacy are more likely to engage in tasks, whereas those with low self-efficacy are prone to task avoidance.

Despite awareness of the benefits of dietary modifications and physical activity, previous studies revealed that women with GDM adhere poorly to these lifestyle changes.[915] However, only a few studies in Malaysia have investigated sociodemographic, obstetric, and behavioral determinants of poor glycemic control in women with GDM. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine self-efficacy, self-care, and physical activity levels, as well as factors, associated with poor glycemic control among women with GDM.

Methods

Participants and sampling

This cross-sectional study was conducted among women with GDM in two districts (Besut and Setiu) in Northern Terengganu who visited primary health clinics for antenatal care. The sample size was determined using the formula for comparisons of two independent proportions for each factor. The largest sample size was 238 samples. To calculate the sample size required for each clinic, the numbers of registered GDM cases in all clinics in both districts were obtained from the districts’ Maternal and Child Health units. To ensure representative sampling, the proportional-to-size stratified sampling method was used. Then, simple random sampling was employed to obtain a total of 238 participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study included women with GDM who were Malaysian citizens aged 18–49 years. However, women with preexisting diabetes were excluded because they were receiving continuous health education regarding dietary modification, physical activity, and self-monitoring of blood glucose, which could have influenced their self-efficacy and/or self-care. Also excluded were women who could not understand the Malay language.

Research tools

A questionnaire with four sections was used. Section A focused on the sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics of the participants (i.e., including their age, ethnicity, marital status, educational level, employment status, household income, parity, BMI, GDM treatment, dietetic counseling, and three latest prebreakfast blood sugar profile readings). The mean of these three prebreakfast blood sugar profile readings was calculated, and values above 5.3 mmol/L were considered indicative of poor control.[16]

Section B was the validated Malay version of the Diabetes Management Self-Efficacy Scale (DMSES), which has high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.951).[17] It consists of 20 questions categorized into five domains: eating plan, physical activity, blood glucose monitoring, foot care, and medications and follow-up. It uses an 11-point Likert scale, with 0 indicating cannot do at all and 10 indicating certainly can do. Higher scores implied that the participants were very confident in performing the activities listed in the questionnaire.

Section C was the validated Malay version of the Summary of Diabetes Self-Care Activities (SDSCA) measure of the number of days spent on self-care activities within the last seven days. The Cronbach’s alpha of this measure is 0.740.[17] It consists of 10 questions across five domains: general diet, specific diet, physical activity, blood glucose testing (BGT), and foot care. However, for this study, the foot care domain was excluded, as in a study by Al-Hashmi et al.[15] The mean score for each domain was calculated separately.[18] A higher score implied better performance in self-care activities, except for item 4, which uses reverse scoring.

Section D was the validated Malay version of the Pregnancy Physical Activity Questionnaire (PPAQ). Its intraclass correlation coefficients are 0.679 for total activity, 0.236–0.776 for activities based on the type of activity and 0.542–0.679 for activities based on their intensity.[19] The PPAQ consists of 33 questions categorized into five types of activity: household chores or caregiving activities, transportation activities, sports or exercise activities, occupational activities, and inactivity. The participants were asked to estimate the time they spent on each activity per day or week in the current trimester. Thus, physical activity was analyzed based on the type of activity and the activity intensity (categorized into sedentary behavior and light-intensity, moderate-intensity, and vigorous-intensity activities).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26. All the data were tabulated for descriptive statistics. Categorical data were presented in terms of frequency and percentage, and numerical data were presented as means and standard deviations (SDs), except for physical activity data, which were commonly not normally distributed and were thus presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Binary logistic regression was used to determine the association of sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, as well as of self-efficacy, self-care activity, and physical activity levels, with glycemic control. All the variables with a P value < 0.25 in the univariable analysis or that were clinically important were included in the multivariable analysis.

Results

Sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics

Table 1 shows the sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants. Their mean (SD) age was 32.29 (4.91) years. Almost all of them were Malay (99.6%) and married (99.6%). Around one-third of them attained tertiary education (37.0%), had a history of GDM (34.5%), and had received dietetic counseling (37.4%). About half of them were housewives (53.8%). A large majority of them were in the bottom 40% of household income (B40) group (83.6%). The mean (SD) BMI was 28.59 (6.20) kg/m2.

Self-efficacy, self-care activity, and physical activity levels

Table 2 shows the mean (SD) scores for the self-efficacy and self-care activity levels, as well as the median (IQR) scores for the physical activity levels. The mean (SD) score for the overall self-efficacy level was 147.00 (23.94), with the most self-efficacious tasks reported as related to medication and follow-up, and the least self-efficacious tasks, to dieting. For the self-care activity level, the overall mean (SD) score was 3.55 (0.74), with specific dietary practices (i.e., consumption of vegetables, fruits, and low-fat foods) ranked highest, and BGT, lowest. The median (IQR) score for total energy expenditure was 137.38 (111.91) metabolic equivalent of task hours per week (MET-h/week). The energy expenditure was highest for household and caregiving activities, and lowest for occupation-related activities. Regarding activity intensity, light-intensity activities were performed most frequently, and vigorous-intensity activities were performed the least.

In the univariable analysis using simple logistic regression, the following variables were found to have a P value < 0.25 and were thus included in the multivariable analysis: four sociodemographic and obstetric variables (educational level, history of GDM, parity, and dietetic counselling), one self-efficacy variable (diet), one self-care activity variable (BGT), and two physical activity variables (inactivity and sedentary behavior). The multivariable analysis showed that only two variables were significantly associated with poor glycemic control: dietetic counseling and diet self-efficacy [Table 3].

Discussion

This study displayed a moderately high level of self-efficacy among women with GDM, with a mean score of 147.00 (23.94). This value is slightly higher than that in another study among women with GDM in Oman,[15] potentially reflecting differences in cultural and sociodemographic contexts between the two regions. The participants displayed the highest self-efficacy in tasks related to medication intake and attending follow-up appointments, similar to findings from previous studies.[172122] A possible explanation for this finding is that tasks associated with medication adherence and follow-up visits are considered straightforward and easy to perform. The least efficacious tasks were related to dietary practices, as Ahmad Sharoni and Wu[17] also found.

As for self-care activities, in the present study, the specific diet practices (i.e., consumption of vegetables, fruits, and low-fat foods) domain had the highest self-care activity score, mirroring the result of a Jordanian study.[23] However, in another Malaysian study,[22] the general diet (i.e., following a healthy diet) domain had the highest self-care activity score. Nevertheless, these findings show that in various studies, dietary practices emerged as the most commonly performed self-care activities within a seven-day timeframe. In contrast, the BGT domain had the lowest self-care activity score, as in previous studies[2223] and in another study conducted in Malaysia,[24] confirming that self-monitoring of blood glucose practices remains deficient in the country. Some authors have pointed out that not owning a glucometer is a contributing factor to the low BGT rates among patients with T2DM or women with GDM.[2223]

Regarding physical activity levels, the total MET-h/week among women with GDM in the present study is higher than that among women with GDM in Vietnam[25] but lower than that among pregnant women in general.[26] The participants spent most of their time on household chores and/or caregiving activities, similar to findings from previous studies.[252627] In a qualitative study among women with GDM in Australia,[28] the participants admitted having difficulties in performing other types of exercise than household chores and walking. Furthermore, a study[29] found that women who were employed and, thus, contributing to the economic status of their families were still the predominant caregivers at home, which explains the large portion of household-related activities in this study. The least reported types of activities were occupation-related, which might be due to pregnant women’s general inclination to engage less in occupation-related work, as Nascimento et al.[27] discovered. Regarding activity intensity, the participants in the present study predominantly engaged in light-intensity activities and allocated minimal time to vigorous-intensity physical activities. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies.[2627] A study in Serbia[30] found that pregnant women tend to discontinue their moderate- and vigorous-intensity exercises despite their prior engagement in these activities before conception. A possible explanation for this finding could be linked to safety concerns, as highlighted in a qualitative study by Harrison et al.[31]

This study shows that dietetic counselling remains the cornerstone of GDM management. Dietitians can provide individualized nutrition education, enabling women with GDM to optimize glycemic control throughout their pregnancy without compromising gestational weight gain and fetal growth. Although dietary behavior is influenced by multiple factors (i.e., cultural–sociodemographic conditions and the food environment), proper nutritional knowledge is still considered the main factor that shapes individuals’ dietary patterns.[32] Individualized nutrition education based on the person’s background characteristics and needs has been proven to be more effective in modifying dietary practices than other interventions.[33]

This study further shows that diet self-efficacy is a protective factor against poor glycemic control. This finding is similar to that of a Jordanian study[21] in which the participants with higher diet self-efficacy were more likely to have lower Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) values, and to the finding of another study in Malaysia[17] that self-efficacy is directly related to self-care behaviors, which subsequently affect glycemic control.

However, this study had several limitations. First, it was confined to only two districts in the state of Terengganu, where the Malay population represents a significant majority (98–99%). Due to this pronounced homogeneity in the sample population, the outcomes of this study and the insights drawn from it may not apply to other ethnic groups in Malaysia. Future studies should consider including a more diverse range of ethnic groups (Chinese, Indian, and indigenous populations) from different regions of Malaysia (urban and rural settings). Second, this study’s reliance on self-reported questionnaires (i.e., the SDSCA measure and the PPAQ) could have introduced bias. These questionnaires require respondents to recall and report their self-care activities within seven days, as well as the duration of their physical activities during the current trimester. Such reliance on memory may introduce inaccuracies or omissions in reporting, thus heightening the risk of recall bias in the study’s findings.

Conclusions

Despite this study’s limitations, it is, to our knowledge, the first to examine the relationship between sociodemographic and obstetric characteristics, self-efficacy, self-care activities, physical activity, and glycemic control among Malaysian women with GDM. Its findings highlight the significance of dietetic counselling and diet self-efficacy on glycemic control in this population. Therefore, policies ensuring access to dietetic counselling must be strengthened to achieve the desired glycemic control and, thus, to reduce the risk of mothers and offspring developing T2DM.
