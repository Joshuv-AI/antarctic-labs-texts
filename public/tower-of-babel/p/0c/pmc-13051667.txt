First reported case of Lawsonella clevelandensis brain abscess in the setting of invasive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma identified via bedside stereotactic aspiration: illustrative case

Lawsonella clevelandensis is a fastidious, partially acid-fast, gram-positive anaerobe of the suborder Corynebacterineae, first formally described as a novel species in 2016.1 Although metagenomic studies suggest it is a commensal microbe of the skin and upper respiratory tract, L. clevelandensis has been implicated in sterile site infections.2,3 Most commonly, it may be misidentified as Nocardia or Mycobacterium sp. due to its filamentous morphology and staining characteristics.1,3 Reported infections are rare and have been limited to case reports describing abscesses of the breast, spine, thorax, and abdomen, often in immunocompromised hosts.4–12 To our knowledge, there has been no previously documented instance of L. clevelandensis causing intracranial infection. We describe the first reported case of an intracranial abscess caused by L. clevelandensis, arising in the context of locally invasive, recurrent facial squamous cell carcinoma with direct extension into the anterior cranial fossa. This case underscores the diagnostic complexity of culture-negative brain abscesses in the setting of malignant skull base invasion and highlights the role of bedside stereotactic drainage and molecular diagnostics in neurosurgical infectious disease management.

Illustrative Case

A 74-year-old woman with a history of facial squamous cell carcinoma, treated with Mohs surgery in 2001, presented with a 2-week history of involuntary shaking of the left lower extremity. At presentation, she wore a mask and dark glasses that partially concealed an exophytic and purulent right facial mass. She reported progression of this mass over the past year with concomitant vision loss. She had been dressing her worsening tissue erosion herself to avoid medical care due to fear and embarrassment (Fig. 1).

Clinical photographs showing the patient’s right facial lesion (left) and left scalp involvement (right) due to invasive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, demonstrating extensive preoperative tissue erosion and ulceration.

The patient was afebrile on admission, with a maximum temperature of 37.8°C orally, but demonstrated evidence of systemic inflammation and infection, including a leukocyte count of 27.6 × 103/µL, C-reactive protein of 6.3 mg/dL, and severe anemia with a hemoglobin of 4.5 g/dL and serum iron < 10 µg/dL. Neurological examination was nonfocal apart from mild tongue deviation to the right on protrusion and nonserviceable vision in her right eye.

Initial noncontrast head CT demonstrated a multilobular right frontal mass measuring approximately 2.5 × 3.8 cm with surrounding vasogenic edema, mild right-to-left midline shift, and compression of the right lateral ventricle. The lesion showed direct invasion through the right frontotemporal calvarium and paranasal sinuses, with extensive bony erosion and mass effect extending into the right orbit (Fig. 2).

Three-dimensional CT rendering demonstrating obliteration of the right frontal bone and forehead with orbital involvement due to tumor invasion.

On hospital day 2, MRI of the brain exhibited ring-enhancing lesions within the right frontal mass, consistent with a superimposed abscess. A bedside biopsy was performed on the cutaneous lesion the same day, along with stereotactic placement of an intracavitary drain for decompression of the abscess. Drain placement was done using the Medtronic AxiEM electromagnetic StealthStation surgical navigation system. We utilized thin-slice CT for navigational sequencing and registered it to the patient with the electromagnetic flat emitter placed underneath the patient’s head and StealthStation electromagnetic noninvasive patient tracker. A plan was then created for an intracranial drain insertion site and target (center of abscess). This drain was placed at the bedside in the intensive care unit. The decision to place the drain at the bedside was based on concern for the patient’s medical instability as she was requiring pressors secondary to sepsis, and difficult airway related to significant facial tumor burden. The specimen gathered from the biopsy was submitted for microbiological workup, including anaerobic, fungal, acid-fast bacilli (AFB), and blood cultures, as well as metagenomic next-generation sequencing with quantitative polymerase chain reaction (NGS/qPCR). On the day of drain removal, NGS/qPCR identified L. clevelandensis as the causative pathogen. The patient’s antibiotic regimen, initially consisting of linezolid, meropenem, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, was narrowed to intravenous ceftriaxone and metronidazole based on known susceptibilities of L. clevelandensis. No other organisms were isolated on the remaining culture panels at this time.

On hospital day 11, follow-up MRI demonstrated enlargement of the right frontal abscess, prompting placement of a second intracavitary drain and a repeat bedside biopsy. As with the prior drain, it was removed after 4 days. No additional organisms were identified on subsequent culture panels, which included anaerobic, fungal, and AFB studies. The patient’s clinical status was stabilized, and she was eventually discharged on hospital day 28 with plans for close monitoring and a scheduled multidisciplinary skull base resection (Fig. 3).

The patient was electively readmitted for preoperative planning. She subsequently underwent an extensive anterior skull base resection involving the neurosurgery, otolaryngology, and plastic surgery teams. This operation included a right craniectomy with supratentorial resection of infected frontal lobe parenchyma, dural excision, orbital exenteration, abscess evacuation, partial rhinectomy, right maxillectomy, right neck dissection, and right parotidectomy. Lyoplant, an artificial dural substitute, was used for duroplasty. Reconstruction was performed using a free right latissimus dorsi muscle flap with microvascular anastomosis and a split-thickness skin graft harvested from the right thigh (Fig. 4).

A–C:Clinical photographs obtained after anterior skull base resection and free-flap reconstruction, demonstrating the postoperative appearance of the reconstruction. D: Axial postoperative CT image showing the extent of resection and reconstruction.

Intraoperative cultures isolated Enterococcus faecium from thioglycolate broth only. No organisms were seen on direct smear. L. clevelandensis was not recovered at this stage, suggesting microbiological resolution of the original abscess.

The patient was monitored in the neurosurgical intensive care unit and initiated on enteral nutrition via feeding tube. Postoperative management included seizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam and treatment of cerebral edema with dexamethasone. Her hospital course was complicated by the development of a left peroneal deep vein thrombosis and a right lower lobe pulmonary embolism, for which she was started on apixaban. She remained hemodynamically stable and was ultimately discharged to subacute rehabilitation approximately 3 weeks postoperatively with continued outpatient follow-up in neurosurgery, otolaryngology, plastic surgery, and oncology clinics.

She remains under the care of dermatology, oncology, and reconstructive surgery teams, with ongoing radiation and systemic therapy for multifocal cutaneous squamous and basal cell carcinomas.

Discussion

L. clevelandensis is an emerging but rare pathogen in sterile site infection in humans. The organism was first described as a novel anaerobe associated with abscess formation in 2013 under the provisional name CCF-01 by Harrington et al.,13 and was later formally classified as L. clevelandensis in 2016 following genomic characterization by Bell et al.1 While a limited number of cases have been described in the literature, mainly through case series or individual reports, the organism has been implicated in abscess formation at sites including the spine, liver, abdomen, breast, vascular grafts, and abdominal aortic aneurysms.4,6–10,12 Most cases involved immunocompromised patients or those with indwelling devices. Several presented diagnostic challenges due to filamentous morphology, partial acid-fastness, and difficulty growing under standard laboratory conditions.1,11,13-16

Observations

While prior neurosurgical infections include spinal subdural empyema in a pediatric patient with a dermoid cyst as presented by Kumaria et al.,6 our case represents the first documented case of intracranial abscess caused by L. clevelandensis. Its potential misidentification as Nocardia or Mycobacterium sp. may lead to delays in appropriate antimicrobial therapy.1,3,13 In this case, the combination of bedside electromagnetic stereotactic aspiration with intracavitary drain placement and NGS/qPCR enabled timely diagnosis and narrowed treatment selection, ultimately stabilizing the patient.

Neurosurgical intervention via bedside stereotactic biopsy and drainage was critical. To our knowledge, this illustrates a rarely described stereotactic drainage of an intracerebral abscess. Using Medtronic Stealth navigation, we achieved a minimally invasive and safe alternative to open operative intervention. This bedside approach has been shown to offer advantages in medically complex or high-risk patients.17 In the present case, this approach minimized anesthesia exposure and operative risk for a medically complex patient with active intracranial infection. Additionally, it enabled rapid acquisition of tissue for pathological and microbiological analysis to guide targeted antibiotic therapy during definitive treatment planning.

The patient’s extensive tumor burden, involving the face and anterior skull base, necessitated complex multidisciplinary coordination with otolaryngology and plastic surgery for definitive oncological resection and free-flap reconstruction. Treating the active infection before proceeding to major skull base surgery was essential to ensure microvascular graft viability and optimize wound healing. The intracavitary drain was maintained for several days to facilitate continued decompression, and when interval MRI on hospital day 11 demonstrated abscess progression, repeat bedside drainage was readily performed. This underscores the versatility and reproducibility of bedside stereotactic aspiration in managing evolving intracranial infections in high-risk patients.

Although empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics remain standard for culture-negative abscesses, molecular diagnostic methods such as metagenomic sequencing enhance detection, particularly for fastidious organisms like L. clevelandensis.14,18,19 This case also illustrates the benefit of repeat imaging and drainage when clinical or radiographic progression occurs, consistent with current infectious disease and neurosurgical guidelines.20,21 The patient’s condition, involving recurrent invasive facial squamous cell carcinoma with anterior cranial fossa extension, created a direct pathway for intracranial spread of infection. Definitive source control required coordinated resection and reconstruction by neurosurgery, otolaryngology, and plastic surgery. This multidisciplinary approach addressed both the abscess and the underlying malignancy but did not eliminate the need for ongoing oncological management.22,23

Empiric therapy was narrowed following NGS/qPCR identification of L. clevelandensis, consistent with prior reports of susceptibility to β-lactam antibiotics, ceftriaxone, and metronidazole.4,10,12,14 Because L. clevelandensis is a fastidious anaerobe sometimes found on skin, contamination was considered; however, several features supported its role as the true pathogen. The organism was identified by NGS/qPCR from a deep intracranial abscess aspirate obtained under stereotactic guidance, with no other organisms detected on initial sampling. Clinical and radiographic improvement following targeted antimicrobial therapy further supported pathogenicity.

On the day of surgery, E. faecium was isolated from deep operative cultures and was managed as a secondary surgical site infection. Antimicrobial coverage was adjusted accordingly, and the patient stabilized without further signs of intracranial infectious progression.

Lessons

This case is, to our knowledge, the first reported intracranial L. clevelandensis abscess. It underscores that culture-negative abscesses in the setting of skull base invasion may reflect fastidious organisms and that molecular sequencing can provide a definitive diagnosis when routine cultures fail. The patient’s course also highlights that image-guided bedside stereotactic biopsy and drainage can serve as a safe, minimally invasive option for urgent diagnosis and decompression in medically complex patients. Finally, definitive source control in the setting of tumor-associated abscess required a coordinated multidisciplinary skull base resection involving neurosurgery, otolaryngology, and plastic surgery. As a single-patient report, these findings are necessarily limited, but they remain relevant for illustrating diagnostic and management strategies in complex neurosurgical infections.
