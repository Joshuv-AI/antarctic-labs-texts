Antimicrobial hydrogel loaded with broccoli exosomes promotes anti-scarring healing of MRSA-infected wounds
Authors: Xuzhou Duan, Jie Li, Runze Gao, Yongchao Zhou, Renzhi Chen, Yan Shang, Hongrui Wang, Jia Chen, Shuogui Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment of methicillin-resistant staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)-infected wounds is severely challenged by antibiotic resistance and persistent inflammation. In this study, a photocrosslinked composite hydrogel material (SK@E-Au@Exos) was designed using silk protein methacryloyl hydrogel (SK) as a carrier loaded with broccoli-derived exosomes (Bro-Exos) and epigallocatechin gallate-gold nanoparticles (E-Au NPs). The material promoted scarless wound repair through synergistic anti-infection combined with immune regeneration modulation. In vitro experiments showed that SK@E-Au@Exos has excellent biocompatibility and can rapidly kill MRSA and E.coli, remove Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), inhibit inflammation, and promote cell proliferation, migration, and vascularization. Importantly, SK@E-Au@Exos can regulate the immune regenerative microenvironment by inhibiting the Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF-kB) pathway and driving the macrophage to an anti-inflammatory phenotype. In a mouse MRSA-infected wound model, SK@E-Au@Exos resisted infection, scavenged ROS, inhibited inflammation, and promoted collagen ordering and neovascularization, ultimately realizing scarless healing of the infected wound. This study demonstrated the ability of SK@E-Au@Exos to promote the temporal regulation of “antibacterial-anti-inflammatory-regenerative” in wounds and revealed the molecular mechanism of SK@E-Au@Exos in regulating the immune microenvironment through key signaling pathways. In summary, SK@E-Au@Exos effectively promotes anti-infection and anti-scar healing of wounds infected with multidrug-resistant bacteria through its photothermal sterilization effect and exosome-controlled release of anti-inflammatory and repair-promoting substances. This provides an innovative strategy for the clinical treatment of wounds infected with drug-resistant bacteria, combining antibacterial and regenerative functions.Keywords: Broccoli exosomes, EGCG-Metal nanoparticles, MRSA, Macrophages, Anti-scar healing

Introduction

The treatment of MRSA-infected wounds faces a dual dilemma: failure of pathogen clearance due to antibiotic resistance and impaired tissue regeneration triggered by persistent inflammation [1]. Conventional therapies, such as topical antimicrobials combined with growth factors, they can control the infection in the short term, but making it difficult to maintain the dynamic balance of the macrophage-dominated immune microenvironment, leading to excessive scar tissue formation or secondary infection [2,3].

Wound healing encompasses four sequential phases: coagulation, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [4]. Within this process, macrophage phenotypic polarization (M1-to-M2 transition) during the inflammatory phase serves as a central regulatory mechanism for wound healing [5,6]. Excessive ROS release during inflammatory induces mitochondrial damage, promoting oxidative stress and sustaining the M1 phenotype. Conversely, overactivated M1-type macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6, exacerbating tissue damage; however, a well-timed switch to the M2 phenotype can initiate regenerative programs through the secretion of anti-inflammatory factors such as TGF-β and IL-10 [7,8]. Nevertheless, under conditions of persistent inflammation due to drug-resistant bacterial infections, it remains a key challenge to precisely regulate macrophage polarization and achieve stage-specific functional output [1].

In recent years, exosomes have emerged as promising therapeutic agents for modulating wound healing owing to their inherentl transcellular signaling capabilitiesy [9]. However, animal-derived exosomes carry risks of pathogen contamination, high production costs, and immune rejection [10,11]. In contrast, plant-derived exosomes offer a novel alternative due to their low immunogenicity, potential for large-scale production, and cross-species modulation ability [12,13]. Studies have shown that plant exosomes, such as ginger and grape, can regulate the inflammatory and reparative pathways in mammalian cells through the delivery of plant-specific miRNAs or metabolites [14,15], but the mechanism of their coordinated “anti-inflammatory-regenerative” temporal regulation in MRSA-infected wounds has not yet been clarified [16]. In contrast, Bro-Exos is an ideal candidate for tissue repair due to its natural safety profile, anti-inflammatory components, pro-repair molecules, and other inclusions [17,18].

Polyphenolic compounds (e.g., EGCG) can form stable nanoparticles with metal ions by one-step self-assembly method by virtue of their multiple coordination ability [19]. Studies demonstrate that EGCG-metal nanoparticles exert bactericidal effects by disrupting bacterial membranes and exhibit efficacy against MRSA and biofilm formation [19,20]. Silk protein methacryloyl hydrogel is a three-dimensional network structure formed from natural filament proteins by methacrylation modification, which possesses excellent biocompatibility, modifiable mechanical properties, and photocrosslinking properties. The photocrosslinking capability of SK enables precise modulation of gel pore architecture and drug release kinetics, thereby offering a spatiotemporally controlled platform for targeted wound therapy [21,22].

In this study, an integrated therapeutic platform (denoted as SK@E-Au@Exos) was developed to synergize active antimicrobial functions with spatiotemporal immune modulation by combining Bro-Exos, E-Au NPs, and SK. E-Au NPs formed stable nanocomplexes by one-step self-assembly through chelation of phenolic hydroxyl groups of EGCG with Au3+ ions. Bro-Exos were extracted by differential ultracentrifugation and verified by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and Western blot (WB) for their typical cup-shaped vesicle structure and exosome marker proteins (CD63, CD9, TSG101) high expression. mRNA sequencing and functional assays demonstrated that SK@E-Au@Exos promotes M1-to-M2 macrophage polarization through dual mechanisms: suppression of NF-κB-mediated pro-inflammatory signaling and upregulation of regenerative factors such as TGF-β and IL-10. In a mouse model of MRSA-infected wounds, SK@E-Au@Exos demonstrated a clear chronological repair profile: no obvious infectious features on the wound surface on day 3; control of inflammation through inhibition of TNF-α (↓59.221 %) and M1 macrophages within 7 days; and scarless healing of wounds through promotion of vascular maturation (Pearson's coefficient increased by 29.885 %), orderly collagen deposition (CVF 53.404 %), and follicle neogenesis from day 7–14.

Results

Extraction, Characterization, and uptake of Bro-Exos

Exosomes were successfully extracted from broccoli by differential ultracentrifugation, and TEM showed their typical cup-shaped vesicle structure with a diameter of about 120 nm or so (Fig. 1A). Nanoparticle size analysis (NTA) showed a concentration of 7.7 × 109 particles/ml with a particle size of about 120 nm or so (Fig. 1B). WB verified its high expression of CD63, CD9, and TSG101 exosome marker proteins (Fig. 1C). The above results were consistent with the results of previous exosome reports [23]. In addition, PKH26-labeled Bro-Exos was efficiently endocytosed by macrophages and L929 cells, confirming that it is expected to play a corresponding biological role in wound healing (Fig. 1D). Bro-Exos was loaded into SK@E-Au hydrogel (Fig. S1), and field emission scanning electron microscopy directly observed that the exosome was encapsulated in the hydrogel, which was still round in shape, and there was no obvious change in the diameter (Fig. 1E). Fluorescence microscopy also revealed that the PKH26-labeled exosomes were uniformly distributed inside, confirming that the exosomes were successfully loaded into the hydrogel (Fig. 1E). Subsequently, the release concentration of exosomes was detected by hydrogel degradation assay, and it was found that exosomes could be released slowly and lasted for more than 7 days (Fig. 1G, Fig. S2).

Preparation and Characterization of hydrogels

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) showed (Fig. 2A) that the -OH absorption peak in E-Au nanoparticles shifted from 3410 cm−1 to 3413 cm−1 and the carbonyl (C=O) absorption peak shifted from 1618 cm−1 to 1616 cm−1 as compared to that of EGCG, and these changes suggest that carboxyl and hydroxyl groups may play a role in the synthesis and stabilization of E-Au nanoparticles. TEM images showed that the E-Au nanoparticles were homogeneous and spherical, and the Au and O elements were well distributed with an average size of around tens of nanometers (Fig. 2B). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed the size distribution of E-Au nanoparticles in deionized water, indicating that the E-Au nanoparticles were well dispersed and had a particle size of about 68 nm (Fig. 2C). The rheological results (Fig. 2D) showed that the energy storage modulus and loss modulus of the hydrogel increased with the increase of the oscillation frequency, and the addition of E-Au nanoparticles made an important contribution to the improvement of the mechanical stability of the hydrogel. The photothermal properties of the hydrogels were investigated by continuously monitoring the temperature fluctuations upon exposure to 808 nm NIR laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. 2E, within 5 min of laser irradiation, the corresponding temperature change of SK hydrogel is almost the same as that of water, which only rises by about 3 °C and does not show any photothermal properties. In contrast, SK@E-Au and SK@E-Au@Exos showed an obvious temperature increase under laser irradiation, generating a certain amount of heat, and the temperature change was able to reach about 33 °C. The gelation behavior of hydrogels was observed by the vial gelation method. As shown in Fig. 2F, the hydrogel precursor liquid has good fluidity before UV illumination; at this time the liquid surface does not change with the vial tilt, keeping parallel to the horizontal plane. After a certain time of UV illumination, all can form stable hydrogel quickly. The microscopic morphology of the hydrogels was furthermore observed by SEM, and the results showed (Fig. 2G) that all groups of hydrogels possessed a connected pore structure, which facilitated cell growth.

(D) Oscillation frequency scanning mode rheology analysis of hydrogel. (E) Corresponding temperature change of the hydrogel within 5 min of laser irradiation. (F) Gel formation diagram. Notably, some particles were observed to be uniformly dispersed in the pores in both the SK@E-Au and SK@E-Au@Exos groups.

Furthermore long-term physiological testing of SK@E-Au@Exos showed that the hydrogel exhibited good stability in different environments and could meet the requirements for use under physiological conditions (Fig. S3) [24]. Concurrently, Zeta potential measurements (Ph = 7.4) indicated a surface charge of −23.63 ± 5.41 Mv (Fig. S4). This negative charge state significantly suppresses particle aggregation through electrostatic repulsion effects, with its absolute value approaching the critical threshold for colloidal stability (|ζ| > 30 mV), signifying a metastable dispersion state [25]. These dual validation mechanisms synergize to ensure long-term functional reliability: surface charge repulsion guarantees initial dispersion homogeneity, while the elastic network resists shear-induced deformation [24,25].

Antimicrobial capacity and biocompatibility of SK@E-Au@Exos

Antimicrobial capacity of SK@E-Au@Exos

The antimicrobial capacity of the hydrogel was evaluated using Escherichia coli (E. coli, Gram-negative bacteria) and MRSA (Gram-positive bacteria). The results of the coated plate method showed (Fig. 3A and. C) that the SK group had almost no antimicrobial effect and the number of colonies was similar to that of the control group as compared to the control group, whereas the SK@E-Au and SK@E-Au@Exos groups had a significant antimicrobial effect and the number of colonies was significantly reduced. The absorbance at 600 nm after co-cultivation of different groups was furthermore determined using an enzyme marker, and the corresponding antimicrobial rate was calculated by the formula. The results showed (Fig. 3B and. D) that the antimicrobial rates of SK against E. coli and MRSA were 1.85 % and 1.37 %, respectively; the antimicrobial rates of SK@E-Au against E. coli and MRSA were 84.36 % and 86.65 %, respectively; and the antimicrobial rates of SK@E-Au@Exo against E. coli and MRSA were 94.57 % and 94.08 %, respectively. This is due to the fact that NIR excites the photothermal effect of the metal polyphenol (EGCG-Au) nanoparticles loaded inside the hydrogel, which warms up and kills the bacteria [19]. It is worth noting that the additional addition of exosomes not only does not affect the antimicrobial effect of the nanoparticles, but also has a positive effect.

Biocompatibility of SK@E-Au@Exos

The groups of hydrogels were co-cultured with cells and then stained for live and dead cells. It was found (Fig. 3E–F) that the hydrogels had no significant effect on the value-added trend of the cells, and the number of dead cells was not high. This indicates that both groups of hydrogels have excellent cytocompatibility. Notably, SK@E-Au showed no cytotoxicity and no cell proliferation activity, whereas the SK@E-Au@Exos group exhibited a higher cell value-added trend, suggesting that the loading of Bro-Exos significantly increased the biological efficacy of the material. To evaluate the biocompatibility of hydrogels under inflammatory conditions, Live/Dead staining assays were performed following co-culture of the respective material groups with RAW 264.7 cells. The results revealed no cytotoxicity for either SK@E-Au or SK@E-Au@Exos, confirming their remarkable biocompatibility under inflammatory conditions (Fig. S5). In addition, to furthermore verify the long-term safety in vivo, SK@E-Au@Exos was injected subcutaneously into rats for in vivo experiments, and HE staining was performed after 3 months (Fig. 3G), which revealed that the key organs such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were intact, and no abnormal lesions were detected in the rats.

SK@E-Au@Exos promotes cell proliferation, migration, and tube formation

The proliferative properties of the cells were tested by in vitro EdU assay. The results showed (Fig. 4A–D) that SK@E-Au@Exos significantly promoted the proliferation of HUVEC and L929 cells after 24 h of co-incubation, with a 1.243-fold increase in the proliferation rate of HUVEC and a 1.204-fold increase in the proliferation rate of L929. The migration ability of the cells was subsequently tested by scratch assay. The results showed (Fig. 4E–H) that SK@E-Au@Exos significantly promoted the migration of HUVEC and L929, and after 48 h of co-incubation, the migration rate of HUVEC was elevated by 1.834-fold, and the migration rate of L929 was elevated by 1.605-fold. In addition, the results of in vitro vascularization experiments showed (Fig. 4I–K) that after 6 h of co-culture, vascular endothelial cells' tubular structure was formed, the number of branches was enhanced by 1.112-fold, and the total branch length was enhanced by 1.172-fold. The above results suggest that SK@E-Au@Exos is expected to promote re-epithelialization and vascularization in injured tissues.

SK@E-Au@Exos promotes ROS clearance and reverses inflammation

The ROS scavenging and reversal of inflammation ability of SK@E-Au@Exos was investigated by immunofluorescence and ELISA experiments. The results showed (Fig. 5A–B) that a large amount of ROS green fluorescence was visible in the H2O2 group, confirming the successful induction of the macrophage oxidative stress model in vitro, whereas the ROS fluorescence was weakened by SK@E-Au and SK@E-Au@Exos treatments, with a high ROS scavenging efficiency of 42.811 % in the SK@E-Au@Exos group. In addition (Fig. 5C–E), CD86 green fluorescence was significantly enhanced after treatment of M0 macrophages by LPS, confirming the success of M1 macrophage induction. In contrast, after SK@E-Au@Exos treatment, CD86 green fluorescence was significantly reduced and CD206 red fluorescence was significantly enhanced, in which CD206 fluorescence intensity was elevated by 2.823-fold and CD86 fluorescence intensity was reduced by 64.486 %. Subsequently, the concentration of cell supernatant-associated inflammatory factors was detected by ELISA. The results showed that SK@E-Au@Exos treatment of M1 macrophages resulted in increased IL6 concentration and decreased IL10 concentration (Fig. 5F–G, Fig. S2). The above results indicate that SK@E-Au@Exos has the biological efficacy of scavenging ROS and reversing inflammation by regulating macrophages, suggesting that it has the potential to synergistically promote tissue regeneration through immune microenvironment regulation.

SK@E-Au@Exos scavenges ROS and reverses inflammation. (A) ROS staining and (B) fluorescence intensity statistics. (C) Immunofluorescence co-staining of Raw264.7 cells. (D) Fluorescence intensity statistics of CD86 and (E) CD206. (F) ELISA for IL6 and (G) IL10.

SK@E-Au@Exos reprogrammed macrophages

The mechanism by which SK@E-Au@Exos regulates the immune microenvironment by reprogramming macrophages was furthermore investigated. mRNA-seq transcriptomics was used to study the anti-inflammatory properties of SK@E-Au@Exos. The results of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed (Fig. 6A) that a total of 7492 differentially expressed genes were identified in macrophages from the LPS and SK@E-Au@Exos groups (FDR <0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1). Heat map analysis showed (Fig. 6B) that the differential genes were mainly enriched in anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative stress, cell proliferation and migration, etc. GO functional enrichment analysis showed (Fig. 6C) that the differential genes were mainly involved in biological processes such as “inflammatory response,” “response to oxidative stress,” and “positive regulation of cell cycle” (FDR <0.05), suggesting that Bro-Exos inhibits inflammation and promotes repair. KEGG pathway analysis focused on the NF-κB signaling pathway (P = 8.9240E-11), a key pathway of inflammatory response (Fig. 6D). SK@E-Au@Exos significantly inhibited the expression of key effector molecules of the NF-κB pathway, P65 and P50, as verified by Western blot [26]. This is consistent with the in vitro macrophage phenotype rescue assay. SK@E-Au@Exos was confirmed to reprogram macrophages and drive M2 polarization by inhibiting pathways such as NF-κB (Fig. 6E). This provides a mechanistic basis for plant exosomes to regulate the immune microenvironment.

SK@E-Au@Exos promotes healing of MRSA-infected wounds in Mice

SK@E-Au@Exos demonstrated excellent antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and pro-repair properties in in vitro experiments, and its therapeutic effect was furthermore verified by a mouse MRSA-infected wound model. Fig. 7A demonstrates the healing timeline of MRSA-infected wound model establishment, treatment, photography, and sampling. Compared with the control group (Fig. 7B), obvious signs of ulceration were visible in the wounds of the infection group on the third day, confirming the successful establishment of the infected wound model. And after treatment with SK@E-Au and SK@E-Au@Exos, no obvious ulceration and pus formation were seen on the wounds, confirming its excellent anti-infection properties. Compared with the infection group (Fig. 7B–C), the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group demonstrated a significant pro-healing effect. Macroscopic healing wound area thermograms and wound healing rate statistics showed (Fig. 7D–E) that the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group showed accelerated wound contraction on day 7 (healing rate 76.892 % ± 1.696 %, vs. 37.581 % ± 0.751 % in the Infection group, P < 0.0001), and essentially complete closure on day 14 (healing rate 99.290 % ± 0.710 %, vs. 89.239 % ± 1.151 % in the Infection group). In addition, the Inf-SK@E-Au group also healed essentially completely on day 14, which was mainly dependent on the hydrogel's early anti-infection, but significant scar formation could be observed on its healed skin surface. Notably, the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group had no obvious scar formation, and a large number of new hairs were visible. This suggests that SK@E-Au@Exos is able to promote high-quality wound healing while achieving early antimicrobial activity.

In vivo SK@E-Au@Exo scavenges ROS and reverses inflammation

The expression of ROS, macrophage phenotype, and inflammatory factors in the microenvironment of MRSA-infected wounds was analyzed by immunofluorescence staining, and it was found that the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group significantly scavenges a large amount of ROS and promotes the polarization of M2-type macrophages to inhibit excessive inflammatory responses. ROS staining on day 3 showed (Fig. 9A–B) that a large amount of ROS red fluorescence was visible in the infection group, while only a small amount of fluorescence was expressed in the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group, which had a high ROS clearance rate of 51.418 %. Double-labeled co-staining of CD206 (M2-labeled)/CD86 (M1-labeled) on day 7 showed (Fig. 9C–E) that CD206 red fluorescence was significantly intensified and CD86 green fluorescence was significantly down-regulated in the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group compared with the Infection group, and that the M2/M1 ratio was elevated from 0.454 ± 0.215 to 1.825 ± 0.608 (P < 0.0001). Immunofluorescence single-label staining showed (Fig. 9G–J) that the mean fluorescence intensity of TNF-α in the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group was decreased by 59.221 % (infection group: 43.899 % ± 1.517 %, Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group: 17.902 % ± 0.901 %, P < 0.0001), whereas TGF-β expression increased 2.408-fold (infection group: 20.833 % ± 1.602 %, Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group: 50.175 % ± 0.92 %, P < 0.0001). The above results are consistent with the previous cellular experiments and mRNA seq data analysis, indicating that SK@E-Au@Exo can remodel the immune microenvironment by clearing ROS, reprogramming macrophages, inhibiting pro-inflammatory factors, and enhancing anti-inflammatory repair signals, thus realizing the “antibacterial-anti-inflammatory-regenerative” time-sequential regulation of wounds.

In vivo SK@E-Au@Exo promotes Re-epithelialization, vascularization

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence analyses furthermore revealed the role of SK@E-Au@Exos in the regulation of the wound regeneration microenvironment. KI67 immunohistochemistry on day 7 showed (Fig. 10A–B) that the proliferative activity of cells in the basal layer of the epidermis at the margins of the wound was significantly enhanced in the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group, with a 2.095-fold increase in the rate of positive cells compared with that of the infected group (33.528 % ± 0.694 % vs. 16.003 % ± 1.313 %, P < 0.0001). The results of the CD31/αSMA immunofluorescence co-staining table on day 14 showed that the average fluorescence intensity of CD31 increased 1.246-fold and the average fluorescence intensity of αSMA was elevated 1.402-fold in the Inf-SK@E-Au@Exos group compared with the infection group (Fig. 10C–D). In addition, the Pearson co-localization coefficient (PCC) was significantly lower in the infected group than in the control group(0.363 ± 0.008 vs 0.408 ± 0.008,P < 0.001), as shown by co-localization analysis (Fig. 10E), suggesting that infection leads to a decrease in the stability of vascular endothelial-pericyte binding [27]. However, PCC after SK@E-Au@Exos treatment was significantly higher than that in the Infection group (0.471 ± 0.006 vs. 0.3625 ± 0.008, ↑29.885 %) and Control group (0.471 ± 0.006 vs. 0.408 ± 0.008, ↑15.306 %). The above results confirmed that SK@E-Au@Exos could promote rapid re-epithelialization of wounds, reverse the destruction of vascular structure caused by MRSA infection, and improve vascular maturation, thus achieving the effect of promoting anti-scarring healing of infected wounds.

Discussion

In this study, the integration of Bro-Exos, E-Au NPs, and the SK delivery system revealed the unique advantages of SK@E-Au@Exos in the temporal regulation of “antibacterial-anti-inflammatory-regenerative” in MRSA-infected wounds through anti-infective, ROS scavenging, and inflammatory-regenerative microenvironment modulation. Although the role of regulating macrophage phenotypic in wound repair has been widely recognized, the precise coordination of inflammation reduction and tissue regeneration in the persistent inflammatory microenvironment caused by drug-resistant bacterial infections remains a key challenge [28].

In this study, SK@E-Au@Exos significantly resisted infection at 3 days, inhibited M1-type macrophage polarization, and upregulated the anti-inflammatory factor TGF-β (↑2.408-fold) at 7 days, significantly alleviated the inflammatory response, and accelerated the re-epithelialization of wounds by promoting the proliferation of cells in the basal layer of the epidermis (KI67 positivity rate ↑2.095-fold). This synergistic effect of anti-infection, anti-inflammation, and proliferation in the early stage lays the foundation for subsequent high-quality healing [28,29]. Notably, SK@E-Au@Exos furthermore demonstrated long-term efficacy in terms of pro-angiogenesis (CD31/αSMA fluorescence intensity ↑1.246–1.402-fold), collagen deposition (CVF 53.404 % vs. 27.499 % in the infection group), and scarring inhibition (keloid index 1.161 vs. 6.328), and even induced the formation of neonatal hair follicles, suggesting a clear temporal direction of its restorative effect. This “anti-inflammatory and then regenerative” regulatory logic contrasts with the limitations of simple antimicrobial hydrogels (Inf-SK@E-Au group), which can control the infection at an early stage but lack the dynamic regulation of the regenerative microenvironment, leading to slow healing and scar formation. The molecular mechanism of this temporal regulation may involve dynamic intervention of the active ingredient in Bro-Exos on macrophage signaling pathways [30].

Although the specific functional molecules of Bro-Exos were not directly identified in this study, it has been shown in the literature that plant exosomes are typically enriched in miRNAs, lipids, and plant-specific metabolites [12,31,32], and that these components can modulate inflammatory and reparative pathways in mammalian cells through cross-species delivery [10]. In the present study, it was observed that SK@E-Au@Exos significantly inhibited the NF-κB pathway to attenuate inflammation, suggesting that it may drive M2 macrophage polarization through a similar mechanism [26]. This finding is consistent with that of animal-derived exosomes [33], but the low immunogenicity and easy-to-scale production properties of plant exosomes provide unique advantages for clinical translation [31]. Future studies could combine metabolomics and single-cell sequencing technologies to systematically identify key active molecules in Bro-Exos and develop ROS/pH-responsive smart hydrogels in order to realize the spatiotemporal controlled release of exosomes in the infected microenvironment[[34], [35], [36]]. In addition, the molecular mechanisms of plant exosome interactions with mammalian cell receptors still need to be explored in depth, such as whether Bro-Exos fuses with macrophage membranes via surface proteins or relies on endocytosis pathways to deliver functional payloads [37,38]. Bro-Exos may directly mediate the regulation of mammalian cells through synergistic actions of their plant-specific components (e.g., small RNAs and phosphatidic acid lipids). Specifically, their small RNA fragments could potentially target human genes via sequence homology, surface lipids may activate innate immune receptors, and secondary metabolites might be converted into bioactive molecules triggering antioxidant pathways. Unlike animal exosomes that primarily rely on homologous protein-mediated delivery, Bro-Exos could achieve more efficient cross-species signaling and metabolic reprogramming by leveraging these unique constituents[[39], [40], [41], [42]]. This suggests the existence of distinct delivery mechanisms in Bro-Exos that differ from those employed by animal exosomes.

In conclusion, this study reveals that SK@E-Au@Exos achieves time-sequential anti-scarring repair of wounds through anti-infective and immune-regenerative microenvironment modulation. This not only provides a therapeutic strategy with both antimicrobial and pro-healing functions for infected wounds with drug-resistant bacteria but also lays a theoretical foundation for the cross-species application of plant exosomes. In the future, we will continue to conduct large-animal experiments to further validate its clinical application potential and systematically elucidate the mechanism of action of broccoli exosomes.
