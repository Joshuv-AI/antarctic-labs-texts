Efficacy and Safety of Telitacicept in Posttransplant Recurrent IgA Nephropathy
Authors: Mingxing Sui, Daqian Tang, Junhao Yu, Yanfeng Li, Lei Zhang, Li Zeng, Wenyu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRecurrent Ig A nephropathy (IgAN; rIgAN) after kidney transplantation is heterogeneous and lacks a guideline or a standard treatment. We evaluated the efficacy and safety of telitacicept as an add-on therapy for rIgAN with persistent proteinuria in kidney recipients.MethodsIn this prospective, single-center, single-arm cohort study, 25 kidney transplant recipients with biopsy-confirmed rIgAN received add-on telitacicept 240 mg subcutaneously weekly for 12 weeks, then every 2 weeks until week 28. Primary end points were 24-hour urinary protein (24h-UP) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR). Secondary assessments included remission, kidney function, laboratory parameters, and mechanistic biomarkers.ResultsTwenty-two participants completed the follow-up. At week 28, 24h-UP and UACR decreased by 51.6% and 62.7% (both P < 0.001), respectively. Complete and partial remission rates were achieved in 47.6% and 57.1% of patients, respectively. Serum albumin increased significantly, whereas serum creatinine (SCr), estimated glomerular filtration rate, and hemoglobin remained stable. Circulating galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1) and asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR) levels declined; Ig levels also decreased, in line with B-cell inhibition and on-target pathways suppression. No treatment-related severe adverse events were reported. The most common adverse events were mild injection site reactions and transient infections.ConclusionAdd-on telitacicept was associated with marked proteinuria reduction and a favorable safety profile in kidney transplant recipients with rIgAN and persistent proteinuria, supporting its potential as a therapeutic option.Keywords: B-cell targeted therapy, galactose-deficient IgA1, kidney transplantation, proteinuria, recurrent IgA nephropathy, telitacicept

IgAN, a highly heterogeneous immune-inflammatory disease prevalent among the East Asian population, could lead to end-stage kidney disease and further kidney transplantation despite advances in treatment.1 rIgAN after transplant is time-dependently increased in its prevalence, reaching ≤15% a decade, even though the recipients were under immunosuppressive therapy.2,3 It is defined by mesangial IgA deposits with a minority of cases exhibiting crescents, presenting with persistent proteinuria and elevated SCr.4,5

Currently, no guideline, expert consensus, or large-scale cohort study has addressed the management of rIgAN because of its even higher heterogeneity compared with primary IgAN. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes Guideline recommends only supportive care with a focus on proteinuria reduction and blood pressure control.6 Although intensified immunosuppression, including mycophenolate and tacrolimus, may offer a protective effect in primary IgAN,7 relapse of IgAN in kidney recipients could not be prevented even though immunosuppressive therapy is routinely administered, indicating the more complex and heterogeneous pathology of the rIgAN. Rituximab was reported to be effective in a few case reports, but failed to reduce Gd-IgA1 levels or exert an antiproteinuric effect despite depleting B cells in a randomized controlled trial.8 Consequently, the graft function in rIgAN could continue to deteriorate under immunosuppression and the supportive care, lacking effective and targeted therapy.

The pathogenesis of rIgAN is complicated and rarely reported, but it could be possibly driven by the formation of IgA immune complexes, which is similar to primary IgAN. It is well-documented that the IgAN process involves elevated serum levels of Gd-IgA1 and the production of autoantibodies against Gd-IgA1.9 Key factors in this immunopathology are B-cell lymphocyte stimulator and A proliferation-inducing ligand, which are critical for B-cell activation and the production of pathogenic antibodies.10

Telitacicept, a novel recombinant fusion B-cell lymphocyte stimulator or A proliferation-inducing ligand inhibitor, has demonstrated efficacy in treating lupus nephritis and primary IgAN.11, 12, 13 Given the pathogenic similarities and previous clinical data, telitacicept might be a potential therapeutic option for the challenging rIgAN. We conducted this prospective single-arm cohort study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of telitacicept as an add-on therapy in kidney-transplant recipients with biopsy-proven rIgAN.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This single-center, prospective, single-arm clinical study was conducted in the Shanghai Changhai Hospital, China, from October 2024 to April 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Changhai Hospital (CHEC2028-081) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study-specific procedure.

All patients received standard triple immunosuppressive therapy including tacrolimus, mycophenolate, and corticosteroids after kidney transplantation and throughout the study period.

Key inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) aged ≥ 18 years, (ii) biopsy-confirmed rIgAN with Lee-Hass classification and Oxford MEST-C scoring, (iii) persistent proteinuria (defined as urine protein qualitatively positive for 3 consecutive months, with 24-hour urinary protein > 500 mg/d at baseline) or elevated SCr despite immunosuppressive therapy and other interventions (e.g. targeted-release formulation of budesonide (TRF-B), rituximab), and (iv) willingness and ability to attend all study follow-up visits. Patients who met the above criteria and received add-on telitacicept were enrolled.

Key exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) concurrent use of medications that may interfere with efficacy evaluation (e.g., traditional Chinese medicines such as Kunxian Capsules and daratumumab) during the study period, (ii) poor compliance and incomplete follow-up, and (iii) treatment-related severe adverse events.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, time since transplantation, and immunosuppressive regimens, were systematically collected for all participants. Background supportive therapies, particularly the use of angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB), angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i), endothelin receptor antagonists (ERA), and TRF-B, was recorded at enrollment.

Treatment Regimen and Follow-Up

Telitacicept 240 mg was administered subcutaneously once a week during weeks 1 to 12, then every 2 weeks during weeks 13 to 28 weeks. Patients were followed-up with at baseline and at weeks 4, 12, and 28 after the first dose. All baseline therapies, including immunosuppressive agents and supportive care medications (e.g., ARB, ACEi, SGLT-2i, ERA and TRF-B) were maintained stable for ≥4 weeks before the first dose of telitacicept and during the study period.

Outcomes and Definitions

The primary outcomes were the change from baseline in 24h-UP and the UACR at each visit point. Secondary outcomes included the following: SCr and estimated glomerular filtration rate calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation; serum albumin; hemoglobin; complete proteinuria remission rate (24h-UP < 300 mg/d or UACR < 30 mg/g); partial remission rate (> 50% reduction in 24h-UP from baseline with an absolute value < 1000 mg/d); changes in serum Igs (IgG, IgA, and IgM); changes in IgAN-related biomarkers, including Gd-IgA1, soluble transferrin receptor, ASGPR, and soluble IgA-Fc fragment receptor (sCD89); the 4 molecular testing kits were provided by CusaBio, Wuhan, China.

Safety Assessments

All adverse events and clinically relevant laboratory abnormalities were recorded to evaluate the safety of telitacicept as an add-on therapy.

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean and SD, whereas nonnormally distributed variables were summarized as median and interquartile range. A 2-tailed P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Mixed model repeated measures (MMRM) was employed to analyze longitudinal changes in clinical parameters, because of its ability to handle missing data efficiently while requiring only the missing at random assumption rather than the more restrictive missing completely at random assumption. In the model, clinical parameters were set as dependent variables, with follow-up time points (baseline, 4, 12, and 28 weeks) specified as fixed effects and baseline values included as covariates. To model the covariance structure of the repeated measurements, a compound symmetry structure was selected. This choice was made after comparing several candidate structures (including first-order autoregressive and Toeplitz) based on the Schwarz's Bayesian Criterion, with the compound symmetry structure demonstrating the best fit for the data.

Estimated marginal means (EMM) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each time point to assess temporal trends in clinical parameters. Pairwise comparisons between each follow-up time point and baseline were performed with Sidak adjustment for multiple comparisons. Type III tests for fixed effects were conducted to evaluate the overall effect of time on clinical parameters. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and figures prepared with GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Changes during Follow-Up

A total of 25 recipients with biopsy-proven rIgAN were enrolled. Three patients were lost to follow-up, and 22 completed the 28-week study and were included in the final analysis. The baseline demographic and pathological characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 1. The mean age at enrollment was 44.91 (1.72) years, and the mean time since kidney transplantation was 81.36 (5.88) months. Among the 22 participants, 10 (45.45%) were on ARB therapy at enrollment and remained on stable doses throughout follow-up; none were receiving ACEi, ERA, or SGLT-2i at enrollment or during the study period. Five patients (22.73%) had a history of TRF-B use before study entry; however, this was discontinued upon initiation of telitacicept.

Proteinuria and Remission Rates

MMRM analysis demonstrated a significant time-dependent reduction in 24h-UP over the study period (P < 0.001). The EMM showed a gradual decrease in urinary protein from 1648 mg at baseline (95% CI: 1396–1901) to 798 mg at week 28 (95% CI: 545–1050), representing a 51.62% reduction (Figure 1a). Following Sidak correction, all follow-up time point reductions remained statistically significant (all P < 0.01), with mean reductions of 614 mg/d at week 4, 593 mg/d at week 12, and 851 mg/d at week 28.

Changes in proteinuria during follow-up. Longitudinal changes in (a) 24h-UP and (b) UACR in kidney recipients with recurrent IgA nephropathy with add-on telitacicept treatment. 24h-UP, 24-hour urinary protein; UACR, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio; ∗P values indicate comparison against baseline: ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

A parallel downward trend was observed in UACR levels during follow-up (P < 0.001). After model adjustment, the EMM for UACR showed a progressive decline over time. Significant reductions were observed as early as week 4 (−415 mg/g, 95% CI: −681 to −148, P = 0.001) and week 12 (−380 mg/g, 95% CI: −643 to −118, P = 0.002) compared with the baseline. The reduction was most pronounced at week 28, with a decrease of 597 mg/g (95% CI: −856 to −339, P < 0.001), corresponding to a 62.70% reduction from baseline (Figure 1b).

Proteinuria remission rates were calculated among 21 patients (1 with baseline 24-UP < 300 mg/d). The results showed that 4 (19.05%) and 9 (42.86%) patients achieved complete and partial remission, respectively 4 weeks after the first dose. By the end of the 28-week follow-up, 10 (47.62%) and 12 (57.14%) patients achieved complete and partial remission, respectively (Figure 2). Three patients (13.64%) exhibited persistent 24h-UP > 1500 mg/d at the end of the 28-week treatment.

Renal Function

Longitudinal analysis of SCr levels indicated a nonlinear trend during follow-up. Paired comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences between any time point and baseline (all P > 0.05 after Sidak correction). Type III fixed-effects testing further confirmed no overall effect of time on SCr after adjustment for baseline values (P = 0.552; Figure 3a). Estimated glomerular filtration rate remained relatively stable throughout the follow-up period, with no statistically significant changes (P = 0.417). Pairwise comparisons between all follow-up time points and baseline showed no significant differences, with P values ranging from 0.317 to 0.943 (Figure 3b).

Hemoglobin and Albumin

MMRM analysis indicated that hemoglobin concentrations remained stable throughout the follow-up period, with no statistically significant changes (P = 0.646). After model adjustment, the EMM across time points showed a slight upward trend, with an average increase of 3.05 (95% CI: −2.83 to 8.95) g/l (2.43%) from baseline to week 28, but this increase was not significant (P = 0.500) (Figure 4a). Serum albumin levels demonstrated a significant upward trend during follow-up (P < 0.001). Model-adjusted estimates showed consistent increases in albumin EMM over time, reaching +3.67 (95% CI: +1.72 to +5.62) g/l (+9.53%) from baseline by week 28 (Figure 4b).

Biomarkers Associated with IgAN

MMRM analysis showed distinct longitudinal profiles in 4 IgAN-related biomarkers. Serum Gd-IgA1 levels declined significantly over 28 weeks (P = 0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed a statistically significant reduction at week 28 (mean difference: −2.32 μg/ml, P < 0.001), but not at weeks 4 or 12 (Figure 5a). The overall time effect was not significant for soluble transferrin receptor (P = 0.219) (Figure 5b). ASGPR presented a statistically significant time effect (P = 0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that ASGPR levels were significantly reduced at week 12 (mean difference: −262.78 ng/ml, P = 0.011) and week 28 (mean difference: −376.25 ng/ml, P < 0.001) (Figure 5c). The overall time effect of sCD89 approached significance (P = 0.050). At week 28, sCD89 decreased by 8.7 ng /ml (P = 0.056) (Figure 5d).

Ig Levels

Safety

No severe adverse events related to telitacicept were reported. The most common adverse events were injection site reactions (5 cases), fatigue (4 cases) and mild infections, including upper respiratory tract infections and urinary tract infections (Table 3), all of which were managed without discontinuation of telitacicept.

Discussion

Telitacicept was reported to significantly reduce proteinuria and improve renal outcomes in patients with lupus nephritis and primary IgAN, consistent with its role in inhibiting B-cell and plasma cell activation and reducing the production of pathogenic antibodies.13, 14, 15 In kidney recipients with rIgAN and persistent proteinuria, the mean 24h-UP and UACR reduced by 51.62% to 62.70% versus baseline, with significant decreases evident at week 4, achieving satisfactory partial and complete proteinuria remission. Renal function remained stable throughout the 28-week study, an encouraging finding, especially considering the high heterogeneity and complexity of rIgAN.

However, 3 patients (13.64%) failed to achieve remission at week 28, with 24h-UP exceeding 1500 mg/d. All 3 patients had severe histological damage (Lee-Haas IV–V) and heavy baseline proteinuria (24h-UP: 2675–5065 mg/d; UACR: 1069–3225 mg/g). After 28 weeks of treatment, their 24h-UP decreased by 6.34% to 42.43%, with UACR change ranging from −65.27% to +30.47%. These findings underscore the need for earlier intervention in rIgAN, because late-stage renal disease responds poorly, and prolonged treatment or intensified combination regimens may be required for such high-proteinuric and advanced cases; however, prospective validation is warranted.

The parallel reduction in circulating lymphocyte subsets and Igs further confirms the on-target inhibition of pathogenic IgA synthesis and supports the sustained antiproteinuric effect observed. These efficacy data, together with the favorable safety results, suggest that telitacicept may be a promising therapeutic option for this challenging refractory population.

We selected 4 biomarkers previously reported to be associated with primary IgAN, to monitor the nephropathy condition and the prognosis in rIgAN, and to further investigate their monitoring value in rIgAN. Serum Gd-IgA1 decreased by 2.49 μg/ml (68.18%) from baseline after 28 weeks of add-on telitacicept treatment, representing a direct molecular response to therapy. However, 4 patients (No. 3, 15, 18, and 20) showed no significant decrease, suggesting that in these individuals, Gd-IgA1 levels were not closely related to the severity of the IgAN. ASGPR, primarily expressed on hepatocytes but not on human mesangial cells, recognizes, internalizes, and degrades Gd-IgA1.16,17 ASGPR declined by 263 ng/ml (29%) at week 12 and 370 ng/ml (41%) at week 28, paralleling the reduced demand for ASGPR-mediated clearance in response to lowered circulating Gd-IgA1; it may serve as an indirect biomarker of treatment efficacy. CD89, an Fc receptor expressed on myeloid cells, exists in a soluble form (sCD89) that binds the Fc region of Gd-IgA1, forming immune complexes implicated in IgAN pathogenesis.18 Elevated levels of Gd-IgA1–sCD89 complexes were reported in recipients with rIgAN compared with nonrecurrent cases and healthy individuals.19 In childhood IgAN, levels of these complexes and free sCD89 correlate with proteinuria and inflammatory renal lesions.20 In this study, sCD89 decreased by 8.73 ng/ml (19.91%; P = 0.056), approaching statistical significance. Transferrin receptor 1 was identified as a receptor mediating mesangial cell binding and endocytosis of Gd-IgA1, with upregulated expression in IgAN.21,22 However, no significant changes in circulating soluble transferrin receptor levels were observed in this study. Further prospective studies are warranted to elucidate the roles of these biomarkers in rIgAN.

Data on rIgAN with persistent proteinuria are sparse, which highlights the practical relevance of clinical reports regarding the posttransplant population. Traditional immunosuppressive therapies, such as corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors, were reported to be variably effective and prone to significant side effects and incomplete response rates in rIgAN with persistent proteinuria.23, 24, 25 Few case reports suggested that gut-targeted corticosteroid could achieve proteinuria reduction and stable graft function in rIgAN, with acceptable tolerability.26,27 Other regimens, including sparsentan, iptacopan, and eculizumab, have also been reported effective in several case reports. Telitacicept has shown a favorable safety profile in our study, with no severe adverse events reported (Table 3). This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating the safety and tolerability of telitacicept in patients with lupus nephritis and primary IgAN.11,13,15

Larger, longer, and randomized controlled trials are required to confirm the efficacy and safety of telitacicept in kidney recipients with rIgAN, to define the optimal dose and duration. Given the special mechanism of telitacicept, it may be beneficial to investigate its use in combination with other emerging therapies, such as SGLT-2i or complement inhibitors, to achieve synergistic effects in managing rIgAN.

This single-arm pilot study lacks a control or placebo group and has a modest sample size; these factors limit causal inference and generalizability. Future studies should address these issues to provide more comprehensive and robust evidence for telitacicept in rIgAN with persistent proteinuria after kidney transplantation.
