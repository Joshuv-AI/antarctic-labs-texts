The descriptive epidemiology of age at colorectal cancer diagnosis in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Nicholas Matovu, Helen G Coleman, Ines Mesa-Eguiagaray, Evropi Theodoratou, Charlene M McShane
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe optimal age for introducing colorectal cancer (CRC) screening remains controversial especially in low-and middle-income countries, where evidence is limited, and the CRC incidence appears to be gradually increasing. In this systematic review, we aimed to summarise the age profile of CRC cases at diagnosis on the African continent to inform the most appropriate age for CRC screening initiation.MethodsFour electronic databases: MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science were searched using relevant subject headings and keywords from 1 January 2012 to 22 September 2023. The third volume of the African Cancer Registry Network (AFCRN) report was also included. Studies of African populations reporting the age profiles of representative CRC patient populations were eligible for inclusion. Random effects meta-analysis was used to pool mean age at CRC diagnosis, and the proportions of early-onset CRC (EOCRC); <40 and <50 years old. Age-specific incidence rates and proportions of EOCRC were calculated from the AFCRN report. The study was registered with PROSPERO, CRD42020187538.FindingsEighty-seven (87) studies reporting on 72,204 CRC cases from 24 African countries were included. The pooled mean age at CRC diagnosis was 53.5 years, with significant regional variations observed. The pooled proportion of EOCRC cases diagnosed <40 years old was 21% (95% confidence interval (CI): 18%–25%; n = 44 studies) and 38% for those <50 years old (95% CI 35%–41%; n = 41 studies). Estimated proportions of EOCRC from published studies and the AFCRN report were comparable.InterpretationWhereas the incidence of CRC is still low in Africa, there is younger age at diagnosis and an overall high proportion of EOCRC. Future CRC screening programs on the continent may need to consider a lower CRC screening age compared to that used in Western countries where EOCRC appears to be less common.FundingThis work was funded by the Faculty of Medicine, Health and Life Sciences (FMHLS), Queen's University Belfast and the Department for the Economy, Northern Ireland in support of the doctoral degree of N.M.Keywords: Colorectal neoplasms, Epidemiology, Age, Africa, Global health, Early-onset colorectal cancer

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Whereas Africa registers the lowest burden of CRC, several countries across the continent have demonstrated gradual increases in incidence of the tumour over the past three decades. Emerging research in distinct African countries has continued to show a divergent epidemiological pattern of CRC in Africa with a relatively younger age of cases at diagnosis compared to what is observed in the Western world. Currently, there is no systematic review and meta-analysis to provide a pooled estimate of the age at which CRC is diagnosed in Africa, neither is there any that summarises other age profiles of CRC cases at diagnosis. A previous review conducted in 2012 only summarised age-specific incidence rates of CRC and also limited its scope to Sub-Saharan Africa alone. In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we use data from published studies searched from four databases i.e. Medline, Embase, Web of Science and Scopus together with summary CRC data from the third volume of the African Cancer Registry Network (AFCRN) report. Databases were searched from 1st January 2012 to 22nd September 2023 to provide comprehensive and updated evidence since the previous 2012 review. We used keywords and MeSH terms related to “colorectal cancer”, “age” and “Africa” with no language restrictions to search for studies reporting on representative CRC patient populations on the entire African continent.

Added value of this study

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of published and grey literature of CRC data (including summary data from the AFCRN report) to provide pooled estimates of several age profile parameters such as the mean age at CRC diagnosis, proportions of CRC cases diagnosed <40 and <50 years old and age-specific incidence rates, stratified by African region and sex. We found that the mean age of CRC at diagnosis in Africa is 53.5 years, with significant variations across African geographical regions. We also established that a relatively large proportion i.e. 21% and 38% of CRC cases are diagnosed among individuals less than 40 and 50 years old, respectively. Overall, the age-specific incidence rates for CRC are still low in Africa, where they were less than 12 cases per 100,000 among all age groups <50 years and between 9 and 89 cases per 100,000 for all age groups ≥50 years.

Implications of all the available evidence

Pooled estimates from this study show that CRC is diagnosed at a relatively younger age and in higher proportions among young adults in Africa compared to estimates from the Western world. These findings are advantageous in informing future CRC screening policies in Africa on the most appropriate age to initiate screening among African populations, which may need to be lower than that implemented in Western countries, provided there is further evidence to justify benefit of early screening and feasibility in a low income setting like Africa. Our findings also call for reinforcing or scaling up awareness and prevention programs for CRC in Africa across the public and among health care professionals on the common signs, symptoms and risk factors of the disease, as suspicion for this tumour may be lower for young individuals.

Introduction

Of the 1.9 million incident colorectal cancer (CRC) cases estimated worldwide in 2022, approximately 70,428 (3.7%) were reported in African countries.1 Geographical variation in the CRC burden exist within Africa, with the highest CRC age-standardised incidence rates reported in La Réunion republic and Algeria (20.7 and 17.1 per 100,000 population, respectively), and the lowest in the Republic of Sierra Leone and Chad (0.24 and 2.3 per 100,000 population, respectively).1 Whereas CRC incidence is reportedly lower in Africa compared to other parts of the world, many African countries are experiencing gradual increases2 reflecting development and broader globalisation trends.3,4

In high income countries, median age of CRC at diagnosis has been reported to be 68 years old in USA5 and greater than 70 years old in countries such as Australia, Canada, Denmark, Ireland, New Zealand, Norway and the United Kingdom.6 In comparison, studies from distinct African countries like Nigeria,7 Ghana,8 and Tanzania9 report relatively lower median ages of CRC at diagnosis, ranging between 46 and 58 years. Similarly, it appears that early-onset CRC (EOCRC), i.e. CRC occurring before age of 50 years old, is more common in Africa compared to other parts of the world. According to a mini-review article by Katsidzira et al. (2017), about 19–38% of CRC cases in Africa occur below 40 years10 compared to 3%–7% of cases reported in high income settings.11,12 This could partly be attributed to differences in CRC molecular profiles across ethnicities. According to a retrospective study in South Africa, black African populations tend to have divergent CRC molecular characteristics that are associated with a younger age at onset, such as being high on microsatellite instability (MSI) (15%), compared to other ethnic groups (MSI, 8%).13 Given that CRC among young people is associated with rapid progression, poor prognosis and higher prospects of metastasis,11,14 this situation could present significant challenges to already overburdened health systems in Africa.

The American Cancer Society in 2018 recommended starting regular CRC screening at 45 years for average risk individuals using highly sensitive stool-based methods or visual examination techniques.15 Within Europe, screening age recommendations vary but typically ranges between 50 and 60 years among asymptomatic individuals with either stool-based test or colonoscopy.16 Unfortunately, similar population-based recommendations do not currently exist for African populations where current evidence points towards a higher burden of EOCRC.

Establishing continental and regional age profiles of CRC at diagnosis is needed to inform CRC screening policies in Africa, especially the timing of screening initiation for the disease. Currently, no study has described the age profiles of CRC cases at diagnosis for the entire African continent. Indeed, since Graham et al. (2012) conducted their review in Sub-Saharan Africa, in which they found an increase of CRC incidence with age,17 there is a lot of newly emerging evidence on the epidemiology of CRC across Africa that may have altered the image and representation of the disease. To address the above gaps, we provide the first review of published and grey literature where we describe the epidemiology of age at CRC diagnosis in Africa to inform recommendations on the timing of mass CRC screening among the African population.

Methods

Four electronic databases, namely, Medline (ALL), Web of Science (core collection), Scopus and Embase were searched from 1 January 2012 (to update the previous review by Graham et al. (2012)17) to 22 September 2023 using medical subject headings and key words for “colorectal cancer”, “age” and “Africa” (Appendix 1). Where possible, searches were restricted to humans, and no language restriction was applied. Additional hand searching of reference lists of included papers was undertaken to identify additional studies not identified by the electronic search. Search results were imported into COVIDENCE online systematic review management software which facilitated removal of duplicates and further screening. We also reviewed grey summary data from the third volume of the African cancer registry network (AFCRN) report to obtain relevant data on incident CRC cases between 2009 and 2017.18 This report included information from 31 cancer registries across 23 countries in Sub-Saharan Africa. Previous volumes/versions of the AFCRN report were disregarded in favour of the most recent version. Since the AFCRN report focuses solely on incident CRC cases in Sub-Saharan Africa, and yet still some countries in Sub-Saharan Africa lack cancer registries, incorporating published studies across the entire African region ensures a more comprehensive and inclusive analysis of CRC data on this subject. Further efforts to search other relevant grey literature (such as from Northern African Cancer Registries) did not yield any results. Data from the AFCRN report and that from published studies was analysed separately to ensure that each data source presents independent results to enable results comparison.

Eligibility of studies included in this review was guided by the Condition, Context and Population (CoCoPop) criteria.19,20 All titles, abstracts and full text were screened by two reviewers (NM (lead reviewer), CMcS and/or HGC) independently against predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Appendix 2). Briefly, only studies reporting age profiles of representative CRC patient populations such as those that recruited newly diagnosed CRC cases using representative sampling methods (consecutive, population-based, or random) were included. Studies with selection bias, such as those focusing on CRC high-risk individuals, advanced CRC cases, or which applied exclusion criteria that could distort patient representativeness were excluded. Also, studies had to have been conducted in African countries and published in the above specified search period. Conflicts were resolved through discussion amongst all three reviewers.

We developed and piloted a data extraction proforma prior to undertaking data extraction. Data extraction was undertaken by NM and a sample was checked for accuracy by another member of the team (CMcS or HGC). Extracted data included the first author's surname, year of publication, country, study design, source of CRC data, sex distribution of CRC and anatomical site. Discrepancies were resolved through re-reviewing the full text articles and rectifying errors. Summary data from the AFCRN report was also extracted by NM for CRC cases defined by International Classification of Disease −10th revision codes for colon (C-18) and rectum (C19-20). Data on registry name, reporting period, country, number of CRC cases and frequency of cases per five-year age group were extracted.

Studies were subjectively assessed for quality and rated as good, fair or poor using the National Institute of Health (NIH) quality assessment tool21 by the lead reviewer (NM), Appendix 3. While a formal Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) analysis was not performed to ascertain certainty of evidence,22 a narrative description outlining the major concerns of our reported outcomes was provided. The protocol for this review was registered with PROSPERO, CRD42020187538.

Statistical analysis

Published studies

All statistical analyses from the published studies were conducted using STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). We used random effects meta-analyses to establish: (i) pooled mean age at CRC diagnosis, (ii) pooled proportions of CRC cases diagnosed <40 years and pooled proportions of CRC cases diagnosed <50 years; with corresponding 95% confidence intervals for these estimates. As a number of the studies had sample sizes <100 cases, heterogeneity variance estimation was based on the restricted maximum likelihood method (REML) in favour of the DerSimonian-Laird (DL) method. REML is preferred in this context as it yields more accurate and less biased estimates of the between-study variance, particularly in meta-analyses with small sample sizes, a limited number of studies, or when heterogeneity is substantial. Simulation studies have shown that DL tends to underestimate the true heterogeneity under such conditions, whereas REML maintains better performance in controlling type I error and coverage probability.23 Pooled mean age at CRC diagnosis was computed separately for studies that directly reported mean age (SD) and in those where the mean was estimated from the median (interquartile range (IQR) or range). Statistical methodologies proposed by Luo D et al.24 and Wan X et al.25 were used to estimate the mean (SD) from the median (IQR or range) via an online mathematical tool.26 Studies that reported mean or median age only without the corresponding standard deviations or IQR/range were not considered in this meta-analysis. The I2 statistic was used to determine the extent of variation across studies due to heterogeneity, where values more than 50% percent represented high heterogeneity.27

We conducted subgroup analyses based on study quality and geographical region to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Post hoc sub-group analyses were also conducted by country (for those with ≥3 studies), study size, data source, study design and country income status. Subgroup analyses by sex were not conducted due to inadequate number of studies. We also conducted simple random effects meta-regression as post hoc analyses to further investigate associations between CRC diagnosis age profiles and certain moderators (including country income status, study size, data source, study design and geographical region).

Publication bias for all main meta-analyses was initially assessed using the funnel plot to check for asymmetry.28 The statistical Egger's test was then used, with results compared with the funnel plot output.29 The trim and fill analysis was thereafter conducted to adjust for publication bias.

Age-specific CRC incidence rates reported in published studies were narratively described as it was not possible to pool the data due to the varying age group categories used across studies.

Reports

Data from the African Cancer Registry Network (AFCRN) Report volume 3 was analysed for age profiles of CRC cases at diagnosis.18 Statistical analyses from the report data were conducted using both STATA version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Data was analysed for (i) age-specific incidence rates of CRC and (ii) proportions of CRC cases diagnosed <40 and <50 years.

Age-specific incidence rates for each reported cancer registry was computed as the average annual age-specific incidence rates due to difference in number of years reported on (e.g. some registries reported on CRC cases in 3 years, while others 2, 4 or 5 years). In six of the registries, average annual age-specific incidence rates could not be computed due to lack of data on registry catchment population breakdown by age.

Proportions of CRC cases diagnosed <40 and <50 years were calculated for each cancer registry as the number of incident CRC cases in each age category divided by the total number of incident CRC cases diagnosed over a specified period, expressed as percentages. Proportions reported were stratified by sex and geographical region.

Role of the funding source

The funder of the study had no role in study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, writing or decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Results

The primary search yielded 2710 references, Fig. 1. Following removal of duplicates, title and abstract screening was undertaken for 1786 references of which 398 proceeded to full-text screening. A total of 73 studies met the eligibility criteria. A further 14 eligible studies were identified by hand searching reference lists of included articles.

Forty-three (43) studies (n = 46,025 CRC cases)7, 8, 9,30,35,36,38,41,43,44,47,50,57,60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 66,68,70,71,73,75,79,81,83,85,86,88,89,92,93,95,97,99, 100, 101,103, 104, 105,107 were included in the meta-analysis of mean age (SD) at CRC diagnosis. Combining all 43 studies, the pooled mean age of CRC cases at diagnosis was 53.5 years (95% CI 51.9, 55.0; Prediction Interval (PI): 43.3, 63.7) with high heterogeneity observed (I2 = 98.9%), Fig. 2. Evidence of publication bias was identified by geographical region, for studies reporting proportions of CRC cases <40 years (Egger's test: p = 0.0068) and those reporting mean age at CRC diagnosis (Egger's test: p = 0.0049) (Appendix 7a). To adjust for publication bias, trim and fill analysis was conducted (Appendix 7 b). For the overall pooled mean age meta-analysis, funnel plot asymmetry was restored with imputation of one study in the West African region using this method (adjusted pooled mean age: 53.25; 95% CI: 51.69, 54.81 Vs original pooled mean age: 53.48; 95% CI: 51.95, 55.01). In the pooled analysis of CRC cases <40 years, trim and fill analysis imputed three studies (two in East Africa and one in South Africa) (Adjusted proportion: 16%; 95% CI: 13%, 20% Vs Original proportion: 21%; 95% CI: 18%, 25%).

Seven studies reported age-specific incidence rates for CRC.31,37,39,40,54,66,78 Meta-analysis was not performed due to different reporting formats. Overall, these studies showed increasing CRC incidence with age. All studies reported lower age-specific CRC incidence rates (i.e. less than 10 cases per 100,000) for age groups less than 50 years while higher incidence rates were generally observed in all age groups above 50 years (i.e. between 5 and 87 cases per 100,000) (Appendix 8).

For the AFCRN report data, cancer registry data from 14 East, 6 West, 4 South and 1 Central African country were included (Appendix 9). The average annual CRC age-specific rates were highest among older age groups especially those aged 50+ in all regions and for both sexes. The average annual age-specific incidence rate for the 50+ age group ranged from 11.9 to 89 cases/100,000 in Eastern Africa, 13.2 to 66.9 cases/100,000 in Southern Africa, 11.0 to 36.4 cases/100,000 in West Africa and 9.1 to 13.7 per 100,000 in Central Africa. These rates are all higher than those in the younger age groups (<50) for each corresponding African region. Eastern African countries had the highest average annual age-specific incidence rates for CRC in both males and females across all age groups. With advancing age, average annual CRC incidence rates rose more steeply among males compared to females in all African regions (Fig. 4 and Appendix 10). Further analysis showed that the CRC age-specific incidence rates were higher for colon cancers in both sexes compared to rectum cancers; and these increased with advancing age (Fig. 4 and Appendix 11).

Certainty of evidence

A formal Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE)22 assessment was not undertaken, which may limit the interpretability of the findings, particularly given the reliance on observational data and concerns regarding inconsistency (heterogeneity) and publication bias across all the outcomes.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis reporting age profiles of CRC cases at diagnosis in the entire African population. Overall, the pooled mean age at CRC diagnosis in Africa was 53.5 years, with regional variations noted, suggesting a younger age of CRC onset in African nations than in Western countries. While overall CRC incidence remains low, compared to high income settings, a higher proportion of CRC cases are diagnosed less than 40 (21%) and 50 (38%) years old in Africa.

The mean/median age at CRC diagnosis reported in this meta-analysis appears lower than that reported in other continents. For instance, data from 19 cancer registries across seven high income nations including the United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, Denmark, Ireland, New Zealand, and Norway reported the median age at CRC diagnosis was above 70 years.6 Other studies among CRC patient populations in the US114,115 and Australia116 report higher median ages between 66 and 69. In Sri Lankan117 and Chinese118 CRC patients, the median age at CRC diagnosis was also reported to be higher (at 60 and 59.3 years, respectively) than in this study. A subgroup analysis conducted in this review showed that South African countries (all studies from South Africa) had the highest pooled mean age at CRC diagnosis reported at 59.9 years compared to all the other African regions. Comparing the pooled estimate from South Africa (mean age = 59.9 years) with that reported in China,118 both being upper middle-income countries, shows similar findings which could point to the fact that age at CRC onset may be associated with income and development status of countries. Moreover, differences in molecular and genetic characterisation of CRC in Africa compared to those in the Western world may also explain the younger age at CRC onset within this setting. Previous studies in Africa consistently report a relatively higher burden of mismatch repair (MMR) gene deficiency in CRC ranging from 11.2% to 42%,119,120 compared to for instance, 5.6% in the USA and 4% in Scotland.121,122 The higher burden of MMR gene deficiency could potentially suggest presence of hereditary syndromes such as Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) and MUYTH associated polyposis (MAP), which are all associated with CRC among young people.123

Our analysis revealed a higher proportion of EOCRC in Africa (i.e. CRC diagnosed <50 years) at 38%, compared to previous results reported in Western settings, for example, USA (12.3%),115 Brazil (13.4%),124 Ireland (7%)125 and Singapore (11.6%).126 The high proportion of EOCRC cases reported here may partly reflect Africa's youthful population structure. When we analysed age-specific incidence rates from the AFCRN population-based registries, which offer a more robust comparison of underlying risk by age group, results confirmed that while absolute CRC risk increases with age, incidence rates among individuals <50 years were still relatively low (<12 per 100,000), consistent with the idea that EOCRC in Africa may represent a mix of hereditary and sporadic disease.127

Future research in this area should consider the following. We were unable to include all studies (particularly those reporting CRC age-specific incidence rates) in meta-analysis due to different reporting formats. Hence, standardised age categories should be used when reporting age-specific incidence rates for CRC, similar to those used in the AFCRN report and the Globocan database to enable meaningful result comparisons. More studies from south and central African countries reporting on age profiles of CRC patients at diagnosis are needed to provide a better representation of all regions on the continent. Lastly, there is need to further explore the risk factors for EOCRC pertinent to Africa using robust study designs.

To ensure our review reflects the most recent evidence, the study team re-ran the search up to 16th July 2025, identifying 16 potentially relevant studies published since the last search. However, half of these studies (n = 8) were based on data sources and populations already represented in the existing review and were therefore excluded. While the remaining eligible studies may provide additional context, their inclusion is unlikely to substantially affect the overall interpretation of our findings.

Our study has several strengths. Use of two distinct data sources (published studies and the AFCRN report data) enabled results comparison and triangulation of findings to provide more robust evidence. The use of two independent reviewers during the screening process potentially eliminated selection bias of included studies. In addition, we applied a rigorous search process exploiting four electronic databases and an extra-search in the reference lists of included studies to ensure that no relevant studies are missed.

On the other hand, our review was limited by the fact that there was a discrepancy in the reporting of proportions of CRC cases <40 and <50 years old within published studies. Some reported this as ≤40 and ≤50 years old which introduced a potential for error whereby CRC cases with 40 and 50 years could have been included in these proportions. Nonetheless, by conducting a subgroup analysis, we did not find significant differences in proportions among studies which used cut-offs as <40 and <50 compared to those that used cut-offs of ≤40 and ≤50 years old, hence this discrepancy is unlikely to have altered the interpretation of the reported findings.

Published studies reporting age-specific incidence rates used differing age groups, which limited the ability to adequately compare and pool these CRC incidence rates across studies. However, a narrative synthesis was adopted to describe available patterns in incidence rates across age groups as appropriate.

Although our eligibility criteria was designed to ensure careful consideration of patient recruitment and minimise the potential for selection bias, we acknowledge the possibility of residual bias due to the predominance of hospital-based data, particularly from tertiary care centres which may affect the representativeness of the CRC patient population in Africa.

Further, a high degree of heterogeneity was observed among studies included in the different meta-analyses. Subgroup and meta-regression analyses identified several significant contributors to heterogeneity across all meta-analyses, including variations in geographical region, country income status, sample size, data source, and study design. Although random-effects models were employed to account for this heterogeneity in the meta-analyses, the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution due to the considerable variability observed between studies. It is also likely that additional sources of heterogeneity such as differences in diagnostic methods or clinical practices were present, though data on these factors were not collected as it was outside the scope of this review. To support interpretation and illustrate the extent of between-study variability, we have reported prediction intervals, which estimate the range within which the true effect sizes of future studies are likely to fall.

Potential publication bias may have influenced our findings, particularly for the pooled mean age and the proportion of CRC cases diagnosed before age 40. Trim and fill analysis suggested slight overestimations in these estimates. Although the adjusted estimates remained broadly consistent with the original results, these findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation, especially given the possibility of unpublished or underreported studies with lower effect sizes.

Again, the underrepresentation of Central and Southern African countries in the meta-analyses of published studies limits the generalizability of our findings across the continent. As a result, the overall pooled estimates may be biased toward regions with greater data availability, potentially obscuring unique CRC patterns in less-represented areas. For instance, our pooled estimates may be disproportionately influenced by trends from Northern, Western and Eastern Africa, given the relative scarcity of data from Central and Southern regions. To address this, we conducted subgroup analyses by region to provide more context-specific insights. Therefore, CRC screening recommendations should be guided by regional estimates where available, and we strongly advocate for increased research on this subject in Central and Southern Africa to support equitable and evidence-informed policymaking.

Lastly, to minimise the risk of double-counting of cases within the meta-analysis of peer-reviewed literature, we carefully cross-checked studies that utilised similar data sources and included only the study with the largest sample size or most recent publication date, in that sequence. We acknowledge potential for overlapping cancer registry data within the published peer-review literature and AFCRN report, however, meta-analyses were conducted separately for data ascertained from the peer-reviewed literature and from the AFCRN report to produce independent estimates thus avoiding double counting of the same cancer registry populations. This approach facilitated narrative comparison and triangulation of findings across the distinct data sources.

Our findings indicate a lower age at CRC onset in African populations compared to Western countries, prompting consideration of an earlier screening age. However, due to limited context-specific evidence on benefits of earlier screening, particularly regarding stage at diagnosis, survival outcomes, and broader public health impact, these considerations should remain cautious. Within the African continent, population-level CRC screening is rare, with only a few published pilot initiatives.128 Opportunistic screening based on symptoms or risk profiles, rather than age, may be more suitable. Given the high prevalence of EOCRC but low overall incidence and constrained resources in low-income settings like Africa,129 future efforts should focus on generating local evidence through pilot studies and cost-effectiveness analyses to guide targeted screening strategies.
