A reversible-zero watermarking scheme for medical images
Authors: Rizwan Taj, Feng Tao, Saima Kanwal, Ahmad Almogren, Ayman Altameem, Ateeq Ur Rehman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe paper addresses the issue of ensuring the authenticity and copyright of medical images in telemedicine applications, with a specific emphasis on watermarking methods. While several systems only concentrate on identifying tampering in medical images, others also provide the capacity to restore the tampered regions upon detection. While several authentication techniques in medical imaging have successfully achieved their goals, previous research underscores a notable deficiency: the resilience of these schemes against unintentional attacks has not been sufficiently examined or emphasized in previous research. This indicates the need for further development and investigation in improving the robustness of medical image authentication techniques against unintentional attacks. This research proposes a Reversible-Zero Watermarking approach as a solution to address these problems. The new approach merges the advantages of both the reversible and zero watermarking techniques. This system is comprised of two parts. The first part is a zero-watermarking technique that uses VGG19-based feature extraction and watermark information to establish an ownership share. The second part incorporates this ownership share into the image in a reversible manner using a combination of a discrete wavelet transform, an integer wavelet transform, and a difference expansion. Research findings confirm that the suggested watermarking approach for medical images demonstrates substantial enhancements compared to current methodologies. Research findings indicate that NC values are often around 0.9 for different attacks, whereas BER values are close to 0. It demonstrates exceptional qualities in being imperceptible, distinguishable, and robust. Additionally, the system provides a persistent verification feature that functions independently of disputes or third-party storage, making it the preferred choice in the domain of medical image watermarking.Keywords: Reversible-zero watermarking, Medical images, Copyright protection, AuthenticitySubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

With the swift innovations in computer technology and the evolution of the internet, the modification and distribution of digital media, including video, audio, and images, have become significantly more accessible and prevalent. Information security in electronic media is a major concern. The adoption of digital watermarking has been advocated to address this issue. The procedure entails embedding certain private data into the host image. This embedded information aids in establishing data ownership when necessary1–4. Digital watermarking is a commonly employed technique in various broadcast monitoring applications, transaction tracking, copyright protection, and authentication5,6. Watermarking embeds information within digital media to ensure protection and authentication, focusing on robustness, imperceptibility, capacity, and security. Cryptography secures information by transforming it into an unreadable format for unauthorized users, maintaining confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity. Steganography hides information within non-secret data to conceal its existence, using techniques like spatial and frequency domain methods. Effective watermarking schemes possess several key characteristics: security, robustness, imperceptibility, and embedding capacity7–10. Imperceptibility pertains to the human eye's incapacity to see any variations between the original data of the host and the data that has been watermarked8. The concept of robustness concerns the capacity of a watermarking system to successfully retrieve the embedded watermark even when confronted to different types of attacks9. Its capacity to withstand decryption attempts determines the watermarking method's robustness. This resistance primarily relies on the secret keys employed during the watermark extraction10. Embedding capacity is the quantity of watermark bits that can be embedded. The categorization of watermarking algorithms may vary based on various characteristics11. Watermarking techniques can be categorized as blind, semi-blind, and non-blind algorithms, relying on the imperative information of the original data during the watermark extraction procedure12. Semi-blind watermarking techniques necessitate limited information from the original data for watermark detection, whereas informed watermarking, or non-blind watermarking, requires access to both the original and watermarked content for extraction, ensuring higher robustness and accuracy but requiring the original media. Zero watermarking, or oblivious watermarking, does not modify the original media; instead, it extracts features to create a separate watermark signature for verification. This method preserves the original quality but is generally less robust to attacks. Informed watermarking is ideal for high robustness needs, while zero watermarking is suited for maintaining original content quality. On the other hand, blind watermarking algorithms are capable of extracting watermarks without any need for the original data. Depending on their use and objectives, watermarking algorithms fall into fragile and robust categories13. Fragile watermarking is typically used in image authentication and tamper detection scenarios.

On the other hand, robust watermarking algorithms find their application in copyright protection14. Watermarking algorithms are characterized by the domain where they will be embedded: spatial domain and transform domain15. In spatial domain algorithms, embedding watermarks typically involves direct modification of the pixels within the host images16,17. Transform domain watermarking methods entail the application of one or more transformations to the host images18. The frequency domain coefficients are modified to embed the watermarks. Compared to transform domain watermarking methods, algorithms operating in the spatial domain typically have lower computational complexity. However, watermarking has limitations in the spatial domain, especially when it involves visibility and robustness19. Image watermarking algorithms commonly employ various transforms, including the Shearlet20–22, Contourlet23,24, Wavelet25,26, Fourier27,28, and cosine transform29–31.

In the aforementioned conventional watermarking methods, the host images experience some level of distortion, regardless of whether the original watermarks are embedded in the transform or spatial domain. Hence, an inherent contradiction emerges concerning the robustness and imperceptibility of the watermark. Traditional watermarking techniques are typically limited when applied to medical32,33 and remote-sensing34 images that require high resolution and minimal distortion. In this situation, Wen et al.35 suggested zero-watermarking, which has garnered much interest from scholars lately. Zero-watermarking is a method that allows the extraction of the essential features of the original data to refrain from making any modifications to the data itself. Therefore, it can resolve the contention between the imperceptibility and robustness exhibited by conventional watermarking techniques. Utilizing high-order cumulants, Wen et al.35 developed a zero-watermark. The proposed method demonstrates robustness against typical image processing attacks and minor scaling rotations. However, it exhibits vulnerability to significant rotations and requires substantial computational effort to calculate the high-order cumulants. The zero-watermarking algorithm suggested by Ye36 utilizes SVD and DCT as its foundation. The algorithm begins by dividing the host image into non-overlapping blocks. The zero-watermark order is obtained by analyzing the numerical connection between the two neighboring blocks' discrete cosine (DC) coefficients. This analysis is performed using SVD and DCT on each block. The algorithm proposed a novel zero-watermarking approach by integrating matrix decomposition and traditional transforms. However, the experiments to evaluate the algorithm's performance employed only minimal attacks. Thanh et al.37 extracted the robust feature, known as the master share, from the host image using a combination of QR decomposition and one-dimensional (1D) discrete cosine transform (DCT). The host images used in the scheme are RGB images.Two zero-watermarking techniques were developed using the host images' Y-component luminance. This component is segmented into unique, non-intersecting blocks, and a master share is created by contrasting the DC coefficients of adjacent blocks. This comparison happened after QR decomposition and 1D-DCT were performed on all blocks. The methods above emphasize a prevalent obstacle numerous zero-watermarking algorithms encounter in their ability to withstand geometric distortions, specifically rotation and translation attacks. Although these algorithms are specifically designed to endure typical image processing operations, they often demonstrate vulnerability when exposed to geometric transformations.

This paper introduces a robust zero-watermarking scheme that is based on VGG19 features-based extraction and Henon map encryption. A reversible watermarking approach is developed using Discrete Wavelet Transform, Integer Wavelet Transform, and Difference Expansion. The empirical findings presented in the research demonstrate that the suggested method is highly effective in resisting standard image processing operations and various conventional and geometric distortions. This suggests that the algorithm could be a reliable solution for maintaining the integrity and security of digital images. The contributions we have made are emphasized in the following approach:

Related work

The classification of existing methods for watermarking schemes in medical image copyright and authenticity verification can be categorized into three primary categories: zero-watermarking schemes, reversible watermarking schemes, and region-of-interest (ROI) lossless watermarking techniques.

Zero-watermarking scheme

Zero-watermarking methods are favored as they ensure that medical images are protected by copyright without altering the original content. A zero-watermarking technique employing the dual-tree complex wavelet and DCT was presented by Liu et al. in38. When this approach is exposed to different image attacks, its performance is usually excellent. On the other hand, it performs inadequately against rotation, median filtering, and high-intensity Gaussian noise attacks. In39, Wu et al. suggested a novel algorithm for multiple watermarking of medical images. This algorithm utilizes contourlet and DCT as the underlying techniques. The algorithm under consideration demonstrates high resistance against various image attacks. However, it exhibits a relatively lower level of robustness when subjected to cropping attacks. A zero-watermarking algorithm was suggested by Cedillo et al. in40, which establishes a correlation concerning medical images, patient diagnosis, and patient data. The algorithm functions within the frequency domain, employing methods of encoding and encryption to manipulate the watermark data. The zero-watermarking approach proposed by Qin et al.41 utilizes Curvelet DCT for visual feature vector extraction and employs a pseudo-random sequence for watermark encryption. While demonstrating commendable robustness against various attacks, the system's performance is suboptimal when subjected to cropping and Gaussian noise attacks.

Robust watermarking schemes

Thanki et al.42 performed robust medical image watermarking by acquiring various frequency coefficients via the Fast Discrete Curvelet Transform. The suggested technique is robust against JPEG compression, signal processing, and geometry attacks. However, its effectiveness is limited when dealing with Gaussian and Pepper & salt noise. The efficiency of noise attack resistance has limitations in43, where a combination of DWT, SVD, and DCT is employed. The watermarking technique proposed by Singh et al.44 utilizes the spread spectrum technique, pseudo-noise sequence, and selective discrete wavelet transform. Wang et al. presented a multi-watermarking method in45 that is based on hybrid multi-bit multiplicative rules' least Bayesian risk criteria. The generalized Gaussian distribution model represents the discrete wavelet transform (DWT) coefficients. Both of these algorithms exhibit suboptimal performance in terms of robustness.

Tampering detection watermarking schemes

In46, Swaraja et al. developed a dual watermarking algorithm for medical images incorporating the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT), Schur transform, and Particle Swarm Bacterial Foraging Optimization algorithm. This algorithm is designed to detect tampering and ensure the authenticity of the medical images. A copyright protection scheme was proposed in a study conducted by Alshan Bari47. The method utilizes principal component-based techniques to strengthen the security of the algorithm. Additionally, a fragile watermarking approach based on the LZW algorithm is incorporated to enable the algorithm to detect tampering attempts effectively. The algorithm effectively incorporates sensitive data into the ROI (Region of Interest) section of medical images, resulting in a highly reversible, imperceptible, and robust algorithm. In48, Hurrah et al. introduced an innovative reversible scheme for authenticating medical images. This scheme serves the purpose of detecting tampering in medical data and verifying copyright ownership. The scheme demonstrates superior imperceptibility and fragility compared to similar schemes, as evidenced by the comparison results. The algorithm's robustness is suboptimal, and its tampering detection functionality is ineffective.

Embedding process

In the context of embedding a watermark inside a watermarking scheme, it is essential to ensure the ability to recover the watermarked image to its original state after extracting the ownership share. This suggests that the process of embedding is reversible and does not result in permanent distortions or loss of information in the original picture. The embedding image may include pertinent details about the patient, such as their personal information, copyright specifics, or any additional relevant data. The embedding procedure involves altering the image to incorporate the watermark while eliminating any perceptible alterations. The suggested robust reversible watermarking technique integrates three essential processes: Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) and Integer Wavelet Transform (IWT) are employed for embedding watermark bits into medical images, ensuring that the process is reversible. DWT offers a multi-resolution image representation, which is key for embedding watermarks at different levels of detail and robustness. The use of IWT guarantees that the original image can be perfectly reconstructed after the watermark is extracted, thanks to its lossless nature. In this methodology, Difference Expansion (DE) is utilized to embed the watermark. DE allows for a high embedding capacity while maintaining the image's visual quality, making it less detectable. The reversibility of the process is paramount, as it ensures that the clinical value of the medical image is preserved after the watermark is removed.

Step 1: discrete wavelet transform (DWT)

DWT stands out as a cornerstone technique in the domain of image processing, offering a blend of spatial and frequency analysis that is invaluable for watermarking. Its ability to decompose images into components of varying resolutions provides a nuanced approach to embedding watermarks in a manner that is both robust and minimally invasive to the original image quality. Step 2: integer wavelet transform (IWT) for reversible transformation

The Integer Wavelet Transform (IWT) is a variant of the Wavelet Transform specifically designed to ensure reversibility, making it ideal for applications where it's crucial to recover the original data perfectly, such as in lossless image compression and reversible watermarking. IWT operates entirely with integer calculations, thus avoiding information loss and ensuring the original image's exact reconstruction.

Similar to DWT, it produces four sub-bands at each decomposition level: LL, LH, HL, and HH. However, the key difference lies in implementing the filters and the down-sampling/up-sampling steps, designed to be reversible using only integer operations. IWT often employs a lifting scheme, a technique that breaks down the wavelet transform into a sequence of simpler filtering steps. This scheme allows for in-place computation, reducing memory usage and computational complexity. The lifting scheme is inherently reversible, enabling the exact reconstruction of the original image from the wavelet coefficients. To ensure reversibility, apply the Integer Wavelet Transform (IWT) to the LL sub-band from the DWT step. IWT provides a lossless version of the wavelet transform, making it possible to reconstruct the original image exactly after embedding.

Conclusion

This paper presents a reversible-zero watermarking scheme, which is purposefully developed to protect the copyright and authenticity of medical images. The experimental results obtained from the proposed scheme demonstrate its successful integration of imperceptibility, distinguishability, and robustness in watermarking. This indicates that the scheme efficiently protects the images, preserves their integrity, and facilitates elementary watermark identification. The suggested medical image security method is multifaceted. It employs the VGG19 model to extract features from the medical images. For generating sequences, the Henon map, known for its chaotic behavior, is used to create logistic sequences. The scheme further integrates three advanced techniques to bolster security: Discrete Wavelet Transform, Integer Wavelet Transform, and Difference Expansion. This combination of feature extraction, chaotic sequence generation, and sophisticated transformation and modulation techniques aims to provide a robust security framework for medical images. Our future work involves enhancing the robustness of the reversible-zero watermarking approach and applying it to color medical images, another avenue for future research includes the integration of machine learning techniques to dynamically adapt the watermarking process based on the content and context of the images, potentially increasing both security and efficiency.
