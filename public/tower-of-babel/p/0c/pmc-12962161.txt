Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into healthcare education has accelerated dramatically since the introduction of ChatGPT in late 2022.1 Large language models (LLMs) represent a paradigm shift in AI capabilities, demonstrating unprecedented performance in natural language understanding, reasoning, and knowledge synthesis across diverse domains.2 In dentistry, these advances have prompted extensive investigation into LLM applications ranging from diagnostic assistance to educational support.3, 4, 5

Recent systematic evaluations have demonstrated that LLMs can achieve passing scores on medical and dental licensing examinations worldwide.5, 6, 7 Notably, studies have reported successful performance on the United States Medical Licensing Examination,8,9 Japanese National Dentist Examination,10,11 and various other professional assessments. These findings suggest that LLMs have achieved a level of medical knowledge synthesis comparable to entry-level practitioners in certain domains. Beyond these general trends, emerging specialty-level evaluations in dentistry have shown that LLMs can demonstrate strong reasoning performance on text-based clinical questions12, 13, 14, 15 while exhibiting variable but gradually improving capabilities in image-based diagnostic reasoning.11,16, 17, 18

Previous research on Korean dental licensing examinations (KDLE) revealed that earlier-generation models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude-3 Opus) could surpass passing thresholds but remained below average student performance.19 These studies identified specific limitations in visual interpretation tasks and clinically integrative questions, highlighting areas where human expertise maintained clear advantages over AI systems.

Since these initial evaluations, the LLM landscape has evolved substantially. Next-generation models including ChatGPT-4o, Claude-4 Opus, Gemini 2.5 Pro, and DeepSeek-V3 incorporate enhanced reasoning architectures, improved multimodal capabilities, and expanded knowledge bases. However, systematic evaluation of these advanced models on standardized dental examinations remains limited.

The KDLE provides an ideal benchmark for LLM assessment due to its comprehensive coverage of dental knowledge, inclusion of both text-based and visual components, and standardized format that enables objective performance measurement. Given the rapid pace of AI development and the potential implications for dental education, updated benchmarking studies are essential to understand current capabilities and limitations.

This study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of 4 state-of-the-art LLMs on the 2024 and 2025 KDLE. Specifically, we sought to: (1) assess overall and subject-specific performance compared to human examinees, (2) evaluate differential performance on text-only versus image-based questions, (3) quantify inter-model agreement and statistical differences, and (4) identify specific strengths and limitations relevant to dental education applications.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This study utilized publicly available examination materials from the Korea Health Personnel Licensing Examination Institute. The research protocol was approved by the Public Institutional Review Board of South Korea (approval number: P01-202506-01-014). Given the use of anonymized, publicly available data without personal identifiers, individual informed consent was waived per institutional guidelines.

Study design and data source

We conducted a cross-sectional comparative analysis using official KDLE question sets from 2024 and 2025. Each examination consisted of 321 multiple-choice questions (total n = 642), distributed across 13 dental subjects: Oral Medicine (OM), Prosthodontics (PT), Pediatric Dentistry (PD), Orthodontics (OD), Oral Pathology (OP), Oral Biology (OB), Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology (OMR), Periodontics (PO), Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (OMS), Conservative Dentistry (CD), Oral Health Science (OHS), Dental Materials (DM), and Health and Medical Legislation (HML).

Approximately 15-20% of items included clinical images, radiographs, or diagnostic figures. Official answer keys served as the reference standard for all analyses.

Four LLMs were evaluated using their official interfaces between June 12 and July 31, 2025:

No major version updates were publicly announced by the model developers during the testing period, and detailed build-level update information was not publicly available or independently verifiable. All evaluations were performed through the official web interfaces of each model. These interfaces do not expose or allow user control of sampling parameters such as temperature, top-p, or seed; therefore, the exact values remain provider-defined and are not reported here. All models were accessed via paid standard subscription accounts from a single geographic region (South Korea), and the same user-facing model labels were consistently used throughout the entire evaluation period.

Question administration protocol

Each examination item was presented individually using a standardized prompt format to ensure consistency across models. For multimodal models (ChatGPT-4o, Claude-4 Opus, Gemini 2.5 Pro), both textual content and original images were provided for visual questions. In contrast, as a text-based model, DeepSeek-V3 received complete items but could only process the layout and textual structure embedded within images—such as the arrangement of labels, numbers, and text blocks—rather than performing true visual interpretation.

Each question was presented using a standardized, self-contained prompt that included all necessary information within the item itself, preventing any carry-over of reasoning or context from previous questions. The standardized prompt format was: "Please answer the following Korean Dental Licensing Examination question. Select the most appropriate answer from the given options and provide your reasoning. Treat the question as an independent item. Do not use any information, context, or reasoning from previous questions or earlier parts of this conversation." No prompt engineering or optimization was applied. Figure 1 provides a simplified example of the relationship between the prompt and the model responses.

Illustrative diagram of how 4 large language models approached representative Korean Dental Licensing Examination items. The figure highlights that, even when models selected the same correct answer, their reasoning strategies and explanatory styles differed considerably.

All questions and prompts were provided in their original Korean language, identical to the official examination materials. The entire testing process—including question input, model reasoning, and response verification—was conducted exclusively in Korean without translation to eliminate potential language-related bias.

All visual materials were obtained from the official KDLE PDFs released by the national licensing authority. Images were exported at native resolution and converted to PNG format for model input. No additional preprocessing was applied.

To ensure item-level independence and eliminate potential carry-over or conversational drift, each question was administered in a newly initialized chat session. No prior prompts, responses, or conversational history were visible to the model at the time of each query, and no context was shared across items or runs. This session reset was applied uniformly across all models and evaluation runs.

Data extraction and scoring

Model responses were systematically parsed to extract selected answers. Two investigators independently verified response classification, with disagreements resolved through consensus. Scores were calculated as the total number of correct responses, with performance analyzed at both overall and subject-specific levels.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed. The primary outcome was overall accuracy, with secondary outcomes including subject-specific performance and accuracy on text-only versus image-based questions. This hierarchical structure was prespecified to distinguish confirmatory analyses (overall accuracy) from secondary modality-level assessments and exploratory subject-wise comparisons.

Within-model repeatability was assessed using a stratified subset of 100 items, constructed to preserve the original distributions by examination year, subject, and presence of image-based content. Each item was independently administered to each model across 5 separate runs conducted in fully independent chat sessions.

Repeatability was quantified using mean pairwise Cohen’s kappa (κ), percentage agreement, and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC). Uncertainty was assessed using nonparametric bootstrap resampling at the item level (1,000 iterations) to derive 95% confidence intervals for κ and percentage agreement.

For overall model comparisons, Cochran’s Q test was applied, followed by pairwise McNemar’s tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction. Effect sizes for proportion differences were quantified using Cohen’s h. Inter-model agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficients, interpreted according to the criteria of Landis and Koch. Model convergence was further evaluated using the Majority Agreement Index (MAI). Subgroup analyses were conducted separately for text-only (n = 510) and image-based (n = 132) questions, as well as at the subject level. Differences across image subtypes were assessed using chi-square tests. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all analyses.

Student performance data

Historical student performance data for the 2024 and 2025 KDLE examinations were obtained from the official item-analysis reports published by the Korea Health Personnel Licensing Examination Institute. The 2024 KDLE included 752 examinees, and the 2025 KDLE included 756 examinees, with publicly available statistics representing aggregate, subject-level performance only. Although overall student performance, score distributions, and item difficulty indices show modest year-to-year variability, the sample sizes for both examinations were sufficiently large that such differences are unlikely to materially affect the contextual comparisons made in this study.

Results

Repeatability and within-model consistency

Across the 4 LLMs, mean pairwise Cohen’s κ values ranged from 0.57 to 0.74, with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals indicating moderate to substantial agreement. Mean percentage agreement ranged from 83.8% to 89.6%, demonstrating stable response patterns across repeated administrations. Intraclass correlation coefficients further supported good within-model consistency across 5 independent runs (ICC range: 0.57-0.74).

Overall performance

All 4 LLMs exceeded the KDLE passing threshold of 180 points across both examination years (Figure 2). Mean scores were: ChatGPT-4o (251.5 ± 15.2), Claude-4 Opus (256.5 ± 12.8), Gemini 2.5 Pro (270.5 ± 18.4), and DeepSeek-V3 (218.5 ± 22.1). ChatGPT-4o, Claude-4 Opus, and Gemini 2.5 Pro achieved scores comparable to or exceeding average student performance, while DeepSeek-V3 consistently underperformed despite maintaining passing scores.

Statistical comparisons

When analyzing text-only items separately (n = 510), significant differences persisted (Q = 109.86, df = 3, p = 1.18 × 10-23). However, for image-based items (n = 132) excluding DeepSeek-V3, no significant differences were detected among the 3 multimodal models (Q = 5.81, df = 2, P = .055).

Performance stratified by image subtype

A total of 132 image-based questions were categorized into 6 visual subtypes (Table 2): radiograph, clinical photograph, schematic illustration, graph, histology and mixed images. Among these, the graph (n = 8) and histology (n = 5) categories contained very few questions and were therefore excluded from the subtype-level comparisons. The mixed images category was defined as questions containing 2 or more image types among clinical photographs, radiographs, and histologic images. Radiographs represented the largest proportion of image-based questions (n = 36) and also constituted the most challenging subtype, with all 3 LLMs showing the lowest accuracy in this category. In contrast, mixed-image items demonstrated the highest mean accuracy (83.3%), followed by clinical photographs (77.8%), indicating that model performance improved when multiple complementary visual cues were available or when interpreting conventional photographic content.

Accuracy of 3 large language models across image subtypes

When accuracy differences across the 4 subtypes (radiograph, clinical photo, schematic illustration, and mixed) were assessed using chi-square tests, subtype-level variability within each model was significant (Claude-4 Opus, P = .040; Gemini 2.5 Pro, P = .005) or borderline significant (ChatGPT-4o, P = .051), confirming that image subtype substantially influenced model performance. However, within each subtype, accuracy differences between the 3 LLMs were not statistically significant (all P > .05). This indicates that although the absolute difficulty of visual subtypes differed markedly, the models performed comparably to one another when interpreting the same type of visual input.

Inter-model agreement

Cohen's kappa analysis demonstrated substantial agreement among models (κ = 0.631-0.778) according to Landis and Koch criteria (Figure 7). The highest agreement was observed between Claude-4 Opus and Gemini 2.5 Pro (κ = 0.778), while DeepSeek-V3 showed the weakest concordance with other models (κ = 0.631-0.637).

Heatmap of Cohen’s kappa coefficients for pairwise agreement among large language models. Values ranged from 0.631 to 0.778, corresponding to substantial agreement (Landis and Koch).

To further characterize the nature of this inter-model concordance, we examined option-level agreement patterns across the 4 LLMs. When 2 models selected the same option, 88% to 94% of these agreements corresponded to the correct answer, indicating that convergence generally reflected clinically valid reasoning rather than spurious alignment.

Convergence across the 4 models was quantified using the Majority Agreement Index (MAI). Across all 642 items, the mean MAI was 0.854 (SD = 0.199), demonstrating a consistently high level of agreement in option selection. Only 9 items (1.4%) exhibited complete divergence (all 4 models selecting different options); of these, 2 involved visual material and the remainder were distributed across various subjects without any thematic clustering, suggesting no identifiable common characteristic.

In contrast, 403 items (62.8%) showed full convergence, with all 4 LLMs selecting the same option. Importantly, 372 of these items (92.3%) were correct according to the key, indicating that unanimous convergence is strongly associated with item difficulty and typically reflects correct clinical reasoning rather than shared erroneous patterns.

Discussion

This study provides the most comprehensive evaluation to date of state-of-the-art LLMs on standardized dental licensing examinations. Our findings demonstrate that contemporary AI models have achieved near-human performance levels in many aspects of dental knowledge assessment, while revealing persistent limitations that have important implications for educational applications.

Advancement in LLM capabilities

The superior performance of ChatGPT-4o, Claude-4 Opus, and Gemini 2.5 Pro compared to previous generations represents a substantial advancement in AI capabilities.19 These models not only exceeded passing thresholds but achieved scores comparable to human examinees, suggesting that LLMs have reached a critical threshold of medical knowledge integration and reasoning capability.

The statistical equivalence between ChatGPT-4o and Claude-4 Opus is particularly noteworthy, as it suggests convergent evolution toward optimal performance levels among leading AI systems. This convergence may indicate approaching theoretical limits for multiple-choice examination performance using current architectures.

Persistent visual processing limitations

Despite significant improvements in multimodal capabilities, all LLMs demonstrated inferior performance on image-based questions compared to text-only items. This limitation aligns with recent findings in radiology and pathology applications, where LLMs have shown promise but remain inferior to specialized computer vision systems.16, 17, 18, 19, 20

The performance gap in Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology specifically highlights the complexity of radiographic interpretation, which requires not only visual recognition but also integration of anatomical knowledge, pattern recognition, and clinical context synthesis. Current LLMs appear to lack the sophisticated visual reasoning capabilities that human clinicians develop through extensive training and experience.

Subject-specific performance patterns

The striking divergence in Oral Biology performance, where LLMs substantially outperformed students, likely reflects the nature of biological knowledge representation in LLM training data. Fundamental biological concepts are extensively represented in scientific literature and educational materials, allowing LLMs to leverage vast knowledge bases effectively.

Conversely, the relative weakness in clinically integrative subjects suggests that LLMs may excel at knowledge retrieval and synthesis but struggle with the contextual reasoning and clinical judgment that characterize expert practice. This finding has important implications for the design of AI-assisted educational tools.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, examination performance may not fully reflect clinical competency, as standardized tests primarily assess knowledge recall and basic reasoning rather than complex clinical decision-making. Second, the publicly available examination images may be of lower quality than those encountered in actual testing conditions, potentially underestimating LLM visual capabilities. Third, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the Korean context of the KDLE. The examination is administered in Korean and reflects Korea-specific subject distribution, item style, and image characteristics. Because LLM performance can vary substantially by language, test design, and cultural context, the accuracy patterns observed here may not be directly applicable to licensing examinations in other countries. In addition, although item-level chat session resets were implemented to minimize carry-over and order effects, residual variability related to latent model updates or deployment-level changes cannot be entirely excluded. In addition, precise internal build identifiers were not accessible via the web-based interface; therefore, despite consistent use of the same user-facing model labels and controlled testing conditions, subtle model drift over time remains a potential source of uncertainty.

Additionally, item-level student response data were not available, as the national licensing authority provides only aggregated, subject-level statistics. Consequently, direct human–AI comparisons at the question level could not be performed, and analyses involving convergence or divergence relative to student performance were not feasible.

Lastly, although the KDLE increasingly incorporates clinical scenarios, radiographs, and decision-making items, examination performance still does not equate to real-world clinical competence. The test cannot fully capture procedural skills, hands-on judgment, or real-time patient management. As real clinical performance metrics were unavailable, correlating LLM performance with actual clinical competence was not feasible in this study.

Future research should focus on evaluating LLM performance in more clinically realistic settings, including case-based decision-making and high-fidelity radiographic interpretation. Systematic investigation of prompt engineering strategies and the practical integration of LLM-assisted tools into real educational or clinical workflows will also be important. Further validation across diverse national licensing examinations will be essential to determine the extent to which these findings generalize beyond the Korean examination system.

Conclusion

State-of-the-art LLMs demonstrate remarkable progress in dental knowledge assessment, with 3 of 4 models achieving performance levels comparable to human examinees on the KDLE. ChatGPT-4o, Claude-4 Opus, and Gemini 2.5 Pro showed particular strength in knowledge-based subjects, while all models demonstrated persistent limitations in visual interpretation tasks.

These findings suggest that LLMs have reached a maturity level suitable for carefully supervised educational applications. However, their limitations in image-based reasoning and clinical integration underscore the continued necessity of human expertise in dental education and practice. Future development should focus on addressing these limitations while leveraging the demonstrated strengths of current systems.

The rapid advancement in LLM capabilities necessitates ongoing evaluation and thoughtful integration strategies to realize the potential benefits while mitigating risks associated with overreliance on AI systems in clinical contexts.
