Epigenetic and transcriptomic analysis revealed ceRNA regulatory network in glioblastoma multiforme
Authors: Zhenghui Li, Yanqing Li, Jenna Z Liang, Tianxiang Geng, Jing Chen, Yangyang Yuan, Yufang Sui, Wanhong Zhang, Qian Chen, Guowei Tan, Qiao Shan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is one of the most common cancers among malignant tumors, and extensive evidence suggests that the prognosis of various human cancers is associated with DNA methylation. Therefore, we analyzed potential GBM prognostic biomarkers by combining GBM methylation sequencing data and transcriptome sequencing data. Biostatistical analysis of the expression of three RNA types (long non-coding RNA, microRNA, and mRNA) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) was performed to determine the correlation between NACAD and GBM prognosis. Additionally, we predicted and validated a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory network associated with epigenetic DNA methylation modifications. The ceRNA regulatory network (DCTN1-AS1–hsa-let-7a-5p–NACAD) was found to be related to overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) of GBM. Furthermore, we identified NACAD as an independent prognostic factor for both GBM OS and DSS. In summary, comprehensive analysis of methylation and transcriptome data led to the identification of genes and pathways in different GBM subtypes, highlighting prognostic genes and ceRNA regulatory networks, and proposing potential candidates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03020-5.

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme, often known as GBM, is a primary brain tumor that develops from glial cells and is extremely cancerous. It is the primary brain tumor that accounts for more than half of all malignant primary brain tumors and accounts for 15% of all brain tumors. It is the most frequent primary brain tumor [1]. GBM is one of the most serious types of brain cancer because of its notoriously aggressive character and its capacity to rapidly penetrate the brain tissue that is in its immediate vicinity. Surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy are all potential treatments for GBM; nevertheless, the general prognosis is not good due to the inherent resistance of GBM to standard treatments and its position within the brain [2]. Patients diagnosed with GBM have an expected survival time of roughly 15 months on average [3].

Research on GBM has recently made strides, leading to the development of innovative therapies such immunotherapy and targeted therapy [4]. Via the targeting of particular molecules or pathways involved in the growth and spread of the tumor, these medicines seek to improve the prognosis for GBM patients. Moreover, more precise radiation therapy methods have been developed, such as stereotactic radiosurgery, which may help GBM patients have a better prognosis [5].

Although the pathophysiology of GBM is not fully understood, it is generally agreed that DNA methylation plays a significant part in the progression of this condition [6, 7]. The formation and progression of glioblastoma multiforme are greatly influenced by DNA methylation, a procedure in which methyl groups are introduced to the DNA molecule. The silence of tumor suppressor genes and the activation of oncogenes are caused by global hypomethylation and gene-specific hypermethylation, respectively, in GBM, according to studies [8, 9]. The promoter region of the O6-methylguanine DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) gene, which is in charge of repairing alkylating agent-induced DNA damage, is one of the best-studied instances of gene-specific hypermethylation in GBM [10, 11]. A potential biomarker for the response to therapy is the hypermethylation of the MGMT promoter, which silences the gene and increases sensitivity to alkylating drugs like temozolomide [12].

In addition to hypermethylation, GBM is also characterized by global hypomethylation which leads to genomic instability, chromosomal abnormalities, and increased expression of oncogenes [13]. Recent studies have shown that DNA methylation inhibitors such as 5-aza-2′-deoxycytidine (5-aza-dC) can reactivate silenced genes, leading to the inhibition of GBM cell growth, migration, and invasion [14, 15].

In this study, a multi-omics approach was employed to identify potential prognostic markers in Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). The GBM transcriptome sequencing data and methylation data from the open GEO sequencing data platform were utilized, in conjunction with public GBM patient prognosis and survival information, to perform a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis. The integration of this multi-omics data allows for a holistic retrieval of transcriptional and epigenetic information. The results of the computational analysis were subsequently validated using multiple cell lines.

Method

Data source

A dataset comprising gene expression RNA sequencing data, DNA methylation data, and stem loop (miRNA) expression from cancer tissue was obtained from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) for the Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) sample set (n = 166; project ID: TCGA-GBM). As a control, normal brain transcriptome sequencing data from the GTEx database (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/) were utilized to represent normal tissue data (n = 408; GTEx V8). Data in TPM format were used in this analysis. GBM or normal brain single-cell RNA-seq data (GSE141946 [16] and GSE162631 [17]) was analyzed in this investigation. Raw read-count matrices from TCGA-GBM (tumor) and GTEx (normal brain) were merged and corrected with ComBat-seq (sva v3.46), specifying study (TCGA or GTEx) as the batch variable and condition (tumor or normal) as the biological covariate, log2 (TPM + 0.001); data were then analyzed with DESeq2 and GSVA and ESTIMATE scoring. All single-cell processing followed the canonical Seurat workflow without combination. No custom or unpublished R packages were used at any step.

Methylation and transcriptome data processing

R (4.2.1) packages ChAMP [18] and limma [19] were utilized to identify differentially expressed methylation probes (DMPs) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs), respectively. We processed the 450 K methylation array data using the ChAMP package (v2.24.0), with its default filtering pipeline enabled. This includes removal of non-CpG probes, exclusion of probes overlapping known SNPs (minor allele frequency ≥ 0.01), filtering of cross-reactive probes, exclusion of probes located on sex chromosomes (X/Y), and removal of probes with > 1% detection failure across samples. These filtering steps are standard in array methylation studies to ensure analytical reliability. The criteria of adj.p < 0.05 and |deltaBeta|≥ 0.2 for DMPs and adj.p < 0.05 and |logFC|≥ 1.7 for DEGs were adopted. The correlation between DEGs and their corresponding DMPs was determined using a Spearman correlation analysis, with a significance level of adj.p < 0.05. The relationship between gene expression of key genes and their DNA methylation status was analyzed using MEXPRESS (https://mexpress.be).

Differentially expressed analyses and enrichment analyses

The statistical package DESeq2 [20] (1.36.0), utilizing the programming language R version 4.2.1, was utilized to conduct a differential expression analysis of genes between cancerous and normal tissue samples. Additionally, the ClusterProfiler package version 3.14.3 [21], also implemented in R version 3.6.3, was employed to perform Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses. In order to identify enrichment of biological pathways, a gene set enrichment analysis was performed on ranked lists of differentially expressed genes using the GSEA software. Significance was determined using the false discovery rate, with a threshold of < 0.25, and the p value was adjusted to < 0.05. TPM-normalized expression data were log-transformed and fed into the ESTIMATE R package (v1.0.13).

Establishment of the ceRNA network in GBM

The miRNA/mRNA predicted to target the indicator gene by multiple algorithms is considered the target RNA. In this study, the lncRNAs potentially targeting miRNAs were predicted using LncBase Predicted version 3 (https://diana.e-ce.uth.gr/lncbasev3). The prediction of mRNAs targeted by differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) was conducted using the miRWalk database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/). The prediction results were then intersected with those obtained from TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/). Additionally, the miRanda R package version 3.3a was utilized to detect base pairing between lncRNA–miRNA and miRNA–mRNA. The base sequence information for lncRNA and mRNA was obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore), while miRNA base sequence information was sourced from miRBase (https://www.mirbase.org/). A protein interaction analysis was subsequently performed using the String database (http://string-db.org).

Immune infiltrate levels related to ceRNA network

The R package GSVA version 1.34.0 [22] was utilized to conduct an expression analysis of RNA sequencing data from patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) for tumor-infiltrating cells and the expression of the competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network. The identified tumor-infiltrating cells included activated dendritic cells (aDC), B cells, CD8 T cells, cytotoxic cells, dendritic cells (DC), eosinophils, immature dendritic cells (iDC), macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, natural killer (NK) CD56bright cells, NK CD56dim cells, NK cells, plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDC), T cells, T helper cells, T central memory cells (Tcm), T effector memory cells (Tem), T follicular helper cells (Tfh), T gamma delta cells (Tgd), Th1 cells, Th17 cells, Th2 cells, and regulatory T cells (Treg). The cell annotation was performed by cross-referencing with the study by Bindea et al. additionally, the correlation between immune cells infiltrating the tumor tissue and the level of ceRNA was analyzed.

Preparation of single-cell sequencing data and annotation for dimensionality reduction

The Seurat package version 4.2.0 [23] was utilized to conduct the analysis of single-cell sequencing data. A screening process was implemented to identify cells that expressed more than 200 genes but no more than 5000 genes, and a threshold of greater than 10% of mitochondrial genes was applied for further analysis. After normalization and scaling, principal-component analysis (PCA) was performed and the top PCs were used for Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embedding. Cell-type annotation was carried out with the SingleR package (v1.12.1, Bioconductor) in cluster mode. As reference we employed the BlueprintEncodeData panel.

Human samples and immunohistochemistry

The pathological sections of patients with glioblastoma who underwent surgery at the Neurosurgery Department of Kaifeng Central Hospital from 2020 to 2022 were selected and were simultaneously reviewed by two attending pathologists. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval no. 2020LL012-KFZXYY). Cortical brain tissue from patients with cerebral hemorrhage-induced fistula was chosen as the control group, and after obtaining informed consent, the specimens were sent to the Pathology Department for embedding and sectioningPublic IHC images were retrieved from the Human Protein Atlas. The NACAD antibody used was HPA020981 (Atlas Antibodies, dilution 1:100).

Human surgical pathology specimens were deparaffinized, rehydrated, permeabilized (0.2% Triton X–100 in PBS), and incubated in blocking solution (5% donkey serum in PBS) for 1 h. Specimens were then incubated (4 °C, overnight) with blocking solution containing primary antibodies targeting NACAD (SC-515113, Santa Cruz Biotech, 1:100). Specimens were washed (PBS) and incubated with anti-mouse (Alexa 488, donkey). After washing (PBS), specimens were mounted using prolong anti-fade reagents containing DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

All core statistical analyses were performed in R (v 4.2.1) under Ubuntu 20.04. A subset of scripts that rely on legacy packages (e.g., clusterProfiler v3.14.3 and igraph v1.2.4) required R (v 3.6.3) because these versions could not be compiled under R ≥ 4.0 on our system. For calculating the differences between the two groups of data, Mann–Whitney U tests (Wilcoxon rank sum tests) and independent t-tests were used. For the assessment of differences between groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Kruskal–Wallis test as well as chi-square test were utilized. For the purpose of correlation analysis, non-parametric tests of correlation were used (Spearman). Cox regressions were performed on candidate genes to analyze the relationship with overall survival (OS). Raw p values were adjusted for multiple testing with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (p.adjust, method = “BH” in R). Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF) calculated with the car package (v3.1–2). Variables with VIF ≥ 5 were considered highly collinear. A statistically significant difference could be implied by p = 0.05. (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).

Result

GBM methylation and correlation analysis

We analyzed differential DNA methylation sites using GBM DNA methylation sequencing data (Fig. 1a). The sites with differential DNA methylation were subsequently mapped, and 709 genes with DNA methylation events were confirmed to occur in GBM. Functional enrichment analysis was performed on these genes (Fig. 1b). It was observed that functions such as main axon and cell–cell junction were positively enriched, while functions such as collagen-containing extracellular matrix and response to mechanical stimulus were downregulated in terms of functional enrichment. In the analysis of the correlation between DNA methylation sites and gene expression levels, we found that 295 genes had statistically significant correlation. Three key genes, CCDC177, PLEKHN1, and NACAD, were ultimately identified using a Venn diagram (Fig. 1c). It was observed that these genes showed significant differential expression in GBM tumor tissue compared to normal brain tissue, and this differential expression was associated with DNA methylation. We detected the expression of NACAD in different glioma cell lines and found that the expression of glioma cell lines was significantly downregulated compared with control cell lines (Fig. 1d).

The mRNA levels of the three genes in tumor and normal tissues were displayed (Fig. 1d): we found that both CCDC177 and NACAD were highly expressed and significantly decreased in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues; PLEKHN1 was expressed at higher levels in tumor tissues than in normal tissues, but at very low levels. Therefore, we stained the pathological tissues of GBM patients with NACAD antibody to study the expression of NACAD in glioblastoma tissues. Consistent with the bioinformatics results, NACAD expression showed significant downregulation in GBM compared with the control group (Fig. 1e). NACAD was selected for further analysis.

Regarding the changes in methylation sites of NACAD, we utilized MEXPRESS to visualize the methylation correlation of multiple sites in NACAD.

GBM single gene transcriptome expression differential analysis

Further analysis was conducted using the clinical data of TCGA-GBM, which revealed that only the level of NACAD differed among patients with different survival outcomes, while the levels of CCDC177 and PLEKHN1 may not be associated with the survival of GBM patients (Fig. 2a). Subsequently, we focused on NACAD and analyzed the correlation between its expression level and that of other genes in GBM samples (Fig. 2b). We selected the top ten genes with positive and negative correlation and presented them in a heatmap. To better understand the role of NACAD in GBM, the expression differences in transcriptome sequencing data between patients with high and low NACAD levels were also analyzed. Based on the differential analysis results, we conducted functional enrichment analysis.

Enrichment analysis using GSEA revealed that the functional differences in genes between high and low NACAD expression groups were mainly clustered in the top of the ranked list, including reactome neurotransmitter receptors and postsynaptic signal transmission, WP synaptic vesicle pathway, reactome protein–protein interactions at synapses, reactome transmission across chemical synapses, and reactome neuronal system. Conversely, KEGG ribosome, reactome binding and uptake of ligands by scavenger receptors, reactome eukaryotic translation elongation, reactome Srp-dependent cotranslational protein targeting to membrane, and reactome scavenging of heme from plasma were predominantly clustered at the bottom of the ranked list (Fig. 2c, d).

Additionally, the results of ORA analysis (GO/KEGG/Reactome) showed that the function of immunoglobulin complex was downregulated, while integral component of postsynaptic membrane, intrinsic component of postsynaptic membrane, and postsynaptic membrane were all downregulated.

Survival analysis of NACAD

Combined with survival information of TCGA-GBM patients, we conducted further analysis on NACAD. Survival curves based on logrank test revealed the relationship between NACAD and overall survival (OS), Disease-Specific Survival (DSS), and Progress-Free Interval (PFI). Patients with low levels of NACAD in tumor tissues had longer OS and DSS (Fig. 3a, b), but no significant statistical correlation was found in PFI (Fig. 3c).

Prognosis analysis of single gene NACAD. A, B, C. Survival analysis (OS, DSS, PFI) between high and low subgroups of NACAD expression median subgroups. D, E. Prognostic array analysis (OS, DSS) between high and low subgroups of median NACAD expression

We first performed univariate Cox proportional hazards analyses for NACAD expression and each candidate ceRNA component (lncRNAs and miRNAs). Variables with p < 0.05 were retained. A multivariate Cox model was then fitted in the TCGA-GBM cohort (n = 158 patients with complete clinical data) using the survival package (v3.5-7). Schoenfeld residual test showed no violation (all p > 0.10). Variance inflation factors ranged from 1.07 to 2.34, indicating no problematic collinearity. In the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, we treated NACAD mRNA and let-7a-5p miRNA expression (both log₂-transformed) as continuous covariates of primary interest. To adjust for established prognostic factors in glioblastoma, we additionally included six clinical variables: the patient’s age at diagnosis (continuous), sex (male or female), IDH1/2 mutation status (mutant vs. wild-type), MGMT promoter methylation status (methylated vs. unmethylated), and the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score (continuous). This set of covariates was chosen to capture demographic influences, key molecular subtypes, treatment-response markers, and functional status, thereby ensuring a robust assessment of the independent prognostic value of NACAD and let-7a-5p.

We found that NACAD and IDH mutation status were common factors influencing the overall survival of GBM patients. All covariates had VIF < 3, confirming the absence of multicollinearity. Higher levels of NACAD and IDH mutation in tumor tissues were associated with poorer long-term overall survival (Table 1). Age, IDH mutation, and KPS also remained significant (Table 2). Harrell’s C-index = 0.73, and calibration plots demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed survival. Similar findings were also found in the DSS indicator (Table 2). However, NACAD did not show any relationship with PFI (Table S1). Based on the multiple factor regression analysis, we used the Nomogram method to integrate multiple predictive indicators and draw them on the same plane according to a certain proportion, in order to express the relationship between the predictive variables in the prediction model. In the analysis of overall survival and DSS, NACAD and IDH mutation status showed strong influence.

Immune infiltration of single gene analysis

In this study, we used the GSVA algorithm to analyze the infiltration levels of aDC, B cell, CD8 T cell, cytotoxic cell, DC, iDC, T helper cells, T cell, and TFH in high and low levels of NACAD. The results showed significant differences in infiltration levels for these immune cell types between high and low NACAD levels. Specifically, aDC, B cell, CD8 T cell, cytotoxic cell, DC, iDC, T helper cells, and T cell showed lower infiltration levels in high NACAD cancer tissue samples, while TFH had higher infiltration levels (Fig. 4a).

Analysis on NACAD single gene level and immune infiltration. A. Differences in immune infiltrating cell types between high and low NACAD expression groups. B–L Correlation analysis of immune infiltrating cell type and NAVAD expression between high and low groups of NACAD expression median grouping. M. Differences in immune infiltration fraction between high and low subgroups of median NACAD expression grouping

We also analyzed the correlation between NACAD molecular levels and the infiltration levels of all immune cells. The infiltration levels of aDC, B cell, CD8 T cell, cytotoxic cell, DC, iDC, T helper cells, T cell, and TFH were all significantly correlated with NACAD levels, with cytotoxic cell and T cell infiltration levels showing a higher correlation with NACAD molecular levels. Similar to the infiltration level results for immune cells in the high and low NACAD groups, aDC, B cell, CD8 T cell, cytotoxic cell, DC, iDC, T helper cells, and T cell were negatively correlated with NACAD mRNA levels in cancer tissue samples, while TFH showed a positive correlation (Fig. 4b, l).

We also evaluated three immune scores, including StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore. Consistent with the trend of immune cell infiltration in high and low NACAD tumor tissues, all three immune scores were lower in high NACAD tissue samples (Fig. 4m).

ceRNA regulatory network construction and verification

Using miRWalk and LncBase Predicted version 3, we screened for the NACAD-related ceRNA regulatory network from the results of differential expression, and validated it using TargetScan. Ultimately, we identified a potential ceRNA regulatory network consisting of DCTN1-AS1–hsa-let-7a-5p–NACAD and drew a diagram showing their base-pairing relationships (Fig. 5a). We further validated the mRNA expression levels of DCTN1-AS1 and NACAD in tumor tissues using TCGA database and found that both genes were downregulated (Fig. 5b). Of the 12 computationally predicted NACAD-targeting miRNAs, only let-7a-5p satisfied the combined criteria of differential expression, inverse correlation and prognostic significance; therefore, it was retained in the final DCTN1-AS1/let-7a-5p/NACAD network.

Analysis of ceRNA regulatory networks. A. Base complementary pairing of key ceRNA regulatory networks. B. Expression levels of NACAD and DCTN1-AS1 in GBM and normal brain tissues. C, D. Immunohistochemical staining of NACAD in normal and GBM tissues (https://www.proteinatlas.org/)

To confirm the expression location of NACAD, we used single-cell sequencing data for dimensionality reduction clustering annotation. The expression of NACAD was shown to be in glial cells in both normal brain tissue and GBM tumor tissue (Fig. 5c, d), which is consistent with our results from tissue immunohistochemical staining in the Protein Atlas.

Discussion and conclusion

Our analysis revealed the association between NACAD and GBM prognosis, with NACAD mRNA levels in GBM tissue being related to OS and DSS. By analyzing sequencing information between high and low NACAD levels in GBM patients, we identified a potential ceRNA three-level regulatory network: DCTN1-AS1–hsa-let-7a-5p–NACAD. Additionally, NACAD and IDH mutation status may be independent prognostic factors for GBM. In this study, we first investigated the changes in NACAD expression in local tissue. Compared with normal brain tissue, NACAD levels were reduced in tumor tissue. We also identified the cellular localization of NACAD in brain tissue, with NACAD expressed in neuronal cells and not in epithelial cell types. Claudia et al. [24] found that the NACAD gene undergoes mutations in the development of prostate cancer.

Study has shown that NACAD deficiency may be associated with cerebral cavernous malformation [25]. The decrease in vascular density (inhibiting angiogenesis and vascular pruning) creates hypoxic areas where a subset of CD8 cells preferentially localize, consistent with reports that hypoxia may activate T cells [26–28]. In our analysis, the increase in TFH levels may be related to T cell activation. Furthermore, the absence of NACAD can detect CD8-positive T cell responses [29], which is inconsistent with our analysis. Studies have shown that hypoxia can create an immunosuppressive microenvironment to suppress the function of other T cells [30]. This may be related to the decreased level of immune infiltration of T cells in GBM patients with high levels of NACAD. Immune cell infiltration in tumor tissue is closely related to tumor prognosis [31]. In GBM, our immune infiltration analysis found that the levels of NACAD were negatively correlated with aDC, B cell, CD8 T cell, cytotoxic cell, DC, iDC, T helper cells, and T cell infiltration, and positively correlated with TFH infiltration. GESA functional clustering analysis revealed a negative correlation between NACAD and the degree of immune cell factors, which may imply a more direct regulatory relationship between NACAD and immune infiltration in GBM tissues. The lower level of immune cell infiltration in GBM tissues with high levels of NACAD may be one of the reasons why tumor patients with high levels of NACAD often have a shorter OS.

Isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) enzymes are essential enzymes involved in several major metabolic processes [32], and IDH mutations are common in human malignant tumors. IDH mutations occur in 73% of secondary WHO grade IV glioblastoma (GBM) cases, but only 3.7% of initial cases [33]. When predicting the prognosis of GBM, IDH mutation is recognized as one of the characteristic diagnostic factors of GBM [34]. Our analysis results demonstrate that the level of NACAD and whether IDH mutation occurs are independent prognostic factors for overall survival and disease-specific survival in GBM.

The ceRNA regulatory network is involved in the occurrence and development of many human cancers [35]. In this study, we attempted to establish a ceRNA network related to NACAD in GBM and link it to GBM diagnosis or prognosis. An effective regulatory pathway, DCTN1-AS1–hsa-let-7a-5p–NACAD, was identified based on sequence alignment verification. DCTN1-AS1 was previously reported to be less associated with cancer, only found to affect the prognosis of breast cancer in a breast cancer study [36]. In another study on Alzheimer’s disease, DCTN1-AS1 was identified as a key lncRNA for Alzheimer’s disease onset [37].

However, there are still caveats to consider despite the promise of the ceRNA-based DCTN1-AS1/hsa-let-7a-5p axis in the diagnosis and prediction of OS and DSS of NACAD. Analysis-predicted lncRNA, miRNA, and mRNA binding affinities must first be experimentally verified. Second, more experimental research is necessary to determine the role and mechanism of the DCTN1-AS1/NACAD axis in GBM.

A network of ceRNAs (DCTN1-AS1–hsa-let-7a-5p–NACAD) and an independent prognostic variable related to the prediction of OS and DSS in GBM, NACAD, were shown in this meta-analysis. Future studies will determine whether this network may be employed as a prognostic model to investigate the pathophysiology of GBM and as a treatment target for GBM.
