Latitudinal manganese gradient dynamics associated with Earth’s major ice ages
Authors: Xubin Wang, Alexandre Pohl, Rosalind E M Rickaby, Zunli Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistinct sedimentary deposits of manganese are known to be associated with extreme glaciations in ancient environments. The redox-sensitive nature of manganese led scientists to propose theories about how oxygen and climate collaborated to cause major perturbations in the global geochemical cycle of this important metal. Temporal analyses of a large sedimentary dataset demonstrate high manganese concentrations in marine sediments were commonly associated with oxygenation events and glaciations in deep time. Here we show spatial analyses of those intervals that reveal a common pattern of relative enrichment of manganese in sediments at high latitudes during the six greatest glaciations of Earth’s history. The pattern of relatively lower manganese concentrations in tropical sediments was most likely driven by ocean oxygen gradients. This manganese gradient increasingly flattened as the oxygen level rose in the atmosphere and ocean over the geological time scale. Our analyses suggest that anoxia frequently occurred during icehouse conditions, challenging the conventional wisdom of warming-driven ocean deoxygenation.Subject terms: Marine chemistry, Element cycles, Palaeoclimate

Introduction

The geologic Mn deposition1 is fundamentally controlled by its solubility in oxygen-poor (anoxic) water as reduced Mn2+ and its rapid precipitation as insoluble Mn oxides when exposed to oxygen2,3. In Earth’s early anoxic oceans (e.g., during the Archean), dissolved Mn could accumulate in vast quantities for millions of years, turning into a manganese-rich reservoir4,5. Oxygenation events, such as the Great Oxidation Event (GOE), could then trigger the formation of massive manganese deposits5–7. In today’s oxygenated deep oceans, Mn oxides precipitate slowly and continuously from seawater, forming polymetallic nodules and crusts on open ocean seafloors rather than distinct sedimentary layers on continental margins as preserved in the ancient geologic record8,9.

Major glaciations of the Proterozoic known as “Snowball Earth” glaciations are linked to Mn deposits, e.g., post-Huronian Kalahari Manganese Field in South Africa10–12 and post-Sturtian Datangpo-type Mn ore in South China9,13. The conceptual model frequently cited in the literature is that ice cover severed the ocean’s contact with the atmosphere, resulting in oxygen loss to build a dissolved Mn pool, which then rapidly precipitated with “cap carbonates” during the deglaciation through ocean re-ventilation14–16. The Pleistocene glacial-interglacial cycles also repeatedly show clear signals of deglacial Mn peaks in sediments tightly linked to increases in seawater [O2] recovering from the deep water deoxygenation during the glacial maximum17. These observations imply that marine redox oscillations associated with climate and ocean circulation changes might have been an important mechanism of sedimentary Mn enrichment. Here we study sedimentary Mn data from DM-SED (Deep-Time Marine Sedimentary Element Database, https://geobiologydata.cug.edu.cn/)18 and cap-carbonate Mn concentrations compiled separately in this study (Supplement Note 1). Temporal analyses were used to examine the timing of high sedimentary [Mn] relative to major glaciations. Spatial analyses allow further investigations into marine redox and climate as potential controls of Mn enrichment. We show that the latitudinal Mn gradient (LMG) most likely reflects the oceanic oxygen gradient, based on redox-sensitive metal concentrations and Earth system model (cGENIE) simulations19. The LMG became subdued over time, largely driven by the rise of atmospheric oxygen and the reduction in the dissolved Mn pool.

Results and discussion

Temporal analyses

The DM-SED database is primarily sourced from the Sedimentary Geochemistry and Paleoenvironments Project (SGP) database20,21, and supplemented with additional new entries, spanning approximately 3,800 Myr and including data points from North America, Europe, Asia, Africa, South America, and Oceania18. Uncertainties of age in the database have been noted in previous studies18,22, which were considered acceptable on timescales longer than 10 Myr in the Neoproterozoic and 5 Myr in the Paleozoic in SGP. The data availability allows temporal analyses of six major glaciations: the Paleoproterozoic Huronian glaciation11, two Cryogenian Snowball Earth events (Sturtian and Marinoan)23, the late Ediacaran ice age (or Gaskiers)24, the Hirnantian (or Ordovician) glaciation25, and the late Paleozoic Ice Age (LPIA)26 (Fig. 1). We used three methods to identify intervals of high sedimentary [Mn] for comparison with glaciations: LOWESS regression, bootstrapping and PAAS filtering (See “Methods”). These analyses were also applied to Mn concentrations separated by lithology to examine the trends in carbonate and shale samples.

The LOWESS regression provides a broad scan of potential Mn enrichment intervals on all raw data. To account for uneven temporal sampling density (Figs. S1, S2), the raw Mn concentration data were binned by time intervals to generate bootstrapped temporal trends. Large numbers of low [Mn] data points in the dataset may also influence the result of temporal analyses. To address this issue, sedimentary [Mn] lower than the post-Archean Average Australian Shale (PAAS) value27 were removed, and the rest of the data were binned by time, and the 75th percentile values of each time bin were plotted as ‘PAAS-filtered’. Mn enrichment time intervals identified by at least two temporal analyses were marked by arrows in Fig. 1. New compilations of cap carbonate Mn concentrations were bootstrapped and plotted as box-plots after three Neoproterozoic glaciations.

The highest sedimentary [Mn] in this dataset was found during the interval of post- Huronian glaciation (red dot in Fig. 1a), an order of magnitude above high concentrations found in other Archean-Paleoproterozoic intervals. Both the existing database and new cap carbonate compilations show high Mn concentrations associated with the Sturtian and Marinoan Snowball Earth. For the Gaskiers glaciation, the cap carbonate compilation and PAAS filtering showed high sedimentary [Mn], whereas no clear enrichment can be seen in the LOWESS regression and bootstrapped time series. High sedimentary [Mn] is clearly associated with the peak to deglacial phase of the late Paleozoic Ice Age (LPIA). The Hirnantian temporal trend for “All samples” is similar to that of the LPIA, while the “Carbonate” Mn peaked at the glacial maximum, and “Shale” Mn showed sporadic peaks before and after the glacial maximum. Intervals of high [Mn] were also found during non-glacial periods: 3000-1800 Ma, 520-460 Ma, and 400-385 Ma.

Latitudinal Manganese Gradient

Latitudinal gradients of the redox-sensitive elemental proxy data (I/Ca) have recently been suggested to record the upper-ocean dissolved oxygen gradient, the evolution of which is controlled by atmospheric oxygen levels28. To better understand the controls of marine Mn enrichments, we examine the Latitudinal Mn Gradient (LMG) of six glaciations, focusing on the Neoproterozoic and Paleozoic due to data coverage. Mncarb is notably enriched in high-latitude cap carbonates compared with tropical regions (Fig. 2a–f). Associated LMGs are distinctively steeper in the northern hemisphere and possibly flat in the southern hemisphere according to the available data (Fig. 2a–d). Such an LMG pattern is visible in different plate reconstruction models (Figs. S3, S4). The Gaskiers data show a steep gradient in the southern hemisphere (Figs. 2e, f, S4). These concave-up patterns are verified by bootstrap and equal-count subsampling (“Methods”), with consistently positive LMG values (Fig. S5).

Positive LMGs are also observed in two Paleozoic glaciations, although the 75th percentiles of latitudinal [Mn] bins (often <1000 ppm) are lower than those in Neoproterozoic cap-carbonates (Fig. 2g–j). The LPIA LMG (ca. 310–270 Ma) shows a symmetrical concave-up pattern but is less steep compared to cap carbonates (Fig. 2i, j). The Hirnantian LMG during peak Mn deposition (ca. 450–440 Ma, Fig. 2g, h) shows a concave-down pattern centered at ~30° S (Fig. 2g, h). The sampling locations from >30° S likely reflect a longitudinal redox gradient across different ocean basins (Fig. S7, Supplementary Note 2), representing potential sampling bias. Excluding data from >30° S, the Hirnantian LMG is comparable to the LPIA LMG. These two Paleozoic glaciations have sufficient sampling sites for additional scrutiny of spatial sampling bias. The Icosahedral Snyder Equal Area (ISEA) gridding method allows Mn concentrations at nearby locations to be grouped together. 75th percentile values of each ISEA grid were then plotted across latitudes for LMG calculations. The LMGs from simple fitting, bootstrapping, and ISEA gridding are consistent with each other (Figs. 2, S6).

Redox control on the LMG

Mn oxide formation and deposition are generally accepted as the main control of high sedimentary [Mn], i.e., redox-dependent. Primary Mn oxide minerals were identified as residues inside/near Mn carbonate minerals in the early Paleoproterozoic Koegas Subgroup and Hotazel Formation in South Africa6,29, and Permian Zunyi-Xialei manganese ore30. The presence of Mn(III) silicate and oxide also highlights the appearance of Mn(II) oxidation and Mn(IV) reduction since Mn(III) is a crucial intermediate during Mn(II)-Mn(IV) valence change30. Although high sedimentary [Mn] is also recorded in carbonates (Fig. 1), several lines of evidence point toward Mn-rich carbonates reflecting the secondary transformation of oxides. In anoxic sediments, Mn oxides are degraded by manganese-reducing microbes and converted to Mn carbonates in black shales. Mn oxide precursors were suggested for such settings based on Mo-U covariation31, carbonate carbon isotopes32,33, cerium anomaly34,35, thallium isotopes36, Mo isotopes37,38, and micro-texture of Mn-carbonate grains39,40. Dissolved oxygen as a requisite for Mn deposits was also affirmed in Mesoproterozoic41, Ediacaran31, and Carboniferous42 Mn carbonates.

Redox-sensitive trace metals provide independent evidence for a redox control on LMGs. Several redox-sensitive trace metals (Mo, U, V) are well-known to enrich in shales under anoxic bottom waters43–45, in contrast to the inference that Mn oxides precipitate in oxygenated waters. These trace metals would be expected to show latitudinal gradients opposite to the LMG. Since it is hard to pinpoint global shale samples coeval with Snowball Earth cap carbonates, we turned our attention to the Hirnantian and LPIA to test the hypothesis that Mn enrichment has a strong connection with ocean redox (Fig. 3). Indeed, shale trace metal enrichment factors (TMEF of Mo-U-V compared to PAAS values) show the latitudinal gradients exactly opposite to the shale-derived LMGs, with a trough around 30–50°S for the Hirnantian and a peak around the equator for the LPIA (Figs. 3a–c, e–g), which points to a redox driver of LMGs and therefore supports the hypothesis that these Mn enrichments are most likely driven by Mn oxides and the presence of oxygenated waters.

The redox conditions associated with the high Mn concentrations can be further examined by cross-plotting MoEF and UEF (Fig. 4). Mn enrichments associated with both Paleozoic glaciations are largely tied to relatively oxic environments and particulate shuttle. Both Mo and U are soluble in oxic conditions and would precipitate in anoxic-euxinic conditions43. The soluble oxidized form of Mo (molybdate, MoO42−) can adsorb to Mn (oxy)(hydr)oxides at the chemocline during particulate transit through the water column, accounting for more than 40% of Mo outflux in modern oceans46,47. By contrast, U is not known to be influenced by a particulate oxide shuttle in the water column, and this difference in behavior allows analyses of Mo-U enrichment pathways in marine sediments43. Low Mn concentrations in the database are more frequently associated with suboxic and sulfidic settings (Fig. 4). These observations support that high sedimentary [Mn] were most likely tied to oxygenated waters (Fig. 1).

A redox control of LMGs is also supported by the comparison of reconstructed LMGs with simulated [O2] gradients. We produced reconstructions of the redox landscape at 445 Ma and during the LPIA (based on simulations for 350 Ma and 300 Ma) using the Earth system model cGENIE19 (See “Methods”). cGENIE simulations tentatively representing the glacial conditions are shown for each event. Modeled [O2] from coastal model grid cells is plotted by water depths to visualize the oxygenation conditions most relevant to the LMGs (Fig. 3d, h). The LMGs for all glaciations show increases in sedimentary Mn concentrations from the equator to at least ~30° or even higher latitudes (Fig. 3). This latitudinal pattern correlates with low upper-ocean [O2] in the tropics simulated for the glaciations (represented at 128 m water depth in Fig. 3d, h), suggesting the key role of equatorial oxygen minimum zones (OMZs) in driving LMGs.

A recent study showed that OMZ-like upper-ocean [O2] gradients are a common feature regardless of continental configuration and climate conditions, as long as atmospheric O2 is above a certain threshold28. This [O2] gradient reflects the combination of biological and physical processes –mostly the increase in O2 solubility in colder waters at higher latitudes and the oxygen consumption at low latitudes related to the remineralization of organic matter produced in upwelling areas. The deep-ocean [O2] gradient (represented at ~3900 m in Fig. 3d, h) is very different, being mostly controlled by the patterns and intensities of deep-ocean ventilation, and consequently does resemble reconstructed LMGs. This suggests that LMGs are more likely to reflect upper-ocean redox conditions.

Dissolved Mn pool

Forming Mn oxides in the water column requires both dissolved oxygen (DO) and dissolved Mn (dMn). The modern open oceans are well-oxygenated compared to the early Paleozoic and Proterozoic oceans. dMn concentrations in today’s open ocean waters, even in OMZs, are mostly below 0.1 μmol/l (Fig. S8), unlikely to represent the dMn pools in early oceans, which were characterized by a strong Mn enrichment. dMn concentrations in modern Baltic Sea, Black Sea, and Cariaco Basin can reach orders of magnitude higher than the maximum dMn concentrations in open-ocean waters (Fig. S8). The Baltic Sea is well-known for the exceptional occurrence of sedimentary Mn carbonate, related to episodic oxygen inflows flushing into the anoxic deep basins, a potential analogue for the formation of ancient Mn deposits48,49. Statistical analyses of the available dMn and DO data show that the highest dMn values are largely associated with DO < 5 μmol/kg, whereas substantial dMn accumulation could occur at DO < 100 μmol/kg with a second step increase in dMn for DO < 50 μmol/kg (Fig. S8). These DO thresholds are not meant to be used for quantitative paleo-reconstructions; nevertheless, they offer a way of comparing the LMGs with Earth system model results.

Evidence shows that anoxia was widespread during Snowball Earth glaciations50–52, allowing the buildup of the dMn pool. Greenalite records of seawater Mn also reveal dMn concentrations in the Proterozoic oceans potentially orders of magnitude higher than modern53. Meridional overturning circulations simulated for Snowball Earth conditions showed that the Northern Hemisphere upper-intermediate waters were poorly ventilated, hence more likely to be anoxic compared to their counterparts in the Southern Hemisphere54,55. Uneven spatial distribution of anoxia may have led to high dMn available for water-column Mn oxide precipitation, providing a potential explanation for the asymmetrical LMG with stronger Mn enrichment in the northern hemisphere (Fig. 2a–d).

Although large areas of the abyssal oceans were anoxic in Hirnantian simulations56, average upper-ocean waters along the continental margins have DO of 50–100 μmol/kg with the exception of lower DO in the tropics (Fig. 3). Based on modern ocean observations (Fig. S8), the low DO commonly found on tropical continental shelves (Fig. 3) would have resulted in relatively higher dMn in the tropics, opposite to the LMG pattern of less sediment Mn enrichment in the tropics, indicating that the DO was probably the more dominant control (rather than dMn) for the concave-up shape LMG. The LPIA simulations show a well-oxygenated upper ocean with large uncertainties in DO levels of the deep ocean (Fig. 3). For comparison, a recent study suggested 4–12% of seafloor anoxia for the peak LPIA57. Our cGENIE simulations overall predict fewer areas of low upper-ocean [O2] for dMn accumulation during the LPIA compared to older glaciations, especially the Snowball Earth.

Non-redox-related factors

Pronounced Mn enrichments in ancient sedimentary rocks are mostly preserved in the form of Mn carbonate minerals (rhodochrosite, kutnohorite, and Mn-calcite) as diagenetic products of primary Mn oxides29,58. Primary Mn carbonates precipitation in the water column was suggested in modern ferruginous lakes59,60, and for the Proterozoic Animikie Basin and the Jurassic Molgano Mn ore59,61, although the majority of Mn enrichments are linked to the “shuttle” concept according to previous studies (e.g., ref. 38). Mn carbonate can also form in the sediment column where porewater dMn concentrations and alkalinity are elevated enough to reach saturation. These processes are highly heterogeneous in space and are unlikely to drive the LMG repeatedly observed throughout Earth history (Fig. 2).

Basin restriction, weathering, and hydrothermal input of Mn were suggested to be the main controls of individual large Mn ore deposits1,42,62,63. Their influence on the spatial pattern of sedimentary Mn enrichment warrants discussion. The Mo-U covariation in the two Paleozoic glaciations suggests most of the samples are not affected by basin restriction (Fig. 4). Weathering intensity was dramatically elevated during the Snowball Earth deglaciations64,65, and the most enhanced chemical weathering still occurred at low latitudes66,67, similar to modern latitudinal weathering profile68. Thus, weathering input of terrestrial Mn is unlikely to be a major control of LMG.

dMn flux from hydrothermal vents41,42 may contribute to the LMG. The crustal production and mid-ocean ridge (MOR) outgassing were consistently low through the Cryogenian Snowball Earth events69. The dMn pool might have been derived from hydrothermal sources and blocked from atmospheric oxygen by a sea-ice cap under Snowball Earth conditions63,70–72. The latitudinal distributions of MOR were calculated from paleogeographic reconstructions (Fig. S9). We do not argue against the importance of hydrothermal activities to some Mn deposits, but the large-scale latitudinal pattern of hydrothermal sources changed through time and is unlikely the key control of the consistent pattern in the observed LMG (Fig. S9).

Glaciation, LMG, and non-glacial Mn enrichments

The frequently cited conceptual model for Mn deposits emphasized a multiple-stage process: the accumulation of Mn2+ in the anoxic oceans (e.g., glacial period), followed by Mn(II) oxidation in the water column (e.g., deglaciation), Mn(IV) reduction at the seafloor or in porewaters, and Mn(II)-carbonate precipitation during early diagenesis (post-depositional alteration)15,16. However, it is challenging to precisely correlate the timing of Mn deposition/recycling to different phases of the glaciation in ancient sedimentary records. The Mn enrichments in cap carbonate cannot rule out some initial particulate Mn accumulation during the Snowball Earth glaciation, which was then recycled and redeposited during the deglaciation. Mn enrichments were also observed before or during Paleozoic glacial maxima (Fig. 1). These broadly defined Paleozoic glacial periods over tens of millions of years also likely included multiple glacial-interglacial episodes73–75. The uncertainty in age constraints of these ancient Mn enrichments cannot provide direct support to the conceptual model linking Mn deposition strictly to the deglaciation, unlike in the Pleistocene sedimentary records17. However, changes in ocean circulation and ventilation remain the best candidate to cause the redox oscillation required for forming Mn oxides.

Our temporal analyses also identified intervals of Mn enrichments during non-glacial periods: 3000–1800 Ma, 520–460 Ma, and 400–385 Ma (Fig. 1). The Archean-Paleoproterozoic Mn enrichments span the short-lived oxygenation events known as the “Whiffs”, the GOE, and the oxygen overshoot of the Lomagundi event76–78. The early Paleozoic Mn enrichments overlap with the Paleozoic Oxygenation Event (POE)79. The highest Mn concentrations during the two Paleozoic non-glacial periods are associated with relatively lower MoEF and UEF and oxic conditions (Fig. S10). In contrast, the highest Archean-Paleoproterozoic Mn concentrations are found in conjunction with relatively higher MoEF and UEF. The high MoEF and UEF for this interval were interpreted to reflect enhanced oxidative weathering and/or submarine weathering related to oxygen rise80,81. The 3000–1800 Ma Mn enrichments were spread over one billion years, with large uncertainties in paleo-geographic reconstructions, and could not be robustly assessed for LMG calculation. Similarly, the 400–385 Ma interval does not contain sufficient latitudinal bins for meaningful LMG calculations. The POE LMG was calculated for the 520–460 Ma interval and compared in the next section to other glacial LMGs.

The role of atmospheric O2

To compare the LMGs of the studied glaciations, 75th percentile values of each latitudinal Mn bin are fitted with quadratic polynomial functions (e.g., Fig. 2). All glacial LMGs are plotted together and evaluated in Fig. 5 by the gradient steepness (as indicated by the coefficient of the quadratic term) and significance (well- or poorly defined, R2). All LMGs associated with glaciations are distinct from the background LMGs calculated for the non-glacial Phanerozoic and non-glacial Proterozoic samples (Fig. 5a), indicating the unique mode of Mn deposition during Earth’s major ice ages. Both the magnitude and latitudinal gradient of the Mn deposits correlate with glaciation spatial extent and atmospheric oxygen levels (Fig. 5b). Two episodes of oxygen rise outside of known glaciations, namely the Neoproterozoic Oxygenation Event (NOE) and Paleozoic Oxygenation Event (POE)79,82,83, also fall on the trajectory defined by the glacial LMGs (Fig. 5b).

Synthesis

Sedimentary Mn enrichments identified in a large database are closely associated with geologic intervals of major glaciations and oxygen rises in the ocean-atmosphere system. Spatial analyses of the Mn data consistently reveal an LMG of relatively higher concentrations at higher paleo-latitudes. Mo-U data and Earth system model results identify paleoredox conditions as the main control of such LMGs. The LMG most likely recorded the spatial pattern of Mn oxide formation in the water column, with a minor contribution from direct precipitation of Mn carbonate (Fig. 6a). Dissolved Mn accumulation mostly occurred in low DO waters. Low DO conditions could be driven by either a low atmospheric partial pressure of oxygen (pO2), poor oceanic ventilation and/or strong oceanic oxygen demand during remineralization of sinking organic matter in highly-productive oceanic regions such as equatorial upwellings (Fig. 6a). The high sedimentary [Mn] at higher latitudes, concave up LMGs, were likely due to preferential ventilation of higher latitudes during climate driven redox oscillations.

Concept cartoon for the main controls of latitudinal manganese gradient (LMG).

a Processes controlling manganese deposition in marine sediments. b Contrasting atmosphere-ocean conditions resulting in relatively steep LMG during the Proterozoic. c The relatively flat LMG during the Phanerozoic.

The evolution of LMG through time reflects the transition of global oceans from widespread high-dMn waters under low atmospheric pO2 conditions to the low-dMn waters under high atmospheric pO2 conditions (Fig. 6b, c). The widespread anoxic waters in Proterozoic-like oceans allow dMn to accumulate while circulation changes associated with deglaciation played an important role in delivering the DO required for Mn deposition. In the relatively well-oxygenated oceans after the POE, high dMn instead of DO became the limiting factor for Mn enrichment, and the role of climate change in sedimentary Mn enrichment switched to building anoxia for dMn accumulation. Hence, the strong glaciations in a low pO2 and high dMn Proterozoic Ocean are most prone to producing extreme Mn enrichment with very steep LMGs (Fig. 5).

This interpretative framework suggests that all six major glaciations considered in this study were associated with redox oscillations, i.e., various spatial extents of anoxia followed by a period of re-oxygenation. These results align well with recent studies suggesting the development of ocean deoxygenation under icehouse conditions during the Hirnantian glaciation57 and LPIA57. Our spatial-temporal analysis of sedimentary Mn enrichments hence suggests that the development of anoxia during major glaciations may have been more common than previously considered, in contrast with the seminal model of warming-driven oceanic anoxic events.

Methods

Time series analyses

The temporal variations in Mn and trace metal concentrations are fitted by a single lowess model84, performed with the Real Statistics Using Excel (https://real-statistics.com/regression/loess-regression/loess-regression-using-excel/). The temporal trend is calculated using a smoothing window of 10% data points, exhibited by gray lines in Fig. 1.

In order to minimize the influence of background values, the samples were filtered by PAAS (Post-Archean Australian Average Shale)27. Only samples with [Mn] higher than [Mn]PAAS are analyzed and binned by every 50/5/3/1 Myrs (depending on the time interval, Fig. 1). The 75th percentile of each time bin is shown in Fig. 1. Because part of the database is removed, the 75th percentiles may be discontinuous in time.

Since the lowess regression is sensitive to the density of samples through time, we also reconstructed the temporal Mn concentration curve by bootstrap. A total of 1001 rounds of bootstrap are performed, and all analyzed samples (without filtering) are evenly selected with replacement. The obtained resampling dataset is binned by every 50/5/3/1 Myrs, and the 75th percentile is extracted from each bin during bootstrap. The full range of 75th percentiles of time bins is shown by the shaded area in Fig. 1. There are time bins with quite few samples (e.g., <5), and these bins may disappear in any round of bootstrap. Time bins appearing less than 700 times are omitted from the data display.

Latitudinal manganese gradient (LMG)

For the Neoproterozoic cap carbonates, the 75th percentiles of each site are utilized in the LMG calculation. The paleocoordinate data is reconstructed by the “rgplates” package and “Kinematics Tool” utility in GPlates software85,86. These [Mn] percentile-paleolatitude data are fitted with quadratic polynomial functions (y = b0 + b1·x + b2·x2), where b2 is referred to as the gradient. Two methods are used to test the robustness of LMG, since the cap carbonate samples are unevenly distributed both temporally and spatially. One is bootstrap: samples from one glaciation event (i.e., Sturtian, Marinoan, or Gaskiers) are combined into one dataset, and are resampled evenly with replacement. Then the resampled dataset is binned by the site paleolatitudes, and 75th percentiles are used in LMG regression. Another way is equal-count subsampling: in one glaciation event, the sample counts in cap carbonate sites (assuming there are M sites) differ from each other, and the minimum sample count is the size of subsampling (Nsub). Nsub samples are evenly subsampled from each cap carbonate site, and a total of M × Nsub samples are resampled. Then the 75th percentiles of subsampled sites are used in the LMG calculation. The combination of M × Nsub samples are used in equal-count subsampling in temporal trend reconstruction in Fig. 1.

As for the Paleozoic glaciation sediments, the samples are binned by fixed bins (i.e., by every 10 degrees; 90°S-80°S-70°S-···-80°N-90°N), and the 75th percentiles of latitudinal bins are used in LMG regression (y = b0 + b1·x + b2·x2). In order to minimize the influence of spatial heterogeneity in sample count, we utilized a bootstrap (similar to cap carbonates) in “All samples” and “Shale” analyses. Because the difference in latitudinal sample density is extremely significant, equal-count subsampling is not applicable. Therefore, we used the icosahedral equal-area grids, particularly the Icosahedral Snyder Equal Area (ISEA) system, to resample the database (Fig. S11). In an ISEA system, Earth’s surface is subdivided into 812 grids (pentagons or hexagons) using the “dggridR” package in R87. All samples are assigned to the grids by paleocoordinates. The center paleolatitude and 75th percentiles of the ISEA grids are used to calculate the LMG.

For other periods, such as the non-glacial Phanerozoic and GOE, the LMG is simply calculated by 75th percentiles of fixed bins.

cGENIE simulations

cGENIE19 offers a coupled representation of physical climate and ocean biogeochemistry in a 3-dimensional ocean. cGENIE includes an oceanic general circulation model, coupled with an energy-moisture energy balance model as the atmospheric component, a dynamic-thermodynamic sea-ice model, and a marine biogeochemistry module. The model is run on a 36 × 36 equal-area grid with 16 or 17 vertical levels in the ocean. The ocean circulation in cGENIE has been shown to compare relatively well with that of more complex models of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project phase 5 generation88. Despite its low resolution, cGENIE has been shown to satisfactorily simulate ocean oxygenation of the modern ocean89. It has been extensively used to simulate ocean oxygenation during various Precambrian and Phanerozoic time slices28,56,90,91.

In an attempt to investigate the potential regions of dMn accumulation and sedimentary Mn precipitation, we produced tentative reconstructions of the redox landscape at 445 Ma, and at 350 Ma, and 300 Ma using cGENIE19. These time slices were selected to represent conditions that prevailed during the Hirnantian glaciation and during the Early Paleozoic Ice Age. For each simulation, we extracted latitudinal averages of coastal [O2] values simulated at various depths to illustrate the vertical structure of ocean oxygenation. Coastal grids in the model were selected to best capture the continental margins where particulate Mn was deposited and preserved. For all time slices, we adopted published GENIE configurations with time-adapted boundary conditions, including paleogeography and solar luminosity. The atmospheric partial pressures of CO2 (pCO2) were chosen to represent icehouse conditions.

For the Hirnantian glaciation, we used the simulation selected by ref. 56 to best represent climate and the redox landscape during the Hirnantian glacial maximum (their scenario #1), using a pCO2 of 8.5 PAL (preindustrial atmospheric level; 1 PAL pCO2 = 280 ppm), an atmospheric pO2 of 0.4 PAL, and a global-mean oceanic [PO4] of 0.4 POL (present oceanic level). For the LPIA, we used two simulations. One simulation was derived from the 300 Ma simulations of ref. 92. The other simulation is for the mid-Tournaisian carbon isotope excursion (TICE) at ~348 Ma, corresponding to LPIA onset (simulation #3 of ref. 93). The TICE simulation was run under a modern pO2, using 2 PAL pCO2 and 1.6 POL [PO4]. Since our goal is not specifically to simulate the conditions during the TICE but rather to provide a plausible representation of environmental conditions during the LPIA, we deliberately do not account for the presumed pO2 rise proposed by the authors93. This allows us to make sure that the simulation captures somehow the anoxic conditions that recurred during the LPIA57. Independent of the choice made in the original publications, all GENIE simulations were re-run for 20000 years with radiative forcing as a function of atmospheric pCO2.
