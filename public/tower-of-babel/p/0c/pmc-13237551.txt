Prognostic value of end-to-end deep learning assessment of myocardial scar and microvascular obstruction on late gadolinium enhancement cardiovascular magnetic resonance

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLate gadolinium enhancement (LGE) cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is the reference standard for assessing myocardial scar and microvascular obstruction (MVO), and is a strong predictor of post-acute myocardial infarction (AMI) outcomes. However, manual segmentation is time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability, limiting clinical scalability. This study develops and validates LGE-CMRnet, an end-to-end deep learning pipeline for automated scar and MVO segmentation on LGE CMR, and evaluates its prognostic value in AMI patients.MethodsA total of 3874 LGE images from 567 AMI patients (409 for training/internal stress-test cohort; 158 for external testing) were analyzed. LGE-CMRnet integrates YOLOv8 for heart localization and nnU-Net for simultaneous segmentation of myocardium, scar, and MVO. Performance was evaluated using Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), correlation, and Bland-Altman analysis against expert annotations. Prognostic value was assessed using Cox regression for major adverse cardiac events (MACE) over a median follow-up of 24.4 months.ResultsLGE-CMRnet achieved rapid processing (0.05 s per image) and high segmentation accuracy. In the external validation cohort, the model achieved mean DSC of 0.83 ± 0.11 for scar and 0.88 ± 0.11 for MVO at the patient level, with strong volumetric correlations to expert reference segmentations (scar: r = 0.90; MVO: r = 0.98, both P<0.0001). Bland-Altman analysis showed minimal bias in volumetric measurements (scar: 2.5 ± 8.9 cm3; MVO: −0.20 ± 0.89 cm3). Furthermore, LGE-CMRnet-derived metrics demonstrated non-inferior discrimination for MACE prediction compared with expert analysis. The differences in C-index were 0.02 for %MVO and 0.01 for %Scar, with the lower bounds of the 95% CIs remaining above the pre-specified non-inferiority margin.ConclusionLGE-CMRnet enables fast and accurate scar and MVO quantification, with prognostic performance comparable to expert analysis, supporting its potential for automated clinical risk stratification after AMI.Keywords: Cardiovascular magnetic resonance, Late gadolinium enhancement, Deep learning, Myocardial scar, Microvascular obstruction, Prognosis

Introduction

Accurate detection and quantification of myocardial scar and microvascular obstruction (MVO) following reperfusion therapy can facilitate prompt and appropriate clinical intervention in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Previous studies have indicated that the presence and extent of scar and MVO are independently associated with adverse ventricular remodeling and patient prognosis [1], [2], [3].

Various techniques measuring slightly different biological and functional parameters are used both clinically and experimentally for the diagnosis of scar and MVO [4]. Biomarkers such as troponin and creatine kinase are utilized to predict scar size (Spearman’s r range [5]: 0.67–0.87) and the presence of MVO (area under the curve [6]: 0.77), but they lack detailed MVO size information. While conventional echocardiography offers a quick and general overview of the state of the myocardium, common indices like left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF) fall short in detecting scar and MVO. Coronary angiography measures the Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow grade. A TIMI flow grade ≤2 without any residual obstructive lesion is generally considered indicative of “angiographic no reflow” [7]. However, this technique is invasive, and MVO can still be present in a substantial number of patients with TIMI flow grade >2 [8]. Myocardial contrast echocardiography can detect microvascular dysfunction and obstruction (with a sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 74%) [9]. Nevertheless, limitations persist, including moderate image resolution, operator dependence, incomplete visualization of the LV (particularly the lateral wall), and semi-quantitative assessment of MVO [9]. Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging is established as the standard of reference for identifying myocardial scar and MVO [4]. However, scar and MVO quantification on LGE CMR often relies on manual delineation of myocardial borders and the hyper-enhanced regions, making it a subjective, laborious, and time-consuming process.

Recent studies have explored deep learning approaches for automated scar segmentation on LGE CMR. Early 2D convolutional neural networks (CNNs), such as U-Net and Enet [10], [11], achieved moderate accuracy (mean Dice similarity coefficients [DSC] 0.58–0.71) in delineating scar tissue. Subsequent 3D CNN architectures incorporated volumetric context, which improved voxel-wise classification of scar regions (DSC up to 0.94) [12]. However, most of these methods focused exclusively on hyper-enhanced scar tissue without addressing concomitant MVO. In parallel, frameworks such as nnU-Net and 'You Only Look Once' (YOLO) have demonstrated robust and generalizable performance for cardiac localization and segmentation across a range of CMR tasks, including myocardium, chamber, and infarct delineation [13], [14]. These developments highlight the growing potential of deep learning for comprehensive myocardial tissue characterization. Despite this progress, there remains a lack of integrated solutions for fully automated segmentation of the myocardium, scar, and MVO within a single framework—an essential step toward objective, reproducible, and clinically scalable CMR analysis.

The aim of this study was therefore to develop and evaluate an automated CNN-based method, LGE-CMRnet, for simultaneous segmentation of the myocardium, scar, and MVO from LGE CMR images, and to assess its prognostic utility in patients with AMI.

Methods

CMR dataset

This study included 3,874 LGE images from 567 patients with AMI who underwent CMR within 1 week after symptom onset. Data were collected from two primary centers in Singapore for model development (training and internal testing) and from one hospital in China for external testing. To minimize training bias, patients for the training and internal testing were stratified into two groups based on an MVO-to-scar volume ratio cutoff of 3.6% (the average MVO volume percentage across all patients). Group 1 comprised patients with an MVO-to-scar volume ratio of ≥3.6%, while Group 2 had a ratio of <3.6%. The two groups were then randomly divided into training and internal testing datasets at a 9:1 ratio. Of the training dataset, 10% of images were further set aside for validation during model training. The training dataset included 2614 LGE images from 368 randomly selected patients (184 with MVO; 50%), while the remaining 335 LGE images from 41 patients (31 with MVO; 76%) were assigned to the internal testing dataset. The external testing dataset comprised 925 LGE images from 158 AMI patients (48 with MVO; 30%). The internal testing dataset, which had a high prevalence of MVO, was used primarily as a stress-test cohort to evaluate model robustness. Final conclusions regarding model performance were based primarily on the external testing cohort. The study protocol was approved by the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board and the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

In the Singapore dataset, LGE CMR imaging was performed using either a 1.5T Avanto MR scanner or a 3T Biograph mMR scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). For the 1.5T Avanto MR, LGE images were acquired 10 min after intravenous administration of 0.2 mmol/kg gadolinium-diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (Gd-DTPA) using the following parameters: repetition time (TR), 6.7 ms; echo time (TE), 3.2 ms; slice thickness, 8 mm; field of view (FOV), 284×350 mm2; matrix size, 208×256; and flip angle, 25°. For the 3T Biograph mMR, LGE images were acquired 10 min after administration of 0.2 mmol/kg gadobutrol (Gadovist, Bayer HealthCare Pharmaceuticals, Leverkusen, Germany) with the following parameters: TR, 4.1 ms; TE, 1.56 ms; slice thickness, 8.5 mm; FOV, 340×276 mm2; matrix size, 256×208; and flip angle, 20°. Short-axis LGE images were acquired with 6–11 slices per subject.

For the external testing dataset, CMR imaging was performed using a 3T Discovery MR750W scanner (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, Wisconsin) equipped with an 8-channel cardiac coil, electrocardiogram triggering, and respiratory gating. Gadobutrol was administered intravenously at a dose of 0.1 mmol/kg with an injection rate of 3.0 mL/s, followed by a 10–20 mL saline flush. LGE imaging was acquired 8–10 min post-contrast using an inversion recovery gradient echo sequence with the following parameters: TR, 6.7 ms; TE, 3.2 ms; slice thickness, 8 mm; FOV, 380×380 mm2; matrix size, 512×512; and flip angle, 25°. Short-axis LGE images were acquired with 5–10 slices per subject.

Reference segmentation

Image analysis was performed in a core laboratory setting using an expert-guided semi-automated reference standard, in which an expert with >10 years of experience in CMR manually delineated both endo- and epicardium using the freely available software Segment (version 4.0 R12067, Medviso AB, Lund, Sweden) [15]. An automatic algorithm [16] was subsequently applied to generate standardized reference segmentations for scar and MVO detection. This algorithm had been rigorously validated against multiple standards: in vivo triphenyltetrazolium-chloride (TTC) staining, ex vivo high-resolution T1-weighted imaging, and expert delineation across multi-center, multi-vendor datasets. The reported performance was highly consistent, with mean biases (infarct size as %LV mass) of −1 ± 4% (magnitude inversion recovery [IR]) and −2 ± 3% (phase-sensitive inversion recovery [PSIR]) compared with TTC (r = 0.84–0.92); 0 ± 4% (magnitude IR) and 0 ± 5% (PSIR) vs. ex vivo T1-weighted imaging (r = 0.79–0.94); and −2 ± 6% (magnitude IR) and 0 ± 5% (PSIR) vs. expert delineation in multi-center clinical studies (r = 0.81–0.89). Manual corrections of scar and MVO regions were applied according to predefined criteria in cases where the reference segmentation did not accurately delineate these areas, which affected approximately 10%–15% of images. All reference segmentations were reviewed by another expert with >20 years of experience in CMR, and in case of disagreement, a consensus was reached. The volumes of myocardium, scar, and MVO were calculated by multiplying the voxel volume by the number of pixels labeled myocardium, scar, and MVO, respectively [17]. The ratio of scar volume to total LV myocardium volume (%Scar), and the ratio of MVO volume to scar volume (%MVO) were then computed.

To assess the reliability of ground truth annotations, intra- and inter-observer reproducibility analyses were performed on 50 randomly selected LGE CMR studies. Two experienced observers independently delineated myocardial scar and MVO regions following identical protocols. For intra-observer reproducibility, the same observer repeated the segmentation after a minimum interval of four weeks, blinded to the previous results. Agreement between segmentations was quantified using the DSC and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for both scar and MVO volumes.

Network architecture

The system architecture streamlines the automated analysis of LGE CMR images (Fig. 1). It begins by identifying and localizing the LV using YOLOv8 [18], ensuring that subsequent segmentation focuses exclusively on the relevant area. The 2D nnU-Net [19] then performs simultaneous segmentation of the LV myocardium, scar, and MVO. The system is structured as a three-stage pipeline:

Stage 1: Heart localization using YOLOv8

The first stage of the pipeline employs the YOLOv8x (YOLOv8 Extra Large) model to detect and localize the LV within LGE CMR images (Supplementary Figure 1). This preprocessing step constrains subsequent segmentation to the relevant anatomical structures, improving computational efficiency and accuracy. All input images were resized to 640×640 pixels to conform to the required dimensions for YOLOv8x inference. The YOLOv8x detection model comprises 365 layers and 68,157,423 trainable parameters. It utilizes the AdamW optimization algorithm with a learning rate of 0.001111 and momentum of 0.9. The model was trained for 100 epochs with an adaptive batch size strategy to optimize GPU memory utilization. Stage 2: Region of interest extraction and image augmentation for segmentation

After the initial localization by YOLOv8, the system extracted the ROI and cropped the region, centering it around the detected LV. The final cropped ROI images were resized to a standardized dimension of 512×512 pixels using bilinear interpolation. To enhance segmentation robustness, image preprocessing techniques such as intensity normalization, mirroring, zooming, and rotation were applied. These augmentations help mitigate overfitting by exposing the model to a wider variety of image variations during training.

Stage 3: Image segmentation with nnU-Net

The third stage of the pipeline employs the 2D nnU-Net architecture for multi-class segmentation of the LV myocardium, scar tissue, and MVO (Supplementary Figure 2). The model generates pixel-wise classification masks, assigning each pixel to the appropriate category.

Clinical outcomes and follow-up

For the external testing dataset, clinical follow-up was performed annually for up to 10 years. The primary outcome measurement was major adverse cardiac event (MACE), defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, myocardial reinfarction, hospitalization for heart failure, and stroke.

Experimental settings

Model development and evaluation were performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel Xeon 12-core processor, 256 GB RAM, 1 TB storage, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, California) with 24 GB of memory. Total training time was approximately 15 h for 1000 epochs. The network architecture was implemented using the Torch deep learning library, built on the PyTorch framework. A detailed list of software dependencies and version numbers is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Evaluation metrics

The overlap between automatic and expert reference segmentations was measured using the DSC and IoU for the LV myocardium, scar, and MVO. Let A represent the area of the automatically segmented region, and G represent the area of the expert reference region. The DSC and IoU are defined as follows: DSC=2A∩G/A+G and IoU=A∩G/A∪G, where A∩G denotes the intersection between A and G, and A∪G denotes the union between A and G. Both DSC and IoU values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a closer match between the expert reference and the automatically segmented results.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (Version 23, Chicago, Illinois). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range), while categorical variables are displayed as counts (percentage). Pearson correlation coefficient r and Bland-Altman analysis were used to assess agreement between automatic and expert reference segmentations of scar volume, ratio of scar volume to total LV myocardium volume, MVO volume, and ratio of MVO volume to total scar volume.

LGE-CMRnet-derived scar and MVO quantifications, along with other clinical and imaging variables, were evaluated as predictors of MACE using univariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis. Variables that showed associations with MACE at P<0.10 in the univariable analysis or were otherwise clinically relevant were subsequently entered into backward conditional multivariable models. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) and Bonferroni corrections were applied to account for multiple testing across separate models. Discriminative performance was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index). Non-inferiority of LGE-CMRnet-derived measurements vs. expert assessment was tested using a pre-specified non-inferiority margin of ∆C-index = 0.05, and prognostic equivalence was examined using the net reclassification index (NRI), which quantifies the proportion of events and non-events correctly reclassified when patients are categorized as “high-risk” or “low-risk” based on the defined thresholds. Event-free survival curves were obtained by Kaplan-Meier analysis and compared by log-rank test. A two-sided P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reproducibility of ground truth segmentations

Intra- and inter-observer reproducibility of ground truth segmentations for both scar and MVO was assessed in 50 randomly selected LGE CMR studies. For scar, intra-observer ICC was 0.964 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.941 to 0.979) with mean DSC 0.86 ± 0.06 (95% CI: 0.84 to 0.88), while inter-observer ICC was 0.950 (95% CI: 0.916 to 0.970) with mean DSC 0.83 ± 0.08 (95% CI: 0.81 to 0.85). For MVO, intra-observer ICC was 0.989 (95% CI: 0.983 to 0.994) with mean DSC 0.87 ± 0.06 (95% CI: 0.85 to 0.89), and inter-observer ICC was 0.969 (95% CI: 0.962 to 0.974) with mean DSC 0.85 ± 0.09 (95% CI: 0.83 to 0.88). Given the high reproducibility of the reference annotations, we subsequently performed ablation studies to evaluate the impact of key architectural and training design choices on model performance.

Ablation studies

To systematically evaluate the contribution of each major design component in the proposed pipeline, we conducted a series of ablation studies assessing the following: (1) the impact of heart localization, (2) the choice of YOLOv8 variant, (3) the nnU-Net architectural configuration, and (4) the sensitivity to loss function weighting in nnU-Net. Unless otherwise stated, all ablation experiments were performed using identical training data, preprocessing steps, and evaluation protocols, with only the component under investigation modified.

Ablation study 1: Impact of heart localization (full-image vs. ROI-based segmentation)

To assess whether the YOLOv8-based heart localization stage contributes to downstream segmentation performance, we compared the proposed two-stage pipeline with a baseline model in which nnU-Net was trained directly on full-resolution images without localization or cropping.

As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the ROI-based approach achieved substantially higher segmentation accuracy for both scar and MVO. The mean DSC for MVO improved from 0.69 to 0.84, highlighting the benefit of heart localization for segmenting small and spatially sparse pathological regions. These results indicate that ROI cropping effectively reduces confounding background structures and enables the segmentation network to focus its representational capacity on the relevant cardiac anatomy.

Ablation study 2: Effect of YOLOv8 variant choice on localization and downstream segmentation

To evaluate the effect of detector capacity on heart localization and downstream segmentation performance, we compared four YOLOv8 variants (YOLOv8n, YOLOv8m, YOLOv8l, and YOLOv8x). Localization performance was assessed on the internal validation set and a held-out test set using standard object detection metrics, including precision, recall, mAP@0.5, and mAP@0.5:0.95. Downstream segmentation performance was evaluated using DSC for scar and MVO quantification.

Across all variants, YOLOv8x achieved the highest mAP@0.5:0.95 on the test set (0.848), indicating more accurate and robust bounding box localization (Supplementary Table 2). While mean DSC values for scar and MVO were comparable across variants, YOLOv8x demonstrated the lowest variance and the most consistent downstream segmentation performance, suggesting improved robustness to localization variability.

Computational efficiency was further evaluated by comparing inference time and peak GPU memory usage across variants (Supplementary Table 3). As expected, larger models incurred higher computational costs; however, the absolute inference time difference between YOLOv8n (∼3.6 ms) and YOLOv8x (∼14.0 ms) was approximately 10.4 ms per case, which is negligible relative to the overall pipeline runtime dominated by image loading, segmentation, and post-processing (approximately 5–10 s). Although YOLOv8x required modestly higher GPU memory, it remained well within typical GPU capacity and did not constrain batch size or downstream processing.

Based on these findings, YOLOv8x was selected for the final pipeline, as it provided the most robust localization under strict overlap criteria and the most stable downstream segmentation performance with an acceptable computational overhead.

Ablation study 3: nnU-Net architecture selection (2D vs. 3D variants)

To investigate the impact of network dimensionality, we compared the proposed 2D YOLO-cropped nnU-Net with a 3D full-resolution nnU-Net trained under identical experimental settings. Both models operated on the same localized ROIs and used the same loss function and optimization strategy.

As shown in Supplementary Table 4, the 2D configuration achieved higher DSC values and lower variance for both scar and MVO segmentation. This observation is consistent with the anisotropic nature of clinical LGE CMR data, in which relatively large slice thickness limits the effectiveness of inter-slice convolutions. Under these conditions, 2D processing preserves in-plane resolution and avoids potential artifacts arising from sparse through-plane context.

Ablation study 4: Loss function sensitivity analysis in nnU-Net

To assess sensitivity of nnU-Net segmentation performance to the relative weighting of Dice and CE losses, we evaluated five Dice:CE ratios (1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 1:2, and 1:3), while keeping all other training settings fixed.

As summarized in Supplementary Table 5, segmentation performance remained highly consistent across all tested weightings. Scar DSC ranged narrowly from 0.87 to 0.88, and MVO DSC remained stable at approximately 0.84, indicating that the proposed framework is robust to reasonable variations in loss weighting and that performance is not driven by a specific tuned Dice:CE ratio. Based on these results, the standard 1:1 Dice:CE formulation was retained in the final model for simplicity and reproducibility.

Quantitatively, LGE-CMRnet demonstrated high agreement with expert annotations on the internal testing dataset. For scar segmentation, the mean DSC was 0.87 ± 0.07 (95% CI: 0.85 to 0.89) per patient and 0.87 ± 0.11 (95% CI: 0.86 to 0.88) per slice, with corresponding IoU values of 0.78 ± 0.10 per patient and 0.78 ± 0.15 per slice. Bland-Altman analysis further confirmed robust agreement, with a mean bias (±1.96 SD) of −1.0 ± 6.5 cm3 for scar volume and −1.6 ± 7.6% for %Scar (Fig. 4C–D).

Performance on external testing dataset

The external testing dataset served as the primary validation cohort for assessing model generalizability and clinical performance. For scar segmentation, the mean DSC was 0.83 ± 0.11 (95% CI: 0.81 to 0.85) per patient and 0.84 ± 0.13 (95% CI: 0.83 to 0.85) per slice, with IoU of 0.73 ± 0.14 per patient and 0.74 ± 0.17 per slice. For MVO, the corresponding DSCs were 0.88 ± 0.11 (95% CI: 0.86 to 0.90) per patient and 0.83 ± 0.20 (95% CI: 0.82 to 0.84) per slice, with IoU of 0.80 ± 0.16 per patient and 0.74 ± 0.23 per slice (Table 2). Scar volumes estimated by LGE-CMRnet remained highly correlated with expert reference annotations (r = 0.90, P<0.0001; Fig. 6A), as did %Scar (r = 0.91, P<0.0001; Fig. 6B). Bland-Altman plots showed good agreement, with a bias of 2.5 ± 8.9 cm3 for scar volume and 1.8 ± 9.2% for %Scar (Fig. 6C-D).

Model performance across cardiac function, risk factor, and imaging protocol subgroups

To examine whether differences in baseline clinical and imaging characteristics affected model generalizability, subgroup analyses were performed for both scar and MVO segmentation. The analyses were stratified by LV function (LVEF), LV volume (end-diastolic volume index), and major cardiovascular risk factors (smoking and hyperlipidemia). As summarized in Supplementary Tables 7A and 7B, no significant differences in segmentation performance, as measured by the DSC, were observed across any of the subgroups in either the internal or external test cohorts.

Additionally, to assess the impact of differences in imaging protocols, we conducted subgroup analyses based on scanner type, field strength, and contrast agent. Results demonstrated that LGE-CMRnet maintained robust segmentation accuracy (DSC) across all imaging subgroups, supporting its generalizability across diverse scanner hardware and contrast protocols.

Prognostic performance of LGE-CMRnet-derived scar and MVO volume

In the external testing dataset, during a median follow-up of 24.4 months (interquartile range: 16.0–33.8 months), MACE occurred in 35 of 158 patients (22.2%): 4 cardiovascular deaths, 22 hospitalizations for heart failure, 5 myocardial reinfarctions, and 4 strokes. Patients who experienced MACE had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus, Killip class >1, increased LV end-systolic volume index, reduced LVEF, and an increased prevalence of MVO, %Scar, and %MVO compared with those without MACE (Supplementary Table 8).

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed, including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, Killip class, LVEF, and LV end-systolic volume index as covariates, selected based on significant differences between MACE and non-MACE groups (P<0.10) and clinical relevance. Two separate multivariable models were constructed to assess the independent prognostic value of scar percentage (%Scar) and MVO percentage (%MVO) (Table 3): (1) %Scar plus covariates; and (2) %MVO plus covariates. In the %Scar model, backward conditional selection retained LVEF (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.92 to 0.97; P<0.0001) and %Scar (HR = 1.05, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.08; P = 0.001) as independent predictors of MACE. In the %MVO model, LVEF (HR = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.93 to 0.98; P<0.0001) and %MVO (HR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.09; P = 0.003) remained significant predictors. After FDR adjustment for multiple model testing, %Scar (adjusted P = 0.002) and %MVO (adjusted P = 0.003) remained significant independent predictors of MACE. Similarly, after Bonferroni correction, both predictors remained significant (%Scar adjusted P = 0.002; %MVO adjusted P = 0.006).

Discussion

In this study, we present LGE-CMRnet, an automated deep learning method for simultaneous segmentation of myocardium, scar, and MVO on LGE CMR images in patients with AMI. LGE-CMRnet demonstrated strong agreement and high correlation with expert reference segmentations across both internal and external testing datasets, supporting its reliability for clinical application. Notably, scar and MVO volumes derived by LGE-CMRnet showed significant prognostic value comparable to expert reference quantification, with higher volumes associated with poorer clinical outcomes, highlighting the potential of LGE-CMRnet for automated prognostic assessment (Graphical Abstract).

Conventionally, a two-stage approach is commonly used to identify scar tissue on LGE CMR images. This process first involves segmentation of the LV myocardium, followed by detection of scarred regions within the myocardium using threshold-based methods [22]. Some strategies attempt to improve segmentation accuracy by integrating both LGE and cine CMR images through non-rigid registration [23]. Current available software package typically employs a similar two-step workflow, either transferring cine-derived contours to LGE or performing AI-assisted segmentation directly on LGE followed by intensity threshold-based scar quantification. Both approaches have certain limitations, including imperfect contour transfer between sequences [24], frequent need for manual adjustment on LGE, and manual outlining of MVO. Our method addresses these gaps by performing simultaneous, end-to-end segmentation of myocardium, scar, and MVO directly on LGE images. This integrated approach improves automation and throughput by eliminating the need for contour transfer and minimizing manual corrections. At the same time, joint segmentation of scar and MVO enhances accuracy by reducing errors that arise when these structures are delineated separately. Importantly, MVO segmentation is less commonly and less robustly available in prior techniques; including MVO as a target with strong performance metrics provides a tangible advantage for post-AMI risk stratification.

Recent studies have explored deep learning techniques for segmenting myocardial scars on LGE CMR. Fahmy et al. [10] used a U-Net architecture to jointly segment the myocardium and scar tissue in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, with moderate performance for scar segmentation (mean DSC 0.58). Another study [11] utilized a fully convolutional network based on ENet [25] for LV scar segmentation in a cohort of 30 subjects, achieving a median DSC of 0.71. Subsequent work incorporated 3D analysis. Early approaches applied a 2D patch-based CNN [26], which was later extended to a 3D CNN model [12]. This method performed binary classification (healthy vs. diseased tissue) at the voxel level within the myocardium, achieving a DSC of 0.94 and an IoU of 0.88. Additional advancements included a 3D CNN utilizing a sliding-window approach [27], which demonstrated improved performance over 2D CNNs (DSC 0.54 ± 0.26 vs. 0.48 ± 0.29, P = 0.02). These approaches differ from the present work primarily in task formulation and implementation choices. Prior studies have explored voxel-wise classification and patch-based or sliding-window strategies, whereas our framework performs end-to-end multi-class segmentation within localized cardiac regions. Given that clinical LGE CMR data are often anisotropic with limited through-plane resolution, our ablation analysis indicates that a 2D nnU-Net configuration provides more robust and stable performance than a 3D alternative in this setting.

YOLO and nnU-Net have been utilized in prior studies for heart localization and segmentation in CMR images. The 2D, 3D, and cascaded 2D/3D nnU-Net frameworks have demonstrated superior performance compared to traditional U-Net in segmenting both long- and short-axis views of the right ventricle in CMR [28]. In another study [29], 2D+time nnU-Net models were trained to segment the LV myocardium from DENSE CMR, achieving a DSC of 0.83±0.05 for short-axis segmentation and 0.82±0.03 for long-axis segmentation. Similarly, in a study involving 78 patients with a history of myocardial infarction [14], nnU-Net was employed to segment the LV myocardium and infarct zone on LGE CMR, demonstrating a strong correlation between automated and manual infarct volume measurements. Cascaded architectures have also been applied to CMR segmentation. In one study [30], a fully automatic LV segmentation method for short-axis CMR datasets was developed using a two-stage CNN approach. The first CNN, based on the YOLO network, identifies the ROI for the cardiac chambers, while the second CNN, based on the U-Net architecture, segments the myocardium and blood pool. This combination demonstrated strong performance compared to existing methods. In another study [13], CMR segmentation was performed in two steps as follows: (1) a YOLO-based detector identified the ROI from sequential diastolic and systolic CMR images, and (2) a fully convolutional network automatically generated segmentation masks from the extracted ROI. Experimental results showed that this method successfully segmented the LV, right ventricle, and myocardium, achieving performance comparable to other state-of-the-art approaches.

The proposed LGE-CMRnet employs a cascaded YOLO and nnU-Net framework to detect and segment both scar and MVO. The optimization objectives of the two stages are intentionally decoupled and complementary. The YOLOv8 stage provides robust geometric localization of the cardiac region and removes irrelevant background, with detection confidence scores not propagated to the segmentation stage. The nnU-Net subsequently performs dense pixel-wise segmentation within an ROI and is solely responsible for segmenting the myocardium, scar, and MVO. We compared our model with previously published automated methods (Supplementary Table 9). Our model achieved among the highest reported DSC values for both scar and MVO segmentation. Additionally, our study included one of the largest patient cohorts, lending support to the robustness and generalizability of the model. Unlike conventional methods that primarily focus on hyper-enhanced regions (i.e., the bright infarcted zone), LGE-CMRnet is specifically designed to accurately capture both infarcted tissue and MVO, providing a rapid and precise assessment of microvascular injury extent. MVO, characterized by low-intensity regions within the infarct, is often difficult to detect using conventional thresholding techniques [16]. Its appearance is influenced by the typical progression of infarction from the endocardium to the epicardium, which produces continuous hyper-enhanced scar tissue that often blends with the blood pool [31]. Within this context, MVO typically manifests as small, dark voxel clusters embedded within the endocardial and hyper-enhanced regions or completely enclosed by the infarcted tissue. In atypical cases, however, MVO can predominantly involve the sub-endocardium, making it difficult to distinguish from normal myocardium and increasing the risk of misinterpretation as non-ischemic cardiomyopathy or LV thrombus [32].

Recent foundation models, including MedSAM [33] and SAM-Med3D [34], have demonstrated strong generalization across diverse medical imaging tasks with limited labeled data. To benchmark against this paradigm, we fine-tuned MedSAM with a ViT-B backbone on our training dataset by freezing the image encoder and optimizing the prompt encoder and mask decoder for 50 epochs using a combined Dice and binary CE loss. Across both internal and external test sets, LGE-CMRnet achieved higher DSC and lower 95th-percentile Hausdorff distances than MedSAM, particularly for MVO segmentation (Supplementary Table 10). This likely reflects architectural specialization: nnU-Net is explicitly optimized for LGE CMR characteristics and the segmentation of small, low-contrast pathological regions, whereas MedSAM is designed as a generalist model. In terms of efficiency, LGE-CMRnet processed each slice in approximately 50 ms compared with approximately 190 ms for MedSAM, highlighting the practical advantages of a domain-specific architecture for LGE CMR segmentation, while acknowledging the broader generalization potential of foundation models.

Clinically acceptable performance thresholds for myocardial scar and MVO segmentation were defined based on published intra- and inter-observer variability for manual LGE CMR segmentation. While few studies report DSC values directly, Qayyum et al. [35] reported intra- and inter-observer DSCs of 0.83 and 0.79 for scar, respectively, and Tao et al. [36] reported an inter-observer DSC of 0.80. These values represent the typical reproducibility range for expert manual scar segmentation. Other studies have reported reproducibility of manual scar quantification in volumetric terms, with 95% limits of agreement of approximately ±7 cm3 (e.g., de la Rosa et al. [37]: −2.2 ± 7.0 cm3; Zhang et al. [38]: −2.4 ± 7.3 cm3; Mikami et al. [39]: 0.3 ± 7.4 cm3). Prior automated segmentation studies have achieved DSCs and volumetric errors consistent with these manual ranges. For example, de la Rosa et al. [37] reported an average DSC of 0.77 ± 0.14 with volumetric error of 1.0 ± 6.9 cm3, while Zabihollahy et al. [12] reported DSC of 0.94 ± 0.03 and volumetric error of 0.8 ± 5.2 cm3. In the present study, our AI model achieved a DSC of 0.87 and a mean bias of −1.0 cm3 with 95% limits of agreement of ±6.5 cm3 for scar volume. These results fall within, or exceed, the range of inter-observer variability for manual LGE quantification, underscoring the model’s potential for reliable clinical deployment. The core image processing is rapid (∼0.05 s per slice), and the total end-to-end processing time—including image loading, inference, and result export—is approximately 5–10 s per case. When deployed in batch mode, the system achieved a throughput of approximately 200–250 cases per hour, demonstrating high efficiency suitable for clinical workflows.

Our survival analyses further reinforce the prognostic value of MVO volume, consistent with findings from prior study [40]. In this work, LGE-CMRnet-derived MVO volumes were significantly associated with MACE in patients with AMI, with HRs and C-index values comparable to those from ground truth measurements. Reclassification analyses showed no significant differences between automated and expert reference MVO quantification, indicating that model-based segmentation preserves risk stratification accuracy. During follow-up, 35 MACE events occurred. According to the commonly applied events-per-variable (EPV) rule for Cox proportional hazards regression [41], at least 10 outcome events per model parameter are recommended to reduce the risk of overfitting and unstable coefficient estimates. In this study, two multivariable models assessing the independent prognostic values of scar percentage (%Scar) and MVO percentage (%MVO) were fitted, both adjusted for key clinical and imaging covariates. The %Scar model retained LVEF and %Scar, and the %MVO model, LVEF and %MVO. With 35 events and two variables in each model (EPV≈17.5), both models meet the EPV criterion, supporting their robustness. Although the number of events was modest, the analyses offer meaningful exploratory insights into prognostic associations, which can be further validated in studies with larger event numbers.

As noted, while the correlation between LGE-CMRnet predictions and expert reference values in the external testing dataset is very high, the slopes of the linear fits (1.11 for %Scar and 0.62 for %MVO) deviate slightly from unity, indicating a modest proportional bias. This bias likely reflects cumulative effects of differences in imaging protocols, scanner characteristics, contrast agents, and patient populations between the external dataset and the internal training cohort. Despite this, the observed proportional bias is small relative to the total dynamic range of scar and MVO volumes and is unlikely to materially impact risk stratification or clinical decision-making. To contextualize the potential clinical impact, we estimated the deviation at clinically relevant risk thresholds. At the 19% %Scar threshold, the regression relationship corresponds to an estimated automated value of 21.6%, representing an absolute deviation of approximately 2.6% scar burden. Similarly, at the 1.4% %MVO threshold, the regression relationship corresponds to an estimated automated value of 1.8%, representing an absolute deviation of approximately 0.4% MVO burden. A sensitivity analysis further showed that only a small proportion of patients (∼6%) crossed predefined risk thresholds, suggesting that the proportional bias is unlikely to meaningfully affect clinical risk classification. Future work could reduce this bias through multi-site training with more diverse imaging data and domain adaptation techniques. Overall, the high correlation observed in the external cohort supports good generalizability, while the proportional bias identifies an area for potential refinement in future work.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, for scar segmentation, approximately 2% of slices in the internal test set and 6% in the external test set had DSC <0.5. For MVO segmentation, about 12% of slices in the internal test set and 9% in the external test set had DSC <0.5. Common failure cases were observed in apical slices, images with very thin myocardium, subendocardial scar patterns, or very small scar/MVO regions. Representative examples of these challenging cases are shown in Supplementary Figure 3. As with other CNN-based methods, performance for very small MVO regions was suboptimal due to limited discriminative features, making segmentation more challenging [42]. Small and irregular scar patterns also lowered DSC values, as even minor spatial misalignments disproportionately affected performance. However, small LGE areas (<5% LV scar volume) generally have limited prognostic significance [27]. Second, there is currently no widely established percentage-based cutoff for %MVO burden in CMR prognostic studies. Therefore, the 1.4% threshold was determined using the Youden index within the external cohort; the resulting risk stratification should be considered exploratory and requires validation in independent datasets. Third, to improve generalizability beyond AMI patients, such as those with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, the use of appropriate transfer learning strategies may be necessary [43]. Fourth, the number of cases with LV thrombus (n = 15) was very limited and therefore these cases were excluded from model development and evaluation. Given the small proportion of excluded cases, the impact on selection bias is likely minimal. Future studies are warranted to assess the model’s performance and optimize its applicability in patients with LV thrombus. Fifth, this study represents proof-of-concept research rather than clinical validation, and further prospective evaluation in larger, independent cohorts will be needed to establish its utility for clinical implementation and automated risk stratification. Sixth, the internal testing cohort is relatively small and has a higher MVO prevalence compared with the external validation cohort. This difference reflects a deliberate case-enrichment strategy during internal testing to ensure sufficient representation of MVO cases for model stress-testing and error analysis. Post-hoc precision analysis demonstrated that, although the internal cohort CI is wider due to its smaller size and enriched prevalence, it was sufficient for internal evaluation of model stability and failure modes. Future work should evaluate the pipeline on additional scanners and institutions to establish robust, multi-vendor performance. We also plan to extend the analysis to the American Heart Association (AHA) 17-segment model in future work to provide a more granular assessment of regional performance and to facilitate clinical translation. Finally, our current prototype is not yet integrated into commercial software platforms or PACS environments. In addition, regulatory approval and extensive external validation across different scanners and vendors are needed before routine clinical deployment. Within the broader landscape of AI cardiology applications, our method complements rather than replaces existing commercial tools. With further development and regulatory validation, our model has the potential to evolve into a fully integrated clinical solution that provides improved automation and precision for scar and MVO quantification. Future implementations of LGE-CMRnet could provide confidence scores for each segmentation to automatically flag cases with lower prediction certainty for expert review. Clinicians could then verify or edit these flagged cases, while high-confidence predictions could proceed directly to reporting with optional verification. Routine quality control may also include periodic auditing of randomly selected cases, monitoring for systematic errors, and recalibrating confidence thresholds as clinical data accumulate.

Conclusion

LGE-CMRnet enabled fully automated quantification of myocardial scar and MVO and showed preliminary prognostic value comparable to expert reference segmentation in the evaluated datasets. Larger, prospective, multi-center validation is warranted to confirm its clinical utility before recommending routine clinical adoption.
