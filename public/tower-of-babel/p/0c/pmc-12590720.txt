Phyllosphere microbes in foxtail millet primarily affect quality by modulating coloration and bitter compounds
Authors: Qi Liu, Dongbing Hu, Yuxin Qiao, Xiaoyu Zai, Xingyu Hao, Yuzheng Zong, Dongsheng Zhang, Xinrui Shi, Fusuo Zhang, Ping Li, Chunxu Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCrop quality is influenced by crop-related factors such as varieties and metabolites, as well as by environmental conditions. Recent studies have shown the important role of rhizosphere microbes in determining crop quality and flavor, but the effects of phyllosphere (aboveground) microbes and metabolites remain unclear. Here, we examined the interaction between phyllosphere metabolites and the microbiome in three foxtail millet varieties with differing eating qualities: two high-eating-quality varieties (Yugu1, Jingu21) and one low-eating-quality variety (Daobaqi) in the field.ResultsThirteen metabolites were found to be depleted in the high-eating-quality varieties, four of which were identified as bitter compounds. These depleted metabolites were negatively correlated with the abundance of two microbial genera, Curtobacterium and Pseudomonas, enriched in the high-eating-quality foxtail millet panicles. Further analyses revealed that the abundance of these microbes was significantly associated with reduced bitterness and improved viscosity in the panicles. Additionally, synthetic microbial community experiments validated that these microbes enhanced foxtail millet quality by increasing the yellow coloration and reducing levels of bitter compounds.ConclusionsIn summary, the higher abundance of Curtobacterium and Pseudomonas in the panicles correlated with reduced bitter compounds and enhanced foxtail millet quality. This work provides new insights into the role of phyllosphere metabolites and microbiomes in determining crop quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40168-025-02214-7.Keywords: Foxtail millet, Phyllosphere microbiome, Eating quality, Metabolites, Bitter compounds

Introduction

Foxtail millet (Setaria italica) was domesticated from the wild species green foxtail (Setaria viridis) over 11,000 years ago [1]. It is widely cultivated in arid and semiarid regions [2], due to its high photosynthetic efficiency [3], low soil nutrient requirements, and exceptional drought resistance [4]. Compared to wheat and rice, foxtail millet is richer in protein, lipids, and carotenoids [5, 6], making it a nutritious and health-promoting food source [2, 7]. Foxtail millet extract exhibits significant anti-inflammatory effects [7], attributed metabolites such as flavonoids, amides, and organic acids, some of which are bitter compounds. Bitter compounds, often essential nutrients, play a key role in balancing nutrition and flavor [8]. Moreover, variations in anti-inflammatory activity are observed among different foxtail millet varieties [7]. However, for consumers, taste is a key factor in food selection compared to perceived nutritional value, with bitterness generally being undesirable [9]. The food industry often removes bitter compounds through various debittering processes [9].

Foxtail millet quality includes multiple attributes, including appearance, milling, nutritional, cooking, eating, and storage qualities [10]. Among these, appearance and eating characteristics are most critical for consumer preference. Appearance quality is defined by physical traits and color parameters, which encompass L* (lightness), a* (red/green), and b* (yellow/blue) values [11]. The intensity of yellow coloration, primarily attributed to carotenoids in dehulled grains, is a key visual indicator influencing consumer selection [5, 7]. Eating quality refers to the palatability and consistency of millet porridge, commonly assessed by viscosity and pasting temperature, with viscosity strongly correlated with starch content [12].

Crop quality is influenced by various factors, including variety, soil type, and management practices [13, 14]. Recent studies suggest significant role of microbes in modulating crop quality and flavor [15, 16] by affecting the concentration of volatile and secondary metabolites in plants [17–20]. For example, the composition of rhizosphere microbial community in mustard correlates with glucosinolate levels in seeds, with specific microbes metabolizing sulfur-containing compounds to drive this relationship [15]. Similarly, microbes in the rhizosphere of Citrus reticulata promote the accumulation of citrus monoterpenes [20], and in high-theanine tea, rhizosphere microbes enhance the theanine content of low-theanine tea, improving flavor [16, 21]. While these studies focus on rhizosphere microbes, the influence of phyllosphere microbes on crop quality and flavor remains underexplored. However, from an ecological niche perspective, phyllosphere microbes may also effectively influence crop quality. For example, Paraburkholderia phytofirmans (PsJN) applied to the reproductive organs of wheat, corn, soybean, and chili during flowering was detected in mature seeds, with PsJN-colonized wheat seeds exhibiting accelerated development compared to controls [22]. These findings underscore the need to investigate the role of phyllosphere microbes in shaping crop quality seed microbiomes.

To investigate the effects of naturally occurring phyllosphere microbes and metabolites on plant phenotypes, we conducted a field study to assess their contribution to foxtail millet quality. Two high-eating-quality varieties and one low-eating-quality variety of foxtail millet [5] were cultivated throughout their full life cycle, from seed to harvest. Studies indicate that 33 flavonoids differ significantly between high- and low-eating quality varieties [5], suggesting these metabolites contribute to quality differences. Here in this study, we hypothesized that phyllosphere microbes modulate foxtail millet quality by affecting metabolite profiles.

Materials and methods

Experimental design and samples collection

In 2023, three foxtail millet varieties with varying eating qualities- Yugu1 (Y1, high-eating-quality), Jingu21 (J21, high-eating-quality), and Daobaqi (DBQ, low-eating-quality), were cultivated in Taigu County, Shanxi Province, China (37°42′N, 112°57′E). Each variety was grown in four replicates, with each replicate occupying a 4 m2 plot (2 m × 2 m) and row spacing of 30 cm. Soil characteristics were as follows: pH 7.79, organic matter 25.49 g kg−1, total nitrogen 1.46 g kg−1, available phosphorus 31.32 mg kg−1, and available potassium 144.83 mg kg−1. Flag leaves and panicles were sampled during the blooming and late filling stages, respectively. For each replicate, flag leaves and panicles from three plants were collected using sterile scissors and combined into a single sample, yielding 48 samples (3 varieties × 2 growth stages × 2 tissues × 4 replicates). Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃. The remaining plants were harvested at full maturity for quality analysis.

Determination of quality

Color measurements were performed using a CR-400 colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan), which provided direct readings for color parameters: L*: lightness, a*: red/green value, b*: yellow/blue value (foxtail millet with a higher b* indicates a more yellow coloration, and often reflecting carotenoid content and consumer-preferred appearance).

For each replicate, mature grains from ten plants were collected and pooled into a single sample. After removing impurities, the sample was ground at room temperature to obtain a homogeneous fresh powder, which was subsequently used for quality assessment.

Carotenoids were extracted following a modified protocol from Shen et al. [23]. Foxtail millet powder (0.1 g) was homogenized in 1 mL of water-saturated 1-butanol for 30 s using a vortex, followed by shaking at room temperature for 3 h. The mixture was then centrifuged at 8000 rpm min−1 for 15 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatant was transferred to a dark centrifuge tube. Total carotenoid content was quantified by measuring absorbance at 450 nm, using water-saturated 1-butanol as a control.

Total starch (BC0705), and amylose contents (BC4265) were measured using microassay kits (Solarbio, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. https://www.solarbio.com/) [24, 25]. For starch content analysis, foxtail millet powder (0.03 g) was treated with 80% ethanol to remove soluble sugar, followed by acid hydrolysis to convert starch into glucose. Glucose concentration was then quantified using the anthrone colorimetric method at 620 nm. For amylose content determination, foxtail millet powder (0.01 g) was similarly treated with ethanol to eliminate soluble sugars, and the starch was reacted with iodine to form a blue-iodine complex. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm and 485 nm to quantify amylose content.

Viscosity properties were analyzed using a rapid viscosity analyzer (TechMaster RVA, Perten Instruments, Hägersten, Sweden). Foxtail millet flour (3.0 g) was mixed with 25 mL ddH2O in an RVA sample container and subjected to the following protocol: held at 50 ℃ for 1 min; heated to 95 ℃ at a rate of 12 ℃ min−1 and held for 2.5 min; then cooled to 50 ℃ at 12 ℃ min−1 and held for 2 min. The following viscosity parameters were recorded: peak viscosity (PV), trough viscosity (TV), final viscosity (FV), pasting temperature (PTM), breakdown viscosity (BD = PV—TV), and setback viscosity (SB = FV—PV). Differences in quality traits among millet varieties were evaluated using one-way analysis with Duncan's multiple range test.

Metabolite extraction and LC–MS/MS analysis

For metabolite extraction, 0.1 g of flag leaf or panicle sample was added to 1.0 mL of either 70% methanol (for water-soluble metabolites) or 100% methanol (for lipid-soluble metabolites) containing 0.6 mg L−1 chrysin. The mixtures were vortexed and incubated overnight at 4 ℃. After refrigeration, the solutions were vortexed again and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. The water-soluble and lipid-soluble extracts were combined in a 1:1 ratio, centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min at 4℃, and the supernatant was collected. The final extract (1.0 mL) was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube for LC–MS/MS analysis. Metabolite separation was performed using a C18 column (Thermo Hypersil Gold, 1.9 µm × 2.1 mm, 100 mm) on a liquid chromatography phase. The solvent gradient was as follows: solvent A-water with 0.1% (v/v) acetic acid, solvent B-acetonitrile. The gradient conditions (A/B, v/v) were: 90/10 at 0 min, 90/10 at 2.0 min, 50/50 at 10.0 min, 20/80 at 10.1 min, 20/80 at 13.0 min, 5/95 at 14.0 min, 90/10 at 14.1 min, 90/10 at 18.0 min. The flow rate was 0.3 mL min−1, the column temperature was set to 30 °C, and the injection volume was 5.0 μL. Metabolite analysis was conducted using a Thermo Q Exactive™ Plus high-resolution mass spectrometer combined with a TSQ Altis triple quadruple mass spectrometer for both qualitative and quantitative analysis. Quality control samples were inserted after every 10 test samples to ensure data reproducibility and reliability.

Metabolite diversity and differential analysis

Metabolite diversity was performed through principal component analysis (PCA) implemented in the FactoMineR package in R (Version 4.3.3). Group differences were evaluated using analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Differential abundance analysis was conducted using the OPLSR.Anal function in the MetaboAnalystR package. Metabolites with a |log2 fold change|≥ 4 and p value < 0.05 were considered significantly different.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Microbial DNA was extracted from parent seeds (after surface sterilization), flag leaves and panicles using the CTAB method [26]. Amplicon library for bacteria was generated using a two-step PCR approach. The hypervariable V5-V7 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the primer pair 799 F (5'-AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG-3') [27] and 1193R (5'-ACGTCATCCCCACCTTCC-3') [28]. Each 30-μL PCR reaction contained 15 μL of 2 × Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 3 μL of 2 µM forward and reverse primers, 10 μL of DNA template, and 2 μL of ddH2O. PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; and a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were extracted from a 2% (w/v) agarose gel after electrophoresis, then mixed and purified with a GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were generated with the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with added index codes. Library quality was assessed using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Finally, libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform at Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd (Tianjin, China), generating 250-bp paired-end reads.

After removing barcodes and primer sequences in Python (Version 3.6.13), raw reads were merged using FLASH (Version 1.2.11) to generate raw tags. Quality control was conducted using Fastp (Version 0.23.1, Parameters: q19, u15) to obtain high-quality clean tags. Chimera sequences were identified and removed by comparing clean tags against the Silva database (Version 138.1), resulting in effective tags. Initial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated from the effective tags using the DADA2 (Parameters: p-trim-left was 0, p-trunc-len was 0) module in QIIME2 (Version QIIME2-202,202). ASVs were annotated based on the Silva (https://www.arb-silva.de/, Version 138.1) database [29]. ASVs belonging to archaea, unassigned, eukaryota, mitochondria, and chloroplast were removed. Subsequently, 5024 bacterial ASVs with 2,588,019 high-quality reads were obtained from 48 flag leaf and panicle samples. The numbers of reads were sufficient to fully capture the bacterial diversity, shown as the rarefaction curves in Fig. S1.

Microbe diversity and differential analysis

Species annotation was conducted using QIIME2 software with the Silva (Version 138.1) database. Alpha diversity at the ASV level was assessed using the observed features Shannon index, and differences between treatments were analyzed using Kruskal–Wallis with Least Significant Difference (LSD) test. ASV tables were normalized using the minimum value normalization method prior to beta diversity analysis. Bray–Curtis distances between samples were calculated with the vegan package in R. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances were performed to visualize compositional differences, and statistical significance was tested using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). Differential abundance analysis was conducted on raw count data without prior normalization using the DESeq2 package in R. ASVs with a |log2 fold change|≥ 2 and p value < 0.05 were considered significantly different.

Correlation analysis

A clustering correlation heatmap and advanced correlation analysis (Cor link with signs) were performed using OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn). A random forest model was constructed to evaluate the importance of differential metabolites and microbes in determining foxtail millet quality. This analysis was performed in R using the randomForest package, and the significance of each predictor was assessed with the rfPermute package. The parameters were ntree1000 and nrep1000.

Construction of bacterial synthetic microbial community (SynCom) from phyllosphere of foxtail millet varieties with varying eating quality

Flag leaves and panicles from Y1, J21 and DBQ were collected, ground, and filtered to obtain filtrates. The filtrates were serially diluted (10–1 to 10–6) in sterile MgSO4 and spread onto Reasoner’s 2 A Agar (R2A) and Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) plates. After 72 h of incubation at 26 ℃, purified colonies were stored at −80 °C in 40% (v/v) glycerol for long-term storage. A total of 62 bacterial isolates, selected based on colony morphology, were subjected to Sanger sequencing for taxonomic identification. Bacterial isolates were identified by amplifying 16S rRNA genes using universal primers [30] 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATYMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-TACGGCTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). PCR amplification was performed in a 50 μL reaction volume containing 25 μL of 2 × T5 Super PCR Mix (Colony) (Beijing Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), 4 μL of 10-μM forward and reverse primers, 2 μL of bacterial culture, and 19 μL of ddH2O. PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 10 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 56 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 50 s; and a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. The sequences were analyzed with the EzBioCloud [31] and NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) databases. Isolates identified as Curtobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Erwiniaceae were selected and subjected to BOX-PCR [32] analysis to eliminate duplicates, resulting in 7 Curtobacterium, 2 Pseudomonas, 4 Pantoea and 1 Rosenbergiella (No isolates of Sphingomonas or Frigoribacterium were detected.). Two synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) were subsequently constructed: SynCom9 (9 strains, including 7 Curtobacterium and 2 Pseudomonas strains) for inoculation onto DBQ cultivar, and SynCom5 (5 strains, including 4 Pantoea strains and 1 Rosenbergiella strain) for inoculation onto the J21 cultivar.

In 2024, DBQ and J21 were planted in sterile soil under greenhouse conditions. The optical density (OD600nm) of each bacterial strain was adjusted to 0.5, and strains were combined in equal proportions. Bacterial cells were collected via centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min, resuspended in sterile water, and applied during the blooming stage. Panicles were sprayed with bacterial suspensions: DBQ-M (SynCom9 on DBQ), J21-M (SynCom5 on J21). Filter paper was used to protect surrounding plant parts, and the control group was treated with sterile water. Samples of panicles were collected during the late filling stages, with panicles from three plants per replicate excised using sterile scissors and pooled into a single sample, yielding 16 samples (2 varieties × 2 inoculation treatments × 4 replicates). Samples were immediately frozen at −80 ℃ for metabolite analysis. Once the plants reached full maturity, seeds were harvested for quality assessment.

Results

Quality difference between high- and low-eating-quality foxtail millet

We compared the quality of two high-eating-quality foxtail millet varieties (Y1 and J21) with one low-eating-quality variety (DBQ). Notable differences in panicle morphology and millet coloration were observed, with J21 exhibiting the most intense yellow color and the highest b* value (yellow/blue value) (Fig. 1A, B). To investigate these color variations, we measured carotenoid content, the primary yellow pigment in foxtail millet. J21 had significantly higher carotenoid content than both Y1 and DBQ (Fig. 1C; p < 0.05).

We also measured amylose content and viscosity, which are related to taste quality. Higher amylose content generally correlates with lower viscosity and reduced quality. Both DBQ and J21 exhibited significantly higher amylose content than Y1 (Fig. 1D; p < 0.05), with DBQ having the highest amylose proportion at 22% (Fig. 1E). DBQ’s final viscosity (FV) was significantly lower than that of Y1 and J21 (Fig. 1F; p < 0.05), indicating a firmer texture and lower eating quality. Setback viscosity (SB) and breakdown viscosity (BD) reflect the stability and texture of foxtail millet starch during gelatinization. Lower SB indicates poorer stability and gel ability during cooling, while higher BD indicates lower stability during heating. DBQ had significantly lower SB than Y1 and J21 (Fig. 1G; p < 0.05), but no significant difference in BD was observed (Fig. 1H). This suggests that DBQ has poorer stability and lower eating quality. No significant difference in pasting temperature (PTM) was found among the varieties (Fig. 1I), indicating similar gelatinization difficulty. These results suggest that the quality differences among foxtail millet varieties maybe attributed to variations in metabolite profiles.

Depletion of bitter compounds in high-eating-quality foxtail millet panicles

PCA revealed significant effects of foxtail millet varieties (Fig. S2A; p = 0.033), sampling tissues (Fig. S2B; p = 0.001), and developmental stages (Fig. S2C; p = 0.006) on metabolite diversity. Metabolite diversity significantly differed between high- and low-eating-quality foxtail millet (Fig. S2D, E; p < 0.05). To further explore the effect of eating-quality on metabolite profiles, we analyzed differential metabolites based on relative abundance between high- and low-eating-quality foxtail millet varieties (Fig. 2A-H). At the blooming stage, Y1 and J21 shared four enriched metabolites (C2239, C0423, C2307 and C0289) in panicles compared to DBQ (Fig. 2C, D; Table S1). At the late filling stage, Y1 and J21 shared one enriched metabolite (C2359) in flag leaves (Fig. 2E, F; Table S1), while 13 metabolites were depleted in the panicles of both varieties compared to DBQ, including C2359 (Fig. 2G, H; Table S1). Notably, metabolites C2307 and C0289 were consistently depleted in both blooming and late filling stages of Y1 and J21 (Table S1). Further comparison with the PlantMolecularTasteDB database (PMTDB http://plantmoleculartastedb.org) revealed that four metabolites depleted in high-eating-quality foxtail millet panicles, C2359, C2241, C0408, and C2316, are associated with bitter flavor, with C2316 also linked to astringent flavor. The remaining 9 metabolites could not be identified using the flavor database (Table 1). This suggests that bitter compounds are depleted in the panicles of high-eating-quality foxtail millet.

Differential microbes enriched in the panicles of high-eating-quality foxtail millet

To investigate the role of microbes in foxtail millet quality, we analyzed the microbial communities in the flag leaves and panicles of varieties with differing eating qualities. Both foxtail millet variety (Fig. S3A; p = 0.035) and sampling tissue (Fig. S3B; p = 0.001) significantly influenced microbial diversity, with tissue type having the most pronounced effect. However, developmental stages had no significant impact on microbial diversity (Fig. S3C). While microbial diversity differences between high- and low-eating-quality foxtail millet were minimal (Fig. S3D, E), variety influenced microbial diversity more in flag leaves than in panicles (Fig. 3A, B). There was a significant difference in the observed features and Shannon index of flag leaves between Y1 and DBQ, but no significant difference was observed in panicles (Fig. 3C-F).

To identify key differential microbes associated with varying eating qualities, we analyzed the differential relative abundance of ASVs. Due to the limited differences observed at the blooming stage, we focused on the late filling stage. Y1 and J21 revealed both enriched and depleted microbes compared to DBQ (Fig. 3G-J). Specially, ASV9 (Curtobacterium), ASV18 (Sphingomonas), ASV36 (Curtobacterium), ASV80 (Pseudomonas) and ASV103 (Frigoribacterium) were significantly enriched in the panicles of Y1 and J21 (Fig. 3I, J; Table S2), while ASV383 (Erwiniaceae) and ASV528 (Erwiniaceae) were depleted in the panicles (Fig. 3I, J; Table S2). Further analysis showed that Curtobacterium was more abundant in the flag leaves of Y1 and J21 compared to DBQ (Fig. S4A, B). In addition, Curtobacterium, Pseudomonas and Sphingomonas were detected in seed microbial communities, with higher abundances in Y1 and J21 (Fig. S5). In contrast, Pantoea was most abundant in DBQ seeds (Fig. S5) suggesting that microbes enriched in the panicles may be vertically transmit to offspring via the seeds.

Association between bitter compounds, differential microbes, and foxtail millet quality

To explore the relationship between differential microbes, metabolites, and foxtail millet quality, we focused on the panicles at the late filling stage and performed correlation analyses. A negative correlation was observed between the enriched microbes (ASV9, ASV18, ASV36, ASV80, ASV103) and the 13 depleted metabolites in high-eating-quality panicles (Fig. 4A), indicating an inverse relationship with the four identified bitter compounds.

Further analysis revealed that viscosity-related indicators (FV, BD, SB) were significantly negatively correlated with depleted metabolites (Fig. 4B; p < 0.05), indicating a negative association between these indicators and bitter compounds. Additionally, BD, b* and carotenoids content showed positive correlations with ASV9 (Fig. 4C; p < 0.05), BD, and carotenoids content showed positive correlations with ASV36 (Fig. 4C; p < 0.05), BD and b* were also positively correlated with ASV80 (Fig. 4C; p < 0.05). However, the correlations between quality indicators and differential microbes were weaker compared to those with differential metabolites.

Design of SynComs with potential effects on millet coloration

To further verify the effect of microbes enriched in high-eating-quality foxtail millet on its quality, we selected 7 Curtobacterium and 2 Pseudomonas strains from the phyllosphere of Y1 and J21 to construct SynCom9. This SynCom9 was inoculated onto DBQ panicles during the blooming stage. Compared to the non-inoculated DBQ control (DBQ-CK), SynCom9 inoculation (DBQ-M) resulted notable changes in coloration, including a significant increase in carotenoid content and a higher b* (yellow/blue value) (Fig. 5A-C; p < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed in starch content or viscosity indicators (Fig. 5D-I).

Conversely, to evaluate the impact of microbes enriched in low-eating-quality foxtail millet, we constructed a SynCom composed of strains isolated from the DBQ phyllosphere, which was inoculated onto J21 panicles. Differential microbial analysis revealed significant enrichment of ASV383 and ASV528 (both belonging to Erwiniaceae) in low-eating-quality foxtail millet (Fig. 3I, J; Table S2). 0.4 Pantoea and 1 Rosenbergiella strains from the DBQ phyllosphere, also belonging to Erwiniaceae, were similarly selected as above to construct SynCom5. Inoculation of SynCom5 onto J21 panicles resulted in noticeable changes in coloration, including a significant decrease in carotenoid content and a lower b* value (Fig. S6A-C; p < 0.01). However, similar to DBQ, no significant differences were observed in starch content or viscosity indicators in J21-M after SynCom5 inoculation (Fig. S6D-I).

SynCom9 affects bitter compounds in panicles

To assess the impact of differential microbes enriched in high- and low-eating quality foxtail millet on metabolite profiles, we analyzed metabolites in the panicles. Post-inoculation with SynCom9 (DBQ-M) resulted in significant changes in the metabolite diversity of DBQ compared to the non-inoculated control (DBQ-CK) (Fig. 6A; p = 0.028). Specially, SynCom9 enrichment in DBQ panicles let to the accumulation of only one bitter metabolite (M328T495), while depleting seven bitter metabolites (M138T58, M279T726, M295T754, M696T712, M138T72, M177T356, M667T398) (Fig. 6B; Table 2). In contrast, the inoculation of SynCom5 on J21 (J21-M) resulted in significant changes in metabolite diversity (Fig. S7A; p = 0.032). Compared to the non-inoculated J21 control (J21-CK), SynCom5 enriched one bitter metabolite (M307T654) and one sour metabolite (M200T263), while depleting three bitter metabolites (M150T55, M423T888, M423T833), two sweet metabolites (M116T54, M87T53), and one acrid metabolite (M133T72) (Fig. S7B; Table 2). These results suggest that SynCom9 effectively reduced seven bitter compounds in DBQ panicles, while the effect of SynCom5 on flavor compounds in J21 was more complex.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of phyllosphere microbes and metabolites on the quality of foxtail millet. While previous studies have explored the effects of rhizosphere microbes on crop quality [15, 20, 21], the role of phyllosphere microbes remains underexplored. We selected foxtail millet varieties with differing eating qualities from earlier studies [33]. Due to the complex factors influencing foxtail millet quality, our evaluation incorporated appearance, palatability, and nutritional composition [34]. Our findings revealed that J21 (a high-eating-quality variety), had a more intense yellow color and higher carotenoid content than DBQ (a low-eating-quality variety) (Fig. 1A-C; p < 0.05). This may be due to the accumulation of carotenoids, which contribute to seed palatability [7]. Additionally, low-quality millet exhibited a higher amylose content and lower viscosity and SB (Fig. 1F, G; p < 0.05), consistent with previous studies showing that higher amylose content correlates with firmer texture and lower cooking viscosity [34, 35].

To investigate quality differences among foxtail millet varieties, we performed metabolomic analysis. At the late filling stage, 13 metabolites were significantly depleted in the panicles of high-eating-quality foxtail millet (Fig. 2G, H; Table S1). These included phenolic compounds, such as C2307 (dihydroconiferyl aldehyde) and C2316 (feruloyltyramine), along with benzene derivatives like C2225 (toluene), C0535 (hordenine), C2241 (tyramine), C2140 (phenylacetaldehyde), and C0408 (cathine), and alkaloids including C0535, C0408, and C0453 (demethylcoclaurine hydrochloride) (Table 1). Several of these metabolites possess bioactive properties: C2182 (salidroside), C2359 (p-coumaroyltyramine), and C2316 are known for antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer activities [36–38], while C0535 has been associated with anticancer effects [39]. Other compounds such as C2241 and C0408 may influence neuromodulation and blood pressure [40, 41], and C2140 is an aroma compound commonly in flowering plants [42, 43]. Bitter compounds, including phenolics, terpenoids, alkaloids, amino acids, nucleosides, and purines, are known for their antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and neuroprotective effects [44]. In this study, we identified C2359, C2241, C2316, and C0408 as bitter compounds in the PMTDB, with C2316 also classified as astringent (Table 1). Additionally, alkaloids and guanine (C2320) are known contributors to plant bitterness [8, 45]. Bitter compounds have low detection thresholds [9] and taste sensation more prolonged than other basic tastes [46], suggesting that their depletion could significantly improve the taste profile of foxtail millet.

Microbiome analysis during the late filling stage revealed significant enrichment of ASV9 (Curtobacterium), ASV18 (Sphingomonas), ASV36 (Curtobacterium), ASV80 (Pseudomonas) and ASV103 (Frigoribacterium) in the panicles of high-eating-quality foxtail millet (Fig. 3I, J; Table S2). Microbial composition analysis suggested that the enriched microbes in panicles might be predominantly seed-derived rather than environmentally recruited (Fig. S5), implying the possible establishment of specific microbial communities during the seed stage and their vertical transmission from parent plants to offspring [47]. Curtobacterium and Pseudomonas are widely present in both rhizosphere and phyllosphere [48–51]. Curtobacterium is associated with organic matter degradation in plant litter [52], while Pseudomonas is recognized for its role in pathogen resistance [53, 54]. In panicles, these microbes were negatively correlated with 13 depleted metabolites, including the four bitter compounds (Fig. 4A). This indicates that the higher abundance of Curtobacterium and Pseudomonas in high-eating-quality panicles is associated with reduced levels of bitter compounds. Previous studies demonstrated that bitter compounds, such as cucurbitacin, can shape microbial communities by selectively enriching taxa like Enterobacter and Bacillus [55], indicating that bitter compounds may influence microbial composition. In addition, viscosity-related traits were negatively correlated with the 13 depleted metabolites (Fig. 4B), while BD showed a positive correlation with Curtobacterium and Pseudomonas (Fig. 4C). However, the impact of differential microbes on viscosity and color was less pronounced compared to that of the metabolites (Fig. 4B, C). The correlation between differential microbes and metabolites was stronger than their direct correlation with quality traits (Fig. 4A), suggesting that microbes primarily influence plant physiological processes by modulating metabolite profiles [56], which subsequently affects quality. In summary, our findings indicate that the enrichment of Curtobacterium and Pseudomonas in panicles contributes to the reduction of bitter compounds and enhances foxtail millet quality, primarily by altering metabolite profiles (Fig. 7). This discovery highlights the potential role of phyllosphere microbes in modulating foxtail millet quality, offering a novel approach to improving crop quality through microbial interactions.

To investigate the impact of SynCom inoculation on grain quality, we focused on panicle inoculation to directly assess the influence of microbial communities on foxtail millet quality. We conducted sequence similarity comparisons, although the similarities with ASV9, ASV36 and ASV80 were not high (Table S3), they still fall into the same genus, so we selected the strains at the genus level. Millet coloration, a key consumer preference indicator [7], was evaluated to identify the factors influencing its appearance. SynCom9 promoted carotenoids accumulation in low-eating-quality foxtail millet, thereby enhancing its yellow coloration (Fig. 5). Conversely, SynCom5 reduced the content of carotenoids, thereby decreasing the depth of yellow color in high-eating-quality foxtail millet (Fig. S6). However, the SynComs had no significant impact on the taste or nutritional quality of foxtail millet (Fig. 5, S6), likely due to their tendency to affect coloration. Therefore, the SynComs associated with foxtail millet of varying eating qualities primarily influenced appearance by modulating coloration, while their effect on palatability and nutritional composition was minimal.

Microbes enriched in high-eating-quality foxtail millet were negatively correlated with bitter compounds (Fig. 4A). We hypothesized that inoculating DBQ with SynCom9 would deplete bitter compounds in its panicles. Our results confirmed that after inoculating DBQ with SynCom9, seven bitter compounds were depleted in its panicles (Table 2). Additionally, among the seven depleted bitter compounds in DBQ-M, M138T72 (tyramine) was also depleted in Y1 and J21 panicles in the 2023 field experiment (Table 1, 2). C2241 (tyramine) showed a significant negative correlation with ASV9 (Curtobacterium), ASV36 (Curtobacterium) and ASV80 (Pseudomonas) (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, M177T356 (ferulic acid) in DBQ-M and C2359 (p-coumaroyltyramine) in Y1 and J21 were cinnamic acid derivatives (Table 1, 2). Thus, the depletion of M138T72 and M177T356 in DBQ after SynCom9 inoculation could be attributed to Curtobacterium and Pseudomonas. Overall, the SynCom9 inoculations significantly impacted the metabolites in foxtail millet panicles, particularly those associated with bitterness. For low-eating-quality SynCom5, we anticipated a shift in the metabolic profile of J21-M toward a more DBQ-like, bitter-rich profile. However, it resulted in a mixed pattern of metabolic changes. This may be attributed to the relatively low abundance of enriched ASVs in DBQ panicles, with only ASV383 and ASV528 identified (Fig. 3I, J; Table S2). Consequently, strain isolation for SynCom5 was guided at the family level (Erwiniaceae), rather than the more targeted genus-level isolation used for SynCom9. This broader taxonomic resolution may have contributed to the observed variability in metabolite modulation. Furthermore, differences between field and greenhouse environments may have influenced the metabolite composition of foxtail millet, as greenhouse-grown plants were cultivated in sterile soil, thereby excluding rhizosphere microbial contributions. In combination with distinct climatic conditions, these factors could have jointly impacted metabolite profiles. Nonetheless, microbes from high-eating-quality foxtail millet exert a stronger regulatory effect on bitter compounds than those from low-eating-quality foxtail millet.

Conclusions

This study investigated the role of phyllosphere microbes in determining foxtail millet quality. Significant microbial differences were observed between varieties of varying eating qualities, revealing a negative correlation between differential microbes and metabolites. In high-eating-quality millet, the enrichment of specific microbes and depletion of bitter metabolites were linked to improved quality. Moreover, the microbes enriched in high-eating-quality foxtail millet can affect its quality by altering its coloration and bitter compounds. These findings highlight the crucial interplay between the phyllosphere microbiome and metabolites in enhancing crop quality and offer a theoretical foundation for optimizing future agricultural practices.
