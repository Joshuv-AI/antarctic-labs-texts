Preparing for the next pandemic requires more than commitments
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Europe
Published: 2026

Two disease outbreaks in May this year have brought a troubling reality into focus. A rare virus aboard a cruise ship took 24 days from the first death to identify as Andes hantavirus, whereas the ongoing Bundibugyo ebolavirus outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo went undetected for months because appropriate diagnostic tests were unavailable. Although both are lethal, neither virus is likely to cause a global pandemic. Yet, both viruses serve as reminders that emerging infectious threats continue to test health systems and surveillance capacities. 6 years after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, Europe has strengthened preparedness, but important gaps in operational readiness for the next major health emergency remain.

Meanwhile, the risks of epidemics and pandemics continue to grow. Infectious diseases can spread rapidly by global travel and trade. Human encroachment into animal habitats and the wildlife trade create new opportunities for zoonotic spillovers. Climate change is expanding the geographical range of vectors carrying diseases, such as dengue, and emerging artificial intelligence could design the next biothreat.

Preparedness is entering a period of uncertainty. Since 2020, pandemic preparedness has been extensively discussed, but one of the essential pillars—international cooperation—has been substantially disrupted in the past 18 months since the USA withdrew from WHO. In May, 2026, the World Health Assembly postponed the adoption of the Pandemic Agreement, a legally binding treaty intended to strengthen the prevention, detection, and response to future health emergencies. The main obstacle remains disagreement over the Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing system, which seeks to balance the sharing of pathogen data with equitable access to the vaccines, diagnostics, and treatments developed from them.

These tensions expose a persistent challenge: balancing national interests, commercial incentives, and global health solidarity. The USA has bypassed these multilateral efforts, signing multiple bilateral agreements on outbreak data and commercial arrangements. Together, these developments reflect a growing fragmentation of global health governance when effective preparedness and response depend on international cooperation.

Acknowledging this new geopolitical reality, the European Commission launched the Global Health Resilience Initiative in May, 2026, to strengthen preparedness at national, regional, and global levels. Its success, however, will depend not on the number of strategies announced, but on how quickly they translate into operational capability. Emerging threats will not wait for policy timelines.

Recent assessments at global and regional levels offer a bleak picture of preparedness. The Global Preparedness Monitoring Board, an independent monitoring and accountability body convened by WHO and the World Bank, identified the erosion of trust and equity and insufficient financing as some of the most important setbacks to pandemic preparedness.

Meanwhile during 2024–26, the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) conducted Public Health Emergency Preparedness Assessments in 15 European countries, revealing progress but also persistent operational gaps that could undermine future responses. In many countries, preparedness plans have not been translated into clear operational procedures. Roles and responsibilities within incident management systems remain unclearly defined. Around half of the assessed countries do not conduct regular response exercises, and after-action reviews are inconsistently used to identify lessons from real events. Monitoring medical countermeasures remains fragmented, mapping of domestic and regional production capacity is incomplete, and demand forecasting often relies on historical consumption rather than risk-based modelling. Preparedness for zoonotic threats is further constrained by scarce cross-sectoral legislation and governance.

These findings point to a broader challenge. Europe does not have a shortage of preparedness plans but has a readiness gap problem. Plans that are not regularly tested, exercised, and translated into action will provide little protection during a rapidly evolving health emergency.

These recent assessments show that preparedness remains uneven and largely theoretical in some areas, and further action is urgently needed. The ECDC should rapidly expand preparedness assessments across the region, and governments should act on their findings. Standardised indicators of preparedness would strengthen accountability and enable progress to be monitored over time. Europe should also continue to champion international cooperation through WHO-led mechanisms, recognising that health security depends on collective action. The lesson from recent outbreaks is not that Europe has failed to learn from COVID-19, rather preparedness remains fragile at a time when epidemic and pandemic risks are increasing. The next health emergency will not wait. Europe should not either.
