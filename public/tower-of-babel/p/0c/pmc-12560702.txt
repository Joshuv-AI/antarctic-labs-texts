Modifying the reduction quality criteria of the Baumgaertner classification may increase its performance in predicting mechanical failure after intertrochanteric nailing
Authors: Germán Garabano, Sebastian Pereira, Fernando Bidolegui, Leonel Perez Alamino, Cesar A. Pesciallo, Michael R. Baumgaertner, Theodore Miclau
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:The main objective of this study was to assess whether modifying the lateral plane alignment criterion of the original Baumgaertner classification from <20 to <10 degrees improved its performance in predicting failure after nailing of an intertrochanteric fracture (ITF).Methods:Patients operated consecutively between January 2016 and January 2021 with a cephalomedullary nail for an ITF, with a minimum follow-up of 12 months, were retrospectively analyzed. The postoperative reduction was classified according to the original Baumgaertner classification and using the modified classification. The performance of the original and modified classifications was analyzed by a comparative analysis of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Univariate and multivariate analysis was performed to identify variables associated with failure.Results:A total of 282 patients were included. The failure rate was 10.63%. The area under the ROC curve of the original classification was 0.66 and of the modified classification 0.78 (P = 0.023). Independent variables that were associated with fixation failure were poor reduction according to the modified Baumgaertner classification (OR 7.81; P = 0.0001), the difference in neck shaft angle on AP pelvis radiograph between injured and uninjured hips (OR 1.22; P = 0.0003), malreduction on the lateral hip radiograph >10 degrees (OR 4.45; P = 0.003), fracture displacement in any direction >4 mm (OR 3.8; P = 0.006), a tip to apex distance (TAD) > 21 mm (OR 3.30; P = 0.019), and TAD >21 mm plus >10 degrees malreduction in the lateral plane (OR 4.70; P = 0.009).Conclusion:Modification of the lateral plane alignment component of the criteria used to grade the quality of intertrochanteric fracture reduction as originally proposed by Baumgaertner, from <20 degrees to <10 degrees significantly improved the classification's ability to predict fixation failure.Level of evidence:IV.

1. Introduction

Hip fractures in adults are a worldwide public health burden.1 As a consequence of the aging population, it is estimated that the incidence of these injuries will increase sharply in the coming years.1–3 In recent decades, the cephalomedullary nail has become one of the most commonly used implants in the fixation of intertrochanteric fractures (ITF).2,4–6 The failure rate after fixation with these devices is reported to be between 3.8% and 13%.6–9

Several variables related to the surgical technique have been associated with the likelihood of failure, including reduction quality and implant position.10–14

Different classifications have been designed that consider different criteria to evaluate the quality of the reduction after fixation of an ITF.14–17 Among them, a classification popularized by Baumgaertner et al14 is one of the most widely used.6,10,14,15 This classification is based on defining the ideal reduction of a intertrochanteric proximal fracture as a neck-shaft angle measured on anteroposterior radiograph of the pelvis being equal or slight valgus compared with the opposite hip, less than 20 degree angulation on the lateral hip radiograph, and displacement of the fragment <4 mm in any direction. Although some authors have validated this classification, others have used their own criteria,6,10,18–20 although these additional modifications have been poorly substantiated.

In the available literature, contradictory results have been reported regarding the ability of this classification to predict the presence or absence of failure after an ITF nailing.10,21 Kasigar et al10 analyzed 170 ITFs treated with cephalomedullary nails and found no significant association between reduction quality and failure. However, Murena et al21 evaluated 813 such cases and demonstrated that poor reduction quality was significantly associated with failure.

Previously, Garabano et al20 reported a series of 251 ITFs treated with cephalomedullary nails, in which it was observed that misalignment in the lateral plane was an independent predictor of failure, although none of the patients had a lateral misalignment >20 degrees. In the same analysis, the quality of reduction using the original classification as described by Baumgaertner et al14 was not identified as an independent variable related to failure. For this reason, we hypothesize that the criterion of reduction in the lateral plane <20 degrees of the original classification could be too broad, limiting the classification's ability to predict failure. Therefore, the main purpose of this study was to determine whether modifying the lateral plane alignment criterion used in the original Baumgartner classification from <20 degrees to <10 degrees improved its performance in predicting failure after cephalomedullary nailing of an ITF. Secondarily, the study sought to assess other variables related to implant positioning that were likely to be associated with failure.

2. Methods

After receiving respective ethics committee approvals from each participating institution, a retrospective analysis of the institutional databases was performed to identify all registered surgeries for ITF where patient follow-up was collected prospectively. We identified all patients with an ITF managed with a cephalomedullary nail fracture between January 2016 and January 2021 consecutively. Patients older than 65 years who were treated with a short cephalomedullary nail and had a minimum follow-up of 12 months were included in the analysis. Pathologic fractures, patients with a history of previous surgery on the affected and/or the contralateral hip, nonambulatory status before injury, multiple fractures, injuries caused by high-energy mechanisms, and diseases affecting bone metabolism were excluded.

All procedures were performed on a traction table under spinal anesthesia. Cephalomedullary nails with 130 degrees angulation, a single lag screw into the head, and static distal interlocking screws were utilized in each of the cases. Three doses of cefazolin (1 g every 8 hours intravenously) and low molecular weight heparin (for 4 weeks) were administered to all patients. Postoperative rehabilitation consisted of walking with full weight bearing according to tolerance from the first postoperative day, using 2 canes for the first 3 weeks, and a single cane until the sixth week. Radiographs were obtained at 3 weeks, 3, 6, and 12 months, with annual follow-up thereafter.

2.1. Variables Analyzed

The medical records were reviewed to record demographic data (age, gender), the presence of osteoporosis (according to bone mineral densitometry), and the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification. Preoperative radiographs were used to classify the fractures as stable (33A1.1, A1.2, A1.3, A2.1) or unstable (33A2.2, A2.3) according to the OTA/AO classification22 and measure the femoral neck-shaft angle (NSA) of the contralateral hip in both views, anterior-posterior and lateral. In the immediate postoperative radiographs (taken in the radiology suite), the NSA of the operated hip was measured, and the difference in degrees concerning the contralateral hip in both planes was calculated.

The antero-posterior (AP) X-ray used to assess the NSA in the coronal plane was taken with a focus on the pubis and 15 degrees of internal rotation of both limbs, from a distance of 1 meter, whereas a frog lateral X-ray was used to assess NSA in the sagittal plane (Fig. 1).

A, AP X-ray of both hips showing the NSA measurement. B, L X-ray showing the NSA in the sagittal plane. The white dotted line identifies the femoral neck, and a parallel line is drawn through the center of the neck.

Finally to assess fragment displacement, displacement >4 mm in the calcar zone of any fragment analyzed in AP and LAT X-ray was considered.

The quality of reduction was analyzed using both the original Baumgartner classification14 and the newly modified classification, where <10 degrees malalignment on the lateral radiograph was considered necessary for a good reduction. As established in the original classification, a reduction was considered good when both criteria were met, and acceptable or poor when only one or none were met, respectively.14 The tip–apex distance (TAD) was calculated using the method described by Baumgaertner et al,14 which considered a distance of <25 mm as satisfactory.

The medical records and radiographs were used to determine the presence or absence of failure in each patient. Failure was defined as the presence of screw cut-out; that is, varus collapse of the fracture with extrusion of the cephalic screw through the upper side of the femoral head or cut-through as axial migration of the cephalic screw with articular penetration. Nonunion was described as the absence of a bone bridge between the fracture fragments 9 months after surgery, lack of progression of healing on the radiographs over the previous 3 months, and presence of pain with weight bearing.

The measurements were performed by 2 independent observers with more than 10 years of experience in trauma and hip surgeries (G.G. and S.P.), using the Pack FujiFilm Corporation digital system. For disagreements on measurements such NSA or TAD, the average was used for analysis, whereas any disagreements on the reduction quality were resolved by consensus. The inter-rater agreement of measurements for neck shaft angle (NSA) alignment, TAD, classification described as Baumgartner and that modified Baumgartner reduction quality classification system was assessed by using Pearson coefficient for 100 radiographs analyzed by each surgeon (values of 0.87, 0.85; 0.91 and 0.91, respectively, were recorded).

2.2. Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were expressed as median and range or mean and SD according to their distribution. Qualitative variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. To analyze the role of each variable in relation to the failures, a Mann–Whitney test or Fisher test was performed and, with those that were significant (P < 0.05), a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. To calculate the performance of the classifications, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were made and the area under the curve (AUC) of each one was calculated. The analysis was performed using SPSS version 23 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

3. Results

Of 320 patients initially identified, 38 were excluded (26 died 12 months after surgery, 5 had contralateral hip surgeries, 1 patient had rheumatoid arthritis and was nonambulatory, 1 patient's fracture originated from a high-energy trauma mechanism, and 5 patients were lost to follow-up). Therefore, the study population included 282 patients with 282 fractures. The failure rate was 10.6% (n = 30; 13 cut-out; 7 cut-through; 10 nonunion). 3.1. Comparative Analysis Between Patients With and Without Failure

Out of 282 patients analyzed, 233 (82.6%) were female, of whom 29 (12.4%) experienced failure, whereas among the 49 males assessed, one (2.0%) failed. The gender distribution revealed that 96.7% of the patients who experienced failure were female, compared with 80.9% among those without failure (P = 0.038). In addition, there were no significant differences between groups in age (P = 0.54), incidence of osteoporosis (P = 0.82), ASA classification (P = 0.36), or fracture type stability (P = 0.91).

3.2. Reduction Quality

With the original classification as described by Baumgaertner et al, there was a significant difference in patients with good (P = 0.0008) and acceptable (P = 0.045) reductions between patients who failed and those that did not. With the modified classification, there were significant differences in patients with good (P = 0.0001) and poor (P = 0.0001) reductions between both groups.

3.3. Variables Related to the Reduction

When analyzing the NSA in the AP views, there were significant differences between both groups (129 vs. 126 degrees; P = 0.018) and in the differences in varus between the contralateral and treated hips in those that failed and those that did not (4.5 vs. 3.0 degrees; P = 0.017). In the lateral plane, there were significant differences between both groups (0 vs. 9.5; P ≤ 0.0001) and in the patients with misalignment >10 degrees (6.74% vs. 23.33%; P = 0.008). Fragment displacement >4 mm was present more frequently in patients who failed than in those who did not (40.0% vs. 16.16%; P = 0.0016). When the presence of fracture displacement was analyzed according to the plane, this difference was significant only in the lateral plane (P = 0.022).

3.4. Tip–Apex Distance

The group with failures had a higher TAD mean than those that did not (25.9 vs. 22.6 mm; P = 0.022). When categorizing patients with TAD >25 mm, the failure group also had a higher percentage (53.3% vs. 27.7% P = 0.005). When categorizing patients with TAD > or ≤21 mm, we observed that the failure group also presented a higher percentage (83% vs. 60%, P = 0.020). Finally, when grouping patients according to TAD (>25 or >21 mm) combined with misalignment in the lateral plane (20 or 10 degrees), only significant differences were found in patients who had a TAD >21 mm and misalignment greater than 10 degrees (P = 0.011).

3.5. Multivariate Analysis

The final gender- and age-adjusted model of the multivariate logistic regression analysis identified a protective effect of good reductions as classified with the modified classification. In contrast, a poor reduction was classified as an independent predictor of failure.

Regarding the NSA, the results demonstrated that the probability of failure increased significantly with each degree of increase in the difference relative to the contralateral hip (in both AP and L views) and in patients with misalignment >10 degrees in lateral view. Fragment displacement >4 mm was identified as an independent variable related to failure, both in its global presence (without discriminating between AP and P) and in L view.

In addition, TAD >25 and >21 mm were also identified as independent variables for predicting failure. Finally, when analyzing these TAD values together with the misalignment in lateral plane (>10 degrees per the modification made in the classification), failure was predicted with greater predictive power (Table 2).

3.6. Comparative Analysis of Both Classifications

Based on the ROC curves, both classifications could predict failure. The original classification presented an AUC of 0.66 (indicating poor correlation), whereas the modified classification was 0.78 (strong correlation). The difference between them indicates that the modified classification presented significantly better performance (P = 0.023) and a stronger correlation to predict failure (Fig. 2). 4. Discussion

The main finding of our study was that with the modification implemented in the criteria defining good lateral plane malalignment of the classification initially described by Baumgaertner et al,14 the performance of the classification improved significantly. This modification was associated with a decrease in the failure rate of the reductions rated as good and increased failure rate in those reductions rated as poor. These findings were demonstrated in the multivariate analysis where, with the use of the modified classification, a reduction rated as poor was identified as an independent variable related to failure. Moreover, reductions rated as good had a protective effect on failure. This increased ability to predict failure with poor reductions, and the protective effect with good reductions, makes the modified classification a more useful tool than the original classification for the surgeon when performing ITF reductions.

Using the original classification as described by Baumgartner et al,14 Morvan et al23 reported reduction quality as an independent variable for the risk of failure. They reported that 0.89% of patients with good reductions and 12.2% of those with poor reductions presented with failure. These results are similar to our findings using the original classification, where 18% of fractures with poor reduction failed. In contrast, an analysis of 3066 ITF found no significant relationship between the quality of reduction and the presence of complications.24

In other series where modifications have been made to the original classification as described by Baumgaertner et al,14 Mao et al25 compared their modified classification with the classification modified by Chang. In their modification, the criterion for the displacement of the 4-mm fragments was changed to that with positive or negative medial support, and the scoring method of the original classification was also modified. The authors reported that although both classifications were able to predict failure, Chang's classification showed better performance, based on a significant increase in the AUC.25 These findings are similar to those of the current study, which found that the AUC of the modified classification increased significantly (0.66 vs. 0.78; P 0.032) relative to the original classification. Another report that evaluated modifications to the Buamgaertner criteria was that of Raghuraman et al.6 Although these authors did not justify the reason for changing the classification, they modified the AP alignment to 130–150 degrees, keeping the rest of the criteria unchanged. Grouping acceptable and poor reductions, they reported that unsatisfactory reductions were significantly associated with the risk of failure after multivariate analysis.

The importance of achieving proper reduction as a modifiable variable that can alter the placement of fixation for an ITF has been established.7,9,13,15 The current study supports this finding; multivariate analysis showed that for each degree of difference in varus in the femoral NSA relative to the contralateral hip, the risk of failure increased 1.22-fold. Varus reductions have been widely associated with failure by increasing loading.26 The current study also highlights the importance of correct lateral plane alignment, showing a significantly increased risk of failure similar to AP plane misalignment (OR 1.19). This finding is supported by a recent series of patients undergoing tomographic evaluation that demonstrated the importance of achieving adequate contact in the medial and anteromedial cortices to avoid failure.17

Relative to reduction, our analysis demonstrated the importance of avoiding fragment displacement as an independent variable related to failure. Song et al27 demonstrated the importance of avoiding a gap between fragments at the calcar in the lateral view and showed that these gaps were independently associated with failures. In contrast, Lobo-Escobar could not demonstrate that a >3-mm diastasis between fragments was an independent variable of failure.28

Regarding implant positioning, multiple studies have evaluated tip-to-apex distance as a variable related to failure.2,21,23,29 In our analysis, values > 25 mm increased the risk of failure by 3.03 times, consistent with previous findings by Baumgaertner et al14 and Morvan et al,23 but in contrast to that reported by Ciuffo et al.26

Given that the group without failure had a mean TAD of 22.6 mm, we decided to evaluate TAD >21 mm as a variable related to failure. This cut-off point proved to be more powerful than TAD >25 alone (OR 3.30 vs. 3.03). In addition, when we combined these TAD values with lateral plane misalignment, we found that TAD >21 mm plus lateral plane misalignment of 10 degrees was a stronger predictor of failure (OR 4.70) than the individual evaluation of TAD >25 or >21 mm in isolation. This reinforces the differences found in the modification of the original classification, proving that by lowering the TAD and the lateral plane misalignment, the chance of failure decreases significantly.

The limitations of this study are those inherent in a retrospective design, where although the percentage of failures was in line with other similar series,6,10,24,26 the study had a low number of cases of failure, which could give rise to type 2 errors. A strength of the study was the utilization of deep statistical analyses, methods selected to reduce bias, and, through multivariate analysis, diminish the possible effect of confounders.

The current study presents a modification of a widely used and straightforward classification that is familiar to most orthopaedic surgeons. The modification does not increase the complexity of the measurements, nor the way that the classification is applied, because it does not alter the way of cataloging the reductions. Finally, these analyses can also be considered as both a validation of the original classification and the modification presented in this study, because each of the criteria that make up the classification was analyzed individually. The modified classification, however, will require additional analysis of its external validity in the future.

5. Conclusion

The modification from <20 to <10 degrees in the reduction quality criterion in the sagittal plane of the Baumgartner classification significantly improves its performance, transforming the classification into a more effective tool to guide the surgeon to better avoid failures. In addition, we believe that the concept of a TAD >25 mm should be reevaluated for nail-treated ITF because a value > 21 mm + lateral plane misalignment (>10 degrees) was shown to be a strong predictor of failure in our analysis.
