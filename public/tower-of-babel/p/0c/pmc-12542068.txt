Genome-wide identification of the WIP family in foxtail millet (Setaria italica) and functional analysis of SiWIP3 in inhibiting growth in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana
Authors: Xiling Chang, Tianqi Song, Jiandong Ren, Xiaoxing Wang, Jianfei Zhou, Yaning Bu, Xiaohong Chen, Yuxin Wang, Siyi Li, Xinpeng Lei, Jishan Xiang, Xiaoke Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe wound-induced protein (WIP) is a member of the A1d subfamily of C2H2 zinc finger proteins and plays a crucial role in plant growth and development. Foxtail millet (Setaria italica) serves as a model organism for research on C4 crops. To date, the WIP family has been identified in several plant species, including Arabidopsis thaliana and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.), but it has not yet been reported in foxtail millet. In this study, we conducted bioinformatics analysis to identify five SiWIP genes in the whole genome of foxtail millet, and further examined their chromosomal distribution, gene structure, cis-elements, and conserved protein motifs. The analysis of tissue expression patterns of SiWIP3, SiWIP4 and SiWIP5 members, as well as their response to exogenous hormone treatments, indicates that SiWIPs demonstrate tissue specificity and exhibit distinct reactions to exogenous hormones. Notably, SiWIP3 displays a certain level of sensitivity to exogenous hormones and shows the highest expression in roots. Further research on SiWIP3 has revealed its nuclear localization, and the overexpression of SiWIP3 in Arabidopsis results in a reduction in leaf size, slower plant growth, and suppressed flowering. However, the inhibition was relieved by the exogenous application of gibberellin (GA3), leading to leaf expansion and promotion of flowering. RT-qPCR results revealed alterations in the expression levels of GA pathway related genes in Arabidopsis overexpressed lines, both before and after GA3 treatment. In addition, the dual luciferase assay demonstrated that SiKNOX1 or SiBPC6 significantly suppressed the expression of the SiWIP3. Protein interactions between SiWIP3 and SiTCP19 were confirmed through yeast two-hybrid and luciferase complementation imaging assay experiments. SiWIP3 may play a crucial role in GA pathway, thereby regulating the growth and development in foxtail millet. This research provides a fundamental basis for further elucidating the mechanism of WIP protein in plant growth and development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12069-9.Keywords: Foxtail millet, Wound-induced protein (WIP), Growth and development, The C2H2 gene family

Introduction

The most apparent morphological change observed during plant growth is the size of organs, which is predominantly influenced by both genotype and environmental factors [1–5]. Previous research has demonstrated that the regulation of organ size through control of cell expansion is primarily associated with plant hormones, including gibberellin (GA), auxin (IAA), abscisic acid (ABA) and brassinosteroids (BRs) [6–9]. The interplay among various phytohormones in regulating cell expansion and consequently influencing organ growth has been well established, with transcription factors (TFs) playing crucial roles in mediating phytohormone signals [4, 5, 10]. For instance, the transcription factor FUSCA3 (FUS3), which encodes proteins belonging to the plant-specific B3 transcription factor ABI3/VP1 family, regulates the synthesis of two hormones, ABA and GA, to control whether Arabidopsis leaves develop into embryonic or adult leaves [11]. Similarly, overexpression of the transcription factor OsAP2-39 in rice, which contains an APETALA2 (AP2) domain, can result in decreased biomass and seed number by mediating the hormone pathways of ABA and GA [12]. Overexpression of MdBPC2 (A BARLEY B RECOMBINANT/BASIC PENTACYSTEINE TF) in apple serves as a suppressor of auxin synthesis, resulting in decreased plant height, modified leaf structure, and hindered root development [13]. ORA47 (octadecanoid-responsive AP2/ERF-domain transcription factor 47) plays a crucial role in the biosynthesis of jasmonic acid (JA) and ABA in Arabidopsis thaliana, and its overexpression results in stunted plant growth and reduced leaf size. Additionally, it is involved in regulating the biosynthesis or signaling of various plant hormone genes when plants experience injury and water stress [14]. Therefore, TFs play a crucial role in regulating plant hormones, which ultimately leads to changes in plant morphology. The identification of TFs involved in regulating plant hormones and the illumination of their functional characterization is significantly importance for uncovering the molecular mechanisms that govern plant growth and development.

Zinc finger proteins (ZFPs) constitute one of the largest families in plants. Based on the sequence and quantity of cysteine (Cys) and histidine (His) in the ZFP structure, ZFP-TFs is categorized into 9 subfamilies: C2H2, C2HC, C2HC5, C3H, C3HC4, C4HC3, C4, C6 and C8 [15]. The C2H2-ZFP family is the predominant TFs group within the ZFPs [16]. The C2H2-ZF domain is defined as a 30-amino acid sequence with two conserved Cys and two His residues that bind to a zinc ion tetrahedron, forming the structural motif CX2–4CX3FX5-LX2HX3−5H, where X represents any amino acid residue [17–19]. The C2H2-ZFP family has been identified in some plant genomes, with 176 members in Arabidopsis thaliana, 189 members in rice (Oryza sativa), 321 members in soybean (Glycine max), 211 members in maize (Zea mays L.), 457 members in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), 145 members in sorghum (Sorghum bicolor (L.) Moench), 301 members in B. rapa (Brassica rapa L.), and 124 members in foxtail millet (Setaria italica) [20–27]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, C2H2-ZFPs is categorized into five groups (A1, B, C1, C2, C3). The A1 subfamily of C2H2- ZFPs consists of four similar ZFs, which are further divided into four distinct subgroups known as A1a, A1b, A1c, and A1d. There are 13 members in subgroup A1a, 3 members in subgroup A1b, 2 members in subgroup A1c and 6 members in subgroup A1d [20]. The majority of C2H2-ZFP members are regulated as transcription factors, and they are associated with regulating plant growth and development, as well as responding to biological or abiotic stress [24, 28–30].

Wound-induced protein (WIP) belongs to the A1d subfamily of C2H2-ZFP, which contains the key sequence Trp-Ile-Pro. The WIP transcription factor possesses a classical WIP domain located at the C-terminal. Arabidopsis thaliana has 6 members: AtWIP1/TRANSPARENT TESTA 1 (AtWIP1/TT1), AtWIP2/NO TRANSMITTING TRACT (AtWIP2/NTT), AtWIP3, AtWIP4, AtWIP5 and AtWIP6 [20, 31–33]. WIP1/TT1 is essential for the proper expression of proanthocyanidin-pigment specific genes in the seed coat of Arabidopsis thaliana, and loss of TT1 results in yellow seeds instead of the wild-type (WT) brown seeds [31]. Furthermore, AtWIP1/TT1 also impacts the accumulation of flavonoids in endothelial cells during Arabidopsis seed development [34, 35]. AtWIP1/TT1, AtWIP3 and AtWIP6 in Arabidopsis thaliana act synergistically to promote normal root formation [36]. AtWIP2/NTT regulates embryonic framework development, and the loss of NTT will impact the development of pollen transport channels [37, 38]. In Cucumis melo, CmWIP1 is correlated with the expression of CmbZIP48 in the carpel primordium of male flowers, which inhibits the development of female organs in the flower, resulting in the formation of unisexual male flowers [39, 40]. In chickpea (Cicer arietinum), CaWIP2 regulates lateral root formation [41]. During gerbera petal growth, GhWIP2 functions as a transcriptional repressor and collaborates with GhTCP7 to suppress cell expansion by regulating phytohormone interactions [4, 5]. The WIP6/TOO MANY LATERALS (WIP6/TML) promotes the development of an appropriate ratio of the lateral to intermediate veins in the leaves of maize [42]. The WIP transcription factors are primarily associated with the growth and development of plant tissues and organs. However, it remains to be investigated whether WIP transcription factors regulate plant growth and development through mediating hormonal pathways.

Foxtail millet (Setaria italica) a fundamental crop in arid and semi-arid regions in Asia and Africa, possesses a compact genome and undergoes self-pollination, rendering it highly suitable as a model crop for comprehensive whole-genome studies of C4 model crop [43–47]. The genome of foxtail millet has been fully sequenced, providing a valuable foundation for the research of foxtail millet growth, development, and stress resistance [48]. The WIP family has been characterized in Arabidopsis and tomato, however, the members of the WIP family have not yet been identified in foxtail millet. Existing research has demonstrated that WIP transcription factors are primarily associated with the growth and development of plant tissues and organs. However, while GhWIP2 has been confirmed in gerbera to be involved in integrating different plant hormones to regulate petal tissue, further experimental investigation is required to elucidate the role of WIP transcription factors in regulating plant hormones. This study identified members of the WIP family in foxtail millet, and predicted and analyzed their evolutionary relationship, subcellular localization, and promoter cis-elements. Furthermore, we measured the expression pattern of SiWIPs (SiWIP3, SiWIP4 and SiWIP5) with exogenous hormones (GA, IAA and ABA) and their tissue expression pattern in foxtail millet seedlings treated. Subsequently, subcellular localization, transcriptional self-activation assay, validation of protein interactions, dual luciferase assay, and phenotypic characterization of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana were conducted for SiWIP3. The objective of our current study is to further investigate the role of the WIP family in regulating plant hormones and thereby modulating foxtail millet growth and development, as well as to establish a theoretical basis for genetic improvement of foxtail millet.

Result

Identification and chromosome distribution of five SiWIP members of foxtail millet genome

The NCBI-CDD and the SMART website were utilized to identify protein sequences containing intact C2H2 and C-terminal WIP domains, resulting in the identification of five candidate SiWIP genes in the foxtail millet genome (Fig. S1). Five SiWIP genes were identified on four different chromosomes of foxtail millet and were named based on their respective chromosome locations (Fig. S2). In the genome, SiWIP genes are unevenly distributed across chromosomes. Specifically, two SiWIPs are located on chromosome Ⅱ, while one SiWIP is present on each of chromosomes Ⅲ, Ⅳ and Ⅵ. In addition, Muthamilarasan et al. (2014) systematically identified 124 C2H2-type zinc finger transcription factors (SiC2H2) in foxtail millet and uncovered the response patterns of 9 candidate genes under salinity, dehydration and cold stress conditions [27]. This study further identified 5 members of the SiWIP family, namely SiWIP1 (SiC2H2_022), SiWIP2 (SiC2H2_027), SiWIP3 (SiC2H2_040), SiWIP4 (SiC2H2_049), and SiWIP5 (SiC2H2_075). The functions of these 5 SiWIP genes have not been reported, and their biological functions remain to be elucidated.

Physicochemical properties, subcellular localization, and expression prediction of SiWIP family members

The five SiWIP proteins of foxtail millet range in size from 355 to 433 amino acids, with relative molecular weights ranging from 38536.66 to 46661.75 Da. Approximately half of the proteins have low isoelectric points (PI < 7.0). Subcellular localization predictions for the foxtail millet WIP family indicate that all five WIP members are distributed in the nucleus (Table 1). The expression patterns of SiWIPs in roots, stems, leaves, and spikes in foxtail millet indicate that SiWIP3 exhibits the highest level of expression in roots, followed by leaves; while the expression of SiWIP1 and SiWIP4 was higher in the spikes. Additionally, the expression of SiWIP2 was higher in the stems, and that of SiWIP5 was higher in the leaves (Fig. S3). These findings suggest that members of the WIP family may play distinct biological roles within the cell nucleus in various tissues during plant growth and development.

Phylogenetic analysis of the WIP family

According to the phylogenetic relationship, SiWIPs in foxtail millet can be categorized into three groups. Among these groups, SiWIP2, SiWIP4, and SiWIP5 are closely related and may have similar functions. The relatives of WIP family proteins in foxtail millet and maize show a closer relationship than those in Arabidopsis (Fig. 1), which is consistent with the evolutionary relationship of plants. Arabidopsis has been investigated as a C3 model crop, while foxtail millet has been studied as a C4 model crop. The close phylogenetic relationship of WIP between foxtail millet and maize suggests that the evolution of WIP in C3 and C4 crops may differ.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of SiWIP family members

Based on phylogeny and gene structure, the five SiWIP members were classified into three clades. Specifically, SiWIP1 and SiWIP3 were grouped into separate clades, while SiWIP2, SiWIP4, and SiWIP5 formed another clade, which was consistent with the phylogenetic relationship (Fig. 2A). Both SiWIP5 and SiWIP2 contain all moieties, while SiWIP4 contains nine motifs except motif 9. Additionally, SiWIP3 contains six motifs except motifs 5, 7, 8 and 10. On the other hand, SiWIP1 contains five motifs (motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, and 9), with motif 1 containing the WIP domain. This indicates that all five genes are members of the WIP family with both the C2H2 domain and WIP domain (Fig. 2B). Exon and intron structure analysis further showed that SiWIPs contain two exons and one intron, and each type having similar genetic structure (Fig. 2C). There are variations in the length of gene sequence composition among SiWIP members, suggesting that the exons and introns of this gene family may have undergone changes during the process of evolution.

Cis-acting element analysis of SiWIP members

Cis-acting elements in the promoters of five SiWIP genes in foxtail millet were further predicted using Plant CARE online website (Fig. S4). The prediction results revealed that, in addition to the basic elements (CAAT-box and TATA-box), a significant number of cis-acting elements associated with light response, environmental stress, hormone response and development were also identified within the promoter regions of the five SiWIP genes. Most SiWIP genes contain photoresponsive elements (G-box, ACE, GT1-motif, GATA-motif, Box 4 and TCT-motif). The SiWIP1, SiWIP2, and SiWIP3 contain drought-induced response elements MBS, while the SiWIP2 and SiWIP5 contain antioxidant stress response elements (ARE) and hypoxia induction elements (GC-motif). SiWIP1, SiWIP2, SiWIP3, and SiWIP5 all contain methyl jasmonate response elements (TGACG-motif) and non-typical auxin-responsive elements (as-1). Additionally, the SiWIPs all contain ABA responsive elements (ABRE). The SiWIP3 contains the GA responsive element (P-box), the low temperature responsive element (LTR) and the WUN-motif. Additionally, some SiWIPs contain elements related to growth and development, such as meristem expression (CAT-box), substance metabolism regulatory element (O2-site), MYB (MER), MYBHv1 binding site (CCAAT-box), and so on. These findings indicate that SiWIPs may be modulated by transcription factors associated with light, stress, and growth and development, thereby contributing to plant photosynthesis, growth and development, hormone regulation, and stress response.

The tissue-specific expression patterns of SiWIPs in foxtail millet and the analysis of their expression patterns under exogenous hormone treatment

To validate the results of cis-element analysis and further elucidate the response of SiWIPs (SiWIP3, SiWIP4 and SiWIP5) genes to different exogenous phytohormones, qRT-PCR was employed to assess the expression of SiWIPs in foxtail millet leaves under normal conditions and following treatment with 10 µM GA3, 10 µM IAA, and 50 µM ABA (Fig. 3 and Fig. S5). In the absence of exogenous hormones, the expression level of SiWIP3 decreased at 1 h and 3 h, increased at 6 h, 12 h and 24 h, and reached its peak at 6 h (Fig. 3). In addition, the expression levels of SiWIP4 and SiWIP5 also reached their peak at 6 h in the control group (Fig. S5). These results indicate that SiWIPs may be regulated by the circadian rhythm. The expression levels of SiWIP3 decreased at 1 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h following treatment with 10 µM GA3 compared to the control, with the most significant decrease being observed at 24 h (Fig. 3A). After treatment with 10 µM IAA, the expression of SiWIP3 decreased at 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h and 24 h compared to the control, reaching its minimum level at 6 h (Fig. 3B). Additionally, SiWIP3 demonstrated an antagonistic regulation pattern after 3 h of treatment with 10 µM GA3 and 50 µM ABA (Fig. 3A and C). It is suggested that SiWIP3 may be regulated by a variety of endogenous hormones, including GA, ABA and IAA. SiWIP4 displayed an antagonistic regulatory pattern at 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, and 24 h after treatment with 10 µM GA3 and 50 µM ABA. Additionally, the expression of SiWIP4 was downregulated at 1 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h and 24 h following treatment with 10 µM IAA compared to the control (Fig. S5A). After treatment with exogenous plant hormones, the expression of SiWIP5 was found to be similar to that of SiWIP3. Additionally, at 3 h post-treatment with 10 µM GA3 and 50 µM ABA, SiWIP5 also exhibited an antagonistic regulatory pattern (Fig. S5B), suggesting that SiWIP5 and SiWIP3 may possess similar functions in response to hormonal regulation.

To determine the tissue expression pattern of SiWIPs, measurements were taken on the roots, stems, and leaves of hydroponically grown foxtail millet at the fifth leaf stage. The results revealed that the expression level of SiWIP3 was highest in the roots, followed by the leaves (Fig. 3D). Additionally, the expression level of SiWIP4 was found to be highest in stems (Fig. S6A), while the expression level of SiWIP5 was highest in leaves (Fig. S6B). These findings were consistent with the predicted results (Fig. S3), suggesting that SiWIPs may serve specific biological roles in various plant parts or tissues throughout the course of plant growth and development. The promoter region of SiWIP3 contains the GA responsive element (P-box), ABA responsive elements (ABRE), auxin-responsive element (as-1) and methyl jasmonate response element (TGACG-motif). Besides, SiWIP3 exhibit higher sensitivity to exogenous hormones and demonstrate the highest expression levels in plant roots. Consequently, we have chosen SiWIP3 for further investigation of its functionality.

Subcellular localization and transcriptional activation assay of SiWIP3

SiWIP3 was predicted to be localized in the nucleus, so subcellular localization was employed to validate this prediction. Co-transfection of nuclear Marker with GFP null into tobacco revealed that the fluorescent signal of the GFP null fusion protein appeared at any position, and the fluorescent signal of the SiWIP3-GFP fusion protein was the same as that of the nuclear Marker, which showed fluorescent signals on the nucleus of the cells (Fig. 4A). Subcellular localization analysis confirmed that SiWIP3 is indeed located in the nucleus, with experimental results largely aligning with the initial prediction. When pGBKT7 and the recombinant vector PGBKT7-SiWIP3 were transferred to yeast, they exhibited similar growth rates in SD/-Trp medium. However, pGBKT7-SiWIP3 did not grow on SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade medium, indicating that SiWIP3 did not have a self-activation effect (Fig. 4B).

The overexpression of SiWIP3 in Arabidopsis resulted in the Inhibition of growth in transgenic Arabidopsis

To analyze the function of SiWIP3 during growth and development, transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing SiWIP3 under the control of the CaMV35S promoter were generated. The T1 generation of Arabidopsis with overexpressed SiWIP3 exhibited dwarfism and a significant reduction in pod numbers (Fig. S7). Then, three independent homozygous T3 lines overexpressing SiWIP3 (OE1, OE2 and OE3) were selected for further functional analysis. After performing RT-PCR, no bands were detected in the WT lines. However, bands were successfully detected in the overexpressed (OE) lines, indicating successful transformation of SiWIP3 in Arabidopsis thaliana (Fig. 5A and Fig. S8; Table S1). WT and transgenic seedlings were cultivated on 1/2 MS medium for a duration of 10 days. The transgenic Arabidopsis exhibited shorter roots and smaller leaves compared to the WT (Fig. 5B and C). WT and transgenic seedlings were grown under normal conditions for 2 weeks. The SiWIP3 overexpressed Arabidopsis exhibited slower growth and smaller leaf size at the seedling stage. However, the application of exogenous GA3 promoted leaf expansion in the SiWIP3 overexpressed Arabidopsis to some extent (Fig. 5D). Under normal growth conditions, the leaf length, leaf width, petiole length, and total rosette area of the largest leaf in transgenic Arabidopsis exhibited significant reductions compared to those of the WT. Exogenous gibberellin treatment significantly enhanced the growth of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana, leading to a significant increase in total rosette area (Fig. 5E - H). Overexpression of SiWIP3 resulted in delayed flowering and smaller plants compared to the WT when grown under normal conditions for 5 weeks. However, exogenous GA treatment promoted flowering in transgenic Arabidopsis (Fig. S9). These findings suggest that the SiWIP3 gene may function as an inhibitory factor in the GA pathway.

The expression of SiWIP3 affects the transcription levels of GA-related genes in Arabidopsis thaliana

To further analyze the phenotypic changes of Arabidopsis thaliana following overexpression of the SiWIP3 gene and to investigate the mechanism by which exogenous GA3 treatment reduces the degree of leaf expansion inhibition, we have selected several genes related to GA synthesis, catabolic metabolism, and signal transduction for observation at the transcriptional level. Two genes related to GA synthesis (AtGA20ox1 and AtGA20ox2), two genes related to GA catabolism (AtGA2ox6 and AtGA2ox7), two genes encoding GA receptors (AtGID1a and AtGID1c), and three GRAS-regulated genes of the DELLA subfamily (AtGAI, AtRGA and AtRGL1) were analyzed for gene expression in WT and overexpression (OE) lines treated with GA3 (Fig. 6). Under normal circumstances, the expression levels of seven GA-related genes (AtGA20ox1, AtGA20ox2, AtGA2ox6, AtGA2ox7, AtGID1a, AtGID1c and AtRGL1) were all up-regulated and significantly different from those of the WT. Specifically, the expression levels of AtGA20ox2, AtGA2ox7 and AtRGL1 were upregulated by 5.66 times, 9.44 times and 3.14 times respectively compared to the WT. After treatment with exogenous GA3, the expression levels of some GA-related genes (AtGA2ox6, AtGA2ox7, AtRGL1) were found to be similar to those of the WT. The introduction of SiWIP3 into Arabidopsis potentially resulted in altered GA pathways, leading to inhibition of leaf expansion, smaller leaves, and slow growth in the transgenic Arabidopsis. However, these effects were alleviated after exogenous GA treatment.

Identification of upstream regulators of SiWIP3

The results predicted by the PlantTFDB v5.0 website indicated that five transcription factors are likely to regulate SiWIP3 (Table S2). Seita.9G109500 (Homeobox protein knotted-1-like 1; SiKNOX1) is a member of the KNOX family and potentially recognizes a core motif of TG(T/A)CAC [49, 50]; Seita.4G020600 (Basic Pentacysteine 6; SiBPC6) is a member of the BPC family possesses the capability to bind to GAGA-motifs [51–53]. Additionally, we conducted an analysis of the SiWIP3 promoter at 2000 bp and identified two potential binding TGTCAC core motifs for SiKNOX1, as well as two potential binding GAGA-motifs for SiBPC6 (Fig. 7A). The determination of LUC/REN activity revealed that both SiKNOX1 or SiBPC6 significantly diminished the expression of the SiWIP3. Additionally, the fluorescence observed in the D-LUC corroborated these findings, indicating that SiKNOX1 and SiBPC6 reduced the fluorescent signal associated with the SiWIP3 (Fig. 7B - F).

Analysis of the proteins interacting with SiWIP3

To identify the proteins interacting with SiWIP3, we performed Y2H point to point experiments using SiPKGII and SiHMG-20 predicted by the String website, as well as SiTCP4, SiTCP19, SiZIP48 and SiWIP3 obtained through homology comparison in the literature. The experimental results revealed that SiWIP3 and SiTCP19 exhibited blue coloration in SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade-X-α-gal. However, SiPKGII did not exhibit normal growth on SD/-Trp/-Leu (Fig. 8A), suggesting that SiPKGII may not be suitable for the yeast system. To further validate the interacting proteins of SiWIP3, we performed LCI assays involving SiTCP19, SiPKGII, and SiWIP3. The results indicated that SiWIP3 and SiTCP19 exhibited protein interactions, whereas SiPKGII and SiWIP3 did not show any interactions (Fig. 8B and C).

Discussion

The wound-induced protein (WIP) family belongs to the A1d subfamily of the C2H2-ZFP family, and it has been demonstrated that members of the WIP family play a crucial role in regulating lateral root development, pollen tube transport, and morphological changes in organs [4, 5, 34, 41]. Roldan et al. discovered that WIP modulates the crosstalk between stress-related and growth-related hormones [54]. To date, six AtWIPs have been identified in the whole genome of Arabidopsis, and six SlWIPs have been discovered in the entire genome of tomato [33, 55]. However, the study of the WIP family in foxtail millet has not yet been reported. In this study, five SiWIPs were identified in the whole genome of foxtail millet, and the results of subcellular localization predictions indicated that these SiWIPs are predominantly located in the nucleus. The analysis of evolutionary relationships revealed that the WIP members in foxtail millet share a close phylogenetic relationship with those found in maize. SiWIPs all contain a single intron and two exons, but variations in gene sequence composition length indicate potential evolutionary changes in the exons and introns of this gene family. The results of the promoter cis-element analysis indicate that SiWIPs contain cis-acting elements associated with light response, environmental stress, hormone signaling, and developmental processes, suggesting that SiWIPs may play a role in plant stress responses and the regulation of plant growth and development. These findings provide us with an initial insight into the attributes of the foxtail millet WIP family.

The tissue expression patterns of SiWIPs and their response to exogenous hormones suggest that SiWIPs are specific to tissues and exhibit distinct hormone response patterns. Notably, the expression level of SiWIP3 was highest in root tissues, and it significantly decreased after a 24 h treatment with the exogenous hormone GA (Fig. 3A and D). The expression level of SiWIP3 also exhibited changes following treatments with the exogenous hormones IAA and ABA, demonstrating a decrease after IAA treatment (Fig. 3B and C). Putative cis-regulatory elements in the promoter region of SiWIP3 comprise gibberellin-binding element (P-box), ABA responsiveness (ABRE), auxin responsive components (as-1) and MeJA-responsiveness components (TGACG-motif) (Fig. S4). We therefore concluded that SiWIP3 may be regulated by multiple endogenous hormones, including GA, ABA and IAA. Further research on SiWIP3 revealed that overexpression of SiWIP3 led to reduced root length, smaller leaf size, slower plant growth, and inhibited flowering in transgenic Arabidopsis. Additionally, treatment with GA promoted leaf expansion and flowering in transgenic Arabidopsis. These findings suggest that SiWIP3 may act as a transcriptional suppressor in the GA pathway.

Leaf development is a complex process that necessitates the mutual regulation and interaction of plant hormones. IAA plays a pivotal role in all aspects of leaf development, while GA and cytokinin are accountable for regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, leaf senescence, and leaf complexity. On the other hand, ABA and ethylene have negative effects on leaf development but play a positive role in promoting leaf senescence [56–60]. GA and IAA are classic plant hormones that play crucial roles in various aspects of plant growth and development [61, 62]. In this study, RT-qPCR analysis revealed a significant increase in the expression of GA catabolic genes AtGA2ox6 and AtGA2ox7 in Arabidopsis thaliana overexpressing SiWIP3. Additionally, the expression levels of genes involved in GA synthesis, AtGA20ox1 and AtGA20ox2, were also upregulated. This is likely due to a reduction in endogenous GA activity within the transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana. However, the decrease in GA concentration will subsequently lead to an upregulation of AtGA20ox1 and AtGA20ox2 expressions due to feedback regulation mechanisms [63–65]. After treatment with GA, the expression levels of GA catabolic genes AtGA2ox6 and AtGA2ox7 in OE1 were found to be consistent with those observed in the WT. Importantly, the changes in AtGA2ox7 were more pronounced. The expression levels of AtGAI, AtRGA and AtRGL1 (inhibitor of GA signaling pathway), members of the GRAS regulatory gene family within the DELLA group, exhibited diverse changes. Specifically, in Arabidopsis thaliana overexpressing SiWIP3, the expression level of AtRGL1 was significantly up-regulated. However, after treatment with GA, the expression level of AtRGL1 returned to that observed in WT. These findings suggest that SiWIP3 may play a role in mediating GA hormone pathways and regulating plant growth and development.

Recent studies indicate that plant hormones are controlled by a sophisticated network, which governs the growth and development as well as stress resistance in plants, with transcription factors playing a crucial role [4, 5, 66, 67]. KNOTTED1-like homeobox (KNOX) TFs play a crucial role in regulating the homeostasis of six major plant hormones, including auxin, GA, cytokinin, ABA, BR and ethylene, and are essential for controlling organ morphogenesis [49, 50, 68–70]. The homeodomain (HD) sequences of KNOX proteins are nearly identical and recognize a core motif of TG(T/A)CAC [49, 50]. Basic Pentacysteine (BPC) TFs participate in the regulation of IAA and GA pathways, regulate leaf morphology and plant height, and exhibit the ability to bind to GAGA-motifs [51–53, 71–73]. Additionally, we conducted an analysis of the 2000 bp promoter region of SiWIP3 and identified two TGTCAC core motifs potentially bound by SiKNOX1, as well as two GAGA-motifs that may be bound by SiBPC6. The D-LUC experiment demonstrated a significant reduction in the expression of SiWIP3 by SiKNOX1 or SiBPC6, suggesting their potential roles as upstream regulators of SiWIP3.

The TCP family can be classified into two types, TCP-P and TCP-C, based on the primary structure of their DNA-binding domains [74–76]. The TCP-P type contains the PCF gene, while the TCP-C type contains the TB1, CYC, and CIN. The TCP family is primarily associated with plant apical dominance, flower development, lateral organ development, and plant growth and developmental processes. It has been demonstrated that the TCP-P type of proteins achieves functional specificity possibly due to altered DNA-binding preferences [77–79]. A total of 22 members of the TCP family were identified in foxtail millet, and SiTCP19 belonged to the TCP-P type, which was found to cluster with AtTCP21 (AT5G08330) in Arabidopsis through cluster analysis [80]. AtTCP21 and AtTCP7(AT5G23280) are closely associated in Arabidopsis thaliana. AtTCP21 (also known as CHE) functions as a transcriptional repressor that binds to TOC1 in order to regulate the expression of CCA1, thereby influencing circadian rhythms [78, 81–83]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtTCP7 is involved in the regulation of cell proliferation and has differential effects on cell size and DNA content in various organs. Transgenic material containing AtTCP7 exhibits growth defects and stunting. Additionally, both AtTCP7 and AtTCP21 play crucial roles in the development of leaves and hypocotyls [78, 84, 85]. Overexpression of GhTCP7 (accession number: CL4336.Contig_P1) in Arabidopsis inhibited leaf expansion, and similarly, overexpression of GhTCP7 in Gerbera hybrida inhibited the cell expansion of gerbera petals [5]. The protein sequences of SiTCP19 in foxtail millet exhibit a similarity of more than 90% to AtTCP7, AtTCP21, and GhTCP7. We hypothesized that SiTCP19 may have similar functions to AtTCP7, AtTCP21 and GhTCP7, and the interaction between SiWIP3 and SiTCP19 may play a crucial role in regulating foxtail millet’s the growth and development. WIP transcription factors in foxtail millet may play a pivotal role in modulating hormonal pathways, thereby influencing the growth and development of the crop. Discrepancies in the genetic control of WIP transcription factors could explain variations in their specific functions in plant development. Our research offers valuable insights into the regulation of plant growth and development by WIP-type ZFPs.

Materials and methods

Identification the distribution of WIP family and chromosomes in foxtail millet

The complete genome sequence data of foxtail millet was obtained from the Phytozome database (https://phytozomenext.jgi.doe.gov/) [86]. The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profile with zf-C2H2 domain (ID: PF13912) was downloaded from the Pfam database (http://pfam.xfam.org/) [87]. The HMMER software was employed to search for zf-C2H2 proteins in the foxtail millet genome data, using a truncation value of 1e−3 for the e-value. Utilizing the NCBI-CDD retrieval tools (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/cdd) [88] and SMART structure domain (http://smart.embl-heidelberg) [89] for validation of foxtail millet zf-C2H2 candidate protein sequences, we conducted an assessment of all potential proteins containing the C-terminal WIP core sequence in foxtail millet. The DNAMAN 9.0 software was employed for comparison and analysis of the WIP protein sequences in foxtail millet. Furthermore, the chromosome localization information of SiWIPs was acquired from the Phytozome database. The visualization analysis of SiWIPs chromosome localization was conducted using the MapGene2Chrom web v2 website (http://mg2c.iask.in/mg2c_v2.1/) [90].

Protein characterization, subcellular localization and tissue-specific expression prediction of SiWIPs

To predict the amino acid number, molecular weight, and isoelectric point of SiWIP proteins, the ExPASy website (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/) [91] was utilized. The subcellular location of SiWIP proteins was predicted using the Cell - PLoc 2.0 online website (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/Cell-PLoc-2/) [92]. Additionally, the SiWIPs expression in various tissues (root, stem, spike, and leaf) was predicted using the Setaria italica Functional Genomics Database (http://structuralbiology.cau.edu.cn/SIFGD/search.php) [93].

Phylogenetic analysis and classification

To analyze the phylogenetic relationship of WIP, phylogenetic trees were constructed using WIP protein sequences of foxtail millet, Arabidopsis thaliana [33], tomato [55], wheat, maize and rice. Protein sequences for Arabidopsis thaliana, tomatoes, wheat, maize and rice members of the WIP family were downloaded from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The ClustalW software was used with default settings for multiple comparisons of the WIP family among Arabidopsis thaliana, tomatoes, wheat, maize and rice. The MEGA 7.0 software (https://www.megasoftware.net) [94] was utilized to build a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree with 1000 bootstrap repeats.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis

The SiWIP gene sequence files (coding sequence and genome sequence) were obtained from the Phytozome database. The gene structure was analyzed using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0, http://gsds.gao-lab.org/) [95]. MEME (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) [96] was utilized for conservative base sequence analysis, with the largest number of parameters set to 10.

Analysis of cis-regulatory elements

Retrieve the genome sequence of each SiWIP member from the Phytozome database (2000 bp upstream of the ATG start codon) and use Plant CARE online tools (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [97] to predict cis-regulatory elements.

Plant materials, growing conditions and treatments

Seeds of foxtail millet variety Yugu 1 were potted under controlled conditions (temperature 28 °C/22 °C, light 15 h/dark 9 h, humidity 60–70%, light intensity 17000 lx). Yugu 1 serves as the reference genome for foxtail millet and was one of the most widely popular varieties in Chinese foxtail millet production during the late 20th century [98, 99]. To assess the response of SiWIPs in foxtail millet to exogenous hormones, foxtail millet seedlings were grown in soil until they reached the three-leaf stage, and then transplanted into 1/2 MS nutrient solution for pre-cultivation. When the seedlings reached the five-leaf stage, seedlings of similar growth size were selected and transferred to a solution containing 10 µM gibberellin (GA3), 50 µM abscisic acid (ABA), and 10 µM indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) for treatment, the plants treated with 0.1% anhydrous ethanol were used as the control for comparison. The entire plant underwent time series treatment (0, 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 h; 3 biological replicates). Furthermore, to determine the tissue expression pattern of SiWIPs, soil-cultured foxtail millet seedlings were grown until they reached the three-leaf stage and then transplanted into a nutrient solution of 1/2 MS for pre-culture. Once the seedlings reached the five-leaf stage, samples of leaves, stems, and roots were collected from each seedling (3 biological replicates) and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored at −80 °C in order to extract RNA for further experiments.

The WT Arabidopsis utilizes the Columbia ecotype, which is preserved in our laboratory, for genetic transformation and functional analysis of the SiWIP3. Arabidopsis was cultivated in a mixed soil (nutrient soil/vermiculite = 3:1, v/v) within a controlled incubator(temperature 25 °C/20 °C, light 15 h/dark 9 h, humidity 60–70%, light intensity 15000 lx). Prior to sowing, all seeds were disinfected with a solution of 3% NaClO and 70% ethanol.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) and tissue-specific expression analysis of SiWIP3, SiWIP4 and SiWIP5

Total RNA was isolated from Yugu 1 foxtail millet leaves using the TRIzol method. The expression levels of SiWIP3, SiWIP4, and SiWIP5 in hydroponic seedlings were quantified at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h under normal conditions and various hormone treatments (10 µM GA3, 50 µM ABA, 10 µM IAA). Furthermore, the expression patterns of SiWIP3, SiWIP4, and SiWIP5 in different tissues were examined. The first-strand cDNA was synthesized through reverse transcription using the EX RT kit (gDNA remover) from Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd (http://zomanbio.com/), item number ZR-108, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on a QuantStudio 3 real-time PCR system using Beijing Zoman Biotechnology Co., Ltd’s 2×HQ SYBR qPCR Mix (item number: ZF502) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Then the relative expressions of SiWIP3, SiWIP4 and SiWIP5 were calculated according to 2−∆∆ct. All samples were treated with a minimum of 3 biological replicates and 3 technical replicates, utilizing SiActin (ID: Seita.5G393000) as the internal control. The specific primers utilized are detailed in Table S1.

Cloning of the SiWIP3 gene in foxtail millet

Utilizing Oligo 7 software, specific primers (Table S1) were designed to amplify the CDS fragment of SiWIP3. The amplification was carried out using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (item no: P505) from Nanjing’s Vazyme Biological (https://www.vazyme.com/Home.html). After purification, the PCR products were ligated into the pEASY T1 cloning vector (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and transformed into DH5α competent cells (Tsingke, Beijing, China) using the thermal shock method. The positive clones were subsequently identified through PCR and sequencing analysis.

SiWIP3 subcellular localization and transcriptional self-activation assay

Protein localization analysis was conducted by determining the subcellular localization of green fluorescent protein (GFP) tags. The SiWIP3 coding sequence, lacking a termination codon, was amplified and inserted into the Pcambia1302-GFP vector under the control of the CaMV35S promoter (refer to Table S1 for primers). The GFP fusion vector and nuclear marker were introduced into tobacco epidermal cells. The co-transformation of an empty vector GFP and nuclear marker was utilized as a positive control. The fluorescence of GFP was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan) 48 h later. The empty vector pGBKT7 and the recombinant vector PGBKT7-SiWIP3 were transferred into the yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) strain (primers shown in Table S1) and cultured in SD/-Trp medium at 30 ℃. Colony growth was observed after 2 to 3 days. Inoculate single colonies into SD/-Trp medium and extract 1 µl for inoculation onto both SD/-Trp and SD/-Trp/-His/-Ade media. Invert the cultures and incubate at 28 ℃ for 3–5 days, observing colony growth and recording the results.

Production and phenotypic analysis of transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing SiWIP3

The open reading frame (ORF) of SiWIP3 was cloned into the pBI121 vector at the BamHI and SacI sites. The PBI121-SiWIP3 construct was then created under the control of the CaMV35S promoter, with primers as shown in Table S1. Subsequently, Arabidopsis thaliana was transformed using the Agrobacterium-mediated transformation method [100]. Inflorescences of wild-type (WT) Arabidopsis thaliana (Columbian ecotype; maintained in our laboratory) were immersed in a transformation medium containing Agrobacterium pBI121-SiWIP3 for 40 s. This allowed the Agrobacterium to effectively penetrate the plant cell wall and plasma membrane, infecting the female gametophyte or embryo sac, and integrating T-DNA fragments with target genes and components into the female gametophyte genome [101]. After infecting WT Arabidopsis thaliana (T0), the seeds of the T0 generation were screened on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin, and transgenic positive seeds of the T1 generation (individual plants) were harvested. Subsequently, the seeds of the T1 generation (individual plants) were screened on 1/2 MS medium containing 50 mg/L kanamycin, and transgenic positive seeds of the T2 generation were collected. The positive seeds of the T2 generation were screened using the same method, and the seeds of the T3 generation (single plant) were collected. The T3 seeds underwent screening on 1/2 MS medium containing 50 mg/L kanamycin before being transplanted for culture. Ultimately, transgenic homozygous lines were obtained, and 3 independent T3 generations were selected for follow-up tests.

In the germination root length assay, WT and transgenic Arabidopsis seedlings were cultivated on 1/2 MS medium for 10 days, followed by photography and measurement of root lengths. In the growth experiment, 7-day-old WT and transgenic Arabidopsis seedlings were cultivated on 1/2 MS medium and subsequently transferred to potted plants filled with a mixture of soil and vermiculite (3:1, v/v) for a period of two weeks. Both WT Arabidopsis and transgenic Arabidopsis were planted in the same pot to mitigate potential growth discrepancies arising from variations in incubator location and light exposure. For the exogenous gibberellin (GA3) treatment, two groups were established: an untreated group and a 10 µM GA3 treatment group. Each group was replicated three times with 6 pots in each replicate. The untreated group was watered normally, while the GA3 treatment group received 10 µM GA3 every 4 days for two consecutive treatments. Phenotypic changes in both WT and transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana were then observed. The leaf length, leaf width, petiole length, and projected rosette area (PRA) of Arabidopsis thaliana were measured using ImageJ software [102]. The largest leaf from Arabidopsis was selected for measurements of leaf length, leaf width, and petiole length. Three technical replicates were conducted for each measurement.

RT-qPCR analysis of GA related pathway genes

Total RNA was extracted from the leaves of transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings to analyze their phenotypic changes under normal growth conditions and GA3 treatment. Gene expression levels of genes involved in GA pathways in Arabidopsis thaliana were detected, with three biological repeats and three technical repeats performed. The internal reference gene used was Tubulin beta-2 (AtTUB2 ID: AT5G62690), and specific primers are shown in Table S1.

Analyses of dual-luciferase activity

The Plant Transcription Factor Database v5.0 (https://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/index.php) [103] was utilized to forecast the upstream regulatory transcription factors for SiWIP3. Furthermore, the SiWIP3 promoter 2000 bp (ATG = 0) was obtained from the Phytozome website (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) [86]. The coding sequences of the candidate protein was fully integrated into the pGreenII 62-SK vector, while the promoter region of SiWIP3 (−1712~ −129 bp) was inserted into the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector. The specific primers utilized are detailed in Table S1. In order to ensure the consistency of the experimental results, the bacterial solution of the control and the target carrier to be detected was injected on different parts of the same leaf to ensure the same growth background. The samples were cultured for 48 h under standard conditions (25 ℃/20 ℃, light 15 h/dark 9 h, light intensity 15,000 lx). Subsequently, the dual- luciferase signal was detected using a low-light-cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) imaging apparatus (PlantView100), with six biological replicates performed. In addition, leaves from the injection site were collected and analyzed using the Dualucif® Firefly & Renilla Assay Kit (No: F6075) provided by UELandy Biotech Co., Ltd. (http://www.uelandy.com/index.html). The experiment consisted of three biological replicates and three technical replicates.

Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) point to point assay and luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay of SiWIP3

The Y2H point to point assay was conducted utilizing the Y2H Gold-GAL4 yeast two-hybrid system strain [104]. Protein interaction was predicted using version 11.5 of the prediction website String (https://version-11-5.string-db.org/cgi/input? sessionId=brLqLTHI4146&input_page_show_search=on) [105]. The potential proteins that may interact with SiWIP3 in foxtail millet were identified through a blast comparison between the interacting proteins of the WIP family in other crops [39, 80], as described in a published article, and the foxtail millet genome available on the Phytozome website (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/blast-search) [86].

The full-length SiWIP3 was fused with the GAL4-DNA binding domain and cloned into the EcoRI and SalI sites of the pGBKT7 plasmid as bait. Additionally, the coding sequence of the candidate protein was inserted into the EcoRI and BamHI sites of the pGADT7 plasmid as prey (primers shown in Table S1). Prey and bait were co-transformed into yeast Y2H Gold for interaction analysis. The transformed yeast colonies were selected on SD/-Trp/-Leu and SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade-X-α-gal medium, and then incubated at 30 ℃ for 3–5 days. For the positive control, pGADT7-largeT and pGBKT7-p53 were transformed, while for the negative control, pGADT7-largeT and pGBKT7-laminC were used.

The Luciferase Complementation Imaging Assay (LCI) was utilized for further validation of the SiWIP3 protein interaction [106]. The stop codon in SiWIP3 was excised and ligated into the N-Luc plasmid at the KpnI and SalI restriction sites, while the coding sequence of the candidate protein was inserted into the C-Luc plasmid at the KpnI and SalI sites. Subsequently, the recombinant plasmid was introduced into agrobacterium for transformation. The bacterial solution of the control carrier and the target carrier to be detected is injected into different parts of the same leaf in order to ensure uniform growth conditions. After 48 h, the LUC signal was detected using a low-light-cooled charge-coupled device (CCD) imaging apparatus (PlantView100), with six biological replicates performed.

Statistic analysis

Excel (2019 edition) and SPSS (2020 edition) were utilized for the purpose of conducting statistical analysis. The experimental data analysis employed student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA, with significance indicated by letter notation when p < 0.05.
