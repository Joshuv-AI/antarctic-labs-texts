Examining the relevance of gait speed resiliencies to different stressors: the ARIC Study
Authors: B Gwen Windham, Hunter C Sylvester, James Henegan, Matthew McMullan, Anna Kucharska-Newton, Priya Palta, Jennifer A Schrack, Thomas H Mosley, Karen Bandeen-Roche, Michael E Griswold
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferent stressors may impact physical resilience differently (resiliencies). Objective metrics assessing different stressors’ “relevance” on associated resiliencies are lacking. We propose an approach for defining relevance metrics and potential moderating effects of sex and mortality on gait speed resiliencies.MethodsAmong 1559 ARIC participants (age 66-80 years) with “good” gait speed (≥1 m/s), 864 (55%) maintained good gait speed at follow-up (mean 6.5 years). Gait speed resiliencies were defined using 11 intermediary stressors (stroke, cancer, depression, etc.). Each stressor’s relevance was examined using Relative Ratios (RRs, 95% CI), comparing the prevalence of maintaining good gait speed among those experiencing the stressor (resilience) against the prevalence among those without the stressor, adjusting for demographics and body mass index. Mortality effects and sex differences were examined.ResultsStressor relevance varied from greatest (self-reported poor health: RR = 0.41, [95% CI, 0.26, 0.65], 59% reduction in maintaining good gait speed), to least influential (poor social support RR = 1.02 [0.89, 1.18]), with 6/11 stressors supported as relevant. Mortality and sex-differences were present; an intermediary fall was more relevant for women, where maintaining good speed versus dying was 76% less likely after a fall, RRWomen = 0.24 (0.12, 0.50), compared to men, RRMen = 0.75 (0.39, 1.44), yielding a 67% lower chance of women having good speed versus dying after a fall than men, relevance RR-ratio = (RRWomen/RRMen) = 0.33 (0.12, 0.86).ConclusionsStudies of physical resilience should use objective metrics to examine the relevance/importance of different combinations of stressors and functional outcomes when defining physical resiliencies. The relevance of gait speed resiliencies can vary by stressors, mortality inclusion in resiliencies, and sex.

Background

Physical resilience is often defined as the ability to recover or resist functional decline following an acutely stressful health event.1 “Physical resilience,” however, is a broad umbrella term that could be defined as resilience in many different functional measures, such as gait speed, robustness (vs frailty), strength, and others. The 2015 National Institute on Aging Workshop on Measures of Physiologic Resiliencies in Human Aging established an expanded concept where “resiliencies” are defined as a group of responses that can differ depending on the specific stressor examined and also the specific functional property of interest. For example, if surgery as a stressor affects gait speed but not grip strength, pre-operative gait rehabilitation could be a preventive therapy; if poor social support but not surgery as a stressor affects frailty, engaging social workers could help identify and mitigate deficits in social services, for example, transportation, meals, medication assistance.2 An evidence base showing specific stressors, for example, an elective surgery or acute hospitalization, are likely to result in less resilience in specific functional measure(s) such as gait speed, strength, or robustness is especially important in assessing risk versus benefit and developing preventive strategies for older adults who have a higher chance of encountering many different stressors.2

Among methodological gaps identified by the 2015 workshop, a key deficiency is an inability to identify the most relevant stressors for a specified physical function. In response to this gap, we detail an approach to constructing objective relevance metrics for assessing resiliencies, theoretically grounded in counterfactual contrasts; we use gait speed as a motivating exemplar physical function measure. Clinical resiliency studies to date have primarily examined functional recovery/maintenance versus decline only in people who experienced a stressor, without comparing these functional changes in people lacking the stressor. We show that this ignores an important and available contrast that helps define the relevance of the resiliencies being examined. That is, if people without the stressor experience the same functional decline as those with the stressor, it demonstrates the stressor may not be relevant for that functional measure. Yet, studies of the relevance of stressors are lacking. Furthermore, resiliencies may vary by characteristics such as sex, age, comorbidities, or other features, and by how deaths in the follow-up period are treated in the resiliency definitions. For example, gait speed differs for men and women, women tend to have higher rates of poor mobility than men, and men generally have higher mortality rates than women.3,4 Objective metrics reflecting both varying resiliencies and their relevance/importance across different characteristics such as sex and mortality risk, however, are lacking. We propose that researchers examine relevance of stressors to functional measures by comparing functional measures among those experiencing the stressor to those who did not experience the stressor. We chose one functional property, gait speed, to illustrate these concepts, anticipating researchers will extend this work using other functional measures, stressors, and analytic models.

Our primary goals were to (1) construct multiple resiliencies defined by pairing several selected stressors with gait speed for illustrative purposes, (2) construct and examine proposed objective relevance metrics for gait speed resiliencies (eg, is poor social support a relevant stressor for gait speed resilience?), (3) examine sex-differences as an example of how to approach factors that may affect gait speed resiliencies and their relevancies (ie, is a fall a more relevant stressor for women than men?), and (4) similarly, examine gait speed resiliencies and relevancies while considering mortality (ie, will stressors linked to higher mortality influence stressor associations with resilience?).

Method

Study participants

The Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) Study is a community-based prospective cohort that was designed to investigate cardiovascular disease in four U.S. communities: Washington County, Maryland; Forsyth County, North Carolina; Minneapolis, Minnesota; and Jackson, Mississippi. The baseline visit (1987-1989) comprised 15 792 men and women aged 45-64 years. Participants were invited to three additional triennial visits (1990-1992, 1993-1995, 1996-1998) and a fifth exam (Visit 5 [V5] 2011-2013) when functional assessments were introduced then repeated at ongoing follow-up visits, including V6 (2016-2017). For this analysis, we used V5 gait speed as the pre-stressor functional resilience measure and V6 gait speed as the post-stressor functional measure.

Gait speed

Gait speed was measured with a stopwatch at the participant’s usual pace over a four-meter course using a protocol standardized across study sites and visits, as previously described; the faster of two usual pace walks (meters/second [m/s]) was used.5 “Good” gait speed was defined as gait speed ≥1.0 m/s, (slow gait speed <1.0 m/s) based on prior work demonstrating gait speed >1 m/s is typical among higher functioning older adults and is associated with independence in activities of daily living, low likelihood of hospitalization, and approximates the mean gait speed similarly across age strata for men and women aged 60-90 years, approximating the age of ARIC participants at the index exam.4,6,7 Choosing a faster gait speed minimizes misclassification of gait speed non-resilient participants as resilient.

Stressors

Eleven prevalent stressors of interest were assessed after V5 and before V6, including three physician-adjudicated recorded hospitalization stressors (date-confirmed acute heart failure, myocardial infarction, and stroke) and eight self-reported stressors obtained during phone calls between V5 and V6 (any hospitalization, falling in the past year, subjective memory complaints, depressive symptoms, incident cancer, poor physical ability, poor overall health, poor social support).

Definitions of stressors

“Any Hospitalization” was defined as a self-reported overnight stay in the hospital that occurred between visits 5 and 6. Hospital records were obtained for cardio- and cerebrovascular event adjudication by physicians, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke as previously described.8 Participants who responded “no” to either “Can you count on anyone to help you when you need to make difficult decisions or talk over problems?” or “Can you count on anyone to help you with daily tasks like grocery shopping, housecleaning, cooking, telephoning, or giving you a ride?” were classified as having poor social support. Falls were self-reported as any fall in the past 12 months.9 Dates of calls were used to determine if events, including falls in the “past 12 months” occurred between V5 and V6; falls were not included as stressors if the 12-month pre-call timeline overlapped with the participant’s V5 exam. We defined the Subjective Memory Complaints stressor as endorsing “Do you have any complaints concerning your memory?”10,11 Depressive symptoms (hereafter referred to as “depression”) was operationalized as self-reported depressive or anhedonia symptoms over the past month using the Patient Health Questionnaire 2.12 Self-reported cancer diagnosis, which has been validated against Medicare claims,13 and date of a cancer diagnosis were reported during semi-annual phone calls. A cancer diagnosis occurring between visits 5 and 6 was considered a stressor. Poor physical ability was defined as any self-reported difficulty with one or more of the following tasks: walking from one room to another on the same level, walking ¼ mile, getting in or out of bed, walking up 10 steps without resting, doing household chores, preparing meals, managing money, dressing, lifting or carrying 10 pounds, standing up from an armless chair, stopping/crouching/or kneeling, and eating (including holding a fork, cutting food, or drinking from a glass).14 During annual follow-up phone calls, participants were asked “Over the past year, compared to other people your age, would you say that your health has been excellent, good, fair or poor?” Poor overall health was defined as self-reporting “poor” health, which is predictive of mortality and functional decline.15

Gait speed resiliencies

For each stressor, gait speed resilience was defined as having good gait speed at V5 and maintaining good gait speed at V6 after experiencing a stressor between V5 and V6 (Figure 1), using two approaches. Dichotomous (good/poor) outcomes: Participants with stressors were considered non-resilient if they either returned to V6 with slow gait speed (<1 m/s) or died before V6. Trichotomous (good/poor/deceased) resilience: A separate, three-level resilience definition was also examined for each stressor, categorizing those after a stressor as having good gait speed (≥1 m/s), slow gait speed (<1 m/s or unable to walk), or deceased at the V6 follow up to assess mortality effects on resiliencies. Participants with good gait speed at V5 who did not experience the stressors were examined to provide comparative background prevalence estimates of aging-related declines (or maintenance) in gait speed in the absence of the stressor. For each outcome (dichotomous and trichotomous), 11 specific gait speed resiliencies and background comparison groups were constructed, one for each of the 11 stressors described above.

Covariates

Sex (male or female), race (Black/African American; White/Caucasian; American Indian, Alaskan Indian, Asian, or Pacific Islander), birthdate, and education (categorized as less than high school; high school graduate or equivalent or vocational training; any college) were self-reported at ARIC V1.16 Other covariates were defined at V5. Height was measured to the nearest centimeter and weight using a beam balance scale with participants wearing lightweight clothes and no shoes. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (meter). Diabetes was defined as non-fasting glucose greater than 200 mg/dL, fasting glucose greater than 125 mg/dL, use of diabetes medications, or self-report of a physician diagnosis. Hypertension was defined as measured systolic/diastolic blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg or higher, use of anti-hypertensive medication, or self-reported physician diagnosis. Current smokers were those reporting ever smoking more than 100 cigarettes who smoked within the prior year, former smokers reported quitting more than 1 year prior, and others were considered non-smokers.

Statistical analysis

Resiliencies and relevance metrics (Objectives 1 and 2) were constructed for each of the 11 stressors by comparing outcome prevalences between those experiencing versus not experiencing each stressor, as follows. We used a three-step doubly-robust causal estimation approach applying (1) generalized linear modeling (logistic/multinomial regression) to obtain adjusted parameter estimates followed by (2) subsequent marginal standardization to obtain outcome prevalence estimates on the community-based ARIC population, and finally, (3) relative ratios (RRs) of the marginally standardized prevalences as objective relevance metrics with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); Figure 1 provides an algorithmic diagram. RRs less than 1.0 indicate relevant stressors, reflecting lower chances of maintaining good gait outcomes for those with the stressor versus those without; models adjusted for Visit 5 age, sex, race, and BMI. To exemplify investigating relevance modifications (Objective 3), we repeated the approach to estimate sex-specific RRs and ratios of these, rRRsex = (RRWomen/RRMen), adjusting for age, race, and BMI. Resiliencies and relevance metrics for trichotomous outcomes (good/slow/deceased) to investigate mortality effects, (Objective 4), denote the three prevalence comparisons as RR(gs) = Pr(good)/Pr(slow), RR(gd) = Pr(good)/Pr(deceased) and RR(sd) = Pr(slow)/Pr(deceased).

We conducted a sensitivity analysis to the acuteness of stressors by excluding 552 participants from the primary analysis who had good gait speed at Visit 5 but had previously experienced any of the stressors prior to Visit 5. This provided a closer examination of incident stressor occurrence and resilience related to acute stressors.

Results

Among 6538 participants who attended V5 (mean age 76.3 ± 5.3 years, 42% male, 24% Black race), 1559 (24%, mean age 74.4, 53% male, 12% Black race) demonstrated good gait speed (mean gait speed [m/s]=1.16 [0.14]) for whom gait speed resiliencies and relevance metrics were constructed and included in the analysis (Table 1). Stressor rates between V5 and V6 varied from 36 (2%) experiencing stroke to 646 (41%) with a hospitalization; women had higher rates of depressive symptoms and men had higher rates of poor social support, hospitalization, heart failure and myocardial infarction (Table S1).

Relevance metrics for good versus poor gait resiliencies

Figure 1 shows an exemplar Sankey plot of the resiliency flow for the hospitalization stressor. Among 1559 participants with good V5 gait speed, 646 (41%) reported a hospitalization stressor between V5 and V6. Of these, 47% (303/646) maintained good gait speed (ie, had hospitalization-resilient gait) compared to 62% (562/913) without a hospitalization stressor. Good gait speed maintenance was therefore 24% less likely among those with a hospitalization stressor compared to those without a hospitalization, RR = 0.76, (95% CI, 0.69-0.83), indicating support for hospitalization as a relevant gait speed stressor. Other resiliencies and corresponding resiliency relevance metrics were constructed similarly (Table S2). For example, 74 (5%) of participants had a heart failure stressor, with 21 (28%) maintaining good gait; comparatively, 57% of those without a HF stressor maintained good gait, RR = 0.46 (0.31, 0.69), indicating relevance support. In contrast, while 83/136 (61%) maintained good gait after reporting a poor social support stressor, a similar percent 655/1093 (60%) maintained good gait without the stressor, RR = 1.02 (0.89, 1.18), suggesting the poor social support stressor may not be as relevant a stressor as hospitalization or heart failure (Figure 2).

Resiliency relevance metrics (RRs) of good gait speed for all examined stressors are given in Figure 2 and Table S2. Additional stressors for maintaining good gait speed included poor overall self-rated health, RR = 0.41 (0.26, 0.65); stroke, RR = 0.57 (0.35, 0.93); poor physical ability, RR = 0.70 (0.62, 0.78), and self-reported poor memory, RR = 0.82 (0.73, 0.91). While these stressors showed high relevance for impacting gait speed, stressor prevalence is another element to consider. RRs in Figure 2 are additionally colored and sized by the stressor prevalence, showing that the poor physical ability and hospitalizations stressors are both relevant and also occur at the highest frequencies. In sensitivity analysis to the acuteness of the stressors, excluding participants with any stressor prior to Visit 5 did not substantively alter findings or ordering of stressor relevance except for stroke, which showed even wider CIs in the remaining small sample (Figure S1).

Influence of sex on resiliencies and relevance metrics

Figure 3 and Table S3 show good gait resiliency relevance metrics (RRs) for each stressor, stratified by sex (Figure 3A) and the ratio of RRs (rRRsex), comparing relevance for women relative to men (Figure 3B). Most relevancies were comparable for men and women. However, having a cancer stressor was associated with a 26% lower chance of having good gait speed at follow-up for women, RRWomen = 0.74 (0.57, 0.96) while the relevance for men was less supported, RRMen = 1.0 (0.85, 1.17); this suggests women may be less likely to maintain good gait after a cancer diagnosis and potentially have an increased relevance of this stressor potentially compared to men, rRRsex = 0.74 (0.55, 1.00).

Relevance metrics considering death

Estimated RRs of good gait speed, slow gait speed, and death as outcomes for stressor presence/absence are shown in Figure 4A and Table S2. For those with a hospitalization stressor versus without hospitalization, adjusted outcome rates at Visit 6 were: good gait (47% vs 62%), slow gait (36% vs 33%) and death (17% vs 5%), giving relevancy metrics a 30% lower chance of good (g) versus slow (s) gait for those with versus without a hospitalization, RR(gs) = 0.70 (0.56, 0.88), a 79% lower chance of good gait versus death (d) (ie, more likely to die than return with good gait), RR(gd) = 0.21 (0.15, 0.31), and a 70% lower chance of slow gait versus death (ie, more likely to die than return with slow gait), RR(sd) = 0.30 (0.21, 0.45) at follow-up. This suggests that when examining gait speed resiliency to a hospitalization stressor, outcomes should consider both returning with slow gait and mortality in resiliency research.

Forest plot of (A) relative ratios (RR) incorporating gait speed and death measures comparing those with versus without stressors, overall and by sex, and (B) Ratios of RRs comparing women to men (rRR = RRWomen/RRMen).

Similar to hospitalizations, deaths were highly influential for considerations of heart failure, stroke, myocardial infarction, falls, and cancer stressors on gait speed resilience (Table S2, Figure 4A). Cancer, for instance, had higher impacts on death, associated with a 60% lower chance of having good gait versus death, RR(gd) = 0.40 (0.26, 0.61) and a 59% lower chance of having slow gait versus death, RR(sd) = 0.41 (0.26, 0.65), but cancer was less relevant comparing survivors; good gait speed resilience versus slow gait, RR(gs) = 0.97 (0.69, 1.37). Similar to cancer, patterns for the falls stressor also showed stronger relevance for death, with good gait versus death RR(gd) = 0.48 (0.30, 0.77) and slow gait versus death RR(sd) = 0.52 (0.32,0.83), compared against good gait versus slow gait resiliency, RR(gs) = 0.93 (0.66,1.29). Several other stressor relevancies did not appear to be driven by death effects, including poor self-reported health, physical ability difficulty, and poor memory. Together these results suggest survival effects are important to consider for resiliencies as they can drive some, but not all, of the relevant relationships.

Influence of sex on resiliencies and relevance metrics considering death

Pooled and sex-stratified relevancy estimates incorporating death as a separate outcome (Figure 4A), accompanied by the ratio for women compared to men (rRRsex; Figure 4B); rates are given in Table S4. For reported falls, deaths appeared to affect women less in the absence of a fall (17% deaths with a fall stressor vs 5% without) than men (13% with vs 11% without). This led to differential relevancies for women than men on good gait versus death after a fall, with women being less likely to return with good gait than to die (rRRsex = 0.33 [0.12, 0.86]) or to return with slow gait versus dying compared to men (rRRsex = 0.26 [0.10, 0.70]), and less support for good gait versus slow gait relevance differences in women compared to men (rRRsex = 1.26 [0.64, 2.46]). Potential sex-differences in relevancies accounting for death were also seen for cancer and myocardial infarction.

Discussion

This study advances several concepts in physical resilience research using gait speed as a functional measure. First, our results empirically support the foundational idea of examining sets of resiliencies, rather than simple, single constructs of resilience. Second, we demonstrate the importance of considering how relevant a proposed stressor is for resilience, using gait speed resilience as an example, with the findings showing highly differential effects using different stressors. We also uniquely illustrate an approach that utilizes the background prevalence of the resilience property in persons without each stressor to construct relevance metrics for each stressor (relevancies of the resiliencies); this background information is ignored in resilience studies. Third, we demonstrated the need to consider differential influences of death on gait speed resilience following some stressors. Fourth, we describe a framework for examining differences in resilience relevancies across groups by showing potential sex differences in the relevancies of some gait speed resiliencies (eg, with cancer and falls as stressors). Collectively these findings demonstrate heterogeneity in stressor associations with resiliencies, the value of including a comparison against age-related declines in physical function measures without a stressor when examining resilience, potential informative missingness due to mortality, and suggest physical resiliencies vary by stressor and sex.

To our knowledge, this is the first work comparing assessments of a physical function resilience property pre- and post-stressor with the assessment of the same physical function metric among people who did not experience the stressor over the same timeframe. The value of this approach is that it helps distinguish stressor-related changes in resilience properties from aging-related changes that occur without the stressor, thereby providing a metric of how influential, or “relevant,” the stressor is to the resilience property at the population-level. We do not suggest this is the only way to conceptualize relevance for physical resiliencies; relevance from a patient-specific perspective may be quite different from a population-level perspective.1,17–21 Nor are these the only metrics one might consider to quantify relevance for resiliencies research, as there are a number of immediate extensions which may be valuable to consider, for example, continuous measures. However, we are not aware of other metrics or frameworks allowing examinations of differential stressor impacts on a physical function metric, and we find those proposed appear valuable.

Our examination demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in the relevance of specific stressors on gait speed resilience. Only half of the stressors showed influential reductions in maintaining good gait speed compared to those who had not experienced the stressor. Self-reported poor-health was one of the most influential gait speed resiliency stressors, with relevance similar to physician-adjudicated heart failure and stroke events, but is a simple and inexpensive measure. For context, a self-reported hospitalization had slightly lower gait-speed relevance than the self-reported poor-health stressor, and a randomized clinical trial has demonstrated that mobility interventions during hospitalization improve post-discharge community mobility among hospitalized older veterans.22 This demonstrates actionable clinical implications of intervening on patients at risk for poor gait speed resilience after, or perhaps even in anticipation of, experiencing a relevant stressor, such as an acute or planned hospitalization. By extension of the relevancy comparisons, clinicians could also consider acting on poor self-rated health. Our proposed relevance metrics help fill a gap in empirical approaches to identify sets of potentially relevant stressors for studying resiliencies in older adults.

Disentangling the influence of deaths can be challenging for any longitudinal study. Mortality appeared to highly influence characterizations of a number of resiliencies, including those defined by heart failure, stroke, myocardial infarction, cancer, and falls stressors. When “non-resiliencies” were further stratified into slow gait speed and deaths, the relevancy metrics showed that people experiencing these stressors were more likely to die than to be observed at follow-up with either good or slow gait speed. This suggests that relevant resiliency inquiries and related interventions for these stressors may need to first address mortality risks, prior to investigating slow gait speed concerns.

Regarding sex differences, self-reported falls were a relevant gait speed resiliency stressor only for women and mainly through higher mortality for women reporting falls along with higher mortality in men without a fall (Table S4), though the small number of men and women reporting any falls could obscure associations. With post-cancer stressors, an explanation for higher relevance (and lower gait speed resilience) in women than in men could be that women developed more aggressive cancers, such as breast and lung cancer, that typically require more aggressive treatments, such as surgery, radiation, and/or chemotherapy, while many cases in men were likely prostate cancer, which are often localized and slow-growing, thus requiring less frequent use of aggressive treatments.23 Since survival rates were comparable after the cancer stressor (Table S4), the surviving women may have experienced a more difficult recovery from cancer treatments than the surviving men. Further research in other populations is warranted, but these findings highlight potential stressors that may be more detrimental for women than men in terms of gait speed outcomes or mortality, and therefore may help guide future research directions.

Some limitations and strengths merit discussion. We were limited by secondary data analysis that did not control timing of gait speed examinations relative to stressors; future studies should be designed to assess stressor acuity/chronicity, severity, and timing of stressors relative to the functional measures along with potential interactions among stressors to understand differential relevance of stressors and cumulative stressor effects. If present, bias would be expected to be toward the null through measurement error. Self-reported retrospective reports of falls and small event numbers for some stressors led to wide relevancy CIs and unstable estimates for some sex-specific relevancy metrics. However, small numbers of the stressors themselves can be helpful in ranking population-level importance. Future work could examine age- and sex-specific thresholds, which we did not include in this analysis. Using a threshold to define gait speed resilience could oversimplify functional trajectories and mask important gradations or nonlinear associations. Choosing a gait speed threshold of 1 m/s, however, approximates the pace needed to traverse a timed crosswalk, regardless of age or sex, allows analytic examinations of age- and sex-effects on resilience, and presents a simpler framework to advance resilience research. Finally, this study focused on resiliency of gait speed, a common and strongly predictive measure of health and function in older adults; we acknowledge other physical function measures to define resiliencies are important for future research. This study has several important strengths, including the large sample size from four different communities across the United States. Clinical events were adjudicated by clinicians with expertise in relevant areas. We were able to examine combinations of gait speed with multiple stressors, including clinically feasible self-report measures. Resiliencies assessed relative to background, age-associated declines in gait speed accounting for mortality are additional novel strengths that contribute to the resilience research framework.

In conclusion, although many studies ask the question “What affects resilience?,” we propose expanding the conversation to ask “How relevant are these resiliencies?” We followed previous consensus guidelines to identify several potential stressors of interest that defined sets of gait speed resiliencies in older adults. The proposed metrics that were developed helped rank the related relevancies, describing which stressors might be more important for gait speed resiliency investigations, which resiliencies might be sensitive to mortality effects, and which resiliencies might be differentially relevant for men and women. Future studies on resiliencies should replicate and expand this work for relevancies of stressor-function choices, incorporating approaches when possible that compare groups with and without stressors and additionally examining death, sex and other risk-factor effects in context of the scientific question.
