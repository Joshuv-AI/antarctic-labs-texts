Optimizing QACT Frequency in Proton Therapy: Analysis of Standard Guidelines Across Treatment Sites
Authors: Rachel B. Ger, Todd R. McNutt, Curtiland Deville, Aditya N. Halthore, Heng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeAnatomical changes during proton therapy are typically monitored using quality assurance computed tomography (QACT) scans. However, excessive QACTs can increase patient burden, resource use, and health care costs. This study evaluates the impact of implementing standardized minimum QACT frequencies across treatment sites.Materials and MethodsAdult patients treated with proton therapy before October 2024 at the Johns Hopkins Proton Therapy Center were reviewed. Head and neck (HN), thorax, and pelvis prostate patients were newly analyzed; cranium and breast guidelines were drawn from prior published work. Based on this, guidelines for minimum QACT frequency were established. The implementation impact was evaluated by comparing QACT usage and adaptive planning rates before (October 2019-April 2023) and after (August 2023-October 2024) guideline deployment. A 1-sided Student t-test was used to determine if the QACT rate was lowered after guideline implementation. A Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to determine if the adaptive planning rate was different before and after guideline implementation.ResultsA total of 681 patients were analyzed prior to guideline implementation and 381 patients afterward. Guidelines were developed for breast, cranium, HN, thorax, and pelvis prostate sites. Following guideline implementation, QACT frequency significantly decreased overall (median reduced from 3 to 2 per patient, P < .0001), and specifically for cranium (P = .008), HN (P = .0009), and pelvis prostate (P < .0001) patients. Adaptive therapy rates were not significantly different between cohorts (P = .22), supporting that reduced QACT use did not compromise identifying needed treatment adaptations. CBCT was effective in identifying anatomical changes that prompted adaptive replanning in pelvis prostate patients.ConclusionEstablishing standard minimum QACT frequencies led to a significant reduction in the number of QACTs performed across several treatment sites. These findings support the utility of structured QACT guidelines to enhance resource efficiency and reduce patient burden in proton therapy.

Introduction

Proton therapy has emerged as a pivotal advancement in radiation oncology, offering superior dose conformality compared to conventional photon-based treatments.1, 2 This enhanced conformality allows for effective tumor targeting while significantly reducing radiation exposure to surrounding normal tissues. Such precision is particularly beneficial in minimizing treatment-related toxicities and improving patient outcomes.

Despite these advantages, proton therapy is notably sensitive to uncertainties arising from inter-fractional anatomical changes. Variations in patient anatomy between treatment sessions can lead to deviations from the planned dose distribution, potentially compromising treatment efficacy. To mitigate these risks, regular quality assurance computed tomography (QACT) scans are employed to monitor anatomical changes and inform necessary adaptive interventions. However, the frequency of QACTs must be carefully balanced to avoid increased patient burden, resource utilization, and health care costs.

Several studies have investigated this particularly in head and neck (HN). Evans et al3 found that weekly QACTs were important to determining the need for adaptive plans. However, their clinic did not have cone beam CTs (CBCTs) available. Yao et al4 demonstrated that the use of CBCTs and plan robustness could greatly reduce the number of QACTs. Similarly, Biswal et al5 demonstrated using CBCTs to indicate needed QACTs for sarcomas and was able to reduce total QACTs by 85%. Collectively, these studies suggest that the integration of CBCT with robust planning methods can substantially decrease the need for routine QACTs while maintaining clinical treatment accuracy and timely detection of anatomical changes.

Establishing optimized guidelines for QACT frequency is essential to enhance the efficiency of proton therapy without compromising treatment quality. Smolders et al6 showed that to account for the additional secondary cancer risk from CBCT and weekly QACTs, 0.3 to 0.4 mm margin reductions are needed. However, in many situations, the margins cannot be reduced for targets as these incorporate subclinical disease or setup uncertainties that cannot be changed. Additionally, while it has been explored to utilize CBCTs for adaptive planning for protons, direct calculation on CBCTs is not recommended due to image quality which creates unreliable proton dose calculations.7 Some groups have demonstrated the potential of utilizing CBCTs for adaptive planning by creating virtual or synthetic CTs8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13; however, these traditionally rely on deformable image registration which can be unreliable for representing true patient anatomy and new methods using generative adversarial networks are still in introductory exploratory phases.14, 15, 16, 17 Therefore, QACTs remain the gold standard for adaptive planning in proton therapy and optimizing the CT use will help to minimize excess risk to the patent.

While prior studies have reported on QACT use for individual disease sites, there remains a lack of systematic, site-specific guidelines to support uniform practice across proton therapy centers. As a result, QACT frequency is often determined by individual physician preference, leading to variability in practice, excess imaging, and unnecessary patient burden. With the global expansion of proton therapy, new centers in particular face the challenge of establishing evidence-based quality systems.

This study presents a retrospective review of patients treated at the Johns Hopkins Proton Therapy Center. The purpose of this work is to provide a practical framework for standard minimum QACT frequencies across common treatment sites, developed from a large institutional data set. By standardizing imaging practices, these guidelines aim to reduce variability, improve resource efficiency, and maintain treatment quality. This work therefore serves both as a model for established centers seeking to optimize imaging utilization and as a reference for new proton facilities establishing their protocols.

Materials and methods

Patients treated at the Johns Hopkins Proton Therapy Center treated between October 2019 and April 2023 were reviewed for developing standardized guidelines for QACTs. Patients were excluded if they were <18 years of age, treated by a pediatric physician, or did not have any QACTs. This study was conducted under an IRB from Johns Hopkins University.

Patients were categorized by their treatment site into the following categories: abdomen, breast, cranium, HN, thorax, pelvis prostate, and other. For each treatment site, groups of patients were reviewed to determine the number of adaptive plans and when those adaptive plans were done to determine when QACTs were needed in order to catch these needed adaptive plans. Breast and cranium patients have been previously reported on for determining their guidelines.18, 19 Abdomen patients were not enough to reliably evaluate for creating guidelines, but were separated for the analysis of adaptive planning and QACT rates due to their distinct treatment site. HN, thorax, and pelvis prostate patients’ analysis for determining QACT rates are described here. There were not enough other pelvis malignancy cases (eg, rectal, gynecological) to analyze, therefore only pelvis prostate was analyzed in this work.

For each disease site, patients were excluded if an adaptive plan was conducted not for patient habitus changes but additional biological data that were obtained or if the full treatment plan was not delivered as proton therapy. Plans were created using 2 to 5 beams with utilization of a range shifter if the target was <4 cm from the surface, except for pelvis prostate patients which always utilized 2 or 4 beams with 2 laterals being used for prostate plans and 4 beams (2 laterals and 2 posterior obliques) utilized for pelvis plans. Treatment plans were optimized robustly with 3 mm setup uncertainty (sometimes 5 mm for thoracic depending on the motion measured for the given patient) and 3.5% range uncertainty. Single-field optimization planning approaches were preferred but multifield optimization was employed to achieve improved normal tissue sparing for some cases when deemed clinically beneficial.

All patients were treated using a ProBeat compact-gantry pencil beam scanning proton therapy system (Hitachi) with a gantry-mounted CBCT. Patients were treated using daily CBCT for alignment. Treatment plans were created using Raystation (version 10a or 2023B; RaySearch Laboratories). Final dose was computed using Monte Carlo with 0.5% statistical uncertainty. All dose reported is using a constant relative biological effectiveness of 1.1.

Each patient was assessed if an adaptive plan was required. If an adaptive plan was required, the number of fractions delivered before the adaptive plan was started was recorded.

HN patients

Patients were divided into 5 groupings based on the primary site: (1) base of skull, (2) jugular, (3) nasal/nasopharynx/sinus, (4) oropharynx/palate, and (5) salivary glands/neck. For each grouping, patients were subdivided based on if they had no neck node targets, unilateral neck node targets, or bilateral neck node targets. Pearson χ2 test was conducted to determine if the adaptive planning rate varied significantly across subsites. The χ2 test was computed with all groupings as well as by individual pairings. Statistical significance was determined at the P < .05 level.

Pelvis prostate patients

Due to the large number of patients, a smaller timeframe was analyzed for these patients. Patients treated between July 2021 and August 2022 were analyzed. Patients were divided into 4 different categories: prostate, prostate bed, pelvis prostate, pelvis prostate bed. If an adaptive plan was required, their QACT was compared to their CBCT to determine if the need for the adaptive plan could be determined from the CBCT.

Thorax patients

For thoracic patients, if motion was over 5 mm, breath hold using Active Breathing Control (ABC, Elekta) was considered and required if motion was over 1 cm. Patients treated without ABC, had a 4DCT and were planned on the average with the treatment plan verified on the minimum and maximum phases as well. Patients were divided into subcategories depending on the treatment site: esophagus, hemithorax, lung, or mediastinum.

QACT frequency and adaptive planning rate assessment

Based on the review of adaptive plan rates and timing, guidelines for the standard minimum QACT frequencies were developed and deployed in the clinic. All patients treated were reviewed and divided into 2 groups: before guideline implementation (patients treated between October 2019 and April 2023) and after guideline implementation (patients treated from August 2023 to October 2024). A small gap was left to allow for the transition period of the guideline implementation. Prior to guideline implementation, all patient QACT frequencies were specified by each physician individually.

The number of QACTs for patients in the cohort before the standards were created and after were collected per treatment site. A 1-sided Student t-test was used to determine if the QACT rate was lowered after the standard minimums were established per site. To determine if the standard minimum QACT guidelines impacted adaptive plans, the proportion of patients with adaptive plans before and after guidelines were compared using a Wilcoxon signed rank test evaluating the whole cohort while pairing each disease site before and after rates. For each individual disease site, the 2-sided 2-proportions z-test without Yates’ continuity correction was utilized to determine if the adaptive planning rate significantly differed. For each test, statistical significance was determined at the P < .05 level.

Pelvis prostate patients

One hundred ten prostate and pelvis prostate patients were reviewed, 6 of whom had adaptive plans. The reasons for adaptive planning were for 1 patient to remove the rectal balloon, 4 patients could not have alignment to both fiducials and bony anatomy anymore with large shifts required when aligning to one or the other, and 1 patient the rectal balloon placement had changed. Supplemental Table 1 shows the results for the number of patients in each category and the adaptive planning information.

Excluding the patient that had the rectal balloon removed, the adaptive plan on average started after 14 ± 3.3 fractions. All except the removal of the rectal balloon could be seen on the CBCT images. The breast and other cohorts both demonstrated a consistent number of QACTs for the patients included after the guideline implementation, as seen in the 0 interquartile range for these compared to before guidelines where both of these had an interquartile range of 1 shown in Figure 2. This is dependent on the patient population and the distribution could vary with different patient populations. Table 3 shows that for breast patients without implants or without a short time between surgery and simulation, only 1 QACT was set as the standard minimum guideline. However, the majority of the patients treated at our proton center after guideline implementation were patients with implants or a short time between surgery and simulation which led to the 0 interquartile range. For the other category, it is also dependent on the types of patients during that time. For both of these, the median value and the lower bound of the interquartile range did not change before and after guideline implementation.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that implementing standardized, site-specific minimum QACT frequency guidelines effectively reduced the overall number of QACTs required for proton therapy patients without negatively impacting adaptive planning rates. Prior to guideline implementation, the frequency of QACT acquisition was left to the discretion of the treating physician, resulting in substantial variability across providers and disease sites. The reduction was particularly significant for the cranium, HN, and pelvis prostate treatment sites, indicating that these sites benefit most from the updated protocols. These findings support that routine QACT imaging can be safely tailored by anatomical site, and in some cases, further reduced when paired with robust CBCT-based monitoring.

The reduction in QACTs was most significant for cranium, HN, and pelvis prostate patients, sites for which tailored guidelines were informed by detailed analysis of adaptive planning timing. In particular, HN patients with nasal cavity or sinus involvement treatments exhibited higher replan rates, justifying more frequent imaging. Conversely, patients treated for less dynamic disease subsites within the HN, such as salivary gland or neck alone, had low adaptive planning rates, supporting less frequent imaging. Similarly, for pelvis prostate patients replans were typically driven by anatomical misalignments between fiducials and bony anatomy or changes in rectal balloon positioning. These discrepancies were reliably visualized on daily CBCT images, suggesting that routine weekly QACTs can often be avoided in favor of selective imaging based on CBCT triggers.

The thoracic cohort, in contrast, did not show a significant change in QACT frequency, reflecting the guideline recommendation to maintain weekly QACTs due to high rates of multiple adaptive plans and the anatomical complexity of lung and mediastinal targets. Lung patients, especially those treated with breath-hold techniques, exhibited the highest rates of adaptation, reinforcing the need for continued frequent imaging in these patients.

The cranial cohort included any targets that were not only within the brain, but also bulky surface targets, optic, and orbit targets. The beams chosen for each target were done in order to limit the beam path to avoid exposing additional normal tissue. Orbital and optic beams were similar to published work20 which have beams with spots in the sinus cavity due to the proximity. Changes to the sinus filling therefore impacted the coverage for these targets, along with bulky surface tumors that often showed response over the course of treatment, which led to adaptive plans. Additionally, the adaptive planning rate that we observed for this site was similar to that reported by others.21

Notably, the pelvis prostate cohort, which represented nearly half of all patients treated, contributed substantially to the overall decline in QACT utilization. This highlights the impact that guideline implementation can have in high-volume treatment sites, where even modest reductions in per-patient QACT frequency can translate to large institutional gains in efficiency, cost savings, and reduced patient burden.

Importantly, the overall median number of QACTs decreased from 3 to 2 per patient, representing a meaningful reduction in resource use and patient burden. This decrease was achieved without compromising treatment quality, as evidenced by the lack of significant difference in adaptive planning rates between the cohorts before and after guideline implementation.

While the need for an adaptive plan for pelvis prostate patients could be reliably determined from CBCT, this was not seen for all other sites. Large weight differences, significant tumor responses, or significant sinus/nasal cavity changes could be observed on CBCT and aligned with the need for an adaptive plan. However, HN and thoracic sites were often planned using more multifield optimization which made these more sensitive to hot spots with smaller changes. Small bone or tissue differences on CBCT sometimes did not impact plan quality and sometimes led to the need for an adaptive plan due to hot spots in normal tissues or coverage changes. But these smaller changes were an unreliable predictor for the need for adaptive planning. Therefore, CBCT was useful in reducing QACT frequency for pelvis prostate, but for other sites could only reliably indicate the need for an adaptive plan when large discrepancies from the planning CT existed.

While this study utilized a large patient cohort and comprehensive evaluation across various anatomical sites, there are several limitations which include the retrospective nature of the study and the reliance on data from a single institution. Future studies involving multi-institutional collaborations could enhance the generalizability of these findings. Additionally, the abdomen and “other” categories were not evaluated due to insufficient patient numbers, which remains a limitation of this study. Also, the bulk of our overall QACT reductions were seen in the pelvis prostate patients. Centers with different patient disease distributions may see different reductions in their overall QACTs. Lastly, this work was performed at a center that has CBCT capability and utilizes it daily for alignment. Centers with different capabilities may need different QACT frequencies. This work can serve as a model for those centers as well.

The primary utility of this work lies in providing a structured, data-driven framework for QACT utilization that can be directly adopted into clinical workflows. By showing that QACTs can be safely reduced without compromising adaptive planning, we provide reassurance for centers seeking to balance quality assurance with efficiency. For high-volume sites such as prostate, even small reductions in per-patient imaging translate into significant institutional resource savings and reduced patient burden.

Importantly, these findings are not intended to replace clinical judgment, but to establish a consistent baseline of practice that can reduce variability across physicians and disease sites. In the context of the rapid worldwide growth of proton therapy, the guidelines presented here can be particularly valuable for newly established centers in the process of developing their quality systems. Furthermore, this study provides a foundation for future multi-institutional collaborations aimed at refining and validating QACT frequency standards across diverse clinical settings.

Overall, establishing standard minimum QACT frequencies led to a significant reduction in the number of QACTs performed across several treatment sites without adversely affecting adaptive planning. These findings support the continued application of these guidelines and suggest that CBCT imaging can further support safe reduction of QACT frequency in proton therapy to enhance resource efficiency and reduce patient burden in proton therapy. Further investigations into refining site-specific protocols, particularly for less commonly treated sites, are warranted.
