Patient-Derived Lung Cancer Organoids for Precision Oncology: A Comprehensive Analysis
Authors: Donglai Chen, Zewen Sun, Jiangtao Li, Xiaofan Wang, Yongzhong Li, Yiming Mao, Yongbing Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to comprehensively assess the accuracy of patient-derived organoids to recapitulate the genomic features of patient tumors, and to quantify the reliability of patient-derived organoid-based drug screening, by pooling the available evidence and the validation using our patient-derived lung cancer organoids (LCOs).MethodsA comprehensive online search was conducted to identify eligible studies. A total of 20 LCOs harvested from surgical samples were also established as our internal validation cohort, with whole-exome sequencing and transcriptomic sequencing performed, respectively. Pooled concordance in genetic profiles between LCOs and original tumors was calculated, along with the overall accuracy of LCOs for drug profiling.ResultsThe pooled concordance in mutational landscape between LCOs and original tumors reached 74% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 61%–84%) in overall populations without heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.918), and that in gene expression achieved 70% (95% CI: 51%–84%; I2 = 8.5%, p = 0.437). Subgroup analyses of our internal LCOs by morphologic patterns suggested that LCOs retained genetic fidelity regardless of intratumoral histologic heterogeneity and pathologic features. The accuracy of LCOs for predicting drug sensitivity achieved 90.7% (95% CI: 77.4%–9.2%) in the pooled populations with mild heterogeneity (I2 = 30.2%, p = 0.231).ConclusionsOur study indicates that LCOs can stably recapitulate the genomic features of patient tumors irrespective of histologic heterogeneity and be a promising precision medicine tool for anticancer drug screening or for predicting drug responses.Keywords: Lung cancer organoids, Drug sensitivity testing, Genetic profiles, Histologic heterogeneity

Introduction

The past decade has witnessed increasingly developed molecular-targeted therapies for actionable alterations, such as EGFR mutations and ALK fusions,1 and expression of biomarkers, such as programmed death-ligand 1 and vascular endothelial growth factor.2 However, lung cancer is characterized by its histologic and genomic heterogeneity.3, 4, 5 It was also found that intratumoral genetic heterogeneity coexists and correlates with histologic heterogeneity,6 which presents considerable challenges to the use of precision medicine.7 Therefore, it is imperative to establish a preclinical model that simulates the morphology-related genomic features of the original tissues, which helps clinicians understand the biological behaviors and pharmacologic responses to targeted therapies.

Despite their easy manipulability and genome editability,8 traditional tumor cell lines are not eligible to reflect individual tumors because of the lack of morphologic and genomic complexity. Conversely, patient-derived xenografts can retain the structure and genetic characteristics of the original tumors and simulate a similar tumor microenvironment. However, patient-derived xenografts and genetically engineered models are costly and time-consuming and require the sacrifice of a large number of experimental animals.7 Unlike conventional two-dimensional cell lines, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) represent an advanced preclinical model that faithfully recapitulates the histologic architecture and genetic landscape of the original tumors.8,9 PDOs preserve patient-specific characteristics, including intratumoral heterogeneity and differential therapeutic responses, thereby providing a more clinically relevant platform for translational research.10 More importantly, PDOs enable ex vivo evaluation of the efficacy and resistance to multiple drugs within a short time window,11,12 bridging the gap between laboratory tests and clinical decision-making. Nevertheless, it has been undetermined regarding the overall accuracy of PDOs to replicate the genetic features of parental tumors and to evaluate drug sensitivity for lung cancer.

In this study, we aimed to comprehensively assess the accuracy of PDOs to recapitulate the genomic features of patient tumors, and to quantify the reliability of PDO-based drug screening, by pooling the available evidence and the validation using our patient-derived lung cancer organoids (LCOs).

Materials and Methods

Ethics Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Institute of the Second Affiliated Hospital of University (JD-HG-2025-056). Informed consent was obtained from all patients before tissue acquisition.

Patients and Sample Collection

Tumor specimens were collected from patients with lung cancer undergoing pulmonary resection at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between September 2022 and May 2024. Each tissue sample was cut into two pieces: one piece for the generation of organoids, the other one for later RNA or DNA extraction.

Organoid Culture

Tumor tissues were washed three times with ice-cold Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline supplemented with 20% penicillin and streptomycin, then minced into 1-mm3 fragments using sterile surgical scissors. Minced tissues were incubated in 2-mL Liberase dissociation solution (37°C, 1 h) within a 15-mL centrifuge tube. Digestion was terminated by adding 5 mL ice-cold advanced 1:1 mixture of Dulbecco's modified eagle medium and Ham's F12 containing 20% fetal bovine serum. The suspension was filtered through a 100-μm strainer into a fresh tube. Residual tissue fragments retained on the strainer underwent secondary digestion with TrypLE Select Enzyme (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) (37°C, 20 min). The combined filtrates were centrifuged (2000 (revolutions per min, 5 min), and the pellet was treated with red blood cell lysis buffer (37°C, 3 min). After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in ice-cold Matrigel, and 105 cells in 50 μL Matrigel were seeded as droplets onto prewarmed 6-well plates. Polymerization was achieved by sequential orientation: 15 minutes upright followed by 15 minutes inverted (37°C, 5% carbon dioxide). After solidification, 2.5 mL prewarmed organoid medium was gently added along the well wall. Medium was replaced every 2 to 3 days on the basis of morphologic assessment. Organoids were passaged after 14 days of culture. The detailed composition of culture medium for establishing our own LCOs is listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Histologic Structure and Immunostaining

Parental tissues and organoids were respectively fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by paraffin embedding, sectioning, deparaffinization, dehydration, and hematoxylin-eosin staining. For immunohistochemistry staining, the tissue sections were prepared using anti–thyroid transcription factor-1 (1:200 dilutions; ZM-0270) and anti–cytokeratin 7 (1:200 dilutions; ZM-0472), which were visualized using the 3,3'-diaminobenzidine detection kit (AR1027-3 [Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, People’s Republic of China]). Images were captured using the IX73 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Whole-Exome Sequencing and Analysis

The total DNA was extracted using the Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands) OR GeneRead DNA formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands). Then the DNA, which was fragmented by using Covaris M220 focused-ultrasonicator (Covaris, Woburn, MA) were subjected to sequencing library construction. Exome capture was performed using the Human Exome 2.0 Plus (Twist Bioscience, San Francisco, CA) or SureSelect Human All Exon V6 Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) following the vendor’s recommended protocol. The final libraries were sequenced for paired-end 150 base pair (bp) using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 Sequencing System (Illumina, San Diego, CA) at LC-Bio Technology Co, Ltd. (Hangzhou, People's Republic of China). Before alignment, the low-quality reads ([1] reads containing sequencing adaptors; [2] nucleotides with a quality score lower than 20) were removed by fastp.13 For the alignment step, Burrows Wheeler Aligner14 was used to align reads to the reference genome hg19. and as the first post-alignment processing step, Picard tools (Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA) (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) were used to identify and mark duplicate reads from the BAM file. In the second postalignment processing step, local read realignment was performed to correct for potential alignment errors around indels. The base quality score recalibration was then performed before variant calling to reduce the systematic biases. Somatic single nucleotide variant and indels were jointly detected by MuTect (Broad Institute).15 ANNOVAR16 was used to add biologic information to the variant set. Copy number variations were detected by FACETS.17 GC content of the sequences was used to normalize the read distribution, and the normalized distribution of aligned reads in slide windows was used to calculate the copy number difference between tumor and normal samples.

RNA Sequencing and Analysis

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Thermofisher, 15596018) following the manufacturer's procedure. The total RNA quantity and purity were assessed using Bioanalyzer 2100 and RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent, 5067-1511), high-quality RNA samples with RNA integrity number greater than 7.0 were used to construct a sequencing library. After total RNA was extracted, mRNA was purified from total RNA (5 μg) using Dynabeads Oligo (dT) (ThermoFisher) with two rounds of purification. After purification, the mRNA was fragmented into short fragments using divalent cations under elevated temperature (Magnesium RNA fragmentation Module [cat.e6150] [New England Biolabs (NEB), Ipswich, MA] under 94°C 5-7 min). Then the cleaved RNA fragments were reverse-transcribed to create the circulating DNA by SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, cat. 1896649 [ThermoFisher]), which were next used to synthesize U-labeled second-stranded DNAs with E. coli DNA polymerase I (cat.m0209, NEB), RNase H (cat.m0297, NEB) and dUTP Solution (cat.R0133, ThermoFisher). An A-base was then added to the blunt ends of each strand, preparing them for ligation to the indexed adapters. Each adapter contained a T-base overhang for ligating the adapter to the A-tailed fragmented DNA. Dual-index adapters were ligated to the fragments, and size selection was performed with AMPureXP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA). After the heat-labile uracil-DNA glycosylase enzyme (cat.m0280, NEB) treatment of the U-labeled second-stranded DNAs, the ligated products were amplified with polymerase chain reaction by the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 minutes; eight cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 15 seconds, annealing at 60°C for 15 seconds, and extension at 72°C for 30 seconds; and then final extension at 72°C for 5 minutes. The average insert size for the final circulating DNA libraries were 300 (±50 bp). At last, we performed the 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing (PE150) on an Illumina Novaseq 6000 (LC-Bio Technology Co., Ltd.) following the vendor’s recommended protocol.

We used R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to perform correlation analyses. Principal components analysis for original tissues and LCOs was calculated by the plotPCA function using the normalized EdgeR counts. The Pearson correlation coefficient (R) was used to assess the correlation between parental tumors and LCOs, which were calculated using the cor function in R.

Search Strategy

The methodology for this meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses protocol.18 Four major electronic databases—PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library—were searched without language restriction, covering all records from their inception to March 2025. Specific retrieval formulas were customized for each database by combining subject terms with free-text words. The main search terms included “lung cancer," “NSCLC,” “lung adenocarcinoma,” “Organoids,” “Whole-exome sequencing,” “Clinical responses,” among others. We also manually searched the reference lists of the previously published research articles or systematic reviews until no additional articles could be identified.

Study Inclusion Criteria

Studies that met the following criteria were included the following: (1)The included patient cohort was analyzed by means of whole-exome sequencing, transcriptome analysis, or drug sensitivity test; (2) studies provided concordance rate between LCOs and original tumor tissues or comparison of drug sensitivity test and clinical response; and (3) the full text was available. Eligible patients were required to provide their lung cancer tissues for generating LCOs successfully. Two independent reviewers (ZS and DC) oversaw identification of the eligible studies. Persistent discrepancies were resolved by means of consultative dialogue or intervention from a designated independent reviewer, ensuring final concordance.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Two investigators (ZS and DC) performed independent extraction of pertinent data from the selected studies. The following data were gathered on the basis of the included studies: first author’s name, year of publication, research country, number of organoids, patient age, histology type, tumor stage, source of samples, establishment rate, and concordance rate. We extracted data from each study and summarized them, including the concordance rates in transcriptomes or exomes between LCOs and original tumor tissues or accuracy rates between drug sensitivity tests in LCOs and clinical responses. The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale criteria.18

Statistical Analyses

Data analysis of the general data was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 27; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), R statistics (R Studio version 4.3.3) was used to perform meta-analysis, and create figures, forest, funnel plots, and genomic landscape (ggplot2, maftools, and meta packages). Study heterogeneity was assessed with the Q test and I2 statistic. Given the potential heterogeneity across studies, this study employed a random-effects model. To stabilize the variances of the proportions and mitigate the impact of extreme values, we performed a Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation before pooling the effect sizes. Summary estimates were then back-transformed to the original proportion scale for interpretation. Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots.

A binary classification framework was employed to assess the concordance between organoid drug testing results and their paired clinical outcomes. Organoid drug responses were categorized as sensitive or resistant according to the assay definition and cutoff reported in each study, and clinical outcomes were categorized as responder or nonresponder on the basis of study-specific clinical criteria. Classification counts (true positives [TP], false positives [FP], false negatives [FN], and true negatives [TN]) were extracted from each publication when available; if not reported, the corresponding counts were reconstructed from the reported results. Accuracy was calculated as (TP+TN)/(TP+FP+FN+TN), sensitivity as TP/(TP+FN), and specificity as TN/(TN+FP). If TP/FP/FN/TN could not be derived, the accuracy reported by the original study was used.

Figure 2A depicts the pooled concordance in genetic alterations between LCOs and parental tumor tissues. The pooled concordance reached 74% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 61%–84%) in overall populations without heterogeneity (I2 = 0%, p = 0.918). It was also revealed that the pooled concordance in gene expression between LCOs and original tumors achieved 70% (95% CI: 51%–84%; I2 = 8.5%, p = 0.437) (Fig. 2B). The funnel plots suggested no significant publication bias in pooling the concordance in genetic mutations and transcriptomic landscape (Supplementary Fig. 3A and B).

Subgroup Analysis of Genetic Resemblance of LCOs by Histologic Features

To evaluate whether intratumoral histologic heterogeneity can affect the genetic similarity of LCOs to original tumors, we conducted subgroup analyses of our internal LCOs by morphologic patterns. Supplementary Tables 4 and 5 revealed that LCOs shared high consistency with the parental tissues in both genetic alterations and transcriptomes, irrespective of intratumoral histologic heterogeneity and pathologic features.

Pooled Concordance in Drug Sensitivity

As illustrated in Figure 3A, the overall accuracy of LCOs for drug profiling reached 90.7% (95% CI: 77.4%–99.2%) in the pooled populations with mild heterogeneity (I2 = 30.2%, p = 0.231). The funnel plots also revealed no significant publication bias in pooling the reliability of LCOs for predicting drug responses (Fig. 3B). It was suggested that LCOs exhibited good concordance with the parental tissues in reflecting therapeutic responses irrespective of targeted therapies and chemotherapy (p = 0.69) (Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

Previous studies have reported the intratumoral heterogeneity of either the molecular features or the pathologic features of lung cancer.6,20 It was also suggested that intratumoral genetic heterogeneity tended to coexist with histologic heterogeneity, not only observed in different growth patterns, but also within the same growth pattern.6 However, few studies have quantified the reliability of LCOs to synchronously recapitulate the histologic and genetic characteristics of the original tissues.

A striking finding of our study was that LCOs maintained high similarity in genetic characteristics to the parental tissues, irrespective of the intratumoral histologic heterogeneity and pathologic features. In our internal samples for establishing LCOs, more than three subtypes were seen in two of the 10 lung adenocarcinomas subjected to exome sequencing and in six of another 10 subjected to transcriptomic sequencing. Subgroup analyses corroborated that LCOs stably reserved the genetic features in both lung adenocarcinomas with three or less growth patterns and in those with more than three growth patterns. Interestingly, the pooled rates on the basis of our samples suggested that the accuracy of LCOs in reflecting transcriptomic landscape seemed to be higher than that in reflecting mutation profiles.

In the era of personalized cancer medicine, new clinical trial designs for drug repurposing tests, drug combination tests, and nof-1 trials are becoming increasingly important compared with classic clinical designs involving large and diverse patient populations.20 Good consistency between drug testing and patient responses in the clinic is the accepted standard for a preclinical model.10 Apart from the success rate and the degree of purity of the models, another challenge in the use of tumor organoids for drug screening is experimental stability. Inter- and intratumor heterogeneity has been widely considered.7 From the pooled data, LCOs exhibited high accuracy in predicting clinical responses in vitro in different studies, with the sensitive ty consistently achieved over 80% in different studies. Nevertheless, a notable drawback was the lack of data on LCOs derived from multiple samples, such as malignant pleural effusion and lymph node–derived LCOs. It is well known that surgical specimens provide larger tissue volumes, higher tumor cellularity, and better viability, leading to higher organoid establishment rates and enabling repeated drug testing. In contrast, for patients with advanced disease, LCO establishment often relies on samples from percutaneous or bronchoscopic biopsies, or pleural effusions, and even metastatic lymph node biopsies, which yield limited cell numbers, increased necrosis, and contamination with nonmalignant cells. Such cases can result in reduced culture success and increased turnaround time with underrepresented intratumoral heterogeneity.27,28 Because the pooled data are based mainly on surgical samples, our findings should be interpreted with great caution when extending them to small biopsy or cytology specimens.

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it was inevitable that potential confounding factors might affect the results, as individual data were unavailable in our pooled analysis, especially LCOs derived from different samples. Second, the current meta-analysis on genetic mutation profiling and drug response prediction merely involved the studies from East Asia. Although a number of studies from Europe or the United States investigated the predictive value of PDOs for drug testing or repurposing in liver cancer,29,30 colorectal cancer,30 and even lung cancer, among others,26 the lack of available data on LCOs from Western populations compromised the robustness and global applicability of our pooled results. More studies on the basis of LCOs from resected samples in Western countries could corroborate our findings to determine whether LCOs reliably recapitulate the original genetic features and therapeutic responses to drugs of the primary tumors in different races.

In conclusion, our study indicates that LCOs can stably recapitulate the genomic features of patient tumors irrespective of histologic heterogeneity and can be a promising precision medicine tool for anticancer drug screening or for predicting drug responses.
