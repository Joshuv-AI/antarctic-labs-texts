A new era in prosthodontics opened by nutritional science
Authors: Ryuji Hosokawa
Published: 2024

Recently, one of my patients failed to attend a maintenance appointment for their implant treatment. Upon calling their home to check on the situation, I was informed by the patient’s wife that her husband had been hospitalized due to a sudden worsening of his diabetes, making it impossible for him to visit the dental clinic. As the patient’s primary care physician was a friend of mine, I had sent an E-mail to inquire about the circumstances. His response was: “Thanks to your excellent implant treatment, my patient’s nutritional management and lifestyle guidance have collapsed, leading to a weight gain of over 15 kg and a deterioration of his Type 2 diabetes, prompting us to decide on hospitalization in consultation with the patient.”

Wait, what?

The patient’s diabetes worsened because of my “excellent implant treatment?” Now when I recall the patient had said, “I can eat anything just like before. Being able to enjoy delicious food every day makes me so happy. Thank you, doctor!” after completion of full mouth implant treatment and I was very satisfied with the outcome of my treatment. However, learning that this treatment had ultimately led to a decline in the patient’s overall health was truly shocking. I realized that the restorating masticatory function does not necessarily contribute to the maintenance or recovery of health. Prosthodontists being health-care providers should never accept outcomes that worsen health due to our interventions.

In recent years, the nutritional significance of prosthodontic treatment is attracting attention. In the past, the main outcome of prosthodontic treatment was the restoration of masticatory function. Although prosthodontic treatment is viewed as rehabilitative medicine related to eating, the desired outcomes should also allow the appropriate intake of food and nutrients facilitated by this recovery, and further, the improvement and maintenance of nutritional status. We are verging into a new era that not only evaluate the treatment outcome based on the improvement in masticatory function but also on the improvement in overall health.

Reports comparing the nutritional status of edentulous patients and those wearing removable full dentures suggest that edentulous patients are more likely to exhibit malnutrition,[1] indicating that wearing removable dentures can improve nutritional status. However, provision of new dentures to existing denture-wearers do not change their nutritional status.[2] Although masticatory ability improves with denture repair or enhancement, it is difficult to improve blood nutritional markers.[3]

There are still few reports on implants and nutritional status and most of them compare implant overdentures with conventional dentures. A systematic review concluded that there is no difference in the impact on nutritional status between implant overdentures and conventional dentures[4] but is questionable whether this conclusion still holds true.

I believe that more high-quality clinical research needs to be conducted in future to demonstrate the nutritional effectiveness of prosthodontic treatments. In addition, the significance of nutritional assessment in implant treatment is beginning to be recognized. For example, a deficiency of Vitamin K2 leads to a decrease in active osteocalcin, affecting bone metabolism. Moreover, the accumulation of advanced glycation end-products in the body due to excessive sugar intake impacts collagen cross-linking, leading to a decline in bone quality (making it hard and brittle). These were demonstrated in clinical studies using bone biopsy samples.[5] Although these factors likely influence implant treatment, research exploring the risks of implant treatment from a nutritional perspective is sparse.

Thus, it is crucial for us to keep in mind the close relationship between prosthodontic clinical practice and nutritional science and to embark on new challenges in this area.
