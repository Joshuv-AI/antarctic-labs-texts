Real-world treatment patterns and early outcomes in advanced intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: a descriptive case series
Authors: Runze Yu, Hong Zhao, Dong Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) has a poor prognosis, with 70–80% of patients presenting advanced/metastatic disease. Conventional first-line chemotherapies have limitations, and targeted-immunotherapy combinations need real-world validation. This retrospective, single-center case series enrolled 12 advanced ICC patients to describe the real-world clinical characteristics, treatment patterns which including lenvatinib-based targeted-immunotherapy with or without Transarterial Interventional Therapies (TAIT) and conventional chemotherapy, as well as early efficacy and safety outcomes; and to generate preliminary hypotheses for subsequent prospective studies, without any intention to compare the efficacy of different regimens. Among the 12 patients, objective response rate was 33.3%, and disease control rate 83.33%. Nine received lenvatinib + PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (targeted-immunotherapy group), and 3 received systemic chemotherapy. Among patients receiving lenvatinib + PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, 22.22% experienced grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) with a median treatment duration of 9.0 months; among patients receiving conventional chemotherapy, 66.7% experienced grade ≥ 3 TRAEs with a median treatment duration of 19.0 months. Early TAIT (≤ 2 weeks) was associated with higher tumor reduction (mean 30.52 vs. 7.69% for non-early), which may help alleviate tumor-related symptoms in patients with high initial tumor burden. Baseline CA19-9 and albumin/globulin ratio showed exploratory potential associations with treatment efficacy without formal statistical analysis; larger initial diameter correlated with better response. This study describes that lenvatinib + Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors (ICIs) combined with early TAIT may be a feasible multimodal strategy used in real-world practice for advanced ICC, with preliminary observations of feasibility. Limitations include small single-center sample and short follow-up. The high heterogeneity of treatment regimens may also limit the comparability of efficacy and safety. Introduction

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC) is the second most common primary intrahepatic malignant tumor [1]. 70–80% of ICC patients present with locally advanced or metastatic disease, resulting in poor prognosis [2]. Prior to 2021, the standard of care (SOC) for advanced ICC remained dual-drug chemotherapy with gemcitabine plus cisplatin (Gem/Cis), as established by the landmark ABC-2 trial [3]. However, this regimen is limited by substantial systemic toxicity and modest survival benefits, with a median Overall Survival (mOS) of merely 11.7 months [3]. Alternative systemic options, such as the three-drug FOLFOX regimen or gemcitabine-based combinations with nab-paclitaxel, have shown marginal improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) but fail to address the unmet need for safer and more effective therapies, especially for patients intolerant to cisplatin [4].

Notably, the SOC for advanced ICC underwent a critical evolution during this study recruitment period (October 2021–May 2025). The TOPAZ-1 trial which was first published in 2022 demonstrated that adding durvalumab (a PD-L1 inhibitor) to Gem/Cis significantly improved mOS and disease control rate (DCR) in advanced biliary tract cancers, including ICC [5]. This combination was subsequently incorporated into clinical practice globally. In China, adoption of this SOC was gradual, Full adoption awaited its elevation to a Category 1A recommendation in the 2023 CSCO guidelines and, the inclusion of durvalumab in the National Reimbursement Drug List only from 2024 onwards[6]. Consequently, during 2021–2025 (the majority of our study period), real-world barriers, including cisplatin ineligibility (e.g., baseline renal dysfunction), prohibitive costs of durvalumab, and patient refusal of chemotherapy, left a large subgroup of advanced ICC patients without access to the updated SOC, and an alternative treatment options need to meet their clinical needs.

Against this backdrop, a multimodal strategy combining targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and local therapy is used in real-world practice for SOC-ineligible patients. High PD-L1 expression in ICC and vascular-rich tumor microenvironment provide a rationale for combining PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors, ICIs) with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) receptor inhibitors [7]. Lenvatinib, a multi-targeted VEGF inhibitor, not only suppresses tumor angiogenesis but also reverses the immunosuppressive microenvironment by reducing regulatory T cell (Treg) infiltration, thereby enhancing ICI efficacy [8]. Retrospective studies have reported that Lenvatinib + ICIs achieves a 28.6% objective response rate (ORR) and 6.1 month mPFS in cisplatin-intolerant advanced ICC, with a manageable safety profile [9]. Moreover, integrating local therapies such as Transarterial Interventional Therapies (TAIT), which include Transarterial Chemoembolization (TACE) and TACE-combined Hepatic Arterial Infusion Chemotherapy (TACE-HAIC), can deliver high-dose chemotherapy locally while minimizing systemic toxicity, potentially synergizing with systemic lenvatinib + ICIs to improve tumor control [10, 11].

The integration of these modalities of “Lenvatinib + ICIs + TAIT” represents a common yet poorly characterized multimodal strategy in real-world practice. Critical gaps persist regarding the early efficacy and safety outcomes of this strategy in SOC-ineligible patients, as well as the impact of TAIT integration timing (early vs. delayed) on short-term tumor response. To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective, single-center case series to describe the clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and early efficacy (tumor reduction rate) and safety outcomes of advanced ICC patients receiving Lenvatinib + ICIs ± TAIT; and to explore, within this cohort, whether the timing of TAIT relative to systemic therapy initiation is associated with differences in the degree of early tumor response, with the sole aim of generating testable hypotheses for future prospective research. We hypothesized that in patients on a Lenvatinib + ICIs backbone, early TAIT (≤ 2 weeks of systemic therapy initiation) would be associated with a higher mean tumor reduction rate on MRI assessment. Such real-world data cannot establish comparative efficacy but can generate hypotheses regarding treatment integration and identify signals for prospective validation.

Method

This retrospective single-center case series aimed to: (1) describe real-world treatment patterns (Lenvatinib-based targeted-immunotherapy ± TAIT vs. conventional chemotherapy), early efficacy (tumor reduction rate), and safety in advanced ICC patient ineligible for standard Gem/Cis ± durvalumab and (2) explore whether TAIT timing (early vs. non-early) is associated with early tumor response, generating hypotheses for future prospective studies. Notably, this study was not designed to compare the efficacy or safety of different treatment regimens nor to establish the superiority of any therapy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study enrolled 12 advanced ICC patients who received systemic antitumor therapy at the Cancer Hospital Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, from October 2021 to May 2025. The inclusion criteria of this study were as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed ICC; (2) diagnosis of advanced ICC, according the 8th Edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM Staging System for Biliary Tract Cancers (T3-T4, or N1, or M1) and the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) Stage C (unresectable, with either vascular invasion, extrahepatic spread, or both); (3) receipt of systemic chemotherapy or targeted-immunotherapy, combined with or without TAIT, including TACE or TACE-HAIC; (4) Child–Pugh classification of A or B; (5) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1; and (6) availability of medical and follow-up data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) age < 18 years or > 80 years; (2) no histopathological confirmation; (3) expected survival time ≤ 3 months; and (4) unavailability of medical and follow-up data.

Treatment strategy

All patients in this study underwent imaging examinations, blood tests, biochemical analyses, coagulation function tests, and measurement of specific tumor markers. Lenvatinib was selected as the targeted drug, administered orally once daily at a dose of 12 mg (for patients with body weight > 60 kg) or 8 mg (for patients with body weight < 60 kg); if patients could not tolerate the dose, it was reduced to 6 mg or 4 mg. Immunotherapeutic drugs included toripalimab, pembrolizumab, tislelizumab, camrelizumab, sintilimab, and durvalumab. These drugs were administered in accordance with relevant guidelines, and the selection of drugs was based on factors including price, applicable charitable donation policies, patients’ medical insurance status, and patients’ own preferences.

Dosing followed standard clinical guidelines and drug labels: toripalimab (240 mg every 3 weeks), pembrolizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks), tislelizumab (200 mg every 3 weeks), camrelizumab (200 mg every 2 weeks), sintilimab (200 mg every 3 weeks), and durvalumab (1500 mg every 4 weeks). Dose adjustments were made based on patient tolerance and renal/hepatic function.

The TAIT employed in this study included standalone TACE and TACE-HAIC. All treatments were performed by interventional radiologists with more than 5 years of experience. The TACE regimen consisted of combined lipiodol embolization, pre-embolization with blank microspheres, and precision embolization using drug-eluting microspheres loaded with epirubicin. The TACE-HAIC regimen, on the basis of TACE, involved local perfusion chemotherapy with a combination of cisplatin and gemcitabine. The definition of “early TAIT” (initiated ≤ 2 weeks after systemic therapy) was based on clinical practice, and the pharmacokinetic data show that lenvatinib reaches steady-state plasma concentrations within 1–2 weeks of oral administration [8], and concurrent TAIT may synergize with lenvatinib-induced tumor vascular normalization to enhance local drug delivery.

Treatment response assessment

Tumor response was assessed per RECIST 1.1 criteria with the following schedule: Baseline MRI was carried within 1 week before treatment initiation. Tumor response was assessed using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 4–6 weeks after the initiation of treatment regimen, and subsequent imaging follow-up was performed every 8–12 weeks regularly. Follow-up assessments included blood tests, biochemical analyses, coagulation function tests, and measurement of specific tumor markers, and MRI result was used to adjust treatment regimens and classify response. Systematic prospective molecular profiling was not part of the routine clinical workflow for this retrospective treatment series. No formal correlation analysis between biomarkers and treatment response was performed due to incomplete data. The study follow-up continued until May 31, 2025.

Statistical analysis

This study adopts a descriptive statistical approach, consistent with its retrospective case series design. All data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages; continuous variables are summarized using median and range. Given the study design as a descriptive case series, statistical analysis focused on summarizing data rather than group comparisons. No formal group comparisons were performed, as the individualized treatment design, small sample size (n = 12), and potential confounding factors precluded valid comparative inference. Tumor response was categorized as partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD) per RECIST 1.1 criteria.

Result

Baseline clinical characteristic analysis of all cases

The baseline characteristics of all 12 patients with advanced ICC in this study are summarized in Table 1, including demographic features, liver function, tumor biomarkers, and immunohistochemical (IHC) profiles. Demographically, the cohort had a median age of 65.2 years (range: 56–75 years), with a slight predominance of male patients (7/12, 58.3%). In terms of functional status, all patients met the eligibility criteria for ECOG-PS (0 or 1), with 8 patients (66.7%) having ECOG-PS 0 (indicating no functional impairment) and 4 patients (33.3%) having ECOG-PS 1 (mild functional impairment). Liver function was preserved in most patients: 8 patients (66.7%) were classified as Child–Pugh A (normal liver function) and 4 patients (33.3%) as Child–Pugh B (mild liver dysfunction), consistent with the exclusion of patients with severe hepatic impairment. Laboratory parameters reflecting liver function and systemic status were as follows: Median alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 85.4 U/L (range: 28.8–339.4 U/L). Median total bilirubin (T-bil) was 46.7 μmol/L (range: 10.3–205.8 μmol/L), and median albumin (Alb) was 42.5 g/L (range: 39.6–47.3 g/L), both within normal limits. The median albumin/globulin (A/G) ratio was 1.5 (range: 1.2–1.9), with no patients showing severe hypoalbuminemia or hyperglobulinemia. Tumor marker levels varied widely: Median carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA19-9) was 368.9 U/mL (range: 2.0–10000 U/mL), with 7 patients (58.3%) having markedly elevated CA19-9 (> 100 U/mL), indicating heterogeneous tumor aggressiveness across the cohort. Tumor and IHC characteristics further defined the cohort: Median baseline maximum tumor diameter was 7.3 cm (range: 3.1–12.1 cm), with 5 patients (41.7%) having large tumors (diameter ≥ 8 cm). All patients had one single primary tumor lesion. In terms of metastasis, Patients 1 and 5 had gallbladder metastasis, while Patients 2, 3, 8, 9, 10 and 11 had lymph node metastasis; no distant metastasis was observed in any patient. All 12 patients showed positive expression of CK7 and CK19 (CK7 + /CK19 +). For biomarkers relevant to targeted-immunotherapy, FGFR2 expression was positive in 2 patients (16.7%), while PD-L1 expression (assessed via combined positive score, CPS) was heterogeneous: one patient had CPS ≥ 10, 2 patients had CPS 1, and 9 patients had no CPS results. The Ki-67 proliferation index (a marker of tumor proliferation activity) ranged from 5 to 50%, and 5 patients had a Ki-67 index ≥ 20%, suggesting moderate-to-high proliferative activity in half the cohort. This study only focused on early efficacy evaluated by tumor diameter reduction, and no survival endpoints including PFS and OS were analyzed due to the short follow-up duration and the hypothesis-generating study design.

Patients 4, 5, 9, and 10 suspended their treatments, and Patient 7 only discontinued targeted therapy; the reasons for treatment discontinuation in these 5 patients are presented in Table 2. Local treatment was widely used in the comprehensive treatment strategy of this study, mainly adopting two interventional approaches: TACE and TACE-HAIC. The drugs used in HAIC are as described in Supplementary Table 3. Among the 9 patients receiving targeted-immunotherapy combined with TAIT, 6 (66.7%) initiated TAIT in the early treatment phase (≤ 2 weeks, defined as early TAIT group), and 3 (33.3%) initiated TAIT after 2 weeks (non-early TAIT group). The tumor diameter reduction rate of the early TAIT group was 21.03–49.41% (mean: 30.52%), higher than the 0–15.38% (mean: 7.69%) of the non-early TAIT group. The early TAIT group showed a higher mean tumor reduction rate than the non-early TAIT group, and this finding only indicates a potential association rather than a causal relationship between early TAIT initiation and greater tumor shrinkage. No causal inference can be drawn from this descriptive observation due to the study design limitations. But this short-term tumor shrinkage may have potential clinical value for patients with high initial tumor burden (e.g., baseline diameter > 8 cm, as seen in Patients 2, 7, and 8 in this group), and rapid reduction in tumor volume could alleviate tumor-related symptoms (such as abdominal pain or biliary obstruction) and reduce the risk of tumor progression-related complications, providing a favorable window for subsequent systemic therapy (Table 3).

The mean baseline diameter of patients in the SD group was higher (8.28 cm), which was higher than that of the 4 patients with partial response (mean 7.33 cm), while the baseline diameter of the PD group was lower (mean 4.15 cm). Further analysis of the PD group (Patients 11 and 12) showed that both patients received a treatment regimen with systemic chemotherapy as the core, combined with targeted or immunotherapeutic agents as adjuvants. Despite the smaller baseline diameter and relatively longer treatment duration, their tumor diameters rebounded after a brief reduction, ultimately presenting as progressive disease. This suggests that the “chemotherapy + immunotherapy” combination model may have limitations in long-term tumor control efficacy for small-volume advanced ICC, and the regimen needs to be optimized by incorporating more individualized factors.

Discussion

As a retrospective descriptive series, our study generates hypotheses rather than establishing comparative efficacy. Specifically, we analyzed the real-world imaging response degree of the multimodal strategy, lenvatinib combined with ICIs (with or without local therapy) in advanced ICC, and summarized the safety profile of these regimens, aiming to provide preliminary signals for subsequent prospective studies. Our clinical exploration yielded the following findings: First, lenvatinib combined with ICIs with or without TAIT is an alternative to systemic chemotherapy as the regimen for advanced ICC patients, offering preliminary clinical insights for patients where standard chemotherapy may not be prioritized. Second, in the multimodal strategy, integration of TAIT was observed to be associated with accelerated tumor response in real-world practice; this observation was more frequent when local therapy was initiated in the early treatment phase (≤ 2 weeks). Third, there may be an exploratory association between baseline characteristics and treatment response: Larger initial tumor diameter was tentatively correlated with greater tumor shrinkage, and the A/G ratio showed potential relevance to efficacy. Furth, there is no consistent correlation between the occurrence of adverse events in targeted-immunotherapy and treatment response. Some cases in the study showed tumor diameter reduction accompanied by lymph node metastasis, suggesting that therapeutic efficacy and metastasis risk need to be evaluated separately.

Our study does not propose a “chemotherapy-free” strategy, but rather a multimodal approach that replaces conventional systemic chemotherapy (e.g., Gem/Cis) with a Lenvatinib + ICI backbone. Consistent with the present study, lenvatinib combined with ICIs has demonstrated favorable antitumor activity as first-line therapy for advanced ICC in previous studies, providing a broader perspective for the treatment. The efficacy of lenvatinib monotherapy in unresectable ICC has been validated in a Phase II trial (NCT02579616) [12]. In recent years, several published retrospective studies have further applied its combination with ICIs as a treatment regimen, and the ORR and mPFS of lenvatinib combined with toripalimab as first-line therapy for advanced ICC were 20.0% and 5.4 months, respectively [13]. Another Phase II trial showed that this regimen achieved an average ORR of 37.2% and an average mPFS of 7.7 months, which were significantly superior to conventional systemic chemotherapy regimens [14, 15]. Additionally, the triple combination regimen of lenvatinib, tislelizumab, and chemotherapy has been confirmed to be a potential effective conversion therapy for advanced ICC before surgery [16]. These cases fully illustrate the clinical application potential of such combination therapies. However, there are significant differences in efficacy among different studies, which may be attributed to variations in baseline characteristics of cohorts, tumor genotypes, and types/doses of targeted-immunotherapy across studies. Therefore, although numerous current studies support the efficacy, more clinical trials are still needed to confirm the efficacy and safety, as well as to establish feasible individualized treatment strategies.

Various PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors (including tislelizumab, toripalimab, sintilimab, pembrolizumab, camrelizumab, and durvalumab) were used in this study; these agents have established antitumor activity in biliary tract cancers in prior studies[17–22], and their selection was based on real-world factors including drug access, patient insurance status, and individual preference. The selection of Lenvatinib + ICI ± TAIT rather than standard first-line therapy (gemcitabine + cisplatin ± durvalumab) in most patients reflects real-world clinical constraints during the study period (2021–2025). These constraints included cisplatin ineligibility (e.g., baseline renal dysfunction), limited access to durvalumab, and patient refusal of systemic chemotherapy. Thus, the regimens described herein represent practical alternatives for patients unable to receive standard care, rather than intentional avoidance of guideline-recommended therapy.

TAIT can improve survival rates and alleviate tumor-related clinical manifestations on the basis of systemic therapy [10, 23]. Numerous studies have validated the combination of TAIT with VEGF-targeted agents for the treatment of advanced tumors. This combination has shown favorable therapeutic effects in other tumor types such as HCC [24, 25] and has also been effectively verified in the treatment of advanced ICC [11, 26, 27]. However, this combination model still has obvious limitations in ICC treatment: First, most relevant studies adopt a retrospective design, with problems such as a small number of studies, small sample sizes, and lack of prospective validation. Second, in clinical practice, there are no standardized guidelines for key decisions regarding TAIT, including the timing of local therapy, drug selection, and dosage determination. These decisions still rely solely on clinicians’ empirical judgment, making it difficult to ensure the consistency and accuracy of treatment. To address the above issues, this study provides practical references within the framework of individualized therapy: Local TAIT is recommended for implementation in the early phase of systemic therapy (≤ 2 weeks). Results showed favorable outcomes in terms of tumor size response. This suggests that the timing of local therapy may be associated with differences in early tumor response, and scientific selection should be made in conjunction with the treatment phase. Patients selected for early TAIT may have differed in baseline tumor burden, liver function, performance status, or local disease aggressiveness. These baseline differences could confound the observed association between early TAIT timing and tumor shrinkage. Thus, the result should be interpreted cautiously and cannot establish a causal effect.

Developing an individualized treatment strategy for patients with advanced ICC requires integrating their laboratory test results and tumor burden level. In this study, the initial tumor diameter at the start of treatment may have a weak correlation with the degree of response to targeted-immunotherapy. Patient 7, with the largest baseline tumor diameter, achieved the most significant tumor shrinkage after targeted-immunotherapy (maximum reduction rate of 49.41%). In contrast, Patient 12, with the smallest baseline tumor diameter, experienced continuous tumor growth within one year. Laboratory tests showed that targeted-immunotherapy can rapidly reduce tumor burden and improve liver function indicators in the initial treatment phase. Further analysis revealed that the baseline data with significant predictive value for therapeutic efficacy are initial CA19-9 and albumin/globulin (A/G) ratio: higher initial CA19-9 correlated with poorer response, even with a smaller tumor diameter. And higher A/G ratio (indicating lower globulin and impaired immune function) may affect immunotherapy response.

Notably, these correlations should be interpreted cautiously due to three unanalyzed confounders. First, our cohort included 8 patients with ECOG-PS 0 and 4 with ECOG-PS 1 (Table 1). Patients with ECOG-PS 0 may have tolerated longer treatment durations and lower rates of treatment interruption, which could exaggerate the apparent association between larger baseline tumor diameter and better shrinkage. Second, extrahepatic disease burden varied across patients: 8 patients had gallbladder or lymph node metastasis at baseline, 4 patients had lymph node metastasis (Table 2), while 8 had no more extrahepatic lesions. Patients without extrahepatic disease typically have slower disease progression, which may overestimate the efficacy of early TAIT in this subgroup. Third, liver function may affect drug metabolism: Child–Pugh A patients had a lower risk of treatment discontinuation due to hepatic adverse events.

In this study, lymph node metastasis occurred in 4 patients after treatment. Among them, Patients 7 and 9 developed lymph node metastases with the tumor diameters continued to shrink, suggesting asynchrony between local tumor control and metastasis risk with targeted-immunotherapy. Additionally, 4 patients experienced adverse events, including gastrointestinal bleeding (16.7%, 2/12) and liver failure (16.7%, 2/12). There was no clear correlation between the occurrence of adverse events and tumor treatment response. Even if patients achieve reduced tumor burden, targeted monitoring and management of adverse events are still required.

The extremely small sample size (n = 12) and high heterogeneity of treatment regimens severely compromise the internal and external validity of this study. For internal validity, the non-uniform treatment regimens lead to unavoidable confounding factors, making it impossible to accurately attribute the observed changes in ORR, DCR, and safety outcomes to a specific therapeutic agent or modality; the small sample size further magnifies the random error of the results, reducing the reliability of the observed ORR (33.3%) and DCR (83.33%). For generalizability, the highly individualized treatment regimens were tailored to the real-world clinical characteristics of the small cohort, and thus the findings cannot be extrapolated to the general advanced ICC population, especially patients eligible for standard first-line chemotherapy. In addition, the heterogeneity of TAIT modalities and drug doses also limits the interpretability of the association between TAIT timing and tumor shrinkage rate, as different TAIT regimens may have distinct local therapeutic effects that interfere with the observation of early tumor response.

As a hypothesis-generating case series, this study was not designed to conduct head-to-head comparisons of different regimens, and thus no conclusion on the superiority of lenvatinib-based targeted-immunotherapy over conventional chemotherapy can be drawn. This study has several limitations: It enrolled a single-center cohort of advanced ICC patients with a small sample size (only 12 cases) and short follow-up duration, makes it impossible to accurately calculate PFS and OS, and thus the clinical significance of the observed early tumor shrinkage remains unclear. The lack of comparable groups and the individualized nature of each regimen preclude any causal conclusions. Second, the short follow-up duration limits the assessment of the clinical value of early TAIT, and although the early TAIT group showed a higher short-term tumor shrinkage rate than the non-early group, the long-term prognostic outcomes of clinical trials for advanced ICC cannot be analyzed. Third, the lack of systematic molecular profiling is also a key limitation. Large-scale prospective multicenter studies are needed to further validate the advantages and long-term outcomes of targeted-immunotherapy combined with early TAIT. Meanwhile, deep exploration should be conducted on core issues such as the development of individualized targeted-immunotherapy strategies and the optimal timing of local therapy intervention.
