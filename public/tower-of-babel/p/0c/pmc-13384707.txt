A hidden tunnel at the skull base: A descriptive anatomical study of the mastoid foramen in dry skulls
Authors: Ceren Uğuz Gençer, Mustafa Deniz Yörük, Zeynep Nisa Karakoyun, Hasan Tetiker, Mehmet İlkay Koşar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The mastoid foramen (MF) is a small but clinically significant anatomical structure located on the mastoid process of the temporal bone, showing considerable individual variation in its morphology and localization. This descriptive morphometric study aimed to provide data relevant to surgical planning by defining the frequency, morphometric characteristics, and relationship of the mastoid foramen with bony reference points. A total of 185 dry human skulls (370 hemiskulls) of unknown sex were examined. The diameter of the largest mastoid foramen, defined as the major mastoid foramen (MMF), and its distances to the asterion and mastoid process were measured using a digital caliper. The relationship of the MF with the occipitomastoid suture and the transverse plane passing through the zygomatic arch was evaluated. In addition, the number, localization, and quadrant-based distribution of accessory mastoid foramina (MFa) relative to the MMF were assessed. The most common finding was the presence of 2 mastoid foramina (37.8%), followed by a single foramen (34.3%). The MMF diameter ranged from 0.60 to 7.65 mm. The mean distance from the MMF to the asterion was 18.10 ± 5.34 mm, and to the mastoid process was 29.60 ± 5.38 mm. The MF was most frequently located anterior to the occipitomastoid suture and inferior to the zygomatic arch line. Accessory mastoid foramina were identified in approximately half of the hemiskulls and were most commonly clustered in the anterosuperior and posterosuperior quadrants. This anatomical study demonstrates significant variations about the mastoid foramen with respect to frequency, diameter, and spatial relationships to anatomical landmarks. A detailed evaluation of the clinical significance of these variations suggests a potential contribution to the prevention of surgical complications; however, confirmation of their applicability in clinical practice would benefit from further support by radiologic imaging and clinical studies.

1. Introduction

The mastoid foramen (MF) is a small but clinically significant anatomical structure located on the mastoid process of the temporal bone, whose morphology and localization show considerable interindividual variability. This foramen contains the mastoid emissary vein (MEV), which enables bidirectional blood flow between the intracranial and extracranial venous systems, as well as a meningeal branch of the occipital artery.[1,2] Since the mastoid emissary vein does not contain valves, venous blood flow is not restricted to a single direction; while this contributes to the regulation of pressure in the intracranial venous sinuses, it also allows infections or thrombi from the external environment to be transmitted to intracranial structures.[3-6]

The MF is most often located in the mastoid part of the temporal bone, on or near the occipitomastoid suture. The clinical significance of the MF lies both in its anatomical variability and in its potential to increase surgical risk. In retrosigmoid craniotomy, mastoidectomy, ear surgery, and various skull base interventions, procedures performed without considering the location and diameter of the MF may increase the risk of uncontrolled bleeding, air embolism, thrombosis, sinus injuries, and serious neurological consequences.[7,8] In addition, in cases where the jugular foramen remains developmentally narrow, the MF may enlarge and assume venous drainage.[1,9,10] This adaptive enlargement, however, may lead to unexpected vascular injuries during surgery. Certain clinical conditions, such as chronic otitis media, may also alter MEV blood flow, affect the size of the MF, and increase surgical risks.[6,11]

In recent years, with the advancement of surgical techniques used in neurosurgery, otorhinolaryngology, and plastic surgery, the clinical significance of the mastoid foramen and the mastoid emissary vein has attracted increasing attention. The positional variability of this structure has become a factor requiring detailed preliminary evaluation in terms of surgical safety.[7,10,12]

Accordingly, this anatomical study aimed to systematically evaluate the mastoid foramen on dry human skulls by determining its frequency, morphometric characteristics, and spatial relationships with established bony landmarks. In addition, the distribution patterns of accessory mastoid foramina (MFa) were analyzed using a quadrant-based topographic approach. We hypothesized that the mastoid foramen exhibits considerable morphometric and topographic variability that cannot be adequately characterized by a single uniform anatomical description, and that detailed mapping of these variations may provide a more reliable anatomical framework for preoperative assessment.

2. Materials and methods

This study was designed as a descriptive cross-sectional observational study. The study included 185 dry skulls (370 half skulls) of unknown age and gender from the bone collection of the Department of Anatomy, Faculty of Medicine, Mugla Sitki Kocman University. Only skulls with preserved anatomical integrity, considered to belong to adults, and suitable for morphometric evaluation were included. Seventeen skulls with fractures, deformities, erosions, trauma, or pathological findings were excluded from the study. The skulls were obtained from the Council of Forensic Medicine and the Cemeteries Directorate and belonged to unclaimed individuals; they are from individuals who lived in the western region of Anatolia within the last 100 to 150 years.

A specific descriptive number was assigned to each specimen; the measurements were systematically recorded in data recording forms, and then they were transferred into digital format. During the measurements, the skulls were positioned on a stable surface, and the standard anatomical position was maintained. Reference anatomical points were defined according to anatomical atlases. Each specimen was examined by 2 authors with respect to the presence of the mastoid foramen, and the observations were discussed together before being recorded.

The presence and number (single, multiple, bilateral) of the MF were determined on each skull. Each opening considered an MF was checked with a 0.4 mm diameter needle, and those with continuity to the canal were included in the evaluation. The diameters of all mastoid foramina were measured in millimeters twice by the same observer using a digital caliper, and the mean of the 2 measurements was used for analysis in order to minimize random measurement variation. In cases where more than 1 MF was present, the 1 with the largest diameter was evaluated as the major mastoid foramen (MMF), while the smaller foramina were defined as accessory mastoid foramina (MFa) for identification purposes. The mean, standard deviation, and median of the largest diameter were calculated. In accordance with the classification used by Yurdabakan et al, mastoid foramina were grouped based on diameter, given that an increased risk of bleeding has been reported in cases measuring 2.5 mm or greater. Accordingly, foramina were categorized as 0.4 to 2.49 mm, 2.5 to 4 mm, and >4 mm.[4,8-10,13] The incidence rates of MF on the right and left sides and the diameter measurements of the foramina were compared. The localization of the MMF with the largest diameter was determined relative to the occipitomastoid suture (anterior, posterior, and on the suture) and to the transverse plane passing through the zygomatic arch (superior, inferior, and on the line), and their percentages were calculated (Fig. 1). In addition, the distances of the MMF to the mastoid process and the asterion were measured (Fig. 2), and the median, mean, and standard deviations were calculated, and the significance levels of the measurements for the right and left sides were compared.

The presence and number of MFas were also recorded in the same way, provided that there was continuity with the canal. Diameter measurements of the MFas were performed separately for each foramen, as in the MMF, and recorded. The relationship of all MFas to the occipitomastoid suture was examined (Fig. 3). Accordingly, they were evaluated in 3 groups as anterior, posterior, and on the occipitomastoid suture. Taking the largest diameter MMF as the center, the localization of the MFa was evaluated in relation to this foramen. Perpendicular and parallel planes to the ground passing through the MMF were determined, and the region was divided into 4 quadrants (Fig. 4). The number and percentage of MFas located in these quadrants were evaluated. The quadrant localization of the MFas (anterosuperior, posterosuperior, anteroinferior, posteroinferior) was recorded. The distance of all MFas to the outer margin of the MMF was measured, and the mean, standard deviation, and median were determined (Fig. 2).

2.1. Statistical analysis

The data analyses were performed using Jamovi 2024, (Version 2.6) Computer Software. Descriptive data were presented with number and percentage, mean, median, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum distribution measures. The conformity of mastoid foramen measurements to a normal distribution was analyzed with the Shapiro–Wilk Test. Since they did not conform to a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was used in the analyses. Due to incomplete data caused by bone deformation during the measurements, the number of skulls analyzed for each parameter is shown in the table.

3. Results

A total of 309 out of 370 half skulls (83.5%) demonstrated the presence of MF. The incidence of MF on the right side was calculated as 84.9%, and on the left side as 82.2%. No statistically significant difference was found between the right and left sides (P > .05). The number of MFs observed ranged between 0 and 4 (Fig. 5). 4. Discussion

The present study anatomically evaluates the mastoid foramen with respect to its prevalence, morphometry, and distribution. This study showed that the mastoid foramen was present in a significant proportion of the examined specimens and showed considerable variability in terms of number, size, and topographic distribution. The mastoid foramen was most frequently located anterior to the occipitomastoid suture and inferior to the zygomatic arch plane. Accessory mastoid foramina were also frequently detected and tended to cluster in superior quadrants compared to the major foramen.

When the literature is searched, it is seen that studies regarding the presence of MF have been conducted on dry skulls, cadavers, and computed tomography scans. In these studies, when the presence of MF was evaluated, it was reported within a rather wide range, between 27.8% and 98%.[1,2,4,7-10,14,15] In the study, the frequency of mastoid foramen occurrence was found to be 83.5%, which falls within the wide range reported in the literature. Among the reasons for this wide range are thought to be differences in sample size within the study groups (n = 22–1500), societal and individual anatomical variations, methodological differences in measurement techniques, and evaluation criteria. In addition, not every externally visible foramen may in fact continue with a canal, and depending on whether this criterion is taken into consideration, the percentage of MF occurrence may vary.[11,16] In addition, because patency was assessed using a fine needle, very small foramina or those with limited canal continuity may have been underdetected.

Mastoid foramen (MF), while absent unilaterally or bilaterally in some individuals, may be detected in considerable numbers in some cases. In the literature, it has been reported that the number of MFs may vary between 0 and 9.[1,2,4,7,10,14] Although Hampl et al reported the highest number of MFs, they emphasized cases with 5 to 9 MFs occurred with a frequency of <0.5% and were therefore considered negligible.[7] The second-highest number of MFs was reported by Ertürk et al as 6, but they observed this only on the left half.[2] In the present study, the maximum number of mastoid foramina observed was 4, identified in a small proportion of skulls on both sides. Overall, the most common configuration was the presence of 2 mastoid foramina. Side-specific analysis showed that a single foramen was most frequent on the right side, whereas 2 foramina were more commonly observed on the left side. In a study conducted by Hampl et al on 295 dry skulls, similar to our study, the most frequent finding was 2 MFs (41.2%).[7] This difference among studies is thought to be due to the wide range in sample size and to technical factors in computed tomography imaging that may have influenced the visibility and number of MFs.

Studies have emphasized that the diameter is as important as the number of MFs. Kim et al suggested that they encountered serious venous bleeding during right mastoid surgery and that the reason for this was an MEV with a 4 mm opening.[10] As seen in this case, however, as the diameter of the foramen increases, the vessel also becomes larger in caliber, and this may bring serious risks of complications such as uncontrolled bleeding, air embolism, or sinus thrombosis during surgical interventions.[11,12] Based on previous literature values, foramina with a diameter >2.5 mm have been accepted as surgically significant.[8,13] This diameter means that the surgeon must not only use electrocautery but also dissect and ligate the vessels.[10] In the study by Kim et al, 15% of the cases had MFs larger than 2.5 mm, and 4.3% had MFs larger than 4 mm.[10] Yurdabakan et al found the largest MF reported in the literature, with a diameter of 10.2 mm.[4] In the same study, they reported that 54.9% of their cases had a diameter of 2 mm, and 28.7% had a diameter >4 mm. In the remaining studies, the largest MF diameter ranged between 4.92 mm and 6.3 mm.[1-3,17] Accordingly, in this study, MF of 2.5 mm and above was found in more than one-third of the cases, and 4 mm and above was found in one-tenth of the cases. In addition, the largest MF diameter was found to be 7.65 mm on the right side and 5.34 mm on the left side (Fig. 6). When the studies were evaluated, it was observed that the numerical ranges were quite wide, and the data were considered insufficient for a definitive conclusion. The most important result that can be drawn in common is that cases above 2.5 mm are numerous enough not to be underestimated. This situation shows that the mastoid foramen is not only an anatomical structure but also a potential risk area that should be considered in surgical planning.[18] Therefore, the individual anatomical characteristics of patients should be taken into account during the preoperative evaluation process, and it is important for clinicians who are familiar with the anatomy of the region to make the evaluation together.[13,18,19] In addition, considering that the clinical importance of the mastoid foramen is increased by the passage of the mastoid emissary vein through it, it would be useful to investigate the MF-MEV relationship and its dimensions in large population-based series in future studies.

To explore the potential surgical localization of the mastoid foramen, its relationship with selected bony landmarks was evaluated. The MMF was most commonly located anterior to or directly on the occipitomastoid suture, and predominantly inferior to the zygomatic arch line. This pattern suggests a relatively consistent anatomical distribution, although notable interindividual variation remains.

Bony landmarks are widely used in anatomical and radiologic studies to describe cranial variability and to support surgical orientation. In the present study, the distances between the mastoid foramen and commonly used bony reference points, namely the asterion and the mastoid process, were evaluated to better define its variable localization. While the mean distance values were generally in agreement with those previously reported,[1,7,8,10,20] the wide range observed between minimum and maximum measurements indicates substantial interindividual variability. This finding suggests that reliance on fixed distance values alone may be insufficient for accurate localization of the mastoid foramen and that individual anatomical assessment remains important in surgical practice.

Since the gender information of the dry skulls used in this study was not available, an evaluation based on gender could not be performed. However, in right–left comparisons of the measured parameters, no significant difference was found. Similarly, in the literature, no difference was reported between the right and left sides, and no significant differences were detected in female–male comparisons.[2,7,10] On the other hand, Berge and Boyd reported that large mastoid foramina and multiple foramina may be observed more frequently on the right side.[17] In the present study as well, the largest mastoid foramen was found on the right side; the mean values were observed to be higher on the right side, but this difference was not found to be statistically significant. Taken together, these data indicate that the mastoid foramen does not show a marked difference according to gender and side, but that significant differences exist between populations. In this respect, our study contributes to the literature by drawing attention to the interpopulation variability of mastoid foramen morphometry.

In addition to the major mastoid foramen, accessory mastoid foramina were frequently observed. These foramina were most often located anterior to the occipitomastoid suture and were more commonly distributed in the superior quadrants relative to the major foramen (Fig. 7). The frequent presence of accessory foramina indicates that control of the largest mastoid foramen alone may not fully eliminate the risk of venous bleeding, as additional emissary veins may be encountered in close proximity. Measurements of the distance between the major and accessory foramina further support this anatomical complexity. Notably, accessory mastoid foramina were found to reach considerable diameters, underscoring their potential relevance during surgical procedures. Taken together, these findings emphasize the need for careful anatomical assessment of the mastoid region, particularly when planning interventions in areas where multiple foramina may coexist.

5. Conclusions

This study examines the morphology of the mastoid foramen in an Anatolian population using a large series of dry skulls. The findings reveal population-specific patterns in the localization of the mastoid foramen and provide an anatomical reference framework that may assist surgical planning. In this study, major and accessory mastoid foramina were analyzed together and mapped separately using a quadrant-based approach referenced to fixed cranial anatomical landmarks. This methodology allows for the identification of regions where not only the main mastoid foramen, but also accessory mastoid foramina are more frequently encountered.

A strong anatomical knowledge plays a critical role in reducing the risk of intraoperative injury, improving surgical outcomes, and enhancing the overall safety of modern neurosurgical care. In this context, future clinical studies that integrate these anatomical findings with imaging and intraoperative data may further support their use in surgical planning and contribute to improving the safety of procedures involving the mastoid region.

6. Limitations

This study has several limitations. The sex and age of the examined dry skulls were unknown, which precluded demographic comparisons. As the study was conducted on dry skeletal material, the flow and hemodynamic characteristics of the mastoid emissary vein could not be evaluated. Minor differences in the preservation status of the skulls and minimal variations related to the measurement technique may also have influenced the results. Although repeated measurements were performed to minimize random measurement variation, formal intra- and inter-observer reliability statistics were not calculated, which should be considered when interpreting the reproducibility of the morphometric findings. In addition, patency of the mastoid foramen was assessed using a 0.4-mm needle; therefore, foramina with diameters smaller than this threshold may have been underdetected. Therefore, it is recommended that the present findings be validated through imaging-based and surgically oriented clinical studies before being applied to clinical practice in living individuals.
