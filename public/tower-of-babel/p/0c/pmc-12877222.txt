Association between malnutrition risk and the prognosis of geriatric heart failure patients undergoing left ventricular assist device implantation
Authors: Rui Shi, Zhenhan Li, Xinyue Duan, Jun Luo, Yuxiang Luo, Qingchen Wu, Dan Chen, Xin Tian, Hongtao Tie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence regarding the association between malnutrition risk and the prognosis of geriatric heart failure (HF) patients undergoing left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation is lacking. Our study aims to investigate how malnutrition risk, assessed using the geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI) values, relates to the outcomes of geriatric HF patients undergoing LVAD, using data from the Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (INTERMACS) registry.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using data from INTERMACS registry. Patients aged 65 and above who received LVAD implantation between 2008 and 2017 were included. The GNRI was used to stratify patients into three groups: absence of risk (GNRI > 98), mild risk (GNRI 92–98), and moderate to severe risk (GNRI < 92). The primary outcome was long-term survival for up to four years following LVAD implantation.ResultsWe included 5429 patients aged 65 and above undergoing LVADs. The malnutrition risk was presented in 75.8% of the patients before implantation, decreasing to 32.4% at 3 months, and 59.5% at 24 months post-implantation. Moderate-to-severe malnutrition risk, both pre-implant and 6 months post-implant, was associated with an increased risk of death (pre-implant: hazard ratio (HR): 1.25 [95% CI: 1.12–1.40], p < 0.001; 6 months: HR: 2.36 [95% CI: 2.01–2.77], p < 0.001). Mortality decreased with increasing pre-implant GNRI up to approximately 100 and 6-month post-implant GNRI up to 104. Patients with malnutrition risk also had an increased risk of infection, poor quality of life (QoL), and reduced functional exercise capacity after the LVAD implantation.ConclusionsMalnutrition risk was highly prevalent in HF patients aged 65 and above undergoing LVADs implantation. The presence of malnutrition risk was associated with an increased risk of death, infection, poor quality of life, and reduced functional exercise capacity.Keywords: Heart failure, LVAD, Nutrition status, GNRI

Introduction

The left ventricular assist device (LVAD) is a life-saving therapeutic option for patients with advanced heart failure (HF) [1,2]. According to the latest ISHLT Guidelines for Mechanical Circulatory Support [3], there are two major indications for mechanical circulatory support: bridge to cardiac transplantation (BTT) or permanent therapy for end-stage refractory heart failure, referred to as destination therapy (DT). Advancements in technology, have led to improved patients outcomes [4], resulting in increased utilization of LVAD, even as DT [5,6]. The prevalence of HF in adults, coupled with the growing geriatric population, has led to an increased demand for LVAD therapy in this patient group [7]. A study using data from the National Inpatient Sample in the United States demonstrated a rise in LVAD implantation incidence from 3.5% to 10.5% over a 10-year period in patients aged 75 years and above [8].

Geriatric patients often have extensive medical histories, significant comorbidity, and disabilities, and malnutrition is highly prevalent in those with HF [9]. There is a vicious cycle between HF and malnutrition. The development of HF involves hormonal dysregulation, neurohormonal and inflammatory activation, leading to an imbalance between anabolism and catabolism. This imbalance is compounded by increased metabolic demands, reduced appetite, and physical limitations that hinder nutritional intake. Gastrointestinal issues associated with HF medications and the disease itself can further impede nutrient absorption. Dietary restrictions, such as limitations on sodium intake, often result in a lack of nutritional variety, increasing the risk of deficiencies. Malnutrition can drive HF progression, and malnourished individuals with HF experience higher rates of hospitalization, longer recovery times after LVAD implantation, and increased risk of mortality [[10], [11], [12]].

The geriatric nutritional risk index (GNRI), which incorporates height, weight, and serum albumin, has been validated as a screening tool for malnutrition risk in patients aged 75 years and above [13,14]. Previous studies have found that malnutrition risk, as assessed by GNRI, is associated with a worse prognosis in HF patients [12,15,16]. However, there is limited data on the malnutrition risk assessed by GNRI in HF patients aged 75 years and above undergoing LVAD implantation. Our study aims to investigate the relationship between the malnutrition risk, as determined by GNRI values, and outcome of geriatric HF patients undergoing LVADs using data from the Interagency Registry for Mechanically Assisted Circulatory Support (INTERMACS) registry (available at: http://www.intermacs.org).

Patients and methods

Data source and ethical considerations

Data of patients aged over 65 who received LVAD implantation between 2008 and 2017 were retrospectively retrieved from the INTERMACS database, including the relative data of quality of life (QoL) of survivors, as defined by the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) and the 6-minute walk test (6MWT). The INTERMACS is a prospective registry of patients with mechanical circulatory device support. The data for this study were requested through the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Biologic Specimen and Data Repository Information Coordinating Center of the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (https://biolincc.nhlbi.nih.gov/home/) (approval date: 2021-09-07, application number: #10242). According to the INTERMACS database, the QoL and 6MWT were examined pre-implant, 3 months follow-up, 6 months follow-up, and every 6 months thereafter the LVAD implantation until the device is explanted or the patient has a transplant [17]. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (2021-410-02). Patients were excluded if they had age less than 65 years old, with total artificial heart, an isolated right ventricular assist device, a history of ventricular assist device or heart transplantation, or a non-continuous-flow LVAD were excluded. Besides, patients lacking information on gender, height, weight, and serum albumin or those with a history of hepatitis or liver dysfunction were also excluded. The detailed patient selection process is described in Fig. 1. The GNRI was calculated by using the following formula: =1.489*serum albumin (g/L))+(41.7*weight kgideal body weight kg) [13,14]. Ideal body weight (IBW) for men (kg) was calculated by =height cm-100-(height-100/4). For women, IBW (kg) was calculated by =height cm-100-(height-150/2). If the patient’s body weight/IBW ≥ 1, then the ratio of weight/IBW was assigned to 1. Patients thus were stratiﬁed into three groups according to the GNRI values: absence of risk: GNRI of ≥98; mild risk: GNRI of 92 to < 98; moderate to severe risk: GNRI of < 92. The primary outcome is long-term survival, up to four years post-implant of LVAD implantation. The secondary outcomes include 1): adverse events including bleeding, cardiac arrhythmia, stroke, infection, right HF, and venous thromboembolism according to the definition of INTERMACS. 2): In survivors, the quality of life (QoL), as defined by the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) and the 6-minute walk test (6MWT).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative and categorical variables are presented as frequency (percentage) and mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were used to verify the distribution of data. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test or one-way ANOVA, while discrete variables were compared using Fisher's exact test or chi-square test, as appropriate.

The association of pre-implant and post-implant malnutrition risk with outcomes was investigated as both categorical and continuous variables. Pre-implant malnutrition risk was indicated by GNRI before LVAD implantation, and post-implant malnutrition risk was assessed by GNRI at 6 months after LVAD implantation and time-varying GNRI from 6 months to 4 years after LVAD implantation.

For the primary outcome of mortality, Kaplan-Meier analysis, Log-Rank test, Cox regression model, and the adjusted Cox regression model were used to determine differences in survival among groups. Besides, a restricted cubic spline (RCS) Cox proportional hazard regression model was performed to assess the dose-response association between malnutrition risk and mortality. Considering the competing effects among the clinical endpoints of death with LVAD, heart transplant, and explant due to recovery, cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) were utilized to illustrate the specific hazard function, and their differences were tested by the Fine-Gray test. Adjusting competing risk analysis was also performed. The adjusted factors included age, gender, year of therapy, current implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) therapy, INTERMACS profile 1–2, dialysis, blood urea nitrogen, total bilirubin, history of cardiac surgery, concomitant cardiac surgery, device indication, and modified Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). The modified CCI was calculated in reference to the previous study [18]. For variables violating the proportional hazard assumption, extended Cox regressions and competing risk regressions with time-dependent covariates were employed.

For secondary outcomes of adverse events, an adjusted competing risk analysis was performed to assess the association of malnutrition risk with time-to-the-first adverse events, accounting for competing events of death, transplant, and explant due to recovery. Finally, the effects of malnutrition risk on QoL and 6MWT that measured in survivors were investigated. Each patient was re-categorized (three groups: no, mild, and moderate to severe malnutrition risk) at each time point according to the calculated GNRI values. Differences in QoL and 6MWT during follow-up were determined by one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate.

Estimated effects were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in Cox regression and sub-distribution HRs (SHRs) with 95% CIs in competing-risk regression. A two-tailed p-value of <0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using Stata/MP 17.0 (Stata-Corp, Texas 77845 USA), and R 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Characteristics of patients

A total of 5,429 patients with the a mean pre-implant GNRI value of 91.1 who underwent LVAD implantation were included (Figure S1). Patients were divided into three groups according to the pre-implant severity of malnutrition risk indicated by GNRI values before LVAD implantation: moderate to severe (2,708), mild (1,409), and absence of risk (1,312) (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of included patients are presented in Table 1 and Table S1. The mean age was 70.2 ± 4.2 years old with 83.7% of male. There were no differences in the terms of the primary diagnosis for HF. Most of the patients (67.8%) used LVAD as the destination therapy. Compared to the group of patients without the malnutrition risk, patients in the group of moderate to severe risk, received more inotrope therapy, mechanical ventilation, and more assist device implantation by either intra‐aortic balloon pumping (IABP) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) before the LVAD implantation. More patients with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class IV were in the groups with malnutrition risk, and they had more severe INTERMACS profiles.

Trends in malnutrition risk before and after LVAD implantation

During the 2-year follow-up after the LVAD implantation, the proportion of patients without malnutrition risk gradually increased from 24.17% before LVAD implantation to 40.53% at 3 months, 54.96% at 6 months, and 67.56% at 24 months after LVAD implantation. Nevertheless, 37.40% of patients still had a malnutrition risk including mild, and moderate to severe risk, even after one year of LVAD implant and this proportion remained relatively stable until 24 months after the LVAD implantation (Fig. 1).

Pre-implant malnutrition risk and 90-day mortality

A significant difference in 90-day mortality was detected by the log-rank test (P < 0.001, Figure S2a). A competing risk analysis was conducted by calculating the cumulative incidence of death (Figure S2b). Increasing pre-implant malnutrition risk was associated with a significantly high 90-day cumulative incidence of death (P < 0.001). Dose-response analysis via RCS Cox regression showed that the mortality hazard decreased significantly along with reduced malnutrition risk until the GNRI increased to about 102 (Figure S2c). Figure S2d summarizes the adjusted estimates from Cox and competing-risk regressions. After adjusting, patients with pre-implant moderate-to-severe malnutrition risk were associated with a significantly increased risk of 90-day mortality (HR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.26 to 1.91, P < 0.001). When the GNRI values were considered as continuous variables, increasing the malnutrition risk (i.e., decreasing GNRI by 5 points) was associated with an increased risk of death (HR:1.10, 95% CI:1.06–1.15, P < 0.001). This association was also verified by multivariable competing risk regression.

Pre-implant malnutrition risk and long-term survival

The 4-year survival estimate for patients without malnutrition risk was 49.8%, for patients with pre-implant mild risk, it was 44.9%, and for patients with moderate-to-severe risk, it was 40.9%. A significant difference was detected by the log-rank test (P < 0.001, Fig. 2a). A competing risk analysis was conducted by calculating the cumulative incidence of death, transplant, and explant due to recovery for these three groups of patients (Fig. 2b). Increasing pre-implant malnutrition risk was associated with a significantly high 4-year cumulative incidence of death (P < 0.001), and a reduced chance of heart transplant (P < 0.001), while not associated with explant due to recovery (P = 0.190). Dose-response analysis via RCS Cox regression showed that the mortality hazard decreased significantly along with reduced malnutrition risk until the GNRI increased to about 100 (Fig. 2c). Fig. 2d summarizes the adjusted estimates from Cox and competing-risk regressions. After adjusting, patients with pre-implant mild and moderate-to-severe malnutrition risk were associated with a significantly increased risk of death (mild: HR: 1.14; 95% conﬁdence interval [CI]: 1.01 to 1.29, P = 0.033; moderate-to-severe: HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.12 to 1.40, P < 0.001). When the GNRI values were considered as continuous variables, increasing the malnutrition risk (i.e., decreasing 5 points of GNRI) was associated with an increased risk of death (HR:1.08, 95% CI:1.05–1.12, P < 0.001). This association was also verified by multivariable competing risk regression. Consistently, the adjusted competing risk analysis showed that pre-implant malnutrition risk was associated with a significantly reduced chance of heart transplant but not associated with explant due to recovery (Table S4).

Post-implant malnutrition risk and long-term survival

The characteristics and laboratory results of patients who survived 6 months after the LVAD implantation are presented in Tables S2 and S3. Consistent with pre-implant malnutrition risk, patients with a high malnutrition risk at 6 months after the LVAD implantation had significantly low 4-year survival rates (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3a). According to the CIF analysis (Fig. 3b), those patients suffered from a high cumulative incidence of death and low chance of heart transplant. Dose-response analysis via RCS Cox regression showed that the mortality hazard significantly decreased along with reduced malnutrition risk until the GNRI approach to about 104 (Fig. 3c). Fig. 3d shows the estimates from multivariable Cox and competing-risk regressions. The adjusted estimates from Cox regressions revealed that an increased risk of death (mild: HR: 1.39; 95%CI: 1.19 to 1.63, P < 0.001; moderate-to-severe: HR: 2.36; 95% CI: 2.01 to 2.77, P < 0.001) associated with malnutrition risk. For every 5 points drop in GNRI at 6 months after LVAD implantation, in productivity, the risk of death will significantly increased (HR: 1.83 95%CI: 1.57–2.13, P < 0.001). Estimates from multivariable competing-risk analysis also support this association when considering heart transplants and explants due to recovery as competing factors.

Since malnutrition risk varies over time, the severity of malnutrition risk and GNRI values after LVAD implantation were analyzed as time-varying variables to avoid immortal time bias. As shown in Figure S3, considering malnutrition risk and GNRI from 6 months to 4 years after the LVAD implantation as time-varying variables yielded similar results in Kaplan-Meier analysis (Figure S3a), CIFs (Figure S3b), RCS analysis (Figure S3c), and adjusted Cox and competing risk analysis (Figure S3d). Consistently, the adjusted competing risk analysis showed that post-implant malnutrition risk was associated with a significantly reduced chance of heart transplant but not associated with explant due to recovery (Table S4).

At 6 months post-implantation, malnutrition risk was associated with significantly increased risk of infection and bleeding. When considering malnutrition risk as a time-varying variable from post-implant 6 months to 4 years post-implantation, the presence of post-implant malnutrition risk was associated with a significantly increased risks of infection and right heart failure. Discussion

Our study uncovered the following key findings: 1) Among patients aged 65 above who undergoing LVAD implantation, the prevalence of malnutrition risk, as assessed by GNRI, was high at 75.83% before the procedure. However, after LVAD implantation, there was a notable improvement in nutritional status, with the prevalence decreasing to 32.44% at 3 months post-implantation and 59.47% at 24 months post-implantation. 2) Both pre- and post-implantation malnutrition risks were found to be associated with to poor prognosis. Enhancing the nutritional status of these patients may lead to an increase in their survival rate. 3) Patients aged 65 above who are at risk of malnutrition were found to have an increased risk of infection, poor quality of life, and reduced functional exercise capacity during the follow-up period after LVAD implantation.

Prior research has shown that the nutritional status is an important prognostic factor for patients with HF [[19], [20], [21]]. Aggarwal et al. conducted a study using a questionnaire to assess the nutritional status of patients who were being considered for LVADs or heart transplantation [20]. Similar to our findings, their study revealed that patients with malnutrition had a higher likelihood of experiencing postoperative complications [20]. In a retrospective study, Uribarri et al. [22] discovered that one-year survival rates after LVAD implantation decreased significantly among patients with varying degrees of malnutrition risk, paralleling our own results. Notably, our study demonstrated an increase in the proportion of patients without malnutrition risk following LVAD implantation. However, as observed in a previous study [23], a notable number of patients continued to experience mild, moderate, and severe malnutrition during the 24-month follow-up period. Therefore, it is crucial for these patients to receive close monitoring and specialized nutritional therapy to avoid the long-term risk of malnutrition.

The physiological mechanisms behind malnutrition and cardiac cachexia in HF patients are complex [24]. Changes in the immune system and activation of neuroendocrine or inflammatory pathways may play a crucial role in the progression of the disease. The activated inflammatory pathways may hinder the synthesis of albumin, and patients, especially those with heart failure, typically experience an increased metabolic rate but reduced nutrition intake due to anorexia [25]. This effect may be more pronounced in geriatric patients, as aging can also contribute to their nutritional status [26]. Ultimately, malnutrition deficits in HF patients may lead to a series of consequences [27], including prolonged hospital stays [28], increased medical costs [28,29], increased mortality [28], increased rate of postoperative (including LVADs implantation) infection [28,30] due to an impaired immune system [28,[31], [32], [33]], impaired platelet function [34], increased incidence of gastrointestinal bleeding events [[35], [36], [37]], especially among geriatric patients [38].

Similar to survival analysis, long-term patient management that focuses on the QoL of these patients is related to their nutrition status. Significant differences were found in the KCCQ and the 6-MWT after 2 years of LVAD implantation among the three groups. As LVAD is increasingly used as a destination therapy for end-stage HF patients, their long-term QoL after the implantation should be considered. In addition, the incidence of heart transplant and LVAD explantation due to recovery was significantly lower in patients with moderate to severe malnutrition risk pre- and post-LVAD implantation than in those who had no risk. Clinically, it is believed that all patients may need a nutrition consult before consider LVAD implantation and should be followed until their malnutrition status is significantly improved and kept stable after LVAD implantation. Early identification, early intervention to correct malnutrition, dynamic evaluation of nutritional status, and appropriate intervention should be integrated into every step of the therapeutic protocol for patients who are candidates for LVAD and after the LVAD implantation.

As proposed by Buzby et al. [39], the Nutritional Risk Index (NRI), was used to evaluate the relationship between nutritional status and postoperative complications. The NRI requires serum albumin, ideal body weight (IBW), and usual body weight to be calculated. However, since half of geriatric patients are unaware of their usual body weight [38], it is challenging to apply the NRI to these patients [40], it is thus difficult to apply the NRI to these patients [41]. To address this issue, Bouillanne et al. proposed the GNRI, which uses the IBW but not the usual body weight, to specifically assess malnutrition risk in geriatric patients [13]. Previous studies had evaluated the predictive performance of GNRI in patients with HF [42], chronic pulmonary disease [43], and cancer [44]. Unlike other parameters used to assess malnutrition risk [45], such as isolated laboratory measurements, an thropometric measurements [46], and other screening tools like Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS 2002), Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), and Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF), the GNRI calculation is simple and can be easily obtained at the bedside during daily practice. For critically ill patients, those in a coma, or those unable to respond to the NRS 2002 score or MNA-SF, GNRI has clinical advantages [47]. Additionally, the accuracy of the classification of GNRI has been validated in a series of previous studies [[48], [49], [50]].

However, it should be noted that although HF can affect weight and albumin levels independent of nutritional status, relying solely on these indicators to assess the nutritional status of these patients may be misleading. This is because inflammation and oxidative stress caused by HF can lower serum albumin levels [51], and its levels can be influenced by other underlying conditions other than nutritional status [52], such as liver disease and hydration [53]. To accurately assess the nutritional status of HF patients, it's also essential to consider factors such as dietary intake, muscle mass, micronutrient levels, and functional status [54]. Thus, the GNRI focuses on important factors for assessing nutritional risk, but it may not fully capture the complexity of a person's nutritional status.

Our study revealed that the GNRI can effectively evaluate the nutritional risk in geriatric patients both before and after they undergo LVAD implantation. The GNRI serves as a straightforward screening tool for geriatric patients being evaluated for LVAD implantation, which may enhance their recovery rate, quality of life, and overall prognosis throughout the entire process [55]. However, caution should be exercised when using GNRI as a measurement of nutritional risk. Patients aged 65 and above with a high GNRI value should undergo a comprehensive nutritional assessment if applicable, to identify those at risk of malnutrition and ensure they receive appropriate interventions to optimize their nutritional status.

Our current study had some limitations. First, our data were obtained retrospectively from a large observational database, thus some data related to medical treatments during the follow-up period may not have been included in the analysis. With regards to laboratory data, although INTERMACS selected the latest values close to a specific time-point, changes in these biochemical data during a short period may not be reflected in the database, potentially causing some bias. In addition, while we accounted for gender bias in the competing risk analysis, there is a significant imbalance in the gender ratio. The reasons for women being a minority among patients receiving advanced surgical heart failure treatments may be multifactoral [56], and the current study may not be enough to fully understand the complex factors contributing to the gender-based difference. Furthermore, the use of the database also severely restricts the availability of nutritional information. It’s important to note that GNRI is a general risk screening tool and does not account for other factors such as dietary intake, muscle mass, micronutrient levels, and functional status. Therefore, the association of malnutrition risk, as indicated by GNRI, and outcomes should be further investigated in prospective studies, and compared to comprehensive diagnostic assessments if applicable, such as the criteria from the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics/American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (AND/ASPEN), the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN), and the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM). Thirdly, the data set did not include specific information on nutritional interventions implemented to enhance the nutritional status of patients during the study period. It is thus still unclear whether the correction of malnutrition status by specific intervention may improve the prognosis of such patients. Our findings indicate that malnutrition risk is associated with poor prognosis in advanced heart failure patients aged over 65, highlighting the importance of addressing nutritional needs in this population. Further studies to evaluate the effect of nutrition treatment on these patients may be needed.

Conclusion

In summary, malnutrition risk identified by GNRI is frequent and associated with increased mortality risk, higher infection, poor QoL, and reduced functional exercise capacity in patients aged 65 and above undergoing LVAD implantation. Malnutrition risk can be used in the risk classification for these patients, and the clinical benefit of timely nutritional intervention in LVAD management needs further investigation.
