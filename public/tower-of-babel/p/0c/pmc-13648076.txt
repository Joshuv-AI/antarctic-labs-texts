Stiff-Person Syndrome: A Comprehensive Review of a Rare Disease with Emphasis on Pathophysiology, Clinical Features, Diagnostic Challenges and Therapeutic Advances
Authors: Mudasir Manzoor Dar, Ansar Ahmad Paray, Shaheena, Irshad Ahmad Najar, Wajid Gulzar, Mir Shabnum Javid, Tawfeeq Ahmad, Suraya Sikandar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stiff-person syndrome (SPS) is an rare autoimmune neurological disorder that is associated with progressive axial and proximal muscle rigidity, stimulus-sensitive painful spasms, and significant functional disability. The disorder is commonly associated with high-titer antibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase-65 (GAD65), leading to impaired gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission, resulting in persistent motor neuron hyperexcitability. Despite the conventional classification of SPS as a motor disorder, the condition commonly occurs with neuropsychiatric symptoms, such as anxiety, phobic behaviors, and depression that often occur before the motor symptoms and can significantly increase diagnostic error or misdiagnosis. This review summarizes existing knowledge of the epidemiology, immunopathophysiology, clinical spectrum, diagnostic dilemmas, and treatment developments in SPS. This review also discusses the expanded concept of the stiff-person spectrum disorders that include classic SPS, stiff-limb syndrome, SPS-plus forms, and progressive encephalomyelitis with rigidity and myoclonus. Complexity in the diagnosis is associated with clinical heterogeneity, the variability of assays in anti-GAD65 testing, seronegative manifestations, and overlap of functional and psychiatric disorders, which highlights the role of electrophysiological validation and intrathecal immune scale. Management includes symptomatic treatment aimed at increasing GABAergic inhibition combined with disease-modifying immunotherapies, specifically intravenous immunoglobulin and B-cell–directed therapies in cases of refractory treatment. Despite progress in mechanistic comprehension and treatment, SPS has not been adequately diagnosed and is not substantiated by sufficient data on high-quality clinical trials. There is a need for early identification, proper diagnostic stewardship, and multidisciplinary care to enhance functional outcomes and quality of life. Future studies should address standard diagnostic criteria, phenotyping based on biomarkers, and specific immunotherapeutics.

INTRODUCTION

Stiff-person syndrome (SPS) is a rare autoimmune neurological disorder associated mainly with antibodies to glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD) or glycine, characterized by intermittent painful spasms, stiffness, and rigidity of the proximal and truncal muscles. SPS occurs in adults between the ages of 30 and 60 years. Its prevalence is estimated at approximately one to two cases per million individuals. The initial clinical findings of SPS are characterized by physical effects, but current research points at its multifaceted pathophysiology and systemic characteristics.1 The main characteristic of SPS is the interference of inhibitory neurotransmission caused by autoantibodies of high titer toward glutamic acid decarboxylase-65 (anti-GAD65), the protein that synthesizes gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), the major inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system (CNS), leading to the clinical manifestation of rigidity and spasms.2 Although motor symptoms (rigidity, spasms, abnormal posture) are the most recognized clinical manifestations, psychiatric symptoms (anxiety, depression, phobic disorders) are also more widely reported in the existing literature and can be the initial sign of motor aberrations.3

SPS is quite frequently misdiagnosed, as patients are often diagnosed with functional neurological disorder, conversion disorder, or primary psychiatric illness before an accurate distinction is made. Several case reports describe prolonged misdiagnosis as anxiety, panic symptoms, or other psychiatric disorders, which validates the necessity of clinician awareness of SPS in patients with unexplained stiffness and psychiatric symptoms.4 Along with anti-GAD65 antibodies, other autoantibodies like anti-amphiphysin have been detected, especially in paraneoplastic and few seronegative patients with clinical features suggestive of SPS, making the diagnosis more difficult. Electromyography (EMG) is also one of the supportive investigations, as it shows the constant activity of motor units of agonists and antagonists.5

Other autoimmune diseases such as type 1 diabetes mellitus, autoimmune thyroid disease, and pernicious anemia are common with SPS. The high prevalence of systemic autoimmunity highlights the need to have a thorough immunologic assessment of suspected cases.6 Even though knowledge has been gained regarding its immunopathogenesis, SPS remains difficult to diagnose and cure. GABAergic (benzodiazepines and baclofen) medications are usually used in symptomatic management, whereas in more severe or refractory cases, immunotherapies (intravenous immunoglobulin [IVIG], plasma exchange, rituximab) are utilized, although the responses are inconsistent, and the supporting evidence is at a high level.7–9 This review studies the latest developments in SPS pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic advances.

METHODOLOGY

This review was conducted to summarize current evidence on the pathophysiology, clinical features, diagnostic challenges, and therapeutic advances in SPS. A structured literature search was performed using the electronic databases PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles. The following keywords and Boolean operators were used: “Stiff-Person Syndrome,” “Stiff Person Spectrum Disorder,” “SPS,” “anti-GAD antibodies,” “glycine receptor antibodies,” “autoimmune neurological disorders,” “diagnosis,” “pathophysiology,” and “treatment.”

Original research studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or relevant case series published in English focused on human subjects that reported clinical, epidemiological, diagnostic, or therapeutic aspects of SPS were included. Duplicate publications, conference abstracts without full text, articles focused primarily on unrelated neurological conditions, and articles with insufficient clinical or methodological detail were excluded. Priority was given to peer-reviewed studies, recent publications, and studies with clear diagnostic criteria or well-defined patient populations. The aim of this approach was to ensure comprehensive coverage of the available literature while maintaining relevance and scientific rigor.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

SPS is a rare autoimmune neurological disorder with both low incidence and low prevalence, which complicates epidemiological research on it at a population level. The literature review reports that SPS has an estimated prevalence of one to two cases per million individuals globally indicating that it is uncommon and underdiagnosed because it clinically overlaps with musculoskeletal and psychiatric disorders.10 The prevalence of the broader stiff-person spectrum disorder (SPSD) was estimated at about 2.11 per 100,000 persons (or about 1 in 47,486) in a Colorado-based cohort study of 2012-2022. The central tendency of this cohort was approximately 0.35 per 100,000 person-years (0.35 million per year), which implied that the incidence could be even greater than its conventional counterparts.3,11 In a case series among antibody-positive patients, the US Veterans Affairs health system had a point prevalence of 2.06 per million.12,13

In terms of demography, SPS usually occurs during middle adulthood, with an age range being the third to fifth decades of life, but the disease has an extensive demographic spread among young adults to the older adult population. Women are affected more, with female-to-male ratios of 2:1 to 3:1.10,14 Pediatric manifestations of SPS are extremely uncommon; systematic reviews suggest that of all reported cases of SPS, a small minority (typically 5%) is in children, and most pediatric cases are only diagnosed in adulthood because of latent diagnosis or uncharacteristic clinical manifestations.5,15 Furthermore, SPS is commonly linked to other autoimmune diseases, in particular, type 1 diabetes mellitus, autoimmune thyroid disease, and pernicious anemia, with characteristics that could add complexity to epidemiology and overlap other autoimmune syndromes. It was reported that the prevalence rate of anti-GAD65-positive SPS may reach up to 60% to 80%, and many of these patients (around 50%) develop insulin-dependent diabetes, influencing epidemiological trends among clinical groups.13,16,17 Although the estimated prevalence of SPS is approximately one to two cases per million individuals, reported estimates vary across different studies and geographic regions. This variability likely reflects several methodological and clinical factors, including differences in diagnostic criteria, referral bias in tertiary-care cohorts, and variations in case ascertainment methods. Moreover, the rarity of the disorder, overlap with other neurological and functional conditions, and frequent misdiagnosis may contribute to significant under-recognition of SPS in routine clinical practice. Consequently, the true global burden of the disease may be underestimated, highlighting the need for improved diagnostic awareness and standardized epidemiological reporting.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

SPS is a rare autoimmune neurological disorder affecting the CNS; it is caused by the inability of the inhibitory neurotransmission to occur, resulting in sustained motor neuron hyperexcitability, muscle stiffness, and involuntary muscle spasms. On the molecular level, most of the neurological dysfunctions seen in SPS are the interactions between autoantibodies, inhibitory neurons, and GABA synthesis pathways.18

Autoimmune Autoantibodies and Neuronal Targets

The best-known immunologic characteristic of SPS is the availability of high-titer anti-GAD65, an enzyme that plays a key role in the production of the primary brain neurotransmitters, GABA. Anti-GAD65 antibodies attach to various epitopes of the GAD65 enzyme and disrupt the enzyme activity and GABA synthesis, thus lowering inhibitory control of motor neurons, at least one factor in the continued muscle rigidity and spasms.19 Additional evidence of the pathogenic role played by autoantibodies is found in experimental models that show that passive transfer of IgG in SPS and positive anti-GAD65 titers can cause SPS-like motor dysfunction in rodents, especially when it comes to central inhibitory circuits, which control motor output.20 Anti-GAD65 is predominant (in about 70% to 80% of classic SPS cases), but other antisynaptic protein antibodies, such as glycine receptors, GABA(A) receptor-associated proteins, dipeptidyl-peptidase-like protein 6, gephyrin, and synaptic components of GABA release and receptor recycling, in seronegative and variant cases of SPS are also found. These alternative antibodies expand the immunopathological spectrum of GAD65 and can be used to justify the clinical presentation and severity heterogeneity.18

Impaired GABAergic Inhibition and Motor Hyperexcitability

The main component of SPS pathogenesis is the loss of inhibitory neuronal control that occurs as a result of decreased GABA levels. GABA usually suppresses excitatory impulses within the cortex, brainstem, and spinal motor pathways, which inhibits overstimulation of motor neurons. The mechanism underlying SPS is auto-antibody-mediated interference with GAD65 and associated proteins that results in GABA deficiency, causing disinhibition of motor neurons, and resulting in long-term muscle contraction, rigidity, and painful spasms that characterize SPS. This pathological hyperexcitability is usually documented in electromyographic research under the form of continuous motor unit activity in the antagonistic muscle groups.5 This inhibitory signaling deficiency is not confined to the spinal circuitry; it occurs across multiple neuraxis levels, such as the brainstem and cortex, to which motor control and sensory modulation are attributed. Such mass loss of inhibition also helps in exaggerated startle reflex and sensory hyperirritability that have been reported in patients with SPS.18

Clonal B-Cell Activation and Intrathecal Immunity

The presence of intrathecal antibody production as measured specifically in serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies of anti-GAD65 antibodies demonstrates local activation of B cells in the CNS and not passive release in the bloodstream. A high level of CSF anti-GAD65 is associated with low GABA levels and severity, which indicate that local production of antibodies closely interferes with inhibitory synapses deep within motor pathways.8

Role of Paraneoplastic Variants

A minor proportion (approximately 5% to 10%) of SPS occurrences are paraneoplastic linked with malignancies in the form of breast, lung, and thymus malignancies. The antibodies frequently attack internal cellular synaptic proteins like amphiphysin and gephyrin that regulate endocytosis of vesicles and expression of receptors at inhibitory synapses. Paraneoplastic antibodies are able to decrease the level of inhibitory receptors and interfere with the recycling of synaptic vesicles, which adds to neuronal hyperexcitability.21

Genetic and Immunologic Susceptibility

There is also emerging evidence that predisposition to both idiopathic and paraneoplastic forms of SPS could be influenced by genetic predisposition through certain HLA-class II alleles (eg, DQB1, DRB1). Such types of HLA are probably involved in antigen presentation and immune tolerance, which involve antigenic attacks of inhibitory neuronal antigens by autoimmune reactions. Genome-environment interactions and possible molecular mimicry of viral epitopes are currently under investigation to reveal the triggers of SPS autoimmunity.5

Contribution of T-Cell Cytotoxicity

In addition to autoantibodies, other studies indicate that T-cell–mediated cytotoxicity is a possible immunopathogenic process. T cells responding to neuronal antigens can invade CNS tissues and secrete proinflammatory cytokines to exacerbate the inhibitory disproportionality that forms due to antibody-mediated GABA disruption. This complex immune attack distinguishes SPS and simple antibody disorders.18

Integrative View of Pathophysiology

In general, SPS pathophysiology is a complicated immune-mediated destruction of inhibitory synaptic integrity with autoantibodies lowering GABAergic tone, B cells producing intrathecally, T cells assisting in inflammatory injury, and further autoantigen targets varying clinical phenotypes. These pathological mechanisms cause the central hyperexcitability that characterizes SPS and its spectrum disorders, with its manifestations running the gamut of muscle rigidity, hypersensitivity, and recurrent misdiagnosis following psychiatric manifestation overlap.18

CLINICAL FEATURES OF STIFF-PERSON SYNDROME

SPS presents a distinctive clinical phenotype characterized by progressive neuromuscular dysfunction affecting axial and proximal limb muscles, often accompanied by exaggerated spasm and neuropsychiatric features. Recognizing these features is crucial for early diagnosis and differentiating SPS from other neurological and psychiatric conditions.

Progressive Muscle Rigidity and Stiffness

The most common cardinal clinical hallmark of SPS is progressively, symmetrically, and predominantly in the trunk (paraspinal and abdominal muscles) rigidity of muscles, and subsequently to the proximal limb muscles. This stiffness is caused by long-term coactivation of agonist and antagonist groups of muscle that causes a boardlike stiffness that slowly causes impairment of functional mobility. Continuous motor unit activity with respect to the clinical rigidity is usually observed in electromyographic results.22

Painful Muscle Spasms

Superimposed on the muscle rigidity of SPS are intermittent painful spasms that exist at spontaneous or unexpected tactile stimulation, a loud sound, emotional stress, or voluntary movement. These spasms may be severe, recurrent, and incapacitating; may continue several minutes to hours; and may result in falls or injuries like dislocations of joints and fractures.23

Exaggerated Startle Response

Some patients may be very sensitive to sensory stimuli such as noise, touch, and other visual triggers, and they give abrupt and exaggerated motor responses. This hypersensitivity is highly related to emotional or environmental stressors and may cause debilitating spasms or falls.17

Abnormal Posture and Gait

With stiffness, patients acquire an abnormal posture and in most cases appear with lumbar hyperlordosis (inward curving of the lower back) since they are in a continuous contraction of the axial muscles. Gait errors, including slow, wide-based, statuelike gait, are frequent and cause frequent falls and functional impairment in advanced disease.24

Psychosomatic and Neuropsychiatric Symptoms

Neuropsychiatric characteristics are considered as emerging within the clinical spectrum of SPS, and anxiety, depression, and phobialike avoidance behavior are common. These psychological symptoms could be attributed to the constant disability of SPS and exaggerated fear of spasms or falls. These symptoms often cause misdiagnosis as a primary psychiatric disorder and delay neurological recognition.25

Variations in clinical phenotypes within the SPS spectrum may also be due to different autoantibody patterns. Although anti-GAD65 antibodies most typically describe the classic form of SPS, which progresses through axial rigidity and muscle spasms, antibodies against the glycine receptor are more often related to the more severe form of SPS called PERM. The disease associated with patients with glycine-receptor antibody tends to manifest a wider neurological spectrum with brainstem dysfunction, exaggerated startle reflexes, and autonomic lability. These differences imply that differences in clinical severity, disease progression, and response to immunotherapy within the SPS spectrum may be partly due to differences in underlying immune targets.17

Electromyographic Correlates

A supportive diagnostic feature is the presence of ongoing involuntary motor unit activity in agonist and antagonist muscle groups even during rest, which is seen with EMG. This activity is suppressed by benzodiazepines, and this is consistent with the effects of treatment.23

Autonomic and Additional Features

Autonomic symptoms include diaphoresis, tachycardia, or hypertension, although they are less frequent and happen during severe exacerbations of spasmodic activity. In severe or advanced disease, respiratory muscle involvement may be seen in rare cases.26

Impact on Daily Function

The effects of stiffness, spasms, and gait abnormalities may severely restrict activities of daily living over time, resulting in dependence on assistive devices, falls, and serious disability. Orthopedic sequelae can also be progressive impairment, which leads to joint contractions.24

Age and Demographic Patterns

Though SPS can develop at any age, onset is normally during middle adulthood, which is in accord with its association with autoimmune diseases. Many series have been found to have female predominance, but clinical features are similar between the sexes.22

DIAGNOSTIC CHALLENGES

Absence of universality of the diagnostic criteria is one of the main barriers to early diagnosis of SPSDs, and this has contributed to the heterogeneity of the definition of cases across centers and studies. This heterogeneity can be directly related to under- and overdiagnosis and makes design of the trials and extrapolation of observational data more complicated.15

Assay Variability and Antibody Testing Limitation

Although central to present-day diagnostic practice, serological testing of anti-GAD65 antibodies has significant limitations: assay variability between ELISA, radioimmunoassay, cell-based assays, and immunohistochemistry cause inconsistency in sensitivity and specificity; low-titer positivity is common in diabetes and a number of neuropsychiatric diseases, and therefore, false positivity (or clinically noninformative positivity) is likely to confuse clinicians unless used within an appropriate clinical context.27 Another related limitation is overreliance on one positive serum antibody test with no CSF testing or electrophysiological verification. High GAD65 antibody concentrations in CSF titers or evidence of intrathecal production carries considerable diagnostic weight, and isolated low serum titers, especially when determined by one nonconfirmatory method, can be nonspecific. This has contributed to the incorrect labeling of patients as autoimmune as well as false negativity on serology.14

Seronegative Stiff-Person Syndrome

Seronegative SPSD is challenging to diagnose. Even a significant proportion of clinically normal cases do not express GAD65, amphiphysin, or glycine-receptor antibodies on routine assays. EMG showing continuous motor unit activity (CMUA) and typical paravertebral physiology are indispensable in such patients, and the inability to apply or appropriately interpret EMG may thus keep many seronegative patients undiagnosed.15

Electrophysiological Pitfalls

Electrophysiological testing comes with technical and inferential traps. EMG confirmation requires proper muscle choice, correct recording signals, and examiner skill; CMUA can be either weak or irregular, and false results of the presence of axial dystonias and other movement disorders that phenomenologically resemble SPS can be interpreted as such. Therefore, false-negative and false-positive interpretations of EMG data contribute to errors in diagnosis.28

Misdiagnosis and Phenotypic Variability

The pathophysiological overlap between functional neurological disorders and primary psychiatric disorders is significant. Anxiety, phobic avoidance, and paniclike reactions are common in SPS and may frequently precede motor difficulties; various functional disorders also present with stiffness and gait imbalance. Large referral-cohort studies indicate that misdiagnosis (functional/nonneurological) occurs more frequently than confirmed SPSD, highlighting the necessity of multidisciplinary assessment.29

The add-on paraneoplastic and variant phenotypes (eg, amphiphysin-positive, glycine-receptor or dipeptidyl-peptidase-like protein 6 antibody phenotypes, and PERM) are even more complicated because antibody specificities vary in sensitivity and oncological association, and paraneoplastic phenotypes may also have unusual manifestations. The inability to pursue the underlying neoplasm at the time of adequate clinical situations may postpone vital oncologic diagnosis and transformation in the management of the disease.16 Imaging (magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]) and regular CSF sampling are frequently either not specific or normal in SPSDs, and therefore, the absence of inflammatory imaging alterations may incorrectly reassure clinicians and delay immune etiology. MRI/CSF to rule out SPSDs, which have no specific autoimmune testing, and EMG can therefore lead to a false sense of security.17

Challenges in Diagnostic Standardization

Lack of laboratory-to-laboratory consistency: There is no uniform cut-off level of high-titer anti-GAD65 antibodies among the other assays and diagnostic centers, and no universally accepted system exists to convert the results of one assay unit to the other. Recent reports indicate useful titer levels of neurological significance, especially extremely high antibody titers. Nevertheless, an international standard of the validity of diagnosis is not yet established by the broader clinical community; thus, it is difficult to have a consistent and reliable diagnosis among institutions.30

In response to these issues, recent expert communities suggested the following useful diagnostic models: core clinical features (trunk/lower-extremity stiffness, hypersensitivity triggers, hyperlordosis), paraclinical validation (high-titer gad65 or disease-specific antibodies and/or characteristic EMG), and elimination of mimics. The use of such combined criteria has had the effect of minimizing false positives and false negatives in contrast to false positives and false negatives when either serology or symptoms are used alone.17 Moreover, there are ethical and treatment implications of misdiagnosis: misdiagnosed patients and patients with SPSD are both being harmed and deprived of cost-effective care and potential disease-modifying therapies (eg, IVIG) and supportive care. Thus, the use of diagnostic stewardship, which is the combination of assay confirmation, electrophysiology, CSF screening where necessary, and multidisciplinary review, is not only clinically but also ethically obligatory.29 For clarity and improved clinical applicability, the key components of the proposed diagnostic framework are summarized in Table 1.

DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

The diagnosis of SPS relies on a combination of clinical features, serological testing, electrophysiological studies, and supportive neuroimaging or CSF findings.

Serological Testing

In classic cases of SPS, anti-GAD65 antibodies are the most frequently found immunologic phenomenon in the diagnostic assessment of the disease, and serological testing remains one of the main elements of the diagnostic process. Anti-GAD65 high-titer antibodies are closely related to SPS and aid in the differentiation between it and neuromuscular or neuropsychiatric mimics. In a 2023 study comparing ELISA and cell-based assay methods, the researchers discovered that those with higher titer thresholds are more specific to the clinical disease than those at lower titer that may be observed in diabetes mellitus type 1 alone; therefore, serology interpretation is necessary.30 Seronegative cases pose a major challenge, although they have diagnostic potential. Even with a distinctive clinical presentation, blood tests can be negative (no antibodies against GAD65 or other proteins of interest, eg, amphiphysin, glycine receptor). These seronegative tests for SPS usually require the use of electrophysiological data and clinical indications in diagnosis. Studies clarify that negative serology does not rule out SPS in case there are convincing clinical and electrophysiological results.14 Other autoantibodies such as anti-amphiphysin and antiglycine receptor antibodies are used to characterize the phenotype of variant SPS or the paraneoplastic forms, especially when the clinical presentation is atypical. The testing of multiple autoantibody classes enhances the sensitivity of diagnosis, particularly in seronegative patients.32

Electrophysiological Studies

EMG is critical in the affirmation of SPS by showing that there is involuntary CMUA in both antagonist and agonist muscles even at rest. It is a mode of central disinhibition of sustained motor neuron activity, one of the most reproducible objective biomarkers in SPS upon interpretation by seasoned neurophysiologists.17 The appearance of CMUA is one of the reasons why SPS is not like a lot of movement disorders and functional neurological conditions, as normal motor unit stability is observed at rest. Nevertheless, technical proficiency and appropriate muscle choice are needed to prevent false-negative outcomes, especially when spasms are intermittent or the test subjects have benzodiazepine or baclofen at the moment of testing.23 Other neurophysiological methods like jump response tests and paired-pulse inhibition protocols can also be used to supplement central disinhibition in the case of equivocality, but they are not as well-validated as conventional EMG.33

Neuroimaging and Cerebrospinal Fluid Studies

MRI of both the brain and the spinal cord is usually normal in SPS, although it helps rule out structural lesions, demyelinating conditions, or other causes of stiffness and spasticity. More complex methods like functional MRI or diffusion tensor imaging are also being researched but are not yet standard diagnostics.3 The CSF examination generally presents with nonspecific results in SPS, mild lymphocytic pleocytosis or increased protein might be found, but results of intrathecal antibody production (eg, anti-GAD65 in CSF) are more specific and have the potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy. Paired serum and CSF antibody testing enhances specificity because it shows the presence of intrathecal immune response and not peripheral spillover.8

Clinical Scales and Differential Assessment Tools

Structured clinical tests such as gait tests, measures of the severity of stiffness, and validated functional scales are what bring objectivity to clinical examination and may be used to monitor progress or response to treatment. Although there is no universal scale used to measure SPS, some scales have been applied in cohort studies by gauging disability and functional impairment, such as the Modified Rankin Scale and Stiffness Severity Score.34 Use of a combination of assessments based on motor, sensory, and emotional domains is a better way of differentiating functional disorders, particularly in cases where the clinical manifestations are difficult or misunderstood by laypeople. Multidisciplinary measurements involving neurology, psychiatry, and neurophysiology have demonstrated better diagnostic quality in complicated cases.28

MANAGEMENT AND THERAPEUTIC ADVANCES

There are various approaches for the management of SPS, such as symptomatic pharmacological therapy (Table 2), immunomodulatory and immunosuppressive therapies, and plasmapheresis and emerging immunotherapies.

Symptomatic Pharmacological Therapy

The first pillar in the treatment of SPS is symptomatic management with a primary focus to decrease muscle rigidity and painful spasms. Benzodiazepines, diazepam and clonazepam being the most commonly used, will continue to be first-line agents as they enhance the GABA-A receptor-mediated neurotransmission of inhibition. Clinical studies demonstrate a significant reduction in stiffness severity and frequency of spasms, but in the long term, this treatment is frequently limited because of sedation, tolerance, and cognitive side effects.35 Oral administration of baclofen as an adjunctive therapy to benzodiazepines has been found to be beneficial in additional cases, especially when the patient does not respond fully to benzodiazepines. Baclofen suppresses the hyperexcitable nature of reactions at the spinal level and could enable the reduction of benzodiazepine dosage, thus reducing side effects. However, abrupt withdrawal should be avoided because there is a possibility of rebound spasticity and autonomic instability.36

Immunomodulatory and Immunosuppressive Therapies

Immunotherapy is a disease-modifying method as opposed to symptomatic control in the face of an autoimmune pathogenesis of SPS. The most evidence-based immunotherapy is IVIG, because based on randomized controlled trials, it shows significant improvement in degree of stiffness, functional mobility, and quality of life. The positive effects are believed to result from antibody neutralization and immune modulation; IVIG is the immunotherapy currently available with the best clinical evidence to demonstrate its effectiveness in SPS. Randomized controlled trials have proven a lot of improvement in severity of stiffness, functional mobility, and quality of life of the affected patients. These results underpin the use of IVIG as an important disease-modifying intervention in patients with moderate to severe SPS.37 Rituximab, a monoclonal anti-CD 20 antibody, has become the second-line choice of patients with refractory disease or incomplete response to IVIG. The observational cohorts of recent studies indicate some clinical stabilization and lesser severity of spasm in select patients, especially those with high antibody titers or high disability. But mixed findings are provided by controlled trials, making it necessary to select patients individually. However, the evidence supporting rituximab therapy in SPS remains limited compared with IVIG and is largely derived from small observational studies and case series. Although some patients with refractory disease demonstrate clinical improvement following B-cell depletion therapy, larger controlled studies are still required to establish its definitive therapeutic role.38

Plasmapheresis and Emerging Immunotherapies

In severely or rapidly progressive SPS, especially paraneoplastic or PERM forms, plasma exchange has been applied. Plasma exchange may result in short-term symptomatic improvement by simply eliminating the circulating pathogenic antibodies, although long-term benefit may be necessary, which is often achieved by combining it with immunosuppressive mediators. The evidence is still in the form of small observational studies and case series.39

New treatment methods are being explored, such as Fc-receptor blockers, complement inhibitors, and antigen-specific immunologic tolerance. Novel therapies intended to lessen systemic immunosuppression and more specifically target the mechanisms involved in pathogenesis are also a promising in future management of SPS.37

Supportive Care and Multidisciplinary Management

In SPS, supportive care is built not only on the basis of pharmacological and immunologic therapies, but physical therapy is also key to maintaining mobility and minimizing disability. Patients are prone to falls, contractures, and secondary musculoskeletal complications that are caused by chronic axial rigidity, painful spasms, and abnormal gait. Individualized rehabilitation programs including stretching, postural correction, balance training, and gradual strengthening have been found to have positive functional outcomes without worsening spasms when paced properly.24 Notably, unsupervised or aggressive treatment can increase the symptoms as startle reflex and stimulus-induced spasms increase. Professional opinion is that therapists who are knowledgeable of SPS pathophysiology should carry out rehabilitation, preferably at the same time as medication is given (eg, after benzodiazepine administration) to reduce the provocation of spasms.26

Multidisciplinary Care Coordination

SPS can only be managed optimally through multidisciplinary care that normally includes neurologists, neuroimmunologists, physiotherapists, psychiatrists, occupational therapists, and in paraneoplastic cases, oncologists. These types of integrated care models will minimize diagnostic delays, maximize treatment sequencing, and provide uniform monitoring of the treatment response and adverse effects.14 Recent reviews based on consensus suggest organized follow-up procedures such as functional evaluation, spasm severity indices, and psychosocial analysis to inform long-term care. In cases that require it, multidisciplinary clinics were found to have better patient satisfaction and a faster escalation to immunotherapy.26

PROGNOSIS AND LONG-TERM OUTCOMES IN STIFF-PERSON SYNDROME

Natural History and Functional Prognosis

SPS is a condition that is usually progressive and chronic in nature, though there are substantial differences in progression and end outcome of the disorder among patients. Historical cohort studies and systematic reviews have always shown that SPS hardly cures itself. Several patients have gradually increasing muscle stiffness, worsening of painful spasms, and progressive impairment of walking over time, especially when immunotherapy and diagnosis are not received at an early stage.24

Longitudinal analyses show that there are relationships between the intensity of symptoms at the onset and the presence of comorbid autoimmune conditions (eg, type 1 diabetes, thyroid disease) under the prism of poor mobility outcomes and higher disability. Patients who have a more pronounced axial rigidity at baseline should start to use assistive devices earlier because falls and secondary musculoskeletal complications are more prevalent in them.14 Although chronicity is present, stabilization or improvement of symptoms is possible in timely and adequate treatment, including IVIG, which indicates that the outcomes may not be as dismal provided that an early and prolonged intervention is applied. Functional scores (eg, gait, activities of daily living) have been serially assessed, showing improvement in treated groups vs untreated controls.40,41

Impact of Treatment on Long-Term Outcomes

Most patients have a changed condition of SPS due to the introduction of disease-modifying therapies, especially regular IVIG, which have greatly changed the natural history of the condition. There is clinical evidence, which is controlled, that treatment is linked to less rigidity, fewer spasms, and improvement of gait, so the benefits increase over months of maintenance therapy. Longitudinal registries point to the fact that patients who are placed on a schedule of IVIG have a better functional status in the long run than patients who are treated solely with the use of symptomatic therapy.15 Immunotherapies that target the basic autoimmune pathways, including rituximab, have demonstrated efficacy in refractory cases and can be of particular use in select patients to extend periods of stability or improvement. Although there is limited randomized evidence, observational cohorts prove that rituximab may help to decrease the frequency of the relapse and decrease the titers of antibodies, which, consequently, are associated with improvement in the clinical setting.42 Notably, immunotherapy that is initiated earlier, preferably within months of symptom onset, has been linked to improved long-term mobility and reduced spasms and complication hospitalizations, which prominently contributes to the fact that the timing of the treatment matters in the outcomes of SPS.43

Quality of Life, Disability, and Survival

Even though SPS is not regarded as a life-limiting diagnosis, quality of life might be affected because of pain, anxiety, gait dysfunction, social disability, and the provision of long-term care services. Measures of standardized quality of life show that physical functioning, mobility, and emotional well-being scores are significantly lower than controls in health and most other chronic neurological illnesses.43 The falls and contractures burden adds to secondary complications such as fractures and chronic pain syndromes that further worsen daily functioning and affect independence. Some of these problems are alleviated by occupational and physical therapy interventions, yet long-term disability is still frequent, particularly in patients with late diagnosis or with individuals who lack access to immunotherapy.24 The survival of SPS is good as compared to other neuroimmunologic diseases, with the majority of patients surviving several years after diagnosis. Nonetheless, comorbid autoimmune diseases (eg, diabetes) and fall-related complications and chronic pain treatment might negatively impact morbidity and demand holistic long-term care plans.14

LIMITATIONS OF CURRENT EVIDENCE

Limited High-Quality Clinical Trials

Although SPS has gained increasing awareness, available evidence has been limited by the lack of randomized controlled trials. The majority of therapeutic recommendations are based on small observational cohort studies, retrospective cohort case studies, or opinion. Patient recruitment in SPS is also rare, which is a great setback to the study of SPS, and the outcome measures are heterogeneous. Consequently, no comparison has been made in their efficacy with a comparative study of immunotherapies (IVIG, rituximab, and plasmapheresis).26 Moreover, the trials that are currently available tend to have no standardized end points to measure stiffness severity, spasm frequency, or functional improvement and thus cannot be compared across studies and meta-analytically synthesized. This changeability of methods undermines the power of evidence-based long-term treatment plans.40

Diagnostic Heterogeneity and Misclassification

Although it is not the primary emphasis of most therapeutic studies, constraints in standard diagnostic criteria and clinical heterogeneity of clinical phenotypes in the SPS spectrum disorders present difficulties in forming a consistent set of study populations to conduct trials. This can also lead to a lack of consistent results and weak subgroup analyses.44 Furthermore, excessive dependence on anti-GAD65 antibody positivity can lead to the neglect of other immune responses, whereas underidentifying psychiatric-dominant manifestations are a contributing factor to subsequent diagnosis. These make it difficult to generalize available epidemiological and therapeutic data.14

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Personalized Clinical Phenotyping and Prognostic Biomarkers

In developing studies, it is suggested that clear subgroups of phenotypic variants of SPS, including classic SPS, SPS-plus, and PERM, might have varied clinical courses and disability outcomes. Cohort studies are starting to define clinical variables that can predict long-term disability, including age at onset and involvement of the brainstem and cerebellum. In the future, these variables can be customized for prognostic models and personalized treatment plans. There is also a connection between early immunotherapy with better results, and future studies should aim to streamline the requirements of early intervention.32

Expanding Diagnostic and Mechanistic Insights

According to detailed reviews, further immunopathogenesis insight, that is more than that of anti-GAD65 itself (ie, as glycine receptor, and other antisynaptic antigen antibodies), is required to improve diagnostic algorithms and particularly to refine seronegative cases. Recognition of mechanistically different subtypes can inform accurate immunotherapy and minimize falsification.44

Innovative Therapeutic Approaches

Case reports and research trends depict consideration of new agents of the acute stage, like ketamine to control spasms that are refractory, particularly when the illness is too severe or resistant to treatment. Although there are currently only a few reports of such explorations, such explorations make plain the possibility of controlled trials of alternative neural modulators in the future in the context of SPS.45

Machine Learning and Real-World Data Integration

Evidence of principal experiments with machine learning of electronic health records indicates that it is possible to use sophisticated computational methods to describe SPS phenotypes and outcomes. This would speed up the identification of predictive markers and help to build large registries.46

CONCLUSION

SPS is a rare autoimmune brain disorder that causes severe muscle stiffness, painful spasms triggered by sounds or touch, and major limits on daily life. It was often missed in the past due to low awareness, but better clinical training and immune tests have improved diagnosis and our understanding of its many forms. The main problem is the immune system attacking GABA, the brain’s key “brake” on muscle signals, which explains both body stiffness and mental health issues. Growing evidence from existing literature shows that early treatment to protect this GABA system greatly improves long-term results. Drugs like benzodiazepines and baclofen help ease symptoms right away, while immune therapies such as IV immunoglobulin offer lasting relief from stiffness, spasms, and disability. New research suggests personalized options, like B-cell targeted drugs, work best based on a patient’s symptoms and immune profile, highlighting the value of tailored care. Even with these advances, challenges remain: SPS is so rare, its symptoms vary widely, and we lack big clinical trials for solid proof. In short, spotting SPS early with high suspicion, starting immunotherapy quickly, and using a team of specialists are crucial. Future studies blending clinical examinations, brain tests, and immune insights will boost diagnosis, treatments, and quality of life for those affected.
