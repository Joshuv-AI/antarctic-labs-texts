The politicization of ESG investing shows why ESG metrics cannot be depoliticized
Authors: Sibo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The growing political tension surrounding environmental, social, and governance (ESG) investing is not simply a dispute over terminology. It reflects a deeper conflict over whether climate and social crises can be governed through financial metrics without confronting the political-economic structures that produce them. ESG was originally framed as the integration of financially material environmental, social, and governance issues into investment decisions; its tools may influence corporate behaviour through stewardship, disclosure pressure, risk pricing, or capital allocation, but such effects are contingent rather than intrinsic to ESG itself. It should therefore not be treated as equivalent to impact investing, where intentional and measurable social or environmental impact is part of the investment mandate. Yet in public discourse ESG is often marketed as if it could simultaneously protect returns, decarbonize the economy, and demonstrate corporate virtue. This slippage has made ESG vulnerable to attack from both the right and the left. On the right, anti-ESG campaigns fold climate politics into broader anti-“woke” narratives, fiduciary-duty claims, and reactionary defences of fossil-fueled social order. On the left, critics target ESG’s vague criteria, weak evidence of real-world impact, and role in legitimizing greenwashing and investor power. Rather than calling for depoliticized ESG metrics, which is self-contradictory since metrics are themselves political choices, I argue for narrower and more contestable uses of ESG metrics: clearer distinctions between ESG integration and impact investing, stronger disclosure and anti-greenwashing rules, and more public accountability over how sustainability is measured and mobilized. For climate policy scholars, the ESG controversy demonstrates both the strategic value and the political limits of economic frames in climate communication.

Introduction

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) investing has become a prominent feature of contemporary sustainable finance, but its political meaning has become increasingly unstable. In its most widely cited institutional formulation, ESG refers to the integration of environmental, social, and governance issues into mainstream investment analysis and ownership practices (UN Global Compact, 2004). Recent reviews define sustainable finance similarly, as the incorporation of ESG issues into financial decision-making, while distinguishing it from impact investing, which is organized around the intention to generate measurable social or environmental impact alongside financial return (Edmans and Kacperczyk, 2022; GIIN, 2019, 2025). Put differently, ESG integration can seek or claim influence over corporate behaviour, but it does not by itself guarantee additional, measurable social or environmental impact in the stronger sense associated with impact investing. This distinction matters because much public commentary continues to conflate ESG integration with impact generation, thereby loading ESG with normative expectations that many investment products were never designed to meet.

Even so, ESG remains economically and politically significant. The Global Sustainable Investment Alliance (2023) estimated that US$30.3 trillion was invested globally in sustainable assets in 2022, even as tighter methodology reduced some earlier market estimates. Meanwhile, Morningstar Manager Research (2024) reported that U.S. sustainable funds registered their first annual outflows in 2023 amid weaker demand and intensifying political backlash. Taken together, these developments suggest that ESG is no longer a niche technical practice but a contested public vocabulary for talking about risk, value, and responsibility in capitalism.

This commentary argues that the politicization of ESG investing stems from a contradiction at its core. ESG is attractive because it translates climate and social concerns into an economic frame legible to investors, corporations, and policymakers. But that same framing narrows the politics of climate action by centering investor judgment, managerial disclosure, and reputational signals. As a result, ESG has become a lightning rod for heterogeneous criticisms: right-wing actors attack it as a form of “woke” market governance, while left-wing critics question its conceptual vagueness, evidentiary weakness, and susceptibility to greenwashing. The key issue, then, is not how to depoliticize ESG metrics, but how to understand what those metrics do politically.

Anti-ESG backlash, anti-“woke” discourse, and reactionary climate politics

The right’s attacks on ESG are best understood as part of a broader reactionary backlash against climate politics, diversity initiatives, and institutional expertise. In the United States, Republican politicians and allied media have repeatedly cast ESG as an attempt to smuggle progressive politics into financial markets under the cover of fiduciary prudence (Ro, 2023; Ritchie, 2023). In Canada and the United Kingdom, similar rhetoric portrays ESG as “woke capital” or “woke capitalism,” reducing a heterogeneous field of sustainable finance practices to a cultural signifier of elite liberalism (Fawcett, 2022; Reinl, 2024). As Davies and MacRae (2023) and Smith et al. (2023) show, “woke” functions less as an analytic category than as a pejorative and elastic discursive weapon through which disparate social justice concerns are bundled together and rendered suspect.

This does not mean that every conservative critic of ESG is motivated by culture-war resentment alone. Reviewer concerns about fiduciary duty, financial returns, and the integrity of labelled products are real features of the backlash. Yet the contemporary anti-ESG campaign gains much of its force by articulating those concerns through a wider reactionary discourse that links climate policy, anti-racism, feminism, and LGBTQ inclusion to elite overreach (Zhang, 2024). In this sense, anti-ESG rhetoric should be read not merely as a technical disagreement about portfolio construction, but as a struggle over who gets to define legitimate economic governance.

This is where Fraser’s (2017, 2018) account of progressive neoliberalism remains useful, but it requires clarification. The point is not that right-wing populists are straightforwardly neoliberal in the same way as centrist advocates of ESG. Rather, Fraser and Phelan (2019) help explain how progressive neoliberalism fused financialized capitalism with a selective politics of recognition, producing a hegemonic bloc in which Wall Street and Silicon Valley could present themselves as socially enlightened while leaving the underlying distribution of power largely intact. Right-populist attacks on “woke capital” target that alliance’s progressive veneer, but they do not necessarily reject market rule itself. What they reject is the idea that capital should be publicly associated with decarbonization, inclusion, or social justice.

Daggett’s (2018) concept of petro-masculinity helps specify why decarbonization has become such an important affective flashpoint within this backlash. Petro-masculinity names the way fossil-fuel dependence becomes entangled with fantasies of autonomy, virility, and civilizational hierarchy. de Nadal’s (2024) analysis of climate misinformation on YouTube shows how climate action is increasingly reframed within culture-war narratives, while Fiorino (2022) documents the convergence of right-wing populism and climate obstruction in the United States. Read through this lens, anti-ESG discourse matters not because ESG is itself transformative, but because it symbolizes an unwanted future in which carbon-intensive lifestyles and patriarchal authority appear less secure.

Recent legal and political conflicts in the United States show how this discourse moves beyond rhetoric. In late 2024, Texas and a coalition of Republican-led states sued BlackRock, State Street, and Vanguard, alleging that their climate commitments amounted to an anticompetitive conspiracy against coal production; Reuters reported that the suit framed ESG as a politicized environmental agenda that distorted energy markets (Reuters, 2024, 2025c). Tennessee also pursued BlackRock over its disclosures regarding the use and claimed benefits of ESG factors (Reuters, 2025a). These cases are important not because they prove anti-ESG critics correct, but because they show how attacks on ESG now combine anti-“woke” discourse with claims about fiduciary duty, disclosure, and market coercion.

Why ESG also disappoints critics on the left

If the right treats ESG as an ideological threat, many critics on the left regard it as an insufficient and often disingenuous response to socioecological crisis. Here the central problem is not that ESG is too political, but that it displaces politics into managerial categories, investor preferences, and disclosure systems. A first source of criticism concerns conceptual slippage. ESG, CSR, and impact investing are frequently collapsed into one another in public debate, even though they have different histories and institutional logics. CSR has traditionally been oriented toward corporate legitimacy and social license, whereas ESG emerged more explicitly as a framework for evaluating financially material non-financial risks and opportunities (UN Global Compact, 2004; Pollman, 2024). Impact investing, by contrast, is organized around intentionality and measurable impact (GIIN, 2019, 2025). When these distinctions collapse, ESG products can be judged against impact-oriented expectations that ESG integration does not necessarily satisfy, while firms can also exploit the ambiguity by marketing ESG as if it necessarily produced measurable social value.

The second problem is empirical. The literature on sustainable investing provides little support for the claim that ESG integration automatically generates meaningful real-world change. This does not mean ESG is pointless or incapable of influencing corporate behaviour; rather, it means that its real-world effects are contingent, mechanism-specific, and often indirect. Kölbel et al. (2020) review the mechanisms through which sustainable investing might produce investor impact—shareholder engagement, capital allocation, and indirect effects—and conclude that engagement has the strongest empirical support, capital allocation only partial support, and indirect effects very little support. Their conclusion is especially relevant here: sustainable investing may diffuse better practices, but it is unlikely to drive deeper transformation without additional policy measures. That finding undercuts the recurring implication in public ESG discourse that better asset screening or portfolio labelling can substitute for political regulation.

A third problem concerns the politics of measurement itself. The call for “better” or “independent” ESG metrics is intuitively appealing, but it risks reproducing the very technocratic logic that made ESG vulnerable in the first place. Porter (1995) famously argues that quantification derives authority from its appearance of objectivity, especially where trust is weak and decision-making must be justified across institutional distance. Merry (2011, 2016) similarly shows that indicators do not merely describe social reality; they translate contested values into commensurable units while often displacing political argument into technical expertise. This insight applies directly to ESG. Metrics depend on decisions about what counts, how categories are defined, which time horizons matter, and whose harms are visible. They are therefore never depoliticized instruments, even when they are presented as neutral devices.

Recent scholarship on ESG metrics echoes this point. Clark and Dixon (2024) argue that the rapid rise of ESG measures is not explained only by a business case for responsible investing; it also helps restore legitimacy to the global asset-management industry by rewriting the terms of risk management and value creation. Archer (2024a) goes further, arguing that ESG data are valued by asset managers not because they produce unique social or environmental benefits, but because they objectify and depoliticize interventions in corporate governance. In his book Unsustainable, Archer (2024b) argues that the contemporary fixation on measurement and reporting narrows sustainability to what can be rendered commensurable and manageable within corporate and financial institutions. From this perspective, demands for more metrics can themselves function as a mode of governance that recenters investor authority instead of resolving the underlying dispute.

This is also why the language of greenwashing should be used carefully. Greenwashing remains analytically useful when firms make claims that are materially misleading or unsupported, as recent anti-greenwashing regulation in the United Kingdom explicitly recognizes (Financial Conduct Authority, 2023). But accusations of greenwashing also rest on contested judgments about what should count as “green,” how much alignment is enough, and whether transitional claims are credible. Aronczyk’s (2024) critique of the debunking paradigm is informative here: simply classifying corporate environmental communication as either authentic or fraudulent can obscure the more important question of how organizations strategically define responsibility in the first place. The problem with ESG, then, is not only hypocrisy. It is that metricized sustainability can authorize narrow, investor-centric definitions of progress while appearing to settle broader political disagreement.

A Canadian case: RBC and the politics of ESG credibility

The controversy surrounding Royal Bank of Canada (RBC) illustrates why ESG conflict cannot be reduced to a simple left-right binary. RBC has repeatedly presented ESG and sustainable finance as part of its corporate strategy, including through annual ESG progress reports and public net-zero commitments (RBC, 2024). Yet it has also faced sustained criticism from environmental and Indigenous advocates because of its financing of fossil fuel expansion and projects such as Coastal GasLink (Myer and Syed, 2024; Woodside, 2024). Ecojustice’s complaint to the Competition Bureau argued that RBC’s climate representations were materially misleading in light of its fossil-fuel financing, turning the case into a dispute not simply about greenwashing in the abstract, but about which metrics and disclosures should count as credible evidence of climate responsibility.

The case also shows why metric repair cannot by itself solve ESG’s legitimacy problem. In 2025, RBC abandoned its C$500 billion sustainable-finance target, saying that methodological problems and amendments to Canada’s Competition Act limited how it could communicate certain sustainability disclosures (Reuters, 2025b). Critics interpreted the decision very differently. For some sustainable-finance advocates, dropping the target signalled retreat and reduced accountability. For others, broad sustainable-finance targets had always been too elastic to serve as meaningful indicators of decarbonization progress. What matters analytically is that both positions are arguments about measurement, disclosure, and credibility—not evidence that a neutral metric could simply end the political dispute.

In short, the RBC controversy shows how ESG communication is attacked from the left as misleading and insufficient, while anti-ESG rhetoric from the right attacks the broader legitimacy of sustainability-oriented finance. The common thread is not shared ideology but the contested authority of financial actors to define what responsible climate action looks like. That is why ESG is so politically volatile: it promises to reconcile profit, disclosure, and social legitimacy, yet repeatedly exposes the tensions among them.

A remedy-oriented reading of the case therefore requires more than either defending or denouncing RBC’s ESG language. RBC could improve the credibility of its claims by distinguishing more clearly between ESG integration, sustainable finance, transition finance, and impact claims; disclosing what its sustainable-finance categories include and exclude; reporting fossil-fuel exposure and financed emissions against transition pathways; and specifying how Indigenous rights and project-level controversies affect its sustainability claims. Regulators, in turn, could require clearer substantiation of ESG and sustainable-finance claims, distinguish product labels from corporate commitments and impact claims, and enforce anti-greenwashing rules that make methodology, exclusions, and limitations visible. The case also suggests that critiques from both right and left should become more precise: conservative critiques should focus on fiduciary clarity, disclosure accuracy, and product integrity rather than generalized anti-“woke” claims, while left critiques should specify whether the problem is fossil financing, weak transition targets, lack of additionality, Indigenous rights concerns, or misleading metrics.

Conclusion

ESG investing will remain politically contested because it condenses multiple and often incompatible expectations into a single acronym. It is asked to function at once as risk management, moral signalling, climate strategy, and proof of corporate responsibility. The RBC case illustrates this condensation: one bank’s sustainable-finance language became a proxy dispute over fossil-fuel finance, Indigenous rights, disclosure credibility, regulatory risk, and the authority of financial institutions to define climate responsibility. The resulting ambiguity has made ESG an easy target for right-wing culture-war politics and an equally compelling object of critique for those concerned with greenwashing, investor power, and neoliberal governance.

Accordingly, the solution is not the development of depoliticized ESG metrics. Metrics are unavoidably political because they embed choices about materiality, causality, comparison, and accountability. What is needed instead is a narrower and more contestable use of ESG measures: clearer distinctions between ESG integration and impact investing, more transparent disclosure about what metrics can and cannot show, stronger anti-greenwashing enforcement, and greater public scrutiny over how financial actors define sustainability. Put differently, the task is not to remove politics from ESG measurement but to make its politics more visible and more accountable.

For climate policy scholars and practitioners, ESG’s appeal lies in its economic framing: it translates climate and social concerns into the language of risk, competitiveness, and fiduciary obligation, thereby making them legible to investors and some corporate elites. The RBC case shows how quickly that frame becomes unstable when sustainability language confronts fossil-fuel financing, disclosure limits, and public demands for accountability. The Engine No. 1 campaign at ExxonMobil is a reminder that such framing can, under some conditions, generate real strategic pressure within firms (Hiller and Herbst-Bayliss, 2021). But this example should be read as a contrast rather than as an escape from the RBC problem: economic frames may produce tactical leverage, but they cannot settle the political dispute over what transition requires or who should define it. Economic frames may broaden coalitions for transition, yet they can also narrow public imagination by treating investor-led governance as the privileged route to climate action. If climate politics is to move beyond the current impasse, it will require not just better measurement but more explicit democratic argument about what sustainability should mean, who gets to define it, and how costs and responsibilities should be distributed.
