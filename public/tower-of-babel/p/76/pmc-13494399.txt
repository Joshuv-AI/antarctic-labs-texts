Fetal Agenesis of the Ductus Venosus with Extrahepatic Shunt
Authors: Shannon R Zhang, Sowmya Balasubramanian, Katherine DeWeert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Agenesis of the ductus venosus (ADV) is a rare fetal vascular anomaly in which the normal connection between the umbilical vein and the inferior vena cava (IVC) is absent. This can result in the formation of intrahepatic or extrahepatic shunts to the right heart. As such, ADV alters physiologic umbilical venous return, which can significantly affect fetal cardiovascular physiology. Fetal echocardiography plays a critical role in the prenatal identification of ADV, characterization of umbilical venous drainage, and monitoring for hemodynamic sequelae, such as cardiomegaly, pericardial effusion, or elevated combined cardiac output.

Here we present a case of ADV with extrahepatic umbilical venous return in which serial fetal echocardiography captured the evolution of cardiac findings and eventual improvement as proximal shunt narrowing developed. The case highlights the critical role of the ductus venosus (DV) in regulating umbilical venous return in early fetal life, a role that diminishes with advancing gestation.

Case Presentation

A 33-year-old G3P1 mother was referred to pediatric cardiology at 21 weeks and 3 days of gestation after prenatal ultrasound demonstrated a dilated IVC and suspected atrioventricular (AV) septal defect (AVSD). Cell-free DNA screening was positive for trisomy 21. There was no significant contributory maternal history.

Fetal echocardiography confirmed a complete AVSD consisting of a large ostium primum atrial septal defect (ASD), a moderate-to-large inlet ventricular septal defect (VSD), and mild left AV valve regurgitation. The DV was not visualized. Instead, a dilated umbilical vein was seen returning directly to the IVC below the hepatic veins, with a prominent, nonrestrictive flow pattern, consistent with extrahepatic umbilical venous drainage (Figure 1, Video 1). The heart-chest area ratio was lower than normal at 0.21 (normal, 0.28-0.33). Biventricular systolic function was normal, and there was no pericardial effusion.

Two-dimensional transabdominal fetal echocardiogram, without (left) and with (right) color-flow Doppler, long-axis view (A) and with pulsed-wave Doppler spectral display (B) obtained at 21 weeks, 3 days of gestation, demonstrates a dilated umbilical vein returning to the IVC below the entrance of the hepatic veins and prominent nonrestrictive flow.

Fetal echocardiogram at 27 weeks and 5 days of gestation showed new narrowing and mild restriction of umbilical vein flow as it entered the hepatic portion of the IVC. The heart-chest area ratio was normal at 0.3. Combined cardiac output index was elevated, at 706 mL/kg/min (normal range for gestational age, 500-525 mL/kg/min). Transabdominal obstetrics ultrasound at 31 weeks and 5 days of gestation showed new fetal growth restriction (estimated at 7% for age) and a small pericardial effusion (Figure 2, Video 2).

Two-dimensional transabdominal obstetrics ultrasound, diastolic 4-chamber view obtained at 31 weeks, 5 days of gestation, demonstrates the AVSD and a small pericardial effusion.

Fetal echocardiogram at 33 weeks and 5 days of gestation demonstrated new cardiomegaly, with a heart-chest area ratio of 0.51 and phasic flow reversal in the umbilical vein (Figure 3, Video 3). Combined cardiac output index was elevated, at 878 mL/kg/min (normal, 400-450 mL/kg/min).

Two-dimensional transabdominal fetal echocardiogram with color-flow Doppler (A), lowered Nyquist limit) and pulsed-wave Doppler spectral display (B), short-axis view obtained at 33 weeks, 5 days of gestation, demonstrates a dilated umbilical vein as it courses within the liver and creates a spectral Doppler profile with phasic reversal of flow.

The findings of worsened cardiomegaly, pericardial effusion, increased combined cardiac output index, and phasic reversal of flow in the umbilical vein at 33 weeks and 5 days of gestation were concerning for evolving hydrops fetalis from high-output heart failure. After a period of inpatient monitoring yielded reassuring results, the mother was discharged with plans for frequent outpatient fetal echocardiograms and nonstress tests (NSTs).

Subsequent fetal echocardiogram at 35 weeks and 4 days of gestation showed slight narrowing of the dilated umbilical vein before returning to the hepatic portion of the IVC (Figure 4; Videos 4 and 5). The umbilical vein flow pattern normalized (Figure 5). The heart-chest area ratio improved to 0.39, and the pericardial effusion resolved (Figure 6; Video 6). Combined cardiac output index remained elevated but improved compared to before, at 706 mL/kg/min (normal, 470-500 mL/kg/min).

Two-dimensional transabdominal fetal echocardiogram, without (left) and with (right) color-flow Doppler, long-axis view obtained at 35 weeks, 4 days of gestation, demonstrates slight narrowing of the dilated umbilical vein before returning to the hepatic portion of the IVC.

Two-dimensional transabdominal fetal echocardiogram, short-axis view without (A) and with color-flow Doppler-guided pulsed-wave Doppler (B) obtained at 35 weeks, 4 days of gestation, demonstrates slight narrowing of the dilated umbilical vein before returning to the hepatic portion of the IVC with slightly more restrictive flow seen on the spectral Doppler profile. Pulsed-wave Doppler spectral display sampling the umbilical vein (C) shows nonphasic low-velocity flow.

Two-dimensional transabdominal fetal echocardiogram, 4-chamber diastolic view obtained at 35 weeks, 4 days of gestation, demonstrates the AVSD, improvement in cardiomegaly, and decrease in the size of the pericardial effusion.

An NST at 36 weeks and 2 days of gestation identified signs of fetal distress, prompting urgent early delivery by cesarean section at a center equipped with both neonatal intensive care and pediatric cardiac critical care. Apgar scores were 7 and 9 at 1 minute and 5 minutes of life, respectively. The patient weighed 2.6 kg (26% for age). Postnatal echocardiogram on day of life 1 demonstrated a complete well-balanced AVSD with large primum ASD, moderate-size secundum ASD, and a VSD likely completely closed by AV valve tissue. A large remnant of the umbilical vein connected directly to the IVC with low-velocity flow, suggesting persistence of the prenatally detected extrahepatic shunt. The patient was discharged at 10 days of life and followed as an outpatient with pediatric cardiology until planned operative repair at 5 months of age.

Discussion

The DV plays a central role in normal fetal circulation by acting as a regulated conduit that preferentially directs oxygenated umbilical venous return through the right atrium (RA) toward the left heart.1,2 In the normal fetal circulation, the DV arises from the intra-abdominal umbilical vein and inserts into the IVC at the level of the portal sinus, just proximal to its entry into the RA. As hepatic vascular resistance falls over the course of fetal life, the proportion of umbilical flow traversing the DV decreases from approximately 40% at 20 weeks to 15% at term.2 Fetal echocardiography serves as the first-line imaging modality for assessment of the DV, offering noninvasive and reliable visualization and Doppler characterization of the DV, as well as comprehensive evaluation for associated congenital cardiac abnormalities. On fetal echocardiography, the DV is typically identified in a midsagittal or slightly oblique sagittal view of the fetal abdomen, which shows the umbilical vein coursing toward the liver. A transverse view of the upper fetal abdomen can also demonstrate the DV in cross section as it arises from the portal sinus and courses toward the IVC. Color-flow Doppler facilitates visualization of the DV, which will appear as a narrow channel with accelerated flow. Pulsed-wave Doppler interrogation at this site characteristically demonstrates a high-velocity, triphasic waveform reflecting transmission of right atrial pressure changes.

Agenesis of the DV is a rare vascular abnormality, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1 in 2,500 fetuses detected in the first trimester.3 Patients are commonly classified based on the site of umbilical venous drainage, either intrahepatic (indirect) or extrahepatic (direct).4, 5, 6 Intrahepatic drainage describes a direct connection between the umbilical vein and the portal venous system. Due to potential hyperperfusion of the liver, these patients may be at increased risk of portal hypertension.1 In contrast, extrahepatic drainage bypasses the hepatic circulation completely. In this variant, the umbilical vein may connect directly to the IVC, RA, or coronary sinus.5,6 This abnormal pathway can increase central venous pressure and result in chronic volume overload, cardiomegaly, and eventual right ventricular dysfunction and high-output heart failure.5

Objective measures of fetal cardiac status such as estimations of heart size and cardiac output can be reliably obtained by fetal echocardiography. The normal fetal heart-to-chest area ratio remains relatively constant throughout gestation, typically ranging from 0.28 to 0.33. Values greater than 0.35 are considered abnormal and indicative of cardiomegaly.7 Fetal cardiac output is estimated by multiplying the ventricular outflow tract cross-sectional area by the Doppler-derived velocity-time integral across the semilunar valve associated with the ventricle and the fetal heart rate. Right and left ventricular outputs are summed to derive the combined cardiac output, which can be indexed to estimated fetal weight. A combined cardiac output index greater than 750 mL/kg/min has been associated with an increased risk of hydrops fetalis and may prompt consideration of early delivery.5

In our patient, ADV resulted in an unregulated, high-volume conduit from the placental circulation directly into the RA. The increased volume load on the right heart was compounded by the presence of an AVSD. Volume overload manifested at 33 weeks and 5 days of gestation as cardiomegaly, pericardial effusion, and phasic reversal of flow in the umbilical vein in the absence of significant AV valve regurgitation, suggestive of diastolic dysfunction of the right heart. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ADV when trisomy 21 and AVSDs coexist, as the vascular anomaly can substantially alter the expected hemodynamic progression of AVSD.3 Of note, ADV is associated with congenital heart disease and chromosomal abnormalities. Structural cardiac defects are present in up to one-third of patients with ADV, although specific lesion types are variably reported across case series. Chromosomal abnormalities are reported in approximately 15% to 30% of cases, with trisomy 21 among the most commonly described aneuploidies.8

Extrahepatic ADV is frequently associated with progressive deterioration toward hydrops fetalis and a significantly worse prognosis compared with intrahepatic ADV.3,5,6 Previous reports that have described stabilization of right heart function over fetal life occurred in patients whose circulation more closely mirrored normal, such as those with normal cardiac anatomy.4 Our case challenges the assumption that the development of phasic flow reversal in the umbilical vein and pericardial effusions in the setting of extrahepatic ADV uniformly necessitates immediate delivery. The spontaneous resolution of these patient markers of heart failure between 33 and 35 weeks of gestation indicates we captured a transient physiological state, rather than a prelude to irreversible decompensation. Our observations support a hypothesis of late-gestational hemodynamic redistribution, suggesting that transient deterioration may not always mandate immediate delivery and that a conservative approach with close prenatal surveillance may be appropriate in select cases. Potential mechanisms for this improvement include two phenomena that occur over the course of gestation: increasing hepatic vascular capacity, allowing partial buffering of umbilical venous return, and reduced dependence on the DV for preferential streaming across the foramen ovale.1,2 Additionally, the patient developed slight narrowing at the connection of the umbilical vein to the IVC, effectively buffering the hemodynamic impact on the right heart. Our case adds to the existing literature by documenting a late-gestational compensatory window, suggesting that the natural history of direct ADV may be more heterogeneous than previously described.

In the stable late-preterm fetus, a period of watchful waiting with serial echocardiography and NSTs may allow for fetal compensatory mechanisms to manifest, potentially avoiding the morbidity associated with early preterm delivery. In our case, the identification by fetal echocardiography in the second trimester of absent DV flow, with extrahepatic shunt, allowed for early preparation for potential complications including the development of intrauterine growth restriction cardiomegaly, heart failure, and hydrops fetalis. The mother was advised to deliver at a hospital with access to neonatal intensive care and to undergo more frequent prenatal monitoring and serial fetal echocardiograms.

Conclusion

In fetal life, the DV is an important resistor in umbilical venous return and optimizes streaming of oxygenated placental venous return to the most essential developing organs. Agenesis of the DV is rare and can result in the formation of either intrahepatic or extrahepatic shunts. Our patient with ADV with extrahepatic shunt initially developed signs of high-output heart failure that resolved over fetal life, illustrative of the uniquely critical and dynamic role of the DV in early pregnancy that decreases over fetal life.
