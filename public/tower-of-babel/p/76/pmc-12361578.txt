Comparing soil microbial diversity in smallholder plantain backyard gardens and main farms in Western and Central Africa
Authors: Manoj Kaushal, Yao Adjiguita Kolombia, Amos Emitati Alakonya, Cargele Masso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), plantains (Musa spp.) are a staple crop and a vital source of income for smallholder farmers. Despite their importance, the microbial diversity of soils in plantain-growing agroecologies remains poorly understood—particularly how it may influence plant performance and resilience through soil–plant interactions. In this study, we used Illumina MiSeq-based 16S rDNA sequencing to characterize bacterial communities in the rhizosphere of plantains cultivated under two distinct agroecological settings: Backyard gardens and main farms. Analyses of alpha and beta diversity (Sobs, Chao1, ACE, Shannon–Wiener, and Simpson indices; P < 0.05) revealed significant differences in species richness and community structure between the two agroecologies. Actinobacteria (55%) emerged as the dominant phylum, followed by Proteobacteria (21%) and Acidobacteria (15%). Beneficial genera such as Bacillus, Streptomyces, Bradyrhizobium, and Paenibacillus were also detected. Functional predictions based on COG and KEGG databases indicated notable differences in microbial functional potential between the two settings. These results suggest that agroecological context and habitat type strongly influence rhizosphere microbial diversity, with important implications for enhancing plant–microbe interactions and supporting crop resilience in SSA’s resource-limited smallholder systems.

Introduction

Plantains (Musa spp.) are a staple crop and critical source of income for smallholder farmers (SHFs) across sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), particularly in Western and Central Africa (WCA). However, plantain production systems face increasing challenges due to a combination of environmental degradation, biotic and abiotic stressors, and socio-economic constraints. In recent years, global crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic and the Ukraine–Russia conflict have disrupted supply chains, increasing food insecurity—particularly for vulnerable SHFs with limited resources and adaptive capacity. In SSA, agricultural systems are often characterized by poor soil health, largely due to nutrient depletion, loss of biodiversity, and land degradation. Approximately 65% of agricultural land in the region is degraded due to unsustainable land use practices, which severely limits productivity and resilience1.

Climate change compounds these problems by altering water availability, species distribution, and increasing the frequency of extreme weather events (e.g., droughts, floods, and heatwaves). SHFs, who rely heavily on rain-fed agriculture, are particularly vulnerable due to limited access to irrigation, inputs, and financial resources2. While the push for sustainable and resilient food systems is gaining traction globally, conventional farming in SSA often emphasizes high-input approaches (e.g., hybrid seeds, fertilizers, machinery), raising questions about long-term sustainability. Despite the region’s rich natural resources and agricultural diversity, WCA continues to face food and nutrition insecurity, with over 70% of the population dependent on farming for livelihoods3.

SHFs in WCA typically employ diverse farming practices—including monocropping, intercropping, and crop rotation—aligned with local agroecologies3. These practices are not marginal alternatives but are deeply integrated into regional food systems. When optimized, they can reduce dependency on external inputs, mitigate environmental degradation, and improve indigenous soil biodiversity. Agroecologies in this study refer to distinct ecological zones shaped by interactions among soil, climate, vegetation, and farming systems. These zones range from humid forest areas to drier savannas and play a central role in shaping cropping systems. Plantain, highly adaptable to these conditions, is especially prominent in the humid forest belt but is also cultivated in transitional and savanna zones depending on soil fertility and water access.

This study focuses on plantain-based cultivation systems in the humid forest agroecologies of Cameroon and Nigeria—two of Africa’s leading plantain producers. In these countries, plantains are typically cultivated by SHFs using traditional, low-input methods. Cultivation systems include backyard gardens and main farms, which differ in size, management intensity, and soil amendment practices. Backyard gardens are smaller and often enriched with organic waste from households and livestock, while main farms are larger and tend to depend more on extensive land use with limited organic inputs.

To ensure consistency in sampling, we selected plantain fields that have undergone continuous or rotational cultivation over the past 2–3 years. This approach helps ensure that observed microbial communities reflect stable agroecosystem conditions, rather than transitional or recently converted fields. Despite the high potential of plantain to support food security and livelihoods—often yielding up to 100% returns—production is threatened by multiple challenges. SHFs face yield losses of 30–50% due to biotic stressors such as black leaf streak disease, banana weevils, and nematode infestations4. These issues are compounded by declining soil fertility, land degradation, rising input costs, and poor access to quality planting material.

Soil microbial diversity, although central to soil health and crop productivity, is often overlooked in intensified systems like plantain cultivation. Beneficial soil microbes are key to nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and ecosystem resilience. Microorganisms such as Bacillus, Streptomyces, Bradyrhizobium, and Rhizobium spp. contribute to pathogen resistance and soil fertility through various mechanisms including nitrogen fixation and phosphorus solubilization5. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), for example, improve soil structure and moisture retention6. While chemical fertilizers offer immediate nutrient boosts, their overuse can disrupt microbial communities, degrade soil structure, and pose environmental and health risks through runoff and food chain contamination.

For plantain, soil microbial communities are influenced by multiple factors including soil pH, organic matter, and input types. In WCA, where plantain is cultivated across diverse agroecologies, these factors vary significantly between backyard and main farm systems, influencing microbial diversity and composition. This study aims to expand our understanding of plantain–microbe associations in WCA by examining the microbial communities in backyard gardens and main farms. We explore how differences in soil inputs and land management shape rhizosphere microbiomes and influence plantain production potential. By characterizing core microbial communities in these two agroecologies, we provide a baseline for soil health monitoring and offer insights into enhancing soil fertility and resilience in smallholder farming systems.

Materials and methods

Study site and rhizosphere sample collection

This study was simultaneously conducted with another research on soil microbiome in diverse agroecologies of WCA, representing the data coordinates for each sampling point in Western and Central African countries of Nigeria and Cameroon3. Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from both the main farms of smallholder farmers (SHFs) and their backyard gardens. Plantains with identical maturity levels, selected randomly, were visually assessed for sampling. Presuming rich, diverse and different microbial diversity in two sites, replicated samples for rhizosphere soil attached to root zones around 15–30 cm depth were collected. We selected this depth to target the main root zone where most plants absorb water and nutrients, a layer with the highest microbial activity. Further sampling at this variable depth provides a more accurate and consistent picture of soil fertility, avoiding surface-level contamination from organic matter or plant residues that haven’t moved deeper into the zones. A total of 8 composite samples were collected, consisting of pooled rhizosphere sub-samples from five randomly selected plantain plants per plot. Sample collection was stratified by agroecologies and by farm type (backyard garden vs. main farm). Sub-samples were taken using sterilized tools to prevent cross-contamination and were combined to generate one representative sample per plot. Pooling was employed to reduce intra-plot microbial variability and reflect plot-level microbial composition. Soil samples were pooled, instantly set in polythene examining bags and carried to the research facility in an ice-cold box. Samples were kept at 4 °C within the research facility until processed. Sample size determination was based on agroecological variation, plot accessibility, and logistical feasibility, aiming to capture representative microbial profiles across farm types and locations. Stratified random sampling was chosen to reduce selection bias and enhance comparison reliability. All methods were carried out agreeing to the regulation rules and controls (International Institute of Tropical Agriculture). Earlier to sampling, permissions were obtained from each farmer in both countries. All the methods for sampling, DNA extraction, sequencing and data mining were performed as previously described in another study3.

Genomic DNA extraction and amplicon generation

Genomic DNA was extracted from 250 mg of each composite rhizosphere soil sample using the ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Soil closely adhering to the root surface was targeted to enrich for rhizosphere-associated microbiota. Samples were homogenized using bead tubes with mechanical lysis. DNA was extracted using Genomic Lysis Buffer and purified via spin-column filtration. DNA quality was assessed using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer, evaluating A260/A280 and A260/A230 ratios. DNA integrity was confirmed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel3. While this primer pair is widely used for full-length 16S amplicons, the ~ 1,500 bp target exceeds the read length of the Illumina MiSeq 2 × 150 bp platform used in this study. As a result, only forward reads were retained for downstream analysis. Although this reduced taxonomic resolution, it provided sufficient information for genus-level profiling and inter-agroecological comparisons, which was the primary aim of the study.

Illumina sequencing library preparation and clustering

Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with barcoded adapters added for multiplexing. Libraries were quantified using Qubit, qPCR, and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform by BGI Genomics (Beijing, China). Following sequencing, raw reads were demultiplexed based on barcodes and underwent quality filtering using QIIME v1.8.07. Primer sequences were trimmed, and paired-end reads were processed using the FLASH tool, though paired-end merging was not feasible due to the long amplicon size. Consequently, only forward reads were retained and used for further analysis. Chimeric sequences were detected and removed using UCHIME v4.2.40, with comparisons made against the GOLD 16S rRNA reference database8. No 18S rRNA or ITS sequencing was performed in this study. Earlier references to the UNITE database have been removed, as no fungal analysis was carried out.

Analysis of community patterns and databases for species annotation

Filtered high-quality reads were clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at 97% sequence similarity using the UPARSE algorithm implemented in USEARCH v7.0.10909. Representative sequences from each OTU were assigned taxonomy using the RDP Classifier v2.2, trained on the Greengenes database (v13.5). A confidence threshold of 0.6 was applied for taxonomic assignment10.

Statistical analysis

Alpha diversity was correlated for analyzing the complexity of species differing qualities through several indices, including observed species, Chao1, ACE, and Shannon and Simpson indices. Observed species value, Chao1, and ACE can reflect the species abundance of the community, and the rarefaction curve was utilized to assess on the off chance that created information is sufficient to cover all species within the community. The indices were calculated by Mothur (v1.31.2), and the comparing rarefaction curves were drawn by the package “Vegan” software R (v3.1.1). A Venn diagram may outwardly show the number of common/unique OTUs in different samples and groups. Based on OTU abundances, OTU of each group were recorded, Venn diagram as drawn by program R (v3.1.1), at that point the common and specific OTU ID were summarized. Beta diversity analysis was utilized to assess differences of samples in species complexity by program QIIME (v1.80). To statistically compare alpha diversity indices across agroecologies and countries, non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests were applied, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests where applicable. Statistical analyses, including t-tests, ANOVA and PERMANOVA were employed to compare beta-diversity metrics and assess differences in microbial community composition between groups. PERMANOVA (Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance) was conducted using the adonis function in the vegan R package with 999 permutations to test for significant differences based on agroecological management (B vs. F) and geographic region. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) utilized to develop 3D chart to show the differences of OTU composition and summarize components. The relative abundance of each OUT sample was calculated and the PCoA of OTU was done by the package “ade4” of software R (v3.1.1). The unweighted pair group method with arithmetic means (UPGMA) was used to interpret the distance matrix produced by beta diversity. To degree the strength of this result to sequencing efforts, we performed a jackknifng analysis and resulting available data set by QIIME. In addition, Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) was performed to further validate group-wise community differences, while jackknifing procedures in QIIME were used to evaluate the robustness of community clustering across random subsets of data. Anosim performed to reveal the similarities and differences of the community compositions and structure between groups and groups of unusual samples3.

Results

Physico‑chemical parameters of the study sites

Soil texture at sampling locations range from sandy clay to sandy loam and sandy clay to clay loam between different sampling sites3. Average soil pH ranging in samples in backyard soils was 6.32–7.2, and main farm 6.8–7.4. A total number of 96 samples were collected from backyard and main farm of SHFs and pooled (NB2, 12; NB4, 12; CB3, 12; CB4, 12; NF1, 12; NF2, 12; CF3, 12; CF4, 12) representing two countries (N, Nigeria; and C, Cameroon), and two agroecologies (backyard (B) and farm (F)). Details on the sampling points are given in the supplementary Table S1. Assuming the difference in inputs, nutritional levels and management practices, the study was conducted to reveal the microbial diversity and differences in rhizosphere microbial communities between two plantain-growing agroecologies (backyard, B and farm, F) in WCA. Backyard (B) found high amount of organic matter content (1.98%-2.21%), total nitrogen (0.18%-0.20%), available phosphorus (5.25 mg kg-1–6.29 mg kg-1) and exchangeable potassium (298 mg kg-1–395 mg kg-1) comparably lower amount of organic matter content (0.59%-0.91%), total nitrogen (0.12%-0.16%), available phosphorus (2.24 mg kg-1–2.95 mg kg-1) and exchangeable potassium (202 mg kg-1–209 mg kg-1) in the farm (F) in all sampling sites. The number of clean reads and mapped reads for 16S of each sample is summarized in Supplementary Table S2. The tags, in terms of bacteria, were clustered into OTUs at 97% shared sequence similarity.

Species richness of plantain farm and backyard

To directly assess the α-diversity of samples, we employed rarefaction techniques using QIIME, resulting in a rarefaction curve based on the Shannon diversity index. This analysis indicated that the sequencing data adequately captured the diversity of observed operational taxonomic units (OTUs) within the samples (Fig. S1). Additionally, the Chao1 estimator and accumulation curves for OTU richness corroborated these findings, suggesting that the sampling was sufficient to detect all relevant OTUs in each group (Fig. S1). Interestingly, the α-diversity in farm (F) samples was significantly higher than in backyard (B) samples (P < 0.05) across metrics including observed species (Sobs), Chao1, and ACE indices (Fig. S2, Table S3). Ecologically, this higher diversity in F soils-despite lower nutrient levels—may reflect more heterogeneous or stressful soil environments that promote niche diversification and reduce dominance by a few taxa, thereby supporting richer microbial communities.

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed to compare the taxonomic compositions and evaluate β-diversity based on differences in Bray–Curtis and both unweighted and weighted UniFrac distances at the OTU level. PCA showed that the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for more than 35% of the variation in taxonomic composition among samples. Significant differences in β-diversity between B and F samples were confirmed (PERMANOVA, P < 0.05). Although some samples from B and F overlapped in ordination space, this was primarily due to greater within-group variation in backyard samples, which increased dispersion and caused partial clustering overlap (Fig. 1A). A phylogenetic tree and heatmap constructed using UPGMA mean methods showed no distinct clades among the B and F (Figs. 1B, 1C). Substantial variations in OTU richness were recorded between the backyard and farm. Among the total of 5299 bacterial OTUs identified in both B and F, 1041 were found in F and 1161 in B of the rhizosphere soil (Fig. S3). Changes in phylum level composition in plantain farm and backyard

Seven dominant phyla were identified across all samples, each exhibiting a relative abundance greater than 1% in both B and F: Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, Firmicutes, Chloroflexi, Planctomycetes, Bacteroidetes, which collectively accounted for 95.9–99.0% of all bacterial taxa in the rhizosphere soil. The bacterial community of plantain in both B and F was dominated by Actinobacteria (55%), Proteobacteria (21%), Acidobacteria (15%) phylum across different samples (Fig. 2, Table S4). Despite minor differences between B and F, microbial communities appeared more closely linked to specific niches, exhibiting higher relative abundances in plantain rhizosphere (P < 0.01). Conversely, Bacteroidetes were primarily found in both B and F while Planctomycetes dominants the rhizosphere soil of F.

Lower-Level changes in plantain farm and backyard

In total, 18 classes were identified, with 6 showing higher relative abundance (> 1%) in both Band F. Some phyla shared dominant classes in both B and F; for instance, Actinobacteria, Acidobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria and Deltaproteobacteria dominated in the rhizosphere of plantains (Fig. 2). The Bacilli class from the phylum Firmicutes was predominant in the rhizosphere soil of F, the order Bacillales was dominant, contributing 4.9–17.5% to the composition. Rhizobiales, the leading order within Alphaproteobacteria, were present in the rhizosphere across both agroecologies of B and F. However, Rhodospirillales from Alphaproteobacteria were more prevalent in F compared to B. A total of 38 bacterial taxa were identified and differentially enriched (α < 0.01), as indicated by LDA effect size scores exceeding 4.0. To visualize these differences in microbial composition between backyard (B) and farm (F) agroecological systems, a cladogram based on LEfSe analysis was generated (Fig. 3), effectively highlighting taxa that are characteristic of each environment. In addition, a phylogenetic tree was constructed using representative bacterial species from previous studies, with sequences retrieved from the NCBI database (Fig. 4). The neighbor-joining tree revealed the presence of 11 major bacterial phyla across all samples. Regardless of agroecology, dominant genera such as Bacillus, Streptomyces, Bradyrhizobium, and Paenibacillus were consistently associated with the plantain rhizosphere, with dominant phyla accounting for 95.5–98.9% of the communities. Notably, Bacillaceae was significantly more abundant in B, while Bradyrhizobium was enriched in F. The inclusion of these phylogenetic analyses was essential for placing the identified taxa into an evolutionary framework, allowing us to better understand how microbial lineages cluster in response to agroecological conditions. Analysis of functional variations in plantain attributes

To further explore potential differences in the functional diversity of microbial communities across agroecologies, functional annotations were obtained by blasting sequences against the Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COGs) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Orthology databases. The sequencing results indicated no significant variations in microbial functionality between B and F. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed differentially abundant genes among B than F agroecologies. The gene abundance observed was significant, with F agroecologies showing high gene counts, and other comparisons revealing a general trend towards differentially abundant taxa (Fig. 6). A total of 22 KEGG pathways were identified across all samples, showing statistically significant enrichment (LDA score > 2.0, p < 0.05) in B and F agroecological comparisons, as determined by LEfSe analysis. The pathways with the highest enrichment included translation, ribosomal structure, biogenesis; carbohydrate transport and metabolism; coenzyme transport and metabolism; transcription; cell wall/ membrane/ envelope biogenesis; inorganic ion transport and metabolism; energy production and conversion and lipid transport and metabolism. Notably, both B and F demonstrated the greatest enrichment in amino acid transport and metabolism and gene function prediction only. Although the lack of significant overall differences in predicted functional genes may seem unexpected given the taxonomic diversity observed, this could be attributed to functional redundancy within microbial communities—where diverse taxa perform similar functional roles, preserving core ecosystem functions despite compositional shifts. Nonetheless, the enrichment of specific KEGG pathways, particularly those related to amino acid metabolism in B systems, suggests that functionally relevant differences do exist and may reflect variation in nutrient inputs, organic matter availability, and farm management practices.Fig. 6Functional enrichment of metabolic pathways in backyard (B) versus farm (F) rhizosphere microbiomes11,12. Discussion

To enhance soil health and improve crop productivity for SHFs in SSA, future research should focus on several key areas. First, understanding the microbial composition in the rhizosphere is essential. Soil life beneath our feet is at the forefront for playing a crucial role in enhancing ecosystem services, which is also vital for the successful cultivation of plantains. This study offers a detailed analysis of rhizosphere microbial communities in two major plantain-producing countries-Cameroon and Nigeria, unveiling practical opportunities to leverage microbial dynamics for ecological and agronomic gains.

Our findings reveal distinct bacterial profiles influenced by geographical location, soil composition, and agricultural/management practices across agroecologies B and F. This reflects the diversity and responsiveness of microbial communities to local conditions. Promoting beneficial plant–microbe interactions must be prioritized, particularly in staple and economically important crops like bananas and plantains, which can enhance productivity while reducing reliance on chemical fertilizers and pesticides, thereby mitigating environmental impacts. This is especially significant for smallholder farmers, who often lack access to industrial agrochemicals or synthetic fertilizers.

Encouraging natural plant–microbe interactions supports low-input agriculture, reducing costs while preserving long-term soil fertility. For instance, practices such as composting, mulching, and the use of cover crops are known to enhance microbial diversity and nutrient cycling. Prior studies have indicated that analyzing specific regions of the 16S rDNA can provide insights into microbial diversity, particularly when amplified for community characterization. Our study adopted amplification of the V3–V4 region of 16S rDNA across all sample sets to optimize the retrieval of classifiable sequences.

Changes in root morphology in perennial plants can also lead to variations in root exudates, which influence the microbial community structure in the rhizosphere suggesting that plant species recruit specific microbiomes for their functional needs. We analyzed the below-ground microbial communities of plantains across two agroecologies under different management practices (B and F) in Nigeria and Cameroon. Using both α- and β-diversity measures, we identified significant variations in microbial diversity and community composition across both B and F agroecologies.

In particular, the distinct clustering patterns in the PCoA analysis suggested that agroecological conditions and management practices may play a critical role in shaping microbial community composition in plantain B and F (Fig. 1). This suggests that smallholders managing backyard plots with more diverse or less intensive practices may be inadvertently creating microenvironments that foster microbial diversity, which is linked to better nutrient cycling and soil resilience.

Our findings also indicate that various bacterial communities dominate different niches within the rhizosphere, emphasizing the need for a nuanced understanding of microbial interactions in these ecosystems. The metagenomic analysis indicated a rich diversity of bacterial taxa in the rhizosphere of plantains, with notable variations between samples from Cameroon and Nigeria. Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria and Firmicutes dominated the communities (90–92.99%), consistent with findings from similar agroecologies.

The variation in community composition can be attributed to differences in soil pH, organic matter content, and farming practices. For instance, the higher organic matter levels observed in Nigerian soils may enhance microbial diversity, fostering beneficial bacteria that promote nutrient cycling13. This highlights the practical value of managing organic matter to sustain soil biological activity.

Our results suggest a strong correlation between the presence of specific bacterial taxa and indicators of soil health. Notably, taxa associated with nitrogen fixation, phosphorus solubilization and antibiotic producers were prevalent, particularly in the F samples. These findings support the assertion that a diverse microbial community is crucial for maintaining soil fertility and crop production14. The identification of beneficial bacteria such as Bacillus, Streptomyces, and Bradyrhizobium (21.5–39.9%) underscores their potential roles in biocontrol and plant growth promotion. These microbial-based improvements are particularly valuable for SHFs, offering ecologically sound, low-cost strategies to maintain soil fertility and yields. Increased microbial diversity has been linked to higher crop yields, primarily through enhanced nutrient availability and disease resistance15,16. The differences in bacterial community structure between Cameroon and Nigeria may also reflect regional agricultural practices, including fertilizer usage, crop rotation, and intercropping systems. In Nigeria, the application of organic fertilizers and cover crops appears to promote beneficial microbial populations, aligning with agroecological principles that prioritize soil health17–19. Conversely, more intensive practices observed in some areas of Cameroon may contribute to a decline in microbial diversity, negatively impacting soil health.

Functional enrichment studies using Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG) and KEGG pathway classifications revealed differentially abundant genes in the B and F groups (Fig. 6). In total, 22 KEGG pathways were significantly enriched, including translation, carbohydrate metabolism, coenzyme transport, transcription, cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis, and energy production. Both B and F agroecologies demonstrated enrichment in pathways related to amino acid transport and host-plant interactions. These enriched pathways point to a reservoir of microbial functionality that SHFs can harness through organic and low-impact soil practices. Encouraging microbial-rich systems not only enhance plant productivity but also builds climate resilience by promoting nutrient-use efficiency and soil biological activity over time.

Our study highlights significant differences in microbial diversity between the rhizosphere of plantains cultivated in backyard gardens (B) and those in large-scale main farms (F). These variations can be attributed to land management practices, soil conditions, and plant–microbe interactions. Backyard systems exhibited greater microbial diversity, consistent with previous studies suggesting that smaller, diversified ecosystems support a wider range of microbial taxa20–23. SHFs practicing diversified planting and applying organic waste inputs are inadvertently preserving microbial richness, enhancing not only productivity but ecological sustainability. In contrast, monoculture and synthetic inputs common in F can reduce microbial diversity, disrupting community composition and soil function24–29.

Our findings show that beneficial taxa like nitrogen-fixing bacteria and mycorrhizal fungi were more prevalent in B, offering SHFs a natural form of “insurance” against stressors such as drought and pests. These agroecologies could provide a more sustainable model for plantain cultivation, promoting soil health and improving yield stability over time. These results also affirm that SHFs practicing low-input, ecologically mindful farming are not only improving their crop outcomes but are also contributing to broader environmental sustainability efforts through enhanced biodiversity and reduced ecological footprints. The enhanced microbial diversity in backyard gardens likely contributes to a more resilient ecosystem capable of withstanding environmental stressors such as drought or pest outbreaks30–32. Integrating organic farming practices, such as crop rotation and cover cropping, could improve soil health and microbial communities in larger farms, mimicking the diversity seen in B33–36.

While these findings offer promising pathways, SHFs face socioeconomic barriers that hinder adoption. These include limited access to organic inputs, technical knowledge gaps, and financial constraints. Governments and NGOs can play a vital role by supporting training programs, providing composting resources, and promoting extension services that deliver microbe-friendly techniques at the community level. Tailoring solutions to local conditions and traditions will ensure higher adoption rates and better outcomes. Long-term studies are needed to explore the evolution of microbial communities under varying farming practices and environmental conditions.

Conclusions

In summary, the productivity and health of backyard gardens and plantain main farms depend on microbial communities abundance and structures. Diverse microbial populations in main farms improve soil health, nutrient availability and uptake, and enhance disease resistance, all of which maximize plantain yields on a broader scale. These microbial communities are essential for both backyard and smallholder farms because they enhance soil fertility and structure, promoting sustainable practices. For SHFs, understanding and leveraging these communities through accessible practices such as composting, intercropping, and organic inputs can provide practical, low-cost solutions to improve yield and resilience. The two environments’ distinct microbial dynamics emphasize the necessity of specialized management techniques that support advantageous microorganisms in order to maintain ecological balance and strong plantain yields. In both situations, putting an emphasis on microbial health can result in more resilient agricultural systems, which will eventually promote SSA food security and sustainable livelihoods dependent on plantains. Furthermore, supporting microbial diversity is essential not only for short-term productivity but also for long-term ecological sustainability, as it fosters healthy soils, supports biodiversity and builds resilience against climate change.
