Walking a tightrope – A multitude of airway challenges in a mediastinal mass excision
Authors: S Gajalakshmi, Shreya B. Shah, Vishesh Jain, Jidin Joy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Providing a satisfactory lung isolation during the video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery in infants can be challenging due to the limited options for one lung ventilation. Additional anatomic pathologies/derangements add to the complexity in managing the airway in these tiny infants. We discuss a case of a 10-month infant scheduled for video-assisted excision of a recurrent bronchogenic cyst that was in proximity to recurrent laryngeal nerves, compressing the trachea and bronchus. The associated severe tracheo-bronchomalacia and the risk of an inadvertent recurrent laryngeal nerve injury demanded a need to exercise caution and necessitated a personalised approach to aid surgery. This article aims to outline the several airway challenges encountered intraoperatively in this infant and thereby contribute to the literature on the management of such cases. While it is imperative to tailor the management strategies to individual case, we endeavour to navigate the intricate complexities and ensure optimal patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Bronchogenic cysts are benign congenital developmental abnormalities that account for 7.5% of mediastinal masses in children.[1] They predominantly originate in the middle and posterior mediastinum and are a rare cause of respiratory distress in children.[12] Excision of a bronchogenic cyst may have significant anaesthetic implications because of the potential for a complete airway obstruction.[12] We hereby describe the intraoperative management of an infant with a recurrent bronchogenic cyst scheduled for a thoracoscopic excision. By exploring the challenges encountered, this case aims to comprehensively address the key airway issues, during the intraoperative management of an infant with a bronchogenic mediastinal cyst.

CASE REPORT

A 10-month infant, 8 kg presented with noisy breathing since birth. Infant was diagnosed with a left bronchogenic cyst for that he underwent cyst excision at 4.5 months. Subsequently following a brief resolution of symptoms, he redeveloped respiratory distress with stridor. On examination, he was tachypnoeic with a respiratory rate of 60/minute, displaying suprasternal retractions. The air entry was decreased on the left. His saturation was 95% when supplemented with oxygen @ 4L/min. Blood chemistry was normal. Chest contrast enhanced computed tomography revealed a superior middle mediastinal cyst (3.7 cm × 3.4 cm × 3.5 cm), displacing the trachea and carina to the right [Figure 1a]. Preoperative bronchoscopy documented severe tracheomalacia, involving 90% of the trachea [Figure 1b]. The cyst was in proximity to recurrent laryngeal nerves. A thoracoscopic excision of the cyst with an intraoperative bronchoscopy to aid in tracheopexy was planned under nerve monitoring. Informed parental consent was obtained for the surgery and its publication.

(a) Preoperative radiography and (b) bronchoscopy demonstrating compromised tracheal lumen

In the operation theatre, standard ASA monitoring was employed. Anaesthesia was induced with 15 μg inj. fentanyl and 20 mg inj. propofol (titrated) intravenously. After confirming ventilation, inj. atracurium 5 mg was administered intravenously. Para-oxygenation was instituted via nasal prongs. A 4.0 mm uncuffed endotracheal tube (ETT) was used as a conduit to facilitate the placement of a 3-Fr Fogarty catheter into the right bronchus using a C-MAC video laryngoscope. The catheter was inflated after confirming its position with a flexible bronchoscope.

To enable recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) monitoring, an adult electromyography adhesive laryngeal electrode (Inomed, Germany compatible with NIM‐ECLIPSE® System, Medtronic, USA) was trimmed by the neuro-physiologist and wrapped around a 3.5-mm cuffed ETT [Figure 2a]. The trachea was intubated in parallel with the Fogarty catheter. However, due to the air leak, the ETT was replaced with a refashioned 4.0 mm cuffed ETT. The radial artery was cannulated and infant was placed in a left decubitus position.

(a) Trimmed adhesive adult laryngeal electrode wrapped around the standard endotracheal tube, (b) Tracheal rent over malacic segment with the exposed Fogarty catheter

Anaesthesia was maintained with isoflurane and the patient was ventilated using lung protective strategy. Total intravenous anaesthesia was utilised through the duration of intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM). Monitoring signals aided tracing and preserving bilateral RLN. The fragile tracheal mucosa over the malacic segment was tearing apart despite suturing the rent [Figure 2b]. Hence, manual ventilation had to be deployed multiple times to tide over the air leak. However, the rent was successfully repaired. The cyst compressing the posterior lower end of trachea and carina was excised in toto. To facilitate the paediatric pulmonologist in delineating the areas of tracheo-bronchomalacia by flexible bronchoscopy, the ETT was withdrawn in the lateral position using the video laryngoscope and subsequently repositioned. Tracheopexy was done over the lower end of trachea.

Analgesia was supplemented with intravenous fentanyl, morphine, and paracetamol. On completion of surgery, bilateral ventilation was resumed. The infant was transferred to the paediatric intensive care unit and trachea was extubated on postoperative day (POD) 1. The patient was discharged uneventfully on POD 4.

DISCUSSION

Acknowledging and addressing the challenges in the perioperative management of a mediastinal mass is crucial. Cardiorespiratory collapse is a critical complication during the anaesthesia for excision of mediastinal cyst, causing significant morbidity and mortality.[12] Through this report the authors brief the readers to a plethora of intraoperative airway challenges that can be encountered in patients with a mediastinal mass and the management thereof. Such issues have never been conversed prior in the anaesthesia literature.

The symptoms, preoperative bronchoscopy and radiographic findings in our patient suggested a possibility of an intraoperative airway obstruction and a need to exercise caution. Stridor and a tracheal cross-sectional area <30%–50% of normal with associated bronchial compression are strong predictors of a total airway obstruction during general anaesthesia in children with a mediastinal mass.[345]

Considering the proximity of the cyst to the RLN in a revision surgery, there was a potential risk to RLN. Furthermore, the associated tracheomalacia would necessitate tracheopexy. Hence as a plan of the intraoperative strategy, neurophysiologist and paediatric pulmonologist were involved to incorporate IONM and bronchoscopy, respectively. An effective multidisciplinary preoperative planning and shared decision-making can help address the unforeseen perioperative challenges, reducing the likelihood of complications.

A stepwise induction to general anaesthesia is imperative in patients with mediastinal cysts. Infant was induced with titrated propofol, followed by muscle paralysis and positive pressure ventilation (PPV), with strict vigilance. A skilled surgeon with a rigid bronchoscope was available to support the airway; should an untoward airway compromise occur. Historically, maintenance of spontaneous ventilation and avoidance of paralysis were advocated to avoid complete airway obstruction in a patient with central airway obstruction.[267] The new understanding of the mechanics of airway obstruction and the effects of anaesthesia and mechanical ventilation in this patient cohort refute the fear of intravenous induction with muscle relaxation.[89] Furthermore, PPV and positive end-expiratory pressure improves the airway calibre and airflow in this population.[9]

Securing airway, involving lung isolation in our infant with a compromised airway posed challenges. Due to the limited lung isolation devices in infants, we guided the flimsy Fogarty catheter through the ETT for easy placement; the position of that was confirmed by bronchoscopy. As age-appropriate endotracheal tube with an integrated surface electrode was not available, a trimmed adult adhesive electrode was manually placed onto the desired standard ETT.

IONM entailed a modification of the airway equipment to cater the paediatric patient, a need to alter the anaesthetic management incorporating total intravenous anaesthesia without muscle paralysis and an effective communication with the surgical team. The neurophysiologist verified the nerve function, providing real-time-feedback to the surgeons. However, challenges inherent to the adhesive electrodes deserve a mention. Improper placement of the electrode and slippage of an adhesive electrode with prolonged exposure to airway secretions can result in a false negative response resulting in unexpected postoperative nerve complications.[10] Drawbacks include an increased stiffness and outer diameter of ETT imparting resistance and causing laryngeal trauma in children.[11]

The associated tracheo-bronchomalacia was another concern. Maintaining adequate ventilation intermittently through the duration of air leak was critical. The confirmation of laryngo-tracheomalacia by bronchoscopy required timed spontaneous respiratory efforts to dynamically identify the areas of collapse. Moreover, the withdrawal and repositioning of ETT in lateral operative position in a tiny infant was crucial. An effective communication with the pulmonologist was essential to ensure adequate oxygenation while managing the shared airway.

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has a role in the airway management of patients with subglottic obstruction and severe symptoms or tracheal diameter <5 mm.[12] Preoperatively patients with mediastinal masses at a high risk of respiratory and/or hemodynamic collapse who could benefit from extracorporeal support should be identified. Cardiopulmonary bypass, venoarterial or venovenous ECMO should be instituted not as a rescue therapy but incorporated as a crucial component of the anaesthesia strategy.[12]

CONCLUSION

Institution and management of one-lung ventilation in an infant with a compromised airway, utilisation of a tailored airway conduit with an alteration in the anaesthesia management to aid preservation of RLN, assistance in identifying areas of Malacia were the milestones during anaesthesia management of this case. Understanding the relation of a mediastinal mass to vital structures and the degree of tracheobronchial and cardiovascular compression, careful preoperative assessment, preoperative multidisciplinary discussions, meticulous anaesthesia management and well-established protocols for sudden cardiorespiratory compromise are pivotal in successful excision of mediastinal cysts.
