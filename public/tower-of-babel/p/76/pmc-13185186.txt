Introducing the Diathesis-Compassion Layered Care Model (DCLCM): an IPA study of dissociation and complex trauma in eating disorders in Iran
Authors: Devendra Singh, Mohadeseh Bita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEating disorders (EDs) frequently co-occur with trauma-related symptomatology, including dissociative experiences. While international research has examined associations between EDs and trauma frameworks such as the dissociative subtype of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder and Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, little is known about how therapists in non-Western contexts conceptualize the trauma-dissociation-ED linkage or integrate trauma-informed and compassion-focused approaches in practice.MethodInterpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) was applied to explore Iranian therapists’ lived experiences in managing ED presentations involving dissociation comorbidity, with a focus on the therapeutic role of self-compassion. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with three therapists working with adolescents and young adults.ResultsAnalysis generated 11 superordinate and 58 subordinate themes. Findings were organized in five interlinked layers: Symptom Recognition, describing multifaceted ED presentations with dissociative features such as trance-like eating, emotional numbing, depersonalization, and identity disruption; Trauma and Vulnerability, highlighting early relational adversity, chronic invalidation, and dispositional traits including perfectionism and low self-esteem; Cultural and Social Context, reflecting body-surveillance norms, gendered expectations, family hierarchy, and stigma; Therapeutic interventions, capturing phased, safety-oriented, integrative treatment strategies; and Self-Compassion as a Transformative Healing Pathway, emphasizing compassion-focused processes for reducing shame and enhancing affect regulation. A Cross Layer Reflective Theme, appraised the utility of stress-vulnerability formulation in structuring case-conceptualisation and psychoeducation within Iranian practice. These themes informed the Diatheses-Compassion Layered Care Model (DCLCM), a five-layer conceptual framework grounded in therapists’ narratives.ConclusionsTherapists frequently interpreted ED presentations involving dissociative features through trauma-informed and diatheses-stress lens. DCLCM offers a culturally embedded conceptual framework that integrates symptom recognition, trauma and vulnerability pathways, sociocultural context, therapeutic sequencing, and self-compassion processes. The model is intended to organize clinical reasoning in culturally sensitive settings and requires empirical evaluation in future research.

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) commonly co-occur with trauma-related conditions, especially PTSD, its dissociative subtype (DPTSD), and Complex PTSD (CPTSD), as many individuals with EDs have histories of developmental trauma, neglect, invalidation, and chronic relational stress that contribute to emotional dysregulation, identity disturbance, and interpersonal difficulties [1–6]. These developmental pathways are particularly relevant to DPTSD and CPTSD, which both emphasise dissociation, relational wounds, and emotion regulation problems-patterns commonly observed in ED presentations.

In recent years, the prevalence of EDs and dissociative symptoms among adolescents and young adults has shown a notable increase globally, including in Iran [7–16]. Clinical evidence suggests a significant overlap between EDs and dissociation, especially among individuals exposed to early trauma, chronic stress, and psychological vulnerability [17–23].

Considering this high comorbidity and its links to early trauma, the diathesis–stress model provides a robust conceptual lens for examining how inherent predispositions interact with life stressors to produce psychopathology, while self-compassion is emerging as a promising therapeutic approach to buffer against the effects of trauma and shame [24–28].

Previous research concluded that self-compassion is linked to a lower incidence of ED psychopathology and can be considered as an intervention for disordered eating prevention [29, 30]. Another research from Iran asserted that compassion-based treatment can effectively reduce symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress in clients suffering from binge eating disorders [31]. However, little is known about how therapists in non-Western settings include trauma history, psychological vulnerabilities, and therapeutic tools such as self-compassion into the treatment of EDs with dissociation. Current studies from Iran rarely reflect therapists' lived clinical experiences, and they haven’t distilled these ideas into a practical, culturally relevant framework. This study seeks to address this knowledge gap by investigating how Iranian therapists use self-compassion-based therapies, understood via the diathesis–stress lens, to comprehend and manage the co-occurrence of EDs, dissociation, and trauma. The central research question for this research is—how do therapists understand and manage the co-occurrence of eating disorders, dissociation, and trauma in adolescents and young adults through therapeutic interventions grounded in self-compassion?

This study uses Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA) to develop culturally grounded insights. It provides a layered conceptual model that links trauma, vulnerability, and therapeutic options, providing advice for clinical practice and culturally sensitive treatment. The study culminates in the development of the Diathesis-Compassion Layered Care Model (DCLCM), a comprehensive five-layer framework encompassing symptom identification, trauma and susceptibility, sociocultural dimensions, therapeutic modalities, and self-compassion as a pivotal avenue for healing. By prioritizing the lived clinical experiences of therapists, the research enriches the theoretical discourse surrounding the diathesis–stress model, broadens the applicability of self-compassion in non-Western contexts, and provides practical strategies for enhancing therapeutic outcomes for adolescents and young adults with comorbidity of EDs and dissociation.

Materials and methods

Study context

The study participants were recruited from the hospital and universities of Iran.

Sample

IPA aims to elucidate how individuals interpret both their personal and societal encounters [32]. Since IPA places a strong emphasis on in-depth idiographic inquiry, a sample size of three to six individuals is usually advised for qualitative rigor since bigger numbers run the risk of diluting the analysis's richness. To ensure the depth of research, this study utilised a small, purposeful sample in accordance with these recommendations [33]. In this study, a purposive sampling technique was employed to select therapists based on specific inclusion/exclusion criteria, ensuring they possessed pertinent expertise in the management of eating disorders (EDs) within the context of Iran. The therapists selected for this inquiry were licensed mental health practitioners, each with a minimum of 5 years of clinical experience, actively working in Iran. They possessed first-hand experience in diagnosing or treating EDs and were qualified to engage in an in-depth interview conducted in Farsi. The second author facilitated the interviews, meticulously recording even the most subtle details and transcribing the responses, which were subsequently translated into English with utmost care to preserve the original meaning. Participants lacking relevant clinical qualifications, those facing disciplinary actions, or individuals who declined to provide informed consent were excluded from the study. This purposive sampling strategy ensured that the participants could offer knowledgeable, experience-based perspectives pertinent to goals of the research.

Participants

P1 is a psychiatrist specializing in neuropsychiatry, with over 10 years of clinical experience. His primary areas of expertise include eating disorders, anxiety disorders, and psychotic disorders. He employs Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy (CBT) as the foundation of his therapeutic approach, integrating evidence-based techniques to achieve effective and sustainable outcomes. Throughout his career, he has worked in a variety of clinical and hospital environments, where he emphasizes a biopsychosocial understanding of mental health and applies neuropsychiatric perspectives to provide individualized, holistic care.

P2 is a female clinical neuropsychologist from Iran with 18 years of professional experience in treating neurological and psychological disorders in children and adults. After 8 years of clinical practice using a CBT approach, she pursued a Ph.D. in cognitive psychology and shifted her therapeutic approach to neuropsychological and Cognitive Rehabilitation Therapy. Her therapeutic interests are paying attention to the cognitive processes underlying behaviour, executive functions and inhibitory control deficits in neurodevelopmental disorders and impulsive behaviours. In addition to her clinical and research activities, she has been serving as a full-time faculty member at a university in Iran for 7 years.

P3 is a psychologist with 7 years of clinical and research experience, specializing in identity, attachment, and ReAttach Therapy. Her professional focus extends to individuals struggling with EDs, anxiety disorders, and personality-related difficulties. She adopts an integrative and evidence-informed therapeutic approach, drawing from CBT, attachment-based interventions, and ReAttach Therapy to foster emotional regulation, self-concept development, and interpersonal growth. Alongside her clinical work, she is actively involved in research on identity formation, attachment dynamics, and therapeutic innovations that bridge psychological theory with practical mental health care.

Process of data collecting and analysis

This research aimed to explore the lived experiences of therapists from Iran in treating EDs. This research effort operated from May–July 2025. All three therapists met the inclusion criteria and gave written informed consent. Two to three interviews with three participants were held in Farsi, each lasting 45 to 60 min. The interviews, which included a semi-structured questionnaire with 13 questions, were performed by the second researcher. The therapists' knowledge and personal experiences with treating EDs in patients, as well as their coping strategies, relationships and support, therapeutic techniques, and the healing process, were all discussed. The second researcher’s laptop was used to record and safely save the interviews. The first researcher created a customized, semi-structured interview schedule in order to answer the study questions. Sample of interview questions

The interviews were manually typed and analyzed using the IPA methodology provided by Smith et al. [33] All interviews were transcribed verbatim, preserving both participant and interviewer speech, including pauses, hesitations, and significant nonverbal expressions. During the analysis, the researcher translated significant portions from the interviews and verified their veracity. The analytical procedure began with several readings of each transcript to gain a general comprehension, followed by exploratory note-taking. A free-text analysis approach was used, with an emphasis on meaningful content, language use, emotional tone, and contextual elements. Emerging themes were identified from these notes and conceptually organized based on patterns of resemblance and meaning. The author developed initial codes and themes, which were then re-examined to improve validity and qualitative rigor. This approach was repeated methodically for each participant to ensure that their unique living experiences were fully documented. The emergent themes across cases were then classified into superordinate clusters based on their conceptual interrelationships. These clusters were polished into a coherent thematic framework, with verbatim quotations from participants serving as evidential anchors for the final interpretations.

It is important to clarify that formal diagnoses of PTSD (ICD 11 6840) or CPTSD (ICD-11 6841) were not independently assessed within this study. References to PTSD, CPTSD, or dissociative presentations reflect therapists’ clinical impressions and are used in this research as interpretative frameworks rather than confirmed diagnostic determinations. Accordingly, trauma related constructs are applied as heuristic lenses to contextualize therapists’ observations rather than as verified categorical diagnoses.

Ensuring data reliability

Participants were invited to offer concrete instances from their experiences, and follow-up questions were utilized to clarify any ambiguity. Following the interview, researchers carefully examined the data, discussing and comparing notes to establish a thorough, interpretative understanding—a process known as the second hermeneutic.

Discussion

This study applied IPA to examine how Iranian therapists understand and manage the co-occurrence of EDs, dissociation, and trauma, and how they integrate self-compassion within their therapeutic practice. Positioned within a sociocultural context where family dynamics, gendered expectations, and stigma shape emotional expression, the findings illuminate the complex mechanisms underlying ED-dissociation comorbidity in Iranian adolescents and young adults. The analysis generated eleven superordinate themes and fifty-eight subordinate themes, which together demonstrate how trauma histories, cultural stressors, psychological vulnerabilities, and therapeutic processes interact in shaping clinical presentations and informing treatment.

The discussion is organised around the four research questions guiding the study, followed by an integrative interpretation that positions the Diathesis-Compassion Layered Care Model (DCLCM) as a theoretically and culturally grounded framework for ED-dissociation comorbidity. The DCLCM represents a clinically grounded conceptual framework derived from therapist narratives rather than a manualized or empirically validated intervention protocol. It is intended to organize clinical reasoning and guide future empirical investigation.

Key findings by research question

RQ1—Patterns of comorbidity

Therapists identified a spectrum of EDs—Anorexia Nervosa, Bulimia Nervosa, Binge Eating Disorder, OSFED/EDNO—with gender differences (thinness in girls vs. muscularity in boys). These disorders were frequently accompanied by dissociative symptoms such as emotional numbing, depersonalization, amnesia, and trance-like eating. These clinical patterns directly reflect mechanisms described in CPTSD, where dissociation serves to modulate overwhelming affect and maintain psychological distance from traumatic material [34]. Subthemes also highlighted recurring psychological dynamics of perfectionism, shame, emotional dysregulation, and cultural pressures unique to Iran—that mirror core CPTSD symptoms. These findings align with global evidence connecting EDs with dissociation, particularly in trauma-exposed populations [18, 23].

RQ2—Trauma and predispositions (CPTSD pathways)

All participants unanimously emphasized the central role of early trauma (neglect, body-shaming, abuse, bullying, coerced eating) and predisposing traits (low self-esteem, perfectionism, insecure attachment) as risk factors in shaping ED-dissociation comorbidity. These early relational experiences produced chronic shame, threat sensitivity, and impaired emotion regulation mechanisms that map closely onto CPTSD pathways [2, 5]. Vulnerability traits such as perfectionism, anxiety sensitivity, low self-worth, and insecure attachment were seen as diatheses that intensified the impact of environmental stressors. The therapists’ accounts resonate with the diathesis–stress model, in which early vulnerabilities interact with external pressures to generate psychological risk.

Dissociation emerged as an acquired coping strategy in high-stress contexts. In line with findings from cross-cultural ED research, therapists highlighted how Iranian cultural elements such as stigma, gendered beauty expectations, and familial invalidation, instigate vulnerabilities [11, 13]. These findings support the diathesis–stress lens—vulnerabilities act as diathesis, activated by stressors, producing ED-dissociation comorbidity.

The therapists’ emphasis on shame is especially notable. Shame appeared not only as an emotional consequence of trauma but also as a culturally reinforced affect, amplified by gendered expectations, familial honour norms, and societal scrutiny of the female body in Iran. This suggests that shame is both a universal consequence of trauma and a culturally intensified emotional schema shaping ED trajectories.

RQ3—Diathesis–stress model application

Therapists described the diathesis–stress model as a useful conceptual anchor for psychoeducation, case formulation, and treatment planning. It provided a shared language through which clinicians could connect predispositions (e.g., perfectionism, low self-esteem, insecure attachment) to stressors (e.g., family criticism, academic pressure, sociocultural demands). The model also helped engage families by reducing moral blame and reframing ED behaviours as responses to stress acting upon vulnerable systems.

However, because Western models sometimes overlook culturally specific risk variables, such as family honour and collectivist identity constraints, they emphasize the necessity of cultural extensions. Specifically, family dynamics, sociocultural expectations, and systemic barriers require integration into the model for Iran. Themes reflected case formulation, phased treatment sequencing, grounding, schema work, family engagement, and relapse prevention, adapted to stress–vulnerability interactions. In Asian and Middle Eastern cultures, similar arguments have been made for trauma models that are sensitive to cultural differences [35–37].

RQ4—Role of self-compassion

Self-compassion emerged as both clinical technique and therapeutic outcome. It is seen by therapists as a way to combat feelings of guilt and self-criticism, which promotes resilience and treatment participation. Many clients later reported better emotional control and a lower chance of relapse, despite their initial resistance to self-compassion since it was culturally associated with "self-indulgence." Therapists described it as a protective buffer against trauma’s long-term effects. This is consistent with other research that showed compassion-based therapies reduced ED pathology and trauma-related distress [31, 38–42]. In this study, self-compassion functioned as both a treatment tool and a resilience factor, moderating the impact of stress on vulnerable individuals and supporting long-term recovery. This positions self-compassion as a crucial element within a culturally adapted diathesis–stress framework.

Consistent with existing literature, therapists’ accounts reflected affect dysregulation, chronic shame, identity disturbance, and relational insecurity frequently associated with CPTSD, alongside dissociative features observed in the dissociative subtype of PTSD. These presentations were embedded in developmental adversity and prolonged interpersonal stress.

Within this context, the DCLCM provides a structures, culturally contextualized framework for conceptualising EDs co-occurring with trauma related symptomology. Rather than proposing new diagnostic categories, the model organises therapists’ clinical insights into a layered roadmap that clarifies how vulnerability, dissociation, culture, and compassion-based regulation interact in practice.

Layer 1—Symptom recognition (surface layer)

This foundational layer emphasizes on identifying ED presentations alongside dissociative features and diagnostic complexity that goes beyond Western diagnostic instruments tailored to Iranian cultural manifestations. It starts with the accurate identification of the common dissociative characteristics and ED phenotypes that clinicians may overlook if they just use Western symptom checklists. Therapists reported a wide spectrum of ED symptoms co-occurring with dissociation, including emotional numbing, depersonalization, derealization, identity fragmentation, and trance-like eating episodes. They also highlighted diagnostic ambiguity, particularly when dissociation obscured affect or mimicked loss of control. These clinical patterns are conceptually consistent with mechanisms described in the CPTSD literature, particularly those involving affect dysregulation and dissociative modulation of distress [43, 44].

Subclinical presentations—such as body shame, obsessive body checking, muscularity concerns in males, and fluctuating identity states—further demonstrate the heterogeneity of ED–dissociation comorbidity. Dissociation (e.g., depersonalization, compartmentalization), which is frequently exacerbated by shame, is linked to eating disorders (EDs); Iranian studies demonstrate a significant ED burden across sexes and ages, including subclinical body-image issues [11, 13, 45, 46].

Layer 2—Trauma & vulnerability pathways

Layer 2 situates EDs and dissociative presentations within developmental adversity and enduring psychological vulnerabilities. Across Superordinate Themes 6 and 9, therapists described early relational stressors—emotional neglect, parental criticism, bullying, coerced eating, exposure to conflict, and abuse—as shaping difficulties in affect regulation, self-concept, and inter personal security. These adversities were understood to leave adolescents vulnerable to shame, self-doubt, and heightened sensitivity to stress. In addition to early experiences, therapists identified recurring dispositional patterns, including perfectionism, low self-esteem, anxiety sensitivity, and insecure attachment. These traits were perceived to intensify responses to environmental pressures and relational invalidation. Dissociation was consistently described as a coping response to overwhelming affect, initially serving a protective function but later becoming intertwined with disordered eating behaviours.

Therapists perceived that persistent early stress and relational adversity increased vulnerability to EDs and dissociation Traits such as perfectionism and poor self-esteem were described as increasing this vulnerability within the diathesis–stress lens. Dissociation occurs as a common coping strategy for overwhelming affect [47–50].

Taken together, this layer reflects a trauma-vulnerability formulation consistent with clinical descriptions of CPTSD, particularly in relation to affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relational disturbance. Rather than positioning, trauma as a singular cause, the DCLCM conceptualizes the ED-Dissociation comorbidity as emerging from the interaction between early adversity and enduring vulnerabilities within specific relational contexts.

Layer 3—Cultural & social context (contextual layer)

Layer 3 reflects the five sociocultural dimensions identified in Superordinate theme 3 that shape the manifestation of EDs and trauma-related dissociation in Iran. Therapists described rigid appearance norms and body ideals, alongside gendered expectations regarding modesty, self-control, and marital suitability, as amplifying body dissatisfaction and shame. Academic and achievement pressures further reinforced perfectionistic striving and fear of failure. Mental health stigma was reported to constrain emotional disclosure, particularly in relation to trauma, while strong family hierarchy and concerns about reputation often limited autonomy and open communication. Within such relational systems, distress was frequently internalised and expressed through eating bahaviours rather than articulated verbally. Positive body-image study conducted in Iran highlights culturally unique dynamics, whereas Iranian data highlights important sociocultural amplifiers (appearance norms, stigma, and gendered expectations) that influence symptom manifestation and help-seeking. Family involvement and culturally appropriate terminology, metaphors, and behaviours are necessary for effective treatment [11, 51].

This layer conceptualizes sociocultural forces as active contextual amplifiers that interact with developmental vulnerabilities (Layer 2), shaping how trauma-related distress becomes embodied and sustained within ED presentation.

While affect dysregulation, shame, and dissociation are widely documented in global trauma literature, the present findings highlight culturally specific amplifiers within Iranian context, including marital expectations, hierarchical family structures, and stigma-related emotional expression. These contextual dynamics appear to intensify and shape the expression of trauma-related mechanisms rather than fundamentally altering their underlying psychological structure.

Layer 4—Therapeutic interventions (clinical layer)

Layer 4 synthesizes Superordinate themes 7 and 10, reflecting both the relational stance and the adaptive, mechanism-focused strategies therapists’ employ in treating trauma-embedded EDs. Across accounts, therapists emphasized the primacy of safety, therapeutic alliance, and emotional stabilization before engaging in trauma focused work. Their narratives aligned with phased treatment sequencing (stabilization → processing → consolidation), with ongoing monitoring of dissociation and affect tolerance throughout intervention [52–58].

Interventions were described as integrative rather than protocol-bound ED-specific approaches (e.g., CBT-E and family base therapy) were combined with trauma-informed techniques, schema-focused interventions for entrenched shame and perfectionism, structured emotional regulation training, and graded exposure to traumatic material. Psychoeducation for adolescents and families, iterative case formulation refinement, and relapse prevention planning were also highlighted as core components of sustained recovery. Therapists described actively managing resistance and ambivalence, particularly when dissociation or avoidance intensified during trauma work. Family engagement was framed not only as support but as a systemic intervention addressing criticism, communication patterns, and relational invalidation. Cultural adaptation in therapeutic language and framing was used to enhance engagement and reduce stigma, referring contextual considerations described in Layer 3. Across accounts, therapists emphasized the need to flexibly balance trauma processing with symptom containment to prevent premature exposure that could worsen dissociation or emotional dysregulation.

This layer conceptualizes treatment as a mechanism driven rather than modality-specific sequenced according to safety, developmental vulnerabilities, dissociative risk and relational context.

Layer 5—Self-compassion as a transformative healing pathway (transformative layer)

Layer 5 integrates Superordinate themes 5 and 8, positioning self-compassion as a central regulatory and restorative mechanism within trauma-embedded EDs. Therapists described introducing self-compassion gradually to counter entrenched shame, harsh self-criticism, and identity fragmentation. Fundamentally, self-compassion is presented as a transformational therapeutic approach that fosters resilience, regulation, and healing while reducing internalized stigma and shame. Self-compassion/compassion-focused methods may be offered in adjunct or independent formats, including Iranian settings, and a growing corpus of research and meta-analyses supports their ability to reduce shame and ED symptoms. Although barriers (such as “self-indulgence” ideas) have been observed, they usually become less severe with psychoeducation and guided practice [42, 59–63].

Across accounts, self-compassion was perceived to enhance affect regulation, reduce internalised stigma, and soften perfectionist standards that perpetuate disordered eating cycles. Therapists noted improvements in emotional tolerance, decreased dissociative reactivity, and greater capacity for self-loathing when compassion based strategies were integrated into treatment. Rather than functioning as a standalone modality, self-compassion was conceptualised as a cross-cutting process that strengthened earlier therapeutic work by stabilising self-concept and interrupting shame-avoidance patterns.

Within the DCLCM, self-compassion represents the transformative layer that consolidates recovery by fostering resilience, relational security, and sustainable emotional regulation in culturally sensitive ways.

Cross-layer reflective theme

In addition to the 10 Superordinate themes, Superordinate theme 11 emerged as one cross-layer reflective theme, capturing therapists’ meta-clinical appraisal of the framework’s practical utility.

Superordinate theme 11 functioned as a meta-clinical reflection on the utility of the diathesis–stress framework with Iranian practice. Therapists described stress-vulnerability formulations as helpful for structuring case conceptualization, organizing symptom development, and facilitating psychoeducation with adolescents and families. The model was perceived to reduce blame by contextualizing EDs and dissociation within interactions between developmental adversity and ongoing stressors. At the same time, therapists acknowledged contextual constraints, including cultural translational challenges, stigma, and resource limitations, which influenced how the framework could be applied in routine practice. Within the DCLCM, this reflective component operates across all five layers, guiding clinical reasoning while remaining adaptable to sociocultural realities.

Clinically, the findings underscore the importance of trauma-informed, culturally attuned approaches that integrate family engagement and compassion focused strategies. Training programs may benefit from incorporating trauma and self-compassion frameworks adapted for collectivist contexts. At a policy level, stigma reduction initiatives and early identification strategies may support timely intervention for EDs and trauma related-distress.

Future investigations should empirically validate the Diathesis-Compassion Layered Care Model (DCLCM) in clinical settings, initiating with pilot studies that assess feasibility, acceptability, and outcomes. A promising direction involves re-contextualizing self-compassion practices to align them with Iranian cultural and religious principles, thereby potentially enhancing acceptance and diminishing resistance among clients. Furthermore, the model ought to be expanded to encompass diverse demographic groups, including males, rural populations, and individuals with trauma-related conditions beyond eating disorders, to evaluate its broader applicability. Longitudinal and mixed-methods research can further enhance the model, providing a culturally attuned framework that reconciles global evidence with local therapeutic practices.

Limitations

As with all IPA studies, the small, purposive sample prioritizes depth over breadth, limiting generalizability. All participants were trained in CBT or integrative approaches, which may shape their interpretive frameworks. Data were based on therapists’ perceptions rather than direct patient accounts, and translation from Farsi may have introduced nuances affecting interpretation. Additionally, systemic constraints in Iran—such as stigma, late referrals, and limited assessment tools—may have influenced therapists’ perspectives.

Additionally, trauma-related diagnoses were not independently verified through standardized assessment instruments, and interpretations regarding PTSD or CPTSD reflect therapists’ perceptions rather than formal diagnostic confirmation. The reliance on clinical accounts rather than formal diagnostic confirmation. The reliance on therapist accounts rather than adolescent interviews may limit insight into lived experiences. As an IPA study, findings represent interpretative constructions shaped by both participant narratives and researcher analysis. Finally, the DCLCM remains a conceptual synthesis and requires empirical validation before broader clinical generalization.

Conclusion

The study explored how Iranian therapists understand and treat the intersection of eating disorders, dissociation, and trauma, revealing that ED presentations in Iran are deeply shaped by early relational trauma, chronic invalidation, shame, and sociocultural pressures. Dissociation was frequently described as a central coping strategy rather than merely a secondary feature of EDs. ED presentations described by therapists can be meaningfully interpreted through trauma-informed frameworks, including those addressing complex trauma and dissociative processes.

Cultural factors—including gendered beauty norms, family hierarchy, emotional stigma, and academic pressure—further intensified vulnerabilities and influenced both symptom development and treatment engagement. Therapists identified self-compassion as a transformative yet culturally sensitive therapeutic pathway that reduced shame, softened self-criticism, improved emotional regulation, and stabilized dissociative responses. Although clients initially resisted compassion-based practices, these interventions became effective when framed in culturally congruent language, supporting their role as resilience-building mechanisms within ED–trauma treatment.

As one of the first IPA studies exploring therapists’ perspectives on ED-dissociation comorbidity in Iran, the research identified 11 superordinate themes and 58 subordinate themes that revealed complex interactions among symptom patterns, trauma-related vulnerabilities, sociocultural factors, therapeutic challenges, and the healing potential of self-compassion. Based on these insights, the study introduced the Diathesis-Compassion Layered Care Model (DCLCM), a culturally grounded framework integrating symptom recognition, trauma pathways, sociocultural influences, tailored interventions, and self-compassion.

The model advances existing ED-trauma frameworks by integrating dissociative processes and culturally embedded dynamics within Middle Eastern clinical contexts. Future research should empirically validate the model, include patient perspectives, and examine long-term outcomes, positioning the DCLCM as a valuable guide for trauma-informed, culturally sensitive ED care in Iran and similar settings.
