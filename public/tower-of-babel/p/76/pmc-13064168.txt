Combined predictive model for prostate cancer screening: Development and validation study
Authors: Yu Li, Fang Yang, Xuebin Liu, Jiping Luo, Siyu Dan, Xiuli He, Guihao Hu, Ling He, Xiachuan Qin, Tao Wu, Wensheng Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of prostate cancer (PCa) remains challenging, as prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing and digital rectal examination (DRE) offer limited specificity. Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) is routinely used for biopsy guidance, but its diagnostic potential for PCa screening is underexplored. We aimed to evaluate TRUS-derived morphological features and develop a nomogram that integrates clinical and TRUS characteristics to improve PCa risk stratification.MethodsConsecutive patients with suspected PCa were enrolled from two tertiary centers (training cohort: n = 154, October 2021–January 2023; validation cohort: n = 51, December 2021–June 2022). Demographic data, laboratory-derived PSA indices (including PSA density), and TRUS parameters (independently assessed by two blinded sonographers) were collected and analyzed. A predictive nomogram was constructed using multivariate logistic regression and externally validated.ResultsIn the training cohort (mean age 70.9 ± 8.0 years; 72 PCa, 82 benign), independent predictors of PCa included elevated PSA density (OR=3.86, 95 % CI: 1.30–11.40, P = 0.015), abnormal DRE (OR=3.06, 95 % CI: 1.09–8.60, P = 0.034), TRUS-defined ill-defined zone boundaries (OR=9.61, 95 % CI: 3.37–39.02, P = 0.002), and hyper-enhancement (OR=7.07, 95 % CI: 2.69–21.89, P < 0.001). The nomogram achieved strong discrimination (training C-index=0.933, 95 % CI: 0.881–0.986; validation C-index=0.907, 95 % CI: 0.792–0.970) with 84.7 % sensitivity, 87.8 % specificity, and 86.4 % accuracy. Pathological concordance was high (kappa=0.726).ConclusionTRUS-derived features (ill-defined zones, hyper-enhancement) significantly enhance PCa detection when combined with clinical parameters. Our nomogram provides a practical, visual tool to guide biopsy decisions and demonstrates robust performance across cohorts.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Prostate-specific antigen, Digital rectal examination, Transrectal ultrasound, Nomogram, Screening

Background

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most prevalent malignancies among men and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1], [2]. PCa induces a variety of physiological, tissue, and molecular changes, including elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, the formation of abnormal masses, increased tissue hardness, neovascularization, and altered blood flow patterns [3], [4]. These changes provide crucial information for the early detection, diagnosis, and treatment of PCa. The standard clinical approach for screening involves PSA testing combined with digital rectal examination (DRE), followed by transrectal ultrasound (TRUS) guided prostate biopsy for definitive diagnosis [5], [6]. Although PSA testing is highly sensitive for detecting PCa, its specificity is limited due to the influence of non-neoplastic factors [7]. DRE, which evaluates changes in prostate firmness [8], may lack sensitivity in identifying transition-zone lesions or small, localized foci, potentially leading to missed diagnoses [9]. Prostate biopsies are invasive, can cause discomfort and infection, and may contribute to the overdiagnosis of low-risk PCa. Accurate pre-biopsy screening is essential to reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, minimize healthcare time and costs, and improve the overall diagnostic efficiency [10].

The limitations of single clinical indicators in both sensitivity and specificity have prompted the exploration of diagnostic methods that combine multiple parameters. Several studies have developed comprehensive nomograms incorporating clinical factors, biomarkers, and multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mp-MRI) for PCa diagnosis [11], [12], [13]. Compared with single indicators, these integrated nomograms significantly improve screening accuracy and support personalized, data-driven decision-making. However, despite their promise, nomograms face challenges such as high costs, technical complexity, limited data quality, and the need for extensive validation [14]. Thus, there is an urgent need for simplified, precise, and user-friendly integrated models to identify biopsy candidates effectively. TRUS is a widely used, noninvasive, cost-effective, and accessible imaging modality for PCa [15]. It is often used for the initial assessment of prostate lesions and to guide diagnostic and therapeutic procedures [16]. Neovascularization plays a critical role in tumorigenesis and tumor progression, including survival and invasion [17], [18]. Transrectal contrast-enhanced (TR-CEUS), by exploiting the nonlinear oscillation of microbubbles, provides detailed visualization of prostate lesion microvasculature and enhances the distinction between malignant and normal tissue [19]. While TRUS and TR-CEUS have shown promise in PCa diagnosis [20], their integration with clinical parameters for screening purposes remains underexplored. This study aimed to integrate clinical data with TRUS-derived features to construct a practical, comprehensive nomogram for PCa assessment and to evaluate its diagnostic performance and clinical utility in screening candidates for biopsy.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study consecutively enrolled suspected PCa patients from two urology centers. From October 2021 to January 2023, 154 patients were recruited from a tertiary medical center (Center 1) to form the training cohort. Additionally, 51 patients were recruited from another tertiary hospital (Center 2) between December 2021 and June 2022 to form the external validation cohort (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of the study design. PSA: prostate-specific antigen; DRE: digital rectal examination; TRUS: transrectal ultrasound; TR-CEUS: transrectal contrast-enhanced ultrasound.

Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (i) Serum PSA > 10 ng/mL; for those with PSA 4–10 ng/mL, supplementary indices (e.g., free/total PSA ratio, PSA density) were incorporated into clinical decision-making; (ii) presence of at least one suspicious finding on any of the following examinations: DRE, TRUS, or mpMRI; (iii) baseline TRUS and TR-CEUS performed within 2 weeks before biopsy or surgery, with definitive histopathological results and adequate clinical data, including demographic and biochemical analyses. Exclusion criteria were: (i) prior prostate treatments such as transurethral resection or laser therapy; (ii) incomplete clinical or imaging records, or severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease; (iii) allergy to ultrasound contrast agents; and (iv) refusal to undergo TR-CEUS.

Before TRUS examination, patients completed a questionnaire that collected information on occupation (manual labor or intellectual work), dietary habits (mainly light diet, high-fat diet, or high-salt/high-sugar diet), smoking status (yes or no), alcohol consumption (yes or no), history of prostatitis (yes or no), number of children, and history of vasectomy (yes or no). The response rate was defined as the ratio of patients who completed the questionnaire and ultimately participated in the study to the total number of recruits, yielding a response rate of approximately 42 %. Information was recorded in MS Excel 2003. Serum biochemical values were obtained from the patients’ medical records.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB number: 2021ER188–1) and registered with the China Clinical Trial Registration (ChiCTR2400082813). All participants provided written informed consent after being thoroughly informed about the study’s objectives and procedures. Prior to the collection of baseline data and TRUS examinations, comprehensive consent was obtained from the patients or their legal representatives, with signed informed consent explicitly granting permission for the acquisition and analysis of ultrasound images without the need for additional consent.

To safeguard patient privacy, a three-tiered approach was implemented: (1) During recruitment, direct communication with researchers was encouraged to avoid disclosure of participant information. (2) All study materials were anonymized or de-identified. (3) Researchers were instructed not to discuss participant information or medical conditions in public settings and to refrain from publishing any participant-related data online. The study did not involve treatment procedures, and no treatment-related costs were incurred. No images included in the manuscript or supplementary materials are identifiable.

DRE

DRE was performed before TRUS. A hard, nodular texture on palpation was defined as abnormal, whereas a medium or soft texture without nodules was considered normal.

TRUS

All enrolled patients underwent baseline TRUS and TR-CEUS examinations using either the Toshiba Aplio 500 (Tustin, CA; now Canon Medical Systems, USA) with a transrectal intracavitary probe or the Mindray Resona 9 ultrasound system with a biplane intracavitary probe (Mindray Medical Solutions). TR-CEUS was performed after baseline TRUS. Sonazoid (GE Healthcare AS, 16 L/stem) was used as the contrast agent. Patients were advised to void their bladder and fully evacuate their rectum before the procedure. Sagittal plane imaging was performed first, followed by multidirectional observation from right to left. For transverse plane imaging, the probe was slowly and meticulously swept across the prostate, from base to apex. Concurrently, longitudinal scanning was performed from the left lateral to the right lateral aspect. The probe was gently manipulated to minimize discomfort.

Baseline TRUS was used to assess prostate size, morphology (regular or irregular), envelope (complete or incomplete), boundary between the peripheral zone (PZ) and transition zone (TZ) (clearly defined or ill-defined), and the presence and location of lesions. Volumetric measurements were calculated using the formula: Volume = [π × (Lateral Diameter × Anteroposterior Diameter × Superoinferior Diameter)] / 6. Irregular prostate morphology, incomplete envelope, ill-defined TZ–PZ margin, and the presence of lesions in the PZ or diffuse distribution were considered indicators of potential malignancy.

TR-CEUS

Venous access was established in the median cubital vein of the left forearm. The second-generation ultrasound contrast agent, Sonazoid lyophilized powder (GE Healthcare AS, 16 L/stem), was reconstituted with 2 mL of proprietary solvent, as per the manufacturer’s protocol, and gently resuspended to ensure complete dissolution.

In real-time dynamic TRUS mode, the focus was adjusted to the base of the image, the mechanical index was set at 0.2–0.3, and the dynamic range was maintained at 110 dB. The largest section of TRUS showing a suspicious nodule was selected as the viewing plane for comparison in biplane mode. If no significant abnormality was observed, the cross-section with the most abundant blood supply was selected. A bolus dose (0.015 mL/kg) of contrast was injected into the median cubital vein, followed by a rapid flush with 5.0 mL of 0.9 % sodium chloride solution. Timing was initiated simultaneously, and dynamic images were stored for 3 min. When an abnormally enhanced region was observed, it was designated as the region of interest (ROI). In cases of diffuse or nonhomogeneous enhancement, the most strongly enhanced region was selected. The ROI enhancement pattern was recorded as follows: wash-in (yes/no), enhancement intensity (hyper or non-hyper), asymmetric vascular structures (yes/no), contrast agent distribution (yes/no), and wash-out (yes/no). Wash-in, hyperenhancement, heterogeneous contrast distribution, asymmetric vascular structures, and wash-out were considered CEUS-positive features.

Image analysis

Preoperative ultrasound images were processed as separate files by a radiologist. After de-identification, images were randomly numbered. Two experienced imaging radiologists (9 and 6 years of TRUS experience, respectively), blinded to clinical and pathological data, independently reviewed the images. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved.

Biopsy and pathology results

All patients underwent TRUS-guided transperineal prostate biopsy using a 10 +X strategy: systematic 10-core sampling plus 2–3 targeted cores from TRUS-visible lesions or CEUS hyperenhancement foci. After local lidocaine anesthesia, 10 prostate tissue strips were extracted using an automated biopsy gun with an 18 G Magnum needle. Samples were immediately fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, labeled, and numbered for pathological analysis. Two experienced pathologists independently reviewed all biopsy specimens and assigned Gleason scores. Final pathology results were obtained from patients who subsequently underwent radical prostatectomy.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 25 (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (χ¯ ± s) and were compared using independent samples t-test. Skewed variables are expressed as median with interquartile range [M (IQR)] and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed with the chi-square test. Inter-reader agreement was evaluated using weighted k-statistics for qualitative variables. Binary logistic regression (backward stepwise) was performed using pathology results, including variables with P < 0.05. Model fit was tested using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with values closer to 1 indicating better fit. Variables from the regression model were used to construct the nomogram. Receiver operating characteristic curves were created using MedCalc for Windows version 120.0 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium), and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. The C-index and AUC have similar interpretations, with a maximum of 1 indicating perfect discrimination and a minimum of 0.05 indicating random discrimination. Nomograms, calibration plots, and decision curve analyses were generated using R version 4.2.3 and the RMS package (Risk Management Solutions, Inc., Newark, CA). Calibration plots compared predicted probabilities from the nomogram with the actual probability of PCa.

All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of the development and validation cohorts

At Center 1, a total of 347 consecutive patients were enrolled between October 2021 and January 2023. Of these, 145 were excluded because of unavailable TRUS images (n = 73), incomplete questionnaires (n = 57), or a PSA level of 4–10 ng/mL without biopsy (n = 63). Therefore, 154 men (mean age, 70.92 ± 8.01 years; range, 51–87 years) formed the training cohort, which included 72 (47 %) patients (mean age, 69.4 ± 8.53 years) in the PCa group and 82 (53 %) (mean age, 72.64 ± 7.04 years) in the benign prostatic lesion group. At Center 2, a total of 139 consecutive patients were enrolled between December 2021 and June 2022. Of these, 88 were excluded because of unavailable TRUS images (n = 41), incomplete questionnaires (n = 21), or a PSA level of 4–10 ng/mL without biopsy (n = 26). Therefore, 51 men (mean age, 69.45 ± 8.76 years; range, 51–86 years) were included in the validation cohort. Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical variables of the benign and PCa groups. Age (P < 0.001), PSA (P < 0.001), free PSA (P < 0.001), and PSAD (median, 9.21 [IQR, 44.42] ng/mL/cm³ vs. 1.47 [IQR, 1.6] ng/mL/cm³; P < 0.001, Fig. 2) were higher, whereas the free-to-total PSA ratio (P < 0.001) was lower in patients with PCa than in those without PCa. Abnormal DRE findings were also more frequent in patients with PCa than in those without PCa (54/72 [75 %] vs. 20/82 [24 %]; P < 0.001). The number of patients living in rural areas was higher in the PCa group (P = 0.047). PSA levels were significantly higher in the training cohort than in the validation cohort (P = 0.029). No significant differences were found in other demographic characteristics between the two cohorts.

Comparison of multivariable models: clinical vs combined

In a multivariable analysis including clinical characteristics (clinical model), DRE (odds ratio [OR], 4.9; 95 % confidence interval [CI]: 2.09–11.5; P < 0.001) and high PSAD level (OR, 12.44; 95 % CI: 5.19–29.81; P < 0.001) were independently associated with PCa. In a multivariable analysis including both TRUS and clinical variables (combined model; Table 3), DRE (OR, 3.86; 95 % CI: 1.3–11.4; P = 0.034), high PSAD level (OR, 3.86; 95 % CI: 1.30–11.40; P = 0.015), ill-defined PZ and TZ (OR, 9.61; 95 % CI: 2.37–39.02; P = 0.002), and hyper-enhancement (OR, 7.67; 95 % CI: 2.69–21.89; P < 0.001) were associated with PCa. The combined model used to develop the nomogram (Fig. 3) showed a stronger discriminatory ability for PCa than the clinical model (AUC, 0.933 [95 % CI: 0.881–0.986] vs. 0.867 [95 % CI: 0.731–0.922]; P = 0.002) (Fig. 4A). Decision curve analysis (Figures S1 and S2) showed that the combined model outperformed the clinical model in both the training and validation cohorts when the threshold probability was between 0.1 and 0.9. The higher predictive validity of the combined model compared with the clinical model was confirmed in the external validation cohort (Tables S2 and S3 in the Supplementary Material).

Nomogram and external validation

Evaluation of the predictive efficacy of the combined model for PCa was performed in a validation cohort of 51 patients. The AUC for the combined model was superior to that of the clinical model (0.907 [95 % CI: 0.792–0.970] vs. 0.785 [95 % CI: 0.648–0.888]; P = 0.0026) (Fig. 4B). Both models demonstrated good calibration, with excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities in the calibration plots (Fig. 4C, D). Discussion

In this study involving 154 patients, we observed that baseline TRUS effectively depicted the overall prostate condition, while TR-CEUS provided insights into the microvascular perfusion characteristics of PCa tissue. Multivariate analysis combining clinical parameters and TRUS identified specific factors independently associated with PCa diagnosis. Based on these findings, we developed a nomogram model integrating clinical indicators, baseline TRUS, and TR-CEUS features. The model demonstrated high predictive accuracy, with AUCs of 0.933 for the training cohort and 0.907 for the validation cohort, indicating its simplicity and efficacy in predicting PCa.

For demographic characterization, 205 patients of Han ethnicity (including both the training and validation cohorts) were included, reflecting the epidemiology of the southwestern region of China. The results showed that older age was associated with a higher risk of PCa development, consistent with the findings of Godtman et al. [21]. In contrast, the relatively low BMI of patients with PCa in our cohort and the higher prevalence of PCa in rural areas compared with urban areas were inconsistent with the 2022 Chinese national guidelines for PCa diagnosis and treatment. These discrepancies may be attributable to the lower standard of living, inadequate nutrition, and insufficient health awareness in Southwest China. Notably, because of the relatively underdeveloped local economy, most patients with PCa presented at middle to late stages of the disease [22]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for more accessible and cost-effective screening and early diagnostic methods to alleviate the disease burden on patients with PCa in this region.

PSA serves as a crucial biomarker for the diagnosis and prognosis monitoring of PCa [23], [24], yet its specificity is insufficient. To enhance diagnostic precision, researchers have developed a series of PSA-derived indicators, including PSAD [25]. PSAD was initially recognized for its utility in distinguishing PSA elevations attributable to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) from those caused by PCa, and it is regarded as a valuable and cost-effective tool for diagnosing PCa and predicting disease progression [26], [27]. San Francisco et al. demonstrated that a PSAD above 0.08 ng/mL/cm³ at initial assessment had a relatively robust predictive value for PCa progression [28]. In contrast, our study identified a PSAD threshold of 0.62 ng/mL/cm³ for the diagnosis of PCa, markedly higher than the commonly employed threshold of 0.15 ng/mL/cm³ . This discrepancy may reflect the fact that most patients with PCa in China are diagnosed at intermediate to advanced stages, characterized by significantly elevated PSA levels and proportionally higher PSAD values. These findings highlight the need to recalibrate diagnostic thresholds in different clinical contexts to optimize the utility of PSAD in PCa diagnosis and management [29].

DRE is a critical physical examination technique for assessing changes in prostatic firmness [30]. Abnormal DRE findings have been identified as an independent risk factor for PCa [31]. However, because of its limited sensitivity, DRE should be used in conjunction with complementary diagnostic modalities, including imaging, to ensure early and accurate PCa detection [32].

PCa diagnosis usually requires invasive biopsy. Therefore, noninvasive screening before biopsy is essential. Nomograms combining MRI or positron emission tomography (PET) features have shown promising results in predicting high-risk PCa [33], [34]. However, the clinical use of MRI and PET remains limited because of high costs and suboptimal reproducibility. By contrast, TRUS offers a user-friendly, reproducible, and versatile imaging modality that does not require specialized software or post-processing [35]. In the present study, we focused on using TRUS to characterize the overall prostate condition [36], including morphological features, envelope integrity, and the distinction between the PZ and TZ, rather than solely concentrating on PCa lesions. Interestingly, our results revealed that an ill-defined margin between the PZ and TZ was independently associated with PCa. This parameter is rarely emphasized in the literature, yet TRUS can readily detect it. In patients with BPH, TZ enlargement compresses the PZ, producing a distinct surgical envelope. In contrast, PCa tissue and microvascular infiltration at the junction result in poorly demarcated interfaces on TRUS [34], making this a potentially useful parameter for PCa risk assessment. TR-CEUS facilitates real-time visualization of microperfusion within prostate tissues and lesions [37]. Our study identified hyper-enhancement as an independent risk factor for PCa [38]. This finding may reflect differences in micro-vessel density between benign and malignant prostate lesions [39], structural variations in tumor vasculature, and the emergence of arteriovenous fistulae within malignant foci [40]. However, because of the heterogeneous and multifocal nature of PCa, CEUS demonstrates variable diagnostic sensitivity (ranging from 54 % to 79 %) and specificity (varying from 42 % to 95 %) [41], [42]. This emphasizes the need to integrate multiple diagnostic approaches to improve PCa detection and screening.

Both single biomarkers and imaging features have distinct advantages and limitations in clinical application [43]. Numerous studies have attempted to improve predictive accuracy by developing comprehensive models [44]. However, these models often focus on specific populations or parameters and therefore inadequately address PCa heterogeneity. To address this limitation, our study developed a PCa prediction nomogram that combines easily accessible clinical data with TRUS features to provide an accurate prediction tool for local patients. The model incorporates four factors independently associated with PCa: DRE, PSAD, ill-defined PZ–TZ margin, and hyper-enhancement. Together, these indicators offer a comprehensive assessment of prostate cancer, accounting for biomarkers, tissue hardness, prostate anatomy, and lesion blood flow. The AUC value and calibration plot confirmed the model’s high consistency and accuracy. External validation further demonstrated strong discriminatory power, with a calibration C-index of 0.907, indicating its broad applicability. We suggest that incorporating TRUS into decision-making algorithms for early PCa detection is an economically viable approach that can increase diagnostic precision, making it an essential component of the clinical diagnostic armamentarium. The accessibility and versatility of TRUS underscore its potential to improve screening and prognostic management in patients with PCa.

This study has several limitations. First, the high proportion of advanced-stage cancers may limit applicability to populations with higher rates of early detection. Second, the study population was restricted to patients attending urology clinics, with a suboptimal response rate. In addition, there was no long-term follow-up for patients with benign biopsy results, which may have introduced false negatives. To improve external validity, future studies should include more diverse and extensive sampling strategies to better represent the wider population. Third, although recognized TR-CEUS qualitative parameters were used, these assessments were subjective and susceptible to observer bias. To minimize this, all observers underwent standardized training in TRUS and CEUS operation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the value of TRUS in characterizing overall prostate features. By combining TRUS characteristics with clinical parameters, we constructed a nomogram that employs common clinical indicators to describe prostate cancer from multiple dimensions, enabling precise prediction. Owing to its convenience and broad applicability, the nomogram serves as a practical visual tool for PCa screening, aiding in the selection and management of potential biopsy candidates.
