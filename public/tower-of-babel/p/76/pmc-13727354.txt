Bisphenol Analog‐Induced Cytotoxicity: Unraveling Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) Stress and Apoptotic Pathways
Authors: Rafia Afroze Rifa, Ramon Lavado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBisphenol A (BPA) is increasingly replaced by structural analogs, yet their safety remains insufficiently characterized. This study investigated whether BPA and selected analogs, bisphenol AF (BPAF), bisphenol AP (BPAP), bisphenol P (BPP), and bisphenol E (BPE), induce cytotoxicity through activation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and apoptosis‐related signaling in a cell‐type‐specific manner. Human intestinal (Caco‐2), hepatic (HepaRG), and microglial (HMC‐3) cells were exposed to environmentally relevant concentrations (0.001–10 μM) of bisphenols for 24 h. Key endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress markers (PERK, eIF2α, ATF4) and the apoptosis‐associated protein cytochrome C were quantified using ELISA‐based approaches. Bisphenol exposure elicited distinct, compound‐ and cell‐type‐dependent activation of the PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4 pathway. HepaRG cells showed the most robust and dose‐responsive ER stress activation, accompanied by consistent increases in cytochrome C and strong correlations between ER stress markers and apoptosis‐related responses. Caco‐2 cells exhibited moderate ER stress activation with limited propagation toward cytochrome C modulation, whereas HMC‐3 cells displayed pronounced ER stress signaling without significant cytochrome C association. Regression analyses confirmed cell‐specific coupling between ER stress and apoptosis‐related responses. These findings demonstrate that BPA analogs are not biologically inert substitutes and highlight the importance of mechanistic, multicell‐based approaches for bisphenol risk assessment and safer chemical design.Keywords: bisphenol analogs, cell‐type‐specific toxicity, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial apoptosis, PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4 signaling

Introduction

Bisphenol A (BPA) has been widely used in the production of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins for decades, but increasing evidence of its endocrine‐disrupting effects has prompted the development and use of structural analogs such as bisphenol AF (BPAF), bisphenol AP (BPAP), bisphenol P (BPP), bisphenol E (BPE), and many more (Chen et al. 2016; den Braver‐Sewradj et al. 2020; Hahladakis et al. 2023; Korbag and Korbag 2023; Rifa and Lavado 2024b; Rocha et al. 2016). While these analogs were initially considered safer substitutes, recent toxicological studies indicate that they may exert adverse effects comparable to or even greater than those of the parent compound (Chen et al. 2016; Rifa and Lavado 2024a, 2024b; Wang et al. 2021; Xu et al. 2024). Our previous work demonstrated that exposure to selected bisphenol analogs induces cell‐type‐specific cytotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disruption of calcium homeostasis (Rifa and Lavado 2024a; Rifa et al. 2025). These disturbances in redox balance, mitochondrial function, and calcium signaling are critical triggers of downstream cellular stress responses, including endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress (Carreras‐Sureda et al. 2018; Görlach et al. 2006; Groenendyk et al. 2021; Krebs et al. 2015).

The ER plays a central role in protein folding, calcium storage, and lipid synthesis (Araki and Nagata 2011; Brostrom and Brostrom 2003; Görlach et al. 2006; Koch 1990). Perturbations that impair its protein‐folding capacity activate a highly conserved adaptive mechanism known as the unfolded protein response (UPR) (Austin 2009; Grootjans et al. 2016; Hetz et al. 2020). While the UPR initially functions to restore ER homeostasis, persistent or severe stress can shift its role from cytoprotective to pro‐apoptotic (Ajoolabady et al. 2022; Malhotra and Kaufman 2011; Sisinni et al. 2019). One of the major branches of the UPR, the protein kinase RNA‐like ER kinase (PERK) pathway, operates by phosphorylating eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) and inducing activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) (Liu et al. 2015; Rozpedek et al. 2016; Taalab et al. 2018).

Several studies have demonstrated that bisphenol analogs activate the PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4 branch of the UPR. For example, in human JEG‐3 placental cells, exposure to BPA and bisphenol S (BPS) was reported to increase PERK expression, eIF2α phosphorylation, and ATF4 protein levels, concomitant with reduced estradiol synthesis and induction of ER stress markers (Li et al. 2023). Similarly, a study in pancreatic β‐cells found that BPA preferentially activated the PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4 pathway, resulting in elevated ATF4 and the DNA damage‐inducible transcript 3/C/EBP homologous protein (DDIT3/CHOP) expression and promoting ER stress‐mediated apoptosis, particularly at higher concentrations that impaired insulin synthesis and processing (Daian et al. 2023). Comparable findings have been reported in prostate and ovarian cell models. In PNT1A prostate cells, BPA, bisphenol F (BPF), and BPS significantly upregulated UPR‐related genes, including EIF2AK3 (PERK), ATF4, and DDIT3/CHOP, indicating robust activation of pro‐apoptotic UPR signaling (Caglayan and Ozden 2024). In human ovarian granulosa (KGN) cells, exposure to these bisphenols was found to activate the PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4‐CHOP axis, along with increased mitochondrial stress and reduced cell viability, supporting a role for maladaptive UPR signaling in bisphenol‐induced cytotoxicity (Yin et al. 2017). Collectively, these findings support the notion that bisphenol analogs can induce ER stress across multiple cellular models.

Prolonged ER stress is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, a relationship mediated in part by calcium signaling between these organelles (Calì et al. 2011; Szymański et al. 2017). Excessive mitochondrial calcium accumulation can trigger permeability transition pore opening and release of pro‐apoptotic factors, such as cytochrome C, into the cytosol, thereby activating the intrinsic apoptotic cascade (Crompton 1999; Morciano et al. 2021; Smaili et al. 2003). Studies have shown that bisphenol‐induced ER stress promotes calcium‐dependent mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, leading to cytochrome C release and activation of the intrinsic caspase cascade. For example, Wang et al. (2019) demonstrated that chronic BPA exposure in human embryonic stem cell‐derived cortical neurons elevated intracellular calcium levels, while simultaneously inducing oxidative and ER stress. This disturbance of calcium homeostasis impaired mitochondrial function, leading to cytochrome C release and activation of the Bcl‐2/caspase‐dependent apoptotic pathway. Padberg et al. (2019) investigated multiple bisphenols, including BPA, BPS, and bisphenol C (BPC), in human liver HepG2 cells and found that BPC exposure led to a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential and caused release of cytochrome C from mitochondria, confirming its ability to instigate mitochondrial apoptosis. Another study showed that BPA exposure in INS‐1 pancreatic β‐cells led to oxidative stress, apoptosis, mitophagy, and mitochondrial dysfunction, including cytochrome C release into the cytosol (Lin et al. 2013).

In this study, we investigated that bisphenol analog‐induced cytotoxicity is mediated by sustained ER stress coupled with mitochondrial‐dependent apoptosis. This approach was guided by our previous studies, which demonstrated that bisphenol analogs induce cytotoxicity accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction, prompting further investigation into upstream stress pathways linking these effects (Rifa and Lavado 2024a; Rifa et al. 2025). To address this, we quantified key molecular markers of ER stress (PERK, eIF2α, ATF4) and apoptosis (cytochrome C) in multiple human cell lines representing different tissue types, including Caco‐2 (intestinal), HepaRG (hepatic), and HMC‐3 (microglial). The intestinal epithelial cell line Caco‐2 is widely used as a model of the human intestinal barrier and chemical absorption, making it relevant for evaluating toxicity following oral exposure (Hidalgo et al. 1989; Sambuy et al. 2005). HepaRG cells, a liver cell line, are notable for their liver‐specific functions, like cytochrome P450 enzyme expression and nuclear receptor activity (Hewitt et al. 2007), which are important for studying hepatic responses to BP analogs. The human microglial cell line HMC‐3 serves as a model for neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity in the central nervous system (Woolf et al. 2025).

By comparing the effects of different analogs, BPA, BPP, BPAP, BPAF, and BPE, this work provides mechanistic insight into their relative toxic potentials. Beyond improving the understanding of bisphenol analog toxicity, these findings have implications for the development of reliable biomarkers, refinement of adverse outcome pathways (AOPs), and support for regulatory frameworks aimed at safer chemical design and use.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Treatment

Experimental exposures included these bisphenol analogs acquired from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA): bisphenol A (BPA; CAS 80‐05‐7; purity > 97%), bisphenol AP (BPAP; CAS 1571‐75‐1; purity 99%), bisphenol AF (BPAF; CAS 1478‐61‐1; purity > 97%), and bisphenol E (BPE; CAS 2081‐08‐5; purity > 99%). Bisphenol P (BPP; CAS 2167‐51‐3; purity > 98%) was acquired from TCI America (Portland, OR, USA). They were administered at concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 10 μM for 24 h (Table 1), based on preliminary cytotoxicity assessments (Rifa and Lavado 2024a). Human intestinal epithelial (Caco‐2, Cat # HTB‐37, American Type Culture Collection [ATCC], Manassas, VA, USA), hepatic (HepaRG, BioPredic International, Rennes, France), and microglial (HMC3, Cat # CRL‐3304, ATCC) cells were used to represent distinct tissue types. Caco‐2 and HMC3 cells were cultured in EMEM (Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 20% and 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), while HepaRG cells were maintained in Williams' E medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) with appropriate supplements. All cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 (Table 2).

Cells were seeded into sterile 96‐well plates (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH, USA) at a density of approximately 20,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere and grow prior to treatment. Negative controls consisted of cells treated with 1% DMSO (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). For positive controls, cells were treated with thapsigargin (1 μM) (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to induce ER stress (the PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4 branch of the UPR) and staurosporine (1 μM) (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) to activate the apoptotic pathway (cytochrome C).

Assessment of ER Stress Markers

ER stress responses were quantified by measuring key components of the UPR, including PERK activation, eIF2α expression, and ATF4 levels, using commercially available ELISA‐based assays (Biomatik, Wilmington, DE, USA). PERK activation was evaluated using the PEK/PERK Colorimetric Cell‐Based ELISA Kit (Biomatik, Cat# EKA50471), while eIF2α expression was assessed with the eIF2α Colorimetric Cell‐Based ELISA Kit (Biomatik, Cat# EKA50194). ATF4 protein levels were determined using a Sandwich ELISA Kit (Biomatik, Cat# EKN43217).

For PERK and eIF2α, cell‐based ELISA assays were performed directly in culture plates to preserve cellular context and protein localization. Briefly, following experimental treatments, cells were fixed with formaldehyde to immobilize cellular proteins and maintain structural integrity. Plates were then incubated with a blocking buffer to prevent non‐specific antibody binding. Primary antibodies specific to PERK or eIF2α were applied, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies. Upon addition of a chromogenic substrate (typically 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine, TMB), enzymatic conversion produced a colorimetric signal proportional to the abundance of the target protein. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

To control for variability in cell number and ensure accurate comparisons across wells, a normalization step using Crystal Violet staining was incorporated. After the initial 450 nm reading, plates were washed and stained with Crystal Violet solution for 30 min to label adherent cells. Excess dye was removed through gentle washing, and plates were allowed to dry. The bound dye was then solubilized with SDS, and the absorbance was measured at 595 nm. These values were used to normalize protein‐specific signals to relative cell density, thereby minimizing well‐to‐well variation and improving data robustness.

ATF4 levels were quantified from whole‐cell lysates using a sandwich ELISA approach, which provides higher specificity and sensitivity for soluble proteins. Following cell lysis, samples were added to microplate wells pre‐coated with capture antibodies specific to ATF4. After incubation, a biotinylated detection antibody was applied, forming an antibody–antigen–antibody “sandwich” complex. HRP‐conjugated streptavidin was then added to bind the biotinylated antibody, and signal development was achieved using TMB substrate. The reaction was stopped, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm. ATF4 concentrations were calculated by interpolation from a standard curve generated using known protein standards.

Assessment of Apoptosis Marker

Apoptotic responses were evaluated by quantifying cytochrome C release, a key indicator of mitochondrial‐mediated apoptosis, using the Cytochrome C Colorimetric Cell‐Based ELISA Kit (Biomatik, Cat# EKA50168). The assay was conducted using a protocol analogous to the PERK and eIF2α cell‐based ELISAs. Cells were fixed, blocked, and incubated with a cytochrome C‐specific primary antibody, followed by an HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody. After substrate addition, absorbance was recorded at 450 nm, reflecting relative cytochrome C levels. As with ER stress markers, Crystal Violet staining was used for normalization to account for differences in cell number across wells. This normalization step ensured that variations in signal intensity were attributable to treatment effects rather than differences in cell density, thereby enabling accurate and reproducible comparisons of apoptotic responses across experimental conditions.

Data Analysis

All treatment conditions were conducted with technical duplicates to ensure measurement reliability and minimize intra‐assay variability. For cell‐based ELISA assays, raw optical density (OD) values at 450 nm were normalized to the corresponding Crystal Violet absorbance at 595 nm. This normalization step accounted for well‐to‐well differences in cell density and ensured that protein expression levels were accurately represented relative to cell number. For ATF4, concentrations were determined by interpolation from standard curves generated with known protein standards and subsequently expressed as fold change relative to solvent control values. All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using the latest version of GraphPad Prism 10. Prior to inferential statistical testing, datasets were evaluated for normality to verify the assumptions required for parametric analyses. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (recommended for small sample sizes), and where appropriate, complemented by visual inspection of data distributions (e.g., Q‐Q plots and histograms). Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Brown‐Forsythe or Levene's test. For datasets that met the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity, differences among treatment groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons post hoc test to identify pairwise differences between groups. In cases where normality assumptions were not met, appropriate nonparametric alternatives (e.g., Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc test) were considered. Statistical significance was defined as a p‐value < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise indicated.

Results

Bisphenol Analogs Activation of ER Stress Signaling

After 24 h exposure, all bisphenols altered ER stress markers relative to control, with responses strongly dependent on compound identity and cell type (Figures 1, 2, 3; Tables S1 – S3). In Caco‐2 intestinal cells, PERK increases were moderate (1.2‐ to 1.5‐fold) and variable (Figure 1A). BPAF and BPAP showed clear induction (e.g., BPAF 1.465 ± 0.233; BPAP up to 1.48 ± 0.382), while BPE produced relatively consistent elevations (1.3‐ to 1.7‐fold). In contrast, BPA remained near the control level, indicating minimal activation. Downstream eIF2α responses were more heterogeneous (Figure 1B), with BPAF reaching up to 3.125 ± 0.050 and other compounds (BPE, BPP, BPA) showing intermittent increases (2.5–3.3‐fold). Despite these elevations, the variability was high (SD often > 0.5), and correlation analyses revealed only partial coordination of the pathway. Specifically, eIF2α and ATF4 showed a moderate but significant correlation (r = 0.5256, p = 0.0173; Table S1), whereas no significant relationships were observed between PERK and cytochrome C (r = 0.4022, p = 0.0787) or between eIF2α and cytochrome C (r = −0.0046, p = 0.9845) (Figure 5; Table S1). These findings indicate that, although ER stress is initiated in Caco‐2 cells, its downstream propagation and linkage to apoptosis remain limited within the timeframe studied.

In HepaRG liver cells, PERK activation was stronger and more consistent (Figure 2), typically ranging from 1.3‐ to > two‐fold compared to control, with several compounds inducing robust responses. BPAP and BPP exceeded two‐fold, while BPA produced the strongest effect, reaching 3.9 ± 0.071, indicating pronounced ER stress activation with relatively low variability. This pattern was mirrored by eIF2α, which showed consistent increases across compounds, with BPA reaching up to 4.19 ± 0.382. Variability remained moderate, supporting reproducibility. Importantly, strong positive correlations were observed between PERK and eIF2α (r = 0.8216, p < 0.00001), PERK and ATF4 (r = 0.6510, p = 0.0018), and PERK and cytochrome C (r = 0.6506, p = 0.0018) (Figure 6; Table S2). These results demonstrate tight coupling between ER stress activation and downstream apoptotic signaling in liver cells, indicating that HepaRG cells are particularly sensitive to bisphenol‐induced ER stress and its progression toward apoptosis.

In HMC‐3 microglial cells, PERK responses were more variable and often nonmonotonic (Figure 3A), with most compounds inducing modest increases (1.0‐ to 1.4‐fold). However, BPA triggered a pronounced response, reaching up to 4.205 ± 0.163‐fold compared to controls, representing the highest PERK induction observed across all models. Similarly, eIF2α responses were selective (Figure 3B), with BPA producing strong activation (5.1 ± 0.09‐fold), while other compounds remained closer to baseline (1.1‐ to 1.6‐fold). Despite this variability, strong correlations were observed between PERK and eIF2α (r = 0.9298, p < 0.000001) and between PERK and ATF4 (r = 0.7192, p = 0.0003) (Table S3), confirming activation of the ER stress pathway. However, no significant correlations were found between cytochrome C and PERK (r = 0.2957, p = 0.2054), eIF2α (r = 0.1295, p = 0.5862), or ATF4 (r = 0.1038, p = 0.6629) (Figure 7; Table S3). These findings indicate that, in microglial cells, ER stress signaling occurs largely independently of apoptotic activation under these conditions.

Bisphenol‐Induced Changes in Cytochrome C

Cytochrome C (CytC) levels further revealed how ER stress translated into mitochondrial apoptotic signaling (Figure 4). In Caco‐2 cells, several bisphenols induced strong apoptotic responses (Figure 4A). BPAF and BPAP increased CytC levels substantially (five‐ to eight‐fold), while BPE produced the highest induction, reaching approximately 13.04 ± 0.891‐fold. In contrast, BPP and BPA showed more moderate or limited responses (one‐ to four‐fold). These results indicate that apoptosis is selectively activated in intestinal cells, although this activation is not strongly correlated with ER stress markers (Figure 5; Table S1).

In HepaRG cells, CytC responses were more consistent but generally lower in magnitude (1.5‐ to 3.5‐fold) (Figure 4B). BPA, BPAP, and BPP produced reproducible increases with relatively low variability (SD typically < 0.5). Importantly, these responses were significantly correlated with ER stress markers (PERK and eIF2α; p = 0.0018, Table S2), supporting a mechanistic link between ER stress and apoptosis in liver cells.

In HMC‐3 microglial cells, CytC responses were moderate and variable (1.5‐ to four‐fold) (Figure 4C). BPA produced consistent increases (two‐ to three‐fold), while other compounds showed weaker or less consistent effects. Notably, these apoptotic responses were not significantly associated with ER stress markers (all p > 0.2, Table S3), indicating a lack of coupling between ER stress and apoptosis in microglial cells.

Correlation and Regression Analyses Reveal Cell‐Type‐Specific Relationships Between ER Stress and Apoptosis

To better understand how ER stress relates to apoptosis, we performed correlation and regression analyses examining relationships among PERK, eIF2α, ATF4, and cytochrome C across all cell types (Figures 5, 6, 7, Tables S1 – S3). In Caco‐2 cells, there was a moderate but statistically significant positive correlation between eIF2α and ATF4 (r = 0.5256, p = 0.0173; Table S1), indicating coordination between early ER stress signaling and downstream transcriptional responses. However, there were no significant correlations between PERK and cytochrome C (r = 0.4022, p = 0.0787) or between eIF2α and cytochrome C (r = −0.0046, p = 0.9845), as shown in Figure 5. This suggests that, in intestinal cells, ER stress does not strongly translate into apoptosis within the time frame studied. In HepaRG cells, the relationships were much stronger. PERK showed significant positive correlations with eIF2α (r = 0.8216, p < 0.00001), ATF4 (r = 0.6510, p = 0.0018), and cytochrome C (r = 0.6506, p = 0.0018; Table S2). Regression analyses (Figure 6) confirmed these linear relationships. These results demonstrate a tight coupling between ER stress activation and apoptosis in liver cells, indicating that increased ER stress is closely linked to mitochondrial apoptotic signaling in this model. In HMC‐3 microglial cells, PERK was strongly correlated with eIF2α (r = 0.9298, p < 0.000001) and ATF4 (r = 0.7192, p = 0.0003), confirming activation of the ER stress pathway (Table S3). However, no significant correlations were found between cytochrome C and PERK (r = 0.2957, p = 0.2054), eIF2α (r = 0.1295, p = 0.5862), or ATF4 (r = 0.1038, p = 0.6629), as shown in Figure 7. This indicates that, in microglial cells, ER stress occurs independently of measurable activation of apoptosis under these experimental conditions.

Together, these results demonstrate that bisphenol analogs induce ER stress in a compound‐ and cell‐type‐specific manner, with clear differences in how this stress translates into apoptosis. Liver cells (HepaRG) show the strongest and most consistent coupling between ER stress and apoptotic signaling, while intestinal (Caco‐2) and microglial (HMC‐3) cells exhibit partial or uncoupled responses.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that exposure to BPA and several widely used BPA analogs (BPAF, BPP, BPAP, and BPE) induced ER stress signaling and modulated mitochondrial‐associated apoptotic pathways in a compound‐ and cell‐type‐specific manner. By integrating key molecular markers of the unfolded protein response (UPR), including PERK, eIF2α, ATF4, and the apoptosis‐associated protein cytochrome C across intestinal (Caco‐2), hepatic (HepaRG), and microglial (HMC‐3) human cell models, this work provided mechanistic insight into how structurally related bisphenols differentially engaged ER‐mitochondrial crosstalk pathways.

These findings were consistent with an expanding body of literature indicating that BPA substitutes are not inherently safer alternatives but may retain or even enhance the capacity to disrupt cellular stress signaling networks (Akash et al. 2023; Pelch et al. 2019; Rifa and Lavado 2024a; Rifa et al. 2025; Rochester and Bolden 2015). Structural differences among bisphenol analogs, such as halogenation and variations in bridging groups, have been proposed to influence lipophilicity, receptor binding affinity, and intracellular accumulation, thereby modulating their toxicological profiles and cellular responses (Kitamura et al. 2005; Pelch et al. 2019). These physicochemical properties likely contributed to the compound‐specific effects observed across the different cell types. The present side‐by‐side comparison with BPA indicates that these replacement compounds should not be viewed as toxicologically neutral simply because they are marketed as alternatives. In several cellular contexts, the responses induced by BPA analogs were comparable to, or more pronounced than, those observed for BPA (Chen et al. 2016; Rifa and Lavado 2024a, 2024b; Rifa et al. 2025), reinforcing concerns that substitution may reduce commercial reliance on BPA without necessarily reducing biological activity.

In intestinal Caco‐2 cells, moderate PERK induction accompanied by variable eIF2α activation suggested partial engagement of ER stress signaling, with a predominance of adaptive rather than apoptotic responses. The observed significant correlation between eIF2α and ATF4, in the absence of strong associations with cytochrome C, indicated that translational attenuation and transcriptional stress responses were activated without full progression toward mitochondrial apoptosis within the 24 h exposure window. This pattern was consistent with the physiological role of intestinal epithelial cells as a first‐line barrier, where transient activation of the UPR supports cellular adaptation to environmental stressors (Luo and Cao 2015; Ma et al. 2017; McGuckin et al. 2010). Additionally, previous studies have shown that PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4 signaling can promote antioxidant responses, autophagy, and protein homeostasis, thereby delaying or preventing apoptotic commitment under moderate stress conditions (Hetz and Papa 2018; Pakos‐Zebrucka et al. 2016). It is also plausible that oxidative stress generated by bisphenols served as an upstream trigger of ER stress by disrupting redox balance and calcium homeostasis (Cao and Kaufman 2014; Xiong et al. 2021). Collectively, these observations suggested that intestinal cells initially engaged compensatory mechanisms, with progression toward apoptosis likely requiring longer exposure durations or higher concentrations (Ma et al. 2017). This finding is particularly relevant because intestinal epithelial cells represent an early site of exposure to bisphenols migrating from food‐contact materials (Almeida et al. 2018; Muncke 2011; Wang et al. 2018). The fact that several BPA alternatives elicited measurable stress responses in this study suggests that replacement bisphenols may remain biologically relevant at the point of entry, even when their effects do not fully converge on apoptosis within the studied exposure window.

In contrast, HepaRG cells exhibited robust and dose‐dependent activation of PERK and eIF2α across multiple bisphenol analogs, accompanied by strong correlations with ATF4 and cytochrome C. This coordinated activation of ER stress and apoptosis‐related markers suggested that hepatic cells were particularly susceptible to sustained ER stress signaling progressing toward apoptotic outcomes. This heightened sensitivity was biologically plausible given the liver's central role in xenobiotic metabolism, where phase I and phase II enzymes may bioactivate bisphenols into reactive intermediates that amplify intracellular stress responses (Fritsche et al. 2023; Guo et al. 2025; Menezes et al. 2007; Mosca et al. 2024). Supporting this interpretation, previous studies demonstrated that BPA and related analogs induced mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and caspase activation in hepatic systems (AbdalHussin et al. 2025; Asahi et al. 2010; Kim et al. 2022; Padberg et al. 2019; Rifa et al. 2025). Mechanistically, prolonged activation of the PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4 pathway has been linked to CHOP induction, calcium dysregulation, and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, thereby connecting ER stress to apoptosis (Ajoolabady et al. 2023; 2022; Hetz and Papa 2018; Shreya et al. 2023). These findings reinforced the liver as a critical target organ for bisphenol‐induced toxicity and highlighted ER stress as a central mediator of hepatocellular injury (Cano et al. 2021; Mosca et al. 2024; Nikolaou et al. 2024). The strong hepatic responses observed for some analogs alongside BPA further support the idea that currently used BPA substitutes may preserve key toxicodynamic features of the parent compound. Because many of these alternatives are used in similar industrial applications, their ability to activate comparable stress pathways raises concern that chemical replacement may shift exposure profiles without eliminating mechanistic hazards.

Microglial HMC‐3 cells demonstrated strong coupling between PERK, eIF2α, and ATF4, indicating efficient activation of ER stress signaling. However, this activation was not accompanied by significant cytochrome C responses, suggesting a dissociation between ER stress signaling and mitochondrial apoptotic execution. Notably, BPA elicited the most pronounced ER stress activation in microglial cells, indicating higher sensitivity of this cell type to BPA relative to its analogs. This uncoupling suggested that ER stress in microglia may have preferentially engaged non‐apoptotic pathways, including inflammatory signaling, autophagy, or metabolic adaptation. Previous studies have shown that ER stress in microglia can activate pro‐inflammatory pathways such as NF‐κB and cytokine production independently of apoptosis (Joshi et al. 2022; Kovacs et al. 2025; Lampiasi et al. 2023). Additionally, ER‐mitochondrial crosstalk involving calcium signaling may alter cellular function without necessarily inducing cell death (Saha et al. 2025). As described by Sprenkle et al. (2017), ER stress in neuroglial cells often contributes to chronic neuroinflammation and neurodegenerative processes rather than acute apoptotic responses. These findings suggested that bisphenol exposure in neural contexts may have significant implications for neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative outcomes, even in the absence of overt cytotoxicity. The marked responsiveness to BPA in this cellular model, relative to other studied analogs, also indicates that substitution does not necessarily produce a uniform toxicological pattern across tissues. Instead, BPA and its alternatives may differ in potency depending on the cellular context (Chen et al. 2016; Rifa and Lavado 2024a; Rosenmai et al. 2014), further emphasizing the importance of evaluating replacement bisphenols across multiple target systems rather than assuming a generalized improvement in safety.

A key finding of this study was the cell‐type‐specific relationship between ER stress activation and modulation of cytochrome C. While cytochrome C is widely recognized as an indicator of apoptosis‐related signaling (Barczyk et al. 2005; Cai et al. 1998; Eleftheriadis et al. 2016; Pullerits et al. 2005; von Harsdorf et al. 1999), its differential regulation across cell types suggested that ER stress did not uniformly translate into mitochondrial apoptotic activation. In Caco‐2 cells, only specific bisphenol analogs induced cytochrome C upregulation, indicating compound‐dependent engagement of apoptosis‐related pathways. In HepaRG cells, consistent elevations in cytochrome C supported a stronger convergence of ER stress toward apoptosis. In contrast, microglial cells exhibited attenuated cytochrome C responses, reinforcing the idea that ER stress may preferentially activate survival or inflammatory pathways in this context. These findings highlighted the importance of interpreting cytochrome C modulation cautiously, particularly in the absence of complementary functional apoptosis assays (Garrido et al. 2006; Green 2022).

Several mechanistic hypotheses may explain the observed differential responses. First, variations in metabolic capacity likely influenced intracellular concentrations and bioactivation of bisphenols, particularly in hepatic cells. Second, differences in antioxidant defenses and mitochondrial resilience across cell types may have modulated susceptibility to oxidative and ER stress. Third, cell‐specific expression of ER stress sensors and downstream signaling components may have determined whether UPR activation resulted in adaptation or apoptosis. Finally, differences in membrane composition and transport mechanisms may have influenced cellular uptake and distribution of bisphenol analogs.

From a broader toxicological perspective, these findings add to growing evidence that BPA alternatives are environmentally and biologically relevant contaminants rather than simple replacements of lesser concern (Adamovsky et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2016; Harnett et al. 2021; Hyun et al. 2021; Li et al. 2023; Rifa and Lavado 2024b). Their continued use in plastics, resins, coatings, and related materials increases the likelihood that human exposure now involves not only BPA, but also a wider range of structurally similar bisphenols (Chen et al. 2016; Rifa and Lavado 2024b). In this context, direct comparison with BPA is essential, because the key regulatory question is not whether an alternative is chemically different, but whether it meaningfully reduces hazardous biological activity. The present data suggest that such assumptions of improved safety should be made cautiously and supported by mechanistic evidence.

Despite the mechanistic insights provided in this study, several limitations should be considered. The study relied on protein expression endpoints at a single time point (24 h), which limited the ability to capture the dynamic progression from adaptive stress responses to irreversible cellular outcomes. Time‐course studies would be necessary to determine whether early ER stress signaling transitions into apoptosis or resolves through adaptive pathways. Additionally, the absence of functional assays, such as caspase activation, mitochondrial membrane potential, ROS quantification, or calcium flux measurements, limited the ability to confirm the mechanistic links between ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. The use of immortalized cell lines, while valuable for mechanistic studies, may not fully recapitulate the in vivo complexity of tissue, including cell–cell interactions, immune responses, and systemic metabolism. Furthermore, the study did not evaluate additional branches of the UPR (IRE1α and ATF6), nor did it assess cross‐talk with other pathways such as autophagy, inflammation, or endocrine signaling, which are increasingly recognized as critical components of bisphenol toxicity (Hetz and Papa 2018; Pakos‐Zebrucka et al. 2016). Another important limitation is the lack of mixture exposure scenarios, which are more representative of real‐world environmental conditions where multiple bisphenols co‐occur.

Overall, this study provided highly relevant mechanistic evidence that BPA alternatives are not necessarily safer and can induce ER stress and apoptosis‐related signaling in a cell‐type‐specific manner. The findings underscored the importance of considering tissue‐specific responses and mechanistic endpoints when evaluating the safety of endocrine‐disrupting chemicals. Importantly, the differential coupling between ER stress and apoptotic signaling across cell types highlighted the need for integrated, multicellular, and systems‐based approaches in toxicological assessments. These results have significant implications for regulatory frameworks and risk assessment strategies, particularly as BPA analogs continue to replace BPA in consumer products. Moreover, the study contributed to advancing new approach methodologies (NAMs) by demonstrating the value of combining multiple human‐relevant cell models and mechanistic biomarkers to better predict human health outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that BPA and its analogs induce compound‐ and cell‐type‐specific ER stress and apoptosis‐related responses, with hepatic cells showing the strongest coupling between UPR activation (PERK‐eIF2α‐ATF4) and cytochrome C modulation. Critically, the direct comparison between BPA and its alternatives revealed that several substitutes elicited similar or even greater biological effects, challenging the assumption that BPA replacements are inherently safer. These findings highlight the importance of systematically evaluating BPA alternatives alongside BPA using mechanistic, multicellular approaches to avoid regrettable substitutions and improve chemical safety assessment.
