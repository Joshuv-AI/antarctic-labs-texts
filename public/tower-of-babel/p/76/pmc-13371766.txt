Why was the cohort set up?

The Soweto and Thembelihle Health and Demographic Surveillance System (SaT-HDSS) was established to support the Child Health and Mortality Prevention Surveillance (CHAMPS) programme. The CHAMPS programme collects standardized data to track and understand definitive causes of childhood mortality [1]. It consists of four workstreams, namely: mortality surveillance, Social Behavioural Sciences (SBS), pregnancy surveillance, and the HDSS. The mortality surveillance component uses minimally invasive tissue sampling (MITS) procedures to determine causes of stillbirths and deaths in children <5 years of age from the study site. These deaths are mainly identified at healthcare facilities and occasionally through informants in the community [2]. The SBS work stream plays a complementary role, initially by working with the community to foster trust and acceptability of MITS. The SBS team engages the community to understand cultural and religious beliefs associated with childhood death and helps to qualitatively contextualize the findings from other quantitative data collection. Furthermore, the SBS play a key role to feed back MITS results at the household and community levels. The pregnancy surveillance that was initiated in the second phase of CHAMPS (4 years ago) work stream monitors pregnancies within the study site to better understand pregnancy outcomes, associated risk factors, and patterns of care during pregnancy. The pregnancy surveillance was also meant to compliment the HDSS in capturing miscarriages, stillbirths, and neonatal deaths, considering that HDSS data collection occurs only once every 6 months. The CHAMPS programme requires a well-defined catchment population to estimate health and demographic measures such as mortality and migration rates. The SaT-HDSS was set up in 2017 to provide population-level demographic characteristics through which childhood deaths occur and the associated demographic events that cause population change: births, deaths, and in- and outmigration. The SaT-HDSS also collects contextual information on epidemiological and socio-economic characteristics of the study area. The socio-economic component helps in understanding the social determinants of health.

The SaT-HDSS is among the first urban HDSS study sites to be established in South Africa. The three HDSSs that were established between 1992 and 2000, predating the SaT-HDSS, are all located in rural areas [3–5]. As a result, the SaT-HDSS offers additional benefits, including a baseline understanding of urban dynamics in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, the SaT-HDSS provides opportunities for multi-site comparative analysis within South Africa (urban township/informal settlement vs rural) and other countries.

The Wits Vaccines and Infectious Disease Analytics (Wits VIDA) research unit, affiliated to the University of the Witwatersrand and based at the Chris Hani Baragwanath Academic Hospital in Soweto, runs the SaT-HDSS. The HDSS is currently funded by the Gates Foundation through the CHAMPS programme.

Who is in the cohort?

The SaT-HDSS is an open population-based cohort. The study considers all individuals residing in the study area for ≥4 months prior to the date of the household visit for data collection. Temporary visitors and all other individuals who have lived in the study site for <4 months prior to the data-collection round are considered temporary residents and are not considered to be residents of the HDSS. The selection of the study areas (clusters) was based on sub-places—a geographical subdivision equivalent to a suburb, ward, village, farm, or informal settlement [6, 7]—that had an under-five mortality rate of >50 per 1000 based on the 2011 South African census. Consequently, the SaT-HDSS study area is made up of non-contiguous sub-places (Fig. 1). Baseline data were collected between November 2017 and May 2019, covering sub-places in Soweto (Bram Fischerville Extension 12, Thulani, Emndeni, Mapetla, Phiri, Senaoane, Mofolo North, Meadowlands East Zone 4, and Meadowlands East Zone 5) and Thembelihle (Thembelihle, Lehae, and Eikenhof). Enumeration of the Soweto clusters occurred from 7 November 2017 to 18 September 2018, while the Thembelihle clusters were enumerated from 8 August 2018 to 29 May 2019. The baseline enrolment, based on the official 2011 South African census data available at the time, was projected to include 184 107 individuals from 49 063 households in the Soweto clusters and 39 064 individuals from 14 607 households in the Thembelihle clusters. The realized baseline population was 91 199 (52.3%) for Soweto and 14 569 (37.3%) for Thembelihle. The under-enrolment was partly due to inaccessible households. How often have they been followed up?

The SaT-HDSS conducts an average of two rounds of data collection per year. The routine data-collection round lasts for ≥6 months, during which data are collected for a minimum of 3 months, followed by another 3 months dedicated to checking, cleaning, and preparing data for the subsequent round. As of December 2024, 10 rounds of data collection had been conducted: Round 1 (2 October 2018 to 19 May 2019), Round 2 (16 July 2019 to 30 December 2019), Round 3 (8 February 2020 to 26 March 2020), Round 4 (21 July 2020 to 13 February 2021), Round 5 (20 February 2021 to 5 August 2021), Round 6 (11 August 2021 to 17 January 2022), Round 7 (11 February 2022 to 9 July 2022), Round 8 (22 August 2022 to 31 December 2022), Round 9 (25 May 2023 to 3 February 2024), and Round 10 (5 June 2024 to 21 September 2024). There was a temporal overlap of visits during data collection, with Soweto beginning its first follow-up visit (2 October 2018 to19 May 2019) before Thembelihle had completed its baseline visits (8 August 2018 to 29 May 2019). The third visit was not completed due to the COVID-19 restrictions at the time in early 2020. On average, the SaT-HDSS follows a population of ∼119 694 (99 455 for Soweto and 20 239 for Thembelihle) from ∼33 495 households (26 695 from Soweto and 6 801 from Thembelihle) per year. These figures are slightly higher than the baseline totals, for several key reasons. First, the follow-up visits successfully captured some of the households that were initially missed during the baseline census. Second, because households and individuals are not always available during every visit, some may have left their residences without a formal record. These units remain classified as active until they meet the specific criteria for being lost to follow-up, typically after three consecutive missed visits. The data indicate a net outmigration averaging 368 people from Soweto and 659 from Thembelihle over the period. For Soweto, the yearly figures were: 2018 (2744), 2019 (–2278), 2020 (–363), 2021 (–5233), 2022 (2269), 2023 (1148), and 2024 (–863). For Thembelihle, the corresponding values were: 2018 (638), 2019 (–1986), 2020 (–1575), 2021 (–1442), 2022 (–165), 2023 (158), and 2024 (–245). While peri-urban population growth was expected, this observed decline reflects a methodological artefact of enumeration lag rather than true population loss. In this setting, recording an outmigration or exit is often more straightforward than identifying new arrivals, especially in backrooms or informal shacks. These new residents may be unknown to the primary respondent or may not yet meet the 4-month residency threshold. Even when in-migrations are eventually captured in subsequent visits, a portion of the population remains lagging in the records.

What has been measured?

Trained fieldworkers visit each household in the study site and request written consent to interview a respondent on behalf of the household. If consent is granted, the fieldworkers then interview the most knowledgeable person in the household, as defined by the household. During the baseline visit, the primary instrument collects information about each individual living in the household on their residential status and associated vital events. Each follow-up visit verifies existing data and records all new events experienced by each household member, i.e. pregnancy outcomes, deaths, and in- and outmigrations (Table 2). For women aged 15–54 years, their maternity history is recorded at baseline and this information continues to be collected from in-migrants in the same age group. The SaT-HDSS also collects information on household characteristics (social-economic status and access to resources). The data collected cover all the essential information needed for the CHAMPS objectives [9]. This includes the longitudinal monitoring of all births, deaths, migrations, and pregnancies within the catchment area to estimate the under-five mortality rates and assess household socio-economic status. Data collection has also been expanded to address a variety of additional research questions, including household and community COVID-19 transmission, barriers to flu-vaccine uptake, and diarrheal patterns within the catchment area.

What has it found?

Table 3 shows the demographic characteristics of the SaT-HDSS over the period 2018–24. The annualized population and households for both Soweto and Thembelihle fluctuate because of varying enumeration coverage in each subsequent visit. The average household sizes remain relatively stable, at just under four people per household for Soweto and about three people per household for Thembelihle. There were about 90 men for every 100 women for Soweto and an equal number of men and women for Thembelihle. About half of the population of Soweto are dependants aged either <15 or >65 years, and this is slightly lower for Thembelihle. The crude birth rate for Soweto ranged from 9.5 to 15.4 live births per 1000 of the population, whereas that for Thembelihle ranged from 19.6 to 27.2 live births per 1000 of the population. The number of deaths per 1000 of the mid-year population was higher in Soweto (range: 7.7–13.8) compared with Thembelihle (range: 6.2–9.3). The under-five mortality rates were relatively lower from 2018 to 2020 (Soweto: 23.1–42.2, Thembelihle: 22.9–39.7), followed by a spike in 2021 (Soweto: 55.9, Thembelihle: 86.3), and the rates appear to stabilize from 2022 to 2023 (Soweto: 43.0–44.5, Thembelihle: 42.5–48.1). The demographic data for 2024, including births and deaths, are currently being updated and will be reconciled with information gathered during future visits.

Data from the Soweto site show that the all-cause mortality during the COVID-19 era was significantly higher than that during the pre-COVID-19 period, with the increase observed across derived COVID-19 vulnerability tertiles [10]. In the SaT-HDSS, the overall under-five mortality rate per 1000 live births of children born within the study site over the period 2018–22 was comparable to the national estimate in 2018 but the neonatal mortality rate was 54% (16.0 [11] vs 10.4 [12] deaths per 1000 live births) higher. Furthermore, an increase in under-five death rates in 2020 compared with 2018 was also observed, which could have been due to COVID-19.

Figure 2 shows the differences in the population age–sex structure for the Soweto and Thembelihle clusters based on the 2019 and 2024 HDSS population data. The Soweto population narrows for the young age groups (0–4 years), suggesting falling fertility, whereas Thembelihle had more children aged 0–4 years. In 2024, Soweto had a higher percentage of children in the age groups of 5–9 years (8.1%) and 10–14 years (8.8%) relative to the age group of 0–4 years (7.2%). For Thembelihle, the populations aged 5–9 years (9.1%) and 10–14 years (9.3%) generally shrank relative to the age group of 0–4 years (9.8%) in 2024, matching the overall population age–sex structure at baseline [9]. The age groups of 0–4 years (8.3% vs 8.7%) and 5–9 years (8.5% vs 8.7%) for both populations suggest either that there were fewer boys than girls born in the HDSS or that there was a net loss of boys relative to girls due to mortality or outmigration. The impact of the HIV/AIDS epidemic, starting from the early 2000s, likely explains the lower proportion of young adults (aged 20–24 years) relative to the adjacent age groups in both study areas.

The SaT-HDSS has also been used as a platform for studying other topics. A study conducted in early February 2020 to investigate diarrhoea patterns within the HDSS population revealed consistently high rates of diarrhoea across the population and found that older individuals often did not seek care at healthcare facilities when experiencing symptoms [13]. The same data were further used to the investigate gaps in hand-hygiene practice to support tailored target audience messaging in Soweto in the context of COVID-19 and found that only 42% of survey respondents practised adequate hand hygiene [14]. Another study explored factors influencing influenza vaccine uptake among adults in the study site and key barriers included mistrust in vaccines and health institutions, reliance on traditional medicine, low perceived risk of influenza, and limited vaccine promotion and accessibility [15]. We also learnt that the risk of teenage pregnancy within the study site increased with age and living in male-headed households, and decreased among teenage girls living in richer households and with access to the internet at home [16].

What are the main strengths and weaknesses?

The SaT-HDSS offers a platform for more than just data collection. It can also serve as a crucial testing ground for new public health interventions. Beyond its surveillance capabilities, the HDSS acts as a reliable interface between research and the community, facilitating essential two-way communication. This means that not only can researchers gather valuable insights, but the community can also directly benefit from and contribute to health initiatives. The HDSS also contributes to capacity building by supporting postgraduate research in population and demography studies and public health. A graduate volunteer programme has been established to provide training opportunities for students in these fields, allowing them to engage with the data-collection processes, analysis, and field operations, thereby enhancing their practical research skills.

A weakness of the SaT-HDSS is that the study site was chosen based on the criteria of high under-five mortality. As a result, the study may not be representative of all Soweto townships, as well as other urban townships and informal settlements in South Africa.

Can I get hold of the data? Where can I find out more?

The SaT-HDSS research team is open to collaboration. Interested partners can send a data request with a detailed description of planned projects via e-mail to info@wits-vida.org or the site co-director, Prof. Ziyaad Dangor (ziyaad.dangor@wits-vida.org). Requests are reviewed and approved on the condition that, among other criteria, at least one of the SaT-HDSS researchers is involved.
