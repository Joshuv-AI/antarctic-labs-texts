Complementary neuroprotective effects of semaglutide and papaya in diabetic encephalopathy via antioxidant and insulin-signaling pathways
Authors: Mohamed M. Zeweil, Mai M. Ali, Marium M. Shamaa, Faten I. Mohammed, Marwa Kamal, Mohamed Samir Ahmed Zaki, Afaf Alharthi, Amera A. Ahmed, Amr Elkelish, Mohamed S. Abdel-Maksoud, Komla Mawunyo Dossouvi, Asmaa F. Khafaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic encephalopathy is a chronic neurodegenerative complication of diabetes mellitus characterized by cognitive decline, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired insulin signaling. Semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, has demonstrated neuroprotective potential; however, whether dietary antioxidant supplementation can provide additional neurobiological benefit remains unclear. This study investigated the neuroprotective effects of semaglutide alone and in combination with papaya fruit pulp in a streptozotocin-induced rat model of diabetic encephalopathy, with emphasis on oxidative stress, insulin signaling, neuroinflammation, amyloidogenesis, and neuronal integrity. Forty male rats were allocated into four groups: control, diabetic (STZ), diabetic treated with semaglutide (0.9 mg/kg), and diabetic treated with semaglutide (0.3 mg/kg) combined with 10% papaya juice for eight weeks. Notably, butyrylcholinesterase activity was normalized only in the semaglutide-papaya group, indicating a distinct cholinergic regulatory effect. Histological examination of the hippocampus and cerebral cortex confirmed superior preservation of neuronal architecture in the combination-treated animals. These findings indicate that while semaglutide mediates the primary metabolic, insulin-sensitizing, and anti-inflammatory effects, papaya provides a complementary neuroprotective contribution through selective reinforcement of antioxidant and cholinergic pathways. Rather than demonstrating global statistical synergy, the semaglutide-papaya combination exhibits pathway-specific enhancement, supporting a biologically meaningful combinatorial strategy for mitigating diabetic encephalopathy.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin production or action1,2. Beyond its well-known vascular complications, DM is increasingly linked to neurodegenerative changes and cognitive impairment, collectively termed diabetic encephalopathy (DE)3. DE contributes to substantial deficits in learning, memory, attention, and executive functions, severely affecting patients’ quality of life and independence4. Emerging research suggests that hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and disrupted insulin signaling are central contributors to DE pathogenesis5,6. These alterations promote lipid peroxidation, activation of nuclear factor-kappa B (NFκB), and excessive apoptosis, culminating in neuronal damage7,8. Additionally, dysregulation of glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK-3β) and reduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels further compromise neuronal survival and synaptic function9–12. Currently, there are no specific neuroprotective treatments approved for diabetic encephalopathy, highlighting the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches.

There is a growing interest in therapies that target both glycemic control and neuroprotection. Semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonist, has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in preclinical studies13, including reducing α-synuclein accumulation, attenuating lipid peroxidation and neuroinflammation, and supporting dopaminergic neuron survival14,15. Furthermore, since both glucagon and GLP-1 receptors are expressed in the endocrine pancreas, GLP-1R agonists such as semaglutide may also indirectly ameliorate inflammatory injury by regulating various metabolic parameters, including glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP), glucagon, insulin, glucose levels, and circulating free fatty acids16,17.

Papaya (Carica papaya) is a nutritionally valuable tropical fruit well recognized for its antiviral, antifungal, and antibacterial properties18. It contains abundant phenols, carotenoids, flavonoids, and essential vitamins (A, E, and C), contributing to its potent antioxidant profile. Moreover, its high fiber, folate, pantothenic acid, and B vitamins support cardiovascular health and help regulate cholesterol levels. Papain and chymopapain, key proteolytic enzymes present in papaya, have been traditionally used to manage inflammation associated with trauma, allergies, and musculoskeletal injuries. In addition to these systemic benefits, recent studies have highlighted papaya’s neuroprotective potential through activation of the nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling pathway, enhancing endogenous antioxidant enzyme expression and reducing neuronal apoptosis19–21. These combined properties make papaya a promising candidate for mitigating neurodegenerative processes, including those associated with diabetic encephalopathy22,23.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the synergistic effects of semaglutide combined with a phytochemical-rich agent such as papaya on multiple neuroprotective pathways in diabetic encephalopathy. We hypothesize that combining semaglutide with papaya exerts a synergistic neuroprotective effect against diabetic encephalopathy in rats through modulation of insulin signaling pathways (IRS-1/GSK3β) and enhancement of neurotrophic factors (BDNF and IGF-1), leading to attenuation of oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis.

Results

Unless otherwise specified, semaglutide monotherapy and semaglutide combined with papaya produced comparable improvements across metabolic, inflammatory, insulin-signaling, and amyloid-related endpoints, with no statistically significant differences between the two treatment groups (p > 0.05). Accordingly, detailed statistical comparisons between these two groups are emphasized only for outcomes showing differential responses.”

Clinical symptoms

During the experiment, rats in the STZ-intoxicated group exhibited symptoms including increased body weight, polyphagia, polyuria, and polydipsia. In contrast, no apparent symptoms or mortality were observed in the control group or in the treatment groups.

Evaluation of diabetic and lipid profile

As presented in Table 1, there was a statistically significant elevation in FBG and HbA1c levels in the STZ-induced diabetic group (FBG: 268.06 ± 5.48 mg/dl; HbA1c: 8.88 ± 0.32%) compared to the control group (FBG: 96.67 ± 3.51 mg/dl; HbA1c: 4.60 ± 0.31%) (p < 0.05 for both). Treatment with semaglutide alone (0.9 mg/kg) significantly reduced these levels (FBG: 162.73 ± 5.21 mg/dl; HbA1c: 6.51 ± 0.40%) relative to the diabetic group (p < 0.05). Similarly, semaglutide combined with papaya juice (0.3 mg/kg SEM + 10% papaya) led to a significant reduction (FBG: 157.50 ± 5.30 mg/dl; HbA1c: 6.33 ± 0.67%) versus the diabetic group (p < 0.05).

Evaluation of oxidative stress and antioxidant defense system

As presented in Table 3, MDA level, a marker of lipid peroxidation, was significantly increased in the STZ-intoxicated group (61.03 ± 6.07 nmol/g tissue) compared to the control group (32.39 ± 2.46 nmol/g tissue) (p < 0.05). Treatment with semaglutide alone or in combination with papaya resulted in MDA levels (43.78 ± 3.50 and 40.23 ± 2.67 nmol/g tissue, respectively) significantly lower than the STZ group (p < 0.05), yet still higher than controls. NO levels were significantly elevated in the STZ group (22.04 ± 1.04 µmol/g tissue) and in the STZ + SEM group (15.26 ± 1.79 µmol/g tissue) compared to controls (9.60 ± 0.69 µmol/g tissue) (p < 0.05). However, the STZ + SEM+Papaya group showed NO levels (12.60 ± 0.72 µmol/g tissue) not significantly different from the control group (p > 0.05). Regarding antioxidants, GSH level was significantly reduced in the STZ group (10.47 ± 0.69 nmol/g protein) versus controls (27.18 ± 3.92 nmol/g protein) (p < 0.05). For GST, activity was significantly lower in the STZ group (9.27 ± 1.25 U/mg protein) and STZ + SEM group (14.61 ± 1.23 U/mg protein) compared to controls (19.50 ± 1.92 U/mg protein) (p < 0.05). The STZ + SEM+Papaya group showed GST activity (17.94 ± 1.20 U/mg protein) without significant difference from the control group (p > 0.05). Similarly, SOD activity was significantly decreased in the STZ group (9.17 ± 1.04 U/mg protein) and STZ + SEM group (16.60 ± 1.60 U/mg protein) versus controls (24.81 ± 2.58 U/mg protein) (p < 0.05). CAT activity was significantly reduced in all treated diabetic groups compared to the control group (CTR: 73.66 ± 5.44 U/mg protein vs. STZ: 28.39 ± 3.37 U/mg protein; STZ + SEM: 46.01 ± 4.70 U/mg protein; STZ + SEM+Papaya: 55.34 ± 3.30 U/mg protein; p < 0.05). However, CAT activity in the STZ + SEM and STZ + SEM+Papaya groups was significantly higher than in the STZ group (p < 0.05).

Evaluation of purinergic and cholinergic activities

As shown in Fig. 1a, ATPase activity was markedly elevated in the STZ-intoxicated group compared with the control group (p < 0.05). Treatment with semaglutide alone or in combination with papaya significantly reduced ATPase activity relative to the STZ group (p < 0.05); however, enzyme activity remained higher than control values in both treated groups, with comparable responses between treatments.

Similarly, ADA activity (Fig. 1b) was significantly increased in diabetic rats compared with controls (p < 0.05). Administration of semaglutide, either alone or combined with papaya, significantly attenuated ADA activity versus the STZ group (p < 0.05), although levels did not fully return to those of the control group and did not differ between treatment regimens.

Regarding cholinergic enzymes, AChE activity (Fig. 1c) was significantly elevated in the STZ group relative to controls (p < 0.05). Both semaglutide monotherapy and combination treatment significantly reduced AChE activity compared with the diabetic group (p < 0.05); however, AChE values remained higher than control levels and were comparable between the two treated groups.

In contrast, BChE activity (Fig. 1d) demonstrated a distinct response pattern. BChE levels were significantly elevated in the STZ and STZ + SEM groups compared with controls (p < 0.05).

Evaluation of β-amyloid protein deposition and neurotropic factors

As shown in Fig. 2a, GSK3β activity was significantly elevated in the STZ-intoxicated group (26.52 ± 2.30 U/mg protein) compared to the control group (7.70 ± 1.20 U/mg protein) (p < 0.05). Treatment with semaglutide alone (16.29 ± 2.47 U/mg protein) or in combination with papaya (15.46 ± 1.03 U/mg protein) significantly reduced GSK3β activity relative to the STZ group (p < 0.05), although levels remained higher than in controls. Similarly, Fig. 2b shows that beta-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (Bace1) activity was markedly increased in the STZ-intoxicated group (151.75 ± 14.11 U/mg protein) compared to controls (34.91 ± 7.06 U/mg protein) (p < 0.05). Semaglutide-treated rats (75.19 ± 7.71 U/mg protein) and the semaglutide–papaya combination group (68.90 ± 9.22 U/mg protein) exhibited significantly reduced Bace1 activity compared to the STZ group (p < 0.05), but levels remained elevated relative to controls.

As illustrated in Fig. 2c, BDNF levels were significantly reduced in the STZ-intoxicated group (47.96 ± 2.56 ng/g tissue) compared to the control group (89.49 ± 2.26 ng/g tissue) (p < 0.05). Treatment with semaglutide alone (64.85 ± 4.79 ng/g tissue) or in combination with papaya (74.73 ± 5.42 ng/g tissue) significantly increased BDNF levels compared to the STZ group (p < 0.05), though they remained below control values. Similarly, Fig. 2d shows that insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) levels were markedly decreased in the STZ-intoxicated rats (4.33 ± 0.48 ng/g tissue) versus controls (13.99 ± 1.13 ng/g tissue) (p < 0.05). Semaglutide-treated animals (8.51 ± 0.31 ng/g tissue) and those receiving the semaglutide–papaya combination (9.80 ± 1.32 ng/g tissue) demonstrated significantly increased IGF-1 levels relative to the STZ group (p < 0.05), although values did not fully return to those of controls.

Discussion

In the current study, the combination of semaglutide and papaya effectively improved glycemic control and lipid profile parameters in diabetic rats. As hyperglycemia is a well-recognized contributor to the pathogenesis of diabetic encephalopathy through promoting oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and insulin resistance, restoring blood glucose to near-normal levels is a critical therapeutic target24,25. Eight weeks of high-fat diet feeding followed by STZ administration successfully induced persistent hyperglycemia in this model, mimicking human type 2 diabetes pathology. Consistent with previous reports26, the diabetic rats displayed marked increases in fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, TAG, and LDL cholesterol, accompanied by reduced HDL cholesterol, confirming a dyslipidemic and hyperglycemic state. Treatment with semaglutide and papaya significantly reduced hyperglycemia and improved the lipid profile. These benefits can be attributed to semaglutide’s insulinotropic and glucose-lowering actions via GLP-1 receptor activation, together with the antioxidant and hypolipidemic phytochemicals in papaya, such as flavonoids and carotenoids, which have been reported to support glycemic and lipid homeostasis26. By addressing these metabolic disturbances, the combination therapy may indirectly protect neuronal integrity and cognitive function.

Oxidative stress is a well-established driver of neuronal injury in diabetic encephalopathy, as chronic hyperglycemia elevates reactive oxygen species, disrupts mitochondrial function, and accelerates lipid peroxidation26,27. In the present study, diabetic rats showed significantly elevated MDA and NO levels, reflecting severe oxidative damage. Additionally, antioxidant enzyme activities (GSH, SOD, CAT, GST) were markedly diminished, indicating an overwhelmed endogenous antioxidant defense. These alterations are consistent with previous observations linking oxidative imbalance to cognitive impairment in diabetes28. Administration of semaglutide plus papaya substantially restored antioxidant status, lowering MDA and NO while enhancing the activities of key antioxidant enzymes. This suggests a neuroprotective effect mediated by redox homeostasis. Papaya’s bioactive phenols, particularly ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid, and caffeic acid, are known to scavenge free radicals, while semaglutide has been reported to indirectly improve antioxidant defense through modulation of metabolic and inflammatory pathways29. Together, these mechanisms support the observed restoration of oxidative balance, which is essential for protecting neuronal structures against hyperglycemia-induced damage. Papaya-derived phenolics appear to selectively reinforce redox-sensitive and cholinergic pathways that are not maximally targeted by GLP-1 receptor activation.

Purinergic signaling plays an important role in maintaining neuronal communication and regulating glial–neuronal interactions within the central nervous system29. In the current model, increased ATPase and ADA activities were observed in diabetic rats, suggesting impaired purinergic neurotransmission and reduced extracellular adenosine availability. Since adenosine acts as an anti-inflammatory neuromodulator, its breakdown by ADA may compromise neuroprotective processes30,31. Treatment with semaglutide combined with papaya significantly reduced these enzyme activities, potentially preserving adenosine’s protective actions and enhancing purinergic signaling. Similarly, cholinergic neurotransmission is essential for cognitive processes, and disruption of acetylcholine balance can contribute to memory deficits in diabetic encephalopathy32,33. Our results showed elevated AChE and BChE activities in diabetic rats, indicating excessive acetylcholine breakdown, which may impair synaptic function and exacerbate cognitive dysfunction. Semaglutide-papaya therapy normalized these cholinesterase activities, which could help maintain cholinergic neurotransmission and support memory-related pathways. The ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid, and caffeic acid in papaya may additionally modulate cholinergic enzyme activities, complementing semaglutide’s neuroprotective role34,35. Overall, these findings highlight a possible synergistic benefit of the combination in preserving both purinergic and cholinergic pathways, contributing to better cognitive resilience under diabetic conditions.

Amyloid protein accumulation is a hallmark of diabetic encephalopathy, contributing to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss36,37. In the current study, STZ-induced diabetic rats showed significantly increased GSK3β and Bace1 activity, enzymes critically involved in amyloid precursor protein processing and β-amyloid deposition38–40. Overactivation of GSK3β in diabetic conditions has been linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, enhanced oxidative stress, and upregulated β-secretase activity, creating a cascade of neuronal injury41–43. Treatment with semaglutide alone or in combination with papaya markedly reduced GSK3β and Bace1 levels, suggesting attenuation of amyloidogenic pathways. This protective effect may result from semaglutide’s modulation of insulin signaling through the PI3K/Akt/GSK3β axis, thereby reducing β-amyloid formation44,45. Papaya, with its antioxidant phenols, may further counteract oxidative stress, which otherwise promotes amyloid aggregation46. These complementary actions potentially explain the improved preservation of neuronal architecture observed histologically in the hippocampus and cortex. Together, these results support a synergistic neuroprotective mechanism targeting amyloid pathology, which is a key driver of cognitive decline in diabetic encephalopathy.

Neurotrophic factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) are fundamental for neuronal survival, plasticity, and synaptic integrity44,45. In this study, STZ-induced diabetic rats showed a significant downregulation of BDNF and IGF-1, consistent with impaired neurogenesis and increased susceptibility to neurodegeneration. Semaglutide alone or combined with papaya markedly restored BDNF and IGF-1 levels, suggesting a supportive environment for neuronal repair47. Moreover, disruption of the insulin signaling cascade, particularly through reduced InsR and IRS-1 expression, is a well-known feature of diabetic encephalopathy48. Impaired insulin signaling leads to unrestrained GSK3β activity, further promoting neuroinflammation and amyloid deposition49,50. The current findings demonstrated that semaglutide–papaya therapy improved the expression of InsR and IRS-1, indicating restoration of insulin signaling and suppression of GSK3β overactivation through scavenging of ROS by papaya antioxidants. This dual regulation of neurotrophic and insulin pathways may explain the significant neuroprotective effects seen with the combination, offering a mechanistic rationale for synergistic action rather than a merely additive benefit. Although formal synergy analyses were not conducted, the data indicate a pathway-selective complementarity rather than global synergism. Semaglutide primarily mediated metabolic and insulin-signaling improvements, whereas papaya selectively enhanced redox homeostasis and cholinergic balance. This division of mechanistic action supports a biologically meaningful combinatorial effect without overstating statistical synergy.

Chronic hyperglycemia in diabetes is closely linked to a persistent inflammatory response, which aggravates neuronal damage and cognitive decline51,52. In the present study, STZ-induced diabetic rats exhibited marked increases in proinflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6, and NFkB-1 within hippocampal tissue, highlighting a pronounced neuroinflammatory state. Such cytokines can disrupt insulin signaling by promoting serine phosphorylation of IRS-1, further worsening insulin resistance in the brain53,54. Treatment with semaglutide alone or in combination with papaya significantly reduced these proinflammatory mediators, suggesting attenuation of neuroinflammation. Semaglutide’s GLP-1 receptor-mediated anti-inflammatory actions, together with papaya’s antioxidant phytochemicals, may work in concert to inhibit upstream inflammatory triggers and restore insulin pathway balance. This synergistic immunomodulatory effect is essential in limiting neuronal apoptosis and preserving cognitive function under diabetic conditions.

Apoptosis represents a key mechanism underlying neuronal loss in diabetic encephalopathy. Markers of apoptosis, including elevated Bax and caspase-3 along with reduced Bcl-2, were significantly dysregulated in the STZ-induced diabetic group in this study, indicating enhanced pro-apoptotic signaling and mitochondrial dysfunction55,56. These molecular events contribute to neuronal degeneration and subsequent cognitive impairment. Semaglutide, either alone or combined with papaya, effectively modulated these apoptotic markers by downregulating Bax and caspase-3 while upregulating Bcl-2. Such modulation suggests protection against mitochondria-dependent apoptotic pathways. The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of papaya phenols and flavonoids may further complement semaglutide’s neuroprotective role, helping to maintain neuronal viability and structural integrity. Together, this combination provides a promising approach to reduce neuronal apoptosis, thereby preserving hippocampal and cortical networks essential for cognitive processes57,58.

Collectively, these findings highlight for the first time the synergistic potential of combining semaglutide with papaya, targeting multiple mechanistic pathways implicated in diabetic encephalopathy. The combination effectively restored glucose and lipid profiles, reduced oxidative stress, suppressed neuroinflammation, modulated amyloidogenic and apoptotic pathways, and enhanced neurotrophic and insulin signaling, offering comprehensive neuroprotection. These effects are attributed to the complementary actions of semaglutide, primarily via GLP-1 receptor-mediated insulin sensitization and anti-inflammatory activity, and papaya, through its phytochemical antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and antiapoptotic properties. While these promising results in the rat model cannot be directly extrapolated to humans, they nonetheless lay a scientific foundation for future translational research and clinical trials to explore combinatorial strategies addressing both metabolic and neurodegenerative complications of diabetes. Study limitations

While this study provides novel insights into the potential synergistic neuroprotective effects of semaglutide combined with papaya in diabetic encephalopathy, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, a semaglutide-only group at the low dose (0.3 mg/kg) was not included, which limits the ability to fully attribute synergistic effects of the combination treatment. Second, no behavioral or cognitive assessments, such as the Morris water maze, were performed to functionally validate the neuroprotective outcomes, leaving questions about effects on learning and memory. This experimental model was designed to investigate diabetic encephalopathy rather than hepatic encephalopathy; therefore, ammonia levels, liver histopathology, or other indices of hepatic dysfunction were not assessed. Immunohistochemical localization of insulin or insulin receptor expression in brain tissue was not performed and represents a valuable direction for future studies. Additionally, the study utilized a single dose regimen without exploring potential dose–response relationships, and the treatment duration was relatively short (eight weeks), which may not reflect long-term outcomes. Dose–response evaluation was beyond the scope of this study and will be addressed in future investigations. Moreover, the findings are based on a single animal model and cannot yet be generalized to human diabetic encephalopathy without further clinical validation. Finally, no histological immunostaining or western blotting for neurotrophic proteins was performed to confirm protein-level changes, which would have strengthened mechanistic insights. Despite these limitations, the study presents a promising therapeutic concept by integrating semaglutide and papaya, highlighting a potential combinatorial approach to target oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and neuroinflammation. These findings lay a valuable foundation for future research in diabetic neurodegeneration.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated, for the first time, the potential synergistic neuroprotective effects of combining semaglutide with papaya in a rat model of streptozotocin-induced diabetic encephalopathy. The combination therapy effectively improved glycemic and lipid profiles, mitigated oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, reduced amyloidogenic and apoptotic markers, and enhanced neurotrophic and insulin signaling pathways, collectively preserving neuronal structure and function. These findings highlight a promising multipathway therapeutic approach for addressing neurodegenerative complications of diabetes. Nevertheless, further preclinical and clinical investigations are warranted to validate these results and explore their translational relevance in human diabetic populations.

Animals and ethical statement

Forty male Wistar albino rats, with an average body weight of 180 ± 10 g, were purchased from the Animal House Colony at the National Research Centre in Cairo, Egypt. The animals were housed under controlled conditions at a temperature of 21 ± 2 °C, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. During the acclimatization period, the rats were fed a standard diet containing 23% crude protein, 7% crude fiber, 3% crude fat, 8% acid-insoluble ash, 1–2.5% calcium, 0.5–1% phosphorus, and 9% sodium. They had free access to water ad libitum. Ethical approval for the animal experiments was obtained from the Hygiene and Preventive Medicine Research Committee, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Damanhur University, Egypt (approval number: DMU/VetMed-2024/032). All procedures were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional and international guidelines and regulations. The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Experimental design

After a two-week acclimatization period, male albino rats aged six weeks were randomly allocated into four equal groups (n = 10 per group). Animals in the negative control group (CTR) were maintained on a standard commercial rat chow, whereas the other three groups were fed a high-glucose, high-fat diet consisting of 15% fat, 10% sucrose, 3% cholesterol, 3% sodium chloride, and 69% standard feed for eight week according to Ramuet al., (2021)59. The high-glucose, high-fat diet was specially formulated and prepared under the authors’ supervision, in collaboration with a local commercial feed vendor, following the specified composition to ensure consistency and quality. After this dietary induction phase, type 2 diabetes was induced in the second, third, and fourth groups via a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (35 mg/kg body weight)37 dissolved in citrate buffer. Control animals received equivalent volumes of citrate buffer alone. Rats were considered diabetic if their fasting blood glucose exceeded 16.7 mmol/L, accompanied by a 50% increase in urine output compared to the control group and significant 24-hour proteinuria60. Following confirmation of diabetes, treatment protocols were initiated. The second group (STZ) served as the diabetic control without further intervention, while the third group (STZ + SEM) received 0.9 mg/kg semaglutide subcutaneously61. The fourth group (STZ + SEM+papaya) received 0.3 mg/kg semaglutide in combination with 1 ml of 10% papaya pulp juice administered orally62. Semaglutide doses were selected based on previously validated neuroprotective and metabolic studies, while the papaya dose was chosen to reflect a nutritionally relevant antioxidant intake without inducing caloric overload. All treatments continued for eight weeks63. At the end of the experiment, animals were anesthetized with ketamine (87 mg/kg) and xylazine (13 mg/kg) before sacrifice by cervical dislocation in accordance with ethical guidelines64. Blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture and centrifuged at 1008 g to separate serum, which was stored at − 20 °C for subsequent biochemical assays, including fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL, and LDL. The cerebral cortex and hippocampus were quickly harvested; the hippocampus was subdivided into three parts: one preserved at − 80 °C for gene expression analysis, another homogenized and stored at − 20 °C for evaluation of neurotrophic factors, enzymes, and oxidative stress markers, and a third fixed in 10% buffered formalin for histological examination. No. 10294), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL; Cat. No. 10284) were evaluated spectrophotometrically using commercial diagnostic kits (Human Scient Co., Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Assessment of oxidative/antioxidative markers in hippocampal tissues

Following euthanasia, the hippocampal tissues were immediately excised, weighed, homogenized, and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min in a buffer containing 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na₂HPO₄, and 2 mM KH₂PO₄ (pH 7.4). The resulting supernatants were used to assess reduced glutathione (GSH; Cat. No. GR 25 11), glutathione-S-transferase (GST; Cat. No. GSH 25 19), catalase (CAT; Cat. No. CAT 25 17), superoxide dismutase (SOD; Cat. No. SD 25 21), nitric oxide (NO; Cat. No. NO 25 33), and malondialdehyde (MDA; Cat. No. MD 25 29), using commercial kits (Bio-Diagnostic Co., Cairo, Egypt) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

Assessment of β-amyloid protein, neurotropic markers, purinergic and cholinergic activities in hippocampus tissues

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were used to quantify several neural biomarkers in hippocampal tissue homogenates, including brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), glycogen synthase kinase-3 beta (GSK3β), beta-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (Bace1), adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase), adenosine deaminase (ADA), acetylcholinesterase (AChE), and butyrylcholinesterase (BChE). All ELISA kits were obtained from MyBioSource (San Diego, CA, USA). The catalog numbers were as follows: GSK3β (MBS766198), Bace1 (MBS2886958), BDNF (MBS824814), IGF-1 (MBS176012), ATPase (MBS264916), ADA (MBS267634), AChE (MBS725468), and BChE (MBS2022207). The assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek ELx800 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, USA), and concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated with each kit. Results were expressed as ng/mg of protein.

Assessment of mRNA expression of InsR, IRS1, TNFα, NFKβ, IL6, caspase3, BAX, and BCL2

Total RNA was extracted from hippocampal tissue samples using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany, Cat. No. 74104), following standard protocols65. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the RevertAid™ First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, Cat. No. K1622) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions66. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed with SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 4309155) in a final reaction volume of 25 µL per sample, using gene-specific primers ranging from 16 to 24 base pairs as listed in Table 5. The amplification process was conducted on a MyCycler thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, Germany). The genes analyzed included insulin receptor (InsR), insulin receptor substrate-1 (IRS-1), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), nuclear factor-kappa B-1 (NFκB-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), caspase-3, Bcl-2-associated protein X (Bax), and B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), with β-actin selected as the endogenous housekeeping gene (Table 6)67. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method68, with results expressed as fold changes compared to the control group. Each qPCR assay was performed in triplicate to ensure accuracy, and statistical significance of differences between groups was evaluated accordingly.

Specimen processing for microscopic examination

Following euthanasia, the rats’ brains were preserved in 10% buffered formalin for 48 h. The samples were then cleared in xylene, gradually dehydrated through ascending grades of ethyl alcohol, and finally embedded and blocked in paraffin. Sections of 4 μm thickness from the different groups were cut using a microtome, mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)69. The stained sections were examined under a trinocular light microscope (Leica DM500), and photomicrographs were captured using a digital camera (Leica EC3; Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

The sample size of 10 animals per group was selected based on comparable preclinical reports to achieve sufficient statistical power. All data were expressed as means ± standard error (SE). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 20 (SPSS, Richmond, VA, USA). One-way ANOVA was applied to test differences among groups, followed by Duncan’s post hoc test to identify pairwise differences, with significance defined at p < 0.05. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to verify assumptions of independence, normality, and homogeneity of variances before applying ANOVA.
