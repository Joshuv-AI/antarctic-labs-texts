HNRNPA0 Acts as a Master Upstream Regulator of NF-κB to Drive Oncogenic Phenotypes in Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: Hongfeng Zang, Wei Wang, Bowen Zhao, Hui Jin, Shan Yang, Chang Li, Liu Yang, Lili Li, Wuliji Saiyin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remains a lethal malignancy with a 5-year survival rate below 50%, necessitating the identification of novel therapeutic targets. While the RNA-binding protein HNRNPA0 is implicated in various cancers, its precise role and mechanism in OSCC pathogenesis are undefined.MethodsHNRNPA0 expression was assessed using TCGA data, clinical OSCC specimens (n = 10) and cell lines. Stable knockdown models were established in CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells via shRNA. Functional impacts on proliferation, metastasis, apoptosis and cell cycle were evaluated through CCK-8, wound healing, Transwell, flow cytometry and xenograft assays. RNA sequencing and rescue experiments with the NF-κB agonist PMA were employed to delineate the underlying mechanism.ResultsHNRNPA0 was significantly upregulated in OSCC tissues and cell lines, and its high expression predicted poor patient survival. HNRNPA0 knockdown potently suppressed tumour growth both in vitro and in vivo. It inhibited proliferation, migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and cell cycle progression, while promoting apoptosis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed a profound impact on the NF-κB signalling pathway. Crucially, activation of this pathway by PMA substantially reversed all anti-tumour phenotypes caused by HNRNPA0 depletion, establishing NF-κB as the critical functional downstream effector.ConclusionOur study identifies HNRNPA0 as a key oncoprotein in OSCC, driving tumour progression primarily via activation of the NF-κB pathway. HNRNPA0 represents a promising prognostic biomarker and a novel therapeutic target for OSCC.Key words: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, HNRNPA0, NF-κB pathway, Tumour progression, Epithelial-mesenchymal transition, Therapeutic target

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), the most prevalent malignancy of the head and neck, represents more than 90% of all oral cancers.1 Global estimates from 2022 recorded approximately 380,000 new OSCC cases and 180,000 deaths, with an annual incidence rising by 2% to 3%.2 Although multimodal treatments – combining surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy – have advanced, the 5‑year survival rate remains under 50%, largely due to local recurrence and distant metastasis.3 Known risk factors include tobacco use, alcohol consumption, human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and betel quid chewing.3, 4, 5, 6 The pronounced tumour heterogeneity and intricate molecular landscape contribute to limited therapeutic success, underscoring the need to delineate key drivers of OSCC progression for the development of targeted strategies.

Heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A0 (HNRNPA0) belongs to the HNRNP family, which is critically involved in various aspects of RNA metabolism, such as transcriptional regulation, mRNA stability and translation.7, 8, 9 Growing evidence implicates other HNRNP members in cancer; for instance, HNRNPA2B1 modulates interferon response via viral DNA sensing and facilitates gastric cancer progression through m6A‑independent mechanisms.10,11 In contrast, the role of HNRNPA0 in cancer remains poorly characterised. Bioinformatic analyses indicate that HNRNPA0 is aberrantly overexpressed in several malignancies, hinting at a possible oncogenic function. Notably, in OSCC, HNRNPA0 expression is markedly elevated and associated with unfavourable prognosis, yet its precise mechanistic contributions are still unclear.

The nuclear factor kappa B (NF‑κB) signalling pathway serves as a central regulator of inflammation, cell survival, proliferation and differentiation.12, 13, 14 In OSCC, constitutive NF‑κB activation promotes tumour progression by upregulating anti‑apoptotic genes (eg, BCL2) and inducing epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT).15,16 Elevated phosphorylation of the NF‑κB p65 subunit is frequently observed in OSCC tissues and correlates positively with depth of invasion and lymph node metastasis.17,18 Moreover, upstream regulators such as TTK kinase activate NF‑κB through the TAK1‑TAB complex, while the metabolic enzyme ALDOA enhances NF‑κB transcriptional activity via nuclear translocation, collectively forming a sophisticated network that fuels OSCC aggressiveness.19 Despite established roles of NF‑κB in OSCC, its full spectrum of upstream regulators remains incompletely defined.

Here, we systematically investigated the expression and function of HNRNPA0 in OSCC. Integrating in‑house clinical samples with publicly available TCGA and GEPIA2 datasets, we confirmed HNRNPA0 overexpression in OSCC. Using shRNA‑mediated knockdown in CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cell lines complemented by in vivo xenograft models, we demonstrated the tumour‑promoting capacity of HNRNPA0. Through bioinformatic prediction and tailored rescue experiments, we further identified and validated a functional link between HNRNPA0 and NF‑κB activation. Together, our findings establish HNRNPA0 as a novel contributor to OSCC pathogenesis, acting in part through NF‑κB pathway induction, and propose it as a potential therapeutic target in this malignancy.

Results

Bioinformatic analysis of HNRNPA0 expression in pan-cancer and OSCC tissues

To systematically characterise the expression profile of HNRNPA0, we initiated a pan-cancer investigation utilising RNA‑Seq data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). This analysis demonstrated consistent and significant upregulation of HNRNPA0 across multiple malignancies when compared with matched adjacent nontumour tissues (Figure 1A-C). Notably, within head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), evaluation of the TCGA‑HNSC cohort revealed substantially elevated HNRNPA0 mRNA levels in tumour specimens relative to normal counterparts (Figure 1D).

We next assessed the clinical relevance of HNRNPA0 by evaluating its prognostic significance in several squamous cell carcinoma settings. Survival analyses indicated that aberrant HNRNPA0 expression was significantly correlated with patient outcomes across multiple SCC subtypes (Figure 1E-H). Subsequent focused examination of the oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) sub‑cohort from TCGA further established that high HNRNPA0 expression was associated with reduced overall survival, underscoring its adverse prognostic impact in OSCC patients (Figure 1I).

HNRNPA0 is upregulated in OSCC tissues and cell lines

To assess the clinical relevance of HNRNPA0 in OSCC at the protein level, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on matched patient specimens. HNRNPA0 protein expression was significantly elevated in OSCC tissues (n = 10) compared with adjacent normal tissues (n = 10) (Figure 2A). Consistent with this observation, Western blot analysis of protein lysates from the same patient cohort confirmed upregulation of HNRNPA0 protein in tumour tissues relative to their matched normal counterparts (Figure 2B and C).

We further evaluated HNRNPA0 expression in a panel of human OSCC cell lines. Both qRT‑PCR and Western blot analyses demonstrated markedly higher HNRNPA0 mRNA and protein levels in SAS, CAL‑27, SCC‑15 and Tca‑8113 cells compared with non‑tumorigenic human oral keratinocyte (HOK) controls (Figure 2D-F). Based on these expression profiles, CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells, which exhibited the highest HNRNPA0 levels, were selected for subsequent functional studies. Given that CAL‑27 cells showed the most pronounced upregulation, this line was further utilised for transcriptomic sequencing to elucidate genome‑wide expression changes associated with HNRNPA0 dysregulation.

HNRNPA0 promotes OSCC tumour growth both in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the functional role of HNRNPA0 in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), we established stable HNRNPA0-knockdown models in CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells using lentiviral vectors encoding specific shRNAs. Efficient depletion of HNRNPA0 was confirmed at both the protein and transcript levels by Western blot and quantitative PCR (qPCR). One shRNA construct yielded the most pronounced knockdown and was therefore selected for all subsequent functional assays (Figure 3A-C, E-G).

We next examined the effect of HNRNPA0 silencing on cell proliferation using CCK‑8 assays. HNRNPA0 knockdown significantly reduced the proliferative capacity of both CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cell lines compared with control cells (Figure 3D and H).

To further evaluate the therapeutic relevance of targeting HNRNPA0 in vivo, we employed subcutaneous xenograft models. Tumour growth was markedly attenuated in HNRNPA0-knockdown groups relative to controls for both cell lines (Figure 3K and O). Consistent with this, tumours derived from knockdown cells were visibly smaller (Figure 3I, J, M and N) and exhibited a significant reduction in average tumour weight (Figure 3L and P).These findings indicate that HNRNPA0 promotes OSCC cell proliferation and tumour growth, underscoring its functional importance in OSCC pathogenesis.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals HNRNPA0 modulates immune and structural pathways via NF-κB

RNA sequencing analysis revealed that knockdown of HNRNPA0 led to extensive transcriptomic reprogramming, with 2999 genes uniquely expressed relative to control samples (Figure 4A). Among these, 529 genes were identified as differentially expressed (DEGs), comprising 333 upregulated and 196 downregulated transcripts (Figure 4B). Unsupervised clustering of the DEGs clearly distinguished control from HNRNPA0‑knockdown groups (Figure 4C). Functional enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs were prominently enriched in biological processes related to immune/inflammatory responses and the maintenance of cellular structural integrity (Figure 4D). Consistent with this, pathway analysis further highlighted significant enrichment of the NF‑κB signalling pathway and associated networks (Figure 4E and F). Through an integrated assessment, the NF‑κB pathway was consistently implicated as the central downstream mechanism underlying the oncogenic functions of HNRNPA0.

HNRNPA0 promotes OSCC cell proliferation via the NF-κB signalling pathway

To determine whether the NF-κB pathway acts as a key downstream effector of HNRNPA0, we conducted a series of rescue experiments. Western blot analysis confirmed that HNRNPA0 knockdown effectively reduced phosphorylation of key NF-κB components, such as p‑p65, in both CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 OSCC cell lines, indicating attenuated pathway activity (Figure 4G, H, J and K). To functionally link HNRNPA0 to NF‑κB signalling, we treated the knockdown cells with the NF‑κB agonist PMA. This treatment restored NF‑κB activation, as shown by recovered phosphorylation of pathway proteins (Figure 4G, H, J and K), supporting the dependence of HNRNPA0‑mediated proliferation on this pathway.

We next examined the phenotypic impact using CCK‑8 assays. HNRNPA0 knockdown significantly suppressed proliferation in both cell lines, and importantly, this suppression was largely reversed by PMA treatment (Figure 4I and L). Together, these biochemical and functional data demonstrate that HNRNPA0 promotes OSCC cell proliferation by maintaining activation of the NF‑κB signalling pathway.

HNRNPA0 knockdown suppresses migration and invasion of OSCC cells by modulating NF-κB signalling

To assess the contribution of HNRNPA0 to the metastatic potential of oral squamous cell carcinoma, we examined its effects on cell migration and invasion using wound healing and Transwell assays, respectively. Silencing HNRNPA0 markedly reduced the migratory ability of CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells, reflected by significantly delayed wound closure compared with controls (Figure 5A-D). Similarly, Transwell invasion assays showed that HNRNPA0 depletion substantially decreased the number of cells penetrating the Matrigel barrier (Figure 5E-H). Importantly, these defects in both migration and invasion were partially yet significantly rescued by treatment with the NF‑κB agonist PMA, indicating the functional engagement of NF‑κB signalling in HNRNPA0‑driven metastatic phenotypes. Quantitative analysis confirmed statistically significant differences among control, knockdown and PMA‑rescued groups in all assays. Together, these results indicate that HNRNPA0 critically supports OSCC cell migration and invasion, at least in part through activation of the NF‑κB pathway.

HNRNPA0 regulates EMT in oral squamous cell carcinoma through the NF-κB signalling pathway

To determine whether HNRNPA0 modulates epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the NF‑κB pathway in oral squamous cell carcinoma, we examined key EMT markers in CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells after HNRNPA0 knockdown. Silencing HNRNPA0 led to a marked increase in the epithelial marker E‑cadherin and a decrease in the mesenchymal markers N‑cadherin and Vimentin relative to control cells (Figure 5I-T). Notably, these alterations in EMT‑related protein expression were largely reversed by treatment with the NF‑κB agonist PMA, underscoring the functional role of NF‑κB signalling in HNRNPA0‑driven EMT. Together, these results indicate that HNRNPA0 promotes EMT in OSCC, a process that depends critically on NF‑κB pathway activation.

HNRNPA0 suppresses apoptosis in OSCC through the NF-κB signalling pathway

To examine the role of HNRNPA0 in regulating apoptosis in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), we performed Annexin V‑FITC/PI staining followed by flow cytometry. HNRNPA0 knockdown significantly increased apoptosis in both CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells compared with controls (Figure 6A-C). This pro‑apoptotic effect was largely attenuated by co‑treatment with an NF‑κB pathway agonist, supporting the involvement of this pathway in the anti‑apoptotic function of HNRNPA0. Correspondingly, Western blot analysis of apoptotic markers showed that HNRNPA0 silencing elevated the Cleaved Caspase‑3/Pro‑Caspase‑3 ratio, a key indicator of apoptotic activation. Consistent with the flow cytometry results, this increase was reversed upon NF‑κB activation (Figure 6D-G). Together, these data demonstrate that HNRNPA0 inhibits apoptosis in OSCC cells in an NF‑κB‑dependent manner.

HNRNPA0 regulates cell cycle progression in OSCC via the NF-κB signalling pathway

To assess the impact of HNRNPA0 on the cell cycle in oral squamous cell carcinoma, we analysed cell cycle distribution by flow cytometry after HNRNPA0 knockdown. Silencing HNRNPA0 led to a pronounced arrest at the G0/G1 phase in both CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells (Figure 6H-J). Notably, this arrest was markedly reversed by treatment with an NF‑κB agonist, indicating that HNRNPA0 regulates the cell cycle through NF‑κB signalling. These results suggest that HNRNPA0 promotes OSCC cell cycle progression by attenuating G1/S checkpoint control, in an NF‑κB‑dependent manner.

Discussion

OSCC remains the most common and aggressive malignancy of the head and neck, characterised by an aggressive clinical course with high morbidity and mortality.20 Despite continued research and therapeutic advances, 5-year survival rates have persistently remained below 50% for decades.3 The clinical management of OSCC continues to present a significant challenge, due largely to considerable tumour heterogeneity, a high frequency of local recurrence and distant metastasis.21 Although multimodal treatment strategies have improved, the lack of reliable molecular biomarkers for accurate prognosis and effective targeted therapy remains a major obstacle to improving patient outcomes. Therefore, clarifying the core molecular drivers of OSCC pathogenesis is essential for developing new diagnostic and therapeutic approaches.

Our observation that HNRNPA0 is upregulated in OSCC aligns with and extends growing pan-cancer evidence implicating members of the HNRNP family in tumorigenesis. For example, the related protein HNRNPU is overexpressed in gastric cancer, where it promotes malignant progression by regulating alternative splicing and other RNA metabolic processes.22,23 The consistent overexpression of various HNRNP members across cancer types suggests a fundamental role in oncogenesis.22, 23, 24 The correlation between high HNRNPA0 expression and poor survival in the TCGA OSCC cohort underscores its potential as a prognostic biomarker, which could assist in patient risk stratification. The pro-tumorigenic phenotypes observed following HNRNPA0 depletion – including impaired proliferation, cell cycle arrest, reduced invasion and increased apoptosis – collectively provide a functional explanation for its association with aggressive disease.

A key finding of this study is the elucidation of a mechanistic link between HNRNPA0 and the NF-κB pathway. Transcriptomic profiling after HNRNPA0 knockdown revealed significant enrichment of NF-κB-related pathways. This bioinformatic prediction was strongly supported by functional rescue experiments: PMA-induced activation of NF-κB substantially reversed the anti-proliferative, anti-migratory, pro-apoptotic, cell cycle-arresting and EMT-inhibitory effects resulting from HNRNPA0 knockdown. These results provide compelling evidence that NF-κB acts as a major downstream effector of HNRNPA0 in OSCC. While the importance of NF-κB in OSCC has been previously recognised,25, 26, 27 and bioinformatic analyses had suggested a possible connection with HNRNPA0, our work offers direct experimental validation of this regulatory axis, establishing HNRNPA0 as a novel upstream regulator.

The functional roles of HNRNPA0 identified here appear to be largely mediated through NF-κB activation. HNRNPA0 promoted proliferation and cell cycle progression, consistent with the known role of NF-κB in driving cyclin D1 expression and facilitating G1/S transition.28, 29, 30 HNRNPA0 also enhanced migration, invasion and EMT – processes critically dependent on NF-κB-mediated upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases, cytokines and transcriptional repressors of E-cadherin.15,16 Furthermore, the anti-apoptotic function of HNRNPA0 aligns with the ability of NF-κB to transactivate anti-apoptotic genes such as *BCL-2* and XIAP.30 Our findings are consistent with prior studies showing that NF-κB p65 is frequently overexpressed in OSCC and correlates with advanced TNM stage and poor prognosis.

The tumour microenvironment (TME) plays a crucial role in OSCC progression.16,31,32 Interestingly, our RNA-seq data following HNRNPA0 knockdown indicated alterations in immune and inflammatory responses. This raises the possibility that HNRNPA0 may influence the OSCC TME, potentially via NF-κB-driven secretion of cytokines and chemokines. While the present study focused on cell-autonomous effects, future work should explore whether HNRNPA0 contributes to shaping an immunosuppressive TME, similar to mechanisms described for other m⁶A regulators such as HNRNPC in head and neck cancer.32 The potential influence of HNRNPA0 on therapy response also warrants investigation, given that resistance to agents like cisplatin remains a major clinical challenge in OSCC. Determining whether HNRNPA0 expression affects sensitivity to conventional chemotherapy or emerging immunotherapies represents a promising direction for future research.

In summary, our study identifies HNRNPA0 as a critical oncoprotein in OSCC pathogenesis and a novel activator of the NF-κB pathway. These findings advance our understanding of molecular networks in OSCC and highlight HNRNPA0 as a promising candidate for the development of prognostic biomarkers and targeted therapies against this aggressive malignancy.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis of HNRNPA0

Survival analysis was conducted to assess the association between HNRNPA0 mRNA expression and overall survival in the TCGA-HNSCC cohort. Patients were stratified into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression level of HNRNPA0. The analysis was carried out using the publicly available GEPIA2 online platform (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/).

Clinical samples

Paired clinical specimens (tumour tissues and matched adjacent noncancerous tissues) were collected from 10 patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) who underwent radical surgery at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, First Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (HMUFAH). Immediately after resection, each tissue sample was divided into 2 portions. One portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin for subsequent morphological examination. The other portion was stabilised in RNA later solution and stored at −80 °C to preserve RNA integrity for molecular analyses.

All patients were treatment-naïve, with no history of preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to inclusion in the study. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of HMUFAH.

Cell lines and culture

Four human oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cell lines were utilised in this study. CAL-27 and SCC-15 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The SAS cell line was acquired from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB), and Tca-8113 was sourced from Aopeisai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. For normal control comparisons, human oral keratinocytes (HOK) were purchased from ScienCell.

All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, cat. no. 6124166) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; HyCyte, cat. no. FBP-C520) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL; NCM Biotech). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, with media refreshed every 2 to 3 days. Routine morphological observation and viability checks were performed to ensure consistent growth prior to experimentation.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from both cell lines and tissue specimens using RNAkey Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Seven Biotech; cat. no. SM109-02). RNA quality and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically. Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised from 1 µg of total RNA using the SevenClever First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Seven Biotech; cat. no. SM136-01), according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the 2 × SYBR Green qPCR MasterMix II (Seven Biotech; cat. no. SM143-01) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Reactions were run in triplicate under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 seconds and a combined annealing/extension step at 60 °C for 20 seconds, with a final extension at 72 °C for 15 seconds.

The expression of the target gene HNRNPA0 was normalised to that of the housekeeping gene GAPDH. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the comparative threshold cycle (ΔΔCt) method. The primer sequences used were as follows:

HNRNPA0:

Forward: 5′-GATTATTGCCGACAAGCAGTCCG-3′

Reverse: 5′-GGACTGCTTTCTTCACCTCCAC-3′

GAPDH:

Forward: 5′-TGTGTCCGTCGTGGATCTGA-3′

Reverse: 5′-CCTGCTTCACCACCTTCTTGA-3′

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from tissue samples and cultured cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Seven Biotech) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Seven Biotech, SW103-02). Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Seven Biotech, SW201-02) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Equal amounts of protein from each sample were resolved by SDS‑polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‑PAGE) and then transferred electrophoretically onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked for 1 hour at room temperature with 5% non‑fat milk prepared in Tris‑buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‑20 (TBST). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour.

Protein bands were visualised using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate, and images were captured with a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, version 1.8.0).

Establishment of stable HNRNPA0-knockdown cell lines

CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells were plated in 6‑well plates and grown to 30% to 50% confluence. Cells were then transduced with lentiviral particles encoding short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting the HNRNPA0 gene (designed and synthesised by Cyagen Biosciences) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Following 24 hours of incubation, the viral supernatant was removed and replaced with fresh complete medium. To establish polyclonal stable knockdown lines, puromycin selection (5 μg/mL) was initiated at 48 hours post‑transduction and maintained for 48 hours. Knockdown efficiency of HNRNPA0 was confirmed at both the transcriptional and protein levels using quantitative RT‑PCR (qRT‑PCR) and Western blot analysis, respectively.

Activation of NF-κB signalling

Cells from the shNC control group and the stable HNRNPA0 knockdown group were serum-starved for 16 hours prior to stimulation. To activate the NF‑κB pathway, cells were then treated with 100 nM phorbol 12‑myristate 13‑acetate (PMA) for 20 minutes. Corresponding control groups for both cell lines received an equal volume of the vehicle (0.1% DMSO) and were processed in parallel. Following treatment, cells were harvested for subsequent functional or biochemical analyses.

Cell viability assay

Following transfection and selection, cells were harvested and quantified. Subsequently, cells were seeded into 96‑well plates at a density of 5 × 10³ cells per well in 100 μL of complete medium and allowed to adhere overnight. Cell viability was assessed at 24, 48 and 72 hours using the Cell Counting Kit‑8 (CCK‑8; NCM Biotech; Cat. No. C6005). Briefly, 10 μL of CCK‑8 reagent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed with at least 5 technical replicates per condition. Data were analysed and visualised using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1).

Wound healing assay

Cell migration was evaluated using a standard wound healing assay. CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells were seeded into 6‑well plates and cultured until >95% confluence was reached. A uniform scratch was then introduced across the cell monolayer using a sterile P200 pipette tip. After gently washing twice with phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) to remove detached cells, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 2% foetal bovine serum (FBS) to minimise proliferation‑dependent closure.

Wound areas were photographed immediately (0 hours) and 24 hours after scratching using an inverted microscope. For each condition and time point, 3 independent fields were recorded per well. The relative wound closure percentage was calculated by comparing the remaining scratch area at 24 hours to the initial wound area at 0 hours, using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

Invasion assay

Cell invasion was evaluated using Transwell chambers (8-μm pores; Corning) precoated with Matrigel basement membrane matrix. CAL‑27 and SCC‑15 cells were suspended in serum‑free DMEM, and 5 × 10⁴ cells were seeded into the upper chamber of each insert. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS as a chemotactic factor. After incubation for 24 hours at 37 °C, non‑invading cells on the upper membrane surface were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had invaded to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, stained using 0.1% crystal violet for 20 minutes and rinsed gently with PBS. Invaded cells were imaged under an inverted microscope, and cell numbers were quantified by counting 3 randomly selected fields per insert using ImageJ software.

Cell cycle analysis

CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells subjected to various treatments were harvested via trypsinisation, followed by 2 washes with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were subsequently fixed in 75% ethanol (prechilled to −20 °C) and stored overnight at −20 °C. After fixation, the ethanol was removed by centrifugation, and the cell pellets were washed once with PBS before being resuspended in 500 µL of staining buffer. Cell cycle distribution was assessed using a commercially available Cell Cycle Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, 25 µL of 20 × propidium iodide (PI) solution and 10 µL of 50 × RNase A were added to each cell suspension. Samples were then incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature in the dark. DNA content was analysed on a flow cytometer (BD FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences). Data analysis was performed using ModFit LT 5.0 software (Verity Software House) to quantify the proportion of cells in G0/G1, S and G2/M phases of the cell cycle.

Cell apoptosis analysis

Following the respective treatments, cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS and resuspended in 500 µL of 1 × Annexin V binding buffer. Apoptosis was evaluated using a commercial Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC conjugate was added to the cell suspension, followed by gentle mixing and the addition of 5 µL of propidium iodide (PI) solution. After a 15-minute incubation at room temperature in the dark, the samples were analysed immediately by flow cytometry. Cell populations were classified as viable (Annexin V−/PI−), early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI−), or late apoptotic/necrotic (Annexin V+/PI+). Quantification of apoptosis was performed using FlowJo software.

In vivo tumour xenograft assay

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of HMUFAH and were conducted under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Five-week-old male BALB/c nude mice (nu/nu) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and randomly divided into 2 experimental groups (n = 5 per group). CAL-27 and SCC-15 cells stably expressing either shNC (scramble negative control) or shHNRNPA0 were collected and resuspended in sterile PBS. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 5 × 10⁶ cells in 200 µL PBS into the right flank.

Tumour formation was monitored regularly, and tumour size was measured every 3 days beginning on day 1 postinjection using a digital calliper. Tumour volume was estimated according to the formula: Volume = (length × width²)/2. On day 22, all mice were euthanised by cervical dislocation under deep anaesthesia. Tumours were carefully dissected, photographed and weighed. For histological examination, tumour tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours and subsequently processed for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens from cancerous tissues and matched adjacent nontumour regions were sectioned at 4 μm using a microtome. Tissue sections underwent deparaffinisation in xylene followed by rehydration through a descending ethanol gradient. To suppress endogenous peroxidase activity, slides were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol for 30 min at room temperature. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides in citrate-based buffer (pH 6.0). Subsequently, nonspecific binding was blocked by incubating sections with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 hour at room temperature.

Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody targeting HNRNPA0 (diluted 1:500 in antibody diluent). After returning to room temperature for 1 hour, slides were washed and incubated for 1 hour with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200 dilution; ZSGB-BIO). Immunoreactivity was detected using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (ZSGB-BIO, Cat. No. ZLI-9018). Cell nuclei were counterstained with haematoxylin (ZSGB-BIO, Cat. No. ZLI-9610). Finally, sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene and mounted with neutral resin for permanent preservation and subsequent microscopic evaluation.

RNA‑seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from control and HNRNPA0-knockdown cells using the RNAkey Total RNA Extraction Reagent. The quality and integrity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, a Qubit fluorometer and an Agilent TapeStation system. Sequencing libraries were then constructed following standard Illumina protocols. Briefly, poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched, followed by cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation and size selection to obtain fragments with an average insert size of ∼200 bp. Libraries were quantified by qPCR and their size distribution was verified by fragment analysis. Qualified libraries were subsequently subjected to paired-end sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform for downstream bioinformatic analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from a minimum of 3 independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 19.0; IBM Corp.) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software). Differences between 2 groups were assessed using a 2-tailed Student’s t-test. For comparisons among multiple groups, 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by appropriate post hoc tests. A P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.
