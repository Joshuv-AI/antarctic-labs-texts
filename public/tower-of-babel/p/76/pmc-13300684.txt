Impact of COVID-19 Infection and Vaccination on Abdominal-based Microvascular Breast Reconstruction Outcomes
Authors: Finn Tobias, Amanda Fazzalari, Sri Harshini Malapati, Colby Hyland, Bradford Sokol, Tina Moon, Tracy Landry, Abbie Ingram, Matthew Carty, Jessica Erdmann-Sager, Andrea Pusic, Erin Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) infection increases thrombotic risk; however, its impact on microsurgical breast reconstruction outcomes remains unclear. This study examines postoperative complications in patients following abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction relative to (1) timing relative to the COVID-19 pandemic, (2) history of COVID-19 infection, and (3) time since COVID-19 vaccination/booster.Methods:A retrospective review of patients who underwent abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction was conducted. Patients were categorized into pre-COVID-19 (January 23, 2017–January 19, 2020, n = 236) and post-COVID-19 (January 20, 2020–October 17, 2022, n = 233) groups. The post-COVID-19 group was further stratified by COVID-19 infection history and vaccination/booster status, within 40 days before surgery. Sociodemographic, clinical, and postoperative complication data were analyzed.Results:The sample included 469 patients with a median age of 50 years and mean body mass index of 28.6 kg/m2. The post-COVID-19 group had more former smokers (74 versus 35, P = 0.001) and more bilateral reconstructions (157 versus 126), but no significant differences in intraoperative anastomotic revisions or major complications. Within the post-COVID-19 cohort, 24 patients (10.4%) had prior COVID-19 infection, 178 (76.4%) were previously vaccinated, and 20 (11.3%) received vaccination/booster within 40 days before surgery. There were no significant differences in complications between patients with or without COVID-19 history, or between those vaccinated within 40 days before versus more than 40 days before surgery.Conclusions:Postoperative complication rates were similar between pre- and post-COVID-19 cohorts. COVID-19 infection or recent vaccination within 40 days before surgery was not associated with increased risk of major complications, including thrombotic events, in abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction.

Takeaways

Question: Does a history of COVID-19 infection or recent COVID-19 vaccination increase postoperative complications in patients undergoing abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction?

Findings: In this single-institution retrospective cohort study of 469 patients (pre-COVID-19: n = 236, post-COVID-19: n = 233), rates of major complications such as flap loss or venous thromboembolism were similar across groups. Within the post-COVID-19 cohort, neither prior COVID-19 infection nor vaccination within 40 days of surgery was associated with increased complications.

Meaning: COVID-19 infection or recent COVID-19 vaccination does not increase postoperative risks in abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction.

INTRODUCTION

Autologous breast reconstruction following mastectomy for breast cancer has become increasingly common with advancements in microsurgery, and abdominal-based flaps remain the most commonly used for reconstruction. Vascular compromise of the flap, most often from venous thrombosis, remains one of the most dreaded postoperative complications as it leads to flap loss. With improved microsurgical techniques and close monitoring for early detection of flap compromise, flap loss rates from vascular complications are low, with a reported rate of approximately 1%–6% with the greatest risk being within the first 24–72 postoperative hours.1–8

Optimizing comorbidities and patient clinical characteristics are critical to ensure the success of autologous reconstruction. One such example is risk stratifying patients with elevated risks of thrombotic complications by screening for preexisting coagulopathies or risk factors for venous thromboembolism.

In recent years, SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) infection has been implicated in coagulopathy: namely, hypercoagulation via endothelial injury and microthrombi, increased circulation of prothrombotic factors, and decreased levels of antithrombin.9–12 COVID-19 vaccination also has been reportedly associated with thrombotic complications, such as deep venous thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE) and immune thrombocytopenic purpuraimmune thrombocytopenic purpura.13–15 Studies have demonstrated increased reoperation rates for patients receiving microvascular breast reconstruction during the COVID-19 pandemic, compared to patients undergoing procedures before the start of the pandemic.16 However, less is known regarding the specific impact of COVID-19 infection and vaccination status on autologous breast reconstruction outcomes. Additionally, studies evaluating postoperative outcomes following microvascular free flap reconstructions in the early pandemic reported conflicting findings, with some demonstrating increased complications, and others stating similar outcomes. Many published studies had a small sample size or had samples with significant heterogeneity. Additionally, follow-up time was short and did not include potential exposures to the additional COVID-19 variants later introduced, including Delta and Omicron, which have demonstrated variable disease severity and increased transmissibility.17 Moreover, few studies have examined the effect of COVID-19 vaccination status on postoperative complications.

This study aimed to determine the effect of COVID-19 infection and COVID-19 vaccination on postoperative complications following abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction. Specifically, we sought to examine differences in minor and major postoperative complications in patients who underwent microvascular breast reconstruction with abdominal-base flaps (1) pre-COVID-19 (before January 20, 2020) versus post-COVID-19 pandemic start in the United States (January 20, 2020–October 17, 2022), (2) with history of COVID-19 infection and without history of COVID-19 infection during the COVID-19 period, and (3) within 40 days of COVID-19 vaccination (completion 2 doses of Pfizer-BioNTech, 2 doses of Moderna, or a single dose of Johnson & Johnson/Janssen COVID-19 vaccine) versus after 40 days of COVID-19 vaccination.

METHODS

A retrospective cohort study was performed using a prospectively maintained database including patients undergoing abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction at a single academic institution in Boston, Massachusetts. Institutional review board approval was obtained before review (protocol no.: 2023P000415). The database was queried for patients who had undergone procedures in the pre-COVID-19 period, defined as date of surgery January 23, 2017 to January 19, 2020, and those who had undergone reconstruction during COVID-19 pandemic (post-COVID-19), defined as date of surgery January 20, 2020 to October 17, 2022. The groups were determined using a date cutoff for the time periods selected based on January 20, 2020, the date of the first case of COVID-19 in the United States.

Sociodemographic and clinical variables including age, race/ethnicity, comorbidities, preoperative hypercoagulability diagnoses, known genetic mutations predisposing to breast cancer and history of neoadjuvant or adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation. Surgical variables included laterality, type of abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction, size of venous coupler used, number of anastomotic revisions at index operations, and reasons for revisions. History of COVID-19 infection, related symptoms if any, COVID-19 vaccination status, type of COVID-19 vaccination and number of days between vaccination/booster and surgery were collected. Postoperative complications were reviewed, and major complications were defined as those requiring reoperation or readmission, including flap compromise, hematoma, infection, mastectomy and/or abdominal incisional necrosis/dehiscence, flap loss, DVT/PE, and pneumonia. Minor complications included fat necrosis, cellulitis, seroma, mastectomy, and/or abdominal incisional necrosis/dehiscence not requiring reoperation or readmission.

Postoperative complication rates were compared between patients who had undergone abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction, in pre-COVID-19 and post-COVID-19 time periods. Within the post-COVID-19 cohort, a subanalysis was conducted to compare complication rates between patients who had been vaccinated, defined as at least 2 COVID-19 vaccines (Pfizer-BioNTech or Moderna) or a single dose of Johnson & Johnson/Janssen COVID-19 vaccine, to those who had been incompletely vaccinated or unvaccinated. Within the fully vaccinated cohort, a subanalysis was conducted to compare complication rates between those who had been vaccinated within 40 days before surgery to those who had been vaccinated more than 40 days before surgery. The 40-day cutoff was chosen because previous studies have shown COVID-19 vaccination-induced thrombosis occurring within 5 to 30 days of vaccination.

The thromboprophylaxis protocol remained consistent throughout the study period, including subcutaneous heparin (5000 units) before induction of anesthesia, sequential compression devices intraoperatively, 325 mg of aspirin in the recovery room immediately postoperatively, and 121.5 mg of aspirin daily for 30 days. Based on Caprini score or individual surgeon preference, additional thromboprophylaxis on hospital discharge may have been included. COVID-19 screening practices evolved throughout the pandemic as testing became more widely available. This represents a potential source of detection bias due to increased identification of asymptomatic infections in the post-COVID-19 period. To address this, outcomes were compared between pre- and post-COVID-19 cohorts in addition to analyses stratified by documented COVID-19 infection status.

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 to determine the minimum sample size required to demonstrate potential differences in flap loss rates.18 With a significance criterion of α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, the required total sample size to detect a medium effect is 128 for independent samples t tests. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0). Patient characteristics and postoperative complication rates were compared using independent samples t test and one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical factors. Results were considered statistically significant when the P value was less than 0.05 (indicated in tables by bold values).

RESULTS

Sample Characteristics

There were 236 patients in the pre-COVID-19 group and 232 patients in the post-COVID-19 group. Mean age, body mass index, and race/ethnicity were similar between groups (Table 1). There were significantly more patients with BRCA2 mutation and other high-risk genetic mutations (PALB2 and CHEK2) in the post-COVID-19 group (P = 0.01). There were significantly more patients who received postmastectomy radiation therapy in the pre-COVID-19 cohort (P = 0.044). Comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, and history of coagulation disorders (diagnosed preoperatively), were similar between groups. There was a significantly greater number of former smokers in the post-COVID-19 cohort (P < 0.001), but there were no current smokers in either group. Within the post-COVID-19 cohort, 24 (10.3%) patients had a history of COVID-19 infection and 174 (75%) were fully vaccinated (completed 2 doses of Pfizer-BioNTech, 2 doses of Moderna or a single dose of Johnson & Johnson/Janssen COVID-19 vaccine) at the time of surgery.

Characteristics of Reconstruction

The type of abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction was similar between cohorts, with deep inferior epigastric artery perforator (DIEP) flaps representing the greatest proportion of reconstructions (89.8% of flaps in pre-COVID-19 and 94.6% of flaps in post-COVID-19 groups). The remaining breast reconstructions were comprised of free transverse rectus abdominus myocutaneous (TRAM), muscle sparing (MS) TRAM, superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA), stacked DIEP, and pedicle TRAM flaps. The distribution of laterality (unilateral versus bilateral reconstruction), timing (immediate versus delayed reconstruction), median sizes of venous couplers and rate of anastomotic revisions during the index operation were similar between cohorts (Table 2). Only 1 flap was lost at the index operation following anastomotic revision, resulting in flap success rates of 93.3% and 100% after anastomotic revision was required in the pre- and post-COVID groups, respectively.

Outcomes Following Reconstruction

Minor complications which were managed conservatively, including fat necrosis, seroma, mastectomy skin flap necrosis and abdominal wound dehiscence were similar between groups (Table 3), with the exception of cellulitis and partial flap necrosis/wound breakdown, which were more significant in the post-COVID group (P = 0.009) and pre-COVID group (P = 0.012), respectively. There was no significant difference in major complications between groups. Within the post-COVID group, there were 2 cases of flap loss and 1 incidence of PE, compared to 1 flap loss and 4 PEs in the pre-COVID group. There were no DVTs in either group and 1 patient developed pneumonia postoperatively in the post-COVID group.

Of the patients who had confirmed COVID infection preoperatively, 5 (20.8%) had been previously fully vaccinated.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates similar outcomes in minor and major postoperative complications in patients undergoing abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction during the first 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic compared to patients undergoing surgery prepandemic at an academic medical center in Boston, Massachusetts. Despite concerns for an increased risk of thrombotic complications in patients with COVID-19, rates of DVT, PE and flap loss were similar in patients undergoing abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction before and after the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Similarly, there were no differences in thrombotic complications or flap loss among patients who had or had not been vaccinated or had or had not received a vaccination within 40 days of surgery, despite prior reports associating the immediate postvaccination period with increased risk of thrombotic complications. These findings suggest that neither prior COVID infection nor recent vaccination should be considered risk factors for postoperative complications following microvascular breast reconstruction.

We elected to specifically examine COVID-19 infection and COVID-19 vaccination due to their well-documented associations with systemic inflammation and thrombotic events, which may plausibly impact microvascular outcomes such as flap survival. Additionally, the widespread nature of COVID-19 infection and vaccination during the study period allowed for sufficient case numbers to permit meaningful analysis. Other infections or vaccinations were not evaluated, as their lower prevalence limited the feasibility of adequately powered analyses within our cohort. Future studies may be warranted to explore whether similar associations exist with other infectious or inflammatory exposures.

Our results support those published by Chen et al, demonstrating similar 30-day outcomes for patients undergoing numerous types of flaps (local, pedicled, or free microvascular) at their institution during the COVID-19 pandemic period when compared to the pre-COVID-19 pandemic period.19 Importantly, although their sample included a total of 220 patients, their sample of patients with a history of COVID-19 infection was small (n = 5), and they did not compare flap loss as an outcome. Additionally, although microvascular procedures represented the majority of cases (74%), it was not the entire sample. Their sample was also more heterogeneous than the one presented in this study, including patients undergoing all types of flap reconstruction of head and neck (58%), breast (28%), trunk (4%), and upper and lower extremities (9%). Nonetheless, in their subgroup analysis of patients undergoing microvascular flap reconstruction, they also reported similar rates of reoperation, readmission, and surgical complications within 30 days.

A more comparable study was performed by Ho et al, comparing postoperative outcomes following DIEP flap breast reconstruction in the pre-COVID-19 period (n = 46) and in the post-COVID-19 period (n = 29).20 Similar to our study, they found no cases of venous thromboembolism or flap failures and similar postoperative outcomes in the post-COVID period compared to their pre-COVID period in the United Kingdom. Their sample size and study period are both smaller and shorter than in our study, and no patients in this study were diagnosed with COVID-19 infection. There were therefore no comparisons of outcomes among patients with or without prior COVID-19 infection.

Our findings are also similar to those published by Zaki et al, who observed no significant difference in major thrombotic events or flap loss in a series of 527 patients, with 108 patients with a history of either COVID-19 infection or vaccination.21 Importantly, they included patients with positive history of either COVID-19 infection or vaccination in the same group, whereas our study extends on these findings by separately comparing patients with history of infection, vaccination and vaccination within 40 days of surgery. This time period was selected given the heightened risk for thrombotic complications. Additionally, our study evaluates not only flap vascular complications but also minor complications and major complications, which included but were not limited to fat necrosis, seromas, wound dehiscence, and cellulitis.

Our findings differ from those published by Elmer et al, who found significantly greater odds of reoperation in patients who underwent autologous breast reconstruction during the COVID pandemic when analyzing data from the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program database.16 They found overall a significant decrease in patients undergoing microvascular breast reconstruction, but a significant increase in the number of patients with higher American Society of Anesthesiologists scores undergoing surgery during the COVID pandemic. This may explain their additional finding of significantly increased rates of reoperation among patients who underwent microvascular breast reconstruction during the pandemic. Yusuf et al similarly found results of increased unplanned reoperations in their National Surgical Quality Improvement Program analysis of patients undergoing microvascular free tissue transfers for breast and nonbreast reconstructions.22 However, this study included only patients undergoing microvascular breast reconstruction in the early pandemic (2019–2020) and lacks granularity as a dataset analysis, specifically regarding history of COVID-19 infection or vaccination.

Prior studies have evaluated outcomes following microvascular reconstruction in head and neck surgery patients during the COVID-19 pandemic, including patients who had tested positive for COVID-19 within the immediate preoperative (10 d prior) or postoperative (7 d following) period, both symptomatic or asymptomatic, and compared postoperative outcomes. They reported a low incidence of flap-related complications and no flap deaths in their cohort of 40 patients. However, they reported a significant incidence of medical complications, including pneumonia, sepsis, acute kidney injury, DVT, PE, ischemic bowel and need for postoperative ventilation in the cohort. Five (12.5%) patients developed postoperative COVID-19 pneumonia, and 3 mortalities occurred in patients with active COVID-19 infection. This study, however, represents patients with significantly different and more significant baseline comorbidities and susceptibility to pulmonary complications.23

In their review of 35 patients with a confirmed documented history of COVID-19 who underwent microvascular free flap reconstruction, for breast, head and neck, or extremity, Han et al found a 4.8 times increased odds ratio of postoperative complications compared with controls. This included a significant increase in the number of anastomotic issues, partial flap losses, and total flap losses. However, there was no difference in the incidence of venous thromboembolism.24

The strengths of this study include its large sample size and comprehensive documentation of patient factors, including history of COVID-19 infection, severity of COVID-19 symptoms if any, vaccination status, and the timing of vaccination relative to surgery. By focusing exclusively on patients undergoing abdominal-based microvascular breast reconstruction, the study maintains a high degree of sample homogeneity, reducing confounding related to surgical technique variability. The extended study period of more than 2 years captures multiple phases of the pandemic, including the emergence of highly transmissible variants and evolving patterns of detection, thereby enhancing the relevance and applicability of the findings.

The findings of our study are overall reassuring regarding the risk for major thrombotic events in patients undergoing abdominal-based free flap reconstruction. Early in the pandemic, our institutional policy required patients to be asymptomatic and screen negative for active COVID-19 infection in the perioperative setting to be eligible for surgery. However, we currently only require patients with a history of a positive COVID-19 screening test to be asymptomatic at the time of surgery. This is primarily due to the broader understanding of the risk of COVID-19 transmission as a whole. However, we are additionally encouraged by the findings of our study that provide further support for the safety of undergoing breast reconstruction with abdominal-based free flaps regardless of an individual patient’s history of COVID-19 infection or vaccination status.

LIMITATIONS

Our study adds to the limited body of literature on this topic by including a larger and more contemporary cohort than the previously published studies by Chen et al and Ho et al, which were conducted early in the pandemic.19,20 However, this study has several limitations. Although our overall cohort was larger compared to prior studies, the number of patients with confirmed COVID-19 infection or recent vaccination is relatively small. This limits our ability to detect more subtle associations, especially for rare events such as total flap loss or venous thromboembolism.

Confirmation of COVID-19 was based on documented diagnosis in the medical record. However, asymptomatic and untested infections may have gone unrecognized and potentially lead to misclassification. COVID-19 infections were also not stratified by severity, vaccine type, or booster regimens. We did not compare outcomes across individual COVID-19 vaccine types, as stratification by manufacturer would have resulted in small subgroup sizes and limited statistical power. Accordingly, vaccination status was analyzed as a binary exposure. Although COVID-19 vaccines differ in mechanism and reported thrombotic risk profiles, vaccine-associated thrombotic events are rare overall, and meaningful vaccine-specific analyses were underpowered in our cohort. We acknowledge this as a limitation and note that future studies with larger cohorts may allow for more granular vaccine-specific comparisons. Future multicenter studies with detailed characterization of infection and vaccination status will be important to validate and expand upon our findings. Finally, this study was a single-institution, retrospective study, which may limit generalizability.

Despite these limitations, we did observe significantly higher rates of minor complications. Specifically, postoperative cellulitis and nonoperative flap necrosis or wound dehiscence in the post-COVID-19 cohort. These differences may reflect broader systemic changes in care delivery during the pandemic, such as delayed follow-up, altered perioperative workflows or limited access to supportive services.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study illustrates similar postoperative complication rates between pre- and post-COVID-19 patients undergoing DIEP flap breast reconstruction. Prior infection and vaccination status within 40 days of surgery was not associated with increased thrombotic events. These findings provide support for autologous microvascular breast reconstruction in the new post-COVID-19 pandemic era.
