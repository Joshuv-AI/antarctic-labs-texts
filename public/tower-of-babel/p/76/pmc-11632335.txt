Commentary: TEVAR or not TEVAR: That is the question
Authors: Kenji Minatoya
Published: 2024

Eighty-six-year-old man with aortic arch aneurysm and shaggy aorta.

Central Message

In older patients, the choice between TAR and TEVAR should also take into account the expected outcome in terms of the patient's ability to live the life he or she desires after treatment.

See Article page 1.

Total arch replacement (TAR) established its position as a standard procedure about 3 decades ago, thanks to advances in cerebral protection represented by antegrade cerebral perfusion. One of the leading institutions in the development of TAR was the University of Bologna.1 On the other hand, with the advent of thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR), the treatment strategy for aortic disease has changed dramatically. The aortic arch has branches that maintain cerebral blood flow, and its anatomy is not easily amenable to TEVAR. Nevertheless, many centers have performed TEVAR for this arch disease with various innovations, and quite a few reports have been published even on TEVAR with zone 0 and zone 1 proximal landing. It is also well known that the biggest problem with TEVAR applied to arch disease is perioperative stroke.2

Di Marco and colleagues3 report an analysis of 15 cases of TEVAR for arch lesions from 2017 to 2023. The mean age of patients was 75 years, in-hospital death was 6.7%, and the incidence of perioperative stroke was 13.3%. The proximal landing zone was zone 0 in 11 cases and zone 1 in 4 cases. In any case, their methods have been reported previously, and although interesting as an initial experience of the team that had mainly performed TAR, it is not new in terms of content.

The most noteworthy aspect of this article is the fact that TEVAR for arch lesions was performed by the group at the University of Bologna, which has been the most pioneering in TAR. Although not detailed in the article, it may be difficult to compare the results of TEVAR with those of TAR because the preoperative conditions of the patients are likely to be very different. At the very least, the results presented here are not necessarily superior to previously reported TAR results.4 Although the average age of the patients is high, even in their TAR reports for advanced age, patients older than age 75 years were not uncommon.5 However, the minimally invasive nature of TEVAR is undeniably overwhelming, and although there is some disagreement about the high need for additional treatment and the fact that lesions treated with TEVAR are not always cured, it is definitely worthwhile, at least as a short-term treatment strategy. It is worth noting why they chose TEVAR, given their extensive experience with TAR.

Even if a patient survives the perioperative period, a high mortality rate at 4 months postoperatively has been reported,6 indicating that the invasiveness of TAR, which is generally considered to be standardized and, with improved outcomes, remains high. In this sense, it is desirable to select the optimal treatment method according to the patient's postoperative life plan, not just short-term results. Obviously, each technique should be further improved and a combination of both techniques should be considered rather than choosing 1 of them.
