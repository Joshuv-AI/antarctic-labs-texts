Association Between Preadmission β‐Blocker Use and Venoarterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation Weaning Failure in Patients With Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Myrthe P J van Steenwijk, Laurens P Uitbeijerse, Lara C A Pladet, Atreyu van Esch, Yannick Taverne, Dirk W Donker, Carlos V Elzo Kraemer, Jacinta J Maas, Jesse R Kimman, Eric A Dubois, Alina A Constantinescu, Joost van Rosmalen, Diederik Gommers, Nicolas M van Mieghem, Rudolf A de Boer, Christiaan L Meuwese, the Dutch ECLS Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreadmission β‐blocker (BB) use may convey cardioprotective effects after onset of cardiogenic shock (CS). It is unknown whether this also holds for patients with the most severe forms of CS receiving venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V‐A ECMO) support.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter observational cohort study including adult patients supported by V‐A ECMO for severe CS between February 2018 and January 2025. Patients who had taken BBs within 48 hours before ECMO initiation as part of their home medication were compared with those who had not. The primary end point was ECMO weaning failure at 30 days; a composite of death, ongoing or renewed ECMO support, or the need for heart replacement therapy.ResultsA total of 307 patients were included in the study, of whom 87 (28.3%) were in the BB group. ECMO weaning failure occurred in 40 patients (46.0%) in the BB group compared with 126 (57.3%) patients in the non‐BB group (log‐rank test, P=0.08). No significant difference was seen in the trend of hemodynamic and metabolic parameters during ECMO support. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses showed that preadmission BB use was significantly associated with a decreased risk for ECMO weaning failure (hazard ratio, 0.61 [95% CI, 0.40–0.91]).ConclusionsIn this study, BB use before the onset of severe CS in patients supported by V‐A ECMO was significantly associated with lower inotrope requirements and lower rates of ECMO weaning failure. Future studies are needed to confirm these results and investigate the potential and feasibility of continuing low dosages of BBs during CS.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT05444764.Keywords: cardiogenic shock, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation weaning, preadmission β‐blocker use, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenationSubject Categories: Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiac Care, Heart Failure

What Are the Clinical Implications?

In clinical practice, preadmission β‐blocker use might favorably influence cardiac recovery and increase the probability for successful ECMO weaning in patients requiring V‐A ECMO support for severe cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest; future studies on this hypothesis are warranted.

Cardiogenic shock (CS) is a life‐threatening condition hallmarked by insufficient tissue perfusion attributable to impaired cardiac output. 1 Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (V‐A ECMO) is increasingly used to restore end‐organ perfusion in severe CS. 2 Despite its powerful support, 30% to 70% of patients cannot be weaned from V‐A ECMO support, leaving 1‐year mortality rates in excess of 50%. 3 These poor outcomes seem largely attributable to an inadequate cardiac recovery during V‐A ECMO support, 4 underscoring the need for treatment strategies that promote cardiac recovery.

β‐Blockers (BBs) can provide cardioprotective effects by preserving myocardial structure, cellular integrity and function (ie, left ventricular ejection fraction) in patients with chronic heart failure. 5 However, because of concerns that adrenergic blockade may further compromise the already severely reduced cardiac output, it has become standard practice to interrupt BB therapy during CS. Nevertheless, an increasing number of studies have suggested that preadmission use of BBs may also convey longer‐lasting benefits in the population with CS, 6 , 7 even after their cessation, although not all studies have been able to replicate these findings. 8

The context of CS managed with V‐A ECMO, or other potent temporary mechanical circulatory support devices, represents a particularly interesting setting because these devices can largely restore and preserve organ perfusion. Such strong hemodynamic support may partly mitigate the adverse effects of BBs in CS, thereby selectively allowing their beneficial effects to emerge in this setting. This hypothesis has, to our knowledge, not yet been studied.

This study aimed to investigate the association between preadmission BB use and ECMO weaning failure, as proxy for insufficient cardiac recovery, within 30 days after V‐A ECMO initiation for severe CS.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

Data from 2 multicenter cohorts were used, which collectively included all consecutive patients who received ECMO support for refractory respiratory and/or cardiocirculatory failure between 2016 and 2025 in intensive care units (ICUs) of participating centers in the Netherlands. The Prediction of Weanability, Survival, and Functional Outcomes After Extracorporeal Life Support cohort (NCT number: NCT05444764) and the Dutch ECLS database (Medical Ethical Committee number 2024‐0813) were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University Medical Center of Utrecht and Erasmus Medical Center, respectively, and thereafter, by local ethical committees of participating centers. Informed consent was obtained in accordance with the requirements of each local ethics committee. The data sets used during the current study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. For this research question, additional data were gathered for eligible patients in 3 participating academic hospitals (Erasmus Medical Center, University Medical Center Utrecht, and Leiden University Medical Center). Approval was obtained through an amendment to the existing study protocols from the respective Institutional Review Boards. No additional informed consent procedures were required in accordance with local ethics committee regulations. The study included adult patients (aged ≥18 years) treated with V‐A ECMO for severe circulatory failure attributable to CS. Patients were excluded if they were admitted before 2018, received V‐A ECMO support only during surgery or another intervention, had undergone heart transplantation, or experienced isolated right ventricular failure (eg, pulmonary embolism or post–lung transplantation). 9 Additionally, patients were excluded if BB use could not be reliably ascertained, if they had received a left ventricular assist device before V‐A ECMO initiation, or if they received V‐A ECMO support for <24 hours. In these cases, cardiac recovery was unlikely to be influenced by preadmission BB use. Patients who received a second ECMO run were excluded from the analysis to prevent duplicate inclusion.

Data Collection

Patient data were collected from their electronic medical records, including general characteristics (eg, age, sex, body mass index, and Charlson Comorbidity Index), comorbidities, pre‐ECMO measures (eg, relevant medications, including BB exposure, and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV score), and detailed V‐A ECMO–related characteristics (Table S1). Additionally, data on hemodynamics and arrhythmias during V‐A ECMO support were collected, along with outcome measures, such as survival and length of ICU and hospital stay.

For the present research question, additional prespecified study‐specific variables on preadmission BB use were extracted through structured review of the electronic medical records at the 3 participating academic centers. Two investigators visited each site to collect and add these data into the same secure, web‐based electronic data capture system used for the original registries (Castor EDC, Castor Research Inc, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). Preadmission BB exposure was systematically and prospectively verified by the hospital pharmacy in consultation with the patient and/or family as soon as clinically possible and cross‐checked with the Dutch national medication register (Landelijk Schakelpunt). Patients were classified as having had preadmission BB exposure (BB group) if they had received BBs <48 hours before V‐A ECMO initiation and had a documented history of BB use as home medication. All remaining patients were classified as the non‐BB group. This definition was chosen to capture both the acute as well as the chronic effects of β‐blockade. Considering that the plasma half‐life of commonly prescribed BBs ranges from 3 to 12 hours, acute effects can only be expected if BBs were used up until 48 hours before V‐A ECMO initiation. 10 In contrast, chronic effects resulting from prolonged BB exposure, involving structural and cellular adaptations, are believed to persist for weeks to months after BB withdrawal. 11 , 12 To assess the robustness of these assumptions, we repeated the primary analysis using alternative definitions of BB exposure, reclassifying patients based on timing of the last administration and home medication status. This resulted in additional comparisons between patients without any reported BB use and patients classified as: (1) both chronic and recent (<48 hours before ECMO initiation) use, (2) chronic use (regardless of recent use), (3) recent use (regardless of chronic use), and (4) any documented use. Patients who did not meet the specific classification criteria for each comparison were excluded from the respective analyses. Additionally, BB type (β‐1 selective or nonselective) and administered dosages of BBs were recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the probability of ECMO weaning failure at 30 days after V‐A ECMO initiation, serving as a proxy for insufficient cardiac recovery. Weaning failure was defined as a composite of death, ongoing or renewed need for ECMO support, or the need for heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device implantation within this time frame. Secondary outcomes included the duration of ECMO support, length of ICU stay, overall length of hospitalization, 30‐day mortality rates, and the occurrence of arrhythmias during ECMO support. The occurrence of arrhythmias was categorized as a supraventricular tachycardia (eg, atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter) or a sustained ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation when these events required intervention and/or medication. Additionally, differences in trends of heart rate, mean arterial pressure, pulse pressure, vasoactive‐inotropic score (VIS), inotropic score, vasopressor score, fraction of inspired oxygen, and lactate levels were assessed during the first 5 days of V‐A ECMO support. VIS is a validated and standardized score, calculated as: 100×epinephrine (μg/kg/min)+100×norepinephrine (μg/kg/min)+50×levosimendan (μg/kg/min)+10×milrinone (μg/kg/min)+dobutamine (μg/kg/min)+dopamine (μg/kg/min)+enoximone (μg/kg/min)+10 000×vasopressin (U/kg/min). 13 The inotropic score and vasopressor score were derived as modified versions of the VIS where summary scores were restricted to inotropic and vasopressor agents only, respectively 14 (Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as numbers and percentages, medians and interquartile ranges, or as mean with SD, as appropriate. Differences in baseline characteristics and outcomes between the BB group and non‐BB group were compared using χ2 tests and Fisher exact test for categorical variables, independent‐sample t‐tests for normally distributed variables, and Mann–Whitney tests for continuous variables that were not normally distributed. The number of ECMO support days, ICU length of stay, and duration of hospitalization were only investigated in patients who survived until 30 days after ECMO initiation to avoid bias by early mortality. In the Prediction of Weanability, Survival, and Functional Outcomes After Extracorporeal Life Support cohort (n=199), consecutive daily hemodynamic and metabolic measurements during V‐A ECMO support were available for analysis. Temporal trajectories of these variables were compared between the BB and non‐BB groups using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing–generated line plots with 95% CIs. Additionally, the association between BB use and the change in the variables over the first 5 days of V‐A ECMO support was analyzed using linear mixed models, including fixed effects for time (modeled with cubic splines), BB group, and their interaction, as well as random intercepts and slopes for time at a patient level. Model assumptions were evaluated using residual‐versus‐fitted and Q–Q plots; variables showing nonnormal residual distributions or heteroscedasticity were log‐transformed before analysis. Model comparisons were conducted using likelihood ratio tests to assess whether inclusion of the group–time interaction significantly improved model fit.

A Kaplan–Meier curve was constructed to illustrate the cumulative incidence of ECMO weaning failure per group over the first 30 days following V‐A ECMO initiation, with statistical significance being assessed by a log‐rank test. Subsequently, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model was fitted to study the association between preadmission BB use and ECMO weaning failure at 30 days unadjusted and adjusted for confounders. No censoring occurred, as complete 30‐day outcome data were available for all patients. Covariates included in the model were: age, sex, body mass index, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation IV score, history of heart failure, history of hypertension, V‐A ECMO indication, VIS before V‐A ECMO initiation, and the need for adjunct left ventricular unloading at V‐A ECMO initiation. Multicollinearity among covariates was evaluated using variance inflation factors. All variance inflation factor values were <2, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. Confounders were selected based on their potential effects on both the probability for preadmission BB use, as well as the probability of ECMO weaning failure. 15 , 16 The Cox proportional hazards assumption was checked using statistical tests and graphical diagnostics based on the scaled Schoenfeld residuals. We found evidence of nonproportionality for ECMO indication and age; therefore, the baseline hazard was stratified by ECMO indication, and time‐dependent effects of age were modeled by including an interaction between age and time. For the secondary outcomes, the effect of BB group on 30‐day mortality was similarly analyzed using a Cox proportional hazards model. The association with the occurrence of arrhythmias during V‐A ECMO support was assessed using logistic regression.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed in which we varied the definitions of BB exposure, as mentioned previously, but also by stratifying the BB group based on receptor selectivity, dividing it into 2 subgroups; selective and nonselective BBs. Selective BBs included atenolol, bisoprolol, and metoprolol, whereas carvedilol, labetalol, propranolol, and sotalol were classified as nonselective BBs. 17 Additionally, to assess a potential dose–response relationship, we selected patients from the BB group and standardized pre‐ECMO BB exposure by converting all BB agents to carvedilol‐equivalent doses, as commonly applied in previous studies. 18 , 19 The resulting BB dose was then analyzed as a continuous variable in the Cox regression analysis. Last, we performed an analysis where we excluded extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) patients, because of an increased risk for neurologic death as a competing risk.

The number of missing data was minimal (Table S2). Importantly, maximum follow‐up was set at 30 days, with survivors censored at that time and mortality verified through the national mortality registry, resulting in complete data for the primary outcome. On‐ECMO data, including complications, were also complete because patients were not transferred to nonparticipating centers while receiving ECMO support. After ECMO decannulation, interhospital transfers resulted in a small number of missing values for ICU and hospital length of stay (3.6% and 4.2%, respectively). As for model covariates, data were only missing for VIS values before V‐A ECMO initiation (n=15, 4.9%). These missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations in R using the mice package (version 3.16.0) with 5 imputed data sets. Two‐tailed P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis and statistical evaluation were conducted using R (version 4.4.2, 2024.09.01).

RESULTS

Of 810 patients who required ECMO support between 2018 and 2025 (Table S3), 307 were eligible for the current analysis (Figure S1). Median age at V‐A ECMO initiation was 58.0 (interquartile range, 48.5–68.0) years. A total of 74% of patients were men, and the majority received V‐A ECMO after cardiotomy (29%), as ECPR (33%), or following myocardial infarction (14%). The vast majority of patients received ECPR for cardiac causes (Table S4). A total of 87 (28%) patients were included in the BB group, of whom 71 (82%) used selective BBs before V‐A ECMO initiation. All patients with reported BB use before V‐A ECMO initiation were taken off BB while on ECMO. The remaining 220 patients were placed in the non‐BB group, including 181 (82%) patients who had never used BBs, 13 (6%) patients who had received BBs within 48 hours before V‐A ECMO initiation but not as home medication, and 26 (12%) who were on BB at home but had not taken them in the 48 hours leading up to V‐A ECMO initiation. Patients in the BB group were older, had a higher VIS before V‐A ECMO initiation, and had a greater burden of comorbidities, as indicated by a higher Charlson Comorbidity Index. Also, the indications for V‐A ECMO were significantly different between the 2 groups, driven by differences in the proportion of patients receiving V‐A ECMO after cardiotomy (P=0.002) and following acute myocardial infarction (P=0.04) (Table 1). The 30‐day mortality rates did not differ between these 2 indication groups (47.7% versus 47.7%) (Table S5).

Of 307 patients, 166 (54%) could not be successfully weaned from ECMO at 30 days. Among these, 3 (2%) required ongoing ECMO support, 25 (15%) underwent direct left ventricular assist device, 2 (1%) received a heart transplantation, and 140 (84%) died, with some patients contributing to >1 component of the composite end point (Table 2). Median follow‐up for patients who died within 30 days was 7.0 (interquartile range, 3.0–9.1) days. The incidence of ECMO weaning failure was highest in the non‐BB group (57%) versus the BB group (46%) (log‐rank test, P=0.08) (Figure 1). After adjusting for predefined baseline confounders, BB exposure was significantly associated with a reduced risk for ECMO weaning failure (hazard ratio [HR], 0.61 [95% CI, 0.40–0.91]) (Table 3 and Table S6). The mortality rate at 30 days was 48% for the non‐BB group versus 41% in the BB group, with no statistically significant difference in either univariable or multivariable Cox regression analysis (Table 3). Also, the incidence of arrhythmias did not significantly differ between both groups (Table 3). Among patients who survived until 30 days, ECMO support duration and length of ICU and hospital stay did not significantly differ between both groups (Table 2).

We repeated the primary analysis using altering definitions of preadmission BB use, which did not substantially change our findings (Figure 3). The strength of the association between preadmission BB exposure and ECMO weaning failure rates diminished after exclusion of patients with ECPR (HR, 0.68 [95% CI, 0.43–1.08]; n=207) (Figure 3 and Table S7). The risk for ECMO weaning failure did not seem different in patients using selective versus nonselective BBs (Figure S2). Within the BB group, no clear dose–response relationship was observed as incorporating the carvedilol‐equivalent dose as a continuous variable in the Cox regression model did not significantly alter the risk for ECMO weaning failure (HR, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.98–1.03]) (Figure S3).

Forest plot illustrating the effect of several sensitivity analyses on the association between BB use and ECMO weaning failure.

Hazard ratios for ECMO weaning failure were assessed using alternative definitions of BB exposure, with the non‐BB group serving as the reference. In this analysis, the non‐BB group included only patients who never received BBs, unlike in the primary analysis, where the non‐BB group comprised all patients who did not meet the BB group criteria. BB exposure was defined in 4 ways: (1) both chronic and recent use (<48 hours before ECMO initiation); (2) chronic use, regardless of recent administration; (3) recent use, regardless of chronic use; and (4) any recorded use (chronic and/or recent). (5) Last, a sensitivity analysis was performed excluding patients with ECPR. All multivariable Cox regression analyses were adjusted for the same confounders as in the primary analysis: age, BMI, sex, APACHE IV score, VIS before V‐A ECMO initiation, ECMO indication, comorbidities heart failure and hypertension, and unloading device at baseline. APACHE indicates Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation; BB, β‐blocker; BMI, body mass index; ECMO, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation; ECPR, extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation; V‐A, venoarterial; and VIS, vasoactive‐inotropic score.

DISCUSSION

In this population of patients receiving V‐A ECMO for severe CS, preadmission BB use was associated with lower inotrope requirements, and we found a significant association with a reduced risk for ECMO weaning failure. These findings allude to the hypothesis that BB use before onset of severe CS may lead to improved cardiac recovery.

Our finding that preadmission BB use was associated with a 11.3 and a (nonsignificant) 6.3 percent lower probability for ECMO weaning failure and 30‐day mortality, respectively, is in line with several older studies in patients with less severe forms of CS. A subgroup analysis of the Dobutamine Compared with Milrinone (DOREMI) trial cohort showed that BB use within 24 hours before CS onset was associated with a significantly lower all‐cause mortality during the first 48 hours of inotropic support, without adversely affecting dobutamine responsiveness or overall hemodynamic stability. 6 A recent subgroup analysis of the Altshock‐2 registry did not confirm these benefits of preadmission BB use in the population with CS; however, these findings are likely confounded by substantial differences in the use of rescue temporary mechanical circulatory support devices between the subgroups. 8 An improved 1‐year survival was also observed in patients with chronic BB use who were admitted following an out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest attributable to nonshockable rhythms. 20 Oppositely, withdrawal of BBs in patients with acute decompensated heart failure on hospital admission was found to be associated with adverse outcomes. 21 In a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with ECPR, the association between preadmission BB use and ECMO weaning success remained directionally consistent but was attenuated in its effect. This result may suggest that preadmission BB use has a greater impact in patients with ECPR, who typically experience more severe ischemia because of cardiac arrest. However, given the considerable reduction in sample size, no firm conclusion can be drawn about potential underlying mechanisms.

The stronger association between preadmission BB use and reduced ECMO weaning failure, compared with mortality alone, suggests that its benefits may be primarily mediated through an enhanced cardiac recovery, reducing the need for cardiac replacement therapies (eg, left ventricular assist device or heart transplantation). This hypothesis seems supported by a somewhat earlier improvement in pulse pressure in the BB group, a parameter frequently described as proxy for cardiac recovery. 22 , 23 And although inotrope dosages were generally lower in the BB group, vasopressor requirements seemed higher. The latter finding could potentially be explained by a higher illness severity. Our observation that BB exposure was associated with arrhythmias in unadjusted but not in adjusted regression analysis implies that the observed difference is likely attributable to a higher baseline arrhythmic risk in the BB group.

Benefits of preadmission BB use on outcomes of patients with severe CS requiring V‐A ECMO could be explained by 2 nonmutual exclusive theories. First, the myocardium of BB recipients may have been partially protected from the deleterious effects of CS, including the endogenous and exogeneous stress imposed by hormones and inotropic drugs. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that β‐1 receptor stimulation consistently causes acute cardiac injury and activates apoptotic signals, an effect that was attenuated by BB treatment. 24 , 25 Second, BB pretreated hearts may exhibit increased sensitivity to inotropes after BB withdrawal, thereby potentially improving cardiac output. Consistent with this, our data show lower inotrope requirements in the BB group. This interpretation is supported by findings indicating that prolonged BB exposure typically upregulates and sensitizes β‐adrenergic receptors, 26 , 27 restores ryanodine receptor function, improves calcium handling, and increases myocardial contractile elements. 28 , 29 , 30 These benefits can persist for days to weeks after BB withdrawal 31 and potentially improve myocardial contractility during early phases of critical illness, even in the absence of continued treatment. Also, BB recipients could have experienced simultaneous β‐1 inhibition and β‐2 stimulation from therapeutic BB levels and dobutamine, a combination that was suggested to enhance myocardial function and recovery more than β‐1 inhibition alone. 32 , 33 The aforementioned discussion is of special relevance in patients receiving temporary mechanical circulatory support as sufficient end‐organ perfusion could be largely guaranteed by extracorporeal blood flow rather than native cardiac output.

Our study is strengthened by its multicenter design, detailed and high‐quality data collection, and a low rate of missing data. For patients included in the analysis, preadmission BB exposure was reliably collected as all home medications for admitted patients are systematically and prospectively verified by the hospital pharmacy in consultation with the patient and/or family, and cross‐checked with the Dutch national medication register (Landelijk Schakelpunt). Nevertheless, patient adherence to prescribed BB therapy remains uncertain, and for some patients, BB use could not be reliably ascertained (eg, because of early death). Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the overall cohort is relatively large for ECMO research, stratification into subgroups resulted in smaller effective sample sizes, limiting precision of subgroup analyses. Additionally, as the study was conducted in Dutch centers only, the generalizability of our findings to other health care systems may be limited. Another limitation is the observational design of the study, which inherently leaves the potential for residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Although a randomized trial assigning patients to preadmission BB use before V‐A ECMO initiation would offer stronger causal inference, such a study would be logistically difficult as patients would need to be randomized before developing CS. Ideally, future studies should also evaluate the effects of chronic BB therapy stratified by the underlying cause of CS and ECMO indication, given the potential differing effects and the observed heterogeneity in the current study population. It is important to note that preadmission BB exposure is a distinct issue from whether initiating BBs during CS is beneficial.

CONCLUSIONS

In this observational study, preadmission BB use in patients with CS was associated with a reduced risk for ECMO weaning failure at 30 days. Future studies are needed to confirm this association, explore the underlying mechanisms, and evaluate whether and how (low dosages of) BBs can be safely continued after the onset of CS in patients receiving V‐A ECMO support.
