Posterior insular cortex hyperactivation drives vestibular migraine and comorbid anxiety
Authors: Tianyu Zhang, Yifei Xiang, Bin Huang, Xiaoping Wang, Ping Yang, Baoming He, Nengwei Yu, Jie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVestibular migraine, a debilitating disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of headache and vertigo, is frequently comorbid with persistent anxiety and depression. The neural mechanisms that integrate these disparate sensory and affective symptoms remain elusive.FindingsHere, we combined clinical characterization with a translational mouse model to identify the posterior insular cortex (pIC) as a critical hub driving the pathophysiology of vestibular migraine. In patients, anxiety and depression scores remained elevated interictally, suggesting a trait-like affective disturbance. Using a validated mouse model combining nitroglycerin (NTG) administration and rotarod vestibular stimulation, which recapitulates vestibular migraine-like phenotypes (hyperalgesia, balance deficits, and anxiety-like behaviors), we observed hyperactivity within the pIC. This region responded to both nociceptive and vestibular challenges. Chemogenetic inhibition of the pIC reversed hyperalgesia and vestibular-motor deficits. Notably, while acute inhibition did not affect anxiety-like behaviors, chronic inhibition produced a full rescue, including the affective component, paralleling the clinical persistence of mood symptoms. Conversely, chronic chemogenetic activation of the pIC was sufficient to promote all core phenotypes under a subthreshold NTG regimen combined with rotarod vestibular stimulus. Furthermore, systemic administration of the NMDA receptor antagonist memantine alleviated vestibular migraine-like symptoms and normalized pIC hyperactivity.ConclusionsOur results establish pIC hyperactivation as a causal neural substrate that converges nociceptive, vestibular, and affective processing in vestibular migraine, and identify pIC suppression as a promising therapeutic strategy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02340-z.

Introduction

Vestibular migraine (VM) is a common and highly disabling neurological disorder characterized by recurrent episodes of headache and vertigo [1, 2]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated its considerable burden, with VM accounting for 7% -16% of patients in neurology clinics and affecting approximately 3% of the adult population in the general community [3, 4]. Notably, its prevalence is particularly elevated in certain migraine subgroups, reaching up to 60% in chronic migraine and 73% in migraine with aura [5]. Besides headache and vestibular dysfunction such as vertigo and imbalance, numerous studies have reported a high comorbidity of affective disorders in VM patients, most notably anxiety and depression [6, 7]. The interplay between headache, vestibular symptoms, and mood disorders is believed to create a vicious cycle, contributing to the complexity, chronicity, and therapeutic challenges associated with VM [8, 9]. Despite these clinical insights, the central pathophysiological mechanisms of VM remain elusive. A critical question is how the disparate symptoms of headache, vestibular dysfunction, and mood disorders converge within the brain.

The neural substrates of migraine comprise a distributed network that involved in nociception, sensory integration, and affective processing [10–12]. Functional neuroimaging studies have consistently identified alterations in key brain regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex, posterior thalamus, and visual cortex, in migraine patients [13–15]. Among these, the insular cortex (IC), particularly its posterior subregions, has emerged as an important hub for integrating nociceptive input with aversive emotional states [16–18]. This functional specialization is supported by rodent studies that delineate a hierarchical organization within the insula: the posterior insular cortex (pIC) primarily receives and converges raw sensory inputs from the thalamus and brainstem, whereas the anterior insular cortex is implicated in assigning negative emotional valence to these sensations, thereby linking aversive sensory experiences to anxiety, depression and fear [19–21].

In the context of VM, the role of insula extends beyond pain processing. The vestibular system, whose dysfunction underlies core symptoms such as vertigo, projects directly to the IC [22]. Anatomical and functional connections from the vestibular nucleus complex to the IC position it as a unique convergent hub, capable of integrating vestibular signals with concurrent nociceptive and interoceptive information [22–24]. Based on this convergent connectivity and functional profile, we hypothesize that pIC serves as a common neural substrate, driving the multifaceted symptoms of headache, vestibular dysfunction, and affective disturbance in VM. However, direct experimental evidence establishing a causal role for pIC in the integrated pathophysiology of VM remains lacking.

Building on this hypothesis, this study was designed to systematically evaluate the role of pIC in the pathophysiology of VM. We first performed a detailed clinical characterization, assessing symptom profiles and comorbid mood disorders (anxiety and depression) in a cohort of VM patients. Next, leveraging the established nitroglycerin (NTG) injection combined with rotarod vestibular stimulus-induced mouse model of migraine, we developed and validated a VM model that faithfully recapitulates the core symptoms of hyperalgesia, vestibular dysfunction, comorbid with anxiety-like behaviors. Using a combination of chemogenetic manipulations, multiple channels in vivo electrophysiology recordings, and pharmacological interventions in this model, we systematically tested the central hypothesis that pIC serves as a key driver of the multifaceted symptomatology in VM.

Materials and methods

Participants

Patients with VM were enrolled from the headache clinic of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. The diagnosis of VM was confirmed by two experienced neurologists according to the consensus criteria for VM proposed by the Barany Society and the International Headache Society [25, 26].

Inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (1) age between 14 and 80 years; (2) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for VM. Exclusion criteria for patients included: (1) presence of other major neurological or psychiatric disorders; (2) inability to complete neuropsychological assessments; (3) history of other neuro-otological conditions or hearing impairment; and (4) a history of alcohol or substance abuse. Healthy controls (HCs) were recruited from the local community and were matched to patients on the basis of age and sex.

Age- and sex-matched HCs were recruited from the local community. HCs were required to be between 14 and 80 years of age and were excluded if they had: (1) a history of vertigo, migraine, other primary headache disorders, or chronic pain syndromes; (2) any major neurological or psychiatric illness; (3) inability to complete cognitive and clinical assessments for personal reasons; (4) prior diagnosis of neuro-otological disease or hearing dysfunction; or (5) current or past substance or alcohol dependence.

Clinical and demographic data collection

Standardized case report forms were used to collect demographic and clinical data, including outpatient identification number, sex, age, body mass index (BMI), age at symptom onset, disease duration, and medication use. The sample size was based on clinical feasibility and was comparable to prior phenotypic studies [27, 28]. Medication use (both preventive and acute) was documented for all VM patients. To minimize confounding, enrolled patients were required to be on a stable preventive regimen for ≥ 3 months. The timing of last acute medication use prior to assessment was recorded.

The interictal assessment was defined as occurring at least 72 h after the complete resolution of the last migraine headache and at least 24 h before the onset of the next headache attack. Headache intensity was assessed using both the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) during both ictal and interictal phases in patients, as well as current or recent pain levels in HCs. The Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6) was administered to evaluate the impact of headaches on daily functioning, work productivity, and social activities. Emotional status was evaluated using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), which was completed during both ictal and interictal periods in patients and reflected current mood in controls. Dizziness-related disability and its effect on quality of life were assessed using the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI), administered under the same temporal conditions as HADS. All assessments were conducted by trained researchers under standardized conditions.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital (approval number: 2025811). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants; for minors, written consent was provided by their legal guardians in accordance with ethical guidelines.

Animals

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. A total of 133 mice were used in this study. Male and female C57/BL6J SPF mice, 8 weeks old, purchased from Changsheng Bio-technology Technology Co. Ltd. (Liaoning, China), were used in this study. Mice were housed in groups of 6 per cage, kept under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (8:00–20:00) at a temperature of 21–25 ◦C and humidity ranging from 40% to 60%. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated random number sequence.

Stereotaxic injection of viral vectors

Mice were deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (0.3%, 10 mL/kg). Each animal was then head-fixed in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., China), and core body temperature was maintained at 37 °C with a feedback-controlled heating pad throughout the procedure. For chemogenetic manipulation, we bilaterally injected 200 nL of one of the following adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) into the pIC; coordinates relative to bregma: AP + 0.45 mm, ML ± 3.35 mm, DV − 4.10 mm): AAV2/9-hsyn-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (for inhibition; Taitool Bioscience, #S0279–9), AAV2/9-hsyn-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (for activation; Taitool Bioscience, #S0487–9), or AAV2/9-hsyn-mCherry (control; Taitool Bioscience, #S0238–9). The viral suspension was delivered using a microinjection pump at a rate of 20 nL /min, and the injection needle was left in place for an additional 5 min post-injection to minimize backflow. Following surgery, all mice were placed on a 37 °C heating pad until fully recovered from anesthesia. A minimal 2-week post-injection period was allowed for robust viral expression before initiating any experimental manipulations, which was confirmed by fluorescence visualization prior to experiments.

VM mouse model induction

The VM model was induced by combining NTG administration with vestibular stimulation. A 5 mg/mL NTG stock solution was diluted to 1 mg/mL in saline and injected intraperitoneally (10 mg/kg) every other day for 9 days. On alternate days, mice received rotarod vestibular stimulation. Each mouse was placed in a ventilated 50 mL tube mounted on a custom rotator. The device accelerated from 5 to 44 rpm over 1 min, then decelerated to 5 rpm over 1 min, repeating this cycle for 30 min. During stimulation, head acceleration ranged from 0.60 to 1.36 g. This dual protocol concurrently evokes both headache-related and vestibular components of VM.

Drug treatment

For chemogenetic manipulation, clozapine N-oxide (CNO; Sigma-Aldrich, #C0382) was first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 5 mg/mL stock solution. For acute inhibition or activation experiments, the stock solution was diluted in sterile 0.9% saline to a working concentration of 0.5 mg/mL immediately prior to injection. Mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of CNO at a dose of 5 mg/kg body weight (or an equivalent volume of vehicle control) 30–45 min before behavioral testing or electrophysiological recording [29]. Control mice expressing AAV2/9-hsyn-mCherry received an identical CNO injection to control for any potential off-target effects of the ligand. For chronic manipulation experiments, CNO was administered via drinking water to achieve sustained receptor modulation. The DMSO stock solution was diluted in the animals’ regular drinking water to a final concentration of 5 µg/mL.

For the formalin pain model, naive mice received a subcutaneous plantar hindpaw injection of 20 µL 5% formalin (FA group) or 20 µL saline. After 90 min, mice were perfused for c-Fos analysis as in Section “Immunohistochemistry”.

In the Memantine treatment experiment, memantine hydrochloride (Beyotime, China, catalog Y268380-50 mg) was dissolved in sterile 0.9% saline to prepare a working solution at a concentration of 5 mg/mL (based on the freebase weight). The solution was administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dosage of 10 mg/kg body weight. Injections were performed 30 min prior to behavioral testing or in vivo electrophysiological record. The mice were injected intraperitoneally with Memantine Hydrochloride.

In vivo multichannel electrophysiology recording

Electrode implantation surgery

Mice were anesthetized and head-fixed in a stereotaxic frame as described for viral injection surgery. A custom-made, movable 32-channel microelectrode array was used for recording. Individual recording electrodes were constructed from nichrome wire (A-M Systems, catalog # 751000). A pure silver wire (99.99%, A-M Systems, catalog # 786500) was placed subdurally over the cerebellar cortex to serve as the reference/ground electrode. The recording electrode array was stereotaxically lowered into the pIC (coordinates: AP + 0.45 mm, ML ± 3.35 mm, DV − 4.10 mm). The entire assembly was then securely anchored to the skull using dental acrylic cement to minimize motion artifacts. Following surgery, mice were placed on a 37 °C heating pad until full recovery from anesthesia and were allowed a minimum 7-day post-operative recovery period before habituation to recording cables.

Recording

Neural signals were acquired using a commercial multichannel recording system (Bonen Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China). Wideband signals were sampled at 30 kHz. For recordings in the VM model and its control, a 10-minute baseline recording session was performed in the animal’s home cage following model establishment. For recordings in memantine-treated mice, a 10-minute baseline session was acquired 30 min post-injection. Offline, the wideband signal was high-pass filtered at 200 Hz to obtain the spike potential (SPKC) signal. Spiking events were detected by applying a threshold set at -4.5 times the standard deviation (σ) of the filtered signal to extract spike waveforms.

Spike sorting and data analysis

Spike sorting and subsequent analyses were performed offline using custom scripts in MATLAB (R2025a, MathWorks). Putative single units were isolated by applying principal component analysis and manual clustering using the MClust toolbox (by A.D. Redish). Only units that were well-isolated according to standardized criteria were included for further analysis: an Isolation Distance > 15 and an L-Ratio < 0.2. For each isolated single unit, the mean firing rate (in Hz) was calculated over the entire 10-minute recording session. For statistical analysis of in vivo electrophysiology data (firing rates), the mean firing rate was first calculated across all well-isolated units recorded from each individual animal. These animal-averaged values were then used as the biological replicate (n = number of mice) for all subsequent between-group statistical comparisons. Spike sorting and the calculation of mean firing rates were performed using data files that were coded to blind the analyst to the experimental condition.

Behavior tests

von Frey test

Mechanical nociceptive thresholds were assessed using a graded series of von Frey monofilaments (ranging from 0.008 to 2.0 g bending force). Mice were acclimatized in individual clear Plexiglas compartments on an elevated wire mesh platform for at least 30 min prior to test. For periorbital sensitivity, filaments were applied perpendicularly to the periorbital regions, specifically the medial aspect of the orbital rim. For hind paw sensitivity, filaments were applied to the plantar surface. At each testing site, the calibrated filaments were applied in an ascending order, using the up-down method, until a withdrawal response (rapid flinching, licking, or guarding) was observed. The 50% mechanical withdrawal threshold was calculated from the response pattern. For each animal, three trials were conducted per region, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Hot plate test

Thermal nociception was assessed using a commercially available hot plate analgesia meter. The apparatus temperature was maintained at a constant 53.5 ± 0.2 °C. Mice were gently placed on the hot-plate within a transparent Plexiglas enclosure. The latency to a defined nocifensive response was recorded with a stopwatch and served as the endpoint. A response was operationally defined as either: (1) a brisk hind paw lick or flick, (2) vigorous shaking of either hind paw, or (3) an attempt to jump/escape from the plate. To prevent tissue damage, a 20-second automatic cut-off time was imposed. For each animal, three trials were conducted per region, and the mean value was used for statistical analysis.

Balance beam test

The balance beam test was used to assess vestibular-motor function and balance. The apparatus consisted of a cylindrical wooden beam (1 cm in diameter, 80 cm in length) positioned horizontally, with a darkened shelter box placed at one end to motivate locomotion. Mice were first habituated to the apparatus. During habituation, each mouse was gently placed at the start of the beam and allowed to traverse the full length to enter the shelter box. After remaining in the box for approximately 2 min, the mouse was removed. This procedure was repeated for a total of 5 trials. For the formal test, the mouse was placed at the start point of the beam. The time to start was defined as the latency from placement to the initiation of forward movement. The total time was recorded as the duration required to traverse the entire 80-cm length of the beam and fully enter the shelter box. Each mouse performed three consecutive trials, and the average values were used for analysis.

Gait test

Gait parameters were assessed to evaluate locomotor coordination and vestibular-motor integration. Mice were first habituated to walking on a strip of absorbent paper (tracking paper) within a narrow, straight runway. Following habituation, the forepaws and hind paws of the mouse were gently coated with non-toxic, water-soluble red and blue dyes, respectively. The mouse was then guided to walk steadily along the paper, leaving a clear footprint trail. Footprint analysis was performed on consecutive, uninterrupted steps. Key gait parameters were quantified as follows: (1) Gait length: defined as the distance between two consecutive footprints of the same hind paw, measured along the axis of progression. The average value was calculated from three consecutive strides per side (six values total). (2) Gait width: defined as the perpendicular distance between the left and right hind paw footprints within the same step cycle. The average value was calculated from six sequential steps. (3) Overlap: defined as the distance between the centroids of ipsilateral forepaw and hind paw footprints within the same step cycle, measured along the axis of progression. A positive value indicates forepaw placement ahead of the hind paw, while a negative value indicates overlap. The average value was calculated from three consecutive step cycles per side (six values total).

Open field test

Spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior were assessed in a square open field arena (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) constructed of matte white acrylic to minimize reflections. During the experimental period, an illuminance of 15 lx was used. At the beginning of each trial, a mouse was gently placed in the exact center of the arena and allowed to freely explore for 10 min. The arena was digitally subdivided into a central zone (defined as the area more than 10 cm from any wall) and a peripheral zone. All trials were recorded by an overhead camera. The recorded videos were analyzed using automated tracking software (SMART v3.0, Shenzhen RWD Life Science Co. Ltd.) to quantify the following parameters: total length (cm), central time (s), and central visit (#).

Elevated plus maze

Anxiety-like behavior was further quantified using the elevated plus maze. The apparatus consisted of two open arms (35 cm × 5 cm) and two closed arms (35 cm × 5 cm × 15 cm high walls), all extending from a central square platform (5 cm × 5 cm). The entire maze was elevated 30 cm above the ground. During the experimental period, an illuminance of 15 lx was used. Each mouse was gently placed onto the central platform, facing an open arm, and allowed to freely explore the maze for a 5-minute trial. All trials were recorded by an overhead video camera. The recorded videos were subsequently analyzed using automated tracking software (SMART v3.0, Shenzhen RWD Life Science Co. Ltd.). The primary anxiety-related parameters quantified were: (1) the percentage of time spent in the open arms [Time open / (Time open + Time closed) × 100%], and (2) the percentage of open arm entries [Entries open / (Entries open + Entries closed) × 100%].

For the VM model characterization, all behavioral tests were performed on the same cohort of mice. Tests were conducted in a fixed order with at least 24-hour intervals between different modalities: Open Field, Elevated Plus Maze, Balance Beam and Gait, von Frey test, and Hot Plate test. This standardized sequence was applied uniformly to all experimental and control groups.

All behavioral tests were video-recorded. The quantification of these videos (e.g., latency measurements, footprint analysis, tracking data) was performed by an experimenter blinded to the group assignment of the animals.

Immunohistochemistry

The mice were deeply anesthetized with a pentobarbital sodium overdose and transcardially perfused with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. The brains were carefully extracted and post-fixed in the same 4% PFA solution at 4°C for 24 hours, and then carefully removed. Coronal brain slices containing the pIC were prepared by cutting them at a thickness of 40 µm using a shaker slicer (Leica VT 1200S). For c-Fos immunostaining, free-floating sections were first incubated in a blocking solution containing 5% normal goat serum (Biosharp, BL210A) and 1% Triton X-100 (Biosharp, BS084) in PBS for 2 hours at room temperature with gentle agitation. Sections were then incubated with a rabbit monoclonal antibody against c-Fos (Cell Signaling Technology, clone 9F6, #2250S) at a dilution of 1:2000 in PBS containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSharp, BS114) for 3648 hours at 4°C on a shaker. Following three 20-minute washes with PBS containing 0.5% Triton X-100, sections were incubated with a CoraLite647-conjugated AffiniPure F(ab’)2 fragment goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Proteintech, #SA00014–9) at a dilution of 1:500 for 2 h at 37 °C in the dark. After three final washes in PBS, sections were mounted onto glass slides with an anti-fade mounting medium.

Imaging and quantification

Images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope under identical exposure settings across all groups. For each animal, three representative coronal sections spanning the pIC were selected for analysis. The number of c-Fos-positive cells within a defined region of interest covering the pIC was manually counted using the Fiji/ImageJ software by an experimenter blinded to the experimental conditions. The average count per section per animal was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0.0). Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise noted. Normality of data distribution was assessed using the D’Agostino-Pearson omnibus test. For comparisons between two independent groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. For comparisons among three or more independent groups, ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons where appropriate (Fig. 1). Statistical significance was indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 or ***p < 0.001.

Sample sizes for animal experiments were determined a priori using G*Power 3.1 software, with the mechanical pain threshold (von Frey test) as the primary outcome, based on pilot experiments and previous studies on nitroglycerin-induced migraine models [10, 30]. A two-tailed unpaired t-test was assumed with an alpha (α) level of 0.05 and a statistical power (1-β) of 0.80. Pilot data indicated a mean difference of 30% between groups with a standard deviation of 21%, corresponding to a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.43). This yielded a minimum required sample size of 8 mice per group for behavioral experiments. For immunohistochemistry and in vivo electrophysiology experiments, which typically exhibit lower inter-animal variability, 4–5 mice per group were used, consistent with standard practice in the field. For the clinical cohort, the sample size was based on clinical feasibility and is comparable to prior phenotypic studies in vestibular migraine [27, 28]. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Results

VM patients exhibit hyperalgesia, vestibular disorder, and persistent comorbidity anxiety and depression

To define the clinical profile of VM, we recruited 35 patients who met the diagnostic criteria and 35 HCs from the community. The two groups were well-matched, with no significant differences in key demographic characteristics, including sex ratio, age, and BMI (Fig. 1A-C). The detailed clinical characteristics of the VM patient cohort, including disease duration, attack frequency, vestibular symptom types, and medication use, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Firstly, we assessed the cardinal symptoms of headache and vestibular dysfunction. Using the NRS and the VAS, we found that VM patients during the attack phase (VM-A) reported significantly higher pain scores compared to HCs (Fig. 1D-E). As expected, these scores were significantly reduced during the interictal phase (VM-I), yet remained elevated over HC levels (Fig. 1D-E). The impact of headache on daily life, as measured by the Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6), was also significantly greater in VM patients than in HCs (Fig. 1F). Vestibular function was evaluated using the DHI, which assesses physical, emotional, and functional domains. VM patients during the attack phase showed markedly elevated scores across all three DHI subscales compared to HCs. Similar to the pain measures, DHI scores in the emotional and functional domains were significantly lower in the interictal phase. However, they remained elevated compared to the HC group, indicating a residual impact of vestibular disorder on emotional state and daily function between attacks (Fig. 1G-J). Notably, we investigated the comorbidity of affective disturbances. Assessment with the HAD revealed that VM patients had significantly higher anxiety and depression scores during the attack phase. Critically, these elevated affective scores, although somewhat reduced, remained significantly higher in the interictal phase compared to HCs (Fig. 1K-L). These findings indicated that clinically significant levels of anxiety and depression persist during the interictal phase in VM patients, representing a sustained comorbid burden rather than merely a transient state during attacks.

Chronic NTG injection with rotarod vestibular stimulus induces VM - and anxiety-like behaviors in mice

Repeated intraperitoneal injection of NTG is a well-established mouse model of migraine [30]. To investigate the neural mechanisms underlying VM, mice received daily intraperitoneal NTG injections combined with alternating-day rotarod vestibular stimulation for nine consecutive days, followed by a comprehensive behavioral assessment to evaluate migraine-associated pain, vestibular function, and anxiety-like behaviors. (Fig. 2A). Firstly, we employed the von Frey test and hot plate test to evaluate mechanical and thermal pain thresholds. VM model mice exhibited significantly reduced mechanical pain thresholds in both the periorbital region and the hind paw, as well as markedly decreased thermal pain thresholds (Fig. 2B-D), indicating the development of hyperalgesia. Vestibular dysfunction, another key symptom of VM, was evaluated using the balance beam test and gait test. In the balance beam test, VM model mice exhibited significant impairments in both anticipatory and reactive balance control, as evidenced by prolonged time to start and increased total time on the beam (Fig. 2E). Further analysis of motor coordination via the gait test revealed a significant reduction in the length of hindlimb, accompanied by increased hindlimb width and overlap, indicating pronounced gait incoordination (Fig. 2F-G). These gait deficits directly reflect impaired vestibular-motor coordination, a core pathophysiology underlying the sensation of vertigo and postural instability in patients. Collectively, these results confirm the successful induction of VM-like behaviors. Beyond hyperalgesia and vestibular dysfunction, VM patient often presented with comorbid mood disorders such as anxiety. Accordingly, we test anxiety-like behaviors using the open field test and elevated plus maze. In the open field test, VM model mice showed a significant decrease in total length, central duration and central visit (Fig. 2H). Moreover, in the elevated plus maze, these mice showed a significant decrease in the percentage of time and visit in open arms (Fig. 2I), indicating decreased locomotor activity and heightened anxiety-like behaviors. In summary, these results demonstrated that chronic NTG injection combined with rotarod vestibular stimulus recapitulates the core symptoms of VM along with comorbid anxiety-like behaviors.

pIC is hyperactivated in the VM mouse model and responds to both nociceptive and vestibular stimulus

Having established an VM mouse model, and based on human structural and functional MRI evidences implicating the insular cortex in migraine and vestibular processing [31–33], and its known anatomical convergence of nociceptive and vestibular thalamic inputs, we hypothesized that hyperactivity of the pIC drives the integrated symptomatology. Therefore, we firstly assessed whether the pIC was functionally hyperactivated in our model. To map the pIC in the VM model, we quantified neurons expressing the immediate-early (IE) gene c-Fos, a well-established marker of neuronal activity. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a significant increase in the number of c-Fos positive cells within the pIC of VM model mice compared to controls (Fig. 3A-B). To verify this finding with greater temporal precision, we employed multichannel in vivo electrophysiology recordings in VM and control mice (Fig. 3C). Consistent with the c-Fos data, we observed a marked increase in the spontaneous firing rate of pIC neurons following NTG injection combined with rotarod vestibular stimulus (Fig. 3D-E). These results strongly indicated that the pIC is hyperactivated in the VM mouse model.

To further explore whether pIC activation is linked to specific sensory components of VM, we examined its response to discrete nociceptive and vestibular challenges with c-Fos staining in wild type mice. In a formalin-induced acute inflammatory pain mouse model, we found a significant upregulation of c-Fos positive cells number in the pIC (Fig. 3F-G). Similarly, mice with a vestibular stimulus via rotational motion also induced a robust increase in pIC c-Fos positive cells number (Fig. 3H-I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the pIC is respond to both pain and vestibular stimulation, and its hyperactivation in the VM mice model positions it as a convergent node for the integration of these distinct sensory pathways.

Chemogenetic inhibition of the pIC alleviates VM- and anxiety-like behaviors in mice

To explore whether pIC hyperactivation is necessary for the expression of VM- and anxiety-like behaviors, an adeno-associated virus (AAV) encoding the inhibitory designer receptors exclusively activated by designer drugs (DREADDs) expressing hM4D(Gi), was bilaterally delivered into the pIC of VM model mice (Fig. 4A-B). This approach enabled targeted suppression of pIC upon administration of CNO. Acute inhibition of the pIC in VM model mice significantly alleviated hyperalgesia, as evidenced by the reversal of reduced mechanical and thermal pain thresholds in both the von Frey test and hot plate test (Fig. 4C-E). Meanwhile, acute inhibition of the pIC significantly reduced both the time to start and total time in the balance beam test (Fig. 4F), and normalized gait abnormalities, including increased gait length, decreased gait width and overlap (Fig. 4G-H). However, acute inhibition of the pIC failed to alleviate anxiety-like behaviors in either the open field test or the elevated plus maze (Fig. 4I-J).

Given this differential effect on anxiety-like behaviors, we next explored the consequences of chronic pIC inhibition. Chronic chemogenetic activation of the pIC is sufficient to promote VM-like and anxiety-like behaviors

To determine whether pIC hyperactivation is sufficient to drive the development of VM- and anxiety-like behaviors, we employed a gain-of-function chemogenetic approach. We leveraged a subthreshold modeling paradigm, in which a 6-days NTG injection combined with rotarod vestibular stimulus, insufficient to reliably induce hyperalgesia on its own, was paired with chronic chemogenetic activation of the pIC (Fig. 6A). Specifically, an adeno-associated virus (AAV) encoding the excitatory DREADD receptors hM3D(Gq) was bilaterally delivered into the pIC, and CNO was administered for four consecutive days during the subthreshold NTG regimen combined with rotarod vestibular stimulus (Fig. 6B-C). The results show that chronic activation of pIC robustly promoted the emergence of VM-like phenotypes. Mice expressing hM3D(Gq) exhibited a pronounced reduction in mechanical and thermal pain thresholds compared with mCherry controls, as assessed by the von Frey test and hot plate test (Fig. 6D-E). While a non-significant decreasing trend was observed in thermal pain latency (Fig. 6F). The lack of significant effects on thermal pain threshold and stride length in this paradigm may be attributable to a ceiling effect of the subthreshold model.

Furthermore, activation of pIC leads to a significant increase in both the time to start and total time in balance beam test, alongside increased gait width and overlap (Fig. 6G-I). The lack of significant effects on thermal pain threshold and stride length in this paradigm may be attributable to a ceiling effect of the subthreshold model. Besides, chronic activation of pIC was also sufficient to promote anxiety-like behaviors. In the open field test, hM3D(Gq)-expressing mice spent significantly less time in and made fewer visit into the center area, similar with a significant decrease in the percentage of time spent in the open arms (Fig. 6J-K). Collectively, these results demonstrate that hyperactivation of the pIC is sufficient to VM- and anxiety-like behaviors.

The NMDA receptor antagonist memantine alleviates VM- and anxiety-like behaviors by suppressing pIC hyperactivity

Having established the pivotal role of pIC hyperactivation in VM, we next sought to identify a potential therapeutic agent targeting this node. We focused on memantine, a clinically used NMDA receptor antagonist known to modulate neuronal excitability (Fig. 7A). The results show that systemic administration of memantine in VM model mice produced a broad therapeutic effect. Treatment significantly alleviated hyperalgesia, reversing the deficits in both mechanical and thermal pain thresholds (Fig. 7B-D). It also restored the function of balance control, significantly reducing the time to start and total time in the balance beam test (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, it normalized gait parameters by increasing gait length while decreasing gait width and overlap (Fig. 7F-G). Furthermore, memantine exhibited a potent anxiolytic effect, increasing central duration and visit in the open field test and percentage of time in the open arms in the elevated plus maze (Fig. 7H-I). We hypothesized that memantine’s therapeutic actions, as an NMDA receptor antagonist, might stem from a normalization of pIC hyperactivity. To test this, we performed multichannel in vivo electrophysiological recordings in pIC (Fig. 7J). Strikingly, memantine treatment significantly reduced the elevated firing rate of pIC neurons in VM model mice (Fig. 7K-L). These findings provide a direct neural correlate for the behavioral rescue and position the suppression of pIC excitability as a key mechanism underlying memantine’s efficacy.

In summary, our results delineate a causal pathway in which pIC hyperactivation drives the core symptoms of VM. We demonstrate that pIC hyperactivity is necessary for the expression of sensory hypersensitive and vestibular deficits, and sufficient to lower the threshold for their development alongside anxiety-like behaviors. Critically, we identify that the chronic dysregulation of pIC underlies the comorbid anxiety, and we validate a therapeutic strategy using memantine to normalize pIC firing and alleviate the multifaceted VM phenotype.

Discussion

VM is characterized by a debilitating triad of headache, vertigo, and frequently comorbid mood disorders [34]. While these clinical features are well-documented, the central mechanisms by which disparate hyperalgesia, vestibular, and emotional signals converge to produce the integrated VM phenotype remain unclear. Bridging clinical observation and VM rodent model, we identify the pIC as a critical neural hub driving this convergence. Through chemogenetic and pharmacological approaches, our findings establish the pIC as a key casual node in the pathology of VM and invite further exploration into the precise circuits and mechanisms through which it coordinates this multifaceted disorder.

In clinical study, our data confirm the expected episodic burden of core sensory symptoms in VM patients, with exacerbation of headache and vestibular impairment during acute periods. However, it further reveals a critical dissociation: while sensory symptoms remit to a degree interictally, the burden of anxiety and depression remains persistently elevated in patients compared to HCs. This pattern suggests that affective disturbances in VM are not merely a transient reaction to acute symptoms, but rather constitute a sustained, trait-like component of the disorder. Such persistence aligns with established models of chronic pain, which can trigger lasting neuroplasticity within limbic and paralimbic circuits—such as the lateral habenula, basal and central amygdala, and hippocampus—that outlasts the initiating stimulus and maintains a negative affective state [35–37]. This clinical observation provided the key rationale for our study: to identify a neural substrate that could not only process acute sensory (pain and vestibular) signals but also drive or interface with the circuits underlying persistent mood disorder comorbidity.

Within the cerebral cortex, the integration of interoceptive and exteroceptive signals is crucial for generating a unified perceptual and emotional experience [38]. Human neuroimaging studies consistently implicate the insular cortex, particularly its posterior subdivision, in migraine pathophysiology [31–33]. The pIC serves as a primary cortical target for thalamic projections carrying nociceptive and visceral information [19, 39]. Notably, it also receives vestibular inputs via thalamic relays from the vestibular nuclear complex [22], positioning its role of integrate the very sensory modalities disrupted in VM. Rodent studies further delineate a functional gradient within the insula, where the pIC acts as a sensory integrator, and the anterior insular cortex associates these signals with emotional valence [21]. Our data extend this framework to VM. We first established that mice VM models exhibited core symptoms such as hyperalgesia and balance control disorders, comorbid with anxiety-like behavior. Based on this model, we found that the pIC is hyperactivated via c-Fos immunofluorescence staining, in response to both nociceptive (formalin injection) and vestibular (rotation) challenges, confirming its role as a vestibular and aversive sensory hub. Crucially, chemogenetic manipulations demonstrated that pIC activity is necessary for the development of VM-associated hyperalgesia and balance control deficits. This aligns with the known physiology of these pathways: both pain and vestibular signals are transmitted via fast-conducting thalamocortical routes, allowing for rapid, state-dependent modulation by cortical areas like the pIC.

Notably, a pivotal and unexpected finding was the temporal dissociation in the pIC’s control over different symptoms. While acute pIC inhibition rapidly normalized sensory and balance control deficits in VM mice, it failed to alleviate anxiety-like behaviors. In contrast, chronic chemogenetic inhibition of the pIC produced a full therapeutic response, including the resolution of anxiety-like behaviors. This dissociation provides a mechanistic parallel to our clinical observation: acute sensory symptoms are directly gated by pIC activity, whereas comorbid anxiety likely arises from downstream, plasticity-dependent adaptations in affective circuits (such as the circuit from pIC to amygdala, prefrontal cortex, and lateral habenula) driven by persistent pIC hyperactivity [40, 41]. Only sustained normalization of pIC output can reverse these maladaptive changes. This model explains why affective symptoms persist interictally and offers a novel framework for understanding the transition from episodic to chronic, refractory VM, centered on the progressive dysregulation of the pIC and its efferent projections to limbic networks. Furthermore, our gain-of-function experiments, in which chronic chemogenetic activation of the pIC promoted VM-like behavior under a subthreshold NTG regimen combined with rotarod vestibular stimulus, provide complementary evidence that pIC hyperactivity is sufficient to drive the disorder. These findings not only solidify the pIC’s role as a critical node but also suggest that its aberrant activity may serve as a key biomarker and a proximal trigger for the symptomatic expression of VM, highlighting its potential as a focal point for preventative intervention. While our study provides causal evidence for the pIC’s central role, we acknowledge that VM likely involves a distributed network. Other regions—notably the cerebellum for vestibular-motor coordination and brainstem vestibular nuclei—are integral to this circuitry. Future unbiased mapping studies should delineate the full network and clarify how hyperactivity in the pIC interacts with these nodes to produce specific symptom domains. Furthermore, our findings suggest a distinct role for the pIC in VM compared to migraine without aura (MWoA). While the insula is involved in both disorders [32], its function in VM may be specialized as a site for integrating vestibular signals with nociceptive and affective processing—a convergence that drives VM’s characteristic symptom triad, which is supported by our data where pIC manipulation directly altered vestibular function, a core deficit not typically associated with MWoA. Thus, the pIC may transition from a modulator of pain in MWoA to a causal integrator of multiple sensory domains in VM.

Given the central role of pIC hyperactivation in driving VM pathophysiology, we next sought to explore a translatable therapeutic strategy aimed at normalizing this aberrant activity. While acute migraine therapies often target nociceptive or vascular pathways, and vestibular suppressants may alleviate dizziness [42, 43], these approaches do not address the integrated neural circuit dysfunction or the persistent affective comorbidity seen in VM. We therefore focused on memantine, a clinically used, well-tolerated NMDA receptor antagonist with documented efficacy in modulating chronic pain, affective disorders, and neuronal hyperexcitability [43]. We found that systemic administration of memantine in a VM mouse model produced a broad-spectrum therapeutic effect, significantly alleviating hyperalgesia, balance control deficits, and anxiety-like behaviors. This comprehensive rescue suggests that memantine’s action converges upon a common upstream mechanism driving the multifaceted symptoms. To directly test whether this mechanism involves the pIC, we performed in vivo electrophysiological recordings. Notably, memantine treatment effectively suppressed the elevated firing rate of pIC neurons in VM mice. The therapeutic effect of memantine is likely mediated through its canonical pharmacological action [44]. The pIC is rich in NMDA-type glutamate receptors, which are pivotal for synaptic integration and neuronal excitability [45]. By antagonizing these receptors, memantine is poised to suppress pathological hyperactivity within the pIC, thereby interrupting the aberrant signal processing that underlies both the sensory and affective dimensions of VM. Our findings validate pIC hyperexcitability as a therapeutically targetable node, and identify memantine, or drugs with a similar mechanism, as promising candidates for the treatment of VM, particularly in cases where sensory and emotional symptoms are intertwined. A limitation is that systemic administration of memantine does not specifically target the pIC. Thus, although our data strongly associate pIC normalization with behavioral rescue, establishing a definitive causal link will require further studies employing localized drug delivery to the pIC.

While our study establishes the pIC as a causal hub in VM, several important questions remain, defining clear directions for future research. Firstly, a key consideration is whether our combined NTG and vestibular stimulus model specifically mimics VM or merely represents chronic migraine with motor stress. We posit its relevance to VM based on the concurrent induction of the core symptom triad (such as hyperalgesia, vestibulomotor deficits, and anxiety) and pIC hyperactivation as a sufficient causal driver for all three domains. This mechanistic convergence argues against a simple additive “migraine with rotarod stimulus” model, instead supporting the integrated pathophysiology characteristic of VM. Secondly, the specific downstream circuits through which the pIC exerts its differential effects have yet to be mapped. It is essential to determine whether projections to sensory, motor, or limbic regions preferentially mediate the sensory-motor deficits versus the persistent affective component. Furthermore, while memantine’s efficacy and its association with normalized pIC activity are compelling, systemic administration lacks anatomical specificity. Future studies employing intracerebral drug delivery or the development of novel, targeted compounds could enhance therapeutic precision and minimize off-target effects.

In conclusion, our findings establish the pIC as a critical driver of VM, where its hyperactivity integrates and generates the disorder’s core sensory, vestibular, and affective symptoms. The distinct outcomes of acute versus chronic pIC inhibition reveal a fundamental dissociation between rapidly modulated sensory pathways and plasticity-dependent emotional circuits. This work not only elucidates a central mechanism underlying VM, but also validates the pIC as a promising therapeutic target for intervention.
