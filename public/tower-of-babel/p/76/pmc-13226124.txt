Single-port robotic resection of gliomatosis peritonei following immature ovarian teratoma in an adolescent: A case report
Authors: Qingchi Zhang, Lingling Sun, Kuiran Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•GP after immature ovarian teratoma may mimic malignant recurrence.•CT, MRI, PET-CT, and tumor markers may not provide definitive diagnosis.•Histopathologic confirmation is essential when recurrence cannot be excluded.•Single-port robotics may facilitate selected multifocal peritoneal resections.

Introduction

Gliomatosis peritonei (GP) is a rare condition characterized by dissemination of mature glial tissue on peritoneal surfaces, most commonly associated with ovarian teratomas, particularly immature ovarian teratoma (IOT) (Guo et al., 2022, Małgorzata et al., 2021, Patel and Meena, 2022). GP is generally regarded as a benign entity despite extensive peritoneal involvement. Its pathogenesis remains controversial, with proposed mechanisms including implantation of glial tissue following tumor rupture and metaplastic transformation of submesothelial cells induced by teratomatous factors (Nasfy et al., 2024). Although histologically benign, GP may closely mimic recurrent immature teratoma or, in some cases, coexist with growing teratoma syndrome (GTS), creating a challenging postoperative diagnostic scenario in adolescents and young women (File et al., 2025, Türkmen et al., 2024).

In clinical practice, the most consequential distinction is not simply between GP and GTS, but between benign mature peritoneal implants and true recurrence of immature teratoma (Zhang et al., 2022). Cross-sectional imaging may reveal multiple new or enlarging peritoneal nodules, and FDG-PET/CT may further heighten concern because GP can be metabolically active (Lavoie et al., 2017). At the same time, serum tumor markers are often normal in both GP and GTS, limiting their discriminatory value (Małgorzata et al., 2021, Mira et al., 2024, Semiz et al., 2022). Definitive diagnosis relies on surgical exploration and histopathological confirmation of mature glial tissue with low proliferative activity (Nogales et al., 2012; Patel and Meena, 2022).

Management of GP remains controversial. While asymptomatic mature glial implants may remain stable for long periods and, in selected cases, may be managed with surveillance, surgery becomes important when recurrence cannot be confidently excluded, when lesions are progressive, or when tissue diagnosis will directly alter subsequent treatment decisions (File et al., 2025, Imterat et al., 2026). The optimal surgical approach for GP has not been established, particularly in pediatric and adolescent patients where fertility preservation and minimization of surgical trauma are paramount (Türkmen et al., 2024). Conventional open or multi-port laparoscopic surgery may be limited in cases with multifocal or anatomically challenging peritoneal lesions (Wang et al., 2024). Here, we report an adolescent with multiple new peritoneal nodules after treatment for IOT in whom normal tumor markers contrasted with suspicious PET-CT findings. The case highlights the diagnostic workup, the rationale for surgical exploration, and the role of histopathology in distinguishing GP from true recurrence. We also describe the use of a single-port robotic approach as a minimally invasive technical option for selected multifocal lesions in a young patient.

Case Presentation

A 12-year-old girl was admitted to our institution after an asymptomatic mixed abdominopelvic mass was incidentally detected. Imaging revealed a solid–cystic lesion originating from the right ovary. She underwent right salpingo-oophorectomy with partial omentectomy, and histopathology confirmed an immature ovarian teratoma. Postoperatively, three cycles of carboplatin, etoposide, and bleomycin chemotherapy were administered.

Three months after chemotherapy, follow-up magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a newly developed pelvic mass, and PET–CT revealed multiple fluorodeoxyglucose-avid nodules in the pelvic cavity and subphrenic region (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Serum alpha-fetoprotein and beta-human chorionic gonadotropin levels remained within normal ranges. Based on these findings, GP or GTS were considered; however, true recurrence of immature teratoma could not be excluded with sufficient confidence. Single-port robotic-assisted resection was performed using the SHURUI single-port robotic surgical system (Shanghai, China) through a 2.5-cm transumbilical incision. Intraoperative exploration revealed multiple pale yellow nodules involving the posterior uterine space, rectouterine pouch, falciform ligament, and diaphragmatic peritoneum (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). All visible lesions were completely excised under robotic guidance, with intraoperative rotation of the robotic platform allowing access to subphrenic lesions without additional ports. Operative time was 180 minutes, with minimal blood loss.

Histopathological examination confirmed mature astrocytic glial tissue positive for glial fibrillary acidic protein and S-100, with a very low Ki-67 index, establishing the diagnosis of gliomatosis peritonei. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient experienced rapid functional recovery with minimal postoperative pain, and pain scores decreased to 1-2 on a numerical rating scale by postoperative day 2 without the need for opioid analgesics. The surgical incision healed well with excellent cosmetic outcome and no wound-related complications (Fig. 3). The patient was discharged on postoperative day five. Discussion

Gliomatosis peritonei is an uncommon but clinically important entity after treatment of immature ovarian teratoma because it may present as new or enlarging peritoneal disease while remaining histologically benign (Imterat et al., 2026). In this postoperative setting, the most clinically consequential distinction is between benign mature peritoneal implants and true recurrence of immature teratoma, rather than simply between GP and growing teratoma syndrome. GP and GTS may coexist, and both may present with normal serum tumor markers; therefore, normal alpha-fetoprotein or beta-human chorionic gonadotropin levels support the possibility of a mature or benign process but do not, by themselves, exclude recurrence (File et al., 2025, Türkmen et al., 2024). Similarly, clinical symptoms are often absent or nonspecific, while larger or strategically located lesions may cause compression or future mechanical risk (Imterat et al., 2026). Cross-sectional imaging is essential for detecting lesion distribution and surgical anatomy, but CT may show peritoneal nodules or thickening that mimic carcinomatosis, MRI may better delineate soft-tissue relationships but remains insufficient for definitive tissue characterization, and PET-CT can be misleading because GP itself may be FDG-avid (Lavoie et al., 2017, Türkmen et al., 2024). Therefore, when CT, MRI, PET-CT, and tumor markers provide conflicting or inconclusive information, especially in the presence of sizeable lesions, progressive disease, or concern for compression, histopathologic confirmation becomes central to management (Patel and Meena, 2022).

The role of surgery in GP should therefore be individualized and should be understood first as a means of establishing diagnosis and guiding subsequent oncologic management (Türkmen et al., 2024). Observation may be reasonable for asymptomatic, stable, mature-appearing implants when clinical suspicion for recurrence is low; however, this strategy is less appropriate when new lesions appear after chemotherapy, when lesions are metabolically active, when tumor volume raises concern for future compression, or when immature teratoma recurrence cannot be confidently excluded (Nasfy et al., 2024). In such situations, biopsy or surgical excision is justified not because GP itself always requires aggressive treatment, but because tissue diagnosis determines whether the patient should undergo surveillance, further chemotherapy, or additional surgery (File et al., 2025). In the present case, the combination of multifocal new peritoneal nodules, FDG uptake, normal tumor markers, and involvement of both pelvic and subphrenic regions created a diagnostic gray zone in which observation alone could not safely exclude recurrence. Surgical exploration was therefore undertaken for definitive histopathological diagnosis. The extent of surgery in GP is more nuanced than a simple choice between biopsy and radical complete resection. The literature does not support routine aggressive clearance of all mature glial implants in every patient, particularly when lesions are diffuse, asymptomatic, and confidently diagnosed as mature GP (Imterat et al., 2026). However, when surgery is already required for tissue diagnosis, and when visible lesions can be removed without substantially increasing operative trauma or risk, a more complete excision of safely accessible disease may offer several practical advantages. First, it allows broader sampling of lesions from different anatomical regions, reducing sampling error and the possibility that an unbiopsied nodule contains immature or malignant elements. Second, it decreases residual lesion burden and may reduce future diagnostic ambiguity during surveillance. Third, when lesions are localized or technically accessible, excision may reduce the risk of future mass effect or repeat procedures. Thus, our intent was not to claim that complete resection is mandatory for all cases of GP, but to clarify that, once surgery was indicated for diagnosis in this patient, complete removal of all safely accessible visible lesions was a reasonable strategy that balanced diagnostic certainty, disease control, and surgical morbidity.

The technical contribution of this case should be interpreted within this decision-making framework. GP frequently involves multiple peritoneal sites, and lesions may be distributed across anatomically distant regions such as the pelvis, omentum, falciform ligament, and subphrenic or diaphragmatic peritoneum. Conventional laparoscopy can manage many such cases, but multiple working angles or additional ports may be required when lesions are located in both pelvic and upper abdominal compartments. In our patient, the single-port robotic platform enabled transumbilical access through a 2.5-cm incision and allowed intraoperative rotation of the platform to address lesions in different directions, including both pelvic implants and subphrenic/diaphragmatic lesions, without additional ports. This technical feature was useful because it allowed comprehensive tissue-confirming resection while preserving the principles of minimally invasive surgery in an adolescent patient. Importantly, we do not suggest that a single-port robotic platform is required for GP management or that it is superior to conventional laparoscopy, multi-port robotics, or open surgery in all settings. Rather, when diagnostic surgery is indicated and multifocal lesions are safely accessible, single-port robotics may represent one feasible minimally invasive option for selected young patients. Long-term surveillance remains essential, because GP may persist for years, may coexist with GTS, and only rarely may show aggressive behavior; therefore, the 12-month recurrence-free follow-up in this case is reassuring but insufficient to define long-term stability, recurrence risk, or fertility outcomes.
