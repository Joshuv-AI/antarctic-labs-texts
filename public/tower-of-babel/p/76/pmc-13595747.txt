Insights into antimicrobial resistance: dissemination and molecular epidemiology of carbapenemase and AmpC genes in uropathogenic Escherichia coli
Authors: Maryam Shirzadian, Fatemeh Foroohi, Abbas Fallah Vosoughi, Shervindokht Ahmadi, Taha Golpasand, Payam Behzadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated the prevalence of acquired/natural AmpC β-lactamase genes (ABLGs) and acquired carbapenemase genes (ACGs) and their association with antimicrobial resistance in uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC).MethodsA total of 105 non-duplicate UPEC isolates recovered from urine specimens collected in Tehran, Iran (August 2023–January 2024), were analyzed. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed by the Kirby–Bauer method. ESBL and AmpC phenotypes were determined phenotypically, and blaACC, blaCMY, blaDHA, blaFOX, blaIMP−1, blaIMP−2, blaVIM−1, and blaVIM−2 were detected by polymerase chain reaction (PCR).ResultsResistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin was observed in all isolates, followed by ciprofloxacin (75.24%), ofloxacin (60.95%), and cefotaxime (60.95%). ESBL and AmpC phenotypes were detected in 37.14% and 11.43% of isolates, respectively. MDR, XDR, and PDR phenotypes occurred in 55.24%, 16.19%, and 2.86% of isolates. The most prevalent β-lactamase genes (BLGs) were blaIMP−2 (16.19%), blaDHA (10.48%), and blaVIM−1 (5.71%), whereas blaACC, blaCMY and blaFOX were not detected. Most isolates (64.76%) lacked the investigated BLGs.ConclusionsUPEC isolates from Tehran exhibited substantial antimicrobial resistance, including MDR, XDR, and PDR phenotypes. ESBL and AmpC producers were common, with blaIMP−2, blaDHA, and blaVIM−1 predominating. Universal ampicillin/amoxicillin resistance and marked phenotypic-genotypic diversity suggest additional resistance mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05328-6.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections worldwide and represent a major cause of morbidity in both community and healthcare settings [1–4]. The treatment of UTIs is increasingly complicated by the emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among Enterobacterales, particularly uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC), the leading causative agent of these infections [5–10]. UPEC exhibits remarkable genomic plasticity, driven by its expansive, open pangenome. This flexibility allows the acquisition of pathogenicity islands and antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), enhancing host colonization and survival. Unlike commensal E. coli, whose genome remains around 4.5 Mb, UPEC genomes often exceed 5 Mb, reflecting additional virulence and adaptive elements [8, 10–16].

Among the most important mechanisms contributing to AMR is the production of β-lactamases, enzymes that hydrolyze β-lactam antibiotics and compromise the effectiveness of commonly used therapeutic agents [3, 13, 17–20]. Among the most clinically significant are AmpC β-lactamases (ABLs) and carbapenemases. ABLs confer resistance to a broad range of β-lactam antibiotics and are poorly inhibited by conventional β-lactamase inhibitors, thereby limiting treatment options [21–24]. Thus, cefepime is a key therapeutic option for the treatment of invasive infections caused by AmpC-producing Enterobacterales [22, 25, 26]. The AmpCs produced by Enterobacterales such as UPEC can be encoded by bacterial chromosome or plasmids [22, 24, 27]. Although AmpC and extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) enzymes act on similar substrates, their functional profiles differ markedly. AmpCs resist inhibition by clavulanic acid and tazobactam and efficiently hydrolyze cephamycins. In contrast, ESBLs are inhibited by these agents, show no activity against cephamycins, and are more often linked to cefepime resistance [22–24, 28]. Carbapenemases, including IMP- and VIM-type metallo-β-lactamases (MBLs), further threaten clinical management by inactivating carbapenems, which are often considered last-resort agents for severe infections caused by multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative bacteria [29–36]. The increasing prevalence of these enzymes has contributed to the global spread of MDR, extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and, in some cases, pan-drug-resistant (PDR) Enterobacterales, resulting in poorer clinical outcomes, prolonged hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs [3, 36–40]. A major factor driving the dissemination of β-lactam resistance genes is the mobilome, which comprises mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as plasmids, integrons, and transposons [3, 20, 22–26, 30–32, 41–46]. These elements facilitate horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and enable the rapid spread of resistance determinants, including AmpC- and carbapenemase-encoding genes, both within and between bacterial populations. Consequently, the acquisition and dissemination of these genes have accelerated the emergence of highly resistant UPEC strains and reduced the effectiveness of available antimicrobial therapies [3, 20, 22–26, 30–32, 41–46].

Although ABLs and carbapenemases are increasingly recognized as important contributors to resistance in UPEC, data regarding the distribution of these determinants and their association with resistance phenotypes remain limited in many geographic regions. The present study investigated the prevalence of acquired carbapenemase genes (ACGs) (e.g., blaIMP−1, blaIMP−2, blaVIM−1 and blaVIM−2) and blaAmpC acquired/natural ABLGs (e.g., blaACC, blaCMY, blaDHA and blaFOX) among UPEC isolates recovered from patients with UTIs (Table 1). Furthermore, comprehensive genotypic and phenotypic characterization was performed to determine the distribution of these resistance determinants and their association with ESBL production, AmpC phenotype, MDR, XDR, and PDR profiles.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample collection

In this cross-sectional study, 105 non-duplicate UPEC isolates were recovered from midstream urine specimens collected from both inpatient and outpatient individuals attending three medical centers in Tehran, Iran, between August 2023 and January 2024. The study was laboratory-based and relied on anonymized microbiological samples obtained through routine diagnostic procedures [3, 46].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Urine samples positive for UPEC were collected from hospitalized and outpatient individuals with the assistance of trained personnel from participating hospitals, clinics, and diagnostic laboratories. Only culture-confirmed positive specimens exhibiting bacterial counts of ≥ 10⁵ CFU/mL were received by the authors for further investigation. There were no specific exclusion criteria [3, 46].

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Islamic Azad University, Shahr-e-Qods Branch (IR.IAU.QODS.REC.1402.003) and followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Informed consent and data privacy

was waived by the ethics committee because all samples and associated data were fully anonymized before analysis. Only patient age and sex were available to the authors. Information regarding clinical symptoms, diagnosis, inpatient versus outpatient distribution, prior antimicrobial exposure, comorbidities, infection severity, and classification of infections as complicated or uncomplicated was not accessible. Therefore, correlations between microbiological findings and clinical characteristics could not be assessed.

Bacterial identification

Specimens were cultured on MacConkey agar (Quelab) and blood agar (IBRESCO), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Presumptive E. coli isolates were identified using standard microbiological and biochemical tests, including Gram staining, IMViC reactions, SIM, triple sugar iron (TSI) (IBRESCO), and urease hydrolysis (Merck). Confirmed isolates were subsequently propagated in Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (IBRESCO) for antimicrobial susceptibility testing and molecular analyses [3, 46, 51–53].

Antimicrobial susceptibility test (AST)

Antimicrobial resistance profiles were assessed using the standard Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar (IBRESCO, Italy). The susceptibility of the isolates was evaluated against 14 antimicrobial agents obtained from Padtan-Teb (Iran), representing several major antibiotic classes: macrolides [azithromycin (AZM) 30 µg], aminoglycosides [amikacin (AN) 30 µg; gentamicin (GM) 10 µg], penicillins [ampicillin (AMP) 10 µg; amoxicillin (AMX) 20 µg], carbapenems [imipenem (IPM) 10 µg; meropenem (MEM) 10 µg], fluoroquinolones [ciprofloxacin (CIP) 5 µg; ofloxacin (OFX) 5 µg], cephamycin [cefoxitin (FOX) 30 µg], and cephalosporins [cefepime (FEP) 30 µg; ceftazidime (CAZ) 30 µg; cefotaxime (CTX) 30 µg; ceftriaxone (CRO) 30 µg]. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used as the quality control strain. Susceptibility interpretations were assigned according to CLSI 2023 guidelines [3, 19, 21, 46, 51, 52, 54].

Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBL) phenotypic detection test

To confirm the presence of extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), isolates exhibiting reduced susceptibility to any of the indicator cephalosporins (FEP, CAZ, CTX, or CRO) during initial screening were subjected to additional testing. Phenotypic verification was performed using the combination disk diffusion assay. Ceftazidime (30 µg) and cefotaxime (30 µg) discs were tested alone and in combination with clavulanate [Ceftazidime+Clavulanate (CZC) (30 µg + 10 µg/disc) and Cefotaxime+Clavulanate (CTC) (30 µg + 10 µg/disc)] (Padtan-Teb, Iran). An isolate was classified as an ESBL producer if the inhibition zone surrounding either cephalosporin–clavulanate disk increased by ≥ 5 mm compared with the corresponding cephalosporin disk without clavulanate [3, 46, 51, 54, 55].

Due to the unavailability of pre-combined cefepime–clavulanate (FEC) and ceftriaxone–clavulanate (CZC) discs from Padtan-Teb, Iran, our study employed an alternative strategy to detect extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production. A modified double-disc synergy test (MDDS) was performed by placing an amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (AMC) disc (20/10 µg) (Padtan-Teb, Iran) at the center of MHA, with FEP (30 µg) and CRO (30 µg) discs (Padtan-Teb, Iran) positioned 20 mm from the central disc. ESBL production was indicated by a noticeable enhancement of the inhibition zone adjacent to the FEP or CRO disc after incubation at 35 °C for 24 h, reflecting the synergistic effect of diffused clavulanic acid. ESBL production was confirmed when the inhibition zone surrounding the β-lactam–β-lactamase inhibitor combination disc was ≥ 5 mm greater than that of the corresponding cephalosporin disc alone (I2 − I1≥ 5 mm). The E. coli strain ATCC 25,922 served as a negative control. This study also screened for isolates with key resistance profiles: Multidrug-resistant (MDR), showing non-susceptibility to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories; Extensively drug-resistant (XDR), susceptible to two or fewer categories; and Pandrug-resistant (PDR), which were non-susceptible to all tested antimicrobial agents [3, 46, 55–57].

Phenotypic determination of AmpC

To assess the presence of AmpC β-lactamases, isolate susceptibility to cefoxitin and cefepime was evaluated. An AmpC-positive phenotype was defined as resistance to cefoxitin (zone diameter ≤ 14 mm) accompanied by susceptibility to cefepime (zone diameter ≥ 18 mm) [58–60].

DNA extraction procedures

Genomic DNA was extracted from confirmed UPEC isolates using boiling method. DNA quality and concentration were assessed spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop™ analyzer and stored at − 20 °C until molecular analysis [3, 43, 46, 51–53, 61].

Multiplex PCR (mPCR) technology and ABLGs and ACGs screening

Multiplex PCR (mPCR) was employed to investigate the distribution of eight BLGs among the 105 UPEC isolates. The assay targeted four ABLGs (e.g., blaACC, blaCMY, blaDHA, blaFOX) and four MBLGs (e.g., blaIMP−1, blaIMP−2, blaVIM−1 and blaVIM−2) [3, 21, 43, 51, 52, 61]. The detection of ABLGs and ACGs was accomplished using two distinct mPCR assays, each prepared in a final reaction volume of 25 µL.

For ABLGs: The ingredients of the cocktail were 12.5 µL Master Mix (2×) (Tris- HCl; pH = 8.5, 3 mM MgCl2), 2 µL template DNA, 1 µL primers (0.5 µL F, 0.5 µL R/per each primer) (blaACC, blaCMY, blaDHA and blaFOX) and 6.5 µL sterile DNase/RNase free distilled water.

For ACGs: The ingredients of the cocktail were 12.5 µL Master Mix (2×) (Tris- HCl; pH = 8.5, 3 mM MgCl2), 2 µL template DNA, 1 µL primers (0.5 µL F, 0.5 µL R/per each primer) (blaIMP−1, blaIMP−2, blaVIM−1 and blaVIM−2) and 6.5 µL sterile DNase/RNase free distilled water.

The mPCR amplicons, produced using the cycling conditions outlined in Table 2, were run on a 1% agarose gel electrophoresis supplemented with Safe Stain.

Statistical analysis

To assess associations between categorical variables, the Chi-square (χ²) test was applied. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and corresponding percentages. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using https://www.icalcu.com/ and https://www.medcalc.org/ website.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns

The antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results are summarized in Table 3. Meropenem demonstrated the highest activity against UPEC isolates, with 84.76% susceptibility, followed by cefoxitin (71.43%) and amikacin (67.62%). In contrast, the highest resistance rates were observed for ampicillin and amoxicillin (100%), followed by ciprofloxacin (75.24%), and ofloxacin and cefotaxime (60.95%). A statistically significant association with patient gender was observed only for resistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin (P < 0.05) (via one-way chi square), whereas no other antimicrobial agents demonstrated a gender-related correlation (P > 0.05).

ESBL producing UPEC Isolates

Among the 105 UPEC isolates, 39 (37.14%) were identified as ESBL producers (Table 4). Although ESBL-producing isolates were more frequently recovered from female patients (n = 32) than male patients (n = 7), the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The susceptibility profile of ESBL-producing isolates differed substantially from that of the overall UPEC collection. Cefoxitin (74.36% susceptible), meropenem (71.80%), and amikacin (53.85%) showed the greatest activity against ESBL-producing isolates. Complete resistance was observed to ampicillin, amoxicillin, and cefepime (100%), while resistance to ceftriaxone (97.44%), ofloxacin (89.74%), and cefotaxime (89.74%) was also highly prevalent.

Phenotypic Determination of AmpC

Phenotypic testing identified 12 of 105 UPEC isolates (11.43%) as AmpC producers. None of the AmpC-producing isolates were simultaneously classified as ESBL producers. AmpC production was detected in all blaDHA-positive isolates except one strain carrying both blaDHA and blaIMP−1. Additionally, AmpC activity was identified in two isolates in which none of the BLGs investigated in the present study were detected.

Multidrug-resistance (MDR), extensively drug-resistance (XDR) and pandrug-resistance (PDR) patterns

As shown in Supplementary Table 1 (S1), MDR strains constituted 58 of the 105 UPEC isolates (55.24%). Although the majority were recovered from female patients (47 isolates, 81.03%) compared with male patients (11 isolates, 18.97%), Chi-square (χ2) analysis indicated that MDR status was not significantly associated with gender (P > 0.05). The 58 clinical MDR isolates displayed substantial heterogeneity, comprising 49 distinct MDR resistance profiles in eight groups.

Two MDR patterns of GM-AMP-AMX-IMP-OFX-CIP-FEP-CAZ-CTX-CRO (5.17%) and AZM-GM-AMP-AMX-OFX-CIP-FEP-CAZ-CTX-CRO (5.17%) ranked first; five MDR patterns of AMP-AMX-OFX-CIP-FEP-CAZ-CTX-CRO (3.45%), AMP-AMX-IMP-OFX-CIP-FEP-CAZ-CTX-CRO (3.45%), AZM-AMP-AMX-IMP-OFX-CIP-FEP-CAZ-CTX-CRO (3.45%), AZM-AN-AMP-AMX-OFX-CIP-FEP-CAZ-CTX-CRO (3.45%) and AZM-GM-AMP-AMX-IMP-OFX-CIP-FEP-CAZ-CTX-CRO (3.45%) ranked second (S1). In addition, 17 of the 105 UPEC isolates (16.19%) met the criteria for XDR, representing 13 unique resistance profiles (S2). Gender-stratified analysis of the XDR isolates showed that the 12 strains recovered from female patients exhibited 11 distinct patterns, whereas the five isolates obtained from male patients displayed four different patterns. Finally, three isolates (2.86%) were classified as PDR (S3).

Multiplex PCR (mPCR) technology for molecular screening of ABLGs and ACGs

Multiplex PCR analysis revealed that blaIMP−2 was the most frequently detected resistance gene, occurring in 17 isolates (16.19%) (Table 5). The blaDHA and blaVIM−1 genes were detected in 11 (10.48%) and six (5.71%) isolates, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1) (Table 5). No significant association was observed between the presence of these genes and patient gender (P > 0.05).

Frequency of the ABLGs and ACGs among 105 UPEC isolates

While the majority of the 105 UPEC isolates (64.76%, n = 68) lacked BLGs, our analysis identified eight distinct patterns among the positive strains, led by blaIMP−2 (13.33%), blaDHA (5.71%), and blaVIM−1 (4.76%) (S4).

Our analysis yielded comprehensive resistance profiles for all isolates, organized according to molecular BLG typing and phenotypic AMR patterns. As summarized in Table 6, the isolates were classified into nine AMR (non-MDR/XDR/PDR) patterns, 49 MDR patterns, 32 ESBL-positive patterns, 13 XDR patterns, 12 AmpC-positive patterns, and three PDR profiles. A notable finding emerged from these data: UPEC isolates lacking an identifiable BLG pattern constituted a substantial proportion of the most resistant phenotypes. Specifically, this group included two of the three (66.67%) PDR strains, nine of 17 (52.94%) XDR strains, 36 of 58 (62.07%) MDR strains, 28 of 39 (71.79%) ESBL-positive strains, and two of 12 (16.67%) AmpC-positive strains.

Discussion

This study updates phenotypic AMR profiles involving MDR, XDR, and PDR patterns and presents ALBGs and ACGs distribution profiles in UPEC strains isolated from UTI patients. MDR isolates accounted for over half of all UPEC strains (55.24%), highlighting the substantial clinical burden posed by multidrug resistance. The diversity of resistance profiles—49 unique MDR patterns and 13 XDR patterns—reflects the genetic and phenotypic heterogeneity of circulating strains. XDR (16.19%) and PDR (2.86%) isolates, although less frequent, represent alarming resistance phenotypes with significant treatment implications. Notably, most isolates lacked detectable BLGs; however, this BLG-negative group contributed disproportionately to MDR, XDR, ESBL, and even PDR phenotypes. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because only a limited subset of β-lactamase genes was investigated. Therefore, the absence of the screened genes among MDR and XDR isolates may reflect the presence of other clinically important resistance determinants that were not evaluated, including blaCTX−M, blaTEM, blaSHV, blaNDM, blaOXA−48, and blaKPC families. Non-enzymatic mechanisms such as altered membrane permeability and efflux pump overexpression may also contribute to the observed resistance phenotypes.

The present study revealed that, most isolates originated from females, though gender showed minimal association with resistance. Carbapenems, cefoxitin, and amikacin remained effective, while extensive β-lactam and fluoroquinolone resistance highlights declining first-line options, aligning with regional reports.

Consistent with previous reports, UPEC remained largely susceptible to meropenem, nitrofurantoin, and aminoglycosides, whereas resistance to ampicillin, tetracycline, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, and fluoroquinolones was common. These findings highlight the continued emergence of MDR UPEC and the declining efficacy of several conventional antimicrobials [3, 41, 42, 46, 51, 52].

ESBL production was detected in 37.14% of UPEC isolates and was associated with marked co-resistance to cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones, leaving limited therapeutic options. Although more common among female patients, the difference was not statistically significant.

Consistent with regional reports, ESBL-producing UPEC are widely prevalent and show no significant association with patient gender, emphasizing their ongoing challenge to empirical UTI management and the need for continuous surveillance [3, 46, 51].

In a study from Egypt, Mohamed et al. [7], reported that 69.6% of UPEC isolates demonstrated an ESBL phenotype. Similarly, Muriuki et al. [62], documented a 24.2% ESBL rate among UPEC isolates in Kenya. In Nepal, Shrestha et al. [63] found that 82.1% of recovered UPEC strains produced ESBLs. Collectively, these findings underscore the growing burden of ESBL-producing UPEC and the importance of ongoing surveillance and evidence-based empirical therapy.

In the current study, phenotypic AmpC producers represented a small subset (11.43%) and showed no overlap with ESBL-producing strains, indicating distinct resistance mechanisms within the population. Interestingly, AmpC activity was identified not only in blaDHA-positive isolates but also in strains lacking any detectable BLGs, suggesting the involvement of uncharacterized or plasmid-independent AmpC determinants.

Sadeghi et al. reported ESBL production in 46% of UPEC isolates, whereas no AmpC producers were detected [64]. In a study conducted by Bahramian et al. [65] in Iran, the AmpC phenotype was identified in 61.7% of E. coli isolates causing UTIs. Maleki et al. found ESBL production in 62.5% of E. coli isolates, with 40% of ESBL-positive strains also exhibiting AmpC activity [66]. Qasemi et al. [67] from Iran, reported high extended-spectrum cephalosporin resistance among biofilm-forming UPEC isolates, with ESBL and AmpC phenotypes detected in 61% and 22% of resistant strains, respectively. Singh et al. reported phenotypic AmpC production in 84.61% of UPEC isolates [68]. Shahbazi et al. reported ESBL production in 67.5% of UPEC isolates, from Iran [69]. In a study from Iraq by Ganjo [70], Ganjo et al. reported ESBL and AmpC production in 48.6% and 38.6% of UPEC isolates, respectively, with 20% co-producing both enzymes. Carattoli et al. [71], reported that 17.2% of E. coli isolates from their study in Italy exhibited an ESBL-producing phenotype. Sharma et al. [72] conducted a survey in India, reporting that 56.92% of the E. coli isolates were ESBL-positive.

Molecular analysis identified blaIMP−2 as the predominant β-lactamase gene (16.19%), followed by blaDHA (10.48%) and blaVIM−1 (5.71%), whereas blaACC, blaCMY, and blaFOX were absent. Although most isolates lacked the investigated β-lactamase genes, MDR, XDR, and PDR phenotypes remained common, often accompanied by ESBL production. Notably, all blaDHA-positive isolates were MDR and exhibited AmpC activity, while blaIMP−2 and blaVIM genes were frequently associated with MDR/XDR phenotypes and ESBL production. Co-occurring gene patterns involving blaDHA were also linked to MDR/XDR isolates and AmpC expression. Ahmadi et al. from Iran [3], reported a high prevalence of MDR UPEC, with lower rates of XDR and PDR phenotypes. Although blaVIM and blaIMP genes were detected in 16.19% and 12.38% of isolates, β-lactamase gene patterns involving these determinants were uncommon [3].

In a study by Sadeghi et al. [64], none of the UPEC isolates carried blaDHA, blaACC and blaFOX.

Similarly, Shahabazi et al. [69] reported the absence of blaVIM and blaIMP among their UPEC strains. In contrast, Maleki et al. [66] found that 2.86% (2/70) and 4.29% (3/70) of ESBL-producing E. coli isolates harbored blaDHA−M and blaFOX−M genes, respectively.

Additionally, in a study from India, Gantasala et al. [73] identified E. coli as the most common uropathogen (65.9%), though none of the isolates possessed blaVIM or blaIMP.

In a study conducted by Gatya Al-Mayahie et al. [74] in Iraq, Gatya Al-Mayahie et al. reported that all B2 UPEC isolates were MDR, with no XDR or PDR strains detected. Among carbapenemase genes, blaVIM was identified in 10.5% of isolates, whereas blaIMP was absent [74].

Conclusions

UPEC isolates from patients with significant UTIs in Tehran, Iran, exhibited a high burden of antimicrobial resistance, with MDR, XDR, and PDR phenotypes identified in 55.24%, 16.19%, and 2.86% of isolates, respectively. ESBL and AmpC phenotypes were detected in 37.14% and 11.43% of isolates, while blaIMP−2, blaDHA, and blaVIM−1 were the most prevalent β-lactamase genes. Notably, most resistant isolates lacked the specific BLGs investigated in this study. However, because several clinically important ESBL and carbapenemase families were not examined, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of novel resistance mechanisms. Comprehensive molecular characterization is required to determine the full spectrum of resistance determinants present in these isolates. Complete resistance to ampicillin and amoxicillin was observed in all isolates. UPEC isolates were recovered most frequently from patients aged 71–80 years (24.77%), followed by those aged 41–50 years (17.14%) and 61–70 years (16.19%). Overall, the findings indicate substantial phenotypic and genetic heterogeneity of AMR feature among UPEC isolates in this setting. Interpretation should remain cautious because clinical information, symptom status, and patient-setting data were unavailable.

Limitations of the study

Due to institutional policies in certain hospitals, clinics, and laboratories, detailed patient information was not accessible to the authors. Thus, clinical signs and symptoms could not be evaluated or reported in association with the patients included in this study. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings obtained from the current study.

In the present study, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using 14 antibiotics representing a limited number of antimicrobial categories; thus, the term “PDR” can be recognized as “resistant to all antimicrobial agents tested” within the current study.

The molecular analysis was restricted to four acquired AmpC genes (blaACC, blaCMY, blaDHA, and blaFOX) and four MBL genes (blaIMP−1, blaIMP−2, blaVIM−1, and blaVIM−2). Other globally prevalent ESBL and carbapenemase determinants, including blaCTX−M, blaTEM, blaSHV, blaNDM, blaOXA−48, and blaKPC, were not investigated. Furthermore, ESBL production was confirmed only by phenotypic methods, and PCR amplicons were not verified by DNA sequencing. Additional phenotypic carbapenemase confirmation assays were also not performed. Consequently, the molecular epidemiology of β-lactam resistance in these isolates may not have been fully characterized.

Strength of the study

We successfully developed and characterized comprehensive BLG profiles, including ABLG and ACG patterns, alongside a broad spectrum of phenotypic AMR categories among clinical UPEC isolates. These analyses yield valuable epidemiological insights, supported by robust data and well-structured tables derived from isolates collected in Tehran, the capital of Iran.
