Soil acidification dismantles a citrulline-mediated microbe-metabolite-host defense axis in watermelon, exacerbating Fusarium wilt
Authors: Zewen Zhang, Leyu Yu, Chunlin Wu, Jiansheng Guo, Lin Zhu, Jianfei Wang, Cheng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil acidification disrupts the structure and function of soil microbiomes, resulting in increased vulnerability to soil-borne pathogens. While the link between soil acidification and disease susceptibility is well-established, the mechanisms underlying the suppression of plant defense remain poorly understood. In this study, we found that soil acidification perturbed the co-evolved assembly process of endophytic microbiomes in watermelon roots, leading to the collapse of a critical microbe–metabolite–host defense axis essential for resistance against Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. niveum (FON). Integrated field surveys and multi-omics analyses revealed that acidification-induced dysbiosis in the root endophytic microbiomes, characterized by the depletion of keystone Pseudomonas species (Pseudomonadaceae), strongly correlated with increased Fusarium wilt incidence. Central to this interaction was citrulline, a metabolite produced by root Pseudomonas endophytes that functioned as a symbiotic effector promoting bacterial colonization and a defense modulator inhibiting FON-induced oxidative burst. Disruption of citrulline biosynthesis abolished these protective effects, whereas exogenous citrulline application restored disease resistance. These findings underscored the role of root endophyte-derived citrulline in sustaining microbial fitness and plant defense, revealing a tripartite interaction impacted by soil acidification. Collectively, this study provides insights for developing microbiome-based strategies to enhance sustainable crop protection in degraded agroecosystems.Subject terms: Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Soil degradation poses an escalating global threat to plant health and agricultural productivity1,2. Among key drivers of this degradation, anthropogenic soil acidification resulting from intensive agricultural practices and atmospheric deposition has emerged as a critical destabilizing factor for terrestrial ecosystems. Concurrently, outbreaks of soil-borne pathogens like Fusarium spp. have been intensifying worldwide3–5. While native soil microbiomes confer disease suppression through competition, antibiosis, and induced systemic resistance (ISR)6–10, acidified soils exhibit increased susceptibility to soil-borne pathogens5. This suggests that soil acidification undermines the microbiome’s natural capacity for biocontrol. However, the mechanistic links connecting soil acidification, microbial dysbiosis, and the impairment of plant defense remain unresolved.

Mounting evidence indicates that soil acidification is correlated with increased prevalence and severity of soil-borne diseases such as Fusarium wilt5. Fundamentally, acidification reprograms the assembly and function of the root-associated microbiome—a critical niche governed by host selection and microbe-microbe interactions that are essential for modulating host defense11–15. This dysregulation likely disables synergistic plant defensive capacities by impairing both (i) intrinsic plant immunity and (ii) microbiome-mediated plant resistance (e.g., via ISR), thereby facilitating pathogen invasion16–18. Critically, despite the recognition of acidification-disease associations5, a significant knowledge gap persists: how does soil acidification specifically disrupt the assembly of root endophytic microbiomes and disable their functional interaction with core host defense pathways under pathogen pressure.

Natural soil microbiomes possess inherent resilience and suppressive capacity, which frequently operate through community-level modulation of host immunity19–21. However, translating individual antagonistic microbes into reliable field biocontrol agents has often proven challenging, largely due to ecological incompetence and context-dependency22–24. This underscores that sustainable disease management requires harnessing consortia-level functionality, particularly within the endosphere—a site shaped by host-microbe co-evolution that defines its assembly rules. Consequently, soil acidification disrupts these co-evolved assembly principles, driving root endophytic microbiomes into a state incapable of supporting effective immune surveillance or antagonism, ultimately disabling key microbiome-mediated defense outputs. While the suppressive effects of acidification on overall soil microbial activity are documented5,25,26, its specific consequences for the assembly and functions of root endophytic microbiomes remain poorly understood.

Watermelon is a globally important crop whose production is seriously threatened by Fusarium wilt, caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. niveum (FON), with its susceptibility sharply exacerbated in acidic soils27. This makes it an important model system to dissect the tripartite interaction between host, pathogen, and environment. We hypothesized that soil acidification compromises watermelon resistance to FON by reshaping the assembly of the root endophytic microbiome and disabling its functional dialogue with host immunity. To test this, we integrated field surveys across natural soil pH gradients with controlled greenhouse experiments. Employing a multi-omics approach (amplicon sequencing, metabolomics and transcriptomics), we investigated how soil acidification: (i) altered the assemblage of root endophytic microbiomes, (ii) reprogramed root metabolome governing host-pathogen interactions, and (iii) impaired the microbiome-mediated defense mechanisms to facilitate FON invasion (Fig. 1). Our findings established a framework linking soil acidification to the dysfunction of root endophytic microbiomes and the collapse of host defense. Crucially, we identified a keystone Pseudomonas endophyte and its secreted metabolite, citrulline, as a critical component of the defense axis, in which citrulline not only promoted microbial colonization but also mitigated the host’s oxidative burst induced by FON infection. Together, these findings revealed actionable targets for designing microbiome-based strategies to enhance crop resilience in acidified soils, offering promising approaches to improve agricultural sustainability under environmental stress.

Results

Soil acidification aggravated plant health and shifted root endophytic microbiomes

Geographically distributed sampling sites across 21 major watermelon production regions were investigated to evaluate the effects of soil acidification on plant health and root endophytic microbiomes. Bulk soil and root samples were collected from field quadrats for analyzing soil physicochemical characterization and root endophytic microbiomes, respectively (Fig. 2a). Analysis of key soil properties (e.g., NO3-, NH4+, SOC and pH) revealed significant correlations with plant health indicators (Fig. 2b). Spearman correlation identified soil pH as the dominant driver of plant performance, exhibiting a positive correlation with plant biomass (R = 0.91, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). However, soil pH values were negatively correlated with disease incidence (R = −0.94, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2d). To further investigate microbial responses to acidification, soil samples were categorized into three groups: strongly acidified (SS, pH < 5.5), acidic (AS, pH 5.5–6.5), and neutral (NS, pH > 6.5). Alpha diversity increased markedly with soil acidification, indicating that bacterial communities in both SS and AS groups exhibited greater bacterial diversity (Shannon index) and richness (Chao1 index) than those in the NS group (Fig. 2e, f). Furthermore, PCoA analysis showed distinct clustering of bacterial communities among the three soil categories, along the first principal coordinate (PCoA1) (Fig. 2g). Bray–Curtis dissimilarity analyses also confirmed significant community differentiation among the SS, AS, and NS groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2h).

Response of bacterial taxa to soil acidification

Soil acidification significantly altered the composition of root endophytic microbiomes across different soil acidification gradients (SS, AS, and NS) (Fig. 3a). At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Patescibacteria increased significantly with progressive acidification, whereas Proteobacteria declined as soil pH decreased. After filtering low-abundance ASVs with relative abundance <0.01% in all samples, Spearman’s correlation revealed 67 core bacterial ASVs that were significantly associated with both disease incidence and plant biomass (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Table 1). Seven ASVs were negatively associated with disease incidence but positively associated with plant biomass (Supplementary Table 2), suggesting potential beneficial roles.

Random Forest modeling selected 11 and 8 ASVs as key predictors for plant biomass and disease incidence, respectively (Fig. 3c, d). Pseudomonas ASV2 emerged as the top predictor for disease incidence, followed by Altererythrobacter ASV88, Dyadobacter ASV106, and unclassified Comamonadaceae ASV100. Notably, Pseudomonas ASV2 was also the strongest predictor for plant biomass among the 8 ASVs. ASV2, ASV16, and ASV53 were significant predictors in both models and selected for further analysis (Fig. 3e). The relative abundance of ASV2 was highest in the NS group, while ASV16 and ASV53 abundances were significantly increased in the SS group compared to the AS and NS groups (Fig. 3f).

Pseudomonadaceae as key indicator taxa responsive to soil acidification

To identify microbial taxa responsive to soil acidification, LEfSe analysis was performed, which revealed five bacterial families significantly enriched in the AS group: Micrococcaceae, Pseudonocardiaceae, Alicyclobacillaceae, Streptomycetaceae, and Rhizobiaceae (Fig. 4a). In contrast, three families, including Micrococcaceae, Weeksellaceae, and Pseudomonadaceae, were significantly associated with the NS group (Fig. 4b). Notably, Pseudomonadaceae and Micrococcaceae were identified as core biomarker families present in both soil types.

Differential abundance analysis at the ASV level revealed that Pseudomonas ASV2, classified within the family Pseudomonadaceae, was a dominant taxon in both the AS and NS groups (Fig. 4c, d). The abundance of Pseudomonas ASV2 was significantly greater in the NS group, as indicated by linear model analysis (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, the relative abundance of ASV2 was strongly positively correlated with increased soil pH values (R = 0.74, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4f). Additionally, ASV2, a top predictor in the Random Forest models, was negatively correlated with disease incidence (R = −0.81, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4g).

Isolation of core Pseudomonas isolates

To assess the beneficial potential of Pseudomonas isolates on watermelon plants, 187 bacterial strains were isolated from watermelon roots (Fig. 5a). Among these, strain ZW98 was genetically identified as Pseudomonas putida via 16S rRNA gene sequencing (Fig. 5b), which showed 100% nucleotide similarity with Pseudomonas ASV2. In dual-culture assays, P. putida ZW98 did not exhibit antagonistic activity against FON. The plant growth–promoting potential of ZW98 was further evaluated. Transparent zones formed around bacterial colonies, indicating the ability to solubilize phosphorus and potassium. Additionally, ZW98 exhibited a strong capacity for producing IAA (Fig. 5c).

To explore the mechanisms underlying ZW98-induced disease resistance, its complete genome was sequenced. The genome of P. putida ZW98 comprised a single circular chromosome (5,836,117 bp; GC content: 62.47%), including 72 tRNAs, 22 rRNAs, 95 noncoding RNAs, and 5343 protein-coding sequences (Fig. 5d). Functional annotation via KEGG pathways revealed significant enrichment in amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, and energy metabolism (Fig. 5e). Notably, the genome contained multiple gene clusters associated with host-microbe interactions, including bacterial secretion systems, chemotaxis machinery and biofilm formation pathways. These systems are known to modulate host metabolic reprogramming and defense responses, suggesting a multifaceted mechanism for the biocontrol activity of ZW98. To further validate bacterial colonization, watermelon roots were inoculated with GFP-tagged ZW98 cells. Laser scanning confocal microscopy confirmed robust colonization, as indicated by intense fluorescent signals within root tissues (Fig. 5f).

Functional characterization of P. putida ZW98

To determine whether P. putida ZW98 contributed to plant growth promotion and disease resistance, watermelon plants were cultivated in sterile soils under greenhouse conditions with contrasting pH regimes (Fig. 6a, b). Under acidic conditions, shoot and root fresh weights were significantly reduced compared with those in neutral conditions. Remarkably, inoculation with ZW98 significantly increased plant height and shoot and root fresh weights, particularly under acidic conditions (Fig. 6c–e). The effects of ZW98 inoculation on plant disease resistance were also assessed (Fig. 6f). Compared with plants inoculated with FON alone, co-inoculation with ZW98 and FON enhanced disease resistance, as reflected by lower disease index (Fig. 6g, h). The biocontrol efficacy of ZW98 reached 52.8% (Fig. 6i). Collectively, ZW98 inoculation not only promoted plant growth but also enhanced Fusarium wilt resistance.

Next, metabolic profiles of the control and ZW98-treated roots were analyzed under acidic conditions (Fig. 7a). PLS-DA revealed that ZW98 inoculation significantly altered the metabolic profiles of watermelon roots (Fig. 7b). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that differentially accumulated metabolites were associated with arginine biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 7c). Notably, roots of ZW98-inoculated plants accumulated markedly higher levels of ornithine, citrulline, and argininosuccinate—key intermediates in the arginine biosynthesis pathway (Fig. 7d, e). To further evaluate the disease-suppressive potential of these metabolites, pot experiments were conducted in which ornithine, citrulline, or argininosuccinate was applied to watermelon plants prior to FON challenge under acidic soil conditions (Fig. 7f). Among these metabolites, only citrulline amendment significantly alleviated Fusarium wilt disease (Fig. 7g, h). However, treatment with ornithine, citrulline, or argininosuccinate did not reduce FON abundance in the soil (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, metabolomic analysis of ZW98 cell-free supernatant (CFS) identified 75 enriched metabolites compared to non-inoculated controls. Citrulline was among the top 20 enriched metabolites, showing a 132-fold accumulation in ZW98 CFS (Supplementary Table 3). Additionally, the root colonizing capacity of ZW98 under acidic and neutral conditions was quantified. While this strain successfully colonized watermelon roots under both conditions, its abundance was significantly lower in acidic soils than in neutral soils (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Consistently, ZW98 inoculation elevated root citrulline levels under both pH regimes compared to non-inoculated controls, with higher accumulation under neutral conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

Citrulline enhanced bacterial colonization and plant disease resistance by attenuating ROS burst

The elevated accumulation of citrulline in the ZW98-inoculated roots prompted an investigation into its role in enhancing plant resistance against FON. A deletion mutant (Δadi) was generated to disrupt the arginine deiminase pathway, which was essential for citrulline biosynthesis. Under acidic conditions, the Δadi mutant failed to protect watermelon plants against FON. This protective effect was fully restored in the genetically complemented strain (CΔadi) (Fig. 8a, b). Consistently, under FON treatment, root citrulline levels in the Δadi-inoculated plants were not significantly different from those in the FON-only control, whereas inoculation with the complemented strain CΔadi resulted in a significant increase in root citrulline accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 2c). To eliminate the possibility of pleiotropic growth defects, in vitro growth profiles of the Δadi mutant were examined. Compared to wild-type ZW98, the Δadi mutant exhibited a modest delay in growth (Supplementary Fig. 3). We next examined whether citrulline contributed to the establishment of plant-bacteria symbiosis. In hydroponic assays, the Δadi mutant showed severely impaired root colonization, whereas the CΔadi strain restored root colonization to wild-type levels. Furthermore, exogenous citrulline supplementation rescued the colonization defect of the Δadi mutant (Fig. 8c).

To further explore the mechanism underlying ZW98-mediated disease suppression, we conducted comparative transcriptomic profiling of watermelon roots (Fig. 8d). A total of 1750 genes were differentially expressed in plants co-inoculated with ZW98 and FON compared to plants treated only with FON. Among these, 1048 genes were down-regulated, and 702 were up-regulated (Fig. 8e). GO enrichment analysis revealed a significant overrepresentation of oxidation–reduction processes among the down-regulated DEGs. The top enriched GO terms included oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016491), antioxidant activity (GO:0016209), and peroxidase activity (GO:0004601) (Fig. 8f, g), suggesting that ZW98 inoculation alleviated FON-induced oxidative stress. Histochemical staining with DAB and NBT further confirmed a strong ROS burst in the FON-infected roots. This burst was largely attenuated by co-inoculation with wild-type ZW98. In contrast, the Δadi mutant failed to suppress ROS burst. Notably, treatment with 0.5 mM citrulline phenocopied the ROS-suppressive effect of wild-type ZW98. In addition, trypan blue staining showed that both ZW98 inoculation and exogenous citrulline markedly reduced the FON-induced cell death (Fig. 8h).

Discussion

Soil acidification, driven primarily by intensive fertilization and industrial emissions, poses a significant threat to agricultural sustainability3–5. Here, we uncovered a previously unrecognized link between soil acidification, root endophytic microbiomes, and plant defense, using watermelon-Fusarium as a model system. By integrating multi-omics (amplicon sequencing, metabolomics, transcriptomics) with functional validation, we demonstrated that soil acidification disrupted a co-evolved microbe-metabolite-host defense axis. Specifically, root Pseudomonas endophytes secreted citrulline to orchestrate host ROS homeostasis and suppress pathogen invasion. This work advanced ecological and molecular understanding of how abiotic stress reshaped plant-microbe interactions, with implications for sustainable agriculture in acidified soils.

Field surveys across major watermelon cultivation regions identified soil pH as the dominant factor governing Fusarium wilt susceptibility, outweighing influences from nutrient availability or organic matter content. This observation aligns with previous reports that soil acidification compromises the indigenous microbiome’s ability to suppress pathogens, due to reduced antagonistic activity and altered community assembly5. Our results revealed that soil acidification triggered profound shifts in the root endophytic microbiomes, characterized by a marked decline in beneficial taxa and the establishment of a dysbiotic state favoring pathogen proliferation. This pattern mirrors observations across global agroecosystems, where soil acidification impairs suppressive functions due to disrupted ecological networks and loss of key antagonistic taxa5,28–30. Within the watermelon root endosphere, soil acidification drove a dramatic taxonomic restructuring, notably a pronounced depletion of Pseudomonadaceae, which were identified as biomarkers of healthy soils. This decline correlated strongly with decreasing soil pH, reflecting a broader trend in which low pH disrupted host-mediated recruitment of beneficial symbionts, favoring functionally taxa. Notably, the progressive loss of a key Pseudomonas ASV2 (identified as P. putida ZW98) emerged as a critical determinant of plant performance, with random forest modeling ranking this strain as the strongest predictor of both disease incidence and plant biomass. As a keystone taxon, its depletion disrupted the evolved assembly rules governing host–microbe defensive symbiosis, highlighting the vulnerability of microbiome-mediated plant defense under abiotic perturbation31–34.

Functional characterization of P. putida ZW98 revealed a sophisticated defense strategy operating independently of direct antagonism toward the Fusarium pathogen. Genomic analysis identified a repertoire of host-adaptation genes, including type VI secretion systems, chemotaxis sensors, and biofilm regulators, which positioned ZW98 as a highly effective root-colonizing bacterium. Metabolomic profiling of watermelon roots colonized by ZW98 under acidic conditions showed a marked accumulation of citrulline. This accumulation was further supported by the detection of actively secreted citrulline in bacterial cell-free supernatant, in which its levels were enriched 132-fold. Although plants can synthesize citrulline endogenously35, inoculation with the Δadi mutant, which was impaired in citrulline biosynthesis, failed to increase root citrulline levels and did not confer disease protection. These results indicated that bacterially derived citrulline was essential for mediating plant defense responses. Citrulline, a non-proteinogenic amino acid, is increasingly recognized for its role in plant stress signaling35,36. Inoculation with ZW98 significantly increased citrulline accumulation in watermelon roots, which contributed to enhancing plant resistance against FON. These results established citrulline as a key signaling molecule in enhancing plant disease resistance, corroborating recent evidence that microbial metabolites play an important role in orchestrating host defense responses37–39. Intriguingly, citrulline also functions as a colonization cue: the Δadi mutant of ZW98 exhibited severely defective root colonization and biocontrol activity, both of which were restored by exogenous citrulline supplementation. This dual role identified citrulline as a symbiotic effector mediating both microbe-host communication and disease suppression.

It is well established that oxidative burst often confers plant disease resistance during plant–biotrophic pathogen interactions by triggering a hypersensitive response. However, excessive ROS-induced cell death promotes susceptibility to necrotrophic pathogens, as it facilitates host colonization40–43. Necrotrophic pathogens exploit the host oxidative burst as a tactic to access nutrients from dead tissues, thereby augmenting their pathogenicity44. In this study, inoculation with ZW98 attenuated FON-induced oxidative stress pathways in watermelon roots, whereas the Δadi mutant of ZW98 failed to suppress pathogenic ROS burst. Central to this dynamic is citrulline, which acts as a suppressor of the ROS burst45–48. This ROS-modulating capacity reveals a critical ecological-evolutionary trade-off: Fusarium exploits host ROS burst to achieve effective infection, while excessive ROS accumulation inhibits beneficial microbial colonization and causes host cellular damage40–44. By scavenging ROS, citrulline simultaneously created a favorable niche for ZW98 and deprived FON of a vital virulence signal, thereby effectively enhancing plant defense. This dual action explained why exogenous citrulline conferred FON resistance despite lacking direct antifungal activity, highlighting cellular ROS homeostasis as a cornerstone of host protection. Comparative evidence from other pathosystems, such as ROS detoxification in wheat resisting Fusarium crown rot, suggests that ROS scavenging is a conserved defense strategy across plant species facing oxidative stress under environmental challenges43,44. In acidified soils, the collapse of citrulline-mediated ROS buffering creates a permissive environment for FON infection, exacerbating disease susceptibility through cascading failure of microbial niche stability and host redox regulation. This mechanistic insight emphasized the intricate interplay between abiotic and biotic interactions, illustrating how soil pH acted as a master regulator of plant-pathogen dynamics.

In conclusion, this work demonstrated that acidic soil compromised plant defense by disrupting a citrulline-dependent dialogue between the host and its keystone Pseudomonas endophytes. The underlying mechanism relied on bacterial secretion of citrulline, which served the dual function of promoting microbial colonization and quenching the host’s pathogenic oxidative burst, thereby conferring disease resistance (Fig. 9). Citrulline thus emerged not merely as a metabolic intermediary, but as a pivotal mediator maintaining tripartite equilibrium in the root endosphere. Targeting this citrulline-ROS axis presents a promising pathway to enhance crop resilience in increasingly acidified and pathogen-challenged agroecosystems.

Disruption of the citrulline-dependent dialogue between P. putida ZW98 and watermelon roots compromises plant defense.

In neutral soils, the keystone endophyte P. putida ZW98 effectively colonized the roots and secreted citrulline. This citrulline-dependent signaling suppressed the FON-induced ROS burst in the host, thereby enhancing plant defense and restricting disease progression. In contrast, in acidified soils, the disruption of the citrulline-mediated signaling led to an exaggerated ROS burst, resulting in cell death and severe Fusarium wilt symptoms.

Methods

Field sampling and site characterization

Twenty-one crop fields were sampled across Anhui province in China (29.45–34.00°N, 116.10–119.30°E; Supplementary Table 4). Watermelon plants (Citrullus lanatus L.) were widely planted across these soil regions. These sites encompassed a diverse range of soil pH gradients influenced by regional agricultural practices and environmental factors. Fields were planted in early April and sampled in late May 2024, shortly before sprout of wilt disease. At each site, three replicate quadrats (each 10 m × 10 m) were randomly established within the field. Disease incidence was assessed by surveying 150 plants per quadrat and calculating the proportion showing wilt symptoms, including leaf yellowing, chlorosis, vascular discoloration, and plant wilting.

For soil sampling, five cores (5 cm diameter, 20 cm depth) were extracted from each quadrat and pooled to form a composite sample per quadrat, yielding three composite soil samples per field. Soil samples were sieved through a 2-mm sieve to remove debris. Each composite sample was divided: one portion was air-dried and sieved for the analysis of physicochemical properties49. The remaining aliquot was kept at 4 °C for subsequent pot experiments. Concurrently, root samples were collected from five representative plants per quadrat using a five-point sampling grid. Root samples from each quadrat were pooled to generate one composite root sample per quadrat, resulting in three composite root samples per field (totaling 63 composite root samples across all fields). A total of 315 individual plants (5 plants × 3 quadrats × 21 fields) contributed to these composites. The roots were transported on ice to the laboratory, then immersed in sterile distilled water in 50-mL centrifuge tubes and shaken at 200 rpm for 15 min to dislodge adhering rhizosphere soil50. For surface sterilization, roots were immersed in 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite for 10 min and then rinsed three times with sterile distilled water51. Sterility was verified by plating the final rinse water on nutrient agar and confirming no growth after 48 h at 28 °C. Root samples were stored at −80 °C until DNA extraction for microbiome analysis.

DNA extraction, sequencing analysis and real-time qPCR

DNA extraction from root samples was performed with the FastDNA™ Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, USA). The V4–V5 segment of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 338 F and 806 R and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA)49. Paired-end reads were merged using USEARCH. High-quality sequences were filtered with the “fastq_filter” command, followed by dereplication. Singletons were removed with the USEARCH-unoise3 algorithm, and chimeras were filtered using the “uchime_ref” command. The cleaned reads were then used to build an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table, and taxonomy was assigned by the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) classifier.

The abundance of FON and ZW98 was quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) using specific primer sets ITS1-F/AFR308R and rpoD-F/rpoD-R, respectively50,51. qPCR reactions (20 µL) contained 10 µL of 2 × T5 Fast qPCR Mix (SYBR Green I; TSE20, Tsingke, Beijing, China), 1 µL of each primer (10 µmol L⁻¹), 1 µL template DNA, and 7 µL sterile water. Thermal cycling began with 95 °C for 5 min, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 20 s and 72 °C for 20 s.

Isolation and characterization of bacterial strains

Root samples were subjected to a rigorous surface sterilization protocol modified from Qiao et al.50. Fresh root segments were immersed in 5% NaClO and shaken for 15 min at 200 rpm. Surface-sterilized roots were sectioned into 1-cm fragments and ground in 5 mL of sterile 0.9% NaCl solution. The resultant homogenate was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min to pellet debris. The supernatant was serially diluted and cultured on R2A, NA, or LB medium. Bacterial colonies were purified and identified by16S rRNA gene amplification with universal primers 27 F and 1492 R52.

The antagonistic activity of bacterial isolates against FON was evaluated using in vitro dual-culture assays on potato dextrose agar (PDA). Plates were incubated at 28 °C in the dark until FON mycelia exhibited extensive growth over the PDA surface. Additionally, selective media were employed to screen for plant growth-promoting traits, including phosphate and potassium solubilization53. For these assays, bacterial isolates were grown in LB medium at 28 °C for 36 h. Subsequently, 2 µL of each culture (adjusted to an OD600 of 0.5) was spotted onto the selective agar plates. The ability of phosphate and potassium solubilization was determined based on the formation of transparent zones surrounding colonies after 7 days of incubation at 28 °C. Furthermore, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production was analyzed using the Salkowski colorimetric method54. Briefly, bacterial isolates were grown in LB medium supplemented with 100 mg L⁻¹ L-tryptophan at 28 °C for 24 h. The supernatants were mixed 1:1 (v/v) with Salkowski reagent and incubated in the dark for 30 min, and the appearance of a pink color indicated IAA production.

Genome sequencing and annotation of P. putida ZW98

Genomic DNA from P. putida ZW98 was extracted with lysozyme pretreatment (20 mg mL−1). Long-read sequencing was performed using PacBio Sequel II, generating average read lengths >10 kb. Raw data quality met the following thresholds: PacBio: Q20 for 95% of bases; Illumina: Q30 for 90% of bases. Hybrid assembly was performed using Unicycler (v0.5.0) with default parameters and polished with Pilon (v1.24). Genome annotation was conducted using Prokka (1.14.6)55. Functional categorization and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were performed using via eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.6) and GhostKOALA (v2.2), respectively56. A circular genome map was generated with shinyCircos-V2.057.

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was isolated from root tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Libraries were prepared and sequenced according to Illumina’s standard procedures. Raw reads were quality-filtered to remove adapters, low-quality sequences, and contaminants. Clean reads were aligned to the watermelon “97103” reference genome with HISAT2 (http://cucurbitgenomics.org), and unigenes were annotated against the Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG databases.

Differential expression analysis was conducted with DESeq2 (v1.30.1) based on transcript quantification by RSEM (v1.3.3). DEGs were defined as those showing a 2-fold change (|log2 fold change | ≥1) and an adjusted P-value < 0.0558. Functional enrichment analyses were performed to determine DEGs significantly enriched (adjusted P-value < 0.05) in GO terms or KEGG pathways relative to the whole-transcriptome background. GO enrichment was implemented using GOATOOLS (v1.0.0), and KEGG pathway enrichment was conducted with the Python SciPy library (v1.7.3)59.

Metabolomic analysis and quantification of citrulline

For extracting bacterial metabolites, the supernatant was mixed with two volumes of extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile, 1:1, v/v). The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected, incubated at −20 °C for 1 h to precipitate proteins, and centrifuged again at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. This supernatant was vacuum-evaporated to dryness, reconstituted in 100 µL of 30% (v/v) acetonitrile, vortexed for 60 s, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. For root metabolites, fresh root samples (100 mg) were homogenized and extracted with 1.0 mL of solvent (water:acetonitrile:isopropanol, 1:1:1, v/v/v) at a 1:3 (w/v) ratio. The mixture was vortexed for 60 s, ultrasonicated at 4 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and incubated at −20 °C for 1 h to precipitate proteins. The resulting supernatant was dried, reconstituted in 200 µL of 30% (v/v) acetonitrile. The final supernatant was subjected to liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis60.

Metabolites were identified by comparing accurate mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) values and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra with an in-house database of authentic standards. Variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were calculated to evaluate each metabolite’s contribution to group discrimination. Significant differences between different treatments were assessed using Student’s t-test, with differentially abundant metabolites defined by VIP > 1.0 and P < 0.0561. In addition, citrulline was quantified following a previously published procedure with minor adjustments62. Approximately 50 mg of lyophilized root tissue was homogenized, resuspended in ice-cold 20 mM HCl, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered, derivatized using an AccQ-Fluor reagent kit (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA), and analyzed by UPLC-ESI-MS/MS on a Waters Acquity/Xevo TQ system. The mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile, and detection was performed in positive-ion MRM mode. Data acquisition and processing were carried out with MassLynx software.

ROS and cell death staining

In hydroponic systems, 2-week-old watermelon plants were transferred to 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution (pH 5.0, adjusted with 0.1 M HCl) with or without FON conidia at a final concentration of 106 spores mL-1 and/or ZW98 cells at 5 × 107 CFU mL−1. The nutrient solutions were replaced every other day. Histochemical staining for ROS was conducted on watermelon roots 3 days post-inoculation with FON and/or P. putida ZW98. For superoxide anion (O₂⁻) visualization, root tips were excised and immersed in 0.5 mM nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) in 10 mM phosphate buffered saline (pH 6.0). H₂O₂ accumulation was detected using 1 mg mL⁻¹ 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB; pH 3.8)63. Stained roots were cleared in 95% (v/v) ethanol at 37 °C for 12 h and imaged. In addition, cell death was assessed using trypan blue staining. Root samples were immersed in 0.08% (w/v) trypan blue prepared in lactic acid–phenol–water (1:1:1, v/v/v), and subsequently destained in 2.5 g mL⁻¹ chloral hydrate solution for 30 min. Stained roots were mounted in 50% glycerol and imaged64.

Construction of GFP-tagged and ADI-deficient P. putida

putida ZW98 was fluorescently labeled by electroporation with pBBR1MCS2-GFP. For root colonization assays, inoculated roots were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope. The arginine deiminase (ADI) knockout mutant (Δadi) was generated in ZW98 by allelic replacement using the suicide vector pEX18Gm. Briefly, homologous fragments flanking the adi gene were amplified by PCR and ligated into pEX18Gm. The resulting construct was transformed into Escherichia coli S17-1 λpir and then mobilized into ZW98. For genetic complementation, the adi coding region, along with its promoter region, was amplified and cloned into pBBR1MCS-5. The constructed plasmid was electroporated into the Δadi mutant, and transformants were selected on LB agar with kanamycin (50 μg mL⁻¹). Bacterial growth was evaluated in LB medium at 30 °C, and optical density at 600 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Primers used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 5.

Effects of P. putida ZW98 on wilt disease resistance

putida ZW98 was cultured overnight in LB medium at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. The cells were then pelleted by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and resuspended in sterile water. Seeds of watermelon (Citrullus lanatus L. cv. “Zaojia 8424”) were surface-sterilized by immersion in 1% (v/v) NaClO for 10 min, followed by three rinses in sterile distilled water. The seeds were germinated on moist sterile filter paper at 25 °C in the dark for 3 days, after which uniform seedlings were selected for experiments.

To evaluate the effects of ZW98 on plant growth and disease resistance, FON conidia at a final concentration of 106 spores g−1 soil and/or ZW98 cells at 5 × 107 CFU g−1 soil were incorporated into sterilized soils. Two-week-old seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots containing the treated soils with 15 replicates per treatment. The plants were maintained in a controlled growth chamber at 28 °C under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod and 70% relative humidity. After 3 weeks, disease severity was assessed using a modified 5-point scale. The disease index (DI) was calculated as [Σ (score × number of plants with that score)]/(5 × total number of plants) × 100. Disease control efficacy was determined as [(DI of pathogen-only control−DI of treatment)/DI of pathogen-only control] × 10050.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using R (v4.3.1) and IBM SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corporation, New York, USA), with a significance level of P < 0.05. Group comparisons were performed by Tukey’s HSD test with one-way ANOVA. Spearman’s rank correlations were evaluated associations (|ρ | >0.6, P < 0.05). Beta-diversity was analyzed by PCoA on Bray–Curtis distances and PERMANOVA. Biomarker taxa were identified using LEfSe (LDA > 2.0, P < 0.05)65. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using MEGA11.0. Random Forest regression ranked predictors by mean decrease in accuracy (randomForest package, 10-fold cross-validation)66. Differential expression was analyzed using DESeq2 (|log₂FC | ≥1.0, P < 0.05).
