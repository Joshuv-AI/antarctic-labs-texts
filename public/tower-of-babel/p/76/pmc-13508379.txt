A qualitative exploration of occupational influences on hydration, urination habits, food patterns, and self-care among patients with urolithiasis
Authors: Akash Nixon, Victor Rakesh Lazar, Karan Sekar, Arpith Penchatholi, Saikiran Pendem, Senthil Kumar Aiyappan, Harini Citheri Rajendran, Sandhiya Chinnakutti, Ananda Kumar Ilangovan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUrolithiasis prevention depends on sustained fluid intake, timely urination, and appropriate dietary and lifestyle practices. However, occupational routines may make these behaviors difficult to maintain. This study explored participants’ perceptions of how occupational routines relate to stone-preventive self-care among individuals with CT-confirmed urolithiasis.MethodsAn observational qualitative exploratory study was conducted at a tertiary care teaching hospital in South India. Adults with CT-confirmed urolithiasis and at least one calculus measuring 3 mm or more were recruited using maximum-variation purposive sampling across physically demanding or heat-exposed, sedentary or professional, travel-based or mobile, and shift-based or irregular work contexts. Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted in Tamil between February and July 2025. Contemporaneous interview notes were expanded after each interview, translated into English, and analysed using thematic analysis. Clinical and CT-related variables were summarized descriptively to characterize the sample.ResultsTwenty-four of 32 approached participants were included. The median maximum stone diameter was 7 mm (interquartile range, 5–10 mm), 15 participants had hydronephrosis and/or obstructive features, and 9 had recurrent stone disease. Six themes were identified: occupationally shaped inadequate hydration; restricted or delayed urination in relation to work setting; disruption of meal timing and food quality; occupational absorption and neglect of self-care; schedule instability, travel, and disruption of daily routines; and stone disease understood as multifactorial, with occupation interacting with other perceived contributors. Across themes, participants described three interconnected pathways through which work routines could make preventive self-care difficult to sustain: infrastructural and access constraints, schedule instability and routine disruption, and cognitive-attentional absorption. Family history, dietary and lifestyle practices, supplements, smoking, alcohol use, and comorbidities were also described as contextual contributors.ConclusionsOccupational routines may influence the feasibility of maintaining stone-preventive self-care among individuals with urolithiasis. The findings support occupation-sensitive counselling and practical workplace strategies that consider water and toilet access, break opportunities, travel demands, shift work, and workload. Longitudinal and implementation studies should assess whether such approaches improve preventive behaviours and stone-related outcomes.Keywords: Urolithiasis, Kidney stones, Occupational health, Hydration, Urination, Self-care, Qualitative research, Stone prevention

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a common and recurrent urological condition associated with substantial clinical, economic, and quality-of-life burdens worldwide [1–3]. Global burden data indicate that stone disease continues to contribute considerably to incident cases, disability, and healthcare utilization across diverse populations [1]. Long-term prevention is therefore important because recurrence and modifiable lifestyle-related factors remain central to patient care [2, 3].

Stone formation is multifactorial; however, low urine volume is one of the most important modifiable contributors to recurrence. Current guidance emphasizes adequate fluid intake and maintenance of sufficient urine output as key preventive strategies [4–6]. Dietary measures, including moderation of sodium and excessive animal-protein intake, maintenance of normal dietary calcium intake, and broader nutritional assessment, are also important components of prevention [5–8].

Occupation may influence the everyday routines through which preventive practices are maintained. Heat exposure, physical exertion, sweating, limited access to drinking water, prolonged travel, restricted breaks, and difficulty accessing toilets may make regular hydration and timely urination challenging for some workers [9–14]. Occupational nephrolithiasis and heat-stress research has particularly highlighted dehydration and heat exposure among workers in physically demanding or hot environments [9–13]. Comparable work-related barriers may also arise outside heat-exposed occupations but remain less well described [14].

Workplace organization may further influence voiding behavior, meal timing, sleep, and other routines relevant to stone prevention. Occupational bladder-health research has shown that time pressure, limited restroom access, workplace norms, and discomfort using public facilities can influence delayed voiding and toileting behavior [15–17]. Although this evidence is not specific to urolithiasis, it demonstrates how workplace structure can shape behaviors relevant to hydration and urination. Similarly, dietary and lifestyle-related risk factors may be influenced by irregular schedules, fatigue, travel, and limited opportunities for planned meals or rest [7, 8, 18].

Previous qualitative studies of stone disease have mainly explored pain, illness experience, quality of life, patient-reported outcomes, and dietary changes after diagnosis [19–22]. However, less is known about how occupational routines shape hydration, urination habits, food patterns, sleep, and self-care among individuals with CT-confirmed urolithiasis. This observational qualitative exploratory study addressed that gap by examining participant-described experiences and perceived pathways linking occupational routines with stone-preventive behaviors and self-management.

Methods

Study design, setting, and reporting

This observational qualitative exploratory study used semi-structured interviews to explore participants’ perceptions of occupational routines and stone-preventive self-care.

The study was conducted in the Department of Radio-Diagnosis at a tertiary care teaching hospital in South India. Participants were approached after CT confirmation of urolithiasis and availability of the radiology report. CT was performed as part of routine clinical evaluation for suspected urolithiasis or follow-up of known stone disease.

During study planning, a urologist with 8 years of experience in managing urolithiasis provided clinical guidance. This input informed the eligibility criteria, interview domains, and stone-related variables included in the study. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [23].

Participants and recruitment

Participants were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had CT-confirmed urolithiasis with at least one calculus measuring 3 mm or more in maximum diameter, could communicate in Tamil, and provided written informed consent. Individuals without CT-confirmed urolithiasis, those with isolated microlithiasis or tiny punctate calculi measuring less than 3 mm, those who were too unwell or distressed to participate meaningfully, those unable to communicate adequately in Tamil, and those who declined participation were excluded [24, 25].

Microlithiasis has been described as a possible early or minimal stage of calculus formation [24]. These cases were excluded to focus on participants with clinically evident stone disease and to reduce heterogeneity in symptom burden, illness perception, and participant reflections.

Maximum-variation purposive sampling was used to recruit participants across four broad occupational categories: physically demanding or heat-exposed, sedentary or professional, travel-based or mobile, and shift-based or irregular occupations [26]. Occupational grouping was based on the dominant work pattern described during screening and confirmed during the interview, rather than job title alone. These categories were used to obtain variation in occupational routines and were not intended for statistical comparison between occupational groups.

Data were collected between 23 February and 13 July 2025. Of the 32 patients approached, 24 provided written informed consent and were included in the analysis. As this was an exploratory qualitative study, no predetermined numerical sample size was set. Sample adequacy was guided by the concept of information power, considering the focused study aim, participants’ direct experience of CT-confirmed urolithiasis, and variation across relevant occupational contexts [27]. Recruitment ended after 24 interviews because subsequent interviews largely reinforced the work-related patterns already identified and did not add substantively new occupational pathways relevant to the study aim.

Interview guide, data collection, and reflexivity

The semi-structured interview guide was developed from the study objectives, relevant literature on occupational nephrolithiasis, and commonly reported behavioral factors. It covered work routine, access to water and toilets, hydration behavior, urination patterns, urine holding, meal timing, food quality, sleep, fatigue, self-care, travel, shift work, and participants’ perceptions of contributing factors. The guide was reviewed by the research team for relevance, clarity, and clinical appropriateness. Minor adjustments to probing and question sequence were made during the early interviews without changing the core domains. The interview guide is presented in Table 1.

Semi-structured interview guide used for data collection

Face-to-face semi-structured interviews were conducted in Tamil by one primary interviewer to maintain consistency in rapport building, question delivery, probing, and documentation. Each interview lasted approximately 15–20 min. Interviews began with a warm-up question and then used open-ended questions and probes to explore participants’ work routines and relevant health practices.

Open-ended questioning encouraged participants to discuss both work-related and non-work-related contributors to stone disease. Family history, diet, supplements, smoking, alcohol use, physical activity, comorbidities, and other lifestyle factors were also explored.

The primary interviewer had a radiology background, and participants were recruited in the Department of Radio-Diagnosis. This professional background and recruitment setting were considered during data collection and interpretation. To address potential influence, occupational and non-occupational contributors were actively explored, and developing interpretations were discussed within the research team during analysis.

Data management and analysis

Interviews were documented through contemporaneous manual note-taking rather than audio recording to minimize disruption to routine post-CT clinical workflow and reduce participant burden when discussing personal habits. Key responses, contextual details, and relevant phrases were documented during the interviews. Notes were expanded immediately after each interview and organized using Obsidian version 1.7. These expanded notes, rather than verbatim transcripts, formed the dataset for analysis.

Expanded Tamil notes were translated into English for analysis and reporting. The English translations were checked against the original Tamil notes by Tamil-speaking members of the research team to preserve intended meaning. Participants were assigned de-identified study codes from P1 to P24, and no personally identifiable information was included in the analysis or reporting.

CT reports were reviewed to record maximum stone diameter, laterality, stone number, stone distribution, and hydronephrosis and/or obstructive features. The maximum stone diameter reported on CT was used for size classification. Laterality was recorded as right-sided, left-sided, or bilateral, and stone number was categorized as single or multiple. Stone distribution was classified at the participant level as calyceal location only, renal pelvic location only, ureteric location only, multiple renal locations without ureteric involvement, or combined renal and ureteric locations. Ureteric locations included upper, mid, distal, and vesicoureteral-junction stones.

Information on first-episode or recurrent stone disease and comorbidities was obtained from available clinical records and supplemented, where necessary, by participant-reported information. Clinical and CT-related variables were summarized descriptively to characterize the sample and provide context for the qualitative findings.

Expanded interview notes were reviewed repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data, followed by thematic analysis [28]. Meaningful segments were coded to identify participant-described work-related practices, barriers, self-care behaviors, and perceived contributors to stone disease. Similar codes were grouped into provisional categories and compared across participant accounts and occupational contexts to identify recurring patterns and meaningful variation.

Although the interview guide provided the main areas for discussion, the thematic structure was developed iteratively through repeated reading of the notes, comparison of participant accounts, and interpretation of participant experiences. The analysis therefore combined predefined interview domains with primarily inductive theme development [28, 29].

Developing codes, categories, and themes were reviewed and discussed within the research team until a shared interpretation was reached. The clinical plausibility of the final interpretations was considered with input from the study urologist.

Recurrent themes

Participants’ accounts were organized into six themes: (1) occupationally shaped inadequate hydration, (2) restricted or delayed urination in relation to work setting, (3) disruption of meal timing and food quality, (4) occupational absorption and neglect of self-care, (5) schedule instability, travel, and disruption of daily routines, and (6) stone disease understood as multifactorial, with occupation interacting with other perceived contributors.

Participants could contribute to more than one theme because different health practices were often interconnected within the same daily routine.

Occupationally shaped inadequate hydration

Across the dataset, hydration was rarely described as a planned or consistently maintained health behavior. Participants commonly reported drinking water reactively, in response to thirst, fatigue, discomfort, heat, or situational access, rather than following a regular preventive routine.

Physically demanding and heat-exposed workers commonly linked inadequate hydration to sweating, outdoor work, and physical exertion. Participants such as P1, P5, and P16 described substantial physical demands but inconsistent water intake. Participants who worked across multiple locations, including P10, P20, and P23, described irregular access to water during the workday. In sedentary and professional roles, participants such as P4, P14, and P19 described irregular water intake despite water being available.

A few participants perceived that intensive exercise, protein supplementation, or higher protein intake increased their hydration requirements. P2 and P18 described their fluid intake as inadequate in relation to these lifestyle demands.

Restricted or delayed urination in relation to work setting

Many participants described difficulty maintaining regular urination patterns because of work-related barriers. These included limited restroom access, inability to leave work tasks, discomfort using available facilities, prolonged travel, and postponement of urination because of work demands.

Among field-based and outdoor workers, delayed urination was commonly linked to poor restroom access, privacy concerns, or discomfort using unfamiliar facilities. Participants such as P5, P7, and P16 described these barriers. In travel-heavy occupations, including P10, P20, and P23, urination was often postponed because frequent stopping was impractical or because workflow could not be interrupted easily.

Participants in sedentary and professional roles described a different pattern. P9, P14, and P19 associated delayed urination with busyness, responsibility, or discomfort using public restrooms, even when facilities were available. Some participants linked altered voiding patterns with burning sensation, hematuria, or discomfort.

Disruption of meal timing and food quality

Meal-related disruption was less consistent than hydration or urination difficulties, but several participants described delayed meals, skipped meals, snack substitution, outside food, or irregular eating patterns.

Participants in desk-based or irregularly scheduled work, such as P4, P13, and P17, described postponing meals, replacing regular meals with snacks, or eating unpredictably because of deadlines and overlapping responsibilities. Travel-based workers, including P10, P20, and P23, described difficulty maintaining stable meal routines while moving between locations. Among physically demanding workers, concerns were sometimes related more to food quality or imbalance than meal timing alone. P5 described reliance on carbohydrate-heavy meals during field work, while P3 described delayed meals followed by overeating after strenuous work.

Food disruption was not universal. Participants such as P8, P11, and P18 reported relatively regular food intake despite difficulties with hydration or urination.

Occupational absorption and neglect of self-care

Some participants described work pressure, responsibility, and sustained concentration as reducing attention to routine self-care. This theme was particularly evident among sedentary, professional, and high-responsibility workers.

Participants such as P6, P14, and P19 described how workload and mental absorption led them to overlook drinking water, eating on time, or going for urination. In these accounts, the issue was not lack of awareness but repeated prioritization of work tasks over basic self-care. Participants also described fatigue after prolonged work demands as reducing their attention to hydration, meals, and general self-maintenance.

Schedule instability, travel, and disruption of daily routines

Several participants described disruption of daily regularity rather than difficulty with a single behavior. This was particularly evident when work involved rotating shifts, night duty, travel, late working hours, or unpredictable schedules.

Participants with rotating shifts, night duty, or irregular schedules, including P12, P13, and P18, described difficulty maintaining regular meal timing, predictable sleep, and stable daily routines. Travel was another important source of disruption. Participants such as P20, P21, and P23 described movement across locations as limiting access to water, toilets, and food while contributing to tiredness and reduced opportunity for self-care.

Stone disease understood as multifactorial, with occupation interacting with other perceived contributors

Although occupational routine was central in many accounts, participants rarely described it as the sole explanation for stone disease. Instead, they described occupation as interacting with family history, dietary habits, supplements, high-protein intake, tea consumption, smoking, alcohol use, and other lifestyle-related factors.

Family history was mentioned by participants such as P4, P20, and P23, who reported stone disease in fathers or siblings. However, these reports could not distinguish genetic susceptibility from shared household diet, water source, or environmental exposures. Participants such as P2, P4, and P18 described protein supplements or higher protein intake as factors that increased the perceived importance of adequate hydration. Some travel-heavy workers, including P10, P20, and P21, described smoking or alcohol use as embedded within work-related routine and social context.

Cross-theme synthesis

Across themes, participants described three overlapping pathways through which work routines could make preventive behaviors more difficult to sustain: infrastructural constraints, including limited access to water or toilets; schedule instability related to travel, shift work, and irregular routines; and cognitive-attentional absorption associated with workload, concentration, and responsibility.

Discussion

This study indicates that occupational routine may shape the feasibility of maintaining stone-preventive self-care among individuals with urolithiasis. Rather than identifying specific occupations as causes of stone disease, the findings extend earlier qualitative work by showing how routine work conditions may influence the practical implementation of prevention advice [19–22].

The findings suggest that barriers to fluid intake and timely urination may arise through different occupational contexts. These barriers were not limited to physically demanding or heat-exposed work; they also occurred in work involving travel, limited breaks, time pressure, concentration, or restricted restroom access. This interpretation is consistent with prevention guidance emphasizing adequate fluid intake and urine output, as well as previous research identifying busy schedules, inconvenience, travel, and limited access to water as practical barriers among stone formers [4–6, 30–32]. Occupational bladder-health literature similarly shows that workplace conditions, restroom access, time pressure, and social norms can influence toileting behavior [15–17]. However, delayed urination in the present study should be interpreted as a participant-described behavior occurring within broader work-related constraints rather than as an independent cause of urolithiasis.

The findings also provide context for dietary and lifestyle-related prevention. Work-related time pressure, fatigue, travel, and irregular schedules may reduce opportunities for planned meals and consistent self-care. This complements existing evidence linking dietary patterns, sodium intake, excessive animal-protein intake, obesity, and other lifestyle-related factors with stone risk [7, 8, 18]. It also aligns with qualitative research showing that dietary change after stone diagnosis can be difficult to sustain in everyday life [21].

These findings suggest that knowledge-based counselling alone may be insufficient when patients encounter practical barriers during work. Prevention strategies may therefore benefit from an occupation-sensitive approach that considers the individual’s work pattern, access to water and toilets, break opportunities, travel demands, and daily schedule. This interpretation is consistent with behavioral and digital approaches to stone prevention, which emphasize reminders, monitoring, routine formation, and environmental support in addition to patient education [31, 33–36]. It also reflects a social-ecological perspective in which health behaviors are shaped by individual, organizational, and environmental conditions [37].

Participants generally understood urolithiasis as multifactorial. Occupational routine was perceived as one practical context interacting with dietary habits, hydration, supplements, family history, smoking, alcohol use, and other lifestyle-related factors. This is consistent with the multifactorial nature of stone disease and supports individualized prevention counselling rather than a single occupation-based explanation [2, 7, 18].

For adults without fluid restrictions, counselling should encourage fluid intake sufficient to achieve a 24-hour urine volume of more than 2.5 L, preferably through regular water intake adjusted for heat exposure, sweating, exercise, and other fluid losses [4–6]. Dietary counselling should include avoidance of excessive salt intake, generally limited to approximately 4–5 g of sodium chloride per day, moderation of animal-protein intake where appropriate, and maintenance of normal dietary calcium intake [5, 7]. These recommendations should be individualized according to stone composition, urinary metabolic findings, comorbidities, and dietary requirements when such information is available.

Practical workplace strategies may include planned drinking schedules, carrying water bottles, access to refill points, scheduled breaks, acceptable toilet access, and realistic meal planning for mobile or shift-based workers. Reminder systems, behavioral coaching, fluid-monitoring tools, and mobile health interventions may provide additional support where work routines make regular preventive practices difficult to maintain [31, 33–36].

Limitations

This single-center qualitative study included 24 participants from a tertiary care teaching hospital in South India. The findings may not be transferable to all occupational groups, geographical settings, or healthcare contexts. The study was not designed to compare occupational groups statistically, estimate disease prevalence, or establish occupational causality.

Female participants were underrepresented, limiting interpretation of gender-sensitive experiences related to privacy, restroom access, and workplace toileting behavior. The five-month recruitment period may also not have captured seasonal variation in heat exposure, hydration requirements, or occupational workload.

Interviews were documented through contemporaneous note-taking rather than audio recording and verbatim transcription. Although notes were expanded immediately after interviews and Tamil-to-English translation was checked by Tamil-speaking team members, some linguistic nuance and interpretive depth may have been lost.

Stone composition and detailed metabolic evaluation were unavailable for most participants. The exclusion of isolated microlithiasis and calculi measuring less than 3 mm may have favored participants with more clinically apparent stone disease. In addition, participant-reported family history could not distinguish genetic susceptibility from shared household diet, water source, or environmental exposures.

Conclusion

Occupational routines may influence the feasibility of maintaining stone-preventive self-care among individuals with urolithiasis. The findings support occupation-sensitive counselling and practical workplace strategies tailored to individual work patterns and constraints. Future longitudinal and implementation studies should assess whether these approaches improve preventive behaviors, urine volume, and stone-related outcomes.
