Whole-genome resequencing identifies adaptive variation associated with complex terrain in southwestern Chinese ducks
Authors: Qinglian Li, Hehe Liu, Liang Li, Anqi Huang, Jiwei Hu, Lili Bai, Chunchun Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The complex and diverse ecological environment of southwestern China has exerted significant influences on the domestication and genetic adaptation of local duck breeds. Although ducks exhibit extensive genetic variation in heterogeneous environments, the genomic mechanisms underlying their adaptation to the complex topography and high-altitude conditions of southwestern China remain unclear. To elucidate the evolutionary processes of genetic differentiation and environmental adaptation in regional duck populations, this study analyzed the genetic diversity, population structure, and selection characteristics based on the whole-genome resequencing data from a total of 500 individuals, encompassing 7 local duck breeds from the southwest region, 2 wild duck breeds, and 6 duck breeds from other regional. Analysis of nucleotide diversity (π) revealed that 2 wild duck breeds exhibited the highest genetic diversity (π = 0.0047–0.0052), Jianchang duck (JC) has the lowest (π = 0.0026). Among domestic breeds, those from plains areas—Sichuan Shelduck (SC), Hanzhong duck (HZ), Kaijiang duck (KJ), and Jianshui yellow-brown duck (JS) (π = 0.00360–0.00377)—showed significantly higher genetic diversity than those from topographically complex regions—Yunnan Sheldrake (YN), Sansui duck (SS), and Jianchang duck (π = 0.00259–0.00311). Populations from complex terrain (JC, YN, and SS) also displayed a high abundance of long runs of homozygosity (ROH). Analysis of the fixation index (Fst) demonstrated low genetic differentiation among YN, SS, and JC, as well as among SC, HZ, and KJ. Genomic scans comparing zFst and the log2(π ratio) identified regions in the top 5% as candidate regions, revealing 20 domestication-related genes, which are functionally associated with nervous system remodeling, angiogenesis, cell adhesion, mitochondrial metabolism, growth and reproduction; 15 regional adaptation-related genes, primarily involved in cell signal transduction, protein synthesis, hormone production, gene expression and DNA damage repair; and 15 candidate genes related to adaptation to the complex high-altitude terrain, participating in nervous system development, immune adaptation, synaptic transmission, sensory systems, cytoskeletal remodeling, and endocrine functions. Collectively, these findings enhance our understanding of evolutionary processes in southwestern Chinese ducks and provide actionable insights for their conservation and breeding programs.

Introduction

Ducks, as one of the most important domesticated poultry species, have successfully adapted to diverse environments following their extensive history of domestication. This adaptation makes them an excellent model for studying genetic responses to environmental challenges. Genetic diversity, serving as the foundation of adaptive potential, is thus a critical research focus in poultry science. Numerous studies have been conducted on the genetic diversity and population genetic structure of ducks using microsatellite markers (Su et al., 2007; Seo et al., 2016) and mitochondrial DNA (Jin et al., 2014; Gaur et al., 2017), which provided preliminary insights but limited resolution. The 2013 de novo assembly and publication of the duck reference genome established a robust foundation for genome-wide studies of genetic variation (Huang et al., 2013). This advancement has facilitated the widespread application of whole-genome resequencing in duck research, enabling multidimensional analysis of origins and domestication (Guo et al., 2021), genetic diversity and population structure (Feng et al., 2021), environmental adaptation (Zhang et al., 2018a), and selection pressures (Zhang et al., 2018b; Wang et al., 2020).

Southwest China, characterized by its complex topography, including high mountains and deep valleys, has experienced slower development compared to the eastern regions, thus preserving its “original ecology” state, and the special geographical and natural environments (such as high altitudes) also lead to genetic variations among species (Li et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015), fostering the long-term flourishing of biodiversity. Studies on other livestock and poultry in this region (such as pigs, chickens, and cattle) have shown that the geographical isolation in the southwest has led to genetic differentiation and adaptive evolution (Li et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015; Lyu et al., 2024; Guan et al., 2025).This suggests that similar evolutionary forces may have shaped the genetic landscape of local duck populations. The Southwest region is one of the most advanced in China for waterfowl production, boasting abundant duck genetic resources, including the Sansui duck, renowned for its early maturation, high egg production, tender meat, palatable flavor, and low cholesterol content. The Jianchang duck, listed in the latest edition of the National List of Livestock and Poultry Genetic Resources Protection, is one of China's premier local duck breeds, distinguished by its rapid growth rate and robust lipid deposition capacity in the liver, despite years of conservation efforts, the outcomes have been limited(Wang et al., 2020). In addition, there have been instances of the endangered Sichuan Shelduck (Yang et al., 2024). Despite their significant ecological and economic importance, current resequencing studies on related duck species have selected few duck breeds or single environments, which fails to fully elucidate the terrain-driven genomic adaptations and diversity patterns. Therefore, it remains unclear how the complex topography of southwestern China has shaped the genomic architecture of indigenous ducks and what this means for their conservation genetics.

In this study, we assembled a diverse panel of duck breeds, including all representative populations across various altitudes in southwestern China, for whole-genome resequencing and comparative analysis against wild and other local breeds. The following scientific hypotheses were proposed: (1) We hypothesize that populations inhabiting high-altitude regions will exhibit lower genetic diversity compared to their low-altitude counterparts, resulting from population bottlenecks or long-term small population sizes driven by extreme environments. (2) We hypothesize that topographic heterogeneity acts as a major barrier to gene flow, leading to a genetic structure in duck populations that is strongly correlated with geographical distribution and altitudinal gradients. (3) We hypothesize that duck populations from different altitudes have undergone distinct selective pressures, with candidate genes associated with high-altitude adaptation being significantly enriched in pathways related to hypoxia response, energy metabolism, and vascular development. By testing these hypotheses, this study aims to provide novel insights into the terrain-driven genomic adaptation mechanisms and the genetic conservation of local duck breeds.

Materials and methods

Sample Collection

This study analyzed a total of 500 individuals from 15 duck breeds (Supplementary Table S1). The sample set comprised 210 blood samples, including 60 Kaijiang ducks (KJ), 50 Sichuan Shelduck (SC), 50 Yunnan Sheldrake (YN), and 50 Sansui ducks (SS); and 52 muscle tissue samples, including 22 Jianchang ducks (JC) and 30 Hanzhong ducks (HZ). All of the above are from the southwest region. Additionally, 53 blood samples from other regions, comprising 27 Cherry Valley ducks (CV) and 26 Putian Black ducks (PT). All these samples were subjected to whole-genome resequencing. Furthermore, 185 publicly available resequencing datasets were incorporated, including 18 Jianshui yellow-brown ducks (JS) from the NCBI database and 167 datasets of various breeds (47 Pekin ducks (PK), 13 Jinding ducks (JD), 18 Anas poecilorhyncha (SB), 28 Shanma ducks (SM), 39 Shaoxing ducks (SX), and 22 Anas platyrhnchos (ML) from the NCBI and GSA databases (Supplementary Table S2). This integrated approach yielded a final dataset of 500 resequencing genomes, which includes 280 from southwest regional breeds and 220 from other breeds. The geographical distribution of duck breeds in the southwest region is shown in Fig. 1. DNA extraction and whole-genome resequencing

Blood was collected from the sub-wing vein, stored in an EDTA anticoagulant tube, and stored in a refrigerator at −20°C. Genomic DNA was extracted using the standard phenol–chloroform protocol. The quality and quantity of DNA were assessed by using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. After the examinations, standard procedures were used to generate paired-end libraries for each eligible sample. The average insert size was about 500 bp, and the average read length was 150 bp. All libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform and stored in FASTQ format.

Reads mapping and SNP calling

The raw data underwent quality control and filtering using fastp software. The Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA aln) was employed to map the clean reads against the duck reference genome (Anas platyrhynchos ZJU1.0) utilizing default parameters. We used HaplotypeCaller of GATK 3.5 to call SNPs after mapping. Finally, PLINK (version 1.90) was then used for further data filtration. SNPs were filtered based on the following criteria: minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 0.05 and a maximum missing rate of less than 0.1.

Population genetics analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on 500 individuals using PLINK's "–pca" command, interpreting variances via eigenvalues, and visualized with R (version 4.2.3). PLINK calculated the genetic matrix for 500 individuals, R drew the phylogenetic tree, and the iTOL version 7 online website (https://itol.embl.de/) was used to enhance its appearance. To investigate the population genetic structure, we inferred the ancestral components using the ADMIXTURE (version 1.3.1). The minimum CV errors were determined as the optimal K value, and the results were visualized in R.

VCFtools (version 0.1.16) was employed with the parameters (-window-pi 40,000 -window-pi-step 20,000) to estimate the genomic nucleotide diversity (π) and genetic differentiation index (Fst) for each duck population. PLINK was utilized with the -het parameter to calculate the average observed heterozygosity (Ho) and expected heterozygosity (He), as well as the average inbreeding coefficient (F) for the duck populations. The linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns among 500 ducks from 15 populations were effectively reflected by calculating the squared correlation coefficient (r²) between pairwise SNPs using PopLDdecay with default parameters (Zhang et al., 2019a). The homozygous ROH (–homozyg) of a duck flock was calculated using the PLINK tool, and the length of ROH and the number of ROH were determined.

Selective signatures analysis

To identify genomic regions under selective pressure, we employed four complementary approaches: analysis of nucleotide diversity (π), fixation index (Fst), cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH), and integrated haplotype score (iHS). Fst values were Z-transformed, and the log2-transformed ratio of pairwise π was calculated. Genomic regions with the top 5% of zFst values and the top 5% of log2(π ratio) values were considered candidate selected regions. Genotype imputation and haplotype phasing were performed using Beagle (version 5.5), and iHS and XP-EHH were calculated using selscan (version 2.1.1) A sliding window approach (40 kb sliding window with a 20 kb step) was applied to calculate the proportion of extreme iHS values (|iHS| > 2) within each window, and windows in the top 5% of empirical p-values were considered candidate regions. In addition, XP-EHH analysis was performed using selscan to detect regions showing evidence of recent positive selection, applying the same sliding window parameters (40 kb window, 20 kb step), and the top 5% of windows were retained as candidate regions. Genes located within these candidate regions were defined as candidate genes. Combining multiple selection detection methods enhances the power to identify signatures of selection(Bello et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025a); therefore, we primarily focused on genes identified by all four approaches.

Functional enrichment

These candidate genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses using the KOBAS 3.0 online website (http://bioinfo.org/kobas/). (P < 0.05).

Results

Population genetic structure and genetic differentiation

To elucidate the genetic structure and relationships among populations, we performed PCA, constructed a phylogenetic tree, and analyzed population structure using whole-genome data from seven indigenous duck breeds in southwestern China, two wild duck populations, and six commercial duck breeds. PCA revealed that individuals from the same population tended to cluster together. Principal components 1 and 2 (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 24.4% and 17.4% of the total genetic variation, respectively (Fig. 2A). The admixture population structure analysis revealed that K = 8 yielded the optimal number of ancestral populations. Fig. 2C displays the inferred population genetic structure across K-values ranging from 5 to 8. When K = 5, distinct clusters emerged as follows: PK and CV formed one group; YN and SS comprised a second group; JC exhibited a mixed phenomenon, suggesting that it was a hybrid population belonging to the first two groups. HZ, SC, and JD constituted a third group; ML and SB grouped as a fourth; PT was in a separate group. Progressive differentiation was observed with increasing K-values: when K = 6, JS diverged from the wild duck populations. K = 7, KJ separated as an independent cluster.

Genetic differentiation analysis revealed pairwise Fst values (Supplementary Table S4). The highest genetic divergence was observed between JC and JD (Fst = 0.147), whereas SB and ML exhibited the lowest differentiation (Fst = 0.023). Most population pairs demonstrated moderate genetic differentiation. Notably, minimal differentiation (Fst < 0.052) was detected among YN, SS, and JC, as well as between SC, HZ, and KJ.

Population genetic diversity

Nucleotide diversity was employed to assess the level of genetic diversity within populations. Two wild duck populations SB (π = 0.0052) and ML (π = 0.0047), exhibited the highest genetic diversity. Populations inhabiting the Sichuan Basin, Hanzhong Plain (SC, HZ, KJ), and near river valleys (JS) in the southwestern region show a moderate π value. This was followed by the commercial duck breeds. Populations located in the Yunnan-Guizhou Plateau and Hengduan Mountains region (YN, JC, SS) showed the lowest diversity (Fig. 3A). Analysis of heterozygosity revealed He values exceeding 0.3 across all populations (Supplementary Table S5). The highest Ho value was observed in HZ (0.2714), with JC demonstrating the lowest (0.1317) (Fig. 3D). Consistent with π, both show that the diversity of duck breeds in complex terrain areas of the southwest region is lower than in flat areas. Discussion

Genetic diversity and population structure

Genetic diversity can, to some extent, reflect a population's adaptability to environments and history of domestication. The relatively isolated and environmentally stable conditions in Southwest China contribute to its high biodiversity (Qu et al., 2014) . Huang et al. analyzed the genetic diversity of chickens from 11 provinces and detected the highest nucleotide diversity in southwestern populations and the lowest in northwestern populations (Huang et al., 2021), which may be associated with the complex ecogeographical environment of Southwest China. The nucleotide diversity results from this study indicate that wild populations maintain the highest level of genetic diversity, consistent with previous studies (Li et al., 2010), suggesting that domestication has reduced genetic diversity (Feng et al., 2021). Genetic parameter analysis (π, F, He, Ho, and ROH) of 7 duck populations in the southwest region revealed that the low nucleotide diversity and high inbreeding phenomenon of JC, YN, and SS may be due to factors such as relatively isolated geographical location and high altitude, resulting in less gene exchange with other populations and more inbreeding. Compared with HZ and SC, KJ shows lower genomic diversity and higher inbreeding. Population structure analysis indicates that KJ formed an independent branch and was included in the National Livestock Genetic Resources Catalog in 2025. Previously, due to ineffective protection and management, the inbreeding coefficient may have increased, thereby affecting its genomic diversity (Shen et al., 2024). These differences reflect distinct evolutionary trajectories among southwestern duck populations. In summary, natural geographic barriers in southwestern China likely restricted gene flow and promoted genetic differentiation among local duck populations (Li et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2015). Some studies have inferred the demographic history of duck populations from different geographic distributions and also found extensive gene flow between adjacent populations (Jiang et al., 2021). thereby offering a different perspective on how geographic barriers affect gene flow.

Genome-wide selective sweep

This study systematically reveals the domestication and adaptation characteristics of duck breeds in Southwest China through three levels of genomic selection scanning. The Southwest–wild comparison reveals genes under universal selection related to domestication; the Southwest–other comparison reveals genes for regional adaptation; and the Southwest–internal comparison further reveals genes for high-altitude adaptation.

Domestication. During domestication, ducks exhibit reduced brain volume and decreased alertness, reflecting changes in neural anatomy and behavior (Ebinger, 1995; Zhang et al., 2018a). We identified multiple domestication-related genes involved in neural remodeling, angiogenesis, cell adhesion, mitochondrial metabolism, growth, and reproduction. Neural genes are under early selection pressure during animal domestication (Lan et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018a). The significant enrichment of the Cell Adhesion Molecules (CAMs) pathway and brain development directly supports neural remodeling as a core mechanism of domestication. CNTN1 plays key roles in neurodevelopment and axon connectivity (Berglund and Ranscht, 1994; Cho et al., 2021). Genomic comparison between mallard and Jianchang duck reveals strong selection signals in CNTN1 (Wang et al., 2020). CNTN1 is also linked to enhanced egg-laying in chickens (Yin et al., 2020), and whole-genome DNA methylation analysis in Shaoxing duck indicates CNTN1 involvement in reproduction, closely associated with high egg production (Xu et al., 2022). NRCAM and NRXN3 are involved in neuronal cell adhesion (Lokapally et al., 2017) and synaptic function (Olivares-Hernández et al., 2022), respectively; the latter also regulates sexual maturation and egg production (Zhang et al., 2026). Calcium-dependent cell adhesion molecules such as CDH2, CDH6, CDH9 are enriched in multiple GO terms. CDH2 regulates neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity (Lin et al., 2014), and CDH9 participates in hippocampal synaptic plasticity and memory (Petersen et al., 2024). This implies that nervous system remodeling is a crucial adaptive mechanism for survival in complex mountainous environments.

MAGI1 regulates cell adhesion, infiltration, and angiogenesis (Alday-Parejo et al., 2021), and is associated with egg production in geese (Yu et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2023). KDR is a key mediator of angiogenesis and permeability, markedly upregulated under hypoxia (Li and Zhao, 2009); it regulates vascular remodeling (Huang et al., 2011) and lipid metabolism (Wu et al., 2016), suggesting dual roles in oxygen and energy homeostasis. PLEKHG1 participates in vascular and neural processes (Xu et al., 2025b). IMMP2L maintains mitochondrial function and reduces oxidative stress (Ma et al., 2023, 2025).Copy number variation (CNV) analysis in horses identified IMMP2L, which promotes energy metabolism efficiency by regulating mitochondrial ATP levels(Ding et al., 2025).

Duck domestication also involves increased body size and stronger bones (Zhu et al., 2021), indicating additional gene networks for growth, metabolism, and reproduction. IGF1R is involved in cell growth (Jiang et al., 2021); it binds its ligand IGF1 to activate downstream signaling, promoting cell proliferation, differentiation, and muscle hypertrophy. Its association with breast muscle traits is confirmed in chickens (Davis et al., 2015) and geese (Ma et al., 2024b), and selective sweep analysis suggests IGF1R may affect duck muscle development (Ma et al., 2024a). IGF1R also participates in ovarian steroidogenesis in ducks(Liu et al., 2021), and is linked to growth (Wang et al., 2018), reproduction (Zhang et al., 2022b), and body weight regulation (Wang et al., 2022b) in pigs. MAPK signaling pathway regulates chicken breast muscle growth and development (Zhang et al., 2019b). Within this pathway, KIT is thought to affect duck muscle growth and development (Zhang et al., 2024b) plumage color (Wang et al., 2022a), and feather growth (Liu et al., 2022). Notably, both IGF1R and KIT in the MAPK pathway are confirmed to influence breast muscle growth and development (Zhang et al., 2019b; Ma et al., 2024a). Since breast muscle is crucial for bird flight (Garant et al., 2023), selection on these genes likely correlates with loss of flight in domestic ducks. KIT is also identified as a potential basis for local environmental adaptation (Sallam et al., 2023). PTN regulates oviduct development and oogenesis, associated with reproductive traits (Talebi et al., 2020). NAALADL2 is involved in glutamate metabolism, affecting neurodevelopment (Schrauwen et al., 2024), and is linked to immune homeostasis (Zhang et al., 2022a; Zhong et al., 2024). TIGAR negatively regulates programmed cell death, potentially enhancing cellular stress resistance. IYD is a key gene for thyroid hormone synthesis; thyroid hormone levels are closely related to embryonic development and fertility in beef cattle (Fonseca et al., 2018). SPTLC3 is associated with hair follicle development (Jin et al., 2020).

Regional Adaptation. The regional adaptive evolution of duck populations in Southwest China involves multiple genes. These genes are mainly involved in cell signal transduction, protein synthesis, hormone generation, gene expression, and DNA damage repair. MAPK10 is enriched in multiple pathways. It regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and transcription. CNV in MAPK10 downregulates its expression in skeletal muscle, brain, and fat, this modulates transcriptional activity and affects bovine phenotypic traits(Li et al., 2025b). Enriched GO terms (JNK cascade, MAP kinase activity) further support the core kinase function of MAPK10 in signal transduction. For CTNND2, it has been confirmed in chickens (Yin et al., 2019) and rabbits (Wang et al., 2025) to be involved in regulating retinal development. Additionally, studies suggest that CTNND2 supports synapse maintenance, regulates synapse maturation, and promotes neuronal integrity in adults (Assendorp et al., 2024).

ARL1 regulates vesicle transport and ensures normal protein transport within cells (Yu and Lee, 2017). Using multi-omics analysis (genomics and transcriptomics) in goats, confirmed that MAN1A2 is associated with amino acid metabolism(Liu et al., 2024). This is consistent with our enrichment results. It suggests that ducks may maintain cellular homeostasis under environmental stress by regulating nutrient metabolism and protein synthesis. HSD3B1 plays dual roles in stress response (via corticosterone) and energy metabolism (via oxidoreductase activity). Studies show that HSD3B1 participates in chicken follicle development by controlling steroid hormone production, thereby affecting laying performance (Sechman et al., 2020; Li et al., 2023). KIF21A is involved in microtubule regulation of actin dynamics in dendritic spines. It is crucial for synaptic plasticity (Sun et al., 2025). ANO3 is associated with calcium homeostasis (Thomsen et al., 2024). SLIT3, a central nervous system ligand, is enriched in multiple GO terms. It participates in muscle and bone development(Gao et al., 2022). SLIT3 also binds ROBO1 on sympathetic neurons, activating Ca²⁺/CaMKII signaling and norepinephrine release to enhance adipocyte thermogenesis (Wang et al., 2021). The identification of CNVs in the duck's entire genome has confirmed its role in thermoregulation(Li et al., 2024).

MMS22L is associated with DNA-dependent DNA replication and recombinational DNA repair(Nazar et al., 2021). NUP205 is a nucleoporin complex. Its dysfunction affects mRNA export, protein import, and transport of DNA damage repair (Arshad et al., 2020; Sumner and Brickner, 2022)factors. Downregulating NUP205 reduces protein synthesis and secretion in the hen magnum(Chang et al., 2024). A similar gene, GPATCH1, is involved in mRNA processing (Habimana et al., 2021) and chicken carcass trait metabolism (Azovtseva et al., 2025). NUP205 and GPATCH1 may be key for rapid response and fine-tuned gene expression during duck adaptation. The ribosome biogenesis gene UTP20 regulates immune function (Li et al., 2025c). TBC1D22A is associated with feather growth and development in chickens (Zhang et al., 2025).

High-Altitude Adaptation. We identified multiple candidate genes for high-altitude adaptation, involved in neurodevelopment, immunity, synaptic transmission, sensory systems, cytoskeletal remodeling, and endocrine regulation. Among these, SEMA5A, a secreted semaphorin, participates in axon guidance and angiogenesis (Sadanandam et al., 2010) and also regulates immune responses in chickens(Lillie et al., 2017). ALCAM (CD166), an immunoglobulin superfamily adhesion molecule, is involved in T-cell activation, leukocyte trafficking, and (lymph)angiogenesis (Willrodt et al., 2019). Additionally, ALCAM is believed to regulate the axonal growth of dopamine neurons in the mouse brain (Bye et al., 2019). CBLB (ErbB signaling pathway) is involved in adaptive immune responses (Caro-Consuegra et al., 2022). NRG3 plays a critical role in chicken nervous system development and differentiation, particularly during central nervous system development (Abe et al., 2017). Based on genome-wide CNV analysis in chickens, NRG3 has been identified as a nervous system-related gene, suggesting its functional importance in chicken domestication and breed differentiation (Chen et al., 2022b), indicating that CNVs are as important as SNPs in high-altitude adaptation. KCNF1 contributes to synaptic memory (Lo et al., 2016) and regulates blood circulation and pressure in Tibetan chickens, facilitating hypoxic adaptation (Zhang et al., 2020). Similarly, high-altitude Andean ducks show increased blood oxygen saturation under hypoxia (Ivy et al., 2019), enhancing circulatory oxygen supply via elevated arterial oxygen content (Laguë et al., 2020). These findings align with our NCALD enrichment analysis, suggesting a universal strategy for hypoxic adaptation. KCNF1 and NCALD optimize firing patterns by modulating electrical activity and neurotransmitter release (Chen et al., 2023), which is essential for processing complex spatial information during flight; STXBP6 supports efficient neural communication by enhancing the precision of synaptic vesicle transport (Vinci et al., 2023).

KCNMA1 is crucial for controlling smooth muscle tone and neuronal excitability (Ren et al., 2023) and exhibits context-specific expression(Chen et al., 2022a). EPS8 is enriched in multiple GO terms (Rac guanyl-nucleotide exchange factor activity, actin binding, stereocilium tip, NMDA receptor complex, and glutamatergic synapse). It participates in neural hypoxia tolerance and cognitive adaptation via Rac-mediated actin cytoskeleton regulation (Offenhäuser et al., 2004). EPS8 also maintains stereocilia growth and mechanosensitivity in mammalian inner ear hair cells (Zampini et al., 2011)—critical for auditory feedback in complex terrains to sense environment and avoid predators, and regulates long-term synaptic plasticity and hippocampal cognition (Wang et al., 2017). Thus, EPS8 is under selective pressure at three levels: actin cytoskeletal remodeling, auditory function, and cognition.

POU1F1, a pituitary-specific transcription factor, participates in pituitary regulation (Li et al., 2025a) and directly activates genes such as GH (growth hormone), PRL (prolactin), and TSHβ (thyroid-stimulating hormone β subunit), regulating the synthesis and secretion of anterior pituitary hormones (Zhao et al., 2021). Genome-wide selection sweep analyses have revealed that POU1F1 is associated with body size in chickens (Yang et al., 2025) and is under positive selection in large-bodied chickens (Yan et al., 2025). RPS6KA2 regulates the mTOR signaling cascade, participating in chicken follicular development (Hao et al., 2024) and influencing chicken breast muscle weight (Chen et al., 2013). SPAG6 is expressed in testicular spermatogenic cells and promotes spermatogenesis and maturation (Liu et al., 2019), as confirmed in both chickens (Xue et al., 2022) and ducks (Zhang et al., 2024a), highlighting its importance for male fertility. Additionally, PTPRO has been reported to be associated with environmental adaptation (Okwasiimire et al., 2026). L3MBTL3 is involved in DNA damage repair (Hall et al., 2022) and may play an important role in hypoxia-induced chromatin remodeling. BRINP3 is one of the most strongly selected genes in high-altitude Andean human populations, related to cardiovascular development and function but not directly involved in hypoxic responses (Crawford et al., 2017); this gene is also associated with fertility in Korean cattle (Lee et al., 2022), suggesting its potential role in high-altitude adaptation.

In summary, these findings elucidate the domestication and adaptation mechanisms of Southwest China ducks, and provide candidate loci—particularly genes involved in neurodevelopment, angiogenesis, hypoxia tolerance, and breast muscle development—for conserving local poultry genetic resources, molecular breeding, and reproduction management. Moreover, the high-altitude adaptation mechanisms described offer insights into convergent evolution of poultry across global high-altitude regions (e.g., Andes) and a theoretical basis for understanding avian high-altitude adaptation worldwide.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically reveals the genetic diversity characteristics and adaptive evolutionary mechanisms of indigenous duck populations in southwestern China, based on large-scale genomic data from 500 individuals across 15 duck populations. We found that low-altitude plain populations (e.g., SC, HZ, JS) exhibit higher genetic diversity and lower inbreeding levels, whereas high-altitude, complex-terrain populations (e.g., JC, YN, SS) show the opposite genetic pattern. Through selection signature analysis, we elucidated key biological mechanisms related to domestication and adaptation: domestication processes mainly involve pathways such as nervous system remodeling, angiogenesis, cell adhesion, mitochondrial metabolism, growth, and reproduction; regional adaptation is closely associated with cell signal transduction, protein synthesis, hormonal regulation, gene expression, and DNA damage repair; and adaptation mechanisms to high-altitude, complex terrain are enriched in neurological development, immune responses, synaptic transmission, sensory systems, cytoskeletal remodeling, and endocrine functions. These findings provide an important basis for the conservation and innovative utilization of genetic resources of indigenous ducks. In particular, the adaptive mechanisms underlying important traits such as hypoxia tolerance and muscle development can offer direct guidance for molecular marker-assisted breeding, thereby facilitating the efficient conservation and sustainable utilization of these indigenous duck genetic resources.
