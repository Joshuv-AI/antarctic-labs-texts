Efficacy of olanzapine for prophylaxis of delayed chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting in patients with breast cancer receiving dose-dense AC with a steroid-sparing regimen: a single-center pilot study
Authors: Manami Tada, Yuichiro Kikawa, Nobuhiro Shibata, Chie Hirai, Hirotsugu Yanai, Masahiro Takada, Tomoharu Sugie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study examined the feasibility of a steroid-sparing approach in a dose-dense AC regimen in breast cancer patients, while integrating olanzapine to manage chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV).MethodsA prospective, single-center study was conducted with 30 patients diagnosed with Stage I–III breast cancer who underwent treatment with a dose-dense AC regimen. Patients were given prophylactic olanzapine from days 1 to 4 along with the standard three-drug combination antiemetic regimen, excluding dexamethasone on days 2 and 3. Nausea, vomiting, and drowsiness were evaluated using a validated 4-point Likert scale. The main outcome measured was the complete response (CR) rate in the delayed phase.ResultsThe CR rates were 73% and 63% in the acute and delayed phases, respectively. The total control and complete control rates in the acute phase were 60% and 73%, respectively, while in the delayed phase, they were 36.6% and 63%. Moderate-to-severe daytime sleepiness was observed in 40% of the patients. The mean nausea score on the EORTC QLQ-C30 remained within the minimally important difference threshold for all four treatment cycles.ConclusionThis study suggests that olanzapine may enable steroid reduction without compromising CINV control in patients receiving dose-dense AC. A steroid-sparing regimen could mitigate steroid-related adverse effects while maintaining antiemetic efficacy. Further large-scale studies are warranted to validate these findings and optimize steroid-sparing strategies in antiemetic protocols.Trial registrationThis study was registered on August 29, 2020, in the University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN) Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN ID: UMIN000041605).

Introduction

Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV) represents a significant adverse effect of cancer treatment. Anthracycline- and cyclophosphamide-based therapy, commonly utilized in breast cancer treatment, is classified as highly emetogenic chemotherapy (HEC) due to its substantial potential to trigger nausea and vomiting. Effective management of antiemetics plays a critical role in enhancing patients’ quality of life (QOL) and ensuring compliance with chemotherapy protocols. Current international guidelines recommend a four-drug antiemetic regimen consisting of a neurokinin-1 receptor antagonist, a 5-hydroxytryptamine-3 receptor antagonist, dexamethasone, and olanzapine for HEC [1–4].

In recent years, dose-dense anthracycline and cyclophosphamide (ddAC) regimens, administered every 2 weeks instead of every 3 weeks, have been increasingly used for breast cancer treatment due to their enhanced efficacy in disease control [5]. Because ddAC is administered at 2-week intervals, supportive medications given with each cycle, including dexamethasone, are delivered over a more compressed time frame than in a 3-week schedule. Although direct comparative evidence linking ddAC (vs. conventional AC) to a higher overall frequency of dexamethasone-related noninfectious adverse effects is limited, dexamethasone is known to cause clinically relevant toxicities, such as insomnia, agitation, and hyperglycemia, particularly with repeated exposure. Notably, a large retrospective cohort study reported Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia in patients treated with ddAC (0.6%) but not in those receiving non–ddAC, and suggested that administration of a similar cumulative corticosteroid exposure over 8 weeks (vs. 12 weeks) may contribute to increased infectious vulnerability [6]. In addition, glucocorticoid-induced adverse effects such as bone loss are known to depend on both dose and cumulative exposure [7].

Addressing these concerns, recent studies have investigated reducing or eliminating dexamethasone in antiemetic regimens while ensuring adequate CINV control [8, 9]. Several meta-analyses have indicated that strategies avoiding steroids can offer antiemetic efficacy comparable to specific chemotherapy regimens [10, 11]. Accordingly, the Japanese antiemetic guidelines provide a weak recommendation for steroid sparing in AC chemotherapy [12].

In pursuit of enhanced control of CINV, researchers have investigated the use of additional antiemetic medications. One such medication is olanzapine, an atypical antipsychotic known for its potent antiemetic effects through its action on various neurotransmitter pathways implicated in nausea and vomiting, including dopamine and serotonin receptors [13, 14]. Among the studies showcasing the efficacy of incorporating olanzapine into standard antiemetic therapy, the J-FORCE trial stands out as a significantly large-scale randomized controlled trial conducted in Japan [15]. In this trial, supplementing antiemetic therapy with 5 mg of olanzapine alongside a platinum-based highly emetogenic regimen resulted in a higher complete response (CR) rate compared to conventional therapy. Furthermore, the side effect of drowsiness, which is often a concern with olanzapine, was mitigated compared with the use of 10 mg olanzapine. In response to these findings, the addition of 5 mg olanzapine to conventional antiemetic therapy has been recommended in multiple guidelines [1–4]. The added benefit of olanzapine has also been evaluated in patients with breast cancer. One of the first large randomized, double-blind phase III trials, conducted by Navari et al., demonstrated that adding 10 mg of olanzapine to standard triple antiemetic therapy improved delayed CR rates (79% vs. 66%) in patients with breast cancer receiving AC chemotherapy or cisplatin-based regimens [16]. Furthermore, in a randomized, double-blind trial conducted by Clemons et al., in which approximately 90% of patients received AC chemotherapy, the four-drug regimen including olanzapine significantly improved nausea control compared with the control group [17].

This led to the hypothesis that enhanced antiemetic efficacy can be ensured through the co-administration of olanzapine, even when implementing steroid-sparing strategies in highly emetogenic chemotherapy regimens. The SPARED trial, reported in 2023, was the first large study to implement steroid-sparing therapy with a conventional three-drug antiemetic regimen under olanzapine co-administration in patients receiving cisplatin-based regimens [18]. Although the study suggested that steroid sparing could be a viable option owing to the absence of significant differences in CR rates, the total control (TC) rate was low, and the authors concluded that its application should be considered individually. More recently, the J-TOP-B trial, a randomized controlled trial evaluating anthracycline-containing regimens and a four-drug antiemetic strategy including olanzapine with a steroid-sparing approach was reported in 2025 [19]. In that study, approximately 30% of the patients received dose-dense AC or EC chemotherapy. The trial demonstrated that the olanzapine group achieved significantly better nausea control than the placebo group.

However, evidence regarding the combination of olanzapine and steroid-sparing strategies in patients with breast cancer receiving the ddAC remains limited. To date, no studies have specifically focused on patients with breast cancer receiving the ddAC regimen, in whom the adverse effects of steroids are of particular concern. Therefore, this study was designed as an initial investigation to provide practical, real-world data in this setting. This study aimed to assess whether olanzapine can serve as a viable component of a steroid-sparing regimen for CINV prevention in patients receiving ddAC chemotherapy, while maintaining optimal nausea and vomiting control. By evaluating the efficacy and safety of a steroid-sparing antiemetic regimen incorporating olanzapine, this study provided valuable insights into the optimization of supportive care strategies for patients undergoing dose-dense chemotherapy regimens.

Methods

Patients

This single-center pilot study involved patients with Stages I–III breast cancer who received ddAC as adjuvant or neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The ddAC treatment regimen consisted of doxorubicin (60 mg/m2) plus cyclophosphamide (600 mg/m2) on day 1 every 2 weeks, with a total of four planned administrations. Chemotherapy dosage adjustments or delays were permitted based on the physician’s judgment regarding adverse events. Patients with contraindications or precautions against olanzapine administration were excluded, such as those with pre-existing diabetes or HbA1c (National Glycohemoglobin Standardization Program) ≥ 6.5% at registration, AST or ALT levels > 100 U/L, current smokers, and individuals taking specific medications, including antihistamines or barbiturate derivatives, 5-HT3 receptor antagonists, NK1 receptor antagonists, oral steroids, or dopamine agonists. The Institutional Review Board of Kansai Medical University approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained.

Antiemetic procedure

Patients received olanzapine 5 mg/day on day 1 before anticancer treatment, along with the standard three-drug antiemetic regimen: NK1 receptor antagonist (oral aprepitant 125 mg), 5-HT3 receptor antagonist (palonosetron hydrochloride 0.75 mg), and dexamethasone 9.9 mg. Aprepitant 80 mg was additionally given orally after breakfast on days 2 and 3. Olanzapine was administered after dinner or before bedtime for four days, starting from the initial anticancer therapy dose.

Patient-reported outcomes

Nausea and vomiting, as well as drowsiness, were assessed using a 4-point Likert scale questionnaire that was previously utilized in the J-FORCE study [15]. The assessments were conducted every 24 h over a period of 5 days. The questionnaires captured information on the frequency of vomiting episodes, the timing of the first vomiting episode, the severity of nausea on a Likert scale (0 indicating no nausea, 1 for mild nausea, 2 for moderate nausea, and 3 for severe nausea), the number of rescue medications administered and the timing of the first dose, the severity of appetite loss (0 for not at all, 1 for a little bit, 2 for quite a bit, and 3 for very much), the severity of daytime sleepiness, the occurrence of concentration impairment due to sleepiness, and the frequency of sleepiness (0 for not at all, 1 for a little bit, 2 for quite a bit, and 3 for very much). The QOL was evaluated using the Japanese version of the EORTC QLQ-C30 Version 3 [20], which comprises 30 questions categorized into four main scales. Assessments were conducted at baseline before treatment and after the completion of cycles 1, 3, and 4.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was the CR rate during the delayed phase (24–120 h post-chemotherapy administration) of the first cycle, evaluated based on patient-reported outcomes. CR was defined as the absence of vomiting or retching and the absence of the need for additional antiemetic treatment. The secondary endpoints, all patient-reported, included CR in the acute phase (0–24 h post-chemotherapy administration) and overall phase (0–120 h post-chemotherapy administration); complete control (CC) rate, defined as the absence of more than mild nausea during complete response in the acute, delayed, and overall phases; TC rate, defined as the absence of nausea during complete response in the acute, delayed, and overall phases; and the incidence of adverse events.

Statistical analysis

This single-center pilot study was designed to investigate the feasibility and initial effectiveness of olanzapine as part of a prophylactic antiemetic regimen in patients with breast cancer receiving ddAC. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, a sample size of 30 patients was chosen based on feasibility rather than formal power calculations. The analysis utilized descriptive and inferential statistical methods to assess the antiemetic effectiveness and safety.

The demographic and clinical characteristics of the enrolled patients are summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were reported as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages.

The main endpoint, CR rate in the delayed phase, was presented as a percentage with a 95% confidence interval (CI) computed using the Clopper–Pearson exact method. Likewise, CR rates in the acute and overall phases, as well as CC and TC rates, were assessed employing the identical statistical method.

Scores on the Japanese version of the EORTC QLQ-C30 were analyzed as continuous variables. The analyses were conducted after excluding missing patient-reported outcome data. The minimally important difference (MID) was defined as the smallest change in the QOL score perceived as important by a patient or a third party, such as a physician. The QOL score was assessed based on the MID for advanced breast cancer as proposed by Musoro et al. [21]. MIDs for group-level changes in QOL scores were global health status/QOL, −8; physical functioning, −10; social functioning, −7; nausea and/or vomiting, −11; fatigue, −8; and appetite loss, −14.

Complete response and control rates

The CR rates were 73% and 63% in the acute and delayed (Fig. 1), respectively. The TC rates were 60% in the acute phase and 36.6% in the delayed phase. The CC rate, characterized by the absence of vomiting and mild nausea only, was 73% in the acute phase and 63% in the delayed phase. Daily control rates during the five-day post-chemotherapy period showed consistent stability.

Patterns of nausea progression

In Fig. 2, we illustrated the day-to-day trend of nausea for each patient throughout the treatment course. The severity of nausea was color-coded and presented in a heatmap-like format. Nausea was evaluated using a 4-point Likert scale questionnaire completed daily by each patient. The figure demonstrates three distinct trends in the occurrence of nausea. Patients who remained nausea-free during the acute phase generally maintained good control during the delayed phase. Conversely, patients who experienced nausea during the acute phase were more likely to continue experiencing symptoms in the delayed phase, despite the inclusion of olanzapine in the antiemetic regimen. Additionally, some patients showed fluctuating nausea patterns, with symptom intensity varying over time. These findings suggest that early-onset nausea may predict prolonged symptoms, emphasizing the importance of proactive management strategies in high-risk patients.

Discussion

This pilot study examined the effectiveness of olanzapine in combination with a steroid-sparing antiemetic regimen (dexamethasone on day 1 with olanzapine alone) in preventing CINV in patients with breast cancer undergoing ddAC chemotherapy. The key outcome revealed a CR rate of 63% during the delayed phase, indicating a substantial number of patients achieved vomiting control without needing rescue medication during this critical period. Furthermore, QOL related to nausea and vomiting remained stable across treatment cycles, suggesting that olanzapine’s side effects did not significantly impact this specific aspect of patient well-being. These initial findings offer support for the potential effectiveness of this steroid-sparing regimen in the demanding context of dose-dense chemotherapy and justify further investigation.

The delayed-phase CR rate of 63% observed in our study was within the range of previously reported outcomes in AC-based chemotherapy, including 67% in the study by Navari et al. with olanzapine added to standard triple antiemetic therapy, 56.6% in the study by Ito et al. using a steroid-sparing regimen without olanzapine, and the result reported in the J-TOP-B trial evaluating a steroid-sparing regimen with olanzapine [8, 9, 16]. These comparisons may suggest that adding olanzapine contributes to maintaining antiemetic efficacy and that steroid sparing after day 1 may be feasible. However, cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

In addition to the delayed-phase CR, we also examined other CINV-related outcomes in the present study. A more detailed analysis of CINV control revealed that the delayed-phase CR rate of 63% was equivalent to the CC rate of 63%, indicating that patients who achieved CR did not experience moderate or severe nausea. However, the TC rate, requiring the complete absence of nausea, was lower at 36.6%. When comparing these rates with results from trials such as J-FORCE and SPARED, several important contextual differences should be considered [15, 19]. First, those studies mainly evaluated cisplatin-containing regimens, whereas our study focused on ddAC in patients with breast cancer. Although both are classified as highly emetogenic chemotherapy, AC and cisplatin-containing regimens differ in the clinical pattern of nausea and vomiting. Therefore, the efficacy observed in cisplatin-based studies cannot be directly applied to the ddAC setting. Second, unlike the inpatient setting of the J-FORCE trial, our study was conducted in an outpatient setting where rescue medications were provided upfront, potentially promoting prophylactic use that could impact the TC metric (no nausea or rescue medication). These differences should be kept in mind when interpreting cross-trial comparisons. As shown in Fig. 2, patient responses were heterogeneous, showing patterns from sustained symptom control to persistent nausea. This variability, coupled with the discrepancy between high CR/CC and lower TC rates, suggests that while the regimen effectively manages vomiting and significant nausea in most patients, complete relief from nausea varies. For patients with good early control, this steroid-sparing approach may be sufficient. However, individuals experiencing substantial early nausea, as depicted in the subgroup in Fig. 2, might benefit from additional dexamethasone or other antiemetics.

Another significant finding was the tolerability of the regimen, particularly concerning daytime sleepiness and the QOL. Approximately 40% of the patients reported moderate to severe daytime sleepiness during both the acute and delayed phases. Somnolence is a recognized dose-dependent adverse effect of olanzapine; the 5 mg dose was selected based on evidence demonstrating significantly less sedation than 10 mg while maintaining comparable antiemetic efficacy [22, 23].

In the present study, the timing of olanzapine administration (i.e., after dinner or at bedtime) was not specified, and it was therefore administered predominantly at bedtime. In contrast, in the J-FORCE trial, olanzapine was administered after dinner, whereas in the J-TOP-B trial, the administration time was defined as within 5 h after chemotherapy. Considering that the peak plasma concentration of olanzapine occurs approximately 5–8 h after oral administration, trials in which olanzapine was administered earlier may have been associated with less daytime somnolence on the following day [15, 19].

Another possible explanation for the relatively high incidence of somnolence observed in this study is the sex distribution of the study population. All participants in the present cohort were women, whereas previous antiemetic trials, including the J-FORCE study, enrolled both male and female patients and did not report sex-specific analyses for somnolence [15]. Olanzapine pharmacokinetics are known to vary according to patient characteristics, including sex. According to the FDA clinical pharmacology review and prescribing information, olanzapine clearance is approximately 30% lower in women than in men, suggesting that systemic exposure may be higher in female patients receiving the same dose [24]. This pharmacokinetic difference may potentially contribute to an increased susceptibility to central nervous system effects such as somnolence, particularly in a predominantly female population. However, because sex-specific safety analyses were not reported in previous trials and pharmacokinetic data were not collected in the present study, this explanation remains speculative and requires further investigation.

Furthermore, our study uniquely evaluated sleep quality using a patient diary, which was not assessed in the J-TOP-B trial. This allowed for a more direct assessment of nighttime rest. Our findings showed a tendency toward improved sleep quality after treatment initiation; however, this observation should be interpreted cautiously. Taken together with the potential influence of administration timing on daytime somnolence, these findings suggest that optimizing the timing of olanzapine administration may help balance antiemetic efficacy and tolerability [19].

This study had several limitations. First, the small sample size and single-arm design precluded direct comparisons with previous studies. Second, olanzapine-related somnolence could not be clearly distinguished from chemotherapy-related fatigue or potential effects of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF). Pegfilgrastim has been reported to cause systemic symptoms such as malaise, myalgia, and decreased appetite in a nationwide post-marketing surveillance study conducted in Japan [25], which may have influenced patient-reported daytime sleepiness. Third, because only the first cycle of AC therapy was evaluated, the potential reduction of steroid-related adverse effects associated with steroid omission could not be assessed. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the efficacy of a steroid-sparing antiemetic regimen incorporating olanzapine in patients with early-stage breast cancer receiving dose-dense AC chemotherapy. Although the regimen evaluated in this study differed from those used in recent large-scale phase 3 trials, favorable CR rates were observed. The overall antiemetic efficacy was comparable to that of traditional steroid-containing regimens. However, individual patient-reported outcomes suggested that olanzapine alone may not provide sufficient control for all patients. Further studies are needed to identify predictors of antiemetic response and to optimize individualized antiemetic strategies, particularly in outpatient settings and dose-dense chemotherapy regimens.
