Training undergraduate students to incorporate new periodontal disease classification system
Authors: Mohan Kumar Pasupuleti, Gautami S. Penmetsa, NVS Sruthima Gottumukkala, KSV Ramesh, Vivek Bypalli, Mathala V. Lakshmi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:A significant update to the classification of periodontal and peri-implant disorders was made during the 2017 World Workshop organized by the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) and the American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) on the classification of periodontal and peri-implant disorders and conditions. The goal of this study was to see how effectively a concentrated training program that helped undergraduate dentistry students integrate the new method of classification for periodontal disease into their clinical practice performed.MATERIALS AND METHODS:An observational study was conducted with 139 dental students. They were trained on the 2017 periodontal disease classification system through lectures, clinical exercises, and case discussions. Assessments were performed before and after the training to measure students’ familiarity, comfort, and clinical performance in identifying periodontal disorders.RESULTS:Following the training, there was a substantial improvement in students’ understanding of the new classification system, with an average increase of 30% in accurate replies on written exams (P < 0.05). Clinical observations showed that students were more confident when using the new classification criteria during patient evaluations and more accurate when identifying periodontal problems. Students expressed great satisfaction with the training format in their feedback, with the majority stating that they understood and could use the upgraded system more effectively in clinical situations.CONCLUSION:This study demonstrates that undergraduate dental students’ theoretical comprehension and clinical practice may be considerably improved by coordinated, multimodal training that assists them in integrating the 2017 periodontal disease classification system.

Introduction

A classification scheme is necessary for clinicians and scientists to diagnose and treat patients and investigate the etiology, pathogenesis, and treatment of periodontal diseases. Common classification systems also provide a common language for clear communication between clinicians, patients, and researchers.[1] The American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) and the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) 2017 used several review papers and consensus reports to develop a new classification systemfor periodontal diseases.[1] In 2017, the American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) and the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP) collaborated to develop a revised and updated classification system for periodontal diseases. This new system, announced at a consensus meeting in Chicago, represents a fundamental shift in the classification of periodontal illnesses. To improve understanding, diagnosis, and treatment of periodontal diseases, the new classification system was based on a thorough review of current research, expert consensus, and a careful study of clinical data.[2]

The new periodontal disease classification system is designed to evolve with advancements in research and clinical practices. It incorporates biomarkers, which aid in early, accurate diagnosis by revealing information on the immune response, microbial composition, and genetic factors. This personalized approach enhances early intervention, improves treatment predictions, and allows for more tailored therapies based on a patient’s biological profile.[3]

The new methodology for identifying periodontal disorders introduces a complicated staging and grading structure, providing a comprehensive approach to diagnosing and treating periodontitis. The staging procedure progresses from Stage I (incipient periodontitis), which has little disease and minor attachment loss, to Stage IV (advanced periodontitis), which has widespread attachment loss, significant tissue destruction, and potential tooth movement. This innovation enables doctors to determine the degree and scope of periodontal disease. In addition to staging, the technique has a grading component that divides disease risk into three categories: low risk (Grade A), moderate risk (Grade B), and high risk (Grade C). The grading system considers the patient’s age, smoking habits, rate of illness progression, and presence of systemic problems, such as diabetes. In addition to providing clinicians with a clear picture of the illness’s current condition, this multidimensional framework supports in disease prediction by guiding treatment decisions and monitoring plans based on each patient’s specific risk profile.[45] These updates aim to support the practice of clinical periodontology by prompting dental providers to comprehensively evaluate the severity, case complexity, and prognosis of each periodontitis case in an individualized manner while communicating disease status with a staging scheme that can be more easily understood by patients.[678]

The purpose of the study was to ascertain if students could effectively apply the novel approach in clinical settings, such as recognizing the stages of periodontal disease and appreciating the significance of grading. The study’s objectives were to pinpoint their knowledge gaps, provide guidance for curriculum enhancements, and eventually enhance their diagnostic skills in periodontal care, guaranteeing that they are equipped to apply the most recent evidence-based procedures in general dentistry practice once they graduate.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The purpose of this observational, non-interventional study is to evaluate how well undergraduate dentistry students are taught to use the new classification system for periodontal disease. Between June 2022 and June 2023, a pilot study was conducted in the Department of Periodontics and Implantology at Vishnu Dental College in Bhimavaram. The study was conducted over the course of one academic semester (approximately 4-5 months) to provide sufficient time for training and observation.

Study participants and sampling

As the students are assumed to be conversant with fundamental periodontal principles, the study will involve a cohort of undergraduate dental students, ideally those in their third or fourth year of a five-year dentistry school.

Inclusion criteria are students currently enrolled in the dental program, students who have completed basic periodontal courses and clinical practice. Exclusion criteria are students who have received prior formal training on the 2017 periodontal classification system. Students who have already undertaken advanced periodontal research or clinical training beyond the undergraduate level were observed. Convenience sampling was used in this study to choose participants from among Vishnu Dental College undergraduate dental program students. Participants were selected based on their availability and willingness to participate in the training sessions due to practical limitations, including time constraints and access to students enrolled in the course. This sampling strategy depends on finding students who were available and able to attend the planned program rather than having a set sample size.

Data collection tool and technique

The study’s main outcome measures include assessing how well students understand the 2017 periodontal classification system, as determined by their pre- and post-test scores, and how well they can use this knowledge in clinical settings, as determined by looking at how they classified cases during patient evaluations or simulated cases. Secondary outcome measures include assessing student self-reported confidence in diagnosing periodontal diseases using the new system before and after the training, through Likert-scale questionnaires, as well as gathering feedback on the clarity and effectiveness of the training program via a post-training survey.

The classification system for periodontal disease will be introduced and explained through a planned, systematic training program consisting of two to three sessions, each lasting 1.5 to 2 hours. The training will be delivered over a period of one week, with sessions occurring twice a week. The content will include lectures on the key elements of the classification, such as the classification of peri-implant diseases, the differentiation between aggressive and chronic periodontitis, and the staging and grading of periodontitis.

Following the lectures, participatory workshops will provide students with hands-on experience applying the classification system to case scenarios, both textual and digital. In case-based discussions, students will classify and identify periodontal disorders using real clinical cases or simulated patient data, promoting interactive learning and critical thinking. This structured approach will ensure students are well equipped to apply the new classification in clinical practice. Students’ knowledge and understanding will be evaluated via a pre- and post-test consisting of multiple-choice (MCQ) and short-answer questions that measure their ability to apply the new classification system.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Ethics Committee Board approved it and exempted it from ethical review. To familiarize the students with the new classification system framed by the American Academy of Periodontology (AAP) and the European Federation of Periodontology (EFP), pre- and post-training assessments were administered to evaluate students’ knowledge and confidence in applying the new system.

Ethical considerations for the observational study included obtaining informed consent from participants, ensuring the confidentiality of their data, and minimizing harm during the training. Students were informed of their right to withdraw at any time, and the study was transparent, with clear communication of its goals and methods. Fair selection of participants was ensured, and the study received approval from an institutional ethics review board. Additionally, participants were debriefed after the study to maintain transparency and trust.

Statistical analysis method

The demographic information of the participants, as well as their pre- and post-test results, will be collected using descriptive statistics, such as means, standard deviations, and frequencies. Depending on the data distribution, the Chi-square test for independence was the statistical test used for all of these responses because the association between two categorical variables (correct/incorrect responses and before/after training or classification systems) was tested. The P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant between the two classifications in the accuracy of disease diagnosis.

Results

The training program, which included a unique method for identifying periodontal disease, involved 139 dental students. Assessments were conducted before and after the training to determine the students’ understanding and comfort level with the system.

Confidence levels

Students reported a significant increase in their confidence in applying the new classification system after the training. On a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 represented “not confident” and 5 represented “very confident,” the average self-reported confidence score increased from [0] to [3] (P < 0.718). Notably [53.2%] of students rated themselves as “very confident” after completing the training.

Clinical application

When tested on their ability to use the classification method in simulated clinical circumstances, students exhibited [53.2%] proper application of the system during post-training evaluations. However, diversity in clinical exposure was seen, with some students unable to apply the approach to real patients due to a scarcity of relevant instances throughout the observation time.

Overall satisfaction and feedback

In terms of overall satisfaction,[74] of students said that the training program fulfilled their expectations, with the majority (53.2%) feeling better equipped to apply the new classification system in clinical practice. Students’ qualitative response emphasized the value of the training materials, but also noted that further hands-on practice and clinical exposure would help to reinforce the system’s implementation.

The results demonstrated significant improvements in students’ knowledge and confidence, as reflected by increased correct responses on assessments and higher self-reported confidence levels. Clinically, while students showed improved ability to apply the system, variability in clinical exposure limited its full application in real patient scenarios. These findings highlight the program’s potential to enhance students’ clinical competencies, though further clinical exposure may be necessary for reinforcing the system’s practical application.

Discussion

A key objective of the 2017 World Workshop was to develop a classification system for periodontal diseases that is practical and easy to implement in general dental practice, where the majority of periodontal diagnoses and treatments occur over 95%, in fact. The system was specifically designed to be user-friendly and clinically relevant for general practitioners, who may not have specialized training in periodontology. It emphasizes clear, actionable criteria that can be applied in everyday clinical settings, such as determining disease stage, severity, and risk of progression based on simple clinical measures like probing depths and attachment loss. By creating a classification that is both comprehensive and straightforward, the workshop aimed to empower general dentists to accurately diagnose, monitor, and treat periodontal disease, improving patient outcomes and promoting early intervention across diverse patient populations. This approach also fosters better communication between general practitioners and specialists, ensuring a more cohesive and coordinated care model.[910]

In 2018, Marlow et al.[11] evaluated the impact of calibration sessions on diagnosis and treatment planning using case-based clinical scenarios. They found that calibrated periodontal faculty demonstrated better agreement and more accurate diagnoses and treatment plans than other groups. These findings align with the present study, where 74% of students showed 53.2% correct application of the new periodontal disease classification system during post-training evaluations, indicating that structured training improves clinical application and diagnostic accuracy, similar to the calibration process observed in Marlow et al.’s study.

In 2021, Ravidà et al.[12] assessed whether clinicians well trained in periodontics consistently applied the new 2017 World Workshop periodontitis classification to severe periodontitis cases. The study found moderate concordance, with clinicians agreeing with the gold-standard panel in six of nine cases. Similarly, in the present study, 74% of students reported that the training program met their expectations, and the majority (53.2%) felt more prepared to apply the new classification system in clinical practice. These findings suggest that structured training programs, like the one in the current study, can enhance students’ ability to effectively use the new classification system, similar to the moderate concordance observed among trained clinicians in Ravidà et al.’s research.

In 2021, Marini et al.[13] evaluated the consistency and accuracy of the periodontitis staging and grading classification system, finding high consistency over time and moderate consistency between examiners. The study also revealed near-perfect accuracy for stage classification and moderate accuracy for grade and extent, suggesting a need for further training efforts for general dentists. These findings align with the present study, where dental students demonstrated significant improvements in diagnosing periodontal disease using the 2017 classification system guidelines. Both studies highlight the importance of structured training to improve diagnostic accuracy and consistency in periodontal disease classification.

In 2021, Abou-Arraj.[14] evaluated the level of agreement among predoctoral and postgraduate students in diagnosing and treating periodontal conditions. The study found that students successfully distinguished between health and disease, proposed appropriate treatment plans, but tended to over diagnose milder conditions. Similarly, students in the present study demonstrated accurate diagnoses of periodontal disease using the 2017 classification system guidelines, further supporting the effectiveness of structured training in improving diagnostic accuracy, as seen in both groups.

Multiple studies highlight the effectiveness of structured training in improving periodontal disease diagnosis. Marlow et al. found that calibrated faculty demonstrated better diagnostic accuracy, similar to the present study, where students showed correct application of the 2017 classification system. Ravidà et al. observed moderate concordance among trained clinicians, paralleling the present study’s findings of increased student preparedness. Marini et al. noted the need for further training to improve consistency, which aligns with the present study’s improvements in diagnostic skills, while Abou-Arraj et al.[11121314] reinforced the importance of training in achieving accurate diagnoses.

These findings show that in order to ensure that future dentists are equipped to use the most modern diagnostic frameworks in periodontal therapy, equivalent training programs should be widely included into dental curriculum.[15]

Limitations and recommendation

The study includes a number of limitations that may affect its results. First, self-report bias may play a role, as students may overestimate their understanding or confidence in applying the new classification system, particularly in post-training surveys when they may be inclined to provide socially desirable responses. Additionally, the study only looked at one academic cohort, which limited how broadly the results might be applied because kids from different schools and educational levels can have different backgrounds or be unable to pick up and employ this approach.

To lessen bias, future research should employ random sampling with a bigger, more varied sample. Long-term follow-up tests can gauge retention over time, and a control group would aid in separating the training’s effects. To more accurately represent real-world abilities, a range of assessment techniques, such as practical tests, should be employed. Pre-testing should be used to account for prior knowledge, and continuing instruction and support, such as refresher courses, will aid students in putting the classification system into practice.

Future perspectives

One strategy for teaching undergraduate students to use the new periodontal classification system is to incorporate it into the dental curriculum through case-based education and clinical exercises, thereby allowing students to use it in real-life situations. The use of digital tools, such as 3D imaging and AI, can improve diagnostic accuracy and keep students up to date with the latest technology. Fostering interdisciplinary collaboration between dental specialties allows for a deeper understanding of periodontology, and continuing education programs keep students up to date on future developments. Furthermore, familiarizing students with both local and global traditions ensures that periodontal treatment is provided consistently and universally across the world.

Conclusion

Training undergraduate dentistry students to use the new periodontal disease classification system is critical to the future of periodontal treatment. With this revised method, dental practitioners will be more prepared to accurately identify and manage periodontal diseases, resulting in better patient care. The future of periodontal education is a balanced strategy that combines academic knowledge with clinical practice, takes use of technology improvements, and encourages ongoing learning.
