Negative social determinants of health are linked to lung cancer screening underutilization
Authors: Vuong V. Do, Eduardo R. Núñez, Fatima G. Wilder, Nhung Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to examine the association between social determinants of health (SDOH) and lung cancer screening (LCS) utilization.MethodsWe analyzed data from 15,957 LCS-eligible individuals in the 2022 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System survey. Primary outcomes included ever having (lifetime) LCS and meeting LCS recommendations (i.e., annual LCS). Multivariable logistic regression models examined associations between LCS outcomes and 12 adverse SDOH factors, controlling for covariates (i.e., demographics, diagnosis of asthma/COPD, and perceived general health status).ResultsLCS-eligible individuals with more adverse SDOH had lower odds of ever having LCS and being up to date. Those with ≥5 adverse SDOH had the lowest odds of having lifetime LCS (AOR = 0.58, 95%CI: 0.45–0.74) and meeting LCS recommendations (AOR = 0.49, 95%CI: 0.36–0.65) compared to those with none. Life dissatisfaction, lack of social and emotional support, housing insecurity, lack of health insurance, and cost as a barrier for needed medical care were independently associated with lower LCS uptake.ConclusionsHaving more adverse SDOH was associated with a lower likelihood of having lifetime LCS and meeting the recommendation, with life dissatisfaction, lack of social and emotional support, housing insecurity, lack of health insurance, and medical care cost being independently associated factors.

Introduction

Lung cancer screening (LCS) with an annual low-dose computed tomography has the potential to decrease mortality from lung cancer by approximately 20% [1,2]. Yet, more than a decade since LCS was first recommended by the US Preventive Services Task Force, only 5–18% of the eligible population in the US has been screened [3]. Significant disparities in LCS exist, and some subgroups have low rates of early diagnosis, treatment, and survival related to lung cancer [4]. Prior work has identified several factors associated with LCS, including age, sex, race, ethnicity, geography, and socioeconomic status [5–7]. However, beyond geographic and demographic factors, little is known about the association between social determinants of health (SDOH) and LCS utilization, and it is unclear to what extent each individual and multiple SDOH factors influence people’s ability to participate in LCS. A better understanding of the underlying relationship between SDOH and LCS is critical to address structural inequities related to LCS uptake.

In 2022, the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) survey introduced a new optional module, “Social Determinants of Health and Health Equity” (SD/HE), which measures life satisfaction, social and emotional support, social isolation or loneliness, employment stability, receipt of food stamps/Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, food security, housing security, stability of utility services (e.g., gas, water), transportation access, and mental well-being [8]. Utilizing a nationally representative sample of US adults, this study aims to examine the association between the number of adverse SDOH and ever having LCS and meeting the LCS guidelines. We hypothesize that people with more adverse SDOH would have lower odds of ever having a LCS as well as meeting the LCS recommendation (i.e., annual LCS for eligible individuals), and that specific individual adverse SDOH are independently associated with lower odds of having LCS outcomes.

Methods

Data source and participants

We used the public and deidentified dataset of the 2022 BRFSS survey, a telephone survey (i.e., landline or cell phone) that collects data from over 400,000 US residents aged 18 years or older regarding their health-related behaviors, chronic health conditions, and use of preventive services in all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and participating US territories [9]. The BRFSS has core modules and optional modules. The core modules include standard questions for all states and territories, while some states and territories collect optional modules. The SD/HE module was optional and collected by most states, except for 11 states (i.e., Arkansas, Colorado, Hawaii, Illinois, Louisiana, Maryland, Michigan, Nebraska, New York, North Dakota, and South Dakota). Detailed information on the BRFSS survey design, sampling, data collection, and weights is described elsewhere [9,10]. The study was exempt from the institutional review board given its use of publicly available de-identified data. The analytical sample included adults who were eligible for LCS (e.g., ages 50 to 80 years who self-reported currently smoking or quitting within the last 15 years with at least a 20-pack-year smoking history).

Measures

We examined two binary (yes/no) LCS outcomes, including (i) ever having (lifetime) LCS and (ii) being up to date with USPSTF LCS recommendations (i.e., had the most recent LCS within the past year). The primary exposure variable was the adverse SDOH score calculated from ten question items of the SD/HE module, plus two additional SDOH measures from the BRFSS Health Care Access Core Section (health insurance coverage and cost as a barrier for needed medical care) (Appendix 1). We followed scoring guidelines from prior studies, with each of the twelve items being scored as 0 or 1, so the total score ranged from 0 to 12 for each respondent [8,11]. The number of adverse SDOH was categorized as 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and ≥5 [8]. Other independent variables include demographic factors, such as age group (50–59, 60–69, 70–80), sex (male, female), race/ethnicity (Hispanic, Non-Hispanic American Indian/Alaska Native, Non-Hispanic Asian American/Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic multiracial, Non-Hispanic White), education (less than high school, graduated high school, attended college or technical school, graduated from college or technical school), income (<$25,000, $25,000 to <$35,000, $35,000 to <$50,000, $50,000 to <$100,000, $100,000 to <$200,000, ≥$200,000), marital status (married, divorced/widowed/separated, never married, a member of an unmarried couple), and metropolitan status (yes, no). We also included smoking status (current, former), lifetime diagnosis of asthma or COPD (yes, no), and perceived general health status (excellent/very good, good, fair, poor) as covariates.

Statistical analysis

The BRFSS uses design weighting and iterative proportional fitting to ensure representative data. Survey weights were applied in all analyses to account for the complex survey design of the BRFSS study. We adjusted the weights based on the questionnaire modules and the data sets used, following guidelines from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [10]. We calculated weighted prevalence estimates with 95% confidence interval (CI) for lifetime LCS and meeting the LCS recommendation by sociodemographic subgroup. We used multivariable logistic regression models to examine the associations between the LCS outcomes and the number of adverse SDOH. In addition, to examine the associations of individual adverse SDOH with each LCS outcome, we conducted a full model fit by including all adverse SDOH measures simultaneously to account for correlations, controlling for covariates. In addition, given that smoking status (current vs. former) may be influenced by certain SDOH factors, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by re-estimating the regression models without adjusting for smoking status to assess potential overadjustment bias in estimating the associations between SDOH and lung cancer screening behaviors. A total of 15,957 participants who had complete data for the variables of interest were included in the analysis. Data were analyzed using Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC). All hypothesis tests were two-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, LCS-eligible individuals who reported more adverse SDOH had a lower prevalence of lifetime LCS and meeting the LCS recommendation compared to those with fewer adverse SDOH (Table 1). In particular, the proportion of lifetime LCS was 33.2% (95% CI: 30.9%-35.5%) among participants with no adverse SDOH and 34.7% (95% CI: 31.5%-38%) among those with one adverse SDOH, compared to 25.1% (95% CI: 21.8%-28.8%) among those with five or more adverse SDOH. The proportion of meeting the LCS recommendation reduced from 20% (95% CI: 18%-22%) among participants with no adverse SDOH to 13.2% (95% CI: 10.5%-16.5%) among those with four adverse SDOH and 13% (95% CI: 10.8%-15.7%) among those with five or more adverse SDOH.

The odds of having lifetime LCS among LCS-eligible individuals decreased as the number of adverse SDOH increased. The odds amongst those with two adverse SDOH was 27% (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.59, 0.90) lower than that of those with none (Table 2). Those with five or more adverse SDOH had the lowest odds of ever having received a LCS (AOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.45, 0.74). A similar pattern was observed for being up to date with LCS recommendations, with the AOR ranging from 0.72 (95% CI: 0.56, 0.92) among those with 2 adverse SDOH to 0.49 (95% CI: 0.36, 0.65) among those with 5 or more adverse SDOH compared to those with no adverse SDOH.

Regarding the relationship with individual adverse SDOH, lack of social and emotional support (AOR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.70, 0.98), housing insecurity (AOR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.60, 0.97), and lack of health insurance (AOR = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.27, 0.56) were significantly associated with lower odds of having a lifetime LCS. In addition, life dissatisfaction (AOR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.56, 0.99), lack of social support (AOR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.61, 0.93), lack of health insurance (AOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.17, 0.52), and cost as barrier for needed medical care (AOR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.54, 0.94) were associated with not meeting the LCS recommendations.

Sensitivity analyses excluding smoking status from the regression models yielded associations between SDOH and lung cancer screening outcomes that were nearly identical to those observed in the primary models (Appendix 2).

Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of US adults, LCS-eligible adults with more adverse SDOH are less likely to have a lifetime LCS or be up to date with yearly LCS recommendations. Furthermore, we identified individual adverse SDOH factors independently associated with lower LCS engagement, including life dissatisfaction, lack of social and emotional support, housing insecurity, lack of health insurance, and financial constraints. These findings could empower policymakers, hospital systems, and providers to use targeted interventions and public health strategies that prioritize resource allocation and structural changes to mitigate the gaps in LCS for socially disadvantaged groups.

While housing insecurity, lack of health insurance, and financial constraints have been linked to lower completion of LCS referrals, our study is the first to demonstrate this in a nationally representative sample and included individuals who may never have been referred for initial LCS [12]. Expanding healthcare coverage and subsidizing screening costs may potentially improve access for disadvantaged groups [3]. Healthcare providers should consider healthcare barriers that disproportionately affect underserved populations, such as financial constraints, lack of awareness, or geographic limitations, to deliver more equitable and effective services for LCS [13,14]. Our study highlights the importance of addressing unmet social needs to improve LCS uptake. Future interventions that leverage newly mandated social needs screenings could be key to reducing inequities and improving access to LCS.

We found that life dissatisfaction and lack of social and emotional support were associated with lower LCS uptake. This is particularly important, as smoking-related stigma and fear of cancer are known barriers to screening. Individuals without social support may struggle to overcome these concerns, making them even more vulnerable to additional obstacles in accessing LCS [15]. Thus, evidence-based interventions and strategies that address social connection and develop environments in communities that are safe spaces for relationship building and support for dealing with loneliness and isolation can be beneficial, while integrating LCS into community health initiatives might make screening more accessible in communities that need it most [13].

Our study has several limitations. First, institutionalized populations are not included in the survey, and 11 states did not collect data on the SD/HE module, which limits the generalizability of the results to the broader US population. Second, self-reported data are subject to recall and social desirability bias. Third, the survey did not include other SDOH that can affect people’s health and LCS uptake, such as discrimination, neighborhood safety, and built environment. In addition, we were unable to evaluate education as part of the cumulative adverse SDOH score, which may underestimate the overall impact of social disadvantage on LCS outcomes. Thus, future research should incorporate a more comprehensive set of SDOH indicators as they evaluate the additive effect of SDOH on LCS. Finally, the cross-sectional design of the survey limited the examination of causation between SDOH and LCS outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides further evidence that demonstrates the existence and degree of disparities in LCS among socially disadvantaged people. More adverse SDOH was negatively associated with lower LCS uptake, with some adverse SDOH factors independently associated with a lower likelihood of having a lifetime LCS and meeting LCS recommendations. The finding underscores the need for multi-level interventions addressing both social and individual barriers to increase access to LCS, which in turn can enhance early detection and reduce lung cancer mortality, advancing health equity for the public.
