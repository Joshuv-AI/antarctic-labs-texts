A web-based deep learning cascade for automated detection and quantification of marginal bone loss
Authors: Niha Adnan, Syed Muhammad Faizan Ahmed, Ayesha Nooruddin, Ali Sadiq, Aamna Khalid, Muhammad Haseeb, Muhammad Huzaifa Ghori, Fahad Umer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMarginal Bone Loss (MBL) around dental implants is a key indicator of peri-implantitis. Existing literature evaluates MBL at a single time point, neglecting baseline comparisons and the temporal nature of disease progression. Conventional radiographic assessments remain limited by subjectivity in angulation and magnification. This study aimed to develop and validate a cascade of six Deep Learning (DL) models for automated detection and quantification of MBL on sequential Periapical (PA) radiographs. It further assessed the system’s accuracy against dental specialists and deployed it as a web-based tool.MethodsA total of 6,453 PAs of dental implants were collected from institutional centers and an open-access dataset. After applying exclusion criteria, 2,960 PAs remained. Sample size calculation was not applicable due to the multi-model architecture; therefore, each model was trained iteratively until convergence was achieved using these PAs. This Artificial Intelligence (AI)-cascade was designed to perform: rotation correction, implant alignment, Region of Interest (ROI) detection, implant classification, mesial-distal detection, and MBL estimation. Each model was assessed using performance metrics appropriate for its specific task. Thereafter, MBL was estimated on forty pairs of PAs by periodontists and AI-cascade; these were compared against a Reference Standard (RS) established by dental specialists. Agreement between AI-derived and RS measurements was evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test (p < 0.05).ResultsRotation correction model achieved 98.0% accuracy and an F1 score of 99.0%. Implant alignment model demonstrated a Mean Absolute Error of 3.02 degrees. ROI detection model achieved a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 99.4%, with 98.3% precision and 98.2% recall. Implant classification model achieved a mAP of 93.9%, with 100% precision and 98.0% recall. Mesial-distal detection model achieved 78.0% accuracy and an F1 score of 78.0%. Finally, the MBL estimation model attained a mAP of 75.0%, with 73.0% precision and 69.8% recall. No significant differences were found in MBL severity scores between the RS and AI-cascade (p > 0.05).ConclusionsThe AI-cascade demonstrated reliable performance in detecting and quantifying MBL across sequential PAs, comparable to expert evaluation. Its deployment as a web-based application enables objective peri-implant monitoring, representing a significant advancement in clinical translation of AI for implant diagnostics.Keywords: Dental implants, Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Convolutional Neural Networks, Dental radiography, Tooth loss, Bone loss

Introduction

Dental implants are a widely accepted solution for restoring aesthetics and function in both partially and fully edentulous patients [1]. However, as with any treatment, complications and failures are inevitable. Mechanical complications are often predictable and relatively straightforward to manage [2]. In contrast, biological complications, such as peri-implantitis, present significant challenges due to their complex nature and initial subtle presentation, making early identification difficult [3]. The diagnosis of peri-implantitis involves certain clinical and radiographic features. Clinically, it presents as bleeding on probing, swelling and pus discharge around implants, while radiographically, it appears as Marginal Bone Loss (MBL) [4, 5].

MBL is considered to be a multifactorial phenomenon and an important prognostic factor for peri-implantitis [6]. It can be assessed using two-dimensional radiographs, such as Periapicals (PAs) and Orthopantomograms (OPG). Three-dimensional imaging modalities like Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) can also be utilized, but the high cost and increased radiation exposure associated with them limits their routine use [7]. Therefore, clinicians mostly rely on PAs taken at varying time points to determine disease progression over time by comparing the follow-up measurements with the baseline [8]. These measurements are somewhat arbitrary and cannot be standardized due to variations such as magnification and angulation of sequential PAs. Automated systems such as Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based softwares for analyzing PAs may help address these errors [9].

However, existing literature on AI-based detection of MBL only detects bone loss at a single time point without comparing it with baseline images, thus overlooking the temporal nature of disease progression [10–13]. Furthermore, despite promising results, the clinical deployment of such algorithms remains limited. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no publicly available software exists capable of automated detection, quantification, and comparison of MBL at multiple time points.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop an AI-based Deep Learning (DL) automated system for detecting, quantifying, and sequentially comparing MBL in PAs to assess peri-implant health over time. The system also incorporates correction mechanisms for angulation and magnification errors. Additionally, the study aims to evaluate the accuracy of the system in comparison with dental specialists and to deploy it as a web-based application [14].

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This validation study was conducted after receiving exemption from the institution's Ethical Review Committee (ERC #: 2023–8434-24001). To ensure anonymity, PAs were de-identified and serial numbers were assigned to them. This experiment is reported adhering to the Minimum Information about Clinical Artificial Intelligence Modeling: the MI-CLAIM checklist [15].

Instead, an empirical approach was adopted, whereby each model was trained iteratively until model convergence was achieved. Data collection

A total of 6,453 PAs were collected from multiple institutional centers and an open-source dataset [16]. For institutional data, all PAs were acquired using standardized exposure settings of 70 kV and 0.70 mAs. After applying exclusion criteria, a final total of 2,960 PAs (before data augmentation) were utilized for model training. Details of the dataset distribution and exclusion process can be found in Fig. 1. Notably a single PA was utilized across multiple algorithm training tasks within the AI-cascade.

From institutional centers, 2,088 PAs were obtained, of which 740 were excluded based on predefined criteria, resulting in 1,348 usable PAs. Annotations

The PAs were annotated based on the algorithm being trained and the specific task it was designed to perform. Annotations were carried out by two general dentists (A.N, A.K) with at least two years of clinical experience. The annotators were trained and calibrated by a specialist restorative dentist (N.A) with five years of clinical experience prior to the commencement of labelling. All annotations by the first annotator were checked by the second and vice versa. The specialist restorative dentist conducted a final review of all annotations before utilizing them for training.

AI-cascade workflow

The AI-cascade workflow was divided into two sections with a total of six segments, leading to the development of the ‘MBL estimation system,’ as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Overview of the AI-cascade workflow for automated MBL estimation in PAs. The system includes two main phases: (1) sequential image registration, involving rotation correction (ResNet50), implant alignment (custom CNN regressor), and ROI detection (YOLOv8s); and (2) MBL quantification, comprising implant classification (YOLOv11n), mesial-distal detection (ResNet50), and MBL estimation (YOLOv8s-seg). PAs: Periapical radiographs, CNN: Convolutional Neural Network, YOLO: You Only Look Once, ROI: Region of Interest, MBL: Marginal Bone Loss, YOLOv8s-seg: YOLOv8s instance segmentation

The first section ‘sequential image registration’, and the second section ‘MBL quantification’, both comprised of three segments with each segment featuring a model trained for a specific task. The first three segments were used for (i) Rotation correction, (ii) Alignment and (iii) Region of Interest (ROI) detection of the PAs, respectively. The last three segments were used for (iv) Implant classification (v) Mesial-distal detection and (vi) MBL estimation across PAs, respectively. The parameters, hyperparameters and performance metrics of each individual model are presented in Table 1. All the proposed algorithms of this study were implemented in Python (version 3.11, Python Software Foundation) and can be accessed on GitHub upon reasonable request.

The first segment, the rotation correction model, involved training an AI algorithm to pre-process PAs by correcting any rotation errors in the dataset. The model utilized the ResNet50 algorithm and was fine-tuned for this classification task. The dataset was prepared by transforming 2,671 PAs to 90°, 270° and 180° angles, categorizing them as upright, right, left, and upside down, based on the implant orientation leading to a total of 10,684 augmented PAs.

The second segment, the implant alignment model focused on addressing angulation errors of the implant in the PAs. To train this model, annotations were performed using the LabelMe annotation tool [17]. The annotators carried out the labelling by drawing a line along the long axis of the implant relative to the vertical axis of the PA as shown in Fig. 3. This allowed for the calculation of implant’s angle (in degrees) relative to the vertical axis of the PA. A Python-based script was then used to quantify this deviation and automatically rotate the implant in the opposite direction to achieve vertical alignment. For instance, if an implant was tilted 13° relative to the vertical axis, it was anti-rotated by –13° to align parallel to the PA’s long axis. A custom Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) regressor algorithm was then trained on 2,371 aligned PAs which were augmented to 40,307 PAs through transformations such as rotations, shifts, zooms, and brightness. The trained model automatically rotated each PA to ensure that the implant was oriented in a perfectly upright (vertical) position. These vertically aligned implants were then passed to the third segment of the AI-cascade workflow.

A line was drawn along the long axis of the implant to calculate the implant angulation relative to the vertical axis of the PA

The third segment, the ROI detection model, was designed to identify implants in single PAs and to simultaneously reduce magnification errors in sequential PAs. To train this model, 2,960 PAs containing vertically aligned implants from the implant alignment model were used. For sequential PAs, one PA was designated as the reference image. In this reference PA, a bounding box encompassing the entire implant from top (implant platform) to bottom (implant apex), called a ROI box, was created using the LabelMe tool [17] as shown in Fig. 4. Identical ROIs with matching dimensions were subsequently applied to the corresponding sequential PAs. The implants in these PAs were standardized to the size of the ROI using controlled zoom-in and zoom-out adjustments. This process ensured consistent implant dimensions across sequential images, thereby eliminating magnification errors. As a result, implants could be accurately overlapped and registered across sequential PAs taken at various time points. This annotated dataset was then used to train the YOLOv8s object detection algorithm.

A Platform-switched implant; B Platform-matched implant. Red bounding boxes denotes Region of Interest (ROI); yellow points indicate the coronal edge and green points indicate apical edge of the implants. The most coronal edge was considered as the top of the implant. The apical point was the deepest point of contact between the bone and the implant

The fourth segment, the Implant classification model, aimed to classify implants into specific brands, including Zimmer©, Straumann©, Nobel©, Bego© and Dentis SQ©. A total of 2,179 PAs were annotated using the LabelMe annotation tool [17]. ROI bounding boxes were drawn around each implant, and annotators then labeled the corresponding implant brand. The dataset comprised 1,013 Zimmer©, 504 Straumann©, 484 Nobel©, 84 Bego©, and 94 Dentis SQ©implants [16]. For PAs collected from clinical institutions, implant brands were verified using patient records, whereas PAs from publicly available datasets were pre-organized into folders labeled according to implant brands, ensuring correct labeling. These annotated images were then used to train the YOLOv11n object detection algorithm.

The fifth segment, the mesial-distal detection model, focused on the determination of the mesial and distal aspects of the implant. A ResNet50-based algorithm was fine-tuned using 1,613 PAs. The dataset was organized into two folders: one labeled ‘distal’, containing PAs where the distal side of the implant appeared on the right, and the other labeled ‘mesial’, containing PAs where the mesial side of the implant appeared on the right. This categorization allowed the model to learn and accurately distinguish between the mesial and distal sides of the implant.

The sixth and the final segment, the MBL estimation model focused on the detection and quantification of MBL in each PA. To train this model, a total of 1,625 ROI PAs, which had been detected and cropped using the previously trained ROI detector model, were annotated using the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) [18]. Annotators were tasked to draw segmentation masks over the areas of bone loss within the ROI. These annotations served as the foundation for training the YOLOv8s instance segmentation (YOLOv8s-seg) model.

All these trained AI models were combined to form a cascade system, which was then integrated into a web-based application for use by dentists [14].

Statistical analysis of the AI-cascade

The performance of the AI-cascade was evaluated according to the type of task as shown in Table 1. For classification tasks, accuracy and F1 score were evaluated. For object detection tasks, mean Average Precision (mAP) at an Intersection Over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5, along with precision and recall, were calculated. For the regression task, Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was used to assess performance. All metrics were computed using Python (version 3.11, Python Software Foundation) during the training and validation phases.

Reference Standard vs MBL estimation model

To assess the performance of the MBL estimation model, 40 pairs of sequential PAs (pre- and post-loading) were analyzed and compared with Reference Standard (RS) dataset. Considering the full length of the implants to be 100% the authors utilized a 0–100% scale for evaluation. Such an assumption is justified because it provides a standardized reference framework for comparison across different PAs, enabling the model to evaluate bone loss proportionally along the implant body. Expressing bone loss as a percentage of the total implant length ensured that all implants, regardless of their actual size, were represented on a uniform relative scale.

A standardized (0–100%) scale was overlaid on each PA to match the output format of the MBL system, which evaluates bone loss along the length of the implant using this graduated scale. This ensured a fair comparison by enabling accurate evaluation and eliminating any advantage the AI system might have from automated scaling. MBL severity was categorized based on the following criteria and these numerical values were converted into ordinal categories: 1 = Normal (≤ 10%), 2 = Early (> 10–25%), 3 = Moderate (> 25–50%), 4 = Severe (> 50%) [13].

The RS dataset was initially created by two general dentists (A.N, A.K) each with at least two years of clinical experience. They independently assessed MBL at both the mesial and distal sides of the implants. Their assessments were reviewed and consolidated through consensus by a specialist restorative dentist (N.A). This dataset was then further evaluated by two periodontists (A.S, K.K) with at least 5 and 15 years of clinical experience to create the final RS dataset which served as the benchmark for evaluating the MBL estimation system. The PAs were viewed on an HP EliteBook 840 G5 (Intel® Core™ i5-7300U CPU @ 2.60 GHz, 1920 × 1080 resolution) by all the assessors. The same 40 pairs of PAs were also processed through the MBL estimation system, and outputs were recorded alongside the RS in a structured Excel sheet.

A Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the evaluations of the RS with the MBL estimation model. All statistical analyses for this comparison were conducted using SPSS version 25 (SPSS Inc., USA) and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

AI-cascade workflow

The rotation correction model achieved 98.0% accuracy and an F1 score of 99.0%. The implant alignment model demonstrated a MAE of 3.02 degrees. The ROI detection model achieved a mAP of 99.4%, with 98.3% precision and 98.2% recall. The implant classification model achieved a mAP of 93.9%, with 100% precision and 98.0% recall. The mesial-distal detection model achieved 78.0% accuracy and an F1 score of 78.0%. Finally, the MBL estimation model attained a mAP of 75.0%, with 73.0% precision and 69.8% recall. Discussion

Implant health is assessed using both clinical examination and radiographic analysis, with MBL being a critical radiographic marker for peri-implantitis [19]. While AI models exist to estimate MBL on PAs at a single time point, accurate diagnosis and effective monitoring require temporal assessment across sequential PAs. Currently, AI solutions that measure MBL changes longitudinally are lacking. The current study seeks to bridge this gap by developing a web-based tool designed to detect and compare MBL across follow-up PAs [14]. The objectives of this study proved to be more complex than anticipated, presenting challenges that required a more advanced approach. A major challenge was the lack of standardization in PAs, including differences in angulation, alignment, and magnifications. Therefore, a cascading AI system was developed, with each segment designed to address specific challenges associated with radiographic data.

The AI-cascade was structured as a two-part AI pipeline. Each part of the AI-cascade involved training a separate AI model for a specific task, with datasets annotated separately to meet each model’s requirements. The output from one model produced a corrected dataset, which was then annotated and used to train the next in the sequence. This stepwise approach resulted in multiple datasets and multiple models, each forming a link in a continuous chain, which was essential in creating the web-based tool [14].

The first stage of the AI-cascade focused on image pre-processing to eliminate errors in the PAs. The initial dataset included images that were rotated in various directions; therefore, a rotation correction model was developed to address inconsistent image orientations. This model demonstrated high performance, consistent with the findings of Lin et al [20], who successfully employed a DL model for automated film mounting and rotation correction in dental radiographs. Subsequently an implant alignment model was used to vertically align implants along the central axis of the image to create a dataset of upright implants. The model also corrected any angulation discrepancies, providing a standardized dataset for further analysis. Without these corrections, MBL estimation across sequential PAs may compromise accuracy of results. The workflow then advanced to isolating implants from the PA image by defining a precise ROI by the ROI detection model. This ROI was bounded superiorly by the implant platform and inferiorly by the implant apex, ensuring that all measurements across PAs were referenced to a consistent baseline. To the authors' knowledge, there is no evidence in the literature of such preprocessing steps being applied to dental radiographs prior to downstream AI analysis.

The second stage of the AI-cascade begins by identifying implant brands using implant classification model which can assist clinicians in planning appropriate interventions, sourcing compatible implant components and improving treatment efficiency, especially when patient records are unavailable. This is clinically relevant, as studies have shown that implant thread design influences MBL, with different implant types exhibiting varying levels of bone loss. For instance, implants with micro-threads tend to preserve crestal bone, whereas those with macro-threads often show greater MBL [21, 22]. Prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility of implant identification using various AI architectures [23, 24]. For instance, a study demonstrated that DL approaches can effectively differentiate among implant systems, achieving reasonably high performance [25]. In contrast, the current model attained superior performance; likely attributed to the broader variety of implant types and larger dataset used in the current study.

The next stage involved identifying the mesial and distal areas in PAs separately, by the medial-distal detection model, since MBL around implants often presents differently in these regions [26, 27]; By quantifying MBL individually for each side, the model can provide more accurate and clinically useful measurements for treatment planning. The advantage of this work lies in the fact that no published model has yet been trained to explicitly label and quantify mesial versus distal aspects of an implant in PAs.

The final stage focused on segmenting MBL within the defined ROIs on preprocessed and corrected PAs by the MBL estimation model. Pixel-wise segmentation enabled the model to achieve higher accuracy than prior approaches that relied on bounding boxes. This method allowed precise detection of MBL while excluding background noise, offering a more detailed and clinically relevant analysis [13, 28]. Furthermore, the current study incorporated sequential radiographic comparisons, a critical feature for assessing disease progression, whereas previous studies did not account for any temporal changes and only detected MBL at a single time point [13, 29, 30]. This approach is novel and has not been described in literature thus far. A comparison was also made between the measurements generated by the MBL estimation model and those of the RS, which indicated that the model performs comparably to expert clinicians.

It is important to note that the evaluation dataset primarily consisted of PAs exhibiting early to moderate MBL, with relatively fewer cases of severe MBL. While this distribution reflects real-world clinical scenarios, where mild and moderate cases are more prevalent, the underrepresentation of severe cases may introduce prevalence bias potentially influencing the performance metrics. However, this imbalance can also be viewed positively, as early and moderate MBL are more challenging to detect radiographically than severe cases, thereby offering a more rigorous test of the model’s diagnostic capability. Additionally, although PAs with and without abutments were not separately used during model training, the evaluation dataset included an approximately equal distribution of both, and the model exhibited comparable accuracy across them, indicating that abutment presence or absence did not adversely affect performance.

A key strength of this study is the use of multicenter data which enhanced the generalizability of the system allowing it to perform accurately on PAs obtained from diverse populations. Another strength is its cascading methodology, which was developed out of necessity to meet the study’s complex objectives. Unlike previous research that has explored individual components of this workflow, this study integrates them into a unified, end-to-end diagnostic tool.

While the results are promising, this study has certain limitations. Clinical correlation data such as probing depth and bleeding on probing were not included, which are essential parameters for a comprehensive diagnosis of peri-implantitis. A formal sample size calculation was not performed due to the involvement of multiple models in the study. For PAs obtained from public datasets, radiographic metadata and corresponding machine settings were unavailable to the authors. Additionally, only the MBL estimation model was compared against RS. The remaining models within the AI-cascade were assessed using internal evaluation metrics only. Another limitation is that the cascading AI approach carries a potential risk of error propagation, whereby minor inaccuracies in earlier stages may influence the final MBL quantification. Finally, implant lengths were also not verified from clinical records, however, this cannot be considered a complete limitation, as a standardized 100% reference framework was employed to normalize implant dimensions and ensure relative bone loss estimation. The absence of failure analysis also limits insight into scenarios where the system might misclassify or underperform. Although data augmentation was applied to improve generalizability, overfitting cannot be entirely ruled out, especially given the specificity of training data to certain implant brands and imaging protocols.

Future advancements should aim to incorporate Electronic Health Record (EHR) and clinical parameters to create a multimodal diagnostic system. Additionally, enhancing the model to identify MBL defect angles and specific prosthetic restorations (e.g., screw-retained vs. cement-retained) would support clinical intervention planning. Further training could also enable the system to identify various types of implant restorations, multi-unit fixed dental prostheses, and different types of abutments. Future work should focus on training models with 3D data to improve diagnostic accuracy, as CBCT offers more precise detection of MBL compared to PAs, which remains the current clinical standard.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a novel AI-cascade workflow to address the critical challenge of detecting and quantifying MBL around dental implants on sequential PAs. The deployment of this workflow as a web-based application provides clinicians with a practical and accessible tool to monitor disease progression and enhance diagnostic precision [14]. By addressing existing gaps in implantology, this study lays the groundwork for advancing AI applications in dental diagnostics.
