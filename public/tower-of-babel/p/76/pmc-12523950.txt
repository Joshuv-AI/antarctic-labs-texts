Cross‐Sectional Radiological and Reconstructive Anatomy of the Paranasal Sinuses in Normal Mesaticephalic Dogs
Authors: Nimet Turgut
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough extensive research has been conducted on paranasal sinuses across various human populations and animal species, studies focusing on dogs remain relatively scarce.PurposeThis study aimed to characterise the anatomical and morphometric structure of canine paranasal sinuses utilising computed tomography (CT) cross‐sectional imaging and three‐dimensional (3D) reformatted models.MethodsIn this study, CT images of 14 clinically normal dogs (7 females, 7 males) with a mean age of 6.2 ± 4.3 years were analysed. The localisation and boundaries of the paranasal sinuses, as well as the orientation and morphology of sinonasal openings, were identified through cross‐sectional imaging, whereas morphometric characteristics were evaluated using 3D models.ResultsDespite variations in shape among all examined animals, the paranasal sinuses bilaterally consisted of maxillary recess (MR) and frontal sinus (FS) (rostral, medial and lateral). In one 4‐year‐old male, the rostral FS was absent. The MR exhibited a volume of 4 cm3 and a surface area of 26 cm2. The lateral FS was the largest, accounting for 90% of total sinus volume and 78% of total surface area in both sexes, whereas the medial FS was the smallest, with respective ratios of 3% and 8%. The MR was identified as the most symmetrically positioned sinus, whereas the medial FS displayed the highest degree of asymmetry. In females, the right lateral FS had a significantly larger surface area than the left. No correlation was found between age and sinus morphometry.ConclusionAlthough this study was conducted on animals of certain age and sex, without considering breed‐specific characteristics, the findings contribute to a more detailed understanding of the cross‐sectional and reconstructive anatomy of the region. Moreover, these preliminary reference data may aid radiologists and clinicians in diagnosing sinonasal diseases.Keywords: carnivore, cone‐beam computed tomography (CT), multiplanar reconstruction, paranasal sinuses, pneumatic bones, skull

Introduction

Dogs were the first domesticated animals and encompass over 400 breeds distributed across diverse geographical regions (FCI 2025). Variability in morphological characteristics, including appearance, size, shape and behaviour, arises due to factors, such as inter‐breed crossing, migration, regional influences, nutrition, mutations and artificial selection (Parker et al. 2017). Significant morphological alterations in the head region have been observed in breeds selectively bred for purposes, such as hunting, protection or companionship (Drake and Klingenberg 2010; Hecht et al. 2019; Brassard et al. 2022). The morphometric information of various tissues and organs, including those in the head region, must be updated alongside the changes resulting from crossbreeding in dogs.

The paranasal sinuses are paired, air‐filled cavities located in the within bones surrounding the nasal cavity, lined by mucoperiosteum (Barone 1997; Hermanson et al. 2020). These sinuses continue to develop throughout nearly the entire lifespan, exhibiting species‐specific morphological variations (Witmer and Ridgely 2008; Brinkschulte et al. 2013; Bahar et al. 2014; Köhler et al. 2021). Various functions have been attributed to paranasal sinuses, including reducing skull weight, providing cranial protection and thermoregulation, enhancing the surface area of the olfactory mucosa, facilitating humidification, mucus production and defence and contributing to nitric oxide gas production (Lundberg et al. 1994; Barone 1999; Farke 2010). In dogs, paranasal sinuses primarily consist of the maxillary recess (recessus maxillaris, MR) and frontal sinus (sinus frontalis, FS) (Schummer et al. 1979; Hermanson et al. 2020). However, the presence of the sphenoid sinus (sinus sphenoidalis, SS) and dorsal conchal sinus (sinus conchae dorsalis, DCS) remains a subject of debate (Nickel et al. 1986; Burk 1992; Barone 1997). Although the general morphological structure of the paranasal sinuses in dogs has been described alongside the head region in classical anatomy texts and atlases (Sisson 1975; Schummer et al. 1979; Nickel et al. 1986; Barone 1997; Dyce et al. 2018; Hermanson et al. 2020), detailed morphometric analyses remain limited.

Computed tomography (CT) is an important and widely accepted diagnostic method, particularly for evaluating in the evaluation of complex structures such as the skull. CT is widely used in diagnosing diseases of the upper respiratory tract, including rhinitis and sinusitis (Wisner and Zwingenberger 2015), sinus mucoceles (Baeza et al. 2023), sinus and nasal aspergillosis (Sharman and Mansfield 2012; Cox et al. 2025) and tumours of the nasal and paranasal sinuses (Birckhead et al. 2023; Mortier et al. 2023) in carnivores. CT has also been used experimental model study of the sinonasal region in dogs. The studies have focused on identifying optimal FS entry points in healthy and aspergillosis‐affected dogs of different breeds (Burrow et al. 2012), evaluating potential areas for MR base elevation in beagles (Zheng et al. 2014) and assessing FS usability in alternative implant applications in mixed‐breed dogs (Bae et al. 2018). Successful diagnosis and treatment of sinonasal region diseases and determination of the most appropriate animal for experimental model studies depend on accurate knowledge of the anatomical structure. The cross‐sectional anatomical structure of the head, paranasal sinuses and nasal cavity has been documented using CT scans in various species, including cats (Losonsky et al. 1997; Conchou et al. 2012; Díaz Martínez et al. 2024), beagles (George and Smallwood 1992), German shepherds (Burk 1992), Belgian shepherds (de Rycke et al. 2003), Anatolian shepherds (Abumandour et al. 2022) and red foxes (Mahdy and Zayed 2020). Nowadays, cone‐beam CT devices that have been specifically developed for use in veterinary medicine can obtain cross‐sectional images of the entire body of animals (Bregger et al. 2019; Stewart et al. 2021). However, cross‐sectional radiological and morphometric information from these devices for different body regions in different species of animal is still needed.

Three‐dimensional (3D) reconstruction using multi‐slice CT (Brinkschulte et al. 2013; Bahar et al. 2014; Köhler et al. 2021; Turgut et al. 2024) or cone‐beam CT images (Morgan et al. 2022; Abate et al. 2023; Azizia et al. 2025) is the gold standard for easy visualisation and volumetric analysis of complex anatomical structures such as the skull and paranasal sinuses. The morphological structure of the FS was analysed by means of CT‐based models using dry skull samples from similarly sized feline species (Siliceo et al. 2011), various carnivorous species (Curtis and van Valkenburgh 2014), arctoid carnivores (Curtis et al. 2015) as well as some wolf species and Akita (Suzuki et al. 2024). The specific 3D reconstructive anatomy and morphometric features of the paranasal sinuses in dogs, which are one of the most popular pets and have very different head structures, have not yet been adequately described in the literature. This anatomical study aims to describe the cross‐sectional and reconstructive anatomical structure and morphometric features of the paranasal sinuses in normal mesaticephalic dogs over 1 year of age using CT imaging and 3D modelling methodologies and to obtain normative preliminary reference data.

Materials and Methods

Selection and Description of Subjects

In this retrospective anatomy study, medical records of dogs that visited the animal hospital between January 2022 and June 2023 and underwent CT scans (cone‐beam CT, Vimago CT, Epica, Landrum, USA) were analysed. CT scanning parameters were as follows: 45 kVA, 100 mAs, 0.3 × 0.3 mm2 and 0.2 × 0.2 mm2 pixel size, 0.2–0.3 mm slice thickness. CT data from 50 animals that were determined to be free of head‐ or bone‐related diseases following veterinary examination were included. CT datasets were transferred to the RadiAnt Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine (DICOM) Viewer (ver. 2025.1, Medixant, Poznan, Poland) software for assessment of image quality, anomalies and asymmetrical development in bone (window level [WL]; 900–1700 Hounsfield Unit [HU], window width [WW]; 2800–4500 HU) and soft tissue window (WL: 300–800 HU, WW: 1000–1550 HU) settings. Additionally, reconstructive models of the head skeleton were generated using the software's 3D modelling tool, allowing for detailed examination of bone and dental structures. Following evaluation, skull length (TL, acrocranion–prosthion) and maximum zygomatic width (ZB, zygion–zygion) were measured on CT images of normal dogs meeting the study criteria using the multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) tool. The skull index (SL, maximum zygomatic width/skull length × 100) was then calculated on the basis of these measurements (von den Driesch 1976; Sokołowski et al. 2020). After calculation, CT data of 14 mesaticephalic dogs (1–17 years old, 10–45 kg, Golden Retriever, Mongrel, Kangal, American Staffordshire Terrier, Pointer), consisting of 7 males and 7 females, with an index value of 50.01–80.00, were archived in DICOM format for use in the present study. All procedures were conducted with the approval of the Selcuk University Faculty of Veterinary Medicine Experimental Animal Production and Research Center Ethics Committee under decision number 2024/075.

Cross‐Sectional Radiological Anatomy

DICOM CT data of 14 animals were analysed using imaging software (RadiAnt DICOM Viewer, Medixant, Poznan, Poland). Reformatted CT images oriented perpendicularly to the palatine bone in the rostrocaudal direction were generated utilising the software's MPR tool. In these planes, the boundaries of the sinuses, adjacent structures and their anatomical relationships were identified on the basis of the maxillary premolar (P) and molar (M) teeth, along with prominent bone landmarks. A CT atlas of the head region, comprising transverse, dorsal and paramedian images, was created separately for female and male dogs.

3D Modelling

The 3D reconstruction of the paranasal sinuses and skull was conducted using the MIMICS software program (Interactive Medical Image Control System, ver. 21.0, Materialise, Leuven, Belgium). DICOM cone‐beam CT data of the head region were imported into the program, and segmentation tools (threshold, region growing, and edit mask) were applied to isolate the paranasal sinuses and skull from surrounding tissues. Although the skull was efficiently modelled using automatic segmentation, the segmentation of the sinuses required semi‐automatic segmentation processing. For these reconstructions, the thresholding range was set at 1400 (maximum) to 3900 (minimum) HU for the skull and −150 (maximum) to −1024 (minimum) HU for the sinuses. The region growing tool was used to select the mask for modelling, and the model mask was subsequently created. Following this step, the sinuses were manually separated using the ‘3D edit mask’ function, allowing for the generation of individual 3D models with distinct colour masks for each sinus. Volume and surface area measurements of the sinuses were obtained from these models. Additionally, the skull was rendered transparent to evaluate the interaction between the sinuses and bone reference points, as well as the orientation and morphology of the sinonasal openings (Bahar et al. 2014; Köhler et al. 2021; Turgut et al. 2024).

3D Model Anatomical Validation

The radiological anatomy atlas and 3D models of female and male dogs were analysed in detail and compared with classical head anatomy texts and anatomical atlases. Additionally, each model was reviewed by a senior anatomy professor (see ‘Acknowledgements’ section). On the basis of the evaluations and recommendations, necessary modifications were manually applied using the editing tools of the modelling software.

Measurement results obtained in the study were expressed in millimetres (mm), centimetres (cm), cubic centimetres (cm3) and square centimetres (cm2) and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Classical anatomical references (Schummer et al. 1979; Hermanson et al. 2020) and Nomina Anatomica Veterinaria (NAV 2017) were used for the nomenclature of anatomical structures. Anatomical expressions were given according to English terminology. These structures were labelled using Adobe Photoshop CC 2015.5 (Adobe Systems, San Jose, CA, USA).

Statistical Analysis

The SPSS statistical package (version 29.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp., USA) was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics, including mean, SD, count and percentage, were reported for categorical and continuous variables. The homogeneity of variances was assessed using the Levene test, whereas the Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to determine the normality of data distribution. For comparisons of right and left sinus values, the paired‐samples t‐test was performed if parametric test assumptions were satisfied; otherwise, the Wilcoxon test was used. In the comparison of data between female and male animals, the independent samples t‐test was applied when parametric test assumptions were met, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was used if assumptions were not satisfied. Correlations between two continuous variables were analysed using the Pearson correlation coefficient when parametric assumptions were met and the Spearman correlation coefficient when they were not. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 and 0.01.

Results

Bone tissue CT images provided clear visualisation of the borders of the paranasal sinuses, their adjacent structures, bone lamellae and sinonasal openings. A CT atlas was developed, focusing on the paranasal sinuses, other clinically significant anatomical structures and specific bony landmarks, using four transverse (two females and two males) (Figure 1), two dorsal (female, male) (Figure 2) and two paramedian (female, male) (Figure 3) plane images. Dorsal images extended from the internal lamina of the frontal bone (roof of the cranial cavity) to the nasal surface (floor of the nasal cavity) of the palatine bone (Figure 2). Paramedian images included the region from the left side of the nasal septum to the body of the maxilla (nasal surface) (Figure 3).

Maxillary Recess

The MR was a crescent‐shaped cavity positioned in the caudolateral wall of the nasal cavity, located rostroventral to the orbit (Figures 2 and 3/MR), between transverse slices passing through levels P3–M2 (Figure 1 /MR). The rostral boundary of the recess was at the P3 level in four females and four males, whereas in three females and three males, it was at the P4 level. The caudal limit was identified at the M1 level in five females and six males, at the M2 level in two females and at the P4 level in one male (Figures 1 and 3/MR). The MR exhibited an almost symmetrical shape in all specimens (Figures 4 and 5/MR). The recess was bounded laterally by the maxilla (body of the maxilla), palatine (perpendicular plate) and lacrimal bone (Figures 2 and 3/MR), medially by the ethmoid bone (orbital plate), and caudoventrally by the maxilla (palatine process) and palatine (horizontal plate) (Figures 1 and 2/MR). It was adjacent dorsolaterally to the nasolacrimal canal (Figure 1/f), ventrolaterally to the infraorbital canal (Figure 1/2I), ventromedially to the major palatine foramen and palatine canal (Figure 1/3I) and rostrolaterally to the incisivomaxillary canal (Figure 2/5IV). Basal lamellae of ectoturbinate 5 and endoturbinalia 2–4 were present on the medial wall of the recess between the caudal end of P4 and the M1 level (Figure 1/MR). The uncinate process of the dorsal nasal concha extended into the MR opening, dividing the recess into a larger dorsal and a smaller ventral compartment (Figure 1/h). In lateral 3D model images (transparent view), the lateral wall of the dorsal compartment of the recess displayed a trace of the nasolacrimal canal (Figure 5a). In 3D lateral model images, the MR was positioned dorsally at the caudal level of the mandibular body (Figure 5a), whereas in dorsoventral and (Figure 4/MR) ventrodorsal images (Figure 5b/MR), the mandible covered this recess.

The nasomaxillary opening of the MR, located at the P4 level and exhibiting a cleft‐like shape, was found to connect to the nasal cavity at the level of the caudal end of the ventral nasal concha. This connection was observed at the rostral end of P4 in five females and four males, whereas in two females and three males, it was at the middle level of P4 (Figures 1, 2, 3). The dorsal opening of the MR was found to open into the middle nasal meatus, whereas the ventral opening led into the ventral nasal meatus (Figure 1/d).

The total volume of the MR in female and male animals was 3.7 ± 1.6 and 4.0 ± 2.7 cm3, whereas the surface areas were 25.3 ± 6.4 and 26.4 ± 12.4 cm2 in female and male animals, respectively (Table 1). No statistically significant difference was observed between the right and left MR parameters in either gender (p > 0.05). A positive correlation was identified between the volume and surface areas of the right and left MR in both sexes (Tables 2 and 3).

The FS

The FS is a complex sinus situated in the dorsal wall of the skull, positioned between the orbit and the nasal and cranial cavities within the rostral two‐thirds of the frontal bone (Figures 1 and 3/RFS, MFS, LFS). In transverse section images, the sinus was observed rostral to the temporomandibular joint at the level of P4 (Figure 1/RFS, MFS, LFS). The FS was divided into right and left compartments by the asymmetrical median septum (septum sinuum frontalium) (Figures 1/8 and 2/2). Separation of the FS from the nasal cavity was formed by the ethmoid bone (Figure 3/RFS, LFS). The total volume of the FS was measured as 20.6 ± 9.4 and 17.9 ± 15.2 cm3 in female and male animals, respectively, and surface areas as 79.8 ± 30.3 and 67.2 ± 47.1 cm2 in female and male animals, respectively (Table 1).

The FS in each body half was found to be divided into three compartments—RFS, MFS and LFS—by two sagittal oblique septa (Figures 1, 2, 3). The LFS, in particular, was observed to be further subdivided into smaller subcompartments interconnected by bone lamellae (lamellae intrasinuales) surrounded by mucosa (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5). Each FS compartment communicated with the nasal cavity independently through a slit‐shaped opening, which opened into the nasal cavity at different levels, ranging from rostral to caudal and medial to lateral (Figures 1 and 3/g).

The RFS

The RFS is a compartment of the FS located at the rostral end of the frontal bone within the caudal portion of the nasal cavity (Figures 3/RFS, 4 and 5/RFS). It is bordered laterally by the orbit, medially by the median and nasal septum (Figures 1/8 and 2/2), and positioned at the level of the frontonasal and frontomaxillary sutures (Figure 2/RFS). Although variations were observed among specimens, the sinus was identified in transverse sections about between P4 and M2 (Figure 1/RFS). The rostral boundary of the sinus was at the P4 level in four females and four males, whereas in three females and two males, it was at the M1 level (Figures 1, 2, 3/RFS). The caudal limit was located at M1 in two females and one male, at M2 in two females and two males, at the caudal end of P4 in one male (Figure 1/RFS) and at the rostral level of the ethmoidal foramen (8.6 ± 1.6 mm caudal to M2) (Figure 1/10) in three females and two males. The RFS was identified in 13 of the 14 animals, with one male terrier (4 years old) lacking this compartment. The sinus was relatively small in five animals, whereas in 62% of the specimens (four females and four males), a caudoventral recess was observed, extending from the rostral end of the sinus toward the median septum. The total volume of the RFS in female and male animals was 2.1 ± 1.6 and 1.4 ± 1.3 cm3, whereas the surface areas were 14.8 ± 9.0 and 9.6 ± 5.0 cm2 in female and male animals, respectively (Table 1). Within the FS compartments, the RFS accounted for 10% of the total volume in females and 5% in males, whereas its surface area ratio was 18% in females and 11.3% in males. No statistically significant differences were detected between the right and left sinus parameters in either sex (p > 0.05) (Table 1). A positive correlation was found between the right and left RFS volumes in females (p = 0.001, r = 0.972) and males (p = 0.019, r = 0.885) (Table 2). A similar correlation was observed for the sinus surface areas in females (p = 0.001, r = 0.970) and males (p = 0.016, r = 0.896) (Table 3). The RFS communicated with the dorsal ethmoidal meatus through a slit‐shaped aperture located at the rostrodorsal end between the P4–M1 levels (Figure 1/g). Additionally, the free ends of ectoturbinate 2 were observed extending into the sinus through this aperture, reducing the width of the opening (Figures 1 and 3/RFS).

The MFS

The MFS was the smallest and most asymmetrically structured sinus compartment, exhibiting considerable variation in shape and irregular morphology (Figures 4 and 5/MFS). It was positioned between the LFS and RFS compartments (Figures 2, 4 and 5/MFS). The medial wall of this compartment was formed by the median septum (Figure 1/8). The rostral boundary of the sinus extended to the M2 level in four females and four males, to the M1 level in three females and to the P4–M1 levels in three males (Figure 1/MFS). The caudal boundary extended to the ethmoidal foramen level (8.5 ± 4.1 mm caudal to M2) (Figure 1/10) in two females and four males, to the cribriform plate (Figure 1/9), in four females to the optic foramen in one female, to the M1 level in two males (Figure 1/MFS), and to the M2 level in one male. Morphometric data on MFS volume and surface area are presented in Table 1, whereas their relationships are detailed in Tables 2 and 3. Among the FS compartments, the MFS was the least subdivided by bony lamellae (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5). In five animals (36%), the caudal end of the sinus was observed to extend near the caudal end of the LFS (Figure 4). The MFS communicated with the dorsal ethmoidal meatus via a slit‐shaped opening located at its rostral end, between the caudal end of P4 and the rostral end of M2. This opening was partially narrowed by the free ends of ectoturbinate 2.

The LFS

The LFS is a triangular‐shaped sinus that occupies a significant portion of the frontal bone, extending toward its caudal and lateral halves (Figures 4 and 5/LFS). The rostral boundary of the LFS was positioned at the M1 level in six females and six males (Figure 1/LFS) and at the M2 level in one female and one male. The caudal boundary was located at the midpoint of the alar canal in three females and two males, at the orbital fissure in three females and one male, at the optic foramen in three males and at the chiasmatic sulcus in one female and one male (Figure 1/LFS). A small lateral recess extended toward the zygomatic process (Figure 1/5), whereas a larger ventral recess extended toward the orbital wall (Figure 3/LFS). Among the FS compartments, the LFS was the most subdivided by bony lamellae (Figures 1, 2, 3/LFS). It was the largest FS compartment, accounting for 86.5% of total FS volume in females and 93.2% in males, with a surface area ratio of 71.9% and 88.1% in females and males, respectively. The surface area of the right FS was significantly larger than that of the left in females (p < 0.05) (Table 1). Relationships between LFS volume and surface area are presented in Tables 2 and 3. The LFS communicated with the dorsal ethmoidal meatus via a slit‐shaped opening positioned at the caudal level of the rostromedial end of P4 (Figure 3/g). Additionally, the free ends of ectoturbinate 3 were observed to narrow this aperture (Figures 1, 2, 3).

Discussion

In the present study, spiral lamellae, ecto‐ and endoturbinate, sinonasal openings and other anatomical structures adjacent to the sinuses were clearly visualised in cone‐beam CT images of mesaticephalic dogs over 1 year of age (Figures 1, 2, 3). However, these images were found to be inadequate for the automatic modelling of the sinuses, which may be attributed to the high bone density surrounding the sinus structures.

The maxillary sinus is a paranasal sinus present in nearly all mammals, exhibiting structural and size variations among species (Witmer and Ridgely 2008; Brinkschulte et al. 2013; Bahar et al. 2014; Ben Khalifa et al. 2021; Turgut et al. 2023). In carnivores, this sinus is described as a large lateral diverticulum of the nasal cavity rather than a true air‐filled cavity (Hermanson et al. 2020). Although it appears as a prominent recess in the nasal cavities in dry skulls, it has been reported to be a small, shallow cavity in fresh cadavers (Sisson 1975; Barone 1999). The size of the recess in carnivores is considered relatively constant and does not change significantly with advancing age (Schummer et al. 1979). The MR has been documented at the P3–M2 level (Mahdy and Zayed 2020), P4–M2 level (Dyce et al. 2018) or M1–M2 level (Sisson 1975) in carnivores. Radiological section images have identified this recess in Beagles (George and Smallwood 1992; Zheng et al. 2014), shepherd dogs (Burk 1992), Belgian shepherd dogs (de Rycke et al. 2003) and Labrador retrievers (Craven et al. 2007). In the present study, the MR was observed as a diverticulum with a volume of 4 cm3 and a surface area of 26 cm2, located in the lateral wall of the nasal cavity (Figures 1 and 2/MR), with two rostrodorsal openings (Figures 1, 2, 3/d). It appeared almost symmetrically in cross‐sectional images (Figures 1, 2, 3/MR), consistent with previous descriptions in carnivores. Additionally, in accordance with previous findings (Schummer et al. 1979), no correlation was identified between the parameters of this recess and age in mesaticephalic dogs (Tables 2 and 3).

In the present study, the medial wall of the MR was found to be formed by the ethmoid bone, the lateral wall by the maxilla (Figures 1/2, 2/5 and 3/10) and the caudolateral wall by the lacrimal (Figures 1 and 2/6) and palatine bones (Figure 3/9), consistent with previous reports (Nickel et al. 1986; Barone 1999; Mahdy and Zayed 2020) (Figures 1, 2, 3). A rounded fundus is formed in the caudal region of dogs. This is where the medial and lateral walls of the MR converge (Hermanson et al. 2020). The MR is divided into a large rostral and a small caudal region in dogs by the uncinate process of the dorsal nasal concha (Schummer et al. 1979). In the present study, the recess was also observed to form a caudal fundus (Figure 3/MR). However, differing from previous findings (Schummer et al. 1979), the uncinate process (Figure 1/h) was found to divide the rostral half of the MR into a large dorsal and a smaller ventral compartment (Figures 1, 2, 3, 5/MR and 5/MR).

In carnivores, the MR has been reported to connect to the middle nasal meatus (Schummer et al. 1979; Hermanson et al. 2020), the ventral nasal meatus (Mahdy and Zayed 2020) or both, through a wide nasomaxillary opening at the P3–4 level (Barone 1999). This opening has also been described as narrowed by the uncinate process of the dorsal nasal concha (Schummer et al. 1979). In the present study, it was determined that, as stated in the literature (Barone 1999), the dorsal compartment opens into the middle nasal meatus, and the ventral compartment opens into the ventral nasal meatus from the rostrodorsal side. In dogs, the opening level of the recessus was at the rostral end or middle level of P4 (Figures 1, 2, 3/d). Additionally, the wide aperture was observed to be narrowed by the uncinate process (Figure 1/h), in agreement with earlier findings (Schummer et al. 1979) (Figure 1/h).

In equids, the FS occupies the entire frontal bone (Bahar et al. 2014; Köhler et al. 2021), whereas in pigs and ruminants, it extends into the parietal, interparietal, occipital and temporal bones (Barone 1999; Schummer et al. 1979; Turgut et al. 2023). In cats, it is relatively simple in structure (Siliceo et al. 2011; Díaz Martínez et al. 2024). In the red fox (Vulpes vulpes), the FS extends from the caudal surface of P3 to the caudal border of the zygomatic process (Mahdy and Zayed 2020). In Canis lupus and Lycaon pictus, it reaches the frontoparietal suture, whereas in Vulpes lagopus, it remains relatively small (Frosali et al. 2023). Some extinct large hypercarnivores exhibit FS extension to the frontoparietal suture, and in certain species, it also pneumatises the parietal bone (Tedford et al. 2009). In some dogs, such as the bush dog, and certain fox species (grey fox, Arctic fox, kit fox, red fox and bat‐eared fox), the FS does not develop, whereas in raccoon dogs, it does not extend to the postorbital process or cross the frontoparietal suture (Curtis and van Valkenburgh 2014). Complete pneumatisation of the zygomatic process in raccoon dogs has been reported (Jurgelėnas et al. 2007). In brachycephalic dogs, the FS is either extremely small or absent (Sisson 1975; Dyce et al. 2018). In the present study, the FS in mesaticephalic dogs extended from the P4 level, positioned between the orbit and the nasal and cranial cavities, caudally to the frontoparietal suture (Figures 1, 2, 3/RFS, MFS, LFS). As the largest and most complex paranasal sinus, it had a volume of 19.3 cm3 and a surface area of 74 cm2 (Table 1). Consistent with previous findings (Jurgelėnas et al. 2007; Curtis and van Valkenburgh 2014), the zygomatic process extended laterally but did not surpass the frontoparietal suture in any of the examined specimens.

Classical veterinary anatomy texts have described the FS in dogs as being divided into three compartments: lateral, medial and rostral (Schummer et al. 1979; Hermanson et al. 2020). Sisson (1975) reported two compartments in the FS: a larger lateral and a smaller medial compartment. Studies conducted on dry skulls have considered the FS as a single compartment (Jurgelėnas et al. 2007; Tedford et al. 2009; Curtis and van Valkenburgh 2014; Frosali et al. 2023). Radiological section images have identified three compartments (rostral, medial and lateral) in the red fox (Mahdy and Zayed 2020) and two compartments (lateral and medial) in German shepherds (Burk 1992), Beagles (George and Smallwood 1992) and Belgian shepherd dogs (de Rycke et al. 2003). In mongrel dogs, the FS has been reported to be divided into three compartments by septa, with the rostral and medial compartments often being very small or sometimes absent (Bae et al. 2018). In the present study, consistent with previous reports (Bae et al. 2018; Hermanson et al. 2020; Mahdy and Zayed 2020; Schummer et al. 1979), the FS was observed to be bilaterally divided into three compartments—rostral, medial and lateral—by two sagittal oblique septa. These compartments were present in all specimens except for one male terrier, in which the RFS was absent (Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5/RFS, MFS, LFS). In mesaticephalic dogs, the lateral compartment was found to be the largest, accounting for 90% of the total FS volume, whereas the medial compartment was the smallest, representing only 3% of the total volume (Table 1).

The RFS has been described in classical sources (Hermanson et al. 2020) as a small, irregular FS compartment located at the junction of the maxilla, nasal and frontal bones, positioned between the orbit and the median septum. Schummer et al. (1979) reported that part of the RFS floor is occupied by ectoturbinate 2, whereas Barone (1997) stated that the entire sinus is filled by ectoturbinate 1. Dyce et al. (2018) described the compartment as being completely occupied by ethmoturbinates. In the present study, the RFS volume was 1.09 ± 0.87 and 0.98 ± 0.78 cm3 on the right and left in females, respectively, and 0.63 ± 0.50 and 0.74 ± 0.80 cm3 in males, respectively. The RFS was observed as the second largest FS compartment, exhibiting an irregular shape and accounting for 8% of the total FS volume (Figures 4 and 5/RFS and Table 1). Its location was consistent with previously reported boundaries (Hermanson et al. 2020). Additionally, in agreement with earlier findings (Schummer et al. 1979), only a portion of the sinus floor was occupied by extensions of ectoturbinate 2 (Figures 1 and 3/RFS).

The MFS is described as a small, variably irregular sinus extending to the median septum (Schummer et al. 1979; Barone 1999). Schummer et al. (1979) reported that the sinus may extend to the caudal border of the LFS. Hermanson et al. (2020) and Barone (1997) stated that the aperture is completely narrowed by ectoturbinate 1 and 2 due to the insufficiency of the inner lamina of the frontal bone. Schummer et al. (1979) indicated that only ectoturbinate 2 contributes to the narrowing of the aperture. In brachycephalic dogs, the MFS has been reported to be small and, in some cases, absent (Hermanson et al. 2020). In the present study, the MFS was identified as the smallest and most irregularly shaped FS compartment (Figures 4 and 5/MFS), with a volume of 0.32 ± 0.34 cm3 in all specimens (Table 1). Consistent with previous findings (Schummer et al. 1979), the sinus extended to the caudal end of the LFS in 36% of the animals, and ectoturbinate 2 was observed to narrow only the sinonasal opening (Figure 1/MFS).

The LFS is identified as the largest FS compartment, occupying the caudal and lateral half of the frontal bone, positioned anterior to the orbitotemporal crest and surrounding the frontoparietal suture (Barone 1999; Dyce et al. 2018). It has been reported that the LFS forms a diverticulum in the zygomatic process of the frontal bone and is separated from the MFS and nasal cavity by an irregularly curved septum (Barone 1999). Additionally, this compartment contains multiple recesses or subcompartments formed by bony lamellae (Schummer et al. 1979; Hermanson et al. 2020). The LFS has been described as large in dolichocephalic dogs (Hermanson et al. 2020) and capable of extending to the level of the temporomandibular joint (Dyce et al. 2018). The aperture of this sinus has been reported to be narrowed by ectoturbinate 3 (Schummer et al. 1979; Hermanson et al. 2020). In the present study, the LFS was identified as the largest FS compartment in mesaticephalic dogs, accounting for 90% of the total FS volume and 78% of the total surface area (Table 1). Its location was consistent with previously reported boundaries, featuring a lateral recess extending toward the zygomatic process (Figure 1/5) and a ventral recess extending toward the orbital wall (Figure 3/LFS). In agreement with previous findings (Schummer et al. 1979; Hermanson et al. 2020), the sinus exhibited increased subdivision into subcompartments, particularly in older animals (Figures 4 and 5). In 50% of mesaticephalic dogs, similar to dolichocephalic breeds, the sinus extended to the level of the temporomandibular joint. The sinus opening was narrowed rostrally by the ectoturbinate 3 (Figure 1/LFS).

The FS volume was measured at 5.9 cm3 in a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐based modelling study conducted on a female Labrador retriever cadaver (Craven et al. 2007). In 3D models created using dry skulls (n = 59) from the Carnivora family (Canidae, Felidae, Hyaenidae), the left FS was found to be larger than the right in all hyena species samples (p < 0.05) (Curtis and van Valkenburgh 2014). Suzuki et al. (2024) reported that Japanese wolves had smaller FS volumes (10.5 ± 0.8 cm3) compared to Wolf ssp. (25.9 ± 1.8 cm3) and Akitas (28.1 ± 5.8 cm3) based on dry skull models. In the present study, the total FS volume and surface area were measured as 20.6 ± 9.4 cm3 and 79.8 ± 30.3 cm2 in female animals and 17.9 ± 15.2 cm3 and 67.2 ± 47.1 cm2 in male animals. No statistically significant differences were observed in FS parameters, except for the surface area in female animals (Table 1).

In carnivores, each FS compartment has been reported to open separately into the dorsal ethmoidal meatus through the nasal aperture (Schummer et al. 1979; Burrow et al. 2012). In foxes, the sinus compartments have been described as ventrally communicating with the ethmoidal meatus (Mahdy and Zayed 2020). Schummer et al. (1979) stated that the RFS opening in dogs appears as a slit in the medial wall of the sinus. In line with previous studies, the RFS, MFS and LFS, respectively, from rostral to caudal, and the MFS, RFS and LFS, respectively, from medial to lateral, opened separately into the ethmoidal meatus via a slit‐shaped aperture (Figures 1 and 3/g).

FS size and shape in carnivores have been reported to vary depending on skull structure and age (Hermanson et al. 2020). Although FS size in dogs generally remains unchanged with age, variations have been observed between individuals and between the right and left halves within the same animal (Schummer et al. 1979). The size and shape of the FS have been associated with the dimensions of the frontal bone, with increased sinus complexity corresponding to larger frontal bone size. Additionally, FS morphology has been suggested to adapt to enhance cranial performance based on ecological factors (Curtis and van Valkenburgh 2014). Frosali et al. (2023) further reported that FS development in carnivores may also be influenced by the dietary adaptations of the species. In the present study, consistent with previous findings (Schummer et al. 1979), no correlation was observed between FS size and age in mesaticephalic dogs aged 1 year and older (Tables 2 and 3). Although shape differences were noted among individuals, no significant differences were detected between sexes in the right and left FS parameters, except for the LFS surface area in female animals (p = 0.043) (Tables 1, 2, 3, Figures 1, 2, 3, 4, 5).

Barone (1997) identified the DCS at the P3–4 levels in the caudal part of the dorsal nasal concha. However, consistent with previous reports by Schummer et al. (1979) and Hermanson et al. (2020), this sinus was not observed in the animals examined in the present study (Figure 1).

The SS is present in all domestic mammals except dogs and small ruminants (Schummer et al. 1979; Dyce et al. 2018). This sinus forms within the presphenoid bone and, with advancing age, may expand into the palatine, basisphenoid or temporal, and occipital bones in horses, cattle and pigs (Schummer et al. 1979; Brinkschulte et al. 2013; Bahar et al. 2014; Goodarzi et al. 2021; Turgut et al. 2024). In species of the Felidae family, the sinus is located within the presphenoid bone and connects to the nasal cavity through the ventral ethmoidal meatus (Díaz Martínez et al. 2024; Conchou et al. 2012). Previous studies have suggested that the SS in dogs is a potential space within the presphenoid bone, as endoturbinate IV occupies most of the caudal nasal recess (sphenoid fossa) (Barone 1999; de Rycke et al. 2003; Hermanson et al. 2020). SS has been demonstrated on CT images in German shepherd dogs (Burk 1992) and red foxes (Mahdy and Zayed 2020). In the present study, endoturbinate IV (Figures 1/i, 2/g and 3/h) was observed to fully occupy the caudal nasal recess (Figures 1, 2/15 and 3/12) in mesaticephalic dogs aged 1 year and older, and no sinus formation was detected within the sphenoid bone, consistent with previous literature (Barone 1999; de Rycke et al. 2003; Hermanson et al. 2020).

CT is a routinely used adjunctive diagnostic method for imaging complex structures, such as the head, examining bone tissue and evaluating nasal tract diseases (Manso‐Díaz et al. 2021). Cone‐beam CT, as a mobile diagnostic method, offers rapid data acquisition, low radiation dose and cost. However, compared to helical multi‐slicer CT, it is susceptible to motion artefacts, has lower contrast resolution for soft tissues and has a narrower field of view (FOV) (Bregger et al. 2019, Stewart et al. 2021). I agree with the authors and believe that the cross‐sectional images provided by cone‐beam CT are sufficient for determining the borders, internal space and relationships of the paranasal sinuses to neighbouring structures and the nasal cavities in dogs. However, when modelling air cavities using automatic segmentation, it was found that these images were insufficient and that semi‐automatic segmentation was needed. Abate et al. (2023) and Azizia et al. (2025) determined the specific threshold level for segmentation of the maxillary sinus using cone‐beam CT, which was developed for use in human medicine. This threshold was found to be between −1024 HU (minimum) and −526 HU (maximum). In a similar study by Morgan et al. (2022), these values were selected as −1024 and −200 HU, with semi‐automatic segmentation employed. In the present study, the optimal conditions for the paranasal sinuses were found to be within the range of −1024 to −150 HU, and modelling was performed using semi‐automatic segmentation. In similar modelling processes, bringing the maximum HU value closer to 0 HU will increase manual operations, thereby extending the modelling time. Conversely, when the opposite is applied, the modelling time will be shortened, but volume losses will occur in the sinus. Researchers can create their own optimal HU values by taking image quality into account in similar modelling studies. This study, which used a cone‐beam CT scanner designed for use in veterinary medicine, shows that a maximum HU limit of −200 and −150 creates optimal conditions. In addition, different imaging protocols can be developed to create higher contrast in the sinuses instead of routine head imaging protocols. Alternatively, multi‐slice CT can be used.

Limitations of the current study include the use of a limited number of dog breeds with a limited age range, the exclusion of breed‐specific characteristics and the scarcity of available morphometric data for dogs. The anatomical and morphometric features of the sinuses were not fully compared among dog breeds in the study. Future breed‐specific studies of sinonasal morphometry and sinus development with larger animal populations are needed.

Conclusion

Despite certain limitations, this study provides the first detailed preliminary reference data on the paranasal sinuses in normal mesaticephalic dogs. The images and descriptions presented in this retrospective anatomical review may serve as a valuable resource for anatomists, radiologists, clinicians and veterinary students.
