Life cycle of Puccinia species producing spermogonial and aecial stages on Persicaria odorata subsp. conspicua
Authors: Yuichi Yamaoka, Chihiro Itozawa, Hiroyuki Suzuki, Izumi Okane, Jun-ichi P. Abe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spermogonia and aecia were found on Persicaria odorata subsp. conspicua (Polygonaceae) in a reed field of Lake Kasumigaura, Ibaraki, Japan. Near these plants, telia appeared to be Puccinia trabutii var. abei, of which the other spore stages were unknown, were found on culms of Phragmites australis. Inoculation experiments using basidiospores and aeciospores demonstrated that the two rust fungi were conspecific and the species was a heteroecious macrocyclic species of Puccinia. In addition to telia, an uredinial stage of the rust species was found on Ph. australis in the field observation and the inoculation experiments with aeciospores. Based on morphological observation including type specimens, the rust species was identified as P. abei. Characteristics of all the spore stages were described.

1. Introduction

Rust fungi are obligate plant pathogens belonging to Pucciniales. More than 7,000 species are accepted (Kirk et al., 2008). Some of the species require host alternation between two kinds of unrelated host plants, i.e., an aecial host and a telial host to complete their life cycle. Elucidating life cycle (host alternation) of rust fungi gives us important ecological information of the fungi, useful for control of plant diseases. This information is also quite helpful for considering taxonomy, phylogeny and evolution of rust fungi related to host specificity (Ono, 2008). Ono (2008) revealed that 763 species (varieties not counted) of rust species are distributed in Japan, but the life cycle of 351 species (46%) has not been determined.

In May 2009, we found spermogonia and aecia of the rust fungus on Persicaria odorata (Lour.) Soják subsp. conspicua (Nakai) Yonek. (Polygonaceae) in a reed field of Lake Kasumigaura (36°04’06”N 140°13’11”E), Takita, Tsuchiura, Ibaraki prefecture, Central Honshu, Japan. This rust fungus was also found in another reed field of the lake (35°59’03”N 140°21’14”E), Futto, Inashiki of the same prefecture, although there is no report that spermogonia and aecia of any rust fungi were observed on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicia in Japan (Hiratsuka et al., 1992). We suspected that the rust fungus is the spermogonial and aecial stage of macrocyclic species of Puccinia, probably Puccinia phragmitis (Schumach.) Körn. because P. phragmitis is one of the common species in the reed field of the lake and uses Phragmites australis (Cav.) Trin. ex Steud. as telial host and Rumex spp. (Polygonaceae) as spermogonial and aecial host. However, inoculation of blades of Ph. australis and other several glasses (Poaceae) with aeciospores from Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua was unsuccessful (unpublished data), so that life cycle of the rust fungus was unknown.

In Apr 2022, telia were found on culm of Ph. australis. The fungus was suspected to have heteroecious life cycle since spermogonia were observed on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua grown close by Ph. australis with the rust in the reed field, Futto (Fig. 1A). More than 20 species of rust fungi parasitic on Ph. australis, which contained Puccinia, Uromyces and Uredo species, were reported (Cummins, 1971; Plant parasites of Europe, 2025; Voronin et al., 2021). Among them, nine species of Puccinia, Puccinia alnetorum Gäum, P. coronata Corda, P. graminis Persoon, P. isiacae (Thüm.) G. Winter, P. magnusiana Körn., P. moriokaensis S. Ito, P. okatamaensis S. Ito, P. phragmitis, and P. recondita Roberge ex Desm., were known to be heteroecious macrocyclic species (Gäumann, 1959; Harada, 1978a; Harada & Hasegawa, 1975, Hiratsuka et al., 1992; Plant parasites of Europe, 2025). Only P. phragmitis used Persicaria which is accommodated in Polygonaceae as one of the aecial host (Hiratsuka et al., 1992; Plant parasites of Europe, 2025). In Japan, telial stage of the five Puccinia species, P. magnusian, P. moriokaensis, P. okatamaensis, P. phragmitis and P. trabutii Roum. & Sacc. var. abei (Hirats. f.) Cummins., have been reported from Ph. australis. Regarding to P. trabutii var. abei, only telial stage has been described, but the other spore stages are still unknown (Cummins, 1971; Hiratsuka et al., 1992). Brief observation showed morphology of the teliospores on Ph. australis collected from Futto were analogous with those of P. trabutii var. abei.

Puccinia trabutii var. abei was first described as P. abei Hirats. f. based on the specimen of telia on Ph. austoralis in Japan by Hiratsuka (1937). When Cummins (1971) reconsidered the taxonomic position of the species, P. abei was demoted to a variety of P. trabutii Roum. et Sacc. because of morphological similarity in telial stage. However, differences between P. trabutii var. abei and P. trabutii were also recognized in the paper. Teliospore walls of P. abei were slightly thickened at apex but uniform overall. On the other hand, those of P. trabutii were obviously thicker at apex than at side.

The aim of the present study was to demonstrate the relationship between the rust fungus producing spermogonial and aecial stages on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua and the one producing telia on Ph. australis.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Field observation

A small reed field (about 10 m wide) along the shore of Lake Kasumigaura, Futto, Inashiki, Ibaraki was selected for the field observation. Lake Kasumigaura is the second largest lake in Japan and is located in Ibaraki pref., Kanto district, Honshu. Phragmites australis is one of the common species in lakeshores of the lake (Suzuki et al., 1981). This reed field contained Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua plants with spermogonia and aecia together with Ph. australis, Zizania latifolia (Griseb.) Turcz. ex Stapf., Miscanthus sacchariflorus (Maxim.) Benth. et Hook.f. ex Franch., Carex dispalata Boott, Lycopus lucidus Turcz. ex Benth., and Salix triandra L. subsp. nipponica (Franch. et Sav.) A.K.Skvortsov as major vascular plants. Development of sori on culms of Ph. australis were observed every month from middle of Apr to Nov in 2022.

2.2. Inoculum

Telia on culms of Ph. australis (Fig. 1A) were collected on 16 and 26 Apr 2022 (Inoculum no. B1 and B2) and 19 Apr 2023 (B3 and B4) in the reed field of Lake Kasumigaura, Futto described above. They were kept in refrigerator (ca. 5 ˚C) until use. Teliospores were able to germinate when cultured on water agar plates at 20 ˚C, so that they were used as inocula for the following inoculation experiments (Table 1) without any treatments to break dormancy of teliospores.

2.3. Inoculation experiments with basidiospores

Plants of Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua were collected in the field and planted in plastic pots. They were grown at the campus of University of Tsukuba, Tsukuba, Ibaraki, Japan for more than years before use. They did not have any sori of rust fungi on the campus. Other polygonaceous plants, Rumex acetosa L., R. japonicus Houtt. and R. obtusifolius L. (Table 1) were collected in fields on the campus and planted in pots. These polygonaceous species were aecial host of P. phragmitis (Hiratsuka et al., 1992) and aecidium production on plants of the species distributed in lakeshores of lake Kasumigaura and near there was confirmed. No sori of rust fungi were produced on these plants at least a few years before use for the following inoculation experiments.

Small pieces (about 3 × 3 mm) of filter paper with mass of teliospores (Kondo et al., 1997) were put on the upper surface of leaves of polygonaceous plants. The plants were kept in a moist chamber at 20 ˚C in the dark for 3 d and then incubated in the incubation room to see sorus development. In the inoculation experiments in 2023, only Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua was used to obtain more aeciospores used for the following inoculation experiments.

2.4. Inoculation experiments with aeciospores

Plants of Ph. australis were collected in the field and planted in plastic or clay pots. They were grown at the campus of University of Tsukuba for more than one year before use. They did not have any sori of rust fungi.

Aeciospores produced on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua grown in the incubation room after collected in the field and ones obtained in the inoculation with basidiospores described above, were used as inocula (Table 2). Some of the older sheaths of Ph. australis were removed to expose younger culms. Small pieces of filter paper with aeciospores were put on the culms and blades of Ph. australis. The plants were kept in a moist chamber at 20 ˚C in the dark for 3 d and then incubated in the incubation room to see sorus development or in a growth cabinet controlled at 25 °C and using sunlight for illumination.

2.5. Morphological observation

Specimens of the rust fungi on Ph. australis and Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua collected in fields and those resulted from the inoculation experiments were used for morphological analyses. In addition, isotype specimens of P. abei (currently P. trabutii var. abei) and P. trabutii, and a few specimens identified as P. trabutii, which were loaned from Arthur Herbarium (PUR) & Kriebel Herbarium (PuL), Department of Botany & Plant Pathology, Purdue University, West Lafayctc, Indiana, USA were also used. Spores or hand sections of sori were mounted on glass slide with Melzer solution or lactic acid and observed under light microscope or differential interference contrast microscope (Nikon OPTIPHOT).

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of aecial stage, aecia from dry specimens were attached on specimen holders by carbon conductive, double coated adhesive tape and coated with platinum-palladium in a high vacuum with a Hitachi E-1030 Ion Sputter for 250 s coating time. They were observed with a JEOL JSM-IT500HR/LV SEM operating at 10-15 kV.

2.6. Molecular studies

Spores from one or two sori were suspended in 100 µL TE buffer. DNA was extracted from the spores using the method of Izumitsu et al. (2012) using microwave (600 W). Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed to amplify the internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) region and D1/D2 region of the 28S large subunit (LSU) by using Rust2inv (Aime, 2006) and NL4 (O'Donnell, 1993). When the PCRs were failed, ITS and LSU were amplified separately with RustITS-F/RustITS-R (Yamaoka, 2017) and Rust28S-F/Rust28S-R (Yamaoka, 2017), respectively, then combined using FASconCAT-G (Kück & Longo, 2014) to obtain ITS2-D1/D2 region. Protocols of the PCRs and purification were described in Yamaoka (2017). PCR products were consigned to Eurofins Genomics (Tokyo, Japan) for sequencing and the raw sequences were assembled using MEGA11 (Tamura et al., 2021). The consensus sequences were deposited in the GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) (Table 3).

The sequences of four isolates of P. magnusiana were obtained from GenBank and combined into a dataset for phylogenetic analyses. The dataset was aligned using MAFFT v. 7 with the G-INS-I option (Katoh et al., 2019). The aligned sequences were deposited in Figshare (DOI: 10.6084/m9.figshare.30937436). The substitution model of TVM+I was selected with jModelTest v. 2.1.5 (Posada, 2008). Maximum-likelihood (ML) analysis was performed using PhyML with 1000 replicate bootstrap analyses (Guindon et al., 2010). Bayesian inference analysis was conducted using MrBayes v. 3.2.1 (Ronquist et al., 2012) to estimate the posterior probabilities of the tree topologies. The analysis was conducted for 3,000,000 generations with four runs and trees were sampled every 100 generations. A 25% burn-in was used to summarize a consensus tree. Puccinia corticioides Berk. & Broome in Berkeley was designated as an outgroup taxon.

3. Results

3.1. Inoculation experiments with basidiospores

Potted plants of Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua together with three Rumex species (R. acetosa, R. japonicus, and R. obtusifolius) were inoculated with basidiospores four times using either of the two telial inocula (B1 or B2). Spermogonia and aecia (Fig. 1B) were produced on all the inoculated plants of Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua 7 to 8 d and 10 to 18 d after inoculation, respectively (Table 1). On the other hands, no spermogonia nor aecia were produced on any Rumex species.

3.2. Inoculation experiments with aeciospores

Potted plants of Ph. australis were inoculated with aeciospores (A1) produced on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua three times in 2022 (Table 2). Striate brown lesions were produced on culms 7-14 d after inoculation, when young and fresh tissues of culms were inoculated. Yellowish brown uredinia (Fig. 1C) were produced on two of the lesions 14 or 16 d after inoculation. One of the uredinia produced on the middle of the sheath was not able to develop fully. However, the other sori produced on the lesion near node gradually expanded and developed into long telia (Fig. 1D-F).

Inoculation with aeciospores on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua resulted from the basidiospore inoculation, were conducted four times (B1-1, B1-6, B1-7, B2-2) in 2022 and three times (B3-2, B4-1, B4-2) in 2023 (Table 2). Telia were formed two times each in both years, although uredinia were confirmed once. The sori were found 35 to 60 d after inoculation on the lesions near nodes. Only when young and fresh tissues of culms were inoculated, uredinia and telia were produced. No sorus was produced on blade.

3.3. Field observation

Spermogonia were found on leaves of Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua in the reed field, Inashiki on 16 and 26 Apr 2022, and mature aecia on 15 May.

There were some dark brown to black striate lesions on culm of Ph. australis on 15 May. On 19 Jun, small sori with yellowish brown to dark brown spores were produced on some of the lesions. Epidermis of host plants on sori were partially ruptured, so that most part of the sori was hidden by the semi-ruptured epidermis. Microscopic observation revealed that spores in the sori were urediniospores and young teliospores. On 10 Jul, lesions and dark brown sori were extremely expanded and elongated (to 7 cm long). Culms with sori tend to be broken easily at the point of nodes with sori.

3.4. Morphology

Since it was demonstrated that the rust fungus producing spermogonial and aecial stages on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua and the one producing telia on Ph. australis in the reed field of Lake Kasumigaura were conspecific, the rust species was referred to as Puccinia sp. 1 here.

Spermogonia and aecia (Fig. 1B) of Puccinia sp. 1 were produced on swollen lesion yellowish to reddish in color on leaves of Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua. Spermogonia (Fig. 2A, B) were amphigenous and flask-shaped (type 4 of Hiratsuka & Cummins, 1963), (78-) 86-133 μm (average: 104.9 μm) in diam. Aecia (Fig. 2A, C) were hypophyllous and Aecidium type with peridium. Peridial cells were irregular polygonal with a concave inner wall, verrucose (Fig. 2H), 19.6-33.3 × (13.7-)15.7-25.5(-29.4) μm (average: 25.6 × 21.0 μm). Inner wall 2.9-5.9 μm (average: 4.0 μm) thick. Outer wall (4.9-)5.9-12.7 μm thick (average: 8.3 μm). Each aecium in a lesion was separated by well-developed hyphoid stroma (Fig. 2A, C). Aeciospores (Fig. 2E-G) were produced in chain with intercalary cells (Fig. 2D) and were subglobose to broadly ellipsoidal, slightly angular, 16.7-23.5(-24.5) × 13.7-20.6(-21.6) um (average: 20.4 × 17.8 μm). Wall was 0.8-1.7 μm (average: 1.1 μm) thick. Surface (Fig. 2F, G) was verrucose, warts and small plugs in the coarse belt (Type 3 of Savile, 1973).

Uredinia of Puccinia sp. 1 were formed on striate brown lesion on culms, but were observed in the early stage of sorus development and were rarely seen. Urediniospores (Fig. 3A, B) were ellipsoid, broadly ellipsoid or obovoid, pale yellow to pale yellowish brown, 23.5-31.4(-32.9) × 17.6-25.9(-27.5) μm (average: 27.4 × 21.7 μm). Wall was 1.6-2.4 μm (average: 2.0 μm) thick. Surface was echinulate. Germ pores were 6-8 in number and scattered (Fig. 3C).

Telia (Fig. 1A, F) of Puccinia sp. 1 were produced on culms, compact and chocolate-brown, confluent in a group, and became significantly long sori, usually 7 cm or longer. Teliospores were 2-celled, ellipsoidal to oval, slightly strict at the septum, 42.6-70.6(-92.2) × 19.6-29.4(-31.4) μm (average: 56.5 × 24.9 μm) (Fig. 4A, B). Wall was slightly thickened at the apex 2.9-7.8 μm (average: 4.9 μm), 2.0-3.9 μm (average: 3.0 μm) at the side. Pedicel was mostly up to 300(-333) μm. One-celled teliospores might be produced (Fig. 4C).

4. Discussion

Results of the present study demonstrated that host alternation of Puccinia sp. 1 between Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua (Polygonaceae) and Ph. australis. Among the rust species on Ph. australis, P. phragmitis was the only species using polygonaceous plants as aecial hosts (Hiratsuka et al., 1992; Plant parasites of Europe, 2025). Harada (1978b, 1987) recognized four biological forms which are specialized on their aecial host plants in P. phragmitis in Japan. One of the four special forms of the species, P. phragmitis f. sp. polygoni-thunbergii Harada, used Persicaria thunbergii (Siebold et Zucc.) H. Gross var. thunbergii (=Polygonum thunbergii Siebold et Zucc.) (Harada, 1987). Other Persicaria species including Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua were not included in the aecial host of the special form. In our field survey near Lake Kasumigaura, there was no record of aecidium production on Persicaria species except for Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua. Results of our inoculation experiments revealed that Puccinia sp. 1 did not use three species of Rumex which were known as aecial host plants of P. phragmitis (Hiratsuka et al., 1992). Thus, host range of Puccinia sp. 1 was limited and most likely differ from P. phragmitis.

Morphological characteristics of the uredinial and telial stages of Puccinia sp. 1 and those in descriptions of Puccinia species on Ph. australis with similar characteristics and of the 5 species recorded in Japan were shown in Table 7. Characteristics of the telial stage of Puccinia sp. 1 was most similar to P. trabutii var. abei, except for wall thickness of teliospores, which were described as uniform at apex and side (Hirastuka, 1937; Cummins, 1971). We confirmed that wall of teliospores of type specimen (PUR F12996) of P. abei (= P. trabutii var. abei) was slightly thicker at the apex than at the side. Thus, morphological characteristics of teliospores of type specimen of P. abei were almost the same as Puccinia sp. 1 and differed from P. trabutii. Urediniospores of Puccinia sp. 1 were morphologically different from those of P. trabuti in wall thickness and arrangement and number of germ pores. Arrangement and number of germ pores were considered to have constant characteristics within species (Cummins & Hiratsuka, 2003; Hiratsuka & Sato, 1982; Kaneko & Hiratsuka, 1982).

Puccinia sp. 1 was also similar to P. phragmitis in morphology of teliospores. However, Puccinia sp. 1 had larger teliospores with longer pedicel than P. phragmitis and produced long telia only on culms, while telia of P. phragmitis were amphigenous. Wall thickness and germ pore arrangement of urediniosproes were also different between the two species. Thus, these two species were distinguishable. The fact that the two species were accommodated into distinct clades in the phylogenetic analysis supports the morphological results. Morphological characteristics of aecial stages, e.g. shape, dimensions, surface structures of aeciospores (Cummins, 1971; Harada, 1978b, 1987; Hiratsuka et al., 1992), surface structures of peridial cells of Puccinia sp. 1 and P. phragmitis were indistinguishable (data not shown).

Based on results of the morphological comparison with descriptions and specimens including isotypes of P. abei and P. trabutii, we concluded that Puccinia sp. 1 was identified as P. abei and proposed that it was treated as distinct species, not a variety of P. trabutii. Because it had urediniospores with different arrangement and number of germ pores from P. trabutii, produced unique telia only on culms, and was recorded only in Japan so far, while P. trabutii was recorded in northern Africa, southern U.S.S.R. and Pakistan (Cummins, 1971). In this study, however, we could not include DNA sequence data of P. trabutii and those from type specimens of both P. abei and P. trabutii in the phylogenetic analysis. Sequences of the rust species parasitic on Ph. australis also could not be included since available DNA sequence data were limited in the GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, accessed 27 Aug 2025). Further molecular phylogenic studies were required.

Puccinia species seemed to be closely related to P. abei contained various taxonomic consideration. Synonymy of some of the species were controversial among scientists. For example, P. trabutii was treated as distinct species by Cummins (1971), while it was treated as synonym of P. isiacae together with P. obtusata Otth (Plant parasites of Europe, 2025; Tranzschel, 1906). Puccinia isiacae had broad host range of aecial host covering several families, like Amaranthaceae, Apiaceae, Boraginaceae, Caryophyllaceae, Lamiaceae, Plantaginaceae, and Valerianaceae (Plant parasites of Europe, 2025). and is distributed in the wide areas, Africa, Europe and Aesia (Baka, 2004; Baka & Gjaerum, 1996; Cummins, 1971). It might contain cryptic species or specialized forms regarding its aecial hosts, like P. phragmitis. Harada (1987) recognized four special forms with differentiated aecial host specificity among P. phragmitis in Japan and suspected the presence of specialized forms in Europe. Since this species is distributed world-wide (Cummins, 1971), more subspecies groups differentiated in aecial host specificity or cryptic species might be present. Comprehensive studies on molecular phylogeny, morphology, life cycle and host specificity of these related rust species were required to clarify biology and taxonomy of these species including P. abei.

Examples of the definite relationship between rust fungi and host plants, i.e. related rust fungi use restricted range of host plant in the same genus or family, were commonly recognized among uredinologists even before introducing molecular phylogenic studies (e.g., Cummins & Hiratsuka, 2003; Savile, 1979), although Puccinia and Uromyces contained species parasitize plants of many families. Yamaoka (2017) conducted molecular phylogenic studies of rust fungi using ITS2-D1/D2 region of rDNA of Pucciniaceae s.l. including P. magnusian, P. okatamaensis, P. phragmitis and P. trabutii var. abei. (as Aecidium sp. 2 on Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua). It was revealed that phylogenetically closely related rust species used host plants in the same genus, subfamily, or family. Puccinia species parasitic on Ph. australis were the good examples, that is P. magnusian and P. okatamaensis which use Rananculaceae, and P. phragmitis and P. trabutii var. abei. which use Polygonaceae as aecial hosts, were closely related in the molecular phylogenic analysis, respectively. In addition, P. arundinis-donacis T. Hirats. on Arundo donax L. was closely related to P. phragmitis and P. trabutii var. abei. Spermogonila and aecial stages of P. arundinis-donacis was unknown, but they might be on polygonaceous plants. To demonstrate its life cycle, inoculation experiments are required. The present studies gave another example that the phylogenetically closely related rust species used closely related host plants. Molecular phylogenetic analysis is a useful tool to find candidates of alternate host of heteroecious rust fungi, although we need to consider the possibility of variation of life cycle, i.e., autoecious or microcyclic life cycles and possibility of host jump (e.g., Savile, 1990).

Phragmites australis is widely distributed from the subarctic zone to the warm-temperate zone (Osada, 1993) and is widespread in Japan. On the other hand, distribution of Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua is limited to Japan, Korea and China (Yonekura, 2012). Persicaria odorata subsp. conspicua is considered as endangered or near-threatened species in some prefectures in Japan (https://ikilog.biodic.go.jp/Rdb/pref, accessed 23 Apr 2024). In the lake shore of Kasumigaura Lake, both plants were closely distributed, so that it is possible for P. abei to complete its life cycle. Nevertheless, P. abei was rarely found on Ph. australis. The reasons might be (1) infection with aeciospores were limited in the young fresh tissues of culm, so that the distance to the nearest plant of Ph. australis and periods possible for infection by aeciospores were restricted, (2) less production of urediniospores reduced expansion of distribution areas by repeated infection with urediniospores on Ph. australis. Thus, the distribution areas of this species may be limited in the areas where both Ph. australis and Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua were living. Puccinia abei might also be endangered or near-threatened, like Pe. odorata subsp. conspicua

Spermogonia and aecia were produced on swollen lesion yellowish to reddish in color on leaves. Spermogonia were amphigenous and flask-shaped (type 4 of Hiratsuka & Cummins, 1963), (78-) 86-133 μm (average: 104.9 μm) in diam. Aecia were hypophyllous and Aecidium type with peridium. Peridial cells irregular polygonal with a concave inner wall, verrucose, 19.5-33.5 × (13.5-)15.5-25.5(-29.5) μm (average: 25.6 × 21.0 μm). Inner wall 3.0-6.0 μm (average: 4.0 μm) thick. Outer wall (5.0-)6.0-12.5 μm thick (average: 8.3 μm). Each aecium in a lesion was separated by well-developed hyphoid stroma. Aeciospores were subglobose to broadly ellipsoidal, slightly angular, 16.5-23.5(-24.5) × 13.5-20.5(-21.5) um (average: 20.4 × 17.8 μm). Wall was 1.0-1.5 μm (average: 1.1 μm) thick. Surface was verrucouse, warts and small plugs in the coarse belt (Type 3 of Savile, 1973).

Uredinia were formed on striate brown lesions on culms, but were observed only in the early stage of sorus development and were rarely seen. Urediniospores were ellipsoid, broadly ellipsoid or obovoid, pale yellow to pale yellowish brown, 23.5-31.5(-33.0) × 17.5-26.0(-27.5) μm (average: 27.4 × 21.7 μm). Wall was 1.5-2.5 μm (average: 2.0 μm) thick. Surface was echinulate. Germ pores were 6-8, scattered.

Telia were produced on culms, compact and chocolate-brown, confluent in a group, and became significantly long sori, usually 7 cm or longer. Teliospores were 2-celled, oblong, oblong-clavate or ellipsoidal, slightly strict at the septum, golden brown or chestnut-brown, 42.5-92.0 × 19.5-29.5 μm (average: 56.6 × 24.9 μm). Wall was slightly thickened at apex 3.0-8.0 μm (average: 4.9 μm) thick, 2.0-4.0 (-5.0) μm (average: 3.1 μm) thick at side. Pedicel was hyaline, mostly up to 300, rarely more than 400 μm.

Basidiospores were 1-celled, ellipsoid with a hilum, hyaline, 15-18 × 8-12 μm (average: 16.6 × 10.7 μm).
