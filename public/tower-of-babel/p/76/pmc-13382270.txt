Individualized assignment to short-term alcohol abstinence: A proof-of-concept human laboratory study using psychopathology dimensions☆
Authors: Evangelia Argyriou, Giorgios Bakoyannis, Wei Wu, Martin H. Plawecki, Jesse C. Stewart, Ann E.K. Kosobud, Melissa A. Cyders
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlcohol risk prevention strategies have recently incorporated temporary, short-term abstinence to reduce overall alcohol risk and health and social impacts on both individuals and society. However, experimental research in animals and humans has shown that, for some, short-term abstinence followed by reinstatement of drinking escalates, instead of reduces, subsequent alcohol use. The goal of this project was to conduct a proof-of-concept study using a novel precision approach to optimize assignment to short-term abstinence.MethodsThe sample consisted of 48 healthy adults aged 21–35 (Median=24.5, Interquartile Range (IQR)= 6, 67% female, 83% White) who endorsed moderate-to-heavy alcohol use. Participants completed two counterbalanced intravenous alcohol self-administration sessions, one following a two-week period of alcohol abstinence and one during usual drinking. Crossover generalized outcome weighted learning was used to estimate an optimal individualized assignment rule for short-term abstinence, based on relevant psychopathology dimensions and other individual characteristics as prescriptive factors.ResultsFindings showed that an estimated rule with higher granularity – higher-specificity traits and demographics – led to lower overall alcohol consumption compared to one-size-fits-all rules. The effect sizes of the difference were small-to-medium but did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the small sample size.DiscussionResults provide preliminary evidence that individualized assignment rules based on simple, accessible characteristics warrant further examination as an approach to identify individuals who could benefit from short-term alcohol abstinence. This line of research has significant long-term implications for tailoring interventions to individual needs, thereby enhancing outcomes. Further research steps are necessary to realize its full impact.

Introduction

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) affects millions of men and women worldwide and is associated with substantial health and psychiatric consequences (Knox et al., 2019, World Health Organization., 2018). Risk prevention strategies often incorporate temporary, short-term abstinence periods (e.g., Dry January in the UK and USA; Dry July in Australia and New Zealand) to reduce health and social impacts on both individuals and society (de Visser et al., 2017). However, experimental research in animals and humans has shown that, for some, short-term abstinence followed by reinstatement of drinking escalates, instead of reduces, subsequent alcohol use (Bell et al., 2008, García-Burgos et al., 2010, Plawecki et al., 2018, Vengeliene et al., 2005). This study tested a proof-of-concept precision approach to optimize assignment to short-term alcohol abstinence among non-treatment seeking individuals who engage into moderate-to-heavy drinking.

Preclinical research on the effect of drinking reinstatement following periods of abstinence has shown robust temporary increase in alcohol consumption, especially when periods of alcohol access are intermittent (Bell et al., 2008, García-Burgos et al., 2010, Hopf et al., 2010, Hwa et al., 2011, Hwa et al., 2016, Kimbrough et al., 2017, Rosenwasser et al., 2013, Vengeliene et al., 2014, Vengeliene et al., 2005). Human research has similarly suggested that alternating periods of abstinence and reinstatement escalates alcohol use over time (Bowen et al., 2022). Only one experimental study has documented differential responses to a short-term period of alcohol abstinence in humans (Plawecki et al., 2018). This study found that, following a short-term period of abstinence, women increased alcohol seeking, whereas men decreased, a phenomenon they referred to as “post-abstinence response.” This finding is supported by evidence from voluntary abstinence challenges, where some participants experienced “rebound effects” (i.e., increased their alcohol consumption following reinitiating use) (Butters et al., 2023, de Visser and Piper, 2020).

Factors related to this rebound are not well understood (Butters et al., 2023). Preclinical models suggest that the genetic background of the animal, sex, duration of the alcohol access period, and withdrawal may contribute to differences in post-abstinence response (Rosenwasser et al., 2013, Sinclair et al., 1973, Vengeliene et al., 2014, Vengeliene et al., 2003, Vengeliene et al., 2005, Wolffgramm and Heyne, 1995). Although human research is limited, hazardous drinking and withdrawal-related processes have been proposed as contributors to rebound drinking (Butters et al., 2023, Castro-Calvo et al., 2018, Sharma and Anand, 2019). In addition, a combination of demographic variables, such as sex and race, have shown to differentially predict alcohol use patterns and may also affect post-abstinence response. Previous research has shown associations of these demographic variables with alcohol consumption, reinitiation of alcohol use, and use patterns (Karyadi et al., 2013, Leigh and Stacy, 2004, Mulia et al., 2009, Plawecki et al., 2018, Sliedrecht et al., 2019, Zapolski et al., 2014). For example, a younger age of onset is associated with higher reinitiation of alcohol use risk (Sliedrecht et al., 2019). Sex was a key predictor following abstinence, with females showing increased and male decreased motivation to seek alcohol (Plawecki et al., 2018). Although the role of sex remains inclusive, evidence shows more rapid progression to severe alcohol use disorder among women (Sliedrecht et al., 2019).

Precision health approaches can be used to predict who reduces or increases drinking after a period of abstinence (Argyriou et al., 2025, Cohen and DeRubeis, 2018). Existing precision medicine approaches for AUD have focused on identifying etiologic mechanisms or biomarkers that explain heterogeneity within alcohol use patterns and risk profiles (Boness and Witkiewitz, 2023, Kranzler et al., 2017). Although these approaches have advanced understanding of AUD heterogeneity they require specialized assessment, are costly and cannot be implemented routinely (Argyriou et al., 2025, Boness et al., 2021). A complementary strategy is to use readily available individual characteristics to estimate individualized assignment rules, which identify which intervention among a set of alternatives, is expected to produce better outcomes for a given individual based on a set of prescriptive factors (Kosorok and Laber, 2019, Lavori and Dawson, 2014). In the present proof-of-concept study, we apply this framework to an experimental short-term abstinence paradigm to examine whether accessible clinical characteristics can improve individualized assignment to abstinence versus usual drinking relative to one-size-fits-all approaches.

A key challenge in developing individualized assignment rules is selecting prescriptive factors that are both clinically accessible and theoretically relevant to alcohol outcomes. Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP) provides one such framework by organizing psychopathology into transdiagnostic dimensions (Kotov et al., 2017). HiTOP captures psychopathology hierarchically, ranging from broad spectra to more specific traits, allowing examination of predictors at multiple levels of specificity (Conway et al., 2019, Kotov et al., 2017, Ruggero et al., 2019). HiTOP dimensions show promise for predicting post-abstinence response, yet they remain underexplored. Traits aligned with HiTOP, such as neuroticism (negative affectivity), antagonism, hopelessness, extraversion (inversely associated with detachment), low conscientiousness, affective lability and negative urgency, impulsivity, novelty seeking, low persistence, low cooperativeness, anxiousness, and low harm avoidance (inversely related to risk taking) have been linked to AUD recurrence and alcohol use consumption (Adan et al., 2017, Atkinson et al., 2020, Brunborg, 2017; Coskunpinar et al., 2013; Creswell et al., 2016; Evren et al., 2012; Foulds et al., 2017; Hakulinen et al., 2015; Lagerberg et al., 2017; Schuckit and Smith, 2011; Simons et al., 2004; Turiano et al., 2012; Virtanen et al., 2015; Willinger et al., 2002).

In sum, evidence suggests that alcohol abstinence has differential effects across individuals, ranging from benefit to harm, and that HiTOP-relevant dimensions may help tailor recommendations based on this heterogeneity. Personalizing abstinence goals to individual characteristics is key to maximizing intervention benefits. This proof-of-concept study examined the utility of HiTOP-dimensions in optimizing short-term alcohol abstinence assignment among non-treatment seeking adults who drink alcohol moderately to heavily. To measure alcohol response consistently, we used an intravenous alcohol self-administration procedure, which eliminates individual variability in brain alcohol exposure (Cyders et al., 2020). We estimated individualized assignment rules using individual characteristics as prescriptive variables to optimize assignment to short-term, two-week period of alcohol abstinence (versus usual drinking). We hypothesized that such individualized assignment rules would yield lower overall alcohol consumption compared to one-size-fits-all rules where everyone is assigned to abstinence. We tested rules using higher-specificity HiTOP dimensions for higher granularity, lower-specificity dimensions for parsimony, and rules based solely on demographics or combining both to assess the added value of HiTOP dimensions.

Method

Participants

This study was a secondary data analysis that utilized data from a parent project (R01 AA027236) with primary goal to examine sex differences in response to abstinence from alcohol. The sample included 48 healthy men and women aged 21–35 who drank moderately to heavily (at least five drinks/week for women, ten/week for men) in the past five weeks. Participants were recruited via ads, word of mouth, campus materials, and community events. Inclusion criteria required English proficiency, BMI between 18.5 and 32 kg/m², and adequate venous access for blood sampling. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or breastfeeding, desire for alcohol/substance use treatment or court-ordered abstinence, medical conditions or medications affecting safety or data, severe psychopathology (e.g., bipolar, psychosis), neurological disease, significant head injury, active or serious suicidal ideation, or positive BrAC on study visit arrival.

Measures

Demographics

Participants self-reported their biological sex, age, and race.

The Personality Inventory for DSM−5 (PID−5)

The PID−5 is a multidimensional self-report scale proposed as an efficient measure of HiTOP (Stanton et al., 2020). The traits assessed in PID−5 are relevant to multiple HiTOP spectra (Krueger et al., 2012, Stanton et al., 2020). It consists of 220 items and evaluates 25 specific facets (each comprising 4–14 items) of personality traits, which combine into five broader domains: Negative Affect (sum of Emotional Lability, Anxiousness, Separation Insecurity), Detachment (sum of Withdrawal, Anhedonia, Intimacy Avoidance), Antagonism (sum of Manipulativeness, Deceitfulness, Grandiosity), Disinhibition (sum of Irresponsibility, Impulsivity, Distractibility), and Psychoticism (sum of Unusual Beliefs & Experiences, Eccentricity, Perceptual Dysregulation). Broader domains correspond to the Internalizing, Detachment, Antagonistic Externalizing, Disinhibited Externalizing, and Thought Disorder HiTOP spectra. The 25 specific facets correspond to the component/trait specificity level of HiTOP. Participants rate how well each item describes them on a 0–3 scale, and scores for facets and domains are averaged. The PID−5 has demonstrated adequate reliability and validity over gold-standard personality trait measures (Fowler et al., 2017, Krueger et al., 2012).

Alcohol Timeline Followback

The alcohol Timeline Followback (TLFB) is a retrospective measure of daily alcohol consumption that asks participants to estimate their day-by-day alcohol use over a period of time (Sobell and Sobell, 1992, Sobell and Sobell, 2003). Participants were presented with a calendar and were asked to write important events as prompts of memory to estimate the amount of daily alcohol use, in standard drinks, over the reporting period. This measure has shown good psychometric properties and is a clinically useful measure of an individual’s drinking (Sobell and Sobell, 2003, Sobell et al., 2003). Based on participants’ reports over the past 35 days before the interview date, the total number of drinks was calculated and used as a measure of baseline drinking.

Procedures

Approval for the study procedures was secured by the Indiana University Indianapolis Internal Review Board. Interested participants completed phone or internet-based screening; those who met initial inclusion were scheduled for a full study interview, which included introduction to study procedures, informed consent and study interview and baseline measures. Participants were randomly assigned to either complete a free access alcohol self-administration condition (N = 48) or a “clamp” condition (N = 50); only data from the self-administration sessions were used for the current study.

Each participant completed two counter-balanced intravenous alcohol self-administration sessions and four abstinence interval interim lab visits. The Resumption session ended two weeks of monitored alcohol abstinence. The Usual session was completed during the participant's usual drinking habits, either before the abstinence interval or 2 weeks after. Abstinence was monitored with an ankle monitor worn continuously throughout the two-week abstinence period. During their initiation of abstinence visit, participants were fitted with the Scram CAM Transdermal Alcohol Sensing device (Scram Systems, n.d.), they were instructed in care of the device, reminded of the protections provided by the Certificate of Confidentiality, and offered a letter indicating that they were participating in a research study. This device continuously monitors and detects alcohol through the wearer’s sweat. During the interval visits, data from the Scram device were downloaded and reviewed.

The Computer-assisted Alcohol Infusion System (CAIS) software controlled the alcohol infusion (Zimmermann et al., 2008, Zimmermann et al., 2008). Participant age, height, weight, and sex were used to customize the model for alcohol distribution and elimination (Plawecki et al., 2007). Infusion rate was calculated to achieve identical BrAC exposures across individuals. During the 2.5-hour free-access session, participants self-administered alcohol or water by pressing a button. Sessions had a safety limit of 150 mg/dl (~0.15%, nearly twice the U.S. legal driving limit).

Data analysis

All analyses were conducted using R statistical software. Preliminary analyses were conducted to assess data quality and order effects in the crossover design by comparing descriptive characteristics for the whole sample and by session order. Due to nonnormal distributions, medians and interquartile range were reported, and nonparametric tests were used for preliminary analyses. The Wilcoxon signed ranks test was used to compare each alcohol consumption outcome between the resumption and usual drinking sessions.

The main outcome was BrAC area under the curve (AUC, mg/dl*hour), representing total alcohol exposure over time (Plawecki et al., 2012). Prescriptive variables included demographics, baseline alcohol use, and HiTOP-related personality traits at higher- and lower-levels (Stanton et al., 2020). Continuous variables were standardized by subtracting the sample mean and dividing by the sample standard deviation, resulting in variables with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 to remove the influence of scale in the main analyses.

For the estimation of the optimal individualized assignment rules, we used the crossover generalized outcome weighted learning approach proposed by Nguyen et al. (2020), which is a machine learning method that utilizes a weighted version of support vector machines and the statistical causal inference framework to estimate an optimal decision function for assigning individuals to two interventions. This method takes into account the crossover design of our study, adjusts for potential carryover effects, and does not impose restrictive model assumptions (Kosorok and Laber, 2019, Nguyen et al., 2020). A linear decision function was used because of the limited sample size and to be able to conduct inference (Bakoyannis, 2023). The performance of the estimated individualized assignment rules was assessed by 1) estimating the value function (Vdopt) – the expected mean AUC BrAC if everyone was assigned to the best for them condition according to the rule and 2) comparing the mean AUC BrAC under the rule to those under the one-size-fits-all rules where everyone is assigned to abstinence (V1), or none is assigned to abstinence (V0).

L2 (ridge) penalization was used to control the complexity of the decision function and avoid overfitting. Leave-one-out cross-validation (jackknife method) was employed to evaluate the out-of-sample performance of different penalty parameters and the estimated optimal rule (Bakoyannis, 2023). Standard errors for the estimated value function were computed using nonparametric bootstrap with 1000 replications.

Five individualized assignment rules were estimated. Rule 1 included only broad low-specificity HiTOP-relevant dimensions as prescriptive factors. Rule 2 included only higher-specificity HiTOP-relevant dimensions. Rule 3 included only demographics (sex, age, and baseline drinking in the past 35 days). Rule 4 included broad HiTOP-relevant dimensions and demographics. Rule 5 included higher-specificity HiTOP-relevant dimensions and demographics. Estimating these rules allows evaluating the joint and separate contributions of different groups of prescriptive factors. The Vdopt of each rule was compared to fixed – one-size-fits-all – rules V1 and V0. An individualized rule was considered superior to the fixed rules if either it 1) showed significantly lower mean BrAC AUC or, 2) in case of non-significance, produced lower mean BrAC AUC with at least a small effect size (> 0.177; see Panjeh et al., 2023). Otherwise, the best of the two fixed rules would be considered superior. It is important to note that, given that the sample size was small, the risk of overfitting cannot be completely avoided despite the use of penalization methods. Therefore, we consider this study as a proof-concept hypothesis-generating preliminary study.

Rule 1 included as prescriptive variables only lower-specificity HiTOP-relevant dimensions (i.e., Negative Affect, Disinhibition, Antagonism, Psychoticism, Detachment). This rule led to estimated mean BrAC AUC of 20189.43 (95% CIs: 14238.40, 26140.46). Mean BrAC AUC when participants were assigned to abstinence or usual drinking in accordance with Rule 1 was not significantly different to mean BrAC AUC when participants were assigned in accordance to the fixed rules, i.e., assigning everyone to abstinence (Vdopt – V1= 0.06, 95% CI: −0.24, 0.37) or assigning none to abstinence (Vdopt – V0 = 0.10, 95% CIs: −0.21, 0.40).

Rule 2 including only higher-specificity HiTOP-relevant dimensions as prescriptive variables led to estimated mean BrAC AUC of 14533.90 (95% CIs: 7419.15, 21648.65). Mean BrAC AUC under Rule 2 was not significantly different to mean BrAC AUC under the fixed rules (Vdopt – V1 = −0.18, 95% CIs: −0.48, 0.13; Vdopt – V0 = −0.15, 95% CIs: −0.45, 0.15). Trends from Rules 1 and 2 analyses show that broad HiTOP dimensions have no utility in abstinence assignment, either alone or in combination with demographics.

Rule 3 including only demographics as prescriptive variables led to estimated mean BrAC AUC of 17791.74 (95% CI: 10821.99, 24761.50). Mean BrAC AUC under Rule 3 was not significantly different to mean BrAC AUC under the fixed rules (Vdopt – V1 = −0.05, 95% CI: −0.35, 0.25; Vdopt – V0 = −0.00, 95% CI: −0.31, 0.30). Trends from Rule 3 analysis show that demographics have no utility in abstinence assignment on their own.

Rule 4 including lower-specificity HiTOP-relevant dimensions and demographics as prescriptive variables led to estimated mean BrAC AUC of 18192.09 (95% CI: 11956.10, 24428.08). Mean BrAC AUC under Rule 4 was not significantly different to mean BrAC AUC under the fixed rules (Vdopt – V1 = −0.04, 95% CI: −0.34, 0.27; Vdopt – V0 = 0.01, 95% CI: −0.23, 0.32).

Rule 5 including higher-specificity HiTOP-relevant dimensions and demographics as prescriptive variables produced an estimated mean BrAC AUC of 12432.51 (95% CI: 11956.10, 24428.08). Mean BrAC AUC under Rule 5 was not significantly different to mean BrAC AUC under the fixed rules (Vdopt – V1 = −0.28, 95% CI: −0.58, 0.02; Vdopt – V0 = −0.22, 95% CI: −0.52, 0.08). However, assignment to abstinence in accordance with Rule 5 was associated with mean reduction of 0.22–0.28 SDs in BrAC AUC compared to the fixed rules (small-to-medium effect). Trends from Rules 4 and 5 analyses show that high-specificity HiTOP dimensions have some utility on their own and are especially useful when combined with demographics.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept study applied a precision medicine framework, grounded in dimensional psychopathology, to explore individualized assignment rules for an experimental short-term alcohol abstinence paradigm. The findings suggest that an individualized rule based on higher-specificity traits and demographics was associated with lower overall alcohol consumption than one-size-fits-all approaches. However, this difference was not statistically significant, likely reflecting the limited statistical power of the study. Although the observed effect was small to medium in magnitude, these findings should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than clinically actionable. They provide preliminary evidence that simple, accessible individual characteristics may have potential to inform more effective abstinence assignment, and should be explored in larger, adequately powered studies.

Preliminary results showed that using HiTOP-based traits and demographics as prescriptive factors produced the lowest BrAC AUC, indicating reduced alcohol consumption compared to assigning everyone to abstinence or usual drinking. This trend aligns with prior research linking high specificity HiTOP-related traits and demographics (e.g., sex) to alcohol use, AUD reinitiation of alcohol use, and abstinence response (Adan et al., 2017, Atkinson et al., 2020; Coskunpinar et al., 2013; De la Rosa-Cáceres et al., 2022; Evren et al., 2012; Foulds et al., 2017; Plawecki et al., 2018; Schuckit and Smith, 2011; Simons et al., 2004; Vengeliene et al., 2014; Vengeliene et al., 2005; Willinger et al., 2002). However, this difference was not statistically significant, and thus higher-specificity HiTOP traits and demographic characteristics warrant further investigation as prescriptive factors for abstinence assignment in larger studies.

Estimated rules using broader HiTOP dimensions (i.e., Negative Affect, Antagonism, Disinhibition, and Psychoticism) did not differ statistically or clinically from the one-size-fits-all rules. This may be due to competing prescriptive effects within the broader traits; for example, intimacy avoidance and anhedonia both fall under Detachment, but abstinence is suggested for higher intimacy avoidance, whereas usual drinking is suggested for higher anhedonia. If replicated, this would indicate that broad HiTOP factors are insufficient to explain individual variation in abstinence response, highlighting the need for more granular, lower-level traits. A key strength of HiTOP is its ability to assess psychopathology at multiple levels of specificity (Conway et al., 2019, Kotov et al., 2018, Kotov et al., 2017, Ruggero et al., 2019, Widiger et al., 2018), allowing for comparison of individualized rules by granularity. Larger studies are needed to directly test whether higher-specifically traits improve abstinence assignment over broader dimensions.

The current study adds to the emerging evidence of individual differences in response to short-term alcohol abstinence in humans (Butters et al., 2023, Plawecki et al., 2018). While those who drink hazardously are often advised to abstain, some may increase their alcohol use after resuming drinking (Bell et al., 2008, García-Burgos et al., 2010, Plawecki et al., 2018, Vengeliene et al., 2005). Given this variability, temporary abstinence efforts (e.g., “Dry January”) may increase risk for some (Butters et al., 2023, de Visser and Piper, 2020). Our findings provide preliminary support that abstinence is not universally beneficial, aligning with growing recognition of individual recovery differences and the need to develop and test harm reduction alternatives (Paquette et al., 2022).

This study was a proof-of-concept to catalyze future research, rather than to provide immediate clinical impact. As such it demonstrates the feasibility of using machine learning to create personalized alcohol abstinence assignment rules. Before clinical application can be considered, several next steps are needed for future research: 1) replicate the study with a larger sample to reduce overfitting (Rudolph et al., 2023) and increase power for rule comparison; 2) test rules using alternative prescriptive factors (e.g., drinking motives, self-efficacy, coping, behavioral tasks, genetics, neurophysiology) and evaluate predictive accuracy and implementation costs (Conway et al., 2019); 3) assess effectiveness across different abstinence durations, as longer abstinence may benefit certain subgroups (Sliedrecht et al., 2019, Sinclair et al., 1973, Vengeliene et al., 2014, Wolffgramm and Heyne, 1995); 4) test the rule in real-world settings; and 5) compare outcomes across individualized, one-size-fits-all, and no-assignment conditions to determine practical utility.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the sample size was small due to study design and COVID−19 delays. Although effect sizes were small to medium, findings should be interpreted cautiously and replicated in larger samples to assess generalizability. Second, due to the small sample, we could not evaluate out-of-sample performance. Relatedly, since Rule 5 included multiple high-specificity PID−5 traits and demographic variables in this relatively small sample, the model may be vulnerable to overfitting, and the estimated coefficients should be interpreted cautiously until replicated in larger, independent samples. Third, most participants identified as White, limiting the inclusion of race as a prescriptive factor and the applicability of results to non-White populations. Fourth, the binary decision rule (abstinence vs. no abstinence) may overlook small benefits but aligns with practical clinical decision-making. Fifth, participants consumed alcohol at moderate-to-heavy levels but were not treatment-seeking, so findings may not generalize to clinical populations. Sixth, although the PID−5 captures the HiTOP structure via trait dimensions, it does not assess symptom components and relies on self-report. Future studies may benefit from clinician-rated measures or tools that capture both traits and symptoms to better represent HiTOP. Finally, the study used intravenous alcohol self-administration, which offers precise control over BrAC and avoids the variability seen with oral ingestion (Cyders et al., 2020). While this method minimizes confounding from absorption kinetics, ethical concerns, including medical risk and contraindications, were considered. These were addressed through careful screening, continuous monitoring, and informed consent.

This study is the first, to our knowledge, to use a machine-learning precision medicine approach to optimize short-term alcohol abstinence assignment based on HiTOP-relevant dimensions and demographics. Results provide preliminary evidence that individualized assignment rules based on simple, accessible characteristics warrant further examination as an approach to identify individuals who could benefit from short-term alcohol abstinence. This line of research has significant long-term implications for tailoring interventions to individual needs, thereby enhancing outcomes. Further research steps are necessary to realize its full impact.
