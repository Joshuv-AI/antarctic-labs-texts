Integrated geotechnical and geomorphological implication of urbanization on slope stability in the vicinity of GRA hills Nsukka southeast Nigeria
Authors: Emmanuela Nnedimma Nwafor, Ogbonnaya Igwe, Kelechi Paulinus Ukor, Kelechi Nnaji Eze, Obinna Hyginus Ozioko, Uchenna Joseph Ezea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the impact of indiscriminate construction on slope stability in Nsukka, Enugu State, Nigeria, through geotechnical analysis and slope stability simulations. Ten soil samples were collected and tested for grain size, Atterberg limits, compaction, shear strength, and permeability. The results showed that the soils were composed of 13.29–20.41% gravel, 67.61–89.61% sand, and 6.00–12.17% fines, indicating sandy soil with medium plasticity (liquid limit: 30.5–38.6%, plasticity index: 11.9–16.2%). Compaction tests revealed an optimum moisture content of 1.64–1.75 kg/m³ and maximum dry density of 13.1–16.5%, suggesting moderate water retention and low compaction. The shear strength tests indicated weak soils with cohesion values between 10.3 and 16 kPa and internal friction angles ranging from 23° to 31°, prone to shear failure. Permeability results ranged from 1.79 × 10⁻⁵ to 2.95 × 10⁻⁴ m/s, indicating poor to moderate drainage capacity. Slope stability analysis revealed potential slide masses of 45.93–130.63 m³ and 44.93–132.63 m³ under unsaturated and saturated conditions, respectively. The factor of safety ranged from 0.80 to 1.30 (unsaturated) and 0.77 to 1.26 (saturated), indicating areas of instability with values < 1. Principal component analysis identified key geotechnical factors as the main contributors to slope failure. Satellite imagery analysis from 2010 to 2022 highlighted significant urbanization, which has exacerbated slope instability. The study concludes that anthropogenic activities, particularly unplanned construction, are the primary cause of landslides in Nsukka. It recommends better urban planning, improved construction practices, and slope stabilization measures to reduce future landslide risks.Keywords: Slope stability, Factor of safety, Sliding, Satellite imagery, GeoStudioSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Natural hazards

Introduction

Slope failures are becoming increasingly prevalent, leading to significant loss of human and animal life, as well as damage or destruction of residential and industrial infrastructure Miller and Patel1. Landslides occur when the stability of a slope transitions from a stable to an unstable condition2. In a study by Igwe3, factors such as slope geometry, soil properties, and the frequency and intensity of precipitation were identified as key contributors to landslide initiation.

The construction of buildings and the development of cities on steep-slope terrain has presented a significant challenge throughout history. The impact on the environment can be significant, particularly in instances where the soil conditions are poor. The disturbance of the fragile slopes can result in severe environmental consequences4. Annually, substantial slope failures cause millions of dollars of damage to infrastructure and have a devastating impact on people and the environment. It is therefore imperative that slope stability assessments are carried out to determine the strength of the slope and its susceptibility to failure in order to implement appropriate mitigation measures. Numerous researchers, including Chowdhury and Flentje5, Igwe and Chukwu6, Dai et al.7, Lateltin et al.8, Igwe and Fukuoka9, and Schuster and Highland10, have highlighted the destructive potential of landslides and the effectiveness of mitigation techniques such as bioengineering, slope reinforcement, slope modification, and retaining wall construction.

Urbanization expansion at GRA and environs in Nsukka, Enugu state, Southern Nigeria, has triggered distinctive active and dormant landslide which have led to several economic and environmental hazards (Fig. 1). The geologic formations that host these landslides are distinct. These formations are characterized by different textural, geotechnical and hydrogeological properties such as grain size, shear strength, porosity and permeability. Therefore, it is possible that the factors that initiate and propagate landslides in the study area could differ. The increase in rate of landslides as a result of natural and anthropogenic factors has endangered lives and properties of the locals which led to destruction of some social amenities. Hence, investigating the engineering-geological properties, assess the stability and comparing their intensity using slope stability simulations to ascertain the effect of indiscriminate anthropogenic activities on the slope stability around the study area is crucial. These would be obtainable by evaluating the geo-mechanical properties influencing the slope failure; developing a slope failure model using Geo studio to determine the intensity of the slope; and evaluate the impact of anthropogenic activities on the slope failure build up over-time with the aid of aerial photographs. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) will also be applied as a multivariate statistical tool to isolate the most influential geotechnical parameters governing slope stability and to interpret the underlying correlations among them. This information will be utilized in filling the missing key findings that previous studies in the region largely overlooked as regards long-term monitoring. This study filled that gap by integrating multi-temporal satellite imagery and geotechnical data to trace the progressive effects of urbanization on slope behavior the technical understanding of how unplanned urban growth exacerbates slope instability in geologically sensitive zones which will be critical for effective planning and management of landslide-prone areas.

Physiography and geology of the study area

The study area is located within the Nsukka Area of Enugu State, and consist of generally undulating topography. The study areas have altitudes in the range of 320–516 m that lies between latitude 60511011 N to 605213011 N and Longitude 702113011 E to 70241011 E. The sites can be accessed through Nsukka – Edem road (Figs. 2 and 3). The study area is located within the equatorial climate, which is characterised by two seasons: the rainy season and the dry season. These seasons are defined by the occurrence of precipitation and the absence of precipitation, respectively11–13. The rainy season starts in April and lasts till October, while the dry season starts in November and lasts till March. The advent of the rainy season is accompanied by the arrival of south-westerly humid air. The study areas have dendritic drainage pattern flows in the N-S direction (Fig. 4). The area of study is situated beneath two distinct geologic formations: the Nsukka Formation and the Ajali Formation (Fig. 5). The Nsukka Formation is laterally extensive where it outcropped. Its lithology as it outcropped in the study area consists of reddish fine to medium-grained sands with lateritic conglomerate (pebble) at the base. The type locality of Ajali Sandstone was also found in the study area, which consists of highly ferruginized reddish sands at the top and whitish-brown fine to medium-grained sandstones below.

Methodology

Three phases were adopted in the course of this research, ranging from desk study to field mapping in which data was acquired, and then laboratory analysis of samples. A mapping exercise was conducted on-site, comprising reconnaissance and a detailed survey, with the objective of investigating the susceptibility of the slopes to failures and the hydrostatic pressure acting within the slopes. This was carried out in accordance with the guidelines set out in Crozier14. The primary fieldwork entailed the use of GPS technology to record geographical coordinates, the study of slope geometry, the observation of observable features, and the collection of other field measurements. A total of ten (10) samples were collected and were tagged E1. E2, E3, E4, E5, E6, E7, E8, E9 and E10. At every sampling point, about 5 kg of soil samples were collected after the removal of humus and/or artificial fertilizer so as to get fresh uncontaminated samples. The samples were taken to the Civil Engineering Laboratory of the University of Nigeria Nsukka for analysis. All tests were carried out in compliance with the relevant American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM) specifications15, 16. The samples were subjected to a series of tests to assess their particle size distribution, Atterberg limits, shear strength, compaction characteristics, natural moisture content, and permeability, including optimum moisture content and maximum dry density. Particle size distribution analysis was carried out according to the guidelines set out in ASTM D42115, using coarse sieve analysis. A shear strength test was performed using a triaxial machine in compliance with ASTM D4767. To ensure the reliability and accuracy of the geotechnical analysis, all laboratory instruments were calibrated according to manufacturer standards prior to testing. Error margins were carefully considered during both field data acquisition and laboratory testing, with repeated measurements conducted where necessary to reduce uncertainties. The results obtained from the field and laboratory were integrated into Slope/W Geostudio 2012 software to produce a modeled diagram of the gully slope morphology and failure prediction on the slope. The integrated results are the slope height, width and slope angles obtained from the field and shear strength parameters (cohesion and angle of internal friction), unit weight obtained from the laboratory. The Morgenstern-Price limit equilibrium method was used to run the slope stability analysis. Statistical analysis was carried out utilizing Microsoft excel sheet. Aerial photographs from Google Earth Pro were used to monitor the landslide for a ten-year period within the study area.

Grain size analysis

Table 2 presents the distribution of various grain sizes within the analyzed soil samples, while (Fig. 7) provides a graphical representation of the corresponding data. The particle size distribution analysis indicates that the slope material predominantly comprises gravel, sand, and silt/clay, with proportions ranging from 13.29% to 20.41%, 67.61% to 89.61%, and 6.00% to 12.17%, respectively. A detailed breakdown of the sand fraction reveals that coarse, medium, and fine sand constitute 26.98% to 39.83%, 19.62% to 36.65%, and 6.68% to 23.13%, respectively. These findings demonstrate that the soil samples are primarily composed of sand, with a notable dominance of medium to coarse sand-sized grains. The particle size distribution curve, as illustrated in Fig. 5, exhibits a steep gradient, suggesting a relatively uniform distribution of grain sizes.

According to Arora18, a well-graded soil is characterized by a coefficient of uniformity (CU) exceeding 6 and a coefficient of curvature (Cc) approximating 1. The analyzed slope materials exhibit CU values ranging from 7.58 to 13.5 and Cc values between 0.64 and 2.36, indicating that the soil is well-graded but poorly sorted. Based on the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS) as described by Arora18, the soil samples can be classified into three primary categories: well-graded sands (SW), well-graded sands with clay (SW-SC), and clayey sands (SC). The observed distribution of particle sizes suggests that the soil’s mechanical behavior aligns with these classifications.

The fines fraction, comprising the smallest proportion of the soil composition, plays a critical role in influencing soil-water interactions.

The relatively low clay content observed in the samples suggests an interconnected system of pore spaces, which enhances permeability. Igwe and Una19, reported that soils with reduced clay content tend to exhibit higher permeability, thereby increasing the likelihood of water infiltration. Consequently, the high permeability characteristics of the studied soil samples could contribute to slope instability and a heightened susceptibility to landslides under saturated conditions.

These geotechnical characteristics, particularly the dominance of coarse sand and the high permeability associated with low fines content, significantly influence slope stability and underscore the material’s vulnerability to failure under saturated or rapidly changing hydrologic conditions.

Moisture content and Atterberg limits

Table 1 presents the results of the moisture content and Atterberg limits tests conducted within the study area. The analysis of natural moisture content (NMC) indicates that the soil moisture levels fall within a moderate range, with values varying between 11.7% and 15.47%. The Atterberg limits assessment further reveals that the liquid limit (LL) ranges from 30.5% to 38.6%, the plastic limit (PL) from 18.4% to 22.4%, and the plasticity index (PI) from 11.9% to 16.2%. The plotted Atterberg limits, positioned above the A-line in the Casagrande chat (Fig. 8a), aligns with the findings from the grain size distribution analysis, confirming the consistency of the soil classification.

According to Seed et al.21, soils with a plasticity index below 12 and a liquid limit under 37% are prone to liquefaction. Based on the consistency limit results and the chart in (Fig. 8b), soil samples E4, E6, and E9 exhibit susceptibility to liquefaction, whereas the remaining samples show a lower tendency to liquefy due to their plasticity index values. The consistency limit analysis further indicates that the soils predominantly exhibit low to medium plasticity, which is consistent with the moderate moisture content recorded in the samples.

The integration of the analysis from the particle size distribution suggests that the fine fraction within the soil samples is primarily composed of clay. This observation is significant as it aligns with the findings of Igwe et al.22, who reported that soils with high clay content tend to restrict water movement, leading to an increase in pore water pressure. The increased pore pressure can negatively impact slope stability and exacerbate the potential for soil failure under saturated conditions.

Furthermore, the combination of moisture content and Atterberg limits results provides valuable insights into the geotechnical properties of the study area. The findings indicate that the soil exhibits moderate moisture content and low to medium plasticity, with certain samples demonstrating a potential risk of liquefaction. The predominance of clay in the fine fraction suggests possible drainage challenges and the potential for elevated pore water pressure, which could influence the overall stability of the soil in the presence of external loading or prolonged saturation, hence slope instability.

Permeability

The permeability test results indicate a range between 1.79 × 10⁻⁷ and 2.95 × 10⁻⁷ m/sec. According to Igwe et al.22,, soils with a permeability coefficient within the range of 10⁻⁷ to 10⁻⁵ m/sec are classified as moderately permeable. Based on this classification, the soil within the study area can be considered moderately permeable and it is in coherence with the grain size distribution analysis. It is well established that soils with a higher proportion of fine particles exhibit reduced permeability, which can significantly impact subsurface water movement. The observed low permeability of the soil contributes to the accumulation of excess pore water pressure, particularly during the rainy season. This phenomenon is primarily driven by elevated water levels, which reduce the effective stress within the soil matrix. Consequently, the reduction in shear strength of the soil material may lead to slope instability and potential failure. Igwe and Una19, further emphasized that increased water infiltration within slopes adversely affects their stability by weakening the structural integrity of the soil. The presence of water within the soil pores alters its mechanical properties, leading to a loss of cohesion and frictional resistance, which are critical factors in maintaining slope stability.

Overall, the permeability characteristics of the soil in the study area suggest that while it is classified as moderately permeable, the potential for slope failure remains a concern due to the influence of water infiltration and the accumulation of pore pressure during periods of heavy rainfall.

Compaction analysis

The results of the soil compaction analysis, as presented in Table 1, indicate that the optimum moisture content (OMC) of the soil ranges from 13.1% to 16.5%, while the maximum dry density (MDD) varies between 1.64 and 1.75 kg/cm³. These values suggest a moderate OMC and relatively low MDD, indicating that the soils within the hillslope areas are loose and possess limited structural support capacity. Consequently, such soils are highly susceptible to detachment and particle movement, increasing the potential for erosion and instability. As depicted in (Fig. 9), the composition and nature of the slope significantly influence the soil’s water-holding capacity and hydraulic conductivity. This observation aligns with the findings of Igwe and Egbueri12, who established a direct correlation between slope characteristics and soil hydrological behavior. The implications of these findings are critical, as continuous water infiltration into the slopes can lead to swelling or bulking of the soil, resulting in a reduction of effective stress. This progressive loss of shear strength may ultimately trigger slope failures and landslides. Furthermore, based on the soil compaction grading criteria outlined by Emesiobi20 (Table 2), the test results indicate that the soils are only moderately compacted. This suggests that the soil particles are loosely arranged, making them prone to detachment and transport with minimal external force, as also noted by Igwe and Egbueri12.

Compaction curve.

Additionally, the bulk density of the soil in the study area ranges from 1.78 to 1.89 kg/m³. According to Tan23, the factor of safety is at its maximum when bulk density (γ) is 1.5 kg/m³, and at its minimum when γ reaches 2.0 kg/m³. Based on this criterion, it can be inferred that higher bulk density values correlate with reduced slope stability, while lower bulk densities correspond to improved stability. The findings from the current study indicate that the bulk density of the soil is relatively low, which translates to a lower overall stability of the hillslope areas.

Furthermore, the soil compaction and bulk density results highlight the vulnerability of the study area’s slopes to instability. The loose soil structure, combined with moderate compaction and low bulk density, suggests that the hillslopes are prone to erosion, detachment, and potential failure under increased water infiltration or external loading conditions.

Shear strength analysis

The cohesion values of the soil within the study area range from 10.3 to 16 kPa, while the intergranular friction angle varies between 23° and 31°, as presented in Table 1. These results indicate that the soil exhibits low cohesion and low to moderate internal friction characteristics. According to Igwe and Egbueri12, Eze et al.24, and Ukor et al.25, an increase in soil cohesion is typically associated with a higher proportion of fine particles, whereas a lower fines content results in reduced cohesion. The analysis of sample E10 revealed a notably low cohesion value coupled with a relatively high internal friction angle, which corresponds with the fines content observed in the particle size distribution analysis (Fig. 10). The findings of this study suggest that shallow landslides observed within the study area are primarily triggered by water infiltration. Rainfall infiltration leads to soil saturation, which in turn increases pore water pressure and decreases shear strength, ultimately resulting in slope failure. This phenomenon is consistent with the observations made by Igwe and Una19, as well as Igwe and Fukuoka26, 27, who emphasized the critical role of water infiltration in slope instability. Additionally, slopes characterized by steep gradients are particularly vulnerable to failure due to the combined effects of increasing shear stress and a concurrent reduction in shear strength. Studies conducted by Mugagga et al.28, and Wati et al.29, have demonstrated that such conditions significantly heighten the likelihood of slope failure. Additionally, research by El Kelchebor4 established that an increase in external loading on slopes with a given inclination contributes to a progressive decline in shear strength. This reduction in strength can ultimately lead to failure along pre-existing planes of weakness within the soil mass.

Furthermore, the geotechnical properties of the soil, including its low cohesion and moderate frictional resistance, coupled with environmental factors such as rainfall infiltration and slope steepness, render the study area susceptible to landslides. The interaction between these factors highlights the importance of implementing appropriate slope stabilization measures to mitigate potential hazards associated with slope failure.

Slope stability analysis

A comprehensive investigation was conducted to assess the stabilityof slopes within the study area under both saturated and unsaturated conditions. The factor of safety (FoS) was calculated to evaluate slope stability. For classification purposes, the stability classification chart developed by Hadmoko et al.30, as presented in Table 6, was utilized. According to this classification system, slopes with FoS values less than one indicate that driving forces surpass resisting forces, leading to instability. Conversely, FoS values greater than one signify a stable slope condition. The results, as detailed in Table 1, demonstrate a range of stability levels for the slopes in their unsaturated state, with FoS values spanning from 0.80 to 1.32. In contrast, the analysis of slopes under saturated conditions revealed a spectrum of instability, varying from unstable to critically unstable, with FoS values ranging between 0.78 and 1.26 (Fig. 11a). These findings suggest that saturation significantly influences slope stability, with increased water content contributing to decreased stability. Additional analysis indicated that during unsaturated conditions, there was an observed increase in FoS. This condition, characterized by limited or negligible water infiltration in hillslopes, suggests improved stability compared to saturated conditions (Fig. 11b). However, despite this relative increase in stability, the slopes were classified as critically to fairly stable and remain vulnerable to potential failure. This susceptibility may be influenced by anthropogenic activities such as land use changes, excavation, and construction activities.

The analysis for the saturated state revealed a decline in FoS, which can be attributed to a reduction in shear strength parameters, specifically cohesion (C) and the internal friction angle (ɸ), coupled with an increase in the unit weight of the soil upon saturation. These findings align with previous studies conducted by various researchers, including Rahardjo et al.31, Igwe17, Onda et al.32, and Nzereogu et al.33. Their research consistently reported a significant reduction in FoS with increasing saturation levels. Igwe and Chukwu34, established a direct correlation between FoS values below one and the occurrence of slope failures. Their findings support the conclusion that when the driving forces exceed the resisting forces, slope failure is inevitable. This highlights the critical importance of continuous monitoring and assessment of slope stability, especially in areas prone to saturation.

Furthermore, it would be worthy to note that the investigation underscores the impact of water infiltration on slope stability. The varying FoS values in unsaturated and saturated conditions indicate the dynamic nature of slope stability and the necessity for appropriate mitigation measures to prevent failure. The study emphasizes the need for implementing slope management strategies, considering both geotechnical parameters and external influences, to ensure long-term stability and safety of the slopes within the study area.

The effects of pore-water pressure and non-pore water pressure on slope stability were simulated under varying soil conditions to better understand their impact. The soil samples, predominantly composed of medium-grained sand, exhibit medium to high permeability in this study. It is well established that pore-water pressure tends to accumulate during the peak of the rainy season, potentially leading to a reduction in slope stability. The relationship between infiltration and pore-water pressure is critical, as increased infiltration rates correspond to a rise in pore-water pressure. Previous studies by Igwe (2014), Igwe and Fukuoka9, and Crosta and Frattini35 have demonstrated that infiltration adversely affects slope stability by reducing matric suction and shear strength, thereby increasing the risk of slope failure.

Moreover, Igwe36 highlighted that when shear forces acting on a slope exceed the mobilized resistance, a significant decline in the strength of slope materials occurs, resulting in a notable reduction in the factor of safety. In conditions of low saturation, the volume of sliding masses ranged between 45.93 and 130.63 m³ (Fig. 12a), whereas under saturated conditions, the sliding mass volumes ranged from 44.93 to 132.63 m³ (Fig. 12b). These results reveal a significant increase in the volume of sliding masses under saturated conditions, compared to a decrease observed in unsaturated states. This indicates that slope saturation levels play a crucial role in influencing the magnitude of sliding masses.

Consequently, it is essential to recognize that the degree of saturation has a direct effect on slope stability. Increased saturation leads to a greater potential for slope instability due to higher pore-water pressures, which reduce soil shear strength and contribute to the expansion of sliding masses. Proper slope management strategies should therefore consider saturation effects to mitigate potential failures and ensure long-term stability.

The analysis revealed potential weaknesses inherent within the trial surfaces, exhibiting similar factors of safety34. The range of factor of safety values obtained from the trial slip surfaces indicates hazard levels varying from low to high. These hazard zones are visually represented within the safety maps using a color gradient, ranging from red to orange. The red-colored areas signify the most susceptible points to failure, classified as high hazard zones, whereas the areas covered by the orange band, positioned below the red zone, represent regions of lower hazard (Fig. 13).

Localized deformation within a thin zone of intense shearing is a common precursor to slope failure. Such minimal yet concentrated shearing forces may not be fully captured by traditional stress-strain evaluations, as numerous physical and anthropogenic factors can influence the overall measurement28, 37. Therefore, a comprehensive assessment incorporating these variables is essential for an accurate representation of slope stability.

The findings underscore the importance of detailed slope stability analysis and highlight the need for implementing targeted mitigation strategies in high hazard zones. Understanding the spatial distribution of slope weaknesses and potential failure points allows for informed decision-making in geotechnical and environmental planning.

Satellite imagery of the study area

A comprehensive geospatial analysis utilizing multi-temporal satellite imagery was conducted to evaluate the progressive impact of anthropogenic activities on slope stability over a 12-year period. High-resolution satellite images were obtained for the years 2010, 2015, 2020, and 2022 to observe and assess changes in land use, vegetation cover, and the extent of urbanization within a geologically sensitive region prone to landslides. The objective of this analysis was to correlate human-induced modifications with evolving geotechnical risks, particularly slope instability. Imagery from 2010 revealed a landscape that remained largely undisturbed, characterized by dense natural vegetation and minimal human interference. The terrain appeared stable with no apparent signs of mass movement or slope failure, indicative of a naturally reinforced environment with well-preserved root structures contributing to slope cohesion. By 2015, subtle changes began to emerge as low-density residential developments and rudimentary access roads were introduced. Although these activities resulted in minor landscape modification, substantial portions of natural vegetation were retained, and the hydrological regime had not yet been significantly disrupted.

By 2020, the region experienced a marked escalation in urbanization. The construction of mid-rise to multi-story residential buildings, commercial establishments, and expanded road infrastructure became evident. These developments increased surface imperviousness and disrupted natural drainage patterns, thereby intensifying surface runoff. Hydrological alterations, combined with vegetation clearance and slope regrading, initiated early signs of slope destabilization. The cumulative effect of these activities began to compromise the natural equilibrium of the terrain, suggesting an increasing susceptibility to landslides.

The 2022 satellite imagery further illustrated an accelerated phase of urban expansion. High-density construction, including numerous high-rise structures, had spread across both the crest and toe regions of the slope. Extensive deforestation and topsoil removal to accommodate these developments significantly reduced the slope’s natural resistance to erosion and gravitational movement. Excavation activities, particularly at the toe of the slope, have weakened the basal support, while overloading at the crest due to construction further exacerbates shear stress. These anthropogenic interventions have collectively intensified slope deformation, posing severe geotechnical hazards to both infrastructure and human lives in the vicinity.

Observation via field work and geotechnical analysis suggests that, while natural factors such as rainfall intensity, subsurface soil characteristics, and lithological variability have contributed to slope instability33, 38, 39, the evidence strongly suggests that these would have remained within manageable thresholds without the significant human-induced modifications. The geospatial data clearly indicate that anthropogenic pressures have acted as a catalyst in accelerating mass movement processes, thereby transforming a once-stable landscape into a high-risk zone. Given the ongoing disturbances and increasing vulnerability, it is imperative to implement immediate slope stabilization strategies. Recommended measures include integrated erosion control systems, the installation of efficient drainage networks to manage surface and subsurface water flow, and the enforcement of stringent land use regulations. These interventions are essential to mitigate further degradation and to safeguard both the environment and surrounding communities (Fig. 15).

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive geotechnical and geological evaluation of slope stability within the rapidly urbanizing terrain of the Nsukka region, Southeastern Nigeria. The integration of field investigations, laboratory testing, slope stability modeling, and multivariate statistical analysis has revealed that both natural and anthropogenic factors critically influence landslide occurrence in the area. The slopes are predominantly underlain by loose, medium to coarse sandy materials with high permeability, low cohesion, and moderate internal friction angles, indicating weak geotechnical profiles that are highly susceptible to shear failure, particularly under saturated conditions. The predominance of shallow translational slides reflects the mechanical behavior of the slope materials and the steep slope geometries observed across the study area.

Statistical techniques, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Pearson correlation, identified key controlling parameters such as fines content, sand and gravel proportions, natural and optimum moisture content, cohesion, and internal friction angle as the primary drivers of slope instability. These factors exhibit complex interdependencies, where increased moisture content and fine particle accumulation adversely affect shear strength and pore-water pressure dynamics. Furthermore, multi-temporal satellite imagery over a 12-year period revealed extensive and unregulated urban development, which has altered natural slope configurations and drainage patterns. The removal of vegetation and encroachment into geologically sensitive zones have further reduced slope integrity, accelerating the rate of failure events.

The findings underscore the urgent need for integrated slope management strategies that consider geotechnical parameters, geomorphological context, and urban planning constraints. Effective mitigation will require the implementation of engineered slope stabilization measures, improved drainage systems, and strict regulatory controls to ensure safe and sustainable development within landslide-prone areas.
