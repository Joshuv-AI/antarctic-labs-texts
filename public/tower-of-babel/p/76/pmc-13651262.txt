Precision planning in congenital heart disease: the impact of 3D printing and virtual simulation
Authors: Khadijah Maghrabi, Osman Al-Radi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical planning in complex congenital heart disease (CHD) often involves challenging decisions between univentricular and biventricular repair. Traditional imaging modalities such as echocardiography, CT, MRI, and angiography provide detailed data but lack intuitive three-dimensional spatial visualization. This study aimed to establish a low-cost, scalable 3D modeling and surgical simulation approach for broader access to this technology in mid-sized or resource-limited pediatric cardiology centers.MethodsWe retrospectively studied 25 patients with complex CHD who underwent 3D modeling to assess suitability for biventricular repair. Patient-specific cardiac models were generated using open-source segmentation and post-processing software and fabricated using a desktop stereolithography (SLA) Formlabs 3D printer. Surgical baffles were simulated digitally and incorporated into the models to assess the feasibility of biventricular repair. Models were reviewed in multidisciplinary team meetings and shared online via a 3D viewing platform (Sketchfab) to allow remote consultation.ResultsOf the 25 patients evaluated, 14 (56%) underwent a change in surgical strategy from univentricular to biventricular repair after review of the 3D models and simulated baffles. All patients who underwent revised biventricular repair had favorable short-term outcomes. The cost of model production ranged from USD 350 to 480 per patient, significantly lower than traditional outsourced workflows. The approach enabled improved spatial understanding of cardiac anatomy and facilitated collaborative decision-making across institutions.ConclusionsLow-cost 3D printed models can significantly impact surgical planning in complex CHD, increasing the feasibility of biventricular repair and reducing reliance on univentricular pathways.

Background

Three-dimensional (3D) printing and visualization technologies have revolutionized congenital heart disease (CHD) surgery by enhancing the understanding and planning of complex cases [1]. CHDs are often characterized by highly intricate and patient-specific anatomical anomalies, which limit the utility of conventional two-dimensional (2D) imaging modalities in delivering comprehensive spatial information [2, 3].

3D modeling technology allows for the creation of patient-specific cardiac models derived from 2D imaging data, providing a tangible and accurate representation of the patient’s unique cardiac anatomy. These models have been shown to improve preoperative planning, allowing surgeons to strategize procedures more effectively, particularly in cases involving complex intracardiac repairs and vascular malformations [4–7]. Complementing 3D printing, advanced visualization technologies - such as augmented reality and virtual reality - facilitate immersive and interactive exploration of complex cardiac morphologies. These platforms aid in diagnosis and planning, while also enhancing communication within multidisciplinary teams and contributing to the education of medical professionals [5, 8].

Despite these advances, routine clinical adoption remains limited due to two main challenges: limited awareness among clinicians of the availability and clinical applications of 3D printing technology and the high cost of outsourcing 3D model production. In Saudi Arabia, the utilization of 3D-printed cardiac models has been reported in a limited number of cases [9, 10], with all models produced externally. Growing international evidence supports the feasibility of using desktop 3D printers for in-house cardiac modeling, significantly lowering costs [11–13]. In addition to cost savings, adopting an in-house 3D printing workflow enables greater design flexibility, accelerates iteration cycles, enhances data governance, and allows direct control over model quality.

This study presents preliminary results from a single-center experience assessing the feasibility, clinical value, and cost-effectiveness of incorporating 3D printing and virtual surgical simulation into the surgical management of complex congenital heart disease. This approach aims to provide an accessible alternative to outsourced 3D modeling, particularly in resource-limited settings, by utilizing in-house desktop printing technologies.

Methods

King Abdulaziz University Hospital (KAUH) maintains an in-house 3D printing laboratory, allowing end-to-end control of the modeling process, including image segmentation, model fabrication, virtual reconstruction, and physical printing. Workflow for generation of patient-specific 3D cardiac model

This retrospective study analyzes data from patients with complex congenital heart disease (CHD) who underwent 3D cardiac modeling to aid in surgical planning between November 2019 and May 2025. The study received ethical approval from the KAUH Institutional Review Board and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Selection of patients for advanced imaging and 3D modeling

All patients considered for surgical repair of complex congenital heart disease were routinely discussed at a weekly multidisciplinary meeting. In cases with a clearly defined surgical pathway - namely, when biventricular repair was unequivocally feasible or unequivocally not feasible - surgical management was planned according to consensus decision-making. In contrast, patients for whom the feasibility of biventricular repair could not be confidently established using conventional imaging alone who otherwise would have been committed to a single-ventricle pathway or undergone intraoperative exploration to guide decision-making, were referred for advanced imaging and three-dimensional model generation. The use of 3D modeling in these cases was intended to facilitate definitive preoperative surgical planning while avoiding both premature single-ventricle commitment and diagnostic intraoperative assessment.

While a subset of patients received care directly at KAUH, others were referred from external institutions. To assess the feasibility of biventricular repair in this patient group, echocardiography was initially performed to evaluate atrioventricular valve morphology, ventricular size, and both systolic and diastolic function, as well as the size and location of the ventricular septal defect (VSD). Patients with unequivocal univentricular anatomy—such as severe hypoplasia of one ventricle, atrioventricular valve (AVV) atresia or significant AVV straddling—were excluded from 3D modeling. 3D modeling and printing were utilized to enhance anatomical understanding and support clinical decision-making in borderline cases where echocardiography and advanced imaging modalities provide adequate visualization of key structures but fail to fully capture the complex geometry and spatial relationship. The workflow is detailed below:

Image acquisition

Patient-specific imaging data were acquired using multiple modalities based on clinical indications. In some cases, Flash CT (Siemens) was utilized, while others underwent cardiac-gated CT scanning; both CT protocols employed a slice thickness ≤ 0.7 mm to ensure high spatial resolution suitable for detailed cardiac anatomy assessment. High spatial resolution was utilized to ensure accurate delineation of intracardiac structures for segmentation. In selected cases, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was utilized for further qualitative assessment of ventricular volumes and function. Cardiac MRI was performed using a 3D whole-heart sequence with isotropic voxel dimensions of approximately 1.0 mm, enabling high-resolution volumetric assessment of cardiac anatomy. MRI imaging was ECG-gated and respiratory-navigated to minimize motion artifacts. Contrast-enhanced sequences were used when clinically indicated. Isotropic imaging datasets were used for all cases. A single optimal imaging dataset was selected per patient for segmentation and 3D reconstruction; no inter-dataset co-registration was required.

Image segmentation

The DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communication in Medicine) datasets were imported into 3D Slicer [14] (version 5.9, Slicer Community), an open-source medical image processing platform (www.slicer.org). Segmentation of cardiac structures—including atria, ventricles, great vessels, and intracardiac defects—was performed using a combination of thresholding, region-growing, and manual refinement.

Initial segmentation used threshold-based algorithms to differentiate contrast-enhanced blood from adjacent tissues, followed by semi-automated region-growing methods. In cases with poor image quality or complex anatomy—such as small vessels or incomplete contrast enhancement—automated approaches were inadequate. These instances required detailed, layer-by-layer manual segmentation, which, although considerably more time-consuming, was essential for anatomical accuracy. Discrepancies or uncertainties, when present, were discussed among advanced imaging experts, and necessary adjustments to the segmentation were made accordingly. Thin anatomical structures such as valve leaflets are difficult to visualize and segment accurately in CT and MRI cross sections. Thin anatomical structures, such as valve leaflets, often lacked clear visualization in CT or MRI. To address this, the attachment zones of the atrioventricular and semilunar valves were extruded by 1 mm to ensure their representation in the final model. While this may overestimate true leaflet thickness, it was intentionally used to preserve spatial orientation relevant to surgical planning rather than to achieve detailed morphological reconstruction.

The resulting 3D models were refined to ensure anatomical accuracy and converted into STL (stereolithography) format at a 1:1 scale for further processing. Segmentation accuracy was validated through comparison with the original imaging data by an experienced pediatric cardiologist. Key anatomical features were evaluated for completeness, continuity, and morphological correctness throughout the process.

Creation of hollow 3D model

At this stage, the virtual 3D representation of the blood pool remains in a preliminary form, often characterized by irregular and rough surface geometry. The model is then refined by surface smoothing and the correction of topological defects such as open boundaries and mesh discontinuities. This was followed by hollowing and final validation.

A uniform shell thickness of 0.8 mm was applied, with the inner surface of the hollowed model corresponding to the outer surface of the contrast-enhanced blood pool, preserving endocardial anatomy. The outer surface is an artificial construct generated through the hollowing process and does not correspond to any anatomical structure. To validate the model’s accuracy, the hollowed geometry was visually compared with the original imaging dataset, focusing particularly on the alignment of the internal surface of the hollowed model with the contrast-enhanced regions in the original imaging dataset. Manual corrections were performed, when necessary, especially in areas with segmentation challenges or incomplete contrast filling. This qualitative validation ensured that the segmented models accurately represented patient-specific cardiac anatomy. The finalized hollowed model was then exported to Meshmixer software (version 3.5, Autodesk Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA), for post-processing, virtual cutting, and simulation of potential surgical repairs. Meshmixer provides advanced STL mesh editing tools that enable detailed manipulation and customization of the model. Surface smoothing was applied conservatively and in a stepwise manner to reduce mesh irregularities while preserving anatomical accuracy. Particular care was taken to avoid over-smoothing in clinically relevant regions, including ventricular septal defect margins and atrioventricular valve relationships.

Simulation of surgical repair

Following anatomical segmentation and 3D model reconstruction, virtual surgical repair was performed by digitally designing and integrating patches or baffles within the hollowed cardiac models using Meshmixer (Autodesk Inc.). Simulations were patient-specific, addressing anomalies such as intracardiac baffles, septal patches, and outflow tract reconstructions. These interventions were conducted entirely in a digital environment, allowing for rapid iteration without repeated physical model fabrication. Each proposed modification was assessed for anatomical fit, surgical feasibility, and its potential impact on intraventricular flow.

For ventricular septation assessments, three digital models were generated: (1) a complete model of the contrast-enhanced blood pool, (2) a hollowed model of the endocardial surface anatomy with a modified sagittal or coronal cut to assess the size and orientation of the VSD in relation to the outflow tracts, and (3) a hollowed model of the endocardial surface anatomy with an inlet view, designed to evaluate the risk of inlet flow obstruction. The latter two models were each modified by incorporating a digitally constructed baffle to simulate the surgical repair and visualize its spatial relationships to surrounding intracardiac structures. The simulations were intended for qualitative spatial assessment rather than precise biomechanical modeling. While deformation of the proposed baffle was considered conceptually during manipulation of the elastic models, quantitative modeling of material behavior (e.g., torsion or compression) was not performed.

Simulated repairs were reviewed during multidisciplinary cardiology-surgical meeting, in person or via video conferencing to assess their plausibility and alignment with standard surgical approaches. Select models were uploaded to an open-access 3D viewer platform (www.sketchfab.com) to facilitate asynchronous review, and trainee and family education. All patient identifiers were removed prior to uploading models to Sketchfab, and anonymized cases were labeled with study numbers linked to a patient identification key securely stored by the authors.

3D printing

Anatomical models—without the simulated surgical modifications—were printed using a single Formlabs SLA 3D printer (Form 3) and Elastic 50A resin (Formlabs, Somerville, MA, USA), offering soft-tissue–like flexibility. Elastic 50A resin was selected for its flexibility and for its ability to provide adequate translucency without extensive post-processing, thereby reducing the need for sanding or polishing and minimizing the risk of altering fine anatomical details. Print preparation and slicing were conducted using PreForm software (version 3.2, Formlabs). Models were oriented to minimize support contact on critical intracardiac surfaces. Support structures were applied with moderate density (70%) and small touchpoint sizes (0.35 mm) to reduce surface artifacts while maintaining print stability. A 100 μm layer height was selected to balance anatomical resolution with print time. High-resolution printing (100 μm layer height, 85 μm in-plane resolution) produced highly detailed, anatomically accurate models. Although not mechanically equivalent to native tissue, the compliance of Elastic 50A (shore hardness ~ 50 A) permitted deformation-based assessment of intracardiac geometry and baffle interaction, enhancing surgical planning.

Process evaluation and postoperative feedback

Model validation was conducted throughout the workflow to ensure anatomical accuracy. During segmentation and hollowing, 3D models were repeatedly cross-referenced with the original CT or MRI datasets to confirm alignment with the patient’s true anatomy, particularly at critical anatomical boundaries. After 3D printing, qualitative evaluation was performed during multidisciplinary reviews. Team members provided structured feedback on the models’ utility in preoperative planning, interdisciplinary communication, and understanding complex anatomy. Additionally, postoperative feedback was obtained by comparing intraoperative findings with the anatomical representations in both the printed 3D models and virtual surgical simulations. Cardiac surgeons provided qualitative assessments regarding the fidelity of the models in replicating patient-specific anatomy and the clinical utility of the digital simulations in anticipating surgical challenges and informing intraoperative decision-making. This feedback loop helped identify strengths and limitations of the modeling workflow and informed iterative improvements for future cases.

Clinical data

Retrospective chart review was performed for the patients referred for evaluation of feasibility of biventricular repair. The following data were collected: (a) demographic data (age at time of evaluation, weight, sex) (b) congenital heart disease, (c) surgical decision before and after 3D modeling, (d) surgical procedure and intraoperative details, (e) postoperative course including echocardiogram results, postoperative complications, 30-day mortality, and need for redo surgery or catheter intervention. Due to the small sample size, only descriptive statistics are given, and no comparative analyses were performed. Data were entered and processed using IBM SPSS (version 24.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were represented by frequencies and percentages.

Adequate image quality for model generation was obtained via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI, n = 11) and computed tomography (CT, n = 14), allowing for the successful fabrication of 25 digital 3D models. Segmentation and validation time ranged from 4 to 13 h, depending on the quality of the imaging data. The mean time required for post-processing and virtual simulation was 45 ± 18 min and 93 ± 48 min, respectively. Median printing time was 18 h, although the total print duration varied significantly with heart size—ranging from 9 to 12 h for infant-sized hearts, and up to 28 h for adult-sized hearts. Print queue prioritization was determined by clinical urgency. Urgent cases were fast-tracked, enabling completion within 48 h for digital models and 96 h for physical models. In contrast, non-urgent cases were scheduled within routine workflow timelines, with turnaround times ranging from 10 to 20 days. In two instances, printing failed due to wall collapse from insufficient support at minima (low points in the model geometry requiring additional support), necessitating repeat printing. The estimated cost range for the printed models was ≈ USD 350–480 per case, with smaller hearts (lower print volume) costing less.

Models were cleaned using manual isopropyl alcohol (IPA) washing and ultrasonic cleaning. Final curing was performed in a 405 nm UV chamber with the models submerged in water to enhance curing uniformity and optical clarity.

Surgical management decision change

After reviewing the data of the patients considered for biventricular vs. univentricular repair in a multidisciplinary cardiac meeting, 3 patients were considered for conservative management and 21 patients were candidates for surgical intervention. Fourteen underwent cardiac septation (one and a half ventricular repair in 2 patients, and biventricular repair in 12 patients), while 8 patients were committed to single ventricular pathway (Table 2). Overall, 3D models and virtual repair simulation modified the surgical decision from univentricular to biventricular in 56% of the patients. Final surgical intervention

Twenty patients underwent surgical repair, with two patients awaiting surgery at the time of writing this manuscript. In all completed cases, the planned procedure was performed without intraoperative decision changes. There was one mortality (case 8), who was a 5-month-old with d-transposition of the great arteries (TGA), VSD, double arch, interrupted left aortic arch, bilateral patent ductus arteriosus (PDAs), discontinuous pulmonary arteries (PAs) with left pulmonary artery from left PDA, and preductal coarctation in right aortic arch. She underwent staged repair; arch repair and branch PA unifocalization after birth. Then at 5 months she underwent Rastelli baffle and RV-PA conduit placement due to severe pulmonary valve regurgitation due infective endocarditis after the first intervention. Despite initial postoperative stability and unobstructive flows on echocardiogram, she died from disseminated sepsis and multiorgan failure. The remaining 19 cases had satisfactory postoperative hemodynamics with no significant residual lesions requiring reintervention. One patient (case 17) developed transient postoperative complete atrioventricular (AV) block that completely recovered by third postoperative day, and one patient (case 6) developed phrenic nerve palsy requiring diaphragm plication.

Discussion

This study explores the feasibility and value of low-cost, freeware‑based 3D printing combined with virtual surgical simulation for preoperative planning in children with complex CHD. Our dual aims were (1) to introduce simulated intracardiac baffling into the 3D modeling workflow and (2) to demonstrate scalability in resource-limited pediatric cardiology centers. We generated patient‑specific 3D models for 25 infants and children using open-source segmentation and post‑processing, printed via a Formlabs SLA printer. Review of the models - including simulated surgical baffles - resulted in a change from preliminary univentricular to biventricular repair in 14 (56%) cases, all with favorable hemodynamic outcomes.

Imaging modalities in complex CHD

Thorough understanding of intracardiac morphology is fundamental to determining the optimal surgical approach [15, 16]. Conventional imaging—namely transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, angiography, CT angiography, and cardiac MRI—remains the mainstay for anatomical and hemodynamic assessment in CHD. Echocardiography is fundamental for structural and functional evaluation, particularly in infants and children. Echocardiography is advantageous over CT and MRI for visualization of the rapidly moving or thin structures such as valve leaflets, chordae and papillary muscles [17–20] but is limited by poor depiction of intracardiac spatial relationships. MRI and CT provide excellent resolution of vascular anatomy and chamber morphology, yet their two‑dimensional displays or even screen‑based 3D reconstructions require mental reconstruction of complex spatial relationships. Collectively, these modalities often fail to provide intuitive, tangible appreciation of complex intracardiac geometries—especially in anomalies involving heterotaxy and abnormal venous connections, complex atrioventricular canal defects, double‑outlet ventricles, and malposed great vessels. Integrating data from multiple imaging modalities into a composite 3D model enhances anatomical understanding and supports more informed surgical planning. Despite advances in 3D printing, certain anatomical features remain difficult to capture with high resolution. These include fine structures such as chordal attachments, straddling of atrioventricular valves, and the potential impact of intraventricular tunneling on tricuspid valve function or right ventricular outflow tract obstruction. These limitations underscore the importance of incorporating insights from multiple imaging sources during model creation and surgical decision-making. Ultimately, the value of a 3D model is amplified when it is informed by the full spectrum of anatomical and functional data available across modalities.

When considering heart septation in complex congenital heart disease, the central question is whether an unobstructed intraventricular baffle can be constructed from the margin of the VSD to the aortic valve (or neo-aortic valve) without adversely affecting the RV inlet, tricuspid valve function, the integrity of both outflow tracts, or the residual volume of the right ventricle. Key anatomical modifiers that determine feasibility of septation include ventricular size and morphology, adequacy of AV valves, alignment of inflow‑outflow tracts, and spatial feasibility of intracardiac baffling [21–23]. In complex defects, especially when the VSD is perceived to be remote from the outflow tracts, cognitive interpretation of these features from traditional imaging can be ambiguous, leading often to default univentricular strategies. In such scenarios, 3D printed models significantly enhance spatial understanding by providing a patient-specific, tangible representation of the defect. They help bridge the gap between imagers and surgeons, reducing the likelihood of misinterpretation or miscommunication, and supporting more informed and precise surgical decision-making [24]. Furthermore, proposed repairs can be virtually simulated by incorporating a digitally designed patch or baffle, allowing surgical strategies to be evaluated and refined before the actual procedure [25].

Multiple systematic reviews and clinical series support the clinical value of 3D printed cardiac models, potentially leading to improved surgical precision and better patient outcomes [26–30]. A systematic review of 28 studies (most single‑center case series, and one multi-center study) found that patient‑specific models accurately replicate complex anatomy, improve surgical planning, decision‑making, and education [31]. Another meta‑analysis including 21 studies and 444 patients showed that 3D modeling changed surgical decision‑making in approximately 52% of cases (95% CI 26.6–77.0%), with a mean reduction in operative time of 22 min (though without statistical significance) [32]. A multicenter clinical trial of “super‑flexible” stereolithographic models (n = 19) reported that 68% of surgeons rated the models as “essential” for decision-making, with dimensional accuracy within ~ 0.5 mm [33].

While 3D printing is an exciting and innovative tool in the field of CHD, it can be time and cost intensive and may not be feasible for routine use in all patients or at every congenital heart center [34–36]. In contrast, 3D intracardiac digital modeling offers a more accessible alternative [37–40]. Centers already performing CT or MRI for CHD can adopt this approach more readily. Digital models can be generated without the need for physical printing and can incorporate simulated surgical repairs, making them particularly valuable for preoperative planning in complex cases [8, 41–45].

Impact of 3D models and surgical simulation on clinical decision

Our study demonstrated that incorporation of simulated intracardiac baffles into physical 3D models permitted surgeons to experiment with potential repair pathways. In 56% of patients, preliminary plans favoring univentricular routes were revised in favor of biventricular repair after model review. This change reflects a deeper understanding of patient-specific anatomy through physical manipulation, enabling assessment of whether an AV valve, VSD location, and chamber geometry can support a biventricular septation. Our findings mirror the roughly 52% change in surgical plan reported across larger series [16]. Beyond its role in surgical planning, the availability of patient-specific 3D models had a meaningful impact on communication with both families and the multidisciplinary care team. The tangible nature of the models facilitated clearer explanation of complex intracardiac anatomy to parents, improving their understanding of the proposed surgical strategies and associated risks. This was particularly valuable in cases requiring decisions between univentricular and biventricular pathways, where anatomical nuances are difficult to convey using conventional imaging alone. Similarly, within multidisciplinary discussions, the models provided a shared spatial reference that enhanced consensus-building between cardiologists, surgeons, and imaging specialists.

Non-routable VSDs

Patients with non-routable VSDs, especially those with heterotaxy syndrome or abnormal venous connections, are typically directed toward a single-ventricle palliation pathway. While outcomes for single-ventricle management have improved significantly, long-term complications remain a major concern [46]. These include progressive heart failure, hepatic cirrhosis, protein-losing enteropathy, and other end-organ dysfunctions [47]. Despite advancements in medical and surgical management of single ventricular patients, freedom from serious adverse events following the Fontan procedure is only 41% at 40 years [19]. Reintervention rates and late mortality also remain substantial [48]. In case 10 (5-year-old, with congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries -cc-TGA-, double outlet right ventricle (DORV) with a small remote VSD, pulmonary valvar and subvalvar stenosis and left ventricular (LV) hypoplasia, Fig. 4), we pursued ventricular septation with VSD closure and a hemi-Mustard procedure thus utilizing the right ventricle as the systemic ventricle and the left ventricle as a subpulmonary ventricle. This strategy is based on the concept that a ccTGA physiology may offer more favorable long-term outcomes than Fontan circulation [49, 50]. Importantly, systemic right ventricular failure in this scenario tends to result in isolated cardiac dysfunction rather than the multisystem deterioration typically seen in failing Fontan physiology [51].

Clinical outcomes and methodological considerations

While this study was not designed to demonstrate a direct improvement in clinical outcomes (e.g. operation times, or critical care length of stay), the observed changes in surgical strategy remain clinically meaningful. Decisions regarding univentricular versus biventricular repair pathways are well established to carry markedly different long-term prognostic implications. As outlined previously, patients undergoing single ventricle palliation culminating in the Fontan circulation face substantial long-term morbidity, whereas, when anatomically feasible, biventricular repair is generally associated with more favorable physiological outcomes.

In this context, the ability of 3D modeling to enhance anatomical understanding and increase confidence in pursuing a biventricular or alternative repair strategy suggests a plausible pathway toward improved long-term outcomes. However, such benefits cannot be directly inferred from the present data.

Evaluating the clinical impact of 3D modeling in CHD presents important methodological challenges. Congenital cardiac lesions are inherently heterogeneous, and even subtle anatomical differences may significantly influence surgical decision-making. Moreover, 3D modeling does not merely support planning but may refine or redefine anatomical interpretation itself. Consequently, cases assessed with and without 3D modeling are not strictly comparable, limiting the validity of matched cohort analyses based solely on conventional imaging.

Similarly, although randomized controlled trials are traditionally regarded as the gold standard for evaluating clinical interventions, their application in this field is constrained by anatomical variability, rapid technological evolution, and ethical considerations related to withholding advanced visualization tools. In this setting, prospective multicenter studies incorporating both quantitative outcomes and qualitative assessments of clinical decision-making may represent a more practical and informative approach.

Cost‑effectiveness

Our study demonstrated that the full 3D modeling workflow—from image segmentation to printing—can be implemented in-hospital using cost-effective tools. We utilized an affordable SLA desktop 3D printer and open-source software (3D Slicer and Meshmixer) for segmentation and post-processing, both validated for this application [52–54]. The cost savings are compounded by enabling remote multidisciplinary consultation eliminating geographic constraints. As the costs of desktop printers, printing materials, and open-source platforms continue to decline, in-house additive manufacturing is becoming increasingly viable for surgical planning. While there may be an initial steep learning curve for software and printer, centralizing the workflow within the hospital, we eliminated the need for outsourcing and enabled real-time adjustments. This streamlined approach resulted in an average production cost of approximately $400 per model.

3D printing vs. digital and virtual platforms

Although 3D printing provides tangible and intuitive models, it is associated with time and cost constraints that may limit routine use. In contrast, digital 3D modeling offers a more accessible and scalable alternative, particularly in centers already performing CT or MRI imaging. Digital models can be rapidly generated and allow incorporation of simulated surgical repairs without the need for physical fabrication.

Emerging technologies such as virtual reality (VR) further expand the capabilities of digital modeling [8]. VR platforms enable immersive, interactive visualization of complex cardiac anatomy and may offer superior representation of fine structures such as valve leaflets and chordal attachments. Studies suggest that VR may complement or, in certain scenarios, surpass physical models in specific aspects of surgical planning [28, 37]. Rather than being mutually exclusive, these modalities should be viewed as complementary tools within an evolving ecosystem of advanced visualization technologies.

Future directions: AI‑enhanced segmentation and surgical simulation

While 3D modeling and simulation technologies show significant potential in congenital heart disease (CHD), their integration into routine clinical workflows remains limited. Most evidence stems from case reports and small series, underscoring the need for large-scale, controlled studies to establish robust guidelines and assess clinical impact [55, 56]. Future research should focus on closing this gap, while also exploring innovative frontiers such as bioprinting and advanced image fusion techniques to further refine the accuracy and effectiveness of CHD interventions [57]. In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI) holds great promise for further streamlining this workflow. Deep learning–based segmentation tools can automate complex anatomical delineation, reducing manual effort and variability. AI-driven simulations may incorporate fluid dynamics and biomechanical modeling to enhance surgical planning while simultaneously reducing manual labor, enhancing consistency and minimizing interobserver variability [58]. Combined with augmented reality platforms, these technologies offer opportunities for remote, real-time collaboration and decision-making [59].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The small sample size (n = 25) reflects a single-center experience and may limit generalizability. Future evaluation is better suited to prospective multicenter studies incorporating both quantitative outcomes and structured qualitative assessments of clinical utility, including end-user feedback from surgeons and multidisciplinary teams. The absence of a control group prevents comparison of outcomes such as operative time or complication rates. As discussed, such comparisons are inherently challenging in this field due to anatomical heterogeneity and evolving diagnostic interpretation. Surgical decision-making, although multidisciplinary, was ultimately subject to individual surgeon interpretation, which may introduce variability. Importantly, our cohort exhibited high anatomical heterogeneity, including a wide range of complex CHD morphologies, making it difficult to standardize decision-making pathways or isolate which lesions benefited most from modeling. While cost was minimized through freeware and in-house printing, indirect costs (e.g. staff training, and maintenance) were not fully quantified. Lastly, while short-term outcomes were favorable, long-term follow-up is needed to evaluate the durability and functional results of decisions informed by model-guided planning.

In addition to the study-related limitations, there are important practical constraints inherent to the 3D printing workflow itself that should be considered. SLA printing with flexible materials offers high geometric fidelity and tactile realism but is associated with longer print times, higher material costs, and increased susceptibility to print failures compared with lower-cost fused deposition modeling (FDM) techniques using rigid materials such as polylactic acid (PLA). In our experience, occasional print failures occurred—most commonly due to inadequate support of thin-walled regions—resulting in reprinting and potential delays in workflow. However, the frequency of such failures decreased over time with operator experience, reflecting a significant learning curve associated with optimization of model orientation, support placement, and post-processing techniques. Certain limitations are intrinsic to SLA technology and cannot be fully eliminated, including the presence of support contact artifacts on model surfaces and the need for post-processing steps such as washing and curing, which may affect surface finish and optical clarity. In contrast, other limitations—such as print failures, suboptimal support strategies, and excessive surface smoothing—can be mitigated through iterative refinement of the workflow. Recognizing the distinction between modifiable and inherent constraints is essential for centers seeking to adopt and scale similar 3D printing approaches in clinical practice.

Conclusion

In summary, our study shows that a low-cost, open-source 3D printing and surgical simulation workflow can significantly impact planning in complex CHD, influencing strategy in over half of cases and enabling more informed decisions toward biventricular repair. With production costs of USD 350–480 per model, this approach offers an accessible alternative to commercial systems. Online 3D hosting and multidisciplinary review further enhance planning and communication. As imaging and simulation technologies continue to evolve, the integration of 3D modeling into routine clinical workflows may further enhance surgical planning and multidisciplinary collaboration. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to better define the downstream impact on clinical and economic outcomes.
