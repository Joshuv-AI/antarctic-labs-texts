Impact of piR_004530 reactivation in lung cancer: implications for recurrence and survival of lung squamous cell carcinoma patients
Authors: Yangyi He, Melissa Acosta-Plasencia, David Sánchez-Lorente, Nuria Viñolas, Daniel Martinez, Tania Díaz, Antonio Altuna-Coy, Risha Na, Yi Liu, Marc Boada, Angela Guirao, Laureano Molins, Ramón M Marrades, Alfons Navarro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) are germline-characteristic small noncoding RNAs whose reactivation has been recently observed during carcinogenesis because of the germline reactivation program, which can be considered a hallmark of cancer. We evaluated the prognostic impact of hsa_piR_004530 reactivation in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and studied its functional role in cell lines and organoids.MethodsA total of 243 NSCLC resected patients were analyzed. Hsa_piR_004530 expression was quantified in tumor (n = 243) and normal tissue (n = 31) using qRT-PCR. Tumor recurrence, disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS) were used as clinical endpoints in survival analysis. Cox regression models were generated, and decision curve analysis for assessment of clinical benefit on disease prognosis was used. The effect of hsa_piR_004530 overexpression was assessed in NSCLC cell lines by cell migration, invasion, proliferation, apoptosis, and colony formation analysis. Patient-derived organoids from SCC patients were generated, and cell viability was evaluated after piRNA overexpression.ResultsHsa_piR_004530 became reactivated on the tumor compared to normal tissue and was overexpressed in SCC patients. The prognosis analysis in the whole cohort showed that patients with high levels of hsa_piR_004530 had shorter DFS and OS. However, the subanalysis by histology revealed that the true prognostic impact of hsa_piR_004530 was specifically on SCC patients. These results became validated in an additional cohort. SCC patients with high hsa_piR_004530 had a higher relapse rate and shorter DFS and OS. Hsa_piR_004530 emerged as an independent prognostic factor in the multivariate analysis. Since the SCC patients who received adjuvant treatment had the best postsurgical prognosis, we focused on the role of hsa_piR_004530 on non-treated patients, which allowed us to identify a high-risk group where the piRNA ameliorated patients’ risk stratification, highlighting superior clinical benefit in postsurgical relapse prediction. Hsa_piR_004530 overexpression was associated with increased migration, invasion, and colony formation. Moreover, the overexpression induced stem cell gene activation and correlated with higher size spheroid formation. In patient-derived organoids, the overexpression boosted organoid cell viability.ConclusionsHsa_piR_004530 became reactivated in NSCLC, where it played a role as an oncogene, and its higher levels correlated with disease recurrence and shorter survival in SCC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04342-1.Keywords: NSCLC, Lung squamous cell carcinoma, PIWI-interacting RNAs, piRNAs, Recurrence, Hsa_piR_004530, TEX29

Background

Despite advancements in modern surgical techniques, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy, lung cancer remains one of the malignancies with the poorest prognosis, making it the leading cause of cancer-related deaths [1, 2]. In 2022, 2.5 million lung cancer cases were diagnosed, and 1.8 million lung cancer-related deaths were reported worldwide [3]. Moreover, only around 16% of patients diagnosed with lung cancer are still alive after 5 years [4, 5]. Most patients (85%) are diagnosed with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). When NSCLC patients are clinically diagnosed with nonmetastatic disease, complete surgical resection remains the primary curative approach, especially for early stages (I–II) [6]. However, recurrence after surgery can develop in one-third of resected patients [7] despite the administration of adjuvant or neoadjuvant treatments [8]. At the histological level, the two most frequent histological subtypes of NSCLC are adenocarcinoma (ADK, 45%) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC, 20%) [9, 10]. The behavior and treatment of both subtypes are quite different. Historically, NSCLC has been treated according to a “uniform” strategy without taking into account the histological subtype, but recent advances in sequencing technology and an improved biological understanding have enhanced a new era of targeted therapy for ADK, initially by the implementation of therapeutic strategies for patients harboring mutations in molecular targets such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) [11–13] and more recently for other target genes such as hepatocyte growth factor receptor (MET) or Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) between others [14, 15]. Unfortunately, SCC continues to have a poor overall prognosis due to the lack of targeted therapies and the scarcity of reliable prognostic markers [16]. SCC is a histological subtype mostly associated with male smoking patients [9] that often arises in the central part of the lungs, especially near the bronchial tubes [17]. At the molecular level, SCC is markedly different from ADK, lacking mutations in EGFR or ALK but exhibiting enrichment in TP53 mutations in most patients. The identification and targeting of driver mutations in patients with lung SCC have exhibited a delay [18, 19], and the effectiveness of immunotherapy can be variable [20]. Accordingly, treatment of SCC remains previously stagnant, often involving surgical resection and platinum-based adjuvant therapy in early stages and requiring further work to identify reliable prognostic markers.

Small noncoding RNAs have been recognized as reliable prognostic markers since their expression is usually deregulated in cancer and correlates with patient outcomes [21, 22]. Most of the past studies have focused on microRNAs, leaving other groups such as PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) understudied and poorly analyzed as prognostic biomarkers [23]. The main reason that initially discouraged studies on piRNAs was the fact that they were described first as germline-specific RNAs [24–27]. Moreover, supporting this, the biogenesis of piRNAs was mediated by PIWI proteins, an Argonaute subfamily of proteins whose expression was mostly limited to the germline cells [28–31]. However, it has been recently reported that both PIWI proteins and piRNAs can be detected in tumor cells, including NSCLC [32, 33]. Their deregulation is more marked in dedifferentiated cells, which aims to postulate that they are involved in the regulation of cancer stem cells [34, 35]. These piRNAs, with a length of 26–32 nt in humans, were associated mainly with the regulation of genome stability and protection against transposons [24], but also other functions have been described such as the regulation of gene expression acting in a similar way to miRNAs [36]. Their deregulation in cancer has been poorly explored [37, 38], and few of them have been identified as prognostic biomarkers[32, 39–41] .

In the present study, we focused on the analysis of hsa_piR_004530 (piR_004530), a piRNA identified in one of the first studies of piRNA profiles [26], which is closely located in the genome to testis-expressed protein 29 (TEX29, also known as C13orf16) [42] (Fig. 1), a germline gene whose hypomethylation was related to worst overall survival (OS) in lung cancer [43]. Moreover, we confirmed a positive correlation between TEX29 and piR_004530 in tumor tissue (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). Additionally, the reactivation of both genes was observed following treatment with 5-aza-2-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-dC), which inhibits DNA methyltransferases (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B), but the specific overexpression of piR_004530 did not induce significant changes in the expression of TEX29 (Additional file 1: Fig. S1C). Therefore, since the reactivation of the “germline program” has been proposed as a mechanism for promoting carcinogenesis [44], we hypothesized that piR_004530 is a germline piRNA that becomes reactivated during the tumorigenesis process and could act as a potential biomarker for NSCLC prognosis. In the present work, we studied the reactivation of piR_004530 in NSCLC and evaluated its role as a relapse biomarker.

Methods

Patients samples

Tumor and paired normal tissue from 245 adult patients diagnosed with NSCLC who underwent complete surgical resection at the Hospital Clinic of Barcelona from May 2007 to October 2019 were prospectively collected. After obtention, the samples were immediately immersed in RNAlater® (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and stored at − 80 °C until processing.

Cell lines

Cryopreserved samples of lung cancer cell lines HCC-15, SK-MES-1, HCC44, A549 (DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany), and H23 (CRL-5800, ATTC, Manassas, VA, USA) and the normal immortalized lung cell line BEAS2B (ECACC, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were received in our laboratory and passaged for less than 6 months. HCC-15, HCC44, and H23 were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific), while SK-MES-1 and A549 were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All of them were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). BEAS2B was cultured using BEGMTM-2 BulletKit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). All cell lines were grown at 37 °C in 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity.

5-Aza-dC treatment

For methylation analysis, the SK-MES-1 cell line (4 × 105 cells) was plated 24 h prior to treatment. The next day, cells were treated with 100-μM 5-aza-dC or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (vehicle control) daily for 5 days and were harvested, and gene expression was assessed.

RNA extraction, mRNA and piRNA quantification

Total RNA was isolated from tissue and cell lines using TRIzol® reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was quantified using NanoDrop ND-1000 device (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PiR_004530 (5′-TCCTCCGTGTTGATGGTTAGAGGATT-3′) was quantified using Custom TaqMan Small RNA Assay (assay ID: CTCE4DG; Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. The reverse transcription reaction was carried out using 10 ng of RNA and the high-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit® (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Real-time PCR was performed using a 7500 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). And hsa-miR-191 (assay ID: 002299, TaqMan microRNA Assay, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as an endogenous control. We performed mRNA quantification of TEX29 (Hs00381012_m1), SOX2 (Hs01053049_s1), OCT4 (Hs04260367_gH), NANOG (Hs02387400_g1), CD133 (Hs01009259_m1), CD44 (Hs01075864_m1), c-MYC (Hs00153408_m1), CDKN1B (Hs00153277_m1), and GAPDH (Hs99999905_m1) as previously described using TaqMan expression assays [45]. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Protein purification and Western blot

Protein purification was performed as previously described [45]. Thirty micrograms (30 µg) of total protein from SK-MES-1 and HCC-15 cells—either transfected with the piR_004530 mimic or control mimic—were separated by electrophoresis on 4–12% Bolt™ Bis–Tris Plus Mini Protein Gels (Invitrogen) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated with a mouse monoclonal anti-Sox2 antibody (66,411–1-Ig, Proteintech) at a 1:1000 dilution and a mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin antibody (clone A5441, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) at the same dilution. A purified goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (clone 31,431, Invitrogen) was used at a 1:2000 dilution. Chemiluminescent signal for Sox2 was detected using the SuperSignal™ West Femto Substrate (Pierce Biotechnology, Boston, MA, USA), while detection of β-actin was performed using the SuperSignal™ West Pico Substrate (Pierce Biotechnology). Band intensities were visualized with the ChemiDoc™ Imaging System and quantified using Quantity One software (Bio-Rad, Barcelona, Spain) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All results were normalized to β-actin levels.

Cell transfection

On day 0, 1.15 × 105 SK-MES-1 cells or 1.25 × 105 HCC-15 or H23 cells were seeded in 24-well plates. On day 1, the cells were transfected with 100 nM of 3′ terminal 2′-O-methyl piR_004530 (5′-UCC UCC GUG UUG AUG GUU AGA GGA UU-3′) or with 3′ terminal 2′-O-methyl RNA control (5′-UCA CAA CCU CCU AGA AAG AGU AGA-3′) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For piR_004530 inhibition experiments, the same methodology was used, but the cells were transfected with an ASO anti-piR_004530 (5′-AATCCTCTAACCATCAACACGGAGGA-3′) or ASO control (5′-GATACCAGGGACATACGCTTGATCCTAGC-3′). To verify the overexpression or silencing of the piRNA, piR_004530 expression was evaluated by real-time PCR 48 h after transfection.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was measured by wound-healing assay by plating 1.15 × 105 SK-MES-1 cells or 1.25 × 105 HCC-15 or H23 cells in a 24-well plate. Twenty-four hours after plating, cells were transfected with piR_004530 or control RNA as previously described. Eighteen hours after transfection, the cell monolayer was scraped in a straight line to create a “scratch” with a p10 pipet tip. Differences between cells growing in HCC-15, SK-MES-1, and H23 were assessed with images taken at 0, 6, 24, 30, 48, 72, and 96 h in IX53 inverted microscope using CellSense Entry 1.7 software (Olympus). The migration area (μm2) was assessed at different time points until the scratch was closed (HCC-15 and H23 cells 96 h, SK-MES-1 cells 48 h), with the 0-h time being set at 0%. Areas were calculated using ImageJ V1.54f.

Cell invasion assay

Invasion capacity was measured using QCM ECMatrix Cell Invasion Assay (ECM550, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck Life Science) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 18 h of transfection, 2 × 105 cells were placed in serum-free media and seeded on the top of the invasion chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 10% fetal bovine serum media. At 24 h, membranes were stained, and images were obtained to visualize the proportion of invading cells. For quantification, membranes were immersed in 10% acetic acid to dissolve the cell stain solution. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 560 nm using the VersaMax™ Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

MTS assay

Cell proliferation was analyzed using CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (MTS assay) (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Eighteen hours after transfection, 1 × 103 cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and proliferation was analyzed at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. For the analysis, cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5%CO2 for 30 min with 20 μl of MTS, and then absorbance at 490 nm was recorded using a VersaMax™ Microplate Reader.

Caspase 3/7 assay

Apoptosis was measured using Caspase-Glo® 3/7 Assay System (Promega) as per manufacturer’s protocol. Eighteen hours after transfection, 1 × 104 cells were seeded on white-coated 96-well plates. An equal volume of Caspase-Glo 3/7 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT). Relative light intensity was measured in each well using a Veritas Microplate Luminometer (Turner BioSystems, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

Clonogenic assays (2D)

A colony formation assay was performed to measure clonogenic capacity for 15 days. Obtained colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at RT and stained with 0.5% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min. Then, the plate was air-dried for 24 h, and images were obtained. For quantification, methanol was added to each well to solubilize the crystal violet, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm using the VersaMax™ Microplate Reader.

Spheroid formation assay

Cultured 2000 cells were seeded into a 96-well plate coated with agarose (1.5%) and cultured with stem cell media composed of DMEMF12, 1 × B27 supplement (without vit. A), EGF (20 ng/ml), bFGF (20 ng/ml), and 1% P/S. Cells were maintained in suspension for 6 days until self-aggregated into spheroids. Cell size and morphology were determined using ImageJ V1.54f.

Patient-derived lung cancer organoid

Patient-derived lung cancer organoids were generated from three different SCC patients. The first was a 61-year-old white male smoker with TNM stage IIB (T1N1M0), who harbored TP53 (c.713GT) and PTEN (c.724GT) mutations; his tumor tissue was PD-L1 positive (20%) as assessed by immunohistochemistry with the mouse anti-PD-L1 clone 22C3 antibody. The second patient was a 63-year-old white male, former smoker, diagnosed with SCC at TNM stage IA3 (T1N0M0); no clinically relevant mutations were detected, although a PIK3CA gain was identified. The third patient was a 77-year-old white male smoker with SCC at TNM stage IA1 (T1aN0M0), and no clinically relevant mutations were detected. Resected tumor tissue from all patients was collected within 6 h of surgery and processed as previously described [46]. Briefly, each tissue was mechanically dissociated with a sterile scalpel into smaller pieces followed by incubation with collagenase type II and DNase I (Merck) in DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with intermittent agitation on an orbital shaker at 37 °C for 2 h. After enzymatic digestion, the suspensions were passed through 100-μm cell strainer and centrifuged. Red blood cell lysis buffer (Sigma Aldrich) was added to lyse the erythrocytes for 5 min at RT. The resulting cells were resuspended in cold Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA), and 30-μl drops were seeded into pre-warmed 48-well culture plates at 37 °C for 10–20 min. Upon complete solidification, 400 μl of organoid culture media from [47] was added to each well, and plates were placed into incubators at 37 °C with a humidity level and 5% CO2. Organoid culture medium contained advanced DMEM/F12 supplemented with 100 µg/ml penicillin/streptomycin, 2-mM GlutaMAX 100 ×, 10-mM HEPES, 1:50 B-27 (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 5-mM nicotinamide, 1.25-mM N-acetylcysteine, 500-nM A83-01, and 500-nM SB202190 (Sigma Aldrich, Merck); 500 ng/ml R-spondin 1 and 100 ng/ml noggin (PeproTech, London, UK); and 100 ng/ml FGF-7, 25 ng/ml FGF-10, and 5-µM Y-27632 (StemCell, Vancouver, Canada).

Organoid nucleofection

Organoids were mechanically dissociated using glass Pasteur pipettes. After cell counting, 2 × 105 cells were resuspended with Lonza P3-Primary cell nucleofection Solution, and 1 μl of piR_004530 or control was added. Then, the solution was transferred to 100-μl nucleofection cuvette (V4XP-3012, Lonza, Basel, Switzerland). Nucleofection was performed using Lonza 4D Nucleofector with X unit using the program EA125. After nucleofection, organoid culture medium supplemented with 10-µM Y-27632 was added to the nucleofection cuvette. The cell mixture was then recovered and cultured in Matrigel drops as previously indicated.

Determination of organoid viability

Around 1 × 103 nucleofected cells were seeded in a 10-µl drop of Matrigel in a white opaque 96-well plate. At day 3, after 30 min of plate equilibration to RT, CellTiter-Glo 3D (Promega) reagent was added to each well in a volume of 1:1. The plate was shaken for 5 min and incubated for 25 min at RT. The luminescence was measured in an Orion II Microplate Luminometer.

Expression arrays analysis

Global gene expression in 10 tumor tissues (3 cases not expressing piR_004530 vs 7 cases expressing it) was analyzed by Affymetrix GeneChip Human Gene 2.1 ST array in the Genomics Core Facility from IDIBAPS. Raw.cel files were provided which were used for further analysis. Bioinformatic analysis was performed as previously described [48] using R version 4.1.1 and Bioconductor packages.

Statistical analyses

To verify that the data followed a normal distribution, Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used. Since data was not normally distributed (p < 0.001 in both tests), we used the Mann–Whitney U-test to compare differences between two groups in continuous variables and Kruskal–Wallis for more than two groups in the correlation with clinicopathological characteristics. The chi-square test was used to compare differences in proportions between two groups.

Median follow-up of the cohort was estimated using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method using prodlim package of R. The primary endpoints analyzed were disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). As a secondary endpoint, time to relapse (TTR) was also analyzed. DFS was calculated from the time of surgery to the date of relapse or death or last follow-up, OS was calculated from the time of surgery to the date of death by any cause or last follow-up, and TTR was calculated from the time of surgery to the date of relapse or last follow-up. Kaplan–Meier curves for DFS, OS, and TTR were drawn and compared by means of a log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted. All factors with a p-value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were included in the Cox multivariate regression analyses. Decision curve analysis (DCA) [49] was used to evaluate the clinical benefit of piR_004530 in TTR and patient’s risk stratification, using 48 months (4-year survival) as the survival time endpoint. The analysis was performed using dcurves package of R.

The optimal cutoff of piR_004530 expression for DFS and OS was identified using X-Tile v.3.6.1 (Yale University 2003–2005) [50]. Patients that log10(2−[(piR_004530 Ct−miR−191 Ct)−12.5]) > 1.19 were classified as high.

For in vitro studies, at least three technical and three biological replicates have been performed in each in vitro experiment. A one-sample t-test is used to compare with the control condition.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v29, GraphPad Prism 10, and R v4.1.1. The level of significance was set to p < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and prognostic predictors

The final analysis included 243 patients with a mean age of 67 years (range 33–85) and predominantly consisted of males (73.3%). Patients tended to be stage I (62.1%) and ADK (56%). A total of 72 (29.6%) patients received adjuvant therapy. None of the patients received neoadjuvant treatment. The median follow-up time was 84 months (interquartile range [IQR]: 65–99). A total of 96 (39.5%) patients experienced recurrence after surgery, and 56 of them had distant metastasis. The main clinicopathological characteristics and their impact on DFS and OS of the whole cohort are summarized in Table 1. Survival analysis was also conducted separately on SCC (n = 92) and non-SCC (n = 151) patients since the impact on prognosis of the piRNA analyzed differed between both histological subtypes. The REMARK diagram of the study is shown in Fig. 1.

Patient’s main clinicopathological characteristics

Most SCC patients were male (90.2%), and all of them were smokers (41.3% current and 58.7% former). The median follow-up time was 82 (IQR: 64–102) months. Patients tended to be stage I (58.7%). A total of 30 (32.6%) patients experienced disease recurrence after surgery. A total of 29 (31.5%) patients received adjuvant therapy, and this was the unique factor impacting DFS and OS in SCC, where patients receiving adjuvant treatment had a better DFS (median survival time: 51.6 months vs not reached; p = 0.004) and OS (66.4 months vs not reached; p = 0.008) than other patients (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A).

Non-SCC patients were 62.3% male and 37.1% female and mostly ADK (90.1%). The median follow-up time was 86 (IQR: 65–99) months. Patients tended to be stage I (64.2%). A total of 66 (43.7%) patients experienced postsurgical recurrence. A total of 43 (28.5%) patients received adjuvant therapy. Large tumor size, positive lymph node involvement, and higher stage were related to shorter DFS (p = 0.004, p < 0.001, and p = 0.007, respectively) and shorter OS (p = 0.08, p = 0.003, and p = 0.014, respectively) (Additional file 1: Fig. S2B).

piR_004530 expression in patient samples

The analysis of the expression levels of piR_004530 in tumor and paired normal tissue showed a significant upregulation in tumor tissue (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2A). Remarkably, in 63% of the normal tissue samples, the piR_004530 was not detectable. ROC curve analysis highlighted the ability of piR_004530 to discriminate tumor from normal tissue (area under the ROC curve [AUC]: 0.9867, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.9716–1, p < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

The correlation of piR_004530 expression with the main clinicopathological characteristics showed that it is significantly downregulated in females (p = 0.0051, Fig. 2C) and upregulated in SCC patients in comparison to ADK (p = 0.0266, Fig. 2D).

Patients with higher levels of piR_004530 had the worst prognosis after surgery

To explore the potential prognostic impact of piR_004530 expression, we determined the optimal cutoff for piR_004530 using X-tile, which identified the 75th percentile as the best discriminant point for survival analysis. Patients were classified into low (n = 182, 75%) or high (n = 61, 25%) expression. Patients with high expression had shorter DFS (38.6 vs.73.6 months, p = 0.008; Fig. 3A) and OS (54.3 months vs. not reached, p = 0.003; Fig. 3B) than patients with low piR_004530 expression.

Univariate Cox proportional regression analysis (Fig. 3A, B) corroborated the shorter DFS (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.637; 95% CI: 1.133–2.366; p = 0.009) and shorter OS (HR: 1.802; 95% CI: 1.212–2.678; p = 0.004) of patients with high piR_004530 expression levels. Moreover, in the multivariate Cox regression analysis, piR_004530 expression levels emerged as an independent prognostic factor for DFS (HR: 1.618; 95% CI: 1.108–2.362; p = 0.013) and OS (HR: 1.786; 95% CI: 1.187–2.686; p = 0.005) adjusted for patient’s sex and stage (Fig. 3C, D).

SCC patients with higher levels of piR_004530 displayed significantly worse outcomes after surgery

Since we observed significant differences in the expression levels between SCC (n = 92) and non-SCC patients (n = 151) (Fig. 2D), we decided to evaluate the prognostic role in each histological group independently (shown in REMARK diagram in Fig. 1).

In SCC patients, interestingly, we observed significant differences in piR_004530 expression levels according to relapse (Fig. 4A), where patients who relapsed after surgery had higher piRNA levels (p = 0.0355). When patients were classified in low (n = 55, 60%) or high (n = 37, 40%) expression, patients with high expression had a higher relapse rate (p = 0.007, Fig. 4B) and had shorter DFS (35.033 vs. 90.5 months, p < 0.001, Fig. 4C) and shorter OS (13.526 months vs. not reached, p = 0.004, Fig. 4D) than patients with low piR_004530 expression.

piR_004530 expression impacts relapse and survival of squamous cell carcinoma patients (SCC). A Box plot analysis of piR_004530 levels between relapsed and non-relapsed SCC patients

Supportively, univariate Cox proportional regression analysis (Fig. 4C, D) confirmed that high piR_004530 levels were related to shorter DFS (HR: 2.668, 95% CI: 1.499–4.751, p < 0.001) and shorter OS (HR: 2.336, 95% CI: 1.292–4.225, p = 0.005).

Moreover, in the multivariate Cox regression analysis, piR_004530 expression levels emerged as an independent prognostic factor for DFS (HR: 2.533; 95% CI: 1.414–4.538; p = 0.002) and OS (HR: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.185–3.9; p = 0.012) adjusted for adjuvant treatment (Fig. 4E, F).

In non-SCC patients (ADK, n = 136; other histology, n = 15), no significant differences were observed either for DFS or for OS according to piR_004530 levels (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A). Neither the analysis of ADK nor that of other histology independently showed any marked prognostic value (Additional file 1: Fig. S3B, C) highlighting their clinical significance in the outcome of SCC patients.

Consistency of piR_004530 prognostic trends in a validation patient cohort

To support the findings obtained in our initial cohort, we analyzed an independent set of 74 NSCLC patients who underwent surgical resection at our institution between November 2019 and October 2023 (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic during this period, the number of available samples was limited, which reduced the size and completeness of this validation series. Nevertheless, we observed a similar trend: patients with high piR_004530 expression showed shorter DFS (24.767 months vs. not reached, p = 0.00887), particularly among SCC cases (24.1 months vs. not reached, p = 0.00356) (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). While in OS the results did not reach significance in the whole cohort, the patients with high piR_004530 had shorter OS in both the whole cohort and in the SCC group (SCC: 48.5 months vs. not reached, p = 0.0528).

Although these results are based on a smaller and single-center cohort, they are consistent with the prognostic value observed in the main cohort and support the relevance of piR_004530 expression as a potential biomarker in NSCLC.

piR_004530 entails superior clinical benefit in the prediction of postsurgical relapse for non-treated patients

Since in SCC patients the piRNA expression and the administration of adjuvant treatment emerged as independent prognostic factors in the multivariate analysis, we decided to integrate both factors to evaluate whether the combination resulted in a superior risk stratification marker for postsurgical recurrence which could be of utility to improve post-surgery treatment decisions. For this analysis, we focused on the secondary endpoint TTR. Kaplan–Meier analysis for TTR unveiled that patients receiving adjuvant treatment after surgery (a clinical decision based on clinicopathological characteristics, including disease stage) had longer TTR (Fig. 5A) than patients not receiving treatment. In the group of patients not receiving adjuvant treatment, we observed that while patients with low piR_004530 did not show significant differences in relation to TTR with the group of treated patients, the group of non-treated patients with high piR_004530 had significantly shorter TTR (p = 0.00213, Fig. 5A). Moreover, this observation was also confirmed for DFS (p = 0.000271) and OS (p = 0.00154) (Additional file 1: Fig. S6). These results suggest that piR_004530 could be useful in identifying a high-risk group among patients, who, based on clinical characteristics, were not given adjuvant treatment after surgery. This group may benefit from adjuvant treatment despite the initial decision. To corroborate this hypothesis, a DCA analysis was applied to assess the clinical net benefit of piR_004530 on the prediction of postsurgical recurrence. Strikingly, multivariate models fitted for piR_004530 resulted in superior prediction of SCC patients’ TTR over tumor stage (control) (Fig. 5B).

piR_004530 overexpression in lung cancer cell lines promotes migration and invasion

We decided to conduct in vitro analysis to confirm the role of the piR_004530 as a biomarker for relapse. The expression analysis of piR_004530 in several lung cancer cell lines showed that the piRNA was underexpressed in two ADK cell lines and upregulated in one SCC cell line in comparison to the normal immortalized lung cell line BEAS2B (Additional file 1: Fig. S7A). We overexpressed piR_004530 in two SCC cell lines, HCC-15 and SK-MES-1 (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B), and in one ADK cell line H23 and evaluated whether the cell migration and invasion capacities of these cell lines were compromised. Overexpression of piR_004530 was associated with increased migration capacity in all cell lines, as observed in the wound-healing assay (Fig. 6A, B, C). Significant differences were observed at 24 h and 30 h for SK-MES-1 (p < 0.001 and p < 0.001, respectively) and for H23 (p = 0.0167 and p = 0.0138, respectively) and at 48, 72, and 96 h for HCC-15 (p = 0.013, p < 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively). We also confirmed that inhibition of piR_004530 using an ASO (Additional file 1: Fig. S7C) produced the opposite effect on migration, with reduced piR_004530 levels associated with decreased cell migration (Additional file 1: Fig. S8).

Additionally, the overexpression of piR_004530 also impacted cell invasion in the three cell lines analyzed. The cells overexpressing piR_004530 had a major invasion capacity through the extracellular matrix compared to control cells (Fig. 6D, E, F). In SK-MES-1, the cell staining quantification showed an increase of 65% (p = 0.0175); HCC-15 showed a rise of 15% (p = 0.0240), and H23 showed a rise of 38% (p = 0.036) compared to control cells.

Evaluation of the effect of piR_004530 overexpression on cell viability and apoptosis of lung cancer cells

The analysis of cell viability by measuring metabolic activity (MTS assay) showed minor or no differences between control and overexpressing piR_004530 cells (Additional file 1: Fig. S9A). Only a modest significant increase in proliferation can be observed in SK-MES-1 in the group overexpressing piR_004530, where a trend to significance was observed at 96 h (p = 0.0504) and a significant difference was detected at 120 h (p = 0.0395).

The apoptosis analysis showed that in two of the three cell lines analyzed, the apoptosis became reduced after piwiRNA overexpression. In SK-MES-1 and H23, a mean reduction of 25.1% (p = 0.0151) and 14.1% (p = 0.0145) in caspase 3/7 activity could be appreciated at 24 h, respectively, while no significant differences in apoptosis were observed in HCC-15 cell line (Fig. 6G, H, I).

piR_004530 overexpression enhanced colony formation

To assess whether piR_004530 impacted the ability of single cells to survive and grow into colonies, we performed a colony formation assay. The overexpression of piR_004530 enhanced colony formation in two of the three cell lines analyzed, HCC-15, H23 cells, but not in SK-MES-1 (Fig. 6J, K, L). After 15 days, piR_004530-overexpressing HCC-15 and H23 cells formed 59.17% (p = 0.0339) and 39.58% (p = 0.0032) more colonies, respectively, than controls.

piR_004530 overexpression induced stemness

Since it has been previously shown that the piRNA-PIWI axis is more active in undifferentiated cells and stem cells [35], we decided to analyze whether the overexpression of our piRNA induced the expression of different stem cell markers, including SOX2, OCT4, NANOG, CD133, CD44, and C-MYC. The overexpression of piR_004530 correlated with significant upregulation of SOX2 mRNA levels in both cell lines (p = 0.0115 for SK-MES-1 and p = 0.0423 for HCC-15; Fig. 7A, B). Moreover, in the SK-MES-1, significant upregulation of NANOG (p = 0.0013) and OCT4 (p = 0.0009) was observed (Fig. 7A). Moreover, we performed protein analysis of SOX2 levels since this stem cell marker was overexpressed in both cell lines. Protein levels of SOX2 increased after piR_004530 overexpression, especially in the HCC-15 cell line (Fig. 7C).

Identification of differential expressed genes between expressing and not expressing piR_004530 patients

To preliminarily address how piR_004530 exerts its oncogenic effect, we performed an analysis of expression arrays using previously generated data comparing patients with high piR_004530 expression (n = 7) to those without piRNA expression (n = 3). From this, we identified a set of 115 differentially expressed genes (Additional file 1: Fig. S10A and Additional file 2: Table S1). With the differentially expressed genes (DEG) identified, we performed a gene enrichment analysis for Gene Ontology terms to identify the biological processes affected by the re-expression of our piwiRNA. A high number of the DEG identified were related to metabolic processes (GO:0031323, GO:0060255, GO:0043170, GO:0019222; GO:0080090, GO:0009893). There was also an enrichment in genes associated with cell communication (GO: 0007154) and cell surface receptor signaling pathways (0007166). Moreover, an enrichment in genes related to response to different stimuli and to stress (GO:0050896, GO:0051716, GO:0042221, GO:0033554, GO:0006950) was also identified (Additional file 1: Fig. S10B).

With the idea that the piRNA could act as a microRNA binding to mRNA targets and inhibiting them, we performed a negative correlation analysis to identify genes whose expression decreased with the reactivation of the piRNA (Additional file 1: Fig. S10C and Additional file 3: Table S2). These genes may represent potential direct targets of this piRNA, and between them, the most significantly correlated gene was SERPINB6 (r = − 0.938, p = 0.0000612). We have also analyzed the prognostic role of SERPINB6 using Kaplan–Meier plotter data, and the lower expression correlated with shorter OS of lung cancer patients (p = 0.0038; Additional file 1: Fig. S10D). Additionally, other highly negatively correlated genes and lncRNAs have been identified in the top 5, including LMNTD2-AS, GRID2IP, LOC202181, or GAK. Low GAK levels were also observed to correlate with shorter OS in lung cancer using Kaplan–Meier plotter database (p < 0.001). No data on Kaplan–Meier plotter database was available neither for GRID2IP nor for the lncRNAs.

Moreover, since our hypothesis on the selection of this piwiRNA was related to reactivation of germline programs, we performed an additional analysis to identify other reactivated germline genes in cases re-expressing the piRNA. First, we identified genes positively correlated with the expression of our piRNA in the array data (Additional file 4: Table S3). Secondly, we cross-referenced the list obtained with the Cancer-Testis Antigens Database (CTDatabase) [51]. Using this database, we identified several genes that correlated positively with piR_004530 expression that has been consistently described as germline-reactivated genes in tumors, including MAGEB1, ARX, ZNF165, TEX101, GAGE13, SPAG4, ODF1, SYCE1, AKAP3, or SPANXC. Moreover, we have also identified in the list other genes that, despite not being included in the CTDatabase, are with high probability germline genes such as CATSPER4, other members of the TEX family (TEX2, TEX22, TEX45, TEX261), other members of the SPAG family (SPAG7, SPATA3), PRSS21, ADAM1A, DEFB108B, and TSKS.

Discussion

In resected NSCLC patients, the recurrence rate remains high (39.5% in our cohort) despite being treated with potentially curative surgery [52]. Adjuvant treatment has been proven to improve the prognosis of resected patients with N1/N2 NSCLC [53]; however, its efficacy in stage I patients remains controversial [54, 55]. Some small tumors are highly aggressive and, despite being detected at a clinically favorable stage, can progress to widespread, fatal disease. Moreover, the treatment does not differ across histological types, but the biological behavior of tumors varies significantly [56]. With the discussion reaching the cellular level, we must consider distinguishing NSCLC based on cellular characteristics, and this entails beginning our research by initially separating the most frequent histological subtypes.

In the present study, we decided to investigate the potential role of piR_004530 expression as a biomarker for NSCLC outcome in resected patients (clinical stages I–II) based on the idea that this germinal noncoding RNA, which is closely located in the genome to TEX29 [42] (six copies of this piRNA are located upstream of TEX29), could become reactivated during the carcinogenesis process and could be used as a prognostic biomarker [44]. First, we corroborated that TEX29 expression positively correlated with piR_004530 (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A), and, secondly, we verified that both genes become reactivated after DNA methyltransferase inhibition by 5-Aza-dC treatment (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B). Moreover, TEX29 was previously identified in the top 10 upregulated genes in CD133 + human prostate cells [57], which allowed us to hypothesize that its expression was related to stemness regulation, as is likely the case for our piRNA. All this makes us think that piR_004530 becomes reactivated together with TEX29 and could be part of a germline reactivation program related to the induction of tumorigenesis. Moreover, in lung ADK patients, it was observed that TEX29 hypomethylation was associated with shorter OS [43]. Additionally, using Kaplan–Meier plotter [58] (TEX29 probe 236853_at), we observed that NSCLC patients expressing high levels of TEX29 had shorter OS than patients with low levels (median survival 63.4 vs. 85 months, p = 0.002; Additional file 1: Fig. S11). However, a limitation of our study is that we did not directly assess the methylation status of TEX29 or its correlation with piR_004530 expression in our patient cohort. Although our in vitro results support the hypothesis of co-regulation by DNA methylation, this mechanism should be confirmed in future studies including methylation profiling of patient samples.

After studying piR_004530 expression in 276 samples (88% tumor tissues), we observed that this piRNA seems to be reactivated in tumor tissue, since in most paired normal tissue samples we were not able to detect its expression. In this line, we have previously shown that the PIWIL1 protein, involved in the biogenesis of piRNAs, becomes reactivated in the tumor tissue of some NSCLC patients, and this reactivation is associated with worse patient outcomes [32]. Both results corroborate that the PIWI/piRNA pathway reactivation, in this case exemplified by piR_004530, is an oncogenic event. Moreover, the higher levels of piR_004530 in tumor tissue were associated with shorter DFS and OS, indicating that we have identified a promising relapse biomarker. Although the analysis in the whole cohort showed in a significant way that higher levels of the piRNA were associated with worse prognosis, the subanalysis by histological groups showed that the effect of the piRNA was more relevant in the SCC subgroup. In this group, the piRNA expression was significantly higher than that in other histologies and was also significantly higher in relapsed patients. Moreover, while in SCC patients the higher levels were associated in a stronger way with shorter DFS and OS, the differences did not remain significant in the non-SCC subgroup. As a further step to support our findings, we performed a preliminary validation using an independent cohort of patients resected at our center between 2019 and 2023. Although the number of available samples was limited due to restricted access to clinical material during the COVID-19 pandemic, the observed results were consistent with those from our initial cohort, particularly in SCC patients, reinforcing the potential prognostic value of piR_004530 in NSCLC. We acknowledge, however, that this second cohort was derived from the same institution, and that external and prospective multicenter validation will be necessary to confirm the generalizability of our findings.

Given the well-established association between smoking and lung squamous cell carcinoma, we evaluated whether smoking history could confound the prognostic significance of piR_004530. We first assessed piR_004530 expression across different smoking status groups (never, former, and current smokers) and found no significant differences. Additionally, in a multivariate Cox regression model that included smoking status, age, sex, and tumor stage, piR_004530 remained an independent prognostic factor. These results suggest that the prognostic value of piR_004530 is not confounded by smoking history and may serve as a robust biomarker regardless of tobacco exposure.

Additionally, since we observed a significant difference in piRNA expression between male and female patients—with lower levels in the latter group—we decided to evaluate whether the piRNA exhibits sex-specific effects on the survival of lung cancer patients. However, in both sexes, higher levels of the piRNA were associated with worse survival (Additional file 1: Fig. S12). Therefore, no sex-dependent effect on survival was observed.

Although the TNM classification system is the most relevant clinical factor used to decide postsurgical strategies in lung cancer patients [59], this does not account for the significant differences in survival rates between different histopathological cell types. Fukui T. et al. analyzed the prognosis after surgery of 1587 patients with ADK (77%) and 472 with SCC (23%) and reported significant survival differences between ADK and SCC patients. Specifically, they observed that stage I SCC patients exhibited markedly worse prognoses compared to ADK patients [60]. Back to our cohort, we discovered that among patients in stage IA, those with SCC exhibited significantly poorer OS compared to those with ADK. Moreover, when we performed the univariate and multivariate analysis, the unique clinical factor that impacted the survival of SCC patients was the administration of adjuvant treatment after surgery, showing that the treated patients had longer OS than non-treated patients, which included most stage I patients. With this context, we decided to evaluate whether our piRNA could be of utility to discriminate patients at high risk of relapse who are not initially considered for adjuvant treatment only taking into account clinical characteristics. Interestingly, we observed that patients not receiving adjuvant treatment with higher piRNA levels had more recurrence and shorter survival, which suggests that the piRNA could be used as a complementary tool to choose patients tributaries of receiving adjuvant treatment after surgery in this group. Supporting this, the DCA analysis showed that the piRNA had a superior net benefit compared to the tumor stage in predicting postsurgical relapse. In this line, Hsiao S. H. et al. have shown that platinum-based adjuvant chemotherapy provides a survival benefit for patients with surgically resected early-stage SCC, but they did not observe significant differences for ADK [61]. Under the current TNM classification system that does not separate by histology, certain SCC patients with poor prognoses are grouped together with ADK patients with good prognoses, and these groups do not receive subsequent treatments. In other tumors, such as esophageal cancer, the last classification system analyzes early-stage patients with different histology separately, which improves patient outcomes [62]. While this approach would undoubtedly increase complexity, it could potentially lead to more tailored and effective treatment strategies. Therefore, it would be crucial to treat all SCC patients at high risk of relapse independently of the stage, and piR_004530 can be of utility in this labor.

To confirm that the role as a biomarker of piR_004530 was related to its oncogenic potential, we performed several in vitro analyses in lung cancer cell lines and in patient-derived lung cancer organoids. The overexpression of the piRNA in the lung cancer cell lines promoted migration and invasion. Moreover, in two of the cell lines, SK-MES-1 and H23, reduced apoptosis, while not significant differences were observed for HCC-15 cell line. Additionally, colony formation was enhanced in HCC15 and H23 cell lines, showing that the overexpression of the piwiRNA impacts clonogenic capacity and long-term cell growth. The potential explanation of the differences observed between cell lines can be associated with intrinsic differences in the molecular characteristics of the different cell lines. However, to our knowledge, this is the first time that the oncogenic role of piR_004530 has been reported. However, in the literature, other piRNAs have been previously identified with a pathogenic role in NSCLC [63], including some of the most studied ones such as piR-651. PiR-651 has been described also as upregulated in NSCLC and playing a function promoting proliferation, inhibiting apoptosis, and even regulating cell migration [64, 65] in a similar way to our piRNA.

Since it has been previously shown that the PIWI/piRNA pathway is involved in the control of stemness [66], we decided to evaluate whether the overexpression of piR_004530 had some impact on stem cell markers and in 3D spheroid formation. Cancer stem cells tend to express stem cell markers such as SOX2, NANOG, CD133, and CD44, among others [63]. Interestingly, we observed that in both cell lines, an upregulation of the SOX2 gene was observed (we also validated this at protein level), and an upregulation of NANOG and OCT4 was also observed in the SK-MES-1 cell line. SOX2 has been described to be related to SCC histology [67], and its downregulation inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in NSCLC cell lines [68, 69], which is in line with the effect observed with our piRNA. Moreover, in relation to OCT4 and NANOG upregulation, it has been reported that in both cases, higher levels of OCT4 [70] or NANOG [71] were associated with worse OS in NSCLC patients. Additionally, its coexpression has been related to the induction of cancer stem cell properties and malignancy [72]. The reactivation of these stem cell genes after induction of piR_004530 is in line with the promotion of a more aggressive phenotype. Moreover, we observed that piR_004530 overexpression was associated with the formation of larger spheroids, which confirms the functional relevance of this piRNA in regulating stemness. Recently, it has been argued that piRNAs may play a potential role in cancer stem cells and contribute to the tumorigenesis process [35]. Although a limited number of studies have evaluated the role of piRNAs in cancer stem cell regulation, some piRNAs have been identified as upregulated, such as piR_823 [73] and piR-932 [74] in breast cancer stem cells, or MW557525, which is necessary to reprogram fibroblasts to cancer stem cells [75].

Finally, we validated the oncogenic role of the piRNA in patient-derived samples using organoids, which are stem cell-based models [46]. We generated patient-derived lung cancer organoids from three SCC patients and evaluated cell viability after the overexpression of the piRNA. In all cases, the piRNA overexpression boosted cell viability, demonstrating that the piRNA is involved in the regulation of the cancer stem cells and the regulation of the organoid growth in a 3D context.

To gain preliminary insight into the potential mechanism through which piR_004530 exerts its oncogenic effects, we performed an exploratory analysis using previously generated expression array data. By comparing patients with high piR_004530 expression to those lacking its expression, we identified a set of DEGs, many of which were enriched in biological processes related to metabolism, cell communication, and stress response. Additionally, a correlation analysis revealed several negatively associated genes, including SERPINB6 and GAK, both of which showed significant prognostic value in lung cancer with lower levels associated with shorter OS. SERPINB6 is an intracellular serine protease inhibitor that regulates proteolytic activity, protecting cells from damage due to uncontrolled protease action. By inhibiting specific serine proteases, it contributes to maintaining cellular homeostasis and preventing excessive stress or apoptosis. In various cancers, SERPINB6 is often downregulated [76], potentially leading to enhanced protease activity and promoting tumor cell invasion, migration, and progression. Its loss may also facilitate extracellular matrix degradation and impair the cellular stress response, contributing to a more aggressive phenotype [77]. In lung cancer specifically, lower SERPINB6 expression has been associated with increased tumor aggressiveness and a poorer prognosis [78]. These findings suggest that piR_004530 may exert part of its function through the repression of specific mRNA targets, such as SERPINB6 and GAK. While these results offer valuable clues, we acknowledge that elucidating the precise molecular mechanism of piR_004530’s action requires further investigation and functional validation, which we propose as a focus for future studies.

Finally, we also identified positively correlated genes with our piRNA, and some of them have been previously validated as cancer-testis tumor-reactivated genes. In this sense, our data support the hypothesis that piR_004530 is part of a germline-associated transcriptional program that becomes reactivated in some patients during the carcinogenesis process.

Conclusions

In summary, we have shown that piR_004530 became reactivated in NSCLC and played a role as an oncogene, enhancing cell viability and promoting migration and invasion in both cell lines and patient-derived organoids. Moreover, this piRNA is a promising biomarker for SCC patients, since its higher levels are associated with a higher probability of disease recurrence after surgery and shorter OS. Although further investigation in additional cohorts of patients is warranted to validate these findings, we believe that we have identified a promising biomarker that can serve as a useful tool to guide treatment decisions regarding adjuvant therapy in SCC patients. Since no other studies detail in-depth research on this piRNA, we will continue to explore the relevant mechanisms involved in the oncogenic effect of this piRNA in NSCLC.
