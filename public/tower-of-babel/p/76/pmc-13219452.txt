From expectation to strain: how pregnancy time perspective shapes postpartum stress in Poland and Italy
Authors: Małgorzata Sobol, Elisa Mancinelli, Alessandra Schiavo, Silvia Salcuni, Michał Meisner, Nicola Cellini, Giovanna Mioni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study examined the associations between couples’ time perspectives during pregnancy and women’s postpartum stress. Using a cross-cultural longitudinal design, 57 heterosexual couples expecting a child were recruited from Italy (n = 28 couples) and Poland (n = 29 couples). Data were collected at three time points: during the first and third trimesters, and within six months postpartum. The women were assessed using the Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (ZTPI) and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), and the men completed the ZTPI. The results indicate the presence of cultural differences in the associations between time perspective and postpartum stress. Among Polish women, a heightened past-negative perspective during pregnancy predicted increased postpartum stress. In contrast, among Italian women, a stronger present-fatalistic perspective during pregnancy emerged as a predictor of elevated postpartum stress. These findings are discussed in relation to cultural tendencies to emphasize particular time perspective and their implications for the development of time-perspective-based interventions for expecting couples.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47775-1.Keywords: Postpartum depression, Culture, Time perspective, StressSubject terms: Health care, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Postpartum stress

The postpartum period constitutes one of the most physically and emotionally demanding stages in a woman’s life, during which she must confront both new responsibilities associated with the maternal role and the physiological changes occurring in her body1. Such changes may lead to heightened postpartum stress, defined as a state of increased psychological tension and adaptive burden that emerges following childbirth2.

Postpartum stress may lead to emotional disturbances3 and is identified as one of the strongest predictors of postpartum depression4. Maternal stress during the postpartum period also exerts a substantial influence on the infant’s physical and psychological development. When persistent, recurrent, or chronic, it can adversely affect child development5,6. The psychological consequences of maternal postpartum stress may remain observable even in later childhood2.

Physical, intrapersonal, and interpersonal stressors can be distinguished during the postpartum period7. Among the physical symptoms reported by mothers, fatigue is consistently identified as the primary concern, resulting mainly from sleep deprivation and disruption of circadian rhythms. Additional physical stressors include somatic complaints such as breast discomfort, numbness in the hands, dizziness, and other ailments. One of the most frequently reported intrapersonal stressors is anxiety related to body weight. Difficulties associated with identifying with the maternal role are also cited as a significant challenge during the postpartum period. With regard to interpersonal stressors, the multiplicity and overlap of roles during this time often constitute a common source of conflict and tension. Changes also occur in the relationship with the partner; a decline in relationship satisfaction or increased marital tension following childbirth is widespread. Interactions with the partner are considered stressors of particularly high intensity7. At the same time, women in the perinatal period demonstrate a heightened need for emotional support from close others, especially from their partner8.

Most of these kinds of stressors, such as sleep deprivation due to continuous, intensive newborn care, fatigue arising from disruption of circadian rhythms, postpartum somatic complaints, discomfort related to changes in body appearance are largely unavoidable9,10. Therefore, knowledge of psychological factors that influence the intensity of perceived postpartum stress is of crucial importance. Such knowledge enables the implementation of preventive and therapeutic interventions that may reduce the risk of heightened postpartum stress. It is particularly important to undertake this type of therapeutic action during pregnancy. Pregnancy is a period during which women typically attend regular medical appointments and participate in antenatal education classes, creating numerous opportunities for healthcare professionals to implement psychological interventions aimed at reducing the risk of elevated postpartum stress. The foundation of such interventions is knowledge about the psychological factors present during pregnancy that influence postpartum stress and are simultaneously modifiable.

Time perspective

Time perspective constitutes, on the one hand, a psychological variable that can be modified through therapeutic interventions11,12. On the other hand, it represents a psychological characteristic that is central to coping with stress associated with major life changes, in which confrontation with the past, present, and future acquires particular significance13,14. At the same time, research shows that coping in stressful situations depends not only on an individual’s time perspective, but also on the time perspectives exhibited by those closest to them12. The influence of a woman’s immediate environment is particularly strong during the perinatal period because women in the perinatal period are very sensitive to emotional cues from their social environment and to the behavior of their closest significant others15,16. Maternal mental health during the perinatal period is significantly shaped by the partner’s behavior and emotional responses17,18. Significant associations have also been demonstrated between the partner’s time perspective and relationship quality in intimate partnerships19.

Time perspective is defined as an individual predisposition to direct attention toward the past, present, or future, accompanied by a positive or negative evaluation of a given temporal dimension12,20. Five basic types of time perspective are most commonly distinguished: past- positive, past-negative, present-hedonistic, present-fatalistic, and future12,20. The past-negative perspective encompasses a pessimistic appraisal of past events, characterized by persistent rumination on negative experiences and difficulty in overcoming them; it is often associated with feelings of regret, anxiety, and strained social relationships. In contrast, the past-positive perspective reflects a nostalgic and grateful view of past experiences, fostering the cultivation and preservation of positive memories; however, its excessive dominance may lead to an overemphasis on the past at the expense of the present and future. A positive future perspective is goal-oriented and associated with planning, internal control, and a proactive approach to life. Nevertheless, excessive focus on the future may limit the ability to derive pleasure from the present moment and increase anxiety levels. The present-hedonistic perspective emphasizes immediate pleasure and spontaneity, often disregarding long-term consequences. While it promotes vitality and risk-taking, its extreme form may result in impulsive and unhealthy behaviors. The present-fatalistic perspective reflects a sense of helplessness and a lack of belief in one’s ability to influence the course of events, which is frequently associated with passivity and low self-efficacy12,20.

Experiencing elevated levels of stress is particularly strongly associated with a past-negative perspective and a fatalistic perspective21–24. It is important to underscore that the association between specific types of time perspectives and adaptive functioning may be contingent upon a range of moderating factors.

Cultural factors as a moderator

Cultural factors are among those that influence the extent to which a given time perspective serves adaptive or maladaptive functions25–27. The impact of cultural factors of this kind is particularly emphasized in the cultural values theory proposed by Kluckhohn and Strodtbeck [see28]. According to this theory, one of the dimensions of culture is orientation toward the past, present, or future, which shapes the adaptiveness of individual time perspectives among members of a given culture. Similarly, in the context of Hofstede’s cultural dimensions theory29, we can speak of cultures that promote respect for tradition and authority, in which a past-negative perspective is particularly maladaptive. In contrast, in societies oriented toward change and the development of new ideas, a focus on a positively perceived future may be more adaptive29.

The moderating role of cultural factors in the relationship between time perspective and psychological functioning is also supported by empirical research. For example, the association between negative time perspective and psychological outcomes such as stress and self-concept clarity differed across cultural contexts, including China, the United States, and Germany30. Similarly, the strength of the relationship between time perspective and life satisfaction varied depending on the country in which the study was conducted31. Cultural factors also exert a substantial influence on both the intensity and the nature of postpartum stress32. Analyses of postpartum post-traumatic stress disorder indicate that the severity of this condition varies across countries33–35. Furthermore, research has addressed the significance of cultural rituals and practices related to the postpartum period. For example, the experience of postpartum stress and psychological difficulties was strongly embedded in traditions such as zuo yue zi—a period of postpartum confinement and recovery36. Studies on postpartum stress among Chinese immigrant mothers in Japan have shown that the inability to adhere to traditional postpartum practices increases the level of stress and psychological difficulties following childbirth37. Cross-cultural studies have also revealed differences in the extent to which sociodemographic and medical factors influence the severity of postpartum stress across countries. Sociodemographic differences had a greater impact on postpartum stress severity in Poland than in Slovakia and Lithuania. Conversely, medical complications had a smaller influence on stress levels in Poland35. It has been demonstrated that unrealistic cultural assumptions about motherhood contribute to work overload, difficulties in transitioning into the maternal role, increased stress, feelings of guilt, and, in some cases, depression38. Cross-cultural research on postpartum stress provides additional and important value by enabling the identification of both universal and culturally specific predictors of emotional disturbances following childbirth.

The present research

Accordingly, the aim of the present study was to analyze the impact of the time perspective of pregnant women and their partners on the severity of postpartum stress experienced by women in Poland and Italy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the influence of couples’ time perspectives during pregnancy on the intensity of postpartum stress. It is also the first study to compare time perspectives during pregnancy and postpartum stress levels between Poland and Italy. Previous research examining the effects of psychological factors during pregnancy on postpartum stress has shown that depression and anxiety in the third trimester of pregnancy intensify postpartum stress39. With regard to existing studies on the relationship between time perspective and postpartum stress, it has been shown that strengthening a positive, balanced time perspective after childbirth through an eight-week mobile application–based intervention reduces stress levels among women experiencing high stress on the second day following cesarean Sect40.

The proposed model of relationships is grounded in time perspective theory12 as well as the theoretical framework of cross-cultural psychology, which posits that an individual’s cultural background fundamentally shapes cognitive processes and patterns of behavior41. Cultures establish conventions that regulate how information is sampled and how much importance is attributed to elements drawn from the environment. The following dimensions are most commonly used to describe culture: complexity (defined, for example, by gross national income per capita and the proportion of the population living in urban areas), tightness (in tight cultures, norms are strictly enforced), collectivism (in collectivist cultures, people are interdependent with their groups, such as the family, and prioritize group goals over individual goals), and individualism (which emphasizes self-reliance, individual achievement, and uniqueness)41.

Poland and Italy constitute an interesting context for examining both universal and culturally specific aspects of time perspective and postpartum stress, as the two countries share numerous sociodemographic characteristics. Poland and Italy, as members of the European cultural sphere, exhibit comparable levels of cultural complexity and societal tightness42,43. These parallels are manifested in their respective degrees of modernization and industrialization, per capita income, ecological conditions, educational attainment, literacy rates, and overall standards of living. Additionally, in both contexts, women predominantly assume the role of primary caregivers during the early postpartum period44,45. Given these general similarities, it can be hypothesized that the prevalence and intensity of postpartum stress among women in the two countries are likely to be similar.

However, there are substantial differences between Polish and Italian populations with regard to lifestyle, climate, culturally embedded childrearing values, and patterns of family relationships. Poland is less individualistic than Western European countries, including Italy43. The lower individualism of Poles is primarily manifested in the strong role of social obligations, the importance attached to others’ opinions in shaping self-perception, and a high acceptance of hierarchy and authority46,47. Moreover, pregnancy and motherhood in Poland are deeply embedded in traditional social models, according to which women are expected to fulfill the role of a self-sacrificing mother, consistent with the cultural ideal of the “Polish Mother”. This archetype symbolizes a woman–mother who is unconditionally devoted to her family and homeland, characterized by self-denial, patience, courage, and often solitary struggles with life’s adversities48. In Italy, individualism, autonomy, and self-expression are culturally salient; however, this individualism coexists with strong familial bonds and is predominantly relational and context-dependent. Multigenerational family structures, often spanning up to three generations, are prevalent, and maintaining positive relationships with both immediate and extended family members carries considerable cultural significance49.

Because the ideal of the “Polish Mother” is still strong in Poland—an independent, resourceful, self-reliant woman who sacrifices herself for her family and copes with adversity on her own—we assume that the fatalistic perspective will have particularly negative consequences for Polish women during the perinatal period. The fatalist perspective is associated with passivity, helplessness, and a sense of lack of control over events, which may greatly hinder the fulfillment of the culturally embedded stereotypical role of the Polish “Mother Pole.” Therefore, in Poland this perspective will most likely be the most strongly associated with perinatal stress.

In Italy, great importance is placed on relationships with one’s family of origin and with tradition, including family traditions45, which provide a sense of security, support, and belonging. Therefore, we assume that the past-negative perspective in Italy will be most strongly associated with increased perinatal stress, because this type of time perspective is related to a lack of a sense of being rooted in a positive past, including tradition, and to a focus on negative experiences from childhood and youth.

Furthermore, given that Italian culture places a strong emphasis on family ties and interdependence, whereas Polish cultural norms continue to uphold the ideal of an independent, self-reliant, and resilient “Polish Mother”48 it was hypothesized that the partner’s negative time perspective would exert a stronger influence on women’s postpartum stress in the Italian context than in the Polish context. Additionally, an exploratory research question was formulated to examine the associations of couples’ past-positive, future positive, and present-hedonistic perspective with postpartum stress within the cultural contexts of Italy and Poland.

Method

Participants

A total of 120 participants were recruited from Italy (n = 30 women; n = 30 men) and Poland (n = 30 women; n = 30 men) between October 2023 and July 2025. Due to financial and organizational constraints, it was possibly to analyze 30 pairs in each country. Exclusion criteria included being under 17 years of age, same-sex couples, absence of a partner, and a history of or current serious psychiatric disorders. In order to be included in the study, participants had to be born in Poland (Polish group) or in Italy (Italian group). Participants voluntarily agreed to take part in the study. In Italy, the couples were recruited at a gynecological clinic in Treviso, while the Polish couples were recruited at a gynecological clinic in Żywiec.

Procedure

Recruitment, both in Poland and in Italy, was conducted by gynecologists during one of the patient’s first gynecological visits during pregnancy. Participants included patients who came to a given gynecologist after reading a flyer about the study, as well as the gynecologist’s regular patients The participants attended three study visits: during the first (T1) and third (T2) trimesters of pregnancy, and between 5 and 13 weeks postpartum (T3). At each assessment, women underwent a medical interview regarding the course of their pregnancy/delivery and postpartum condition, as well as a standard gynecological examination. After the medical visit, the women completed online questionnaires assessing time perspective and stress levels, for which they received links. During the first assessment, sociodemographic data were additionally collected via an online survey. Men completed the online time perspective questionnaire at each of the three assessments, and during the first assessment, they also completed an online survey collecting sociodemographic data.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment, and they received monetary compensation of €93. Assessments were conducted during scheduled gynecological visits, during which participants were also provided with access to online questionnaires. All procedures were conducted in accordance with established ethical standards, with approval of the study protocol being obtained from the institutional ethics committee.

Measures

Sociodemographic and medical data were collected using structured surveys. The instruments included items assessing participants’ age, place of residence, occupation, educational attainment, and income. Medical history was evaluated through questions regarding prior mental health conditions, chronic illnesses (e.g., “Do you suffer from a chronic disease?” with response options “Yes”/“No”), and current medication use. Additionally, the questions concerned the course of pregnancy and childbirth. Standard gynecological assessment results were also recorded.

Perceived stress, defined as the degree to which individuals evaluate situations in their lives as stressful—encompassing experiences such as feeling upset, nervous, overwhelmed, out of control, or unable to cope during the past month—was assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10)50 in its Polish51 and Italian adaptation52. The scale comprises ten items measuring the frequency of stress-related experiences, with responses provided on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (very often). In the present study, the PSS-10 demonstrated good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.84 for Poland and 0.80 for Italy.

Time perspective was assessed using the Zimbardo Time Perspective Inventory (ZTPI)12, as adapted into Polish53 and Italian54. The ZTPI comprises 56 items that evaluate five distinct time perspectives: past-negative, reflecting a tendency to focus on negative past experiences; past-positive, indicating a nostalgic and pleasurable view of the past; present-fatalistic, representing a sense of helplessness and a lack of control over the present and a belief in destiny/fate; present-hedonistic, denoting a focus on immediate pleasure and gratification; and future positive, reflecting a goal-oriented and optimistic outlook toward the future. Responses are recorded on a five-point Likert-type scale. In the present study, the internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) of the ZTPI subscales ranged from 0.73 to 0.85 for Italy and Poland.

Statistical analyses

To examine the cross-cultural comparability of the measures, measurement invariance was tested using multigroup confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA). Four increasingly restrictive models were estimated: Model 1: configural invariance, assuming the same factor structure across groups; Model 2: metric (weak) invariance, constraining factor loadings to be equal across groups; Model 3: scalar (strong) invariance, constraining both factor loadings and item intercepts to be equal across groups; and Model 4: strict invariance, constraining factor loadings, item intercepts, and residual variances to be equal across groups.

Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Following commonly recommended criteria, values of CFI and TLI ≥ 0.90 were considered indicative of acceptable fit, whereas RMSEA and SRMR ≤ 0.08 indicated adequate model fit. Measurement invariance across groups was assessed by examining changes in fit indices between nested models. Consistent with recommended guidelines, invariance was assumed when the change in CFI (ΔCFI) was ≤ 0.01 and the change in RMSEA (ΔRMSEA) was ≤ 0.015 between successive models.

Changes in women’s perceived stress across the transition to parenthood were examined using a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), with time as a within subjects factor (first trimester, third trimester, and postpartum) and country as a between subjects factor (Poland vs. Italy). To examine the relationships between mothers’ and fathers’ time perspective and the level of stress experienced by mothers, we conducted correlation analyses. Given the large number of tested associations, the Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons and reduce the risk of Type I error55. To examine whether women’s and men’s prenatal past-negative and present-fatalistic time perspectives predict women’s postpartum stress, a series of linear regression analyses were conducted. Separate models were estimated for women and men, with analyses performed independently for the first and third trimesters of pregnancy, within each national sample. To directly compare the strength and direction of these relationships across countries, a moderated regression analysis was performed. In this model, country was included as a moderator, and interaction terms between country and each predictor (past-negative perspective × country; fatalistic perspective × country) were introduced. This approach allowed us to test whether the associations between time perspective and postpartum stress differed significantly between the Polish and Italian samples, thereby providing insight into the potential cross-cultural specificity of the examined relationships.

All structural equation modeling analyses were conducted using the lavaan package in R56. Descriptive statistics, independent-samples t tests, Pearson correlation coefficients, and linear regression analyses were estimated using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.

Results

Characteristics of the study sample

The analyses included data from 28 couples from Italy and 29 couples from Poland. In the Italian group, one woman underwent a pregnancy termination in the third month, and the partner of one woman refused to participate in the study without providing a reason. In the Polish group, one woman declined to participate in the study in the third trimester of pregnancy, without giving a reason. Participants who dropped out did not differ significantly in sociodemographic characteristics from those included in the analyses.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted to evaluate the statistical power of the analyses given the sample size. For the comparison using an independent-samples t test with α = 0.05 (two-tailed), the analysis indicated that the study had adequate power (approximately 0.80) to detect large effects (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.80), whereas the power to detect medium-sized effects (d ≈ 0.50) was substantially lower (approximately 0.47). For the linear regression analyses the statistical power was sufficient primarily for detecting large effects (Cohen’s f² ≈ 0.35; power ≈ 0.75–0.78), whereas the power to detect medium-sized effects (f² ≈ 0.15) remained limited (approximately 0.42–0.44).

Comparisons and correlations between variables

The repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed no significant main effect of time, indicating that women’s perceived stress did not significantly change across the three measurement points, F(2, 108) = 1.47, p = 0.235. There was also no significant main effect of country, suggesting that overall levels of perceived stress did not differ between Polish and Italian women, F(1, 55) = 0.16, p = 0.694. Additionally, the interaction between time and country was not significant, F(2, 108) = 1.04, p = 0.358, indicating that the pattern of changes in perceived stress over time was similar for Polish and Italian participants.

The results of the comparative analyses indicate that the Polish women exhibited a stronger hedonistic perspective during pregnancy than the Italian women. As for the men, Polish participants were more focused on the positive past and demonstrated higher levels of hedonism and fatalism than their Italian counterparts (Table 2).

In the Polish sample, couples’ place of residence was significantly correlated with postpartum stress, with higher stress levels observed in larger cities. Additionally, significant associations were found between women’s higher past-negative perspective in the first trimester and increased postpartum stress. Regarding men’s time perspective, a high past-negative perspective in the third trimester was associated with increased postpartum stress in women (Table 3).

Prediction of postpartum stress based on couples’ time perspective during pregnancy

Linear regression analyses were performed to assess the predictive value of couples’ past-negative and present-fatalistic time perspectives on postpartum stress levels in Polish and Italian participants.

Results of regression analyses in the Polish group

In the Polish sample, the model including women’s time perspectives assessed in the first trimester proved to be statistically significant, F(2, 26) = 4.56, p = 0.02, accounting for 26% of the variance in the dependent variable (R² = 0.26). An examination of the regression coefficients indicated that women’s past-negative time perspective was a predictor of women’s postpartum stress (β = 0.59, t(26) = 2.94, p = 0.007). This finding suggests that a higher level of past-negative perspective in women during the first trimester was associated with higher levels of postpartum stress. Multicollinearity diagnostics did not indicate any problems related to correlations among the independent variables. For both predictors, tolerance values were 0.70 and VIF values were 1.43, indicating that the explanatory variables were not excessively correlated and that their effects on the dependent variable were stable and reliable.

With respect to the third trimester, the regression model predicting women’s postpartum stress in Poland, was also statistically significant and explained 25% of the variance in the dependent variable (R² = 0.25), F(2, 26) = 4.30, p = 0.024. Again, women’s higher past-negative time perspective emerged as a predictor of women’s increased postpartum stress (β = 0.63, p = 0.007). VIF values for both explanatory variables were 1.59, indicating no multicollinearity and confirming the stability of the model estimates.

For men’s time perspectives in the Polish sample, during the woman’s first trimester of pregnancy, the regression model predicting women’s postpartum stress was statistically significant and accounted for 34% of the variance in the dependent variable (R² = 0.34), F(2, 26) = 6.73, p = 0.004. In this group men’s higher past-negative perspective (β = 0.55, t(26) = 2.20, p = 0.037) was a predictor of women’s increased postpartum stress. VIF values for both independent variables were 1.00, indicating no multicollinearity and confirming adequate model stability. In the third trimester, the regression model predicting women’s postpartum stress was also statistically significant and explained 21% of the variance in the dependent variable (R² = 0.21), F(2, 26) = 3.47, p = 0.046. Men’s higher past-negative perspective emerged as a predictor of women’s increased postpartum stress (β = 0.36, t(26) = 2.04, p = 0.05). VIF values for both predictors were 1.05, again indicating no multicollinearity.

Results of regression analyses in the Italian group

In the Italian sample of women, for the first trimester, the regression model predicting women’s postpartum stress was statistically significant and explained 32% of the variance in the dependent variable (R² = 0.32), F(2, 21) = 5.07, p = 0.016. In this group, higher women’s present-fatalistic time perspective was a predictor of increased women’s postpartum stress (β = 0.50, p = 0.045). VIF values for both independent variables were 1.72, indicating no multicollinearity and satisfactory model stability. For the third trimester, the regression model predicting women’s postpartum stress was not statistically significant. With regard to men’s time perspectives in the Italian sample, regression models predicting women’s postpartum stress were not statistically significant in either the first or the third trimester of pregnancy. Differences in the relationship between time perspective and postpartum stress in Poland and Italy

A hierarchical regression analysis including interaction terms was conducted to test whether the effects of past-negative and present-fatalistic perspectives during pregnancy on postpartum stress differed between Polish and Italian participants. For the first trimester, in the group of women, the interactions between past-negative perspective and country (β = −1.26, p = 0.037), as well as between present-fatalistic perspective and country (β = 1.59, p = 0.05), were significant, indicating that the associations between these time perspectives in the first trimester and postpartum stress differed across groups. For the third trimester, in the group of women, the interaction between past-negative time perspective and country was also significant (β = −0.48, p = 0.041), indicating that the association between past-negative perspective and postpartum stress differed across cultural groups.

With respect to men’s time perspectives, in the first trimester the interactions between past-negative perspective and country (β = −1.16, p = 0.016) was significant, indicating that the associations between men’s past-negative perspective and women’s postpartum stress differed across cultural contexts. In the third trimester, the interaction between men’s past-negative perspective and country was also significant (β = −1.12, p = 0.046), indicating cross-cultural differences in the association between men’s past-negative perspective in late pregnancy and women’s postpartum stress.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to examine couples’ time perspectives as predictors of postpartum stress among women in Poland and Italy. The obtained results partially supported the proposed hypotheses. As expected, levels of postpartum stress reported by women in both countries were comparable. The mean level of stress was moderate and consistent with findings from previous studies conducted in Poland59 and Italy60.

Contrary to expectations, couples’ past-negative time perspective during pregnancy significantly predicted women’s postpartum stress in the Polish sample, but not in the Italian sample. The stronger the past-negative perspective in the first trimester of pregnancy, the higher the level of postpartum stress reported by Polish women. These findings suggest that, among various temporal orientations, a tendency to focus on a globally negatively evaluated past exerts a particularly detrimental effect on maternal mental health during the perinatal period in Poland. A past-negative perspective reflects a generalized, pessimistic, and aversive orientation toward one’s past experiences and the manner in which they are interpreted. Given the reconstructive nature of autobiographical memory, entrenched negative attitudes toward the past may stem both from actual exposure to adverse or traumatic events and from biased, negatively distorted reinterpretations of neutral or ambivalent memories, or from the co-occurrence of both mechanisms12. Individuals with a high level of past-negative perspective, when confronted with stressful situations, are particularly prone to becoming “stuck in the past,” which increases the likelihood of developing symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression61. Numerous studies have demonstrated a strong association between a negative past perspective and elevated stress levels12,62,63.

In contrast, among Italian women, an intensified present-fatalistic perspective during pregnancy in the first trimester, emerged as a predictor of heightened postpartum stress. A fatalistic perspective is associated with a perceived lack of influence over life events and, consequently, with feelings of helplessness and powerlessness. Such an orientation may intensify feelings of being overwhelmed by difficulties and lacking control over the situation, which are characteristic of stress experiences. These findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating associations between a strong fatalistic perspective and increased stress12,64.

The observed differences between Poland and Italy in the associations between time perspective and postpartum stress may be interpreted in light of cultural differences related to attitudes toward the past and the future. In Polish historical identity, the past plays a particularly prominent role. It is often emphasized that in Polish narrative constructions of history—especially over the past 200 years—the dominant motif is suffering resulting from occupations, wars, and exploitation65,66. In contrast, fatalism is frequently identified as a common life orientation in Italy12. Ruiu67 explains fatalistic tendencies in Italy as arising from the interaction between cultural factors—particularly religious beliefs—and historical institutional experiences. According to Ruiu67, conditions conducive to certain forms of fatalism exist in Italy. The motif of fatalism, especially in southern Italy, is also prevalent in literature and cinema68.

These differences can also be understood through the lens of collective trauma and identity theories, which suggest that cultures that have undergone collective traumas—such as wars, humiliation, or defeat—develop transgenerational interpretive frameworks that shape perceptions of the world in terms of suffering, threat, and guilt. In difficult circumstances, these narratives are automatically reactivated, leading individuals to interpret their personal difficulties as part of a broader, inevitable story of failure69,70. Our findings suggest that during the perinatal period—considered one of the most challenging stages of life—the activation of culturally entrenched schemas focused on a negatively perceived past and the recurrence of past injuries and failures has particularly destructive consequences for women in Poland. In contrast, among women in Italy, the activation of culturally reinforced feelings of helplessness and lack of control over life appears to be especially maladaptive.

With regard to the associations between partners’ time perspectives and women’s postpartum stress, these relationships were stronger in the Polish sample than in the Italian sample, contrary to our expectations. This may suggest that Polish women, compared to Italian women, are more sensitive to their partners’ behaviors and emotions, particularly those related to a negative attitude toward the past. Likely, partners speaking pejoratively about past experiences, expressing regret or sadness related to the past, or having experienced past traumas, may have a particularly detrimental impact on the mental well-being of pregnant women in Poland71.

The findings of the present study have important practical implications, particularly in light of evidence indicating that time perspective is a psychological construct amenable to modification through targeted interventions12. Our results indicate that an intensified past-negative perspective—especially early in pregnancy—constitutes a risk factor for heightened postpartum stress in Poland. These findings underscore the need for therapeutic interventions targeting pregnant women in Poland that focus on strengthening a more positive appraisal of the past. In Italy, in turn, the results identify a fatalistic perspective as a predictor of elevated postpartum stress and highlight the need for psychological interventions aimed at enhancing a sense of control over time and personal agency. Furthermore, our findings draw attention to the importance of time perspective among expectant fathers during their partners’ pregnancies. These results support the inclusion of time perspective assessment in prenatal screening procedures to enable early identification of women at risk for heightened postpartum stress.

The present study also has certain limitations. The most important of these is the relatively small sample size. The small sample sizes in this study limit statistical power, thereby increasing the risk of Type II errors and reducing the likelihood of detecting small to moderate effects. Furthermore, regression parameter estimates are likely to be unstable and highly variable, which undermines both the reliability and generalizability of the results. The limited number of participants also heightens the risk of overfitting, as the models may reflect sample-specific noise rather than true underlying relationships. In addition, small samples constrain the inclusion of interaction terms, restricting the complexity of models that can be estimated with confidence. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings is limited, as results obtained from such small samples may not adequately represent the broader population. In addition, despite controlling for key participant characteristics, the influence of unmeasured confounding variables cannot be ruled out. Future research should seek to replicate these findings in larger and more diverse samples and to account for additional confounding factors, such as levels of social support during the perinatal period or exposure to significant stressors known to increase the risk of elevated postpartum stress symptoms. Among the limitations of our study, it should also be noted that we did not measure perinatal stress in fathers, which is likely a variable influencing the relationships we analyzed.

In summary, the present study suggests that a heightened couples’ past-negative time perspective during pregnancy is a risk factor for increased postpartum stress in Polish women. Conversely, in the Italian sample, a stronger fatalistic time perspective among women during pregnancy predicted higher postpartum stress. These results highlight notable cultural differences in time perspective and in the experiences of women during the perinatal period in Poland and Italy.
