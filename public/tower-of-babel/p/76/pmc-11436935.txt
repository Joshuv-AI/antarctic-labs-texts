Introduction

Detailed investigations on the randomness of the turbulent flows in the boundary layer got impetus with the pioneering works of Kline et al.,1, Corino and Brodkey2, and Wallace et al.,3. These studies revealed the existence of organized motions in the turbulent layer, called coherent structures, that produce, modify and dissipate the boundary layer turbulence4. A coherent structure is loosely defined as a three-dimensional region of the flow where at least one fundamental flow variable (temperature, density, or velocity) exhibits significant self-correlation or correlation with other fundamental variables over a range of space and time scales4,5. Studies show that these coherent structures play an important role in the turbulent transport of heat and momentum5. Most of the early studies on the coherent structures of turbulent flow are from laboratory experiments1,2; flow measurements above forest cover6,7; over low vegetations8–12, except for a few studies like Boppe and Neu13, Boppe et al.14 and Katsouvas et al.15 who studied the flow properties over the marine atmospheric boundary layer (MABL).

Understanding coherent structures help develop insight into the boundary layer turbulence. There are a number of statistical methods to understand the coherent structures within the turbulent flow, like the variable-interval time average (VITA) method16,17; the quadrant method3,18; the u-level method,19etc. All these methods describe the nature of "repetition" of turbulence with varying levels of sophistication. After a detailed evaluation of all the event detection algorithms, Bogard and Tiederman20 concluded that quadrant analysis is the most effective among other methods to understand the episodic nature of turbulence as this method has the highest probability of detecting coherent structures and a low probability of false detections. In the quadratic analysis method, the coherent structure characteristics are studied by mapping the kinematic Reynolds stress (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}{\prime}{w}{\prime}$$\end{document}u′w′) into four quadrants based on the sign of fluctuating velocity components (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}{\prime}$$\end{document}u′ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}{\prime}$$\end{document}w′). The quadrant method provides the statistical description of the turbulent flux time series in terms of events of different kinds, which has a wide range of advantages in many geophysical flow situations21,22. A detailed description of the quadrant analysis method is provided in “Quadrant Analysis”.

The "bottom-up" mechanism, in which active turbulence forms mainly at the surface of the boundary layer and grows up into the outer layer, is the widely accepted theoretical basis for developing coherent structures in the turbulent surface layer23,24. The turbulent boundary layer is often classified as stable, neutral, or unstable based on the dominant mechanisms that generate turbulence and stability. These variations in stability affect the energy transport across the boundary layer through the formation and development of coherent structures. The fact that the surface buoyancy flux affects the atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) turbulence under stable, unstable, or neutral situations that are typically not taken into account in lab experiments is a significant distinction between the coherent structures in the atmospheric boundary layers and lab studies25. One additional complication that MABL has to account for characterizing the coherent structures compared to the ABL over land is the pliant nature of the air-sea boundary layer through the action of winds and waves.

While many studies mentioned above have advanced our understanding of the characteristics of turbulent flow, several open questions remain about the turbulence structure and characteristics over the MABL, like how they are modulated by MABL stability, how they affect ocean–atmosphere momentum transfer, etc. Most studies on turbulent coherent structures rely on a short period of data (a few hours to a few days) to draw their conclusions. A more extended study period is, hence, required to fully understand any periodic or seasonal variation related to weather or environmental changes in turbulent production in MABL. The present work proceeds using quadrant analysis as a primary tool for inferring the episodic nature of turbulent events in the MABL of the northern Bay of Bengal (BoB) using a 16-month-long eddy covariance dataset that help to gain insight into the mechanisms behind the quasi-cyclic production of momentum flux. We confine our focus to mainly unstable to near-neutral conditions (z/L < 0).

The rest of the article is organized as follows. “Data and processing methods” describes the eddy covariance data and processing methods. The data analysis methods are described in detail in “Analysis methods” and main results are described in “Results”. We present a summary and detailed discussion in “Summary and discussions”.

Data and processing methods

This study uses data from a mooring-based Eddy covariance (EC) Flux System in the northern BoB (18°N, 89°E), deployed by the Indian National Centre for Ocean Information Services (INCOIS) as part of the Ocean Mixing and Monsson (OMM) program. Data were recorded at 20 Hz, in stretches of 20 min every hour for 16 months, from May 2019 to August 2020 (Fig. 1a). The data collection period includes two summer monsoons (June to September – JJAS), one winter monsoon (December to February—DJF), one pre-monsoon (March to May – MAM), and one post-monsoon (October to November – ON) period, covering different weather conditions. The three-dimensional wind vector (u, v, and w) and the sonic temperature were recorded with a Gill R3-50 sonic anemometer mounted at 3.47 m above the mean sea level. The EC system has a LI-COR Infrared Gas Analyzer, LI-7500RS, to measure gas concentrations and a 3-axis motion sensor package for measuring platform motion. Before analysis, the entire dataset was subjected to a series of corrections and selection procedures. As the measurements are made from a buoy, and the presence of waves modulates its motion, it is essential to remove the influence of platform motion on high-frequency wind measurements. All major and minor events are included in the sampled scatter defined by the quadrant analysis. Results

In this section, we employ the above-mentioned conditional sampling technique to understand the momentum flux characteristics in the MABL of the Bay of Bengal. One of the objectives of the study is to look into the seasonality of the coherent structures in line with the varying seasons of the Bay. For example, the monsoon winds reverse twice a year, south-westerly during summer (JJAS) and north-easterly during winter monsoon (DJF). There are two monsoon transition periods; March–May (MAM) and October–November (ON), where winds are relatively weaker. In “Seasonal analysis of flux events using quadrant and exuberance methods”, we explain the quadrant and exuberance analysis results. Characteristics of flux events and their seasonality are presented in “Event detection and characterization”.

Notwithstanding the seasonality of distribution, the contribution of each quadrant to the total stress is nearly uniform across seasons. The averaged momentum flux that we calculate over a period (hourly) results from a series of sweeps, ejections, and inward and outward interaction processes. These coherent structures constructively and destructively interact with each other and contribute to the total flux. Our analysis suggests that, generally, in all seasons, sweeps and ejections contribute to ~ 140% of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}{\prime}{w}{\prime}$$\end{document}u′w′ and the interaction quadrants provide a counter contribution of ~ 40% (Fig. 3). Further, ejections (Q2) are found to be the most efficient method contributing 80% of the total stress, compared to sweeps contributing ~ 60%. Figure 3 shows that the ws during sweep events are stronger than in the ejections, even though ejections contribute majorly to the total stress. Quadrant analysis does not provide an answer on this aspect, and it will be addressed in “Event detection and characterization”. The counter gradient interaction processes contribute equally, ~ -20%, to the total stress.

The cumulative contribution of sweeps and ejections increases sharply as the ws increases. Weak winds (ws < 3 m/s) are prevalent during unstable stratification (z/L < -0.2), indicating convective activity. Further when the local winds are weak (ws < 3 m/s), swells dominate the wave field compared to wind sea, as evidenced by the larger size bubbles. Hence, when ws is weak and swell dominates the wave field, there is a substantial fraction of upward momentum transfer from the sea to the air in all seasons. The average E for ws < 3 m/s is 0.83 though there are several instances in which upward momentum transfer happens over the 20 min intervals (E > 1). This finding has a significant consequence for the Monin–Obukhov Similarity Theory (MOST) based bulk flux estimation, where the transfer coefficient is generally estimated as a function of ws28–30. A detailed discussion on this point is provided in the last section. The takeaway point from analyzing Figs. 3 and 4 is that coherent structures can be scaled as a function of ws for winds greater than 3 m/s, while for lower winds a wave dependency of flux transfer should be considered.

Summary and discussions

Quantitative prediction of the evolution of turbulent flows is an important aspect of fluid turbulence, and there are ongoing efforts by research groups the world over. The pioneering works of Kline et al.,1 Corino and Brodkey2, and Wallice et al.,3 suggested certain types of coherent motions in the turbulent flow, that often repeats to produce the averaged kinematic stress in a fluid parcel. In several studies, the concept of coherent structures is used to statistically describe turbulence13,19,34. The present work builds and expands on these studies to understand the characteristics of turbulence over MABL. A 16-month-long, 20 Hz turbulent fluctuations of winds (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}{\prime}$$\end{document}u′ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}{\prime}$$\end{document}w′) over the Bay of Bengal is used to study the kinematic stress transfer mechanisms and its seasonality, making it one of the most extensive and continuous data sets reported in a scientific study to analyze turbulent coherent structures.

Based on the sign of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}{\prime}$$\end{document}u′ and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${w}{\prime}$$\end{document}w′, the coherent structures can be mapped into four quadrants19. Exuberance (E) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}{\prime}{w}{\prime}$$\end{document}u′w′, which represents the ratio of upward to downward stress transfer, shows a wave dependency on flux transfer for low winds (ws < 3m/s) and ws dependency for medium-range winds (ws > 3m/s).

Across all seasons, for ws < 3 m/s, fractional contributions of each of the events are quite higher, and often the combined contributions of outward and inward interactions exceed that of combined sweeps and ejections to the total flux, which is consistent with the exuberance analysis (Fig. 4). Our analysis suggests that E > 1 (E < 1) for ws < 3m/s during MAM and ON (DJF and JJAS) seasons, which characterize inter-monsoon (monsoon) periods. Weak mean winds prevail during the inter-monsoon periods, and swells dominate the wave fields. It is found that for weak winds (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ws$$\end{document}ws< 3 m/s), the number of turbulent events (N) is less, but they frequently repeat with more considerable flux per event (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{f})$$\end{document}f^), with outward and inward interactions (sweeps and ejections) dominating during intermonsoon periods (monsoon periods). Further, though the duration of sweep events is larger than ejections and with comparable flux energy per event (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\widehat{f}$$\end{document}f^), the larger number N of ejection events makes it the dominant stress transfer mechanism in the Bay in all seasons.

These results are in general agreement with several of the previous works on turbulence using quadrant analysis. For example, Corino and Brodkey2, in a study on turbulent flow in a pipe, found ~ 70% of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${u}{\prime}{w}{\prime}$$\end{document}u′w′ contribution from ejection events. Another laboratory study by Lu and Willmarth19 reported 77% and 55% contributions from ejections and sweeps respectively, on the total kinematic stress. Chambers and Antonia34 found that 80% of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u{\prime}w{\prime}$$\end{document}u′w′ are from ejection events in MABL. Boppe and Neu13 reported comparable results in MABL that ejection contributes 78%, and sweeps contribute 52%, of the total \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u{\prime}w{\prime}$$\end{document}u′w′ and interactions provide a counter contribution to the total flux. The present study shows that ejections contribute 80% and sweeps contribute 60% to total \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u{\prime}w{\prime}$$\end{document}u′w′ with a counter contribution of 20% each from interaction processes. All the above studies, irrespective of laboratory-based, over land or ocean, agree that ejections are dominant in transferring kinematic stress followed by sweeps. This indicates that there is a dynamical behavior to turbulence rather than statistical similarity, which should be universally valid.

In conclusion, we note that the turbulent characteristics of MABL in the Bay of Bengal follow the universal characteristics of turbulence as reported in other studies. In marine meteorology, the air-sea flux exchange is often estimated using bulk formulations in which the surface drag coefficient (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{d}$$\end{document}Cd) is generally estimated as a function of mean surface winds29. Figure 6a of Edson et al.,30 shows the scatter of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{d}$$\end{document}Cd as a function of mean ws, suggesting that for low ws, the scatter of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${C}_{d}$$\end{document}Cd is many orders higher than the mean fit. Our analysis also shows that for low ws and swell conditions, flux parameterizations should consider the wave state as well in addition to the local winds to account for the reverse contribution of kinematic stress from the ocean to atmosphere. Further research is needed in this direction.
