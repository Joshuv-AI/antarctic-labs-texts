Gut microbiota-driven metabolites modulate the development of stress-related mental disorders
Authors: Minlan Yuan, Fanglin Qin, Lingyu Wu, Nan Jiang, Jiayu Du, Biao Yang, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stress-related mental disorders, including depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder, represent a major global health burden, yet their underlying biological mechanisms and effective therapeutic strategies remain incompletely understood. Growing evidence from both preclinical and clinical studies indicates that alterations in gut microbiota composition and function are closely associated with the onset and progression of these disorders. A central role of the gut microbiota is the biotransformation of dietary and host-derived substrates into diverse metabolites that enter systemic circulation and influence host physiology. In this review, we highlight gut microbiota–driven metabolites. short-chain fatty acids, amino acid–related metabolites, bile acids, and monoamine-related metabolites, as key mediators of gut–brain communication that influence neural, immune, epigenetic, and endocrine processes involved in stress-related mental disorders. We summarize emerging microbial and metabolic signatures identified in animal models and human studies. Furthermore, we discuss microbiome-targeted strategies for the prevention and treatment. However, the complexity of the gut microbiota and pronounced inter-individual variability limit causal inference from current clinical studies. Consequently, translating these findings into clinical practice will require standardized study designs, longitudinal clinical investigations, and the integration of multi-omics approaches to advance precision microbiome-based interventions for psychiatric disorders.

Introduction

Psychological stress is a major risk factor for a broad spectrum of psychiatric disorders, most notably major depressive disorder (MDD), anxiety disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) [1–3]. MDD typically manifests as persistent low mood, loss of motivation, and anhedonia, whereas anxiety disorder is defined by excessive, uncontrollable worry and restlessness [2, 4]. PTSD develops following exposure to traumatic events and is characterised by intrusive re-experiencing (flashbacks), nightmares, and hypervigilance [4, 5]. According to the Global Burden of Disease report, MDD and anxiety disorder are the most common mental disorders, and rank among the top three causes of years lived with disability worldwide [6, 7]. The overall PTSD prevalence was 23.95% according to a recent umbrella review [8]. These stress-related conditions collectively impose a significant burden on occupational, social, and daily functioning. However, stress-related mental disorders exhibit substantial clinical heterogeneity, and current diagnosis and treatment largely rely on empirical approaches, resulting in relatively limited diagnostic accuracy and treatment efficacy [9–11]. Therefore, the identification of early objective biomarkers, biological changes reflecting symptom severity, and potential intervention windows is crucial for improving diagnostic precision and guiding targeted interventions.

The etiology of stress-related mental disorders involves complex interactions among genetic, environmental, and biological factors [12]. A series of molecular and physiological changes may serve as early biomarkers of these disorders [2, 13–16]. Classical mechanisms include disturbances in neurotransmitter systems such as 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT), γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and dopamine [17, 18]. In addition, individuals with these disorders often exhibit hyperresponsiveness of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and dysregulation of autonomic nervous system function [15, 19]. Epigenetic alterations, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and RNA modifications, have also been proposed as potential early diagnostic markers [13, 20, 21]. Moreover, elevated circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin‑1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF-α), are associated with stress exposure and the development of anxiety and depressive symptoms [16, 22]. Changes in circulating C-reactive protein levels may also be related to the therapeutic response to antidepressant treatment [14, 23].

Recent studies suggest that stress-related mental disorders often present as complex psychosomatic comorbidities, with gastrointestinal symptoms frequently reported. The microbiota–gut–brain (MGB) axis has been increasingly recognized as playing a critical role in disorders such as MDD, anxiety, and PTSD [24–26]. Gut microbiota and their metabolites may influence emotional and cognitive functions through the MGB axis by modulating the HPA axis, the autonomic nervous system, neurotransmitter systems, epigenetic regulators, and immune-inflammatory pathways [24, 25, 27–29]. Notably, the HPA axis and the autonomic nervous system interact bidirectionally with the MGB axis [30]. Top-down signals originating from the HPA axis and the sympathetic nervous system can influence gut microbial composition through glucocorticoids and enterochromaffin cell signaling [30, 31]. Conversely, bottom-up signals from the gut to the central nervous system also play an important role in regulating emotion and cognition. For example, intestinal nutrient signals can be transmitted to the brainstem within milliseconds via vagal pathways through specialized neuropod cells, revealing a potential rapid neural pathway involved in reward-related responses [32].

Alterations in gut-derived signals are largely driven by changes in the gut microbiota and their metabolites. Although both metabolism and the gut microbiota independently contribute to mental health, the mechanisms by which they interact to influence brain function remain incompletely understood [33]. Shifts in the gut microbial community can lead to alterations in microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), tryptophan metabolites, bile acids (BAs), and proline-related metabolites, which in turn influence intestinal permeability, neurotransmitter release, epigenetic regulation, and immune responses [13, 24, 25, 27–30]. Microbial metabolites can also modulate the activity of intestinal enteroendocrine cells and regulate the release of 5-HT, thereby influencing central neurotransmitter systems through tryptophan metabolism [34] (Fig. 1). Furthermore, the immune signaling could be a key mediator in the microbiota–brain interaction. Alterations in the gut microbiota and their associated metabolites can exacerbate both peripheral and central inflammatory responses [22, 29, 35] (Fig. 1). Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota may increase intestinal permeability, and the occurrence of “leaky gut” facilitates the translocation and release of harmful substances such as endotoxins and lipopolysaccharide [36, 37]. These microbial-derived signals can subsequently propagate along ascending pathways to the brain and influence the activation of the central NLRP3 inflammasome, which has been implicated in several neuropsychiatric disorders, including MDD [38]. In addition, metabolites produced by specific microbial taxa may influence adenosine production and regulate epigenetic modifications (Fig. 1). For instance, these metabolites can promote neuroinflammation and neuronal death by modulating H3K9ac histone acetylation to upregulate the transcription of EZH2, ultimately contributing to depression-like behaviors [39]. Although the aforementioned studies indicate that gut-derived immune activation is a necessary step for microbial changes to induce emotional and cognitive dysfunction, some studies suggest that microbial metabolites or gut-derived signaling influence brain function independently of immune activation. For example, bacteria can stimulate the enteric nervous system and the vagus nerve directly via neurotransmitters (like serotonin) without requiring an intermediate immune response [40]. Chronic stress has been shown to increase the abundance of inflammatory monocytes from the splen, which can in turn promote anxiety-like behaviors in response to subthreshold stimulation [41].Therefore, when discussing the relationship between the immune system, the microbiome, and stress-related mental disorder, we need to assess the direct and indirect roles of immune signals in this context more comprehensively.

Previous studies have reported that the gut microbiota can influence mental health through the MGB axis, which is closely associated with microbial regulation of host metabolite levels [42, 43]. Moreover, supplementation with dietary components or other microbiota-targeted interventions may represent a promising and effective strategy for the treatment of stress-related disorders [26]. However, systematic syntheses addressing which specific microbial taxa regulate particular metabolites, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms through which these metabolites exert their effects, and the clinical efficacy of microbiome-targeted interventions remain limited.

In this review, we provide a comprehensive overview of microbiota-derived metabolites—including SCFAs, tryptophan-related metabolites, proline metabolites, BAs, and monoamine-related metabolites—as well as the microbial taxa associated with their production. We further discuss how these metabolites influence neural, immune, and endocrine pathways implicated in stress-related mental disorders. Moreover, we summarize current microbiome-targeted clinical strategies, including dietary interventions, traditional Chinese medicine approaches, probiotics/prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation, with particular emphasis on their translational potential and therapeutic efficacy.

Short-chain fatty acids derived from microbial fermentation show heterogeneous effects on mental health

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)—primarily acetate, propionate, and butyrate—are the major fermentation products of dietary fiber by gut microbiota [44, 45]. The correlation analysis between SCFAs levels and relative bacterial abundance in fecal samples found that butyric acid was negatively correlated with Bacteroidetes and positively correlated with Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes (Roseburia) at the phylum level [46, 47]. Acetate, the most abundant SCFA and a major constituent of vinegar [48], modulates systemic metabolism via the acetyl-CoA pathway [49]. Fecal acetic acid levels in mice were positively correlated with class Alphaproteobacteria (Proteobacteria), family Streptococcaceae (Firmicutes), family Staphylococcaceae (Firmicutes), genus Staphylococcus (Firmicutes), and genus Akkermansia (Verrucomicrobiota) [50]. Gut bacteria may convert pyruvate to acetate by either the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway or acetyl-CoA [51], with Akkermansia muciniphila involved in the acetyl-CoA pathway and Streptococcus spp. involved in the Wood Ljungdahl pathway [52, 53]. Propionate levels were correlated with the Firmicutes (Roseburia), Bacteroidetes (Bacteroidaceae), Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Spirochaetota [47, 50]. Propionate synthesis occurs mainly via the succinate pathway in Bacteroidetes and certain Firmicutes, where succinate serves as a key precursor [54, 55]. Bacteroides spp. utilize the succinate pathway, while Ruminococcus obeum contributes through the propanediol pathway [52, 56]. The majority of colonic butyrate-producing bacteria are gram-positive anaerobes within the phylum Firmicutes, producing butyrate through acetyl-CoA, glutamate, lysine, or succinate pathways [57–59]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota and the resulting changes in metabolites are associated with stress-related mental disorders. SCFAs derived from the gut microbiota appear to be inversely correlated with mental disorders. Studies have shown that the plasma butyric acid levels of MDD patients [47] and the levels of butyric acid in feces, serum and brain of depressive mice are reduced respectively [46]. In tumour-bearing animals, negative affective behaviours are linked to reduced Akkermansia muciniphila(A. muciniphila) and lower butyrate levels [60]. Additionally, A. muciniphila or butyrate supplementation improves both prognosis and mood-related behaviours [60]. Reduced fecal and serum propionate levels, along with diminished plasma butyrate, have been reported in MDD patients and depressive animal models [47, 50]. Isoalantolactone improves depression-like behaviour in mice following chronic social defeat stress, which is associated with the restoration of butyrate levels in the gut. In a rat model of type 1 diabetes, depletion of acetate-producing bacteria accelerated cognitive decline, which was reversed by acetate supplementation [61]. In contrast, chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) in adolescent rats produced elevated serum and brain acetate levels alongside reduced Firmicutes and enriched Bacteroides, changes not normalized by non-absorbable antibiotic rifaximin [62]. Human metabolomic and clinical studies further suggest that vinegar ingestion, particularly apple-cider vinegar, may improve mood and reduce depressive symptoms [63]. Clinical studies suggest that daily apple cider vinegar ingestion lowers depression scores in healthy college students.

Accumulating pre-clinical evidence suggests that the beneficial effects of SCFAs on mental disorders may be partly attributed to their protective roles in the gut. SCFAs play a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the intestinal barrier. Through the Ca²⁺-CaMKKβ- AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, butyrate leads to reduced phosphorylation of myosin II regulatory light chain and increased phosphorylation of Protein kinase C beta II (PKCβ2), which coordinately limit cytoskeletal contraction and promote tight junction reassembly [64]. Acting as agonists at G-protein-coupled receptors (e.g., GPR41, GPR43, GPR109A) and modulators of AMPK, SCFAs promote nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (NRF2) dependent cytoprotective signaling [65–68]. Propionate enhances cell speed and persistence to promote intestinal epithelial turnover and repair through GPR43- Signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway [69] (Fig. 2b).

In addition, they can modulate mucosal immunity by suppressing pro-inflammatory signaling pathways and promoting the transcription of anti-inflammatory genes [64, 70, 71] (Fig. 2b). Butyrate regulates regulatory T cell differentiation through histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition–mediated epigenetic reprogramming of the forkhead box P3 (Foxp3) locus, thereby promoting immune tolerance and mucosal homeostasis [70, 71]. Propionate inhibits nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) activation, thereby reducing chronic low-grade inflammation [72]. Propionate and butyrate function as natural HDAC inhibitors, increasing histone acetylation and enhancing transcription of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory genes such as NRF2 and Zinc finger protein 36 (ZFP36) [60, 73, 74]. For instance, butyrate activates the GPR109A–AMPK axis to strengthen the NRF2-mediated antioxidant response [73].

Pre-clinical studies further indicate that the gut microbiota can influence the activity of central glial cells and neurons through SCFAs-mediated signaling. SCFAs cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), where they enhance barrier integrity, regulate neurotrophic factors and neurotransmitter synthesis, and alleviate glial dysfunction [75, 76]. SCFAs further influence astrocyte morphology and attenuate neuroinflammation [77, 78]. By inhibiting HDAC activity, butyrate enhances neuroplasticity-related gene expression and mitigates depressive-like behaviors [79–81]. Thus, butyrate-producing bacteria may play an important role in the management of stress-related mental disorders like depression. These mechanisms collectively demonstrate that SCFAs produced by microorganisms promote mental health by enhancing blood-brain barrier integrity, suppressing inflammatory responses, and supporting neurotrophic signalling pathways. On the other hand, emerging evidence also suggests that SCFAs may exert certain detrimental effects. A clinical study reported that SCFAs levels in patients with MDD were positively correlated with scores on the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale [82].In a preclinical CUMS model, low-dose propionate exerted antidepressant-like effects by modulating GABA reuptake, whereas high-dose propionate promoted depressive-like behaviours [83]. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates that, in the presence of Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists, butyrate and propionate can inhibit HDAC activity, suppress the transcription of FLICE-like inhibitory protein and IL-10, thereby inducing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and promoting pro-inflammatory responses [78] (Fig. 2b). SCFAs may exert beneficial or detrimental effects depending on their concentration, immune milieu, and disease state, underscoring the need for caution when interpreting their role as therapeutic targets in mood disorders.

In summary, converging evidence from preclinical and clinical studies indicates that SCFAs concentrations are generally positively associated with the relative abundance of Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, Actinobacteria, and Spirochaetota, and negatively associated with Bacteroidetes. However, some investigations have reported no consistent associations between SCFAs levels and specific dominant bacterial taxa [82]. Functionally, elevated SCFAs availability appears to ameliorate depressive symptoms and enhance stress resilience through anti-inflammatory, epigenetic, and neurotrophic mechanisms, although a few studies have shown their detrimental aspects. These metabolites thus act as integral components of MGB communication that maintain neural homeostasis. From a translational perspective, probiotics are modifiable interventions that may influence the gut–brain axis and affective states [84]. Targeting SCFA-producing microbiota can be framed within the emerging concept of psychobiotics, acting as adjunctive strategies through immune, inflammatory, and stress-related pathways.

Amino acid metabolites from gut microbiota-associated enzymatic metabolism are involved in neural mechanisms of stress-related disorders

Tryptophan metabolism

Tryptophan (Trp), an essential amino acid obtained exclusively from dietary sources, serves as a biochemical precursor for several neuroactive compounds [85]. The three primary pathways of Trp metabolism are kynurenine (Kyn) metabolism, indole metabolism, and 5-HT metabolism [86]. The metabolites generated along these pathways play a significant role as neurobiological mediators in a variety of psychiatric illnesses [87–89]. Pre-clinical studies have consistently shown gut microbiota dysbiosis across multiple stress-based animal models, closely linked to alterations in tryptophan metabolism [90–93] (see Table1). Correlation analyses of microbial metabolites identified several bacterial taxa—including Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria—that are strongly associated with tryptophan-derived metabolites such as 5-HT and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) [90, 91, 93, 94]. Experimental supplementation with tryptophan has been shown to alleviate anxiety- and depressive-like behaviours in stressed mice [90].

Trp is metabolised through the kyn pathway by multiple enzymes to generate quinolinic acid (QUIN) and kynurenic acid (KYNA) [95–97] (Fig. 3b). These metabolites regulate intestinal function and are influenced by the gut microbiota, while also affecting mood and cognition through the gut–brain axis. Gut microbiota can enhance the activity of indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), thereby increasing the production of kynurenine pathway intermediates [95–97]. Preclinical studies have shown that Kyn levels are associated with major bacterial phyla, including Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Actinobacteria, and Proteobacteria [91, 92, 94]. KYNA concentrations increase along the gastrointestinal tract, where it exerts immunomodulatory and mucosal protective effects [95–97]. Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria are negatively correlated with QUIN levels [93, 98]. The effects of QUIN appear to be concentration dependent. During its synthesis, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺) is produced, which can reduce inflammation and enhance host defence [99]. However, excessive QUIN may induce neuronal injury by overactivating N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs) and the HPA axis [100]. For the indole pathway, Lactobacilli and Escherichia coli both contain tryptophanase, an enzyme that changes Trp into indole [101]. Defects in the intestinal G-protein-coupled receptor 35 lead to Parabacteroides distasonis enrichment, which in turn increases levels of indole-3-lactic acid (ILA) and decreases levels of indole-3-carbaldehyde (IAld). These two compounds have opposing effects in neuroplasticity of the nucleus accumbens [102]. The gut microbiota of honeybees mainly affects tryptophan metabolism by converting Trp into indole derivatives including ILA, indole-3-pyruvic acid, and serotonin, while inhibiting the host’s Kyn pathway [103]. The levels of ILA in the gut and hippocampus of depressed mice were significantly reduced, while supplementation with psychobiotic Bifidobacterium breve restored normal ILA levels [104]. Moreover, clinical evidence indicates that treatment response to escitalopram in patients with MDD is associated with baseline gut microbial composition and levels of the microbial metabolite indole-3-propionic acid (I3PA) [105] (Fig. 3b).

Another important product of Trp metabolism, 5-HT, has been consistently implicated in the pathophysiology of depression [106]. Clostridium sporogenes in the tryptophan metabolism allows for the decarboxylation of Trp, which in effect produces the neurotransmitter 5-HT [107]. The gut synthesizes nearly all systemic 5-HT, which serves as a critical gastrointestinal signaling molecule influencing intestinal motility and secretion [108]. A review reported that individuals with depression exhibit markedly lower 5-HT levels compared with healthy controls [109]. Liver TDO regulates plasma Trp availability and thereby modulates serotonergic function, potentially contributing to MDD pathophysiology [110].

Microbiota–tryptophan interactions are complex, and stress-related disorders likely involve coordinated alterations across multiple metabolic pathways rather than a single metabolite. Rifaximin ameliorates depression-like behaviour in CUMS rats by regulating intestinal microbiota and hippocampal Kyn and 5-HT metabolism [91]. Tryptophan supplementation improves behavioural outcomes in CUMS mice by shifting metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway while suppressing serotonin and indole pathways [92].

These studies show changes in kynurenine metabolites may reflect immune activation, neuroinflammatory status, and microbiota–host metabolic interactions, and have been associated with symptom severity and antidepressant treatment response. Interventions targeting gut microbiota or tryptophan metabolism may therefore influence kynurenine pathway activity and represent potential adjunctive therapeutic strategies. However, longitudinal human studies linking microbiota-driven kynurenine alterations to clinical symptom trajectories remain limited, highlighting the need for prospective investigations to establish causal and predictive relationships.

Proline metabolism

Proline is a multifunctional amino acid that plays essential roles in extracellular matrix integrity, cellular metabolism, immune regulation, and neurotransmitter balance [111]. Recent evidence has identified proline metabolism as an important microbiota-dependent pathway linked to stress-related mental disorders [112–114].

Proline homeostasis is regulated by both microbiota-dependent and host-derived mechanisms. Gut microbial composition influences circulating proline levels through modulation of amino acid metabolic pathways [112]. Glutamate is converted to Δ¹-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C) by Aldehyde Dehydrogenase 18 Family Member A1(ALDH18A1), and then Pyrroline-5-Carboxylate Reductase 1(PYCR1) convert P5C to L-proline. Patients with depression have decreased levels of circulating glutamate, the main excitatory neurotransmitter, and increased levels of proline [115] (Fig. 3d). Compared to healthy control rats, elevated levels of PYCR1 and ALDH18A1 were detected in both peripheral and central tissues of depressed rats [116]. Abnormal proline metabolism is observed in depression-like animal models and is associated with Actinobacteria. [117, 118]. Research on depression rat models indicated that Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes show correlations with 2 metabolites in amino acid metabolism: L-Glutamate and N-Acetyl-L-glutamic acid [119, 120] (Fig. 3d). These studies indicate that gut microbiota can regulate the metabolic conversion of glutamate to proline, and stress-related pathogenesis is associated with microbial dysbiosis and excessive L-proline accumulation.

Host-derived proline originates from dietary intake, intestinal metabolism, and extracellular matrix collagen turnover, which represents a major reservoir of proline (Fig. 3d). Clinical and preclinical evidence shows that dietary proline intake in both humans and mice is positively associated with depressive phenotypes and correlates with alterations in gut microbial composition, including Parabacteroides, Prevotella, and Actinobacteria (including Bifidobacterium) [112]. Proline is primarily stored in the extracellular matrix (ECM) in the form of collagen. Chronic stress activates matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), leading to collagen degradation and the release of proline [118, 121]. Excessive proline release not only reflects structural disruption of the ECM but also promotes reactive oxygen species production [121, 122], disrupts the glutamate/GABA balance, and modulates NMDAR activity, thereby influencing learning, memory, emotional regulation, and neurotoxicity [111, 114]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) studies further demonstrate that alterations in gut microbiota can modulate central proline levels and MMP activity, which are associated with depression-like behaviors [112]. The microbiota–proline–extracellular matrix axis reveals a novel mechanistic pathway linking peripheral metabolic alterations with central nervous system dysfunction, providing important implications for the prevention and treatment of stress-related mental disorders through gut microbiota–targeted interventions. Bile acids converted by gut microbiota generate different effects in the brain

The liver converts cholesterol into bile acids (BAs), which are hydroxylated sterols [123]. The BAs are often categorized into three categories: primary, secondary, and tertiary. In the liver, primary BAs are synthesized from cholesterol and subsequently secreted into the intestine in small quantities, where Clostridium and Eubacterium species transform them into secondary BAs, including lithocholic acid (LCA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA) [124–129]. The relationship between 23-nordeoxycholic acid (NorDCA) and Deinococcus-Thermus was positive [130]. Tiny amounts of primary BAs go into the intestines, where Clostridium and Eubacterium convert them into secondary BAs: lithocholic acid (LCA) and deoxycholic acid (DCA) [124–129]. Actinobacteria and secondary BAs are positively correlated [130]. Due to reabsorption in the intestine, further biotransformation into tertiary Bas, ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), occurs in the liver. BAs are hydrolyzed by bile salt hydrolases (Lactobacillus, Bacteroidetes, and Bifidobacterium) [135–138]. High levels of BAs raise BBB permeability. BA ligands penetrating the BBB can attach to FXR or TGR5 to influence depression [142, 143]. In the prefrontal cortex (PFC), FXR activation suppresses the activity of NLRP3 and caspase-1 and alleviates depression-like behaviors [149]. In hippocampal (HIP), FXR activation suppresses cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB)/Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) and promotes NF-κB/NLRP3, which is associated with depression-like behaviors [149]. TGR5 exerts antidepressant effects through suppresses the activity of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) [145] in HIP and promoting GABA/CaMKII in Lateral Hypothalamic Area (LHA) [144]. Dysregulation of BAs caused by gut microbiota imbalance may be one of the initiating factors in stress-related mental disorders [131, 132]. Both clinical and preclinical studies have shown that decreased serum levels of primary BAs and increased levels of secondary BAs are associated with anxiety- and depression-like behaviors [133, 134]. It was found that Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Verrucomicrobiota in the murine model of depression were negatively associated with cholic acid and some other metabolites involved in BAs metabolism [135–138]. In clinical studies, the divergent gut microbiota and BAs in MDD patients were analyzed, and the findings revealed a negative connection between primary BAs and Bacteroidetes, and a positive correlation between Actinobacteria and secondary BAs [130]. In one recent study, Actinobacteria enrichment, downregulation of BAs, and upregulation of glyceric acid/glutaconic acid specifically marked a subgroup of MDD patients comorbid with irritable bowel syndrome [139]. These reveal a potential relationship between BAs dysregulation and stress-related mental disorders(Fig. 4).

BAs are related to stress-related mental disorders through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of inflammation, oxidative stress, and BBB permeability [140, 141]. All three types of BAs can cross the BBB and exert their effects by activating receptors such as takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5(TGR5) and farnesoid X receptor (FXR) [142, 143]. However, activation of these receptors may produce region-specific effects in different brain areas (Fig. 4).

Preclinical studies have shown that activation of TGR5 exerts antidepressant effects, with secondary BAs acting as the most potent agonists [143]. The TGR5 pathway alleviates synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and neuronal apoptosis, thereby improving depression-like behaviors, through the GABA → dorsal hippocampal CA3 (dCA3) → CaMKII → dorsolateral striatum GABA long-range projection circuit originating from the lateral hypothalamic area [144]. Another TGR5-mediated pathway involving CA3 pyramidal neurons projecting to somatostatin-expressing GABAergic neurons also produces antidepressant effects [141]. In addition, TGR5 signaling activated by tertiary BAs can inhibit NF-κB signaling in the hippocampus and rescue impairments in synaptic plasticity [145].

The effects of BAs mediated through FXR activation may be region-specific. In peripheral organs, FXR activation primarily regulates bile acid synthesis and maintains metabolic homeostasis [146]. In the central nervous system, FXR activation influences neuroinflammation and hippocampal synaptic plasticity. Chronic stress induces overexpression of FXR2 in the hippocampal CA1 region, which interferes with the nuclear translocation of CRTC2, suppresses CREB activity, reduces BDNF expression, and is associated with depression-like behaviors [147]. Upregulation of FXR expression in the prefrontal cortex suppresses the activity of NLRP3 and caspase-1, reduces the production of inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-17, and alleviates depression-like behaviors in chronically stressed mice [148]. However, other studies have reported activation of the FXR/NF-κB/NLRP3 signaling pathway in the hippocampus of chronically stressed animals, which is associated with depression-like behaviors [149]. These region-dependent differences suggest that FXR may play distinct roles in regulating cognitive and emotional functions.

The effects of BAs are also concentration dependent, and excessively high bile acid levels may exert neurotoxic effects [150, 151]. Elevated circulating BAs can disrupt BBB integrity, promote neuroinflammation, and contribute to depressive phenotypes [142, 152, 153]. Their hydrophobicity and chemical reactivity can trigger lipid peroxidation and reduce BDNF expression, thereby impairing neuroplasticity and neuronal survival [143]. Increased concentrations of cholic acid or deoxycholic acid can exacerbate neurofunctional decline associated with inflammatory conditions [150]. Elevated DCA levels have been linked to cognitive impairment in patients with chronic kidney disease [154].

Gut microbiota-related monoamine metabolism is involved in the dysfunction of the reward circuit

Numerous neuropsychiatric disorders are thought to arise from dysfunction within the brain’s monoaminergic circuits, which are essential for regulating emotion, motivation, and adaptive behavior [155]. The monoamine deficiency hypothesis posits that decreased levels of 5-HT, norepinephrine (NE), and dopamine (DA) underlie depressive symptoms [156].

Clinical and preclinical studies suggest that the impact of gut microbiota dysbiosis on monoaminergic systems may represent a key pathogenic mechanism underlying mood disorders [157–159]. Postmortem analyses have revealed differences in dopamine and catecholamine levels between patients with depression and healthy controls. Emerging evidence indicates that gut microbiota composition influences monoaminergic neurotransmission, including both dopaminergic and noradrenergic pathways [160]. It has been observed that Streptococcus species, Escherichia species, and Bacillus species are associated with dopamine production [161]. Intestinal microbiota, Bacillus species and Escherichia species, produce norepinephrine [161]. A clinical study suggests that salivary 3-Methoxy-4-hydroxy-phenylglycol, (MHPG) may predict the later development of PTSD symptoms [162]. Clinical studies have shown that Coprococcus is reduced in the gut of depressed individuals and positively correlated with the potential for 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC) synthesis [163]. Roseburia Intestinalis has the ability to enhance Bifidobacterium longum abundance and encourage homovanillic acid (HVA) generation [164]. The reward pathways rely on the DA system to a great extent [167]. The lateral habenula (LHb) exerts inhibitory control over dopaminergic signaling via GABAergic projections from the rostromedial tegmental nucleus (RMTg) and direct modulation of DA neurons projecting to the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) [168]. Hyperactivity of LHb neurons attenuates dopamine-mediated reward signaling, thereby contributing to anhedonia and depressive phenotypes [168]. Dopamine acts through GPCRs and dynamically interacts with other neurotransmitter systems to maintain synaptic and network homeostasis [169]. NE is synthesized from dopamine via dopamine β-hydroxylase [170]. The limbic system, which governs emotional regulation, is richly innervated by NE projections, and postmortem analyses have revealed alterations in the NE system between depressed and healthy individuals [171]. The primary NE metabolite, MHPG, readily crosses the BBB and participates in both central and peripheral sympathetic neurotransmission [172, 173] (Fig. 5).

Evidence from fecal microbiota transplantation and monoaminergic intervention studies indicates that gut microbiota-induced disturbances in dopamine and catecholamine signaling may act as critical initiating factors in mood disorders. Transplantation of gut microbiota from patients with bipolar depression into healthy mice induces depression-like behaviors, which are associated with abnormalities in central dopamine transmission and synaptic function [174]. In fecal microbiota transplantation experiments using congenitally depressive Fawn-hooded rats, microbiota from depressive donors exacerbated depression-like behaviors in recipient rats, whereas microbiota from healthy donors alleviated these behaviors [175]. These changes were associated with alterations in hippocampal serotonin and norepinephrine levels [175]. In individuals with depression, alterations in gut microbiota have been associated with reduced levels of the key dopamine metabolite HVA [164]. Animal studies have shown that HVA administration alleviates depression-like behaviors in chronically stressed mice, an effect associated with inhibition of Sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1)/p62 and reduced degradation of microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3) [164]. However, chronic stress can also reshape gut microbiota composition and function via neural and endocrine pathways, thereby secondarily influencing catecholamine levels. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are required to disentangle these relationships.

Clinical application and translation

Given that the gut microbiome is highly diverse, dynamic, and influenced by factors such as environment, diet, and lifestyle, and considering the mediating role of the gut–brain axis in mental health, modulation of gut microbiota has emerged as a promising therapeutic avenue for stress-related mental disorders [176]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of targeting gut microbiota through dietary interventions, physical exercise, supplementation with probiotics/prebiotics, and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in alleviating symptoms of stress-related mental diseases [177–179].

Dietary interventions and exercise training have demonstrated significant clinical benefits in improving stress-related affective disorders. Recent studies suggest that these beneficial effects may be mediated through the regulation of the gut microbiota and their metabolites [180, 181]. Flavonoid-based therapies have been associated with improvements in depressive symptoms among young adults, likely mediated by increased microbial richness [182]. Similarly, in adolescents with subthreshold depression, aerobic group exercise reduced depressive symptoms and favorably reshaped gut flora composition [183]. The antidepressant effects of traditional Chinese medicine formulations have similarly been linked to metabolic reprogramming involving trypsin, taurine, lipid, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate, fatty acid, valine–leucine–isoleucine, and arginine–proline pathways [184–186].

Several clinical studies have shown that probiotic interventions can improve symptoms of depression and anxiety, and these effects are associated with the modulation of metabolites [187–189]. Evidence from multiple studies indicates that Lactobacillus plantarum JYLP-326, Bifidobacterium breve CCFM1025, Lactobacillus plantarum PS128, Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum, as well as multi-strain probiotic formulations, can alleviate anxiety and depressive symptoms in patients with MDD [187, 188, 190–192]. Besides, these improvements are associated with alterations in gut microbiota composition, changes in serotonin metabolites, and reductions in inflammatory responses (Table1). Probiotics combined with other interventions may produce stronger regulatory effects on the gut microbiota. A probiotic formulation containing Lactobacillus helveticus and Bifidobacterium longum reduced BDI scores more effectively than the prebiotic galactooligosaccharide in patients with MDD, and this improvement was associated with alterations in the kynurenine/tryptophan and tryptophan/isoleucine ratios [192]. Combining probiotics with gluten-free diets or vitamin B7 supplementation modifies the gut microbial profile and reduces intestinal permeability, potentially alleviating depression via anti-inflammatory mechanisms [193, 194]. Probiotics may enhance the efficacy of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and reduce circulating kynurenine levels, supporting a synergistic mechanism between microbial and pharmacological modulation [195]. Nevertheless, two small-sample clinical trials reported no significant changes in microbiota composition following probiotic treatment, highlighting variability across studies. [191, 196]. In addition, differences among studies in probiotic strain specificity, quantification of outcome measures, and duration of intervention have led to inconsistent findings regarding metabolite changes and inflammatory responses. These methodological variations represent important limitations of the current clinical evidence and highlight the need for larger-scale, standardized, and well-controlled trials.

Compared with single-strain or multi-strain probiotic interventions, FMT due to its greater microbial diversity, has emerged as a promising strategy for modulating the MGB axis. A randomized controlled clinical trial has shown that FMT administered via enema in patients with MDD is safe and acceptable [197]. In female patients with anorexia nervosa accompanied by anxiety and depression, FMT resulted in changes in the gut microbial gene profile and fecal characteristics [198]. FMT has been shown to alleviate depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with functional constipation [199] and irritable bowel syndrome [200]. These effects are accompanied by reduced serum 5-HT concentrations in functional constipation and SCFAs levels in both fecal and serum samples of IBS patients [199, 200]. A clinical meta-analysis has shown that FMT can significantly improve depressive symptoms in patients with MDD, particularly in those with comorbid IBS [201]. Summary and future prospects

To date, extensive preclinical evidence indicates that gut microbial metabolites regulate brain function through immunological, neuronal, epigenetic, and endocrinological pathways at both peripheral and central levels. However, direct clinical validation of these mechanisms in stress-related mental disorders remains limited. Recent findings highlight that nutritional status, host metabolism, and disease conditions profoundly shape the overall impact of microbial metabolites on health and pathology [202–204]. To comprehensively elucidate the multilayered relationship among diet, gut microbiota, the circulating metabolome, and brain function, it is imperative to integrate metagenomic, metabolomic, and metatranscriptomic approaches within well-characterized human cohorts that include detailed nutritional, behavioral, and clinical profiles. Such multidimensional data integration will enable a systems-level understanding of gut–brain communication, thereby laying the groundwork for personalized, microbiota-based interventions in the prevention and treatment of neuropsychiatric disorders.

In the field of stress-related mental disorders—including depression, anxiety, and PTSD — numerous studies have reported an association between gut dysbiosis and disease status, although specific microbial alterations remain inconsistent across reports [205, 206]. Because gut microbiota composition is highly sensitive to external factors such as diet, medication, and lifestyle, future investigations must rigorously control for, or statistically adjust, these confounders. Furthermore, large-scale prospective and longitudinal studies are needed to: (1) Identify characteristic microbial and metabolic changes that vary in parallel with symptom dynamics; (2) Determine pathogenic and beneficial bacterial taxa, clarifying their causal relationships with disease; and (3) Elucidate gut–brain mechanisms, particularly how microbial metabolites influence neural, immune, epigenetic, and endocrine processes. Such efforts will ultimately facilitate the creation of a comprehensive database of disease-associated microbial strains and metabolites, accelerating the development of microbiota-targeted therapeutics, including probiotics, prebiotics, metabolite analogs, and FMT.

Overall, gut microbial metabolites (SCFAs, amino acids, BAs, and catecholamine) have emerged as key molecular mediators of the gut–brain dialogue and hold significant promise as biomarkers and therapeutic targets for stress-related mental disorders. However, the translation of these preclinical discoveries into human clinical applications will require sustained multidisciplinary efforts that integrate omics-based technologies, computational modeling, and precision medicine frameworks to fully realize the therapeutic potential of the microbiota–metabolite–brain axis.
