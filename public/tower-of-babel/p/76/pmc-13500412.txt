WISP2 Overexpression Is Associated with Reduced Vasculogenic Mimicry and Epithelial–Mesenchymal Transition in Gastric Cancer
Authors: Xinning Xu, Yecheng Chen, Longtao Zhang, Pengyi Pan, Ting Duan, Zenong Cheng, Guoyu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association between WISP2 overexpression, vasculogenic mimicry (VM), and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in gastric cancer.MethodsPathological specimens from 50 patients who underwent radical gastrectomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College between February 2022 and February 2023 were analyzed. WISP2 expression and VM formation in tumor and adjacent tissues were evaluated using GEPIA database analysis and immunohistochemistry. Gastric cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were assessed using CCK-8, scratch wound, and Transwell assays, respectively. Nude mouse xenograft models were established to evaluate tumor growth and VM formation in vivo. The expression of VM- and EMT-related molecules was examined by quantitative PCR and Western blotting.ResultsVM structures were observed in gastric cancer tissues, and WISP2 expression was significantly lower in VM-positive specimens compared with VM-negative specimens (P < 0.05). Overexpression of WISP2 significantly inhibited gastric cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (all P < 0.05). Increased WISP2 expression was associated with suppression of EMT features and reduced VM-forming capacity (both P < 0.05). In xenograft models, tumors overexpressing WISP2 showed decreased tumor growth and reduced VM formation compared with controls (P < 0.05). Western blot analysis demonstrated that WISP2 overexpression was associated with downregulation of p-β-catenin (Ser675), total β-catenin, p-GSK-3β (Ser9), and TCF4 (P < 0.05).ConclusionWISP2 overexpression is associated with reduced vasculogenic mimicry in gastric cancer, potentially through suppression of EMT and inhibition of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10620-026-09801-0.Keywords: Gastric cancer, WISP2, Vasculogenic mimicry, Epithelial–mesenchymal transition

Introduction

Globally, gastric carcinoma is ranked fifth in malignancy incidence and represents the third primary contributor to cancer mortality rates [1].Global cancer statistics indicate over 1 million new cases annually [2]. This malignancy is frequently symptomatically insidious, often progressing to advanced stages at diagnosis, thereby missing optimal treatment windows [3]. Although therapeutic strategies (surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy) have moderately improved survival rates, overall prognosis remains poor [4]. Tumor recurrence and metastasis are critical contributors to unfavorable outcomes, with tumor angiogenesis being closely linked to metastatic dissemination [5]. Notably, anti-angiogenic therapies have shown limited survival benefits in gastric cancer patients [6] and may inadvertently promote VM formation, a key factor underlying anti-angiogenic treatment resistance [7]. Consequently, elucidating the molecular mechanisms governing VM holds promise for developing novel therapeutic approaches and prognostic biomarkers in gastric cancer management.

Unlike conventional tumor angiogenesis, VM comprises tumor cell-derived vessel-like structures that supply blood independently of endothelial cell [8]. Multiple mechanisms drive VM formation, including EMT, cancer stem cells (CSCs), and diverse signaling pathways [9]. Notably, EMT constitutes a critical driver in VM-competent tumor cells, facilitating malignant dissemination through diverse molecular mechanisms [10]. Studies indicate that VM correlates with poor prognosis in gastric cancer and other malignancies [11]. Consequently, elucidating key factors and mechanisms governing VM formation in gastric cancer holds significant therapeutic potential for improving patient outcomes through targeted VM inhibition.

EMT is a dynamic process in which epithelial cells lose polarity and intercellular junctions, downregulate epithelial markers, and acquire mesenchymal traits [12]. Molecularly, this transition manifests as concurrent suppression of epithelial signature markers (notably E-cadherin) and induction of mesenchymal determinants including N-cadherin and vimentin, indicating cancer progression and metastasis, serving as one hallmark of tumor metastasis [13]. During EMT, tumor cells acquire enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities, thereby facilitating tumor metastasis and dissemination [14]. The EMT process shares similarities with VM formation [15]. Some studies suggest that EMT progression may promote VM formation in tumor cells; to date, investigations into the interplay between EMT and VM mechanisms within gastric carcinoma microenvironments remain undocumented in the scientific literature [16].

WISP2, a member of the CCN family, regulates diverse physiological processes [17] and exhibits therapeutic potential in inhibiting cancer progression and metastasis [18]. Studies indicate that wild-type WISP2 protein encoded by this gene controls cell cycle initiation and prevents genomic instability leading to malignant transformation [19]. Research has also reported associations between WISP2 and malignancies including breast cancer, colorectal carcinoma, and adenoid cystic carcinoma [20]. Additionally, WISP2 has been observed to correlate with VM [21], with studies noting its function that WISP2 effectively suppresses VM formation in breast cancer research [22]. To date, no studies have investigated the relationship between WISP2 and VM specifically in gastric cancer.

This investigation employed bioinformatic mining of cancer databases integrated with institutional gastric cancer patient records to delineate WISP2 expression patterns between tumor and adjacent normal tissues. Complementarily, cellular assays and animal models were utilized to elucidate the functional influence of WISP2 overexpression on the induction of EMT and VM networks in gastric cancer. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights for prognostic assessment and therapeutic targeting in gastric cancer.

Materials and Methods

Case Information

This study retrospectively analyzed 50 gastric cancer patients undergoing radical gastrectomy between February 2022 and February 2023. Inclusion Criteria: Patients with clinico-pathologically confirmed primary gastric cancer who underwent radical gastrectomy and had not received preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy. Exclusion Criteria: (1) Concurrent malignancy at other sites; (2) Incomplete clinicopathological record; (3) Deaths attributable to non-gastric cancer causes within the 5-year postoperative period following radical gastrectomy; (4) Inadequate follow-up documentation. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks of gastric cancer and adjacent non-tumor tissues were retrieved from the Department of Pathology. Tissue sections were prepared and subsequently subjected to hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College (Approval No. Lunke Pizi [2025] No. 196).

Main Instruments and Reagents

Anti-WISP2 and Anti-CD34 were purchased from Abcam; Anti-E-cadherin, Anti-β-catenin, Anti-Vimentin, Anti-Snail, Anti-TWIST1, Anti-VEGFR2, Anti-CD31, Anti-VEGFA, and Anti-GAPDH antibodies were purchased from Proteintech. The enzyme-labeled goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer was purchased from Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing. Goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP was obtained from Absin. Fetal bovine serum, RPMI 1640 medium, penicillin–streptomycin dual antibiotic mixture, PBS buffer, and trypsin (containing 0.02% EDTA) were purchased from GIBCO, USA. The CCK8 kit was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science and Technology Co., Ltd. The PAGE gel rapid preparation kit was purchased from Shanghai Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The BCA protein concentration assay kit was purchased from Beyotime. The sensitive ECL chemiluminescence detection kit was purchased from Proteintech; the Matrigel and Transwell chambers were purchased from Corning, Inc.; the lentiviral vector containing the WISP2 gene and the control empty vector plasmid were constructed by Obio Technology (Shanghai) Corp., Ltd. The inverted imaging microscope was purchased from Carl Zeiss, Germany.

Detection Methods

HE Staining

Gastric cancer and para-carcinoma tissue paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4-μm thickness. After baking, staining was performed according to the protocol of the Solarbio HE High-Definition Staining Kit.

Immunohistochemical Detection

Tissue sections from gastric carcinoma and paired adjacent normal specimens underwent baking, followed by sequential deparaffinization in xylene, and distilled water rinsing, followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer for 30 min. Subsequent steps comprised endogenous peroxidase blocking for 30 min and overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against WISP2 (Proteintech, 1:1000), extensive PBS washes, 1-h incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG polymer secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO, PV-9001), and final processing through PBS rinsing, DAB chromogenic development, hematoxylin nuclear counterstaining, neutral resin-based permanent mounting, and brightfield whole-slide image acquisition.

Cell Culture and WISP2 Gene Overexpression in Gastric Cancer Cell Lines

Gastric cancer cell lines were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. At 70–80% confluence, logarithmic-phase cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h to achieve ~ 70% confluency. Cells were then transduced with pre-titrated lentiviral particles (determining optimal multiplicity of infection) expressing either control (OE-NC) or WISP2-overexpression (OE-WISP2) constructs. Following 24-h incubation at 37 °C, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. At 72-h post-transduction, transduction efficiency was assessed via fluorescence microscopy prior to selecting stable transformants through 7-day puromycin treatment (1 µg/mL). Subsequently, serum-starved OE-NC and OE-WISP2 cells were treated for 6 h, followed by 24-h incubation with EMT Inhibitor 1 (2 µg/mL), establishing the following experimental groups: EMT inhibitor 1 group and the OE-WISP2 + EMT inhibitor 1 group.

Angiogenesis Assay

To investigate the fundamental relationship between WISP2 expression and VM capability, this section primarily describes the VM formation assay using the AGS cell model. Data from normoxia/hypoxia control experiments are presented as background validation in Fig. 2I–L. The gastric cancer cell lines were divided into AGS (experimental group), KAO-III, and MKN-45 (control groups). HUVECs were passaged to generations 3–5. The experimental groups included NC-OE, WISP2-OE, EMT inhibitor 1, WISP2-OE + EMT inhibitor 1, normoxia group (21% O2), and hypoxia group (1% O2, using a tri-gas incubator). To induce VM, cells were cultured in a tri-gas incubator at 1% O2 concentration for 48 h continuously. The Transwell co-culture system was employed as follows: gastric cancer cells (1 × 105/well) were seeded in the lower chamber of 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h until reaching 80% confluence. In the upper chamber, HUVECs (5 × 104/well) were seeded onto the Matrigel-coated surface of Transwell inserts (Matrigel was thawed overnight at 4 °C, coated at 50 μL/well, and polymerized at 37 °C for 1 h prior to cell seeding). Place the Transwell chamber into a 6-well plate and co-culture with gastric cancer cells for 48 h (conditioned medium: serum-free RPMI 1640). After thawing the Corning Matrigel, it was evenly coated onto pre-cooled 96-well plates (50 μL/well) and allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 1 h. The co-cultured HUVEC cells were collected, resuspended in serum-free medium, and seeded onto the Matrigel-coated plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well. Following incubation at 37 °C for 6–24 h, tubular structure formation was dynamically observed under an inverted microscope. After Calcein-AM staining for viable cells, five random fields were selected for quantitative analysis, with both branch points and total tube length measured using ImageJ software.

CCK-8 Assay for Evaluating the Effect of WISP2 on Gastric Cancer Cell Proliferation

Human gastric carcinoma AGS cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 103 cells/well, cultured under standard conditions for 24 h to ensure proper adherence before adding 1 μL of CCK-8 reagent to each well, and maintained for 3 h of light-protected incubation, followed by microplate spectrophotometer quantification of absorbance at 450 nm.

Evaluation of WISP2’s Impact on Gastric Cancer Cell Migration Capacity Through Transwell and Scratch Assays

① Transwell Assay: Transwell chambers were employed for this experiment, with the upper chamber seeded with transfected AGS cells (6 × 104 cells/mL in 100 μL serum-free medium) and the lower chamber containing 600 μL DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS. Following 24-h incubation under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with crystal violet for nuclear visualization, and quantified by counting five random high-power fields per membrane using an inverted microscope. ② Scratch assay: Scratch assay were performed by seeding WISP2-transfected cells in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well. Following 24-h incubation, standardized wounds were generated using sterile pipette tips, washed with PBS, and replaced with complete growth medium. Wound closure dynamics were documented at 0-h and 24-h post-injury. Migration rates were calculated as follows: [(Initial wound width − Final wound width)/Initial wound width] × 100%. Quantitative analysis incorporated three randomly selected fields per group with results representing mean values from three independent experimental replicates.

Transwell Assay Evaluating the Effect of WISP2 on the Invasive Capacity of Gastric Cancer Cells

For invasion assays, 40 μL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel® was polymerized in Transwell inserts at 37 °C for 30 min to establish basement membrane matrices. Following matrix solidification, transfected AGS cells (2.5 × 105 cells/mL in serum-free medium) were seeded in the upper chamber with 200 μL suspension, whereas the lower compartment received 600 μL DMEM containing 20% FBS chemoattractant. Subsequent processing mirrored the migration assay protocol (Sect. 1.2.7), with matrix-invaded cells quantified after traversing the Matrigel barrier.

Fluorescence Quantitative PCR Detection

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent-based extraction, followed by cDNA synthesis with reverse transcription kits. Quantitative RT-qPCR amplification proceeded under 40 cycles of the following thermal profile: denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 34 s. Primer sequences for target gene quantification were as follows:

E-cadherin (F): 5'-CGAGAGCTACACGTTCACGG-3′, E-cadherin (R): 5′-GGGTGTCGAGGGAAAAATAGG-3′; Snail (F): 5′-TCGGAAGCCTAACTACAGCGAT-3′, Snail (R): 5′-AGATGAGCATTGGCAGCGAG-3′; Vimentin (F): 5′-GACGCCATCAACACCGAGTT-3′, Vimentin (R): 5′-CTTTGTCGTTGGTTAGCTGGT-3′; TWIST1 (F): 5′-TCAAAGAAACAGGGCGTGGG-3′, TWIST1 (R): 5′-CAGAGGTGTGAGGATGGTGC-3′; β-catenin(CTNNB1) (F): 5′-AAAGCGGCTGTTAGTCACTGG-3′, β-catenin(CTNNB1) (R): 5′-CGAGTCATTGCATACTGTCCAT-3′; VEGFA (F): 5′-AGGGCAGAATCATCACGAAGT-3′, VEGFA (R): 5′-AGGGTCTCGATTGGATGGCA-3′; CD31 (F): 5′-AACAGTGTTGACATGAAGAGCC-3′, CD31 (R): 5′-TGTAAAACAGCACGTCATCCTT-3′; VEGFR2 (F): 5′-GGCCCAATAATCAGAGTGGCA-3′, VEGFR2 (R): 5′-CCAGTGTCATTTCCGATCACTTT-3′; WISP2 (F): 5′-GCGACCAACTCCACGTCTG-3′; WISP2 (R): 5′-TCCCCTTCCCGATACAGGC-3′; GAPDH (F): 5′-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3′, GAPDH (R): 5′-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3′. The mRNA expression levels were normalized using GAPDH as the internal reference and analyzed by relative quantification with the 2-ΔΔCt, method.

Western Blot Analysis

Protein lysates from gastric cancer cell lines and xenograft tumors were extracted using RIPA buffer, quantified via BCA assay, and electrophoresed (40 μg protein/lane) through 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels under constant voltage (80 V for stacking gel, 30 min; 120 V for separating gel, 60 min). Proteins were subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes via wet transfer at 400 mA for 30 min, blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h, and probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against: WISP2 (#21933-1-AP), E-cadherin (#20874-1-AP), β-catenin (#51067-2-AP), Vimentin (#10366-1-AP), Snail (#13099-1-AP), TWIST1 (#25465-1-AP), VEGFR2 (#19035-1-AP), CD31 (#28083-1-AP), VEGFA (#19035-1-AP), p-β-catenin (Ser675; #14601-1-AP), TCF4 (#22327-1-AP), p-GSK-3β (Ser9; #14876-1-AP), and GSK-3β (#22104-1-AP) (all Proteintech, 1:1000 dilutions). After TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abbkine, #AS014; 1:10,000) for 1 h, developed with ECL substrate, and imaged using a ChemiDoc MP system. The relative expression levels of target proteins were analyzed using Image J software.

Construction of Xenograft Tumor Model in Nude Mice

Sixteen 4–6-week-old BALB/c nude mice (certified specific pathogen-free status) were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., and randomly allocated into Vector and OE groups (n = 8/group). Cells were prepared by digesting AGS cells overexpressing WISP2 with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS at 5 × 107 cells/mL. Subsequently, 200 μL of this suspension was subcutaneously inoculated into the OE-group mice, while the Vector group received parental AGS cells lacking WISP2 modification. Tumor dimensions were measured every 3 days (tumor volume = 0.5 × length × width2). After 21 days, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, with tumors excised for photographic documentation and further analysis. This protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Bengbu Medical University (Approval No. 504[2025]).

Bioinformatics Analysis

Public data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were utilized to analyze the expression levels of WISP2 messenger RNA (mRNA) in cancer tissues versus adjacent normal tissues.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Independent samples t-test was employed for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among three or more groups. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

VM Was Observed in Gastric Cancer Tissues, with WISP2 Showing Low Expression

To detect VM in gastric cancer, immunohistochemical staining for CD34 and PAS double staining were performed on cancerous and adjacent tissue sections (Fig. 1A). Results revealed the presence of VM in subsets of gastric cancer tissues. To further define the relationship between VM and WISP2, we first assessed WISP2 expression in gastric cancer tissues and adjacent normal tissues using publicly available data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, Fig. 1B). Concurrent immunohistochemical staining for WISP2 on VM-positive gastric cancer tissues and adjacent normal sections demonstrated that tissues with higher VM formation exhibited lower WISP2 expression, while those with lower VM formation displayed higher WISP2 expression (Fig. 1C).

Reduced WISP2 Expression Correlates with VM Formation in Gastric Cancer Cells

To investigate the relationship between WISP2 expression and VM in gastric cancer cells, we examined WISP2 expression across cell lines. Compared to normal human gastric epithelial cells (GES-1), WISP2 expression was significantly elevated in KAO-III and MKN-45 cells (P < 0.05) but reduced in AGS cells (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A–C). VM assays revealed that AGS cells exhibited stronger VM-inducing capability than KAO-III and MKN-45 cells (Fig. 2D–F). Consequently, AGS was selected for subsequent experiments. The Western blot analysis was performed to examine the expression of WISP2 in hypoxia-induced gastric cancer cells, while the tube formation assay was conducted to assess the angiogenic capacity of the two cell groups. The results demonstrated that the hypoxia-treated AGS gastric cancer cells exhibited enhanced angiogenic potential along with reduced WISP2 expression (Fig. 2G–L).

WISP2 Overexpression Inhibits EMT in Gastric Cancer Cells

To investigate the effect of WISP2 overexpression on EMT, we employed lentiviral transfection to modulate WISP2 protein expression in AGS cells. The CCK8 assay results demonstrated that, compared to the NC-OE group, the WISP2-OE group exhibited a significant reduction in cell proliferation capacity. Furthermore, upon treatment with EMT inhibitor 1, the WISP2-OE + EMT inhibitor 1 group showed an additional decrease in cell proliferation compared to the WISP2-OE group alone (Fig. 3A–C, E). The results of the scratch assay and Transwell experiments demonstrated that compared with the NC-OE group, the WISP2-OE group exhibited significantly reduced migration and invasion capabilities (P < 0.05). After treatment with EMT inhibitor 1 in the WISP2-OE group, the WISP2-OE + EMT inhibitor 1 group showed a further significant decrease in cell migration and invasion abilities compared to the WISP2-OE group (Fig. 3D, F–I); the PCR results demonstrated that the expression levels of E-cadherin and WISP2 were significantly higher in the WISP2-OE group compared to the NC-OE group (P < 0.001), while showing decreased expression abundance when compared to the WISP2-OE + EMT inhibitor 1 group (P < 0.01, Fig. 3J–O). The Western blot results demonstrated that compared with the OE-NC group, the WISP2-OE group exhibited increased expression of E-cadherin and WISP2, along with decreased expression levels of β-catenin, Vimentin, Snail, and Twist1. In the WISP2-OE + EMT inhibitor 1 group, the expression levels of E-cadherin and WISP2 were further elevated relative to the WISP2-OE group, while the expression of β-catenin, Vimentin, Snail, and Twist1 was reduced (Fig. 3P–V).

Angiogenesis assay revealed that the WISP2-OE group exhibited significantly reduced branch points and tube length compared to the NC-OE group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the WISP2-OE + EMT inhibitor 1 group demonstrated decreased branch points and tube length relative to the WISP2-OE group (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A, C, D). As determined by PCR and Western blot analyses, the expression levels of VEGFR2, CD31, and VEGFA were reduced in the WISP2-OE group compared to the NC-OE group. Relative to the WISP2-OE group, the WISP2-OE + EMT inhibitor 1 group exhibited decreased expression of VEGFR2, CD31, and VEGFA (P < 0.05, Fig. 4B, E–J).

The Effect of WISP2 Overexpression on Tumor Formation in Nude Mice and Tumor Angiogenesis in Mice

The results of tumor formation in nude mice demonstrated that the tumor weight and volume in the OE group were significantly reduced compared to the Vector group (P < 0.01, Fig. 5A–D). Histopathological examination (Hematoxylin and Eosin staining) revealed that tumor cells in the Vector group exhibited diffuse growth patterns, densely packed arrangements, and relatively abundant cytoplasm. In contrast, the OE group exhibited reduced cellularity, partial pale cytoplasmic and nuclear staining, nuclear lysis, and cellular disintegration. CD34-PAS double staining and immunofluorescence assays demonstrated decreased VM and reduced CD31 expression in the OE group compared to the Vector group (Fig. 5E–G). The Western blot results demonstrated that the expression level of E-Cadherin was significantly increased in the OE group (P < 0.01), while the expression levels of VEGFR2, β-catenin, vimentin, Snail, VEGFA, and Twist1 were significantly decreased (P < 0.01, Fig. 5H–O).

WISP2 Overexpression Inhibits the Wnt Pathway

The molecular mechanisms by which WISP2 overexpression suppresses EMT and VM require further investigation. We analyzed the expression levels of WISP2 mRNA in cancerous and adjacent non-cancerous tissues using public data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Protein–protein interaction (PPI), Gene Ontology (GO), and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that the Wnt pathway may play a role in gastric cancer progression (Fig. 6A–C). Western blot results indicated decreased protein levels of p-β-catenin, β-catenin, p-GSK-3β, and TCF4 in the WISP2-OE group (Fig. 6D–I). Further PCR analysis of mRNA expression in two groups of gastric cancer cells demonstrated significantly reduced expression of β-catenin and TCF4 in the WISP2-OE group (P < 0.05), while GSK-3β expression showed no significant difference between the two experimental groups (Fig. 6J–L).

Discussion

This study integrated archival gastric cancer pathology specimens, in vitro cellular experiments, and in vivo animal models to investigate the regulatory role of WISP2 in VM and EMT in gastric cancer. Bioinformatics enrichment analyses combined with experimental validation revealed that WISP2 partially inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby suppressing VM through EMT regulation in gastric cancer.

Accumulating evidence indicates that WISP2 exerts inhibitory effects on metastatic progression in esophageal malignancies through modulation of ERK signaling cascades and E-cadherin regulatory networks [14]. Concurrent research confirms low WISP2 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma, where it exerts anticancer effects [23]. Knockdown of WISP2 enhances invasion and motility in colorectal cancer cells [24]. Overexpression of WISP2 inhibits proliferation and induces apoptosis in leukemia cells [25]. However, the relationship between WISP2 and VM in gastric cancer remains unreported. This study first identified decreased WISP2 expression in gastric cancer tissues through bioinformatics databases. Validation using our institutional cohort combined hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining confirmed reduced WISP2 levels in VM-positive gastric cancer tissues.

VM constitutes a distinct pattern of neovascularization in malignant tumors, wherein neoplastic cell lineages recapitulate endothelial functions to self-assemble extracellular matrix-containing tubular networks independently of endogenous endothelial components. These structures can connect with the host microvasculature system and primarily consist of three components: plastic tumor cells, remodeled extracellular matrix, and connections to the host microcirculation. These VM channels exhibit PAS-positive but CD34-negative staining characteristics and these processes demonstrate significant mechanistic involvement in neoplastic dissemination and metastatic progression [26]. In the in vitro hypoxia-induced angiogenesis experiment, WISP2 expression was significantly decreased in AGS cells under hypoxic conditions, indicating a negative correlation between the angiogenesis-inducing capacity of AGS gastric cancer cells and WISP2 expression. Collectively, this finding establishes the microenvironmental modulation by WISP2 as a mechanistic determinant of malignant transformation in gastric oncogenesis. EMT represents a dynamic cellular reprogramming event wherein polarized epithelial cells undergo dissolution of apical–basal polarity, fundamentally restructuring their phenotypic identity toward mesenchymal characteristics. This transformation involves the activation of various epithelial-derived molecules, including transcription factors, cytoskeletal remodeling, and dissolution of pericellular-basal lamina connections [27]. Studies have shown that VM may be associated with EMT. To investigate whether WISP2 expression in gastric cancer cells can regulate VM and further explore whether WISP2 modulates VM through EMT, we constructed a WISP2-overexpressing gastric cancer AGS cell line using lentiviral transfection technology. The WISP2-overexpressing AGS cells were then treated with EMT inhibitor 1. CCK8, scratch wound healing, and Transwell assays demonstrated that WISP2 overexpression inhibited the proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis of gastric cancer cells. PCR and Western blot analyses were performed to examine the expression of EMT-related and angiogenesis-associated proteins. The results preliminarily confirmed that WISP2 overexpression could suppress the EMT process. Subsequent hypoxia-induced angiogenesis assays in gastric cancer cells, along with the detection of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGFA, CD31, and VEGFR2) expression, revealed that EMT inhibition significantly reduced the VM capacity of gastric cancer cells. These findings indicate that WISP2 overexpression can attenuate the ability of gastric cancer cells to induce VM by suppressing EMT. To further validate the impact of WISP2 overexpression on gastric cancer in vivo, this study established a WISP2-overexpressing mouse model. The xenograft tumor assay demonstrated that WISP2 overexpression significantly inhibited tumor growth in mice. Histopathological analysis of tumor tissue sections via H&E staining, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence revealed a marked increase in VM phenomena in WISP2-overexpressing tumors. Additionally, Western blot and PCR results showed significantly reduced expression levels of EMT-related proteins and angiogenesis-associated proteins. These collective findings confirm that WISP2 overexpression suppresses VM and EMT, though the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.

EMT is regulated by altered signaling pathways, including Wnt and TGF-β signaling [28]. Wnt proteins represent secreted glycoproteins that combinatorially engage cell surface receptors to mediate signaling cascades. This pathway fundamentally orchestrates developmental programs across diverse tissue and organ systems. This interaction triggers phosphorylation and dephosphorylation cascades of downstream proteins, leading to the accumulation of β-Catenin [29]. Cytoplasmically liberated β-Catenin undergoes nuclear translocation, dynamically engages E-cadherin at adherens junctions to orchestrate complex assembly, and co-regulates target gene transcription as a canonical Wnt pathway effector. Aberrant expression or activation of this pathway has been implicated in tumorigenesis [30]. Pathway hyperactivation of Wnt/β-Catenin signaling implicates its mechanistic contribution to the co-acquisition of mesenchymal transition signatures and stem-like phenotypes during colorectal carcinogenesis [31]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that Wnt signaling promotes angiogenesis and tumor growth in lung cancer [32]. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that WISP2 expression in gastric cancer may correlate with the Wnt/β-Catenin signaling pathway. Consequently, we hypothesized that the negative regulatory effects of WISP2 overexpression on EMT and angiogenesis might involve Wnt/β-Catenin signaling. The expression levels of pathway-related mRNA and proteins in gastric cancer cells were evaluated using PCR and Western blot analyses. These results confirmed that WISP2 overexpression suppresses both EMT and VM by inhibiting the activation of the Wnt/β-Catenin pathway.

This study confirmed that WISP2 expression is significantly downregulated in gastric cancer tissues. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments demonstrated that WISP2 may regulate EMT and VM in gastric cancer by inhibiting the signaling pathway of Wnt/β-Catenin, thereby enriching our understanding of the biological functions of WISP2 in gastric cancer.

Although this study provides preliminary evidence that WISP2 is associated with inhibition of vasculogenic mimicry (VM) and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) in gastric cancer based on clinical specimens, in vitro experiments, and animal models, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, in the analysis of clinical samples, we observed a trend toward lower WISP2 expression in VM-positive gastric cancer regions. However, due to limitations in laboratory resources, study timeline, and funding, systematic semi-quantitative immunohistochemical evaluation (such as H-score analysis) and comprehensive statistical comparisons across all tissue samples could not be performed. As a result, precise quantitative assessment of WISP2 expression relative to adjacent non-tumor tissues was not achieved. In addition, this was a single-center retrospective study with a limited sample size, which may reduce statistical power, introduce selection bias, and limit the generalizability of the findings.

Second, regarding mechanistic investigation, this study primarily focused on the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Although bioinformatic analyses and experimental results consistently supported the involvement of this pathway, direct causal relationships between WISP2 and Wnt/β-catenin activity were not established. Pharmacological activation or genetic rescue experiments were not performed, which constrains the depth of mechanistic interpretation. Future studies incorporating pathway rescue approaches, such as Wnt agonists including CHIR99021, will be necessary to further clarify the regulatory role of WISP2 in this signaling axis.

In conclusion, this study suggests that WISP2 may be involved in the regulation of EMT and VM in gastric cancer, potentially through modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying VM formation and highlight WISP2 as a potential therapeutic target. Further multi-center studies with larger clinical cohorts and more comprehensive mechanistic analyses are warranted to validate and extend these observations.
