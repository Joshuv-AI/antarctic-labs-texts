Pretreatment With Vancomycin Prevents Staphylococcal Biofilm Formation on Marlex Mesh
Authors: Christina A Chao, Tyler D Hoskins, Mohammed Hammad, Suenghwan Jo, Mathias P Bostrom, Alberto V Carli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolypropylene (PPE) mesh is a popular surgical treatment for extensor mechanism disruption in revision knee arthroplasty, but development of infection can be catastrophic. Pretreating orthopedic materials with antibiotics has precedence, but has not yet been described for PPE mesh. The purpose of the current study is to determine if mesh pretreatment with vancomycin could effectively prevent biofilm formation.MethodsSterile PPE mesh was cut into 10-mm diameter circles. PPE circles were soaked in saline for 20 minutes with one of the following vancomycin concentrations: 1) 0.625 mg/ml, 2) 1.25 mg/ml, 3) 2.5 mg/ml, 4) 5.0 mg/ml, and 5) 10.0 mg/ml. To simulate surgical irrigation prior to closure, samples were rinsed with saline 0, 1, 2, or 3 times. Each group had 9 samples. Rinsed PPE circles were placed in a 48-well plate, inoculated with 106 colony-forming units (CFUs) of methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, cultured in tryptic soy broth for 24 hours, rinsed to remove planktonic bacteria, and sonicated in fresh media for 30 minutes. Sonicated fluid was serially diluted and plated for CFUs. Infection prevention was defined as 0 CFUs. PPE circles were imaged with scanning electron microscopy to visualize vancomycin crystals and biofilm.ResultsPretreatment of PPE mesh with 10 mg/mL of vancomycin was the only condition that prevented S aureus biofilm formation in all conditions. Scanning electron microscopy confirmed that vancomycin pretreatment deposited antibiotic crystals on the mesh surface and that biofilm was not present.ConclusionsVancomycin pretreatment of PPE mesh can prevent biofilm formation even after rinsing.Keywords: Marlex mesh, Revision knee arthroplasty

Introduction

The prevalence of revision total knee arthroplasty (rTKA) in the United States has been steadily increasing [1]. Extensor mechanism disruption is considered to be among the most disabling complications of rTKA, with poor outcomes reported even when experienced arthroplasty surgeons attempt primary repair [2]. A widely accepted technique for reconstruction the extensor mechanism was initially described by Hanssen and involves securing a polypropylene (PPE) mesh within the proximal tibia and then weaving it through the remaining quadriceps and peripatellar tissue [3]. Although this technique has demonstrated favorable functional outcomes [4,5], a feared complication is the development of a subsequent periprosthetic joint infection (PJI). A PJI occurring after PPE mesh reconstruction is a particularly devastating event, as it mandates mesh resection, precludes future extensor mechanism function, and can even be a precursor for eventual above knee amputation.

Reducing the risk of PJI following PPE mesh reconstruction often involves optimizing rTKA patient comorbidities, administering systemic antibiotics and carefully handling and closing the fragile soft tissue envelope. Unfortunately, none of these interventions addresses a potential source of infection: the mesh itself. We have previously demonstrated that if bacterial contamination of the mesh occurs during surgery, biofilm can rapidly form and mature within 72 hours [6]. Although antiseptic solutions can be used to remove planktonic bacteria that may deposit on a PPE mesh, this intervention does not confer long-term protection for the mesh from being contaminated. Employing a method that could prevent biofilm formation on the mesh surface during the vulnerable wound healing phase that occurs immediately after surgery could be an effective means for preventing subsequent PJI. One potential method to protect PPE mesh could involve presoaking it in vancomycin. This technique has been employed in the field of sports medicine for anterior cruciate ligament allografts, with a body of evidence confirming that it reduces postoperative infection rates without compromising a graft’s biomechanical properties [7].

Consequently, the purpose of the current study is to determine if pretreating PPE mesh in various concentrations of vancomycin can subsequently prevent bacteria from colonizing and forming biofilm on the mesh surface. Given that surgeons often irrigate surgical wounds several times prior to skin closure, we defined successful infection prevention occurring only if bacteria and biofilm could not be detected on a mesh surface after it was rinsed 3 times following pretreatment with vancomycin.

Material and methods

Bacterial strain

Methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA; Xen 36, derived from American Type Culture Collection 49525, PerkinElmer) was chosen to represent common gram-positive species in clinical PJI [8,9] and Escherichia coli (E coli; American Type Culture Collection 25922). Xen 36 originated from a clinical isolate known to reliably form biofilms in vitro [10]. Bacteria were streaked onto agar plates and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours in tryptic soy broth (TSB) (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey) with kanamycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri; 200 μg/mL). After incubation, cultures were subculture and diluted to a final concentration of 1 × 106 colony-forming units (CFUs)/mL using a cuvette spectrophotometer (SmartSpec Plus, Bio-Rad, Hercules, California).

Polypropylene mesh and vancomycin pretreatment

A 10-inch × 15-inch PPE mesh sheet (Marlex Mesh, product no. 011266, CR Bard, Davol Inc, Warwick, Rhode Island) was cut using a 10-mm diameter hole punch into circle shapes (Fig. 1a). This shape was chosen so that uniform pieces could fit securely within the wells of a 48-well plate (Fig. 1b). After cutting, PPE mesh circles were sterilized with 70% ethanol and subsequently dried under sterile laminar airflow. Prior research has confirmed that this sterilization method does not affect the structural integrity or surface characteristics of the PPE mesh [6].

Photo of Marlex mesh circles on flat surface (a) and submerged in a 48-well plate (b) for vancomycin pretreatment (b).

PPE mesh circles were then submerged in 1 of 5 different concentrations of vancomycin (Fresenius Kabi, Lake Zurich, IL) in saline: 0.625 mg/mL, 1.25 mg/mL, 2.5 mg/mL, 5.0 mg/mL, and 10.0 mg/mL. Each sample was submerged for 20 minutes to simulate a relevant duration that could occur clinically within the operating room. PPE mesh circles were weighed before and after being submerged to quantify vancomycin that remained on the surface. PPE mesh circles were then randomized to 4 treatments: no rinsing, 1 rinse, 2 rinses, or 3 rinses. A rinse involved dipping a PPE mesh circle into phosphate buffered saline for 3 seconds. This was performed to simulate a mesh being irrigated in the operating room. In total, there were 20 experimental groups involving 5 vancomycin pretreatment concentrations and 4 rinsing conditions.

Mesh culture and bacterial quantification

Following vancomycin pretreatment and rinsing, PPE mesh circles were placed within individual wells in a 24 well plate and covered with 450 μL of TSB inoculated with 1 × 106 CFU Xen 36. All samples were left to culture overnight in a static incubator at 37°C. Following incubation, samples were gently rinsed to remove planktonic bacteria, immersed in fresh TSB, and then sonicated on ice for 30 minutes at 52 kHz and 137 W (550HT, Aquasonic, VWR Scientific Product, Radnor, Pennsylvania) to detach biofilm-associated bacteria. The resulting bacterial suspension was serially diluted, plated in 50 μL aliquots on TSB agar, and incubated overnight at 37°C. CFUs were counted to assess viable biofilm bacterial growth.

The primary outcome of the study was to determine which pretreatment conditions could successfully produce 0 CFUs from sonicated suspensions. Each experimental group was tested 9 times and a ratio of failure (bacterial growth) to total tests was reported and graphed on a heat map. Experimental groups with 0 failures were considered effective for infection prevention (green color), groups with 1 or 2 failures were considered partially effective, and groups with ≥3 failures were considered ineffective for infection prevention.

Visual confirmation of biofilm growth

Scanning electron microscopy (Gemini 300 FE-SEM, Zeiss, Cologne, Germany) was utilized to determine if staphylococcal biofilm could be visualized on PPE mesh circles (Fig. 2a). PPE mesh circles that had either been 1) not pretreated with vancomycin or 2) pretreated with 10 mg/mL vancomycin were cultured in 106 CFUs/mL of Xen 36 S aureus for 72 hours, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer for 20 minutes, and then rinsed 3 times with phosphate buffered saline. They were then dehydrated through a series of increasing ethanol concentrations until dry and sputter-coated with gold. This coating was applied to minimize thermal damage and enhance the secondary electron signal for improved topographical imaging of nonconductive bacteria. Images were captured at 2 different magnifications and both control and pretreatment groups were compared. S aureus was identified as ∼1-micron spherical objects organized in cluster, while biofilm was identified through finding extracellular fibrinous extensions interconnecting adjacent bacteria [11]. Vancomycin was identified as crystalline structures accumulating in overlapping mesh fibers [12].

Low and high magnification scanning electron microscopy images: (a) Sterile Marlex mesh; (b) Mesh with 24 hours MSSA biofilm visualized as cocci in clusters (red arrows); (c) Mesh pretreated with 10 mg/mL vancomycin with crystal visible on surface (blue arrows); (d) Pretreated mesh after being grown in broth containing methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus. No biofilm is present. MSSA, methicillin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus.

Results

Pretreatment efficacy

PPE mesh pretreated with 10.0 mg/mL vancomycin was the only condition to consistently meet criteria to effectively prevent infection, with 0 CFUs growing in 9 of 9 samples after 1, 2, and 3 rinses, respectively (Table 1). The 5.0 mg/mL treatment met criteria for being partially effective, with 0/9 samples growing CFUs except the 3-rinse group, where 2 of 9 grew S aureus colonies. All other doses were ineffective in preventing infection. A dose-response and rinse-dependent effect was observed (Table 1), with lower vancomycin and a higher number of rinses producing a higher number of prevention failures. This was most evident in 0.625 mg/mL group, with over 3 failures in all rinsing conditions.

Visual analysis of surfaces

Inspection of the PPE mesh circles using a scanning electron microscope demonstrated, in control conditions with no vancomycin pretreatment, numerous staphylococci aggregating in clusters with interconnected extracellular biofilm (Fig. 2c). Conversely, PPE mesh circles that were pretreated with 10 mg/mL vancomycin demonstrate no identifiable bacteria or biofilm (Fig. 2d). Finally, inspection of PPE mesh circles that were pretreated with 10 mg/mL vancomycin, but then never immersed in bacterial media demonstrates numerous vancomycin crystalline structures covering the mesh fibers (Fig. 2b).

Discussion

The rising incidence of rTKA and complications such as extensor mechanism disruption underscore the need for research which enhances the outcome of PPE mesh reconstruction. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate vancomycin pretreatment of PPE mesh for infection prevention in the context of rTKA. Our in vitro results demonstrate that immersing PPE mesh in a 10 mg/mL vancomycin solution effectively prevents bacterial colonization on the mesh surface, even after multiple saline rinses. This suggests that vancomycin creates a robust antimicrobial coating on the mesh fibers. In practical terms, a 10 mg/mL concentration can be achieved by dissolving roughly 1–2 g of vancomycin in 100–200 mL of normal saline, respectively. Such a volume is sufficient to fully submerge the entirety of a PPE mesh and can be easily prepared using standard vancomycin vials available in the operating room.

The process of loading antibiotics onto implantable materials is not new to orthopaedic surgeons or in the PPE literature. A familiar example is the “vancomycin wrap” technique for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, first described by Vertullo [13], whereby grafts are soaked for 10–15 minutes in gauze saturated with a 5 mg/mL vancomycin solution. This technique has been associated with significantly lower surgical site infection rates and no increased risks in wound healing problems, graft failure, or arthrofibrosis [14,15]. When looking specifically at the PPE mesh literature from the field of general surgery, the effects of antibiotic pretreatment have been mixed. Fundamentally, vancomycin pretreatment in a 10 mg/mL solution for 15 minutes was shown to lead to marked decreases in bacterial counts in a rat ventral hernia model [16]. This result is congruent to the findings of the current study, confirming that vancomycin can remain bound to PPE mesh surface in-vivo and provide a sustained antimicrobial effect. Clinical results have not been so clear, with a randomized control trial observing no reduction in surgical site infections with vancomycin-soaked mesh in ventral hernia repair [17]. A notable limitation in this trial was the low number of cases, making it difficult to determine if it was adequately powered for its primary outcome. An additional distinguishing factor is that gram-negative infections are more common following hernia surgery, unlike the skin-based gram-positive infections commonly identified following rTKA.

Alternatives to antibiotic soaking include application of antiseptic solutions or precoating PPE mesh with antimicrobial coatings during the manufacturing process. We have previously published that antiseptic solutions such as undiluted povidone-iodine can be effective in eliminating mature staphylococcal biofilm formed in vitro on mesh surfaces [6]. However, when investigating these solutions for preventative effects, we found that a single saline rinse removed all the povidone-iodine and its antimicrobial effects from the mesh surface. Interestingly, the literature affirms the need for antiseptics to be chemically bound to PPE to bear a preventative effect. Specifically, meshes loaded with chlorhexidine palmitate bound to antimicrobial metals (such as a silver-chlorhexidine coating) have been shown to have strong protective effects against infection [18,19]. Yet despite these promising results, the influence of antiseptics and bound metals on mesh-tissue integration as well as local wound healing remains unknown. Future efforts directed toward in-vivo testing of these materials are needed before introducing them into the fragile wound bed found in a revision knee arthroplasty.

We do acknowledge several limitations in the current study. Firstly, experiments were conducted under in vitro conditions that do not reproduce the volume of potential bacterial contamination, the complex 3-dimensional structure of a folded mesh, or the presence of the host immune system. Although the mesh does have a net-like structure that permits solution to flow through it, specific subsequent testing should be performed to confirm this. Furthermore, the duration of protection offered by vancomycin-coated mesh in vivo can be influenced by tissue perfusion which could wash out the antibiotic over time, or proteins in blood might bind vancomycin and diminish its antibacterial efficacy. We attempted to simulate a worst-case dilution scenario by performing multiple rinses, and encouragingly the antimicrobial effect persisted through those challenges. However, the true persistence of efficacy in the revision knee milieu is unknown. Secondly, although S aureus is a common PJI pathogen, the spectrum of potentially offending organisms is broader, and future studies should assess the efficacy of vancomycin pretreatment against other pathogens, including methicillin-resistant strains and polymicrobial infections. Finally, the experimental simulation of surgical irrigation through rinsing does not completely replicate the mechanical and volumetric conditions that occur when pulsatile lavage is utilized. Further investigations quantifying the effect of low or high-speed pulsatile lavage on vancomycin-soaked mesh would bear clinical relevance. Until then, we recommend that surgeons minimize any irrigation of pretreated mesh in order to avoid diminishing the antimicrobial effect of vancomycin.

Conclusions

The findings from the current study provide a compelling argument for orthopaedic surgeons to consider pretreating PPE mesh with vancomycin in the setting of extensor mechanism. Although PJI can still occur through contamination of devascularized tissue or arthroplasty implants, keeping the mesh free of adherent bacteria is unlikely to be deleterious on its ability to tolerate mechanical load and integrate with host tissues. Future efforts are needed to validate these findings through in-vivo modeling to determine if wound infection burdens can be reduced, and also to determine how long-term the vancomycin can remain in proximity to the mesh following wound closure.
