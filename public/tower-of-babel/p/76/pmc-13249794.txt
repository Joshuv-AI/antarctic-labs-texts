Five‐year change in brain metabolism across the spectrum of cognitive impairment in older adults: a quantitative MRI study
Authors: Jiani Wu, Kumiko Oishi, Anja Soldan, Corinne Pettigrew, Zixuan Lin, Yuxin Zhu, Dengrong Jiang, Xin Li, Yifan Gou, Abhay Moghekar, Peiying Liu, Arnold Bakker, Kenichi Oishi, Marilyn Albert, Hanzhang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONIt is desirable to measure brain metabolism without needing ionizing radiation and elucidate its time trajectory. This study employed a positron emission tomography ‐validated, non‐invasive magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technique to characterize longitudinal changes in cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO2) in both cognitively normal and impaired older adults.METHODSParticipants received serial MRI‐based CMRO2 scans between 2015 and 2025. Levels of AD pathology were measured in cerebrospinal fluid and plasma at the initial MRI.RESULTSAt the initial visit, participants with MCI/dementia had lower CMRO2 than normal participants. In the longitudinal analyses, CMRO2 decreased over time in those who were cognitively impaired but remained unchanged in normals. Among normal participants, higher initial levels of total tau and phosphorylated tau at threonine 181 were associated with an increase in CMRO2 during follow‐up, especially in amyloid‐positive individuals.DISCUSSIONAside from the well‐known metabolic slowdown in the later phases of AD, the brain exhibits tau‐dependent hypermetabolism during the preclinical stage.

BACKGROUND

The Alzheimer's disease (AD) continuum comprises a preclinical stage characterized by pathological changes that begin decades before symptom onset, including amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau deposition. 1 , 2 , 3 These changes are then followed by anatomical and physiological changes (i.e., brain atrophy 4 , 5 and hypometabolism 6 ) and ultimately overt cognitive impairment which marks the clinical onset of the disease. 7 Brain hypometabolism, commonly measured by 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET), is a well‐established hallmark in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and dementia, 8 , 9 reflecting reduced energy demand associated with neurodegeneration. However, brain metabolic changes in the early stage of AD have not been sufficiently characterized. Several recent FDG‐PET studies reported glucose hypermetabolism during the asymptomatic or early symptomatic stages, 10 , 11 suggesting a potentially biphasic pattern of metabolism across the clinical spectrum of AD. For instance, increased FDG‐PET metabolism has been reported in cognitively normal individuals with abnormal amyloid levels 12 and genetic risk for AD. 13 In two studies of participants with MCI 14 or normal cognition, 15 higher FDG uptake was found to be associated with higher tau‐PET burden in amyloid‐negative individuals.

However, most prior reports were conducted in cross‐sectional settings, limiting understanding of the temporal trajectory of hyper/hypometabolism across the spectrum of cognitive impairment. Likely due to the involvement of ionizing radiation in FDG‐PET, longitudinal studies of brain metabolism in cognitively normal participants have been scarce. Furthermore, the absence of blood sampling for the measurement of plasma radiolabeled glucose concentrations prevents absolute quantification of glucose metabolism 16 in most studies, introducing potential measurement biases associated with the signals (e.g., non‐specific bindings or abnormalities) in the reference tissue. Recent advances in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) technologies have allowed quantitative imaging of brain oxygen metabolism without radiation or contrast agents. 17 To the extent that oxidative metabolism is the predominant form of energy production in the brain, that is, oxygen and glucose consumption approximately follows a ratio of 6:1, oxygen metabolism is expected to be closely related to glycolysis measured by FDG‐PET. In this study, we employed a PET‐validated MRI technique 17 , 18 to characterize longitudinal changes in cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO2) across the clinical spectrum of AD. We first compared CMRO2 levels between cognitively normal and MCI/dementia groups cross‐sectionally. Then we examined longitudinal trajectories of CMRO2 separately in cognitively normal and impaired participants. To further understand brain metabolism changes during the earliest phases of AD, we additionally evaluated differences in longitudinal CMRO2 trajectories between participants with high and low AD pathology as measured from cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma biomarkers.

METHODS

Participants and consensus diagnosis

This study utilized data from the ongoing longitudinal Biomarkers of Cognitive Decline among Normal Individuals (BIOCARD) study aimed at understanding the early phases of AD. The BIOCARD study was conducted at the National Institutes of Health (NIH) from 1995 to 2005 and, after a pause from 2005 to 2009, was reestablished at Johns Hopkins University (JHU). 19 , 20 By design, approximately 75% of the participants had a first‐degree relative with a history of dementia of the Alzheimer's type. Consensus diagnoses, cognitive assessments, and blood plasma measures have been completed annually since study inception. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and 3T MRI scans have been collected approximately every other year at JHU since 2015. The CMRO2 data in these analyses were collected from 2015 to 2025. Only biofluid data obtained at the same visit as the initial 3T MRI CMRO2 assessment were used in this study because longitudinal biofluid data of sufficient sample size are not yet available.

Annual consensus diagnoses were implemented by the staff of the BIOCARD clinical team. The diagnostic criteria followed the recommendations incorporated into the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer's Association working group reports for the diagnosis of MCI and dementia due to AD. Each participant's diagnosis was determined based on a review of (1) longitudinal cognitive test scores with comparison to published norms; (2) clinical data related to participants’ medical, neurological, and psychiatric status; and (3) cognitive changes reported by the participant and an informant during Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) interviews. Four possible diagnoses were available: (1) cognitively normal, (2) impaired not MCI, (3) MCI, and (4) dementia. Participants received an impaired not MCI diagnosis if there was contrasting information from the CDR interview and the cognitive test scores. These participants were included in the cognitively normal group, consistent with prior reports. 19 Diagnoses (and determination of likely etiology) were made without knowledge of biomarker measures.

Cross‐sectional and longitudinal analyses were conducted in this study. The cross‐sectional cohort includes participants with at least one CMRO2 assessment (N = 191). If a participant had multiple CMRO2 scans, their first time point was used in the cross‐sectional analysis. A subset of participants with at least one follow‐up CMRO2 measurement was included in the longitudinal analysis (N = 157). Participants in the longitudinal cohort were categorized into three diagnostic groups: (1) Normal: participants who were cognitively normal at their initial CMRO2 assessment and remained normal over the course of follow‐up; (2) Progressed: participants who were cognitively normal at the initial CMRO2 assessment but progressed to MCI or dementia. For these participants, only the time points corresponding to the last cognitively normal and later were used in the longitudinal analyses, so that the time of conversion does not become a confounding factor; (3) Impaired: participants with a diagnosis of MCI or dementia at the initial CMRO2 assessment. Due to the relatively small sample sizes of groups 2 and 3, we combined them for the longitudinal analyses described below, acknowledging that there is heterogeneity in this progressed/impaired group.

MRI image acquisition and processing

All MRI scans were collected on a 3T MRI system (Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). CMRO2 was estimated from the arterio‐venous difference in oxygen content 21 using the following equation 17 : CMRO2=CBF×(Ya−Yv)×Ca, where CBF refers to global cerebral blood flow; Ya and Yv are oxygenation in arterial and venous blood, respectively; and Ca represents the capacity of blood to carry O2. Ya is close to unity and has been reported to be minimally affected by age, 22 and therefore it is assumed to be 98% in this study. Ca was derived from hematocrit levels. As hematocrit has been reported to be lower in females than in males and to decrease with age in older adults, Ca was assumed to be 8.15 µmol O2/mL blood for females and 8.56 µmol O2/mL blood for males at age 50 years and was adjusted for an age‐related decrease of 0.014 µmol O2/mL per year for females and 0.034 µmol O2/mL per year for males, based on previous literature. 23

Global CBF was measured by phase‐contrast (PC) MRI 24 , 25 (Figure 1A,B). The parameters of the PC MRI scans were as follows: single‐slice acquisition intersecting the internal carotid and vertebral arteries at the C2/3 spine; repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 19 ms/9 ms; field of view (FOV) = 200 × 200 × 5 mm3; voxel size = 0.5 × 0.5 × 5 mm3; flip angle = 15°; maximum velocity encoding = 40 cm/s; scan duration for each artery = 15 s. Total CBF was quantified by summing up the flux in the four major feeding arteries including the left and right internal carotid arteries and the left and right vertebral arteries. CBF in a unit volume of the brain was obtained by normalizing the total CBF to brain volume derived from a magnetization‐prepared rapid acquisition of gradient echo (MPRAGE) image, which used the following parameters: TR/TE = 6.8 ms/3.1 ms; flip angle = 8°; FOV = 240 × 256 × 204 mm3; voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1.2 mm3; scan duration = 5 min 59 s. Quantification of brain volume from MPRAGE image was conducted by an automatic processing tool, MRICloud (https://www.MRICloud.org JHU, Baltimore, MD). 26

Global venous oxygenation, Yv, was estimated by T2‐Relaxation‐Under‐Spin‐Tagging (TRUST) MRI 27 (Figure 1C,D). The parameters of TRUST imaging were as follows: TR/TE = 3000 ms/3.6 ms; flip angle = 90°; FOV = 220 × 220 × 5 mm3; voxel size = 3.44 × 3.44 × 5 mm3; four effective TEs (1, 40, 80, and 160 ms) with a refocusing pulse interval (τCPMG) of 10 ms; labeling thickness = 100 mm; scan duration = 1 min 12 s. The TRUST MRI signal at four TEs was used to fit for venous blood T2, which was then converted to Yv using a calibration plot. 28 The 95% confidence interval of the estimated 1/T2 (delta R2), representing the uncertainty of Yv measurement, was calculated. Yv data with a delta R2 greater than 5 Hz were excluded from further analysis.

CSF assessments

CSF measures for the levels of total tau (t‐tau), phosphorylated tau 181 (p‐tau181), amyloid beta 42 (Aβ42), and Aβ40 were derived from specimens collected from participants via lumbar puncture after an overnight fast. After collection, CSF was aliquoted into low‐bind polypropylene cryotubes, kept on dry ice, and transferred to a −80°C freezer for long‐term storage. For the present analysis, samples were thawed for the first time since collection to run the fully automated chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassays (Lumipulse G1200 platform; Fujirebio Diagnostics). 29 T‐tau, p‐tau181, and Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio were used to index AD pathology.

Blood plasma assessments

Blood plasma samples were assayed using the NeuroToolKit (cobas Elecsys assays, Roche Diagnostics). 30 Plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio was obtained using blood samples collected at the same visit as the initial 3T MRI CMRO2 assessment. Values > 3 standard deviations (SD) higher or lower than the transformed sample mean were defined as outliers and excluded from analyses (0.6% to 5.5% of values for each biomarker).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using in‐house MATLAB (version 2018b, MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) scripts and R (version 4.3.2).

To compare the demographic variables between diagnostic groups in the cross‐sectional and longitudinal cohort, t‐tests and Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests were used for normally and non‐normally distributed variables, respectively.

Linear regression analysis was used to investigate the cross‐sectional association between diagnostic status and CMRO2, where CMRO2 was the dependent variable, and diagnostic status (coded as 0 for cognitively normal and 1 for MCI/dementia), sex, and age were the independent variables.

To investigate the longitudinal changes in CMRO2 in cognitively normal and progressed/impaired groups, linear mixed‐effects (LME) models were first applied separately to each group, with longitudinal CMRO2 as the dependent variable and follow‐up time (in years), initial age, sex, initial age × time, and sex × time interactions as independent variables. Additionally, an LME model was applied in which CMRO2 data from both normal and progressed/impaired groups were included, and a diagnosis × time interaction effect was examined.

Next, we evaluated the association of initial levels of CSF AD biomarkers (i.e., biomarker levels at the initial CMRO2 assessment) with longitudinal changes in CMRO2 among the subset of participants with CSF data available. In separate LME for each diagnostic group, longitudinal CMRO2 was the dependent variable, and the models included the following independent variables: initial age, sex, follow‐up time, initial AD biomarker levels (t‐tau, p‐tau181, Aβ42/Aβ40, t‐tau/Aβ42, and p‐tau181/Aβ42 in separate models), initial age × time, sex × time, and AD biomarker × time terms. The aforementioned models were additionally performed using clinically derived cutoff thresholds for the CSF AD biomarkers rather than continuous values. In these models, initial levels of AD biomarkers were binarized based on thresholds established by Dakterzada et al., 31 which used the same assay as was used in the present study. Specifically, higher t‐tau level (t‐tau ≥ 399 pg/mL), higher p‐tau181 level (p‐tau181 ≥ 51 pg/mL), and lower Aβ42/Aβ40 level (Aβ42/Aβ40 ≤ 0.070) were coded as 1, and all others were coded as 0.

To additionally focus our analysis on the preclinical AD participants, we further evaluated the association of initial levels of CSF AD biomarkers with longitudinal changes in CMRO2 in amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative groups. Participants were stratified based on CSF and plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 levels. Low and high CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 groups were defined using the clinical cutoff thresholds described in the literature. Since cutoff thresholds for plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 levels were not established for this assay, a median split was used to divide participants into low and high plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 groups. Separate LME models were employed for the low and high Aβ42/Aβ40 groups, in which longitudinal CMRO2 was the dependent variable, and initial age, sex, follow‐up time, initial AD biomarker levels, initial age × time, sex × time, and AD biomarker × time were the independent variables.

Prior to model fitting, all continuous independent variables except follow‐up time were mean‐centered and sex was coded as −0.5 for female and 0.5 for male. All LME models were run using random intercepts. In all analyses, a two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

CMRO2 longitudinal changes accounting for CSF t‐tau, p‐tau181, and amyloid

Among the 157 participants included in the longitudinal analysis, 127 (95 cognitively normal and 32 progressed/impaired) had CSF data collected at the same visit as their initial CMRO2 scan. CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 were slightly higher and Aβ42/Aβ40 was slightly lower in the progressed/impaired group, but the differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). Figure S1 shows the distribution of CSF t‐tau, p‐tau181, and Aβ42/Aβ40 in the two diagnostic groups.

Among the cognitively normal participants (N = 95), a significant association was observed between the change in CMRO2 over time and initial levels of t‐tau (p = 0.002) or p‐tau181 (p = 0.003), but not with initial Aβ42/Aβ40 levels (p = 0.21) (Table 4). Specifically, participants with higher levels of t‐tau or p‐tau181 showed an increase in CMRO2, relative to those with lower levels. A similar although less pronounced association was observed when using t‐tau/Aβ42 (p = 0.03) or p‐tau181/Aβ42 (p = 0.06) as the CSF biomarker (Table S2). After binarizing t‐tau, p‐tau181, and Aβ42/Aβ40 levels using established clinical cutoff thresholds, 31 changes in CMRO2 over time remained significantly associated with t‐tau (β = 1.92 ± 0.80, p = 0.02) and p‐tau181 (β = 2.49 ± 0.71, p < 0.001). No association was observed between CMRO2 change and binarized Aβ42/Aβ40 levels (p = 0.57). When the cognitively normal participants were further stratified based on CSF amyloid positivity (Table S3), a significant association was observed between longitudinal changes in CMRO2 and baseline levels of t‐tau (p = 0.02) and p‐tau181 (p = 0.02) in the low CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 group, whereas no such associations were found in the high CSF Aβ42/Aβ40 group (Table S3). No associations were observed in the high plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 group (Table S4).

For the progressed/impaired group (N = 32), there were no significant associations between change in CMRO2 and initial levels of t‐tau (p = 0.13), p‐tau181 (p = 0.10), or Aβ42/Aβ40 (p = 0.98) (Table S5, Figure S2), although it should be noted that the coefficients had an opposite sign from those in the cognitively normal group. The main effect of time remained in this analysis.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated time‐dependent changes in brain oxygen metabolism in a prospective cohort across the clinical spectrum of AD, which, to our knowledge, represents the first longitudinal CMRO2 report to date. Brain metabolism was lower cross‐sectionally in participants with MCI/dementia compared to those with normal cognition, reproducing prior findings, using an MRI radiation‐free technique. In longitudinal analyses, the progressed/impaired group showed a metabolic decline over time. On the other hand, among cognitively normal participants who remained cognitively normal, brain metabolism trajectories differed by initial levels of CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181, especially in amyloid‐positive individuals. Participants with low CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 showed a relatively stable metabolic rate, whereas those with high CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 exhibited increases in brain metabolism over time.

A major finding of this study is the tau‐dependent increase in brain metabolism during the preclinical phase of AD. Specifically, among cognitively normal participants who remained cognitively normal, higher initial CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 levels were associated with an increase in CMRO2 over the following 5 years. This effect appears to be more pronounced in cognitively normals with low CSF/plasma Aβ42, which may represent individuals with preclinical AD. This finding adds to growing evidence that hypermetabolism precedes the clinical onset of AD. 10 , 32 It also suggests that brain hypermetabolism is related to the early accumulation of AD pathology. Prior studies reported similar patterns of hypermetabolism under AD‐related pathological conditions at the asymptomatic stage. For example, prior PET studies reported positive associations between the levels of tau and amyloid deposition and brain glucose metabolism in cognitively normal adults. 15 , 33 A previous report in autosomal dominant Alzheimer's patients reported that mutation carriers ∼25 years prior to their estimated age of onset of symptoms showed higher glucose uptake than non‐carriers, 34 revealing that brain hypermetabolism occurred in the earliest phases of AD due to AD‐related genetic factors. Additionally, given that metabolic and neural activity are correlated, 35 there are reports of tau‐related hyperactivation in the brain detected with functional MRI (fMRI), where increased hippocampal activity was associated with higher tau‐PET and CSF tau levels in cognitively normal older adults. 36 , 37 , 38 However, most prior studies were conducted in a cross‐sectional setting. Thus, it was not clear whether those with elevated pathological levels had high metabolism all along, due to, for example, genetic variations and body temperature, or there was an increase in metabolism later in life. The observations in the present study revealed that CMRO2 levels did not differ between high‐ and low‐tau groups at initial assessment but showed divergent trajectories over time. While CMRO2 and CMRglu measurements are based on different substrates, both seem to show evidence of heightened levels at the early stage of AD. A caveat of our study is that we did not observe an amyloid‐dependent difference in CMRO2 change. According to the ATN temporal trajectory, neurodegeneration/brain metabolism follows more closely tauopathy than amyloidosis. Additionally, it remains to be studied whether the high‐tau, CMRO2‐increasing participants are more susceptible to future cognitive decline.

It remains an open question as to which pathological factors contribute to hypermetabolism, as prior studies implicated diverse mechanisms, including genetic risk, tau aggregation, amyloid plaque, and tau and amyloid in their soluble forms. This study suggests that CSF tau may be one of the major contributors to hypermetabolism in early AD. Tau is a microtubule‐associated protein that binds and stabilizes neuronal microtubules. In the early stages of disease, tau exists primarily in its low‐molecular weight soluble form, which can be measured in CSF, as performed in this study. As AD progresses, soluble tau forms oligomers and eventually aggregates into insoluble neurofibrillary tangles, which are measured by PET imaging, 14 , 15 , 36 as in most prior studies. While insoluble tau aggregates predominate in late phases of neurodegenerative diseases, soluble tau is thought to be the most toxic species that modulates changes in neuronal function in the early stages of disease. 39 , 40 , 41 Accordingly, soluble tau assessed in CSF may represent a particularly relevant target for examining the effect of AD pathology on early metabolic changes. The relationship between tauopathy and elevated neural activity has also been suggested in several animal studies. 41 , 42

This study also allowed us to characterize CMRO2 changes in a group of participants with MCI and dementia. In contrast to the findings in the cognitively normal group, we observed that CMRO2 declined over time in cognitively impaired participants. This observation is consistent with prior FDG‐PET reports 43 and suggests that hypometabolism is a late event in the disease course. When accounting for CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181 levels, high‐tau individuals in the progressed/impaired group did not show a metabolic elevation. In fact, the impaired participants with high t‐tau and p‐tau181 levels showed a slightly faster, though non‐significant, decline in brain metabolism compared to low‐tau participants. It should also be noted that the main effect of follow‐up time on CMRO2 remained after accounting for t‐tau or p‐tau181. This may imply that, at a late disease stage, synaptic function is disrupted, and therefore brain metabolism decreases independently of CSF AD biomarkers, representing overt neurodegeneration in the disease process. 44 As part of future work, it will be of interest to examine whether changes in CMRO2 are associated with tau aggregation measured by tau‐PET, 45 which represents the predominant form of tau burden in late‐stage disease.

Although this study primarily focused on longitudinal CMRO2 findings, cross‐sectional comparisons were also conducted. Our observation of a lower CMRO2 in impaired participants was in good agreement with previous studies. 18 It should be noted, however, that there are considerable overlaps in CMRO2 levels between the normal and MCI/dementia groups. Further investigation by dividing the normal group into “cognitively normal” and “impaired but not MCI” participants revealed that the overlap was mainly attributed to the “impaired but not MCI” subgroup (Figure S3), in that they have a lower CMRO2 than the “cognitively normal” participants. Thus, single‐time‐point CMRO2 is not sufficient to be a clinically diagnostic biomarker for MCI.

This study also observed differences in CMRO2 between male and female participants. Compared to males, females had a higher CMRO2. The sex differences in CMRO2 are in agreement with prior cross‐sectional studies showing higher cerebral oxygen/glucose metabolism in females compared to males. 22 , 46 Females and males also showed different rates of change in CMRO2, separate from the pathological factors. One possible confounding factor is the hematocrit. Since the hematocrit values in this study were based on assumptions and hematocrit has an effect on CMRO2 estimation, our observations of male‐versus‐female differences may have some dependence on these assumptions. However, based on our simulations, the hematocrit assumptions cannot fully explain the observed sex differences in CMRO2. Ongoing work in our lab is focused on simultaneous estimations of CMRO2 and hematocrit using MRI, thereby avoiding assumptions and associated confounding factors.

A few technical points are worth noting. This study measured brain metabolism using a non‐invasive MRI technique. 17 This represents a major technical advantage compared to FDG‐PET, as the MRI technique does not involve ionizing radiation and can be repeated as frequently as needed without safety concerns. This MRI sequence can be completed in less than 5 min, thereby representing a cost‐effective approach to assessing brain metabolism. Together, these features enable longitudinal assessment of brain metabolic changes over extended follow‐up periods, particularly in individuals at the preclinical stage of disease. The MRI technique is also more quantitative in that it is capable of providing a physiological value in the units of micro‐mole oxygen per 100 g tissue per minute. Such quantification in FDG‐PET would require the continuing sampling of blood to provide an arterial input function. To our knowledge, very few FDG‐PET studies have performed blood sampling, and in those studies, the sample sizes tend to be small. 47

This study has a few limitations. First, 75% of the participants in our cohort had a family history of dementia, so our results or the effect size may not generalize to the community population. Second, we did not examine the association between changes in brain metabolism and cognitive performance. Since the majority of participants in this study were cognitively normal, their cognitive function changes slowly and we do not yet have a sufficient number of participants who progressed to MCI. Third, our follow‐up time of approximately 5 years has not allowed us to observe metabolic change during the entire course of the disease on an individual level. It would be important to conduct a study with a longer duration of follow‐up to observe whether the increasing CMRO2 trajectories of the high‐tau participants eventually decline with disease progression. Fourth, this study used CSF p‐tau181 and t‐tau; however, newer measures, such as p‐tau217, may represent a more accurate measure of tauopathy. Our team is in the process of conducting these assays and will report relevant findings in a future report.

A technical limitation of the study is that we only performed a global (and predominantly cortical tissue‐based) measurement of CMRO2. Several emerging MRI techniques have been proposed to assess regional CMRO2, 48 , 49 , 50 which may facilitate spatially specific investigations of the associations between AD pathology and brain physiological alterations. As these newer techniques are sufficiently validated, they should be applied to study longitudinal changes in regional brain oxygen metabolism. Given that AD pathology progresses in a region‐specific manner, it would be informative to investigate whether regional metabolic changes would follow the trajectory of tau or amyloid spread or manifest its own spatial pattern.

In conclusion, this study characterized longitudinal alterations in brain metabolism across a broad clinical spectrum of AD. Our findings suggest that brain metabolism follows a biphasic change during the course of the disease. Aside from the well‐known metabolic slowdown in the late stage of AD (i.e., hypometabolism), the brain exhibits a hypermetabolism during the preclinical phase that appears to be related to levels of CSF t‐tau and p‐tau181. These results offer new insights into the impact of tau on brain function at the asymptomatic stage of AD. Findings from this work may inform early therapeutic strategies for AD and also emphasize that interpretations of metabolic changes must account for disease stage and relevant pathological biomarkers.
