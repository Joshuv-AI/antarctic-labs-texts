Management of an intracranial meningioma in pregnancy in low resource setting: A case report and literature review
Authors: Rebecca Mwambegele, Henry Said, Paul Mwami, Raymond Makundi, Alvin Miranda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceMeningiomas are rare in pregnancy and craniotomies for tumour excision are non-obstetric surgeries that have a life-saving role in pregnant women. However, because of their rare occurrence, health facilities in resource limited areas may hesitate to provide surgical care due to maternal condition, and may therefore withhold necessary craniotomies due to minimal exposure or experience. This case report intends to show how resource-limited settings can perform craniotomies on pregnant women and have positive outcome for both mother and child.Case presentationWe review a case of a 28-year-old female in her early second trimester who presented with new onset seizures and was diagnosed with an anterior falx meningioma.Clinical discussionThis case highlights the challenges we faced throughout case management in resource limited setting, highlighting what can be done in the absence of intraoperative foetal monitoring.ConclusionResource limited areas should not withhold necessary craniotomies in pregnant women but instead conduct necessary pre, intra and post operative planning using the available resources in order to ensure a good outcome for both mother and child.

Introduction

Meningioma is one of the commonest benign tumour, representing a third of all tumours in the central nervous system [1]. It is twice more common in the female population more than male, and much more common among the older compared to the younger age groups [1]. This makes meningiomas extremely rare in pregnancy [2]. Diagnosis of intracranial space occupying lesions often occurs after presenting symptoms such as headaches and seizures [3], which can cause a significant delay in onset of treatment.

Current World Health Organization (WHO) protocols do not advocate for antenatal screening of intracranial space occupying lesions [4], conditions which although rare pose significant maternal and foetal risks, including death [5]. In pregnancy, due to hormonal changes, the rate of previously existing meningioma growth is higher and therefore can associate with symptoms that put the mother's health (and therefore pregnancy) at risk, necessitating its excision [6].

The ethical dilemma surrounding maternal and foetal research related risks causes them to be excluded in many studies [7], making evidence-based treatment much more rare as compared to other groups with similar pathologies. This study aims to review management and challenges in managing such cases in resource limited settings, including decision on when to operate and intraoperative foetal heart rate monitoring. This case has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [8].

Patient information

The patient was a 28 yr old female, G3P2L2 at 14 weeks GA, with no previous comorbidities. She presented with multiple episodes of new onset generalised tonic-clonic seizures associated with episodes of headache and vomiting. There were otherwise no positive CNS symptoms such as blurry vision, weakness/paralysis or impaired speech. There was also no familial history of seizures or hypertension and no history of hypertension during her previous pregnancies.

Clinical findings and main diagnosis

On examination, she was normotensive with an otherwise normal CNS exam. Foetal assessment by obstetric ultrasound showed a viable singleton foetus in utero with a normal heart rate and anatomy for age. The MRI done showed an extra-axial lesion on the anterior falx with dura tail, enhancing on contrast highly suggestive of meningioma. It measured 3.6 × 3.6 × 3.5 cm and had no surrounding oedema as well as no evidence of intra-tumoral haemorrhage or necrosis, and the rest of the brain was normal. This was the first neuroimaging that she had ever done in her life, therefore there was no way to ascertain if the tumour was present before her current pregnancy. Although seizures had resolved with medication, the patient still maintained a severe headache (rated by the Pain Numerical Rating Scale) which was persistent in spite of medication. Therapeutic interventions

Preoperative bloodwork was done; patient's haemoglobin was 11.6 g/dl with a normal renal and liver panel. Another obstetric ultrasound was done early morning before operation and revealed a normal intrauterine pregnancy. There were no facilities for intraoperative foetal monitoring. The surgery was done on the fifth day after admission after adequate patient optimization. Intraoperatively, patient was in the semi recumbent position to promote foetal-maternal circulation. There was no manipulation after the patient was initially positioned.

Drugs used for induction include pethidine, propofol and atracurium and patient was intubated with Endotracheal tube (ETT) size 6.5. She was maintained on sevoflurane with a minimum alveolar concentration (MAC) of 2. Atracurium and fentanyl were also used to maintain the patient throughout the surgery. Drugs such as suxamethonium were avoided due to its anaphylactic risk [9] and mannitol due to its risk of haemodynamic instability [10]. A tight control of the maternal blood pressure and heart rate was ensured to prevent episodes of hypotension/hypertension or bradycardia/tachycardia. Because we were not able to perform intraoperative foetal heart rate monitoring, we tried to ensure consistent hemodynamics of the mother as a reflection of foetal hemodynamics.

A bifrontal craniotomy was done, and tumour was found in the anterior falx invading the frontal bone, Simpson's grade II achieved. The procedure took a total of 2 h and 40 min, estimated blood loss was 400 cc and received two units of packed red blood cells, one intraoperatively and another post operatively. Outside of bleeding, there were no other intraoperative complications noted. Atropine and neostigmine were used for reversal and patient was extubated and sent to the ICU for close neuromonitoring. 18 h post operatively, another obstetric ultrasound was done and was normal.

Outcome and follow-up

After two days of ICU care, patient was observed in HDU and was later discharged from the hospital with oral antiseizures and routine clinic review after a total of 11 days of admission. Haemoglobin post operatively was 11.2 g/dl. Post operative imaging shows no tumour residue; and biopsy results show meningothelial meningioma WHO grade I. She was kept on intravenous progesterone therapy for five days after surgery to lower any risk of pregnancy loss. Following discharge, the patient also had routine visitations with both the neurosurgery and obstetrics clinic and was able to deliver vaginally at term a female baby weighing 2.4 kg. Currently, two years later, both the baby and mother are doing well. The patient is on daily antiseizures pending a drug holiday. Follow up CT scan shows complete tumour excision with no signs of recurrence. She is now under 6 month spaced follow ups in the neurosurgical clinic.

Discussion

Meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumours, which arise from arachnoid cap cells [11]. Incidence is higher in females to males by a three to one ratio [12]. Peak incidence is 45 yrs of age, although higher rates of occurrence have been noted in females within range of 40-60 yrs of age, with 90 % being benign, 6 % atypical and 2 % malignant [13]. It is estimated that there are 5.6 cases of meningiomas in pregnancy [6]. One study showed the average age of diagnosis for pregnant women is 32 yrs [14].

Meningiomas typically grow faster in pregnant compared to non-pregnant women, and this is due to increase in human placental lactogen (hPL) and prolactin (PRL) which stimulate tumour propagation and decrease in luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle stimulating hormone (FSH) which inhibit tumour propagation. Pregnancy also causes hemodynamic changes, particularly increased stroke volume and haematocrit count which promote tumoral blood supply and therefore increase tumour size [6]. The rise in progesterone during pregnancy has been a controversial phenomenon, because much lower rates have been found in post-menopausal women who have an increased incidence compared to the young population [6]. Some studies show that at least 3 % of autopsies of patients aged above 60 yrs have a meningioma [11].

Decision on timing for tumour excision in pregnant women is based on a number of factors. The most important factors we considered were the presenting symptoms, age of pregnancy and location of the tumour as well as tumour size and (expected) rate of growth. Presenting symptoms play a key role in making a surgical decision. One study showed that the most common presenting symptoms were visual related and cranial nerve palsies [14]. In our case, the patient experienced multiple episodes of seizures which although remained controlled with monotherapy, the risk for continued tumour growth, current volume of the tumour and persistency of symptoms was higher as she was on her early second trimester. She also lived in a region where neuroimaging was not available therefore it was difficult to observe tumour growth until delivery. It was also difficult to discharge as there was no neurosurgeon near her home-based community. We weighed it unethical and unsafe and therefore the best decision was to proceed with surgery.

Gestational age less than 12 weeks pose a danger to the foetus due to exposure to both antiepileptics and anaesthetics that may interfere with organogenesis. Some surgeons have preferred delivery prior to craniotomy, however this has been attached with lower survival rates for infants as compared to term pregnancy [12]. This is the reason why we chose to proceed with surgery while the pregnancy was still in utero.

A study showed that meningiomas on the skull base are likely to require excision [14], possibly due to their likelihood of causing noticeable symptoms. In some cases where pregnant women are diagnosed with intracranial space occupying lesions, symptoms can improve after delivery [15]. Generally, the preferred treatment is surgery [16]. The tumour size also needs to be put into consideration, and although there is no literature for preferred size above which necessitates removal in pregnant women regardless of presenting symptoms. Other factors such as maternal age at diagnosis and parity have no association with surgical outcome [17] and were therefore not considered in this scenario.

Although we were unable to perform intra operative foetal heart rate monitoring, this method is still encouraged for better foetal outcome. Neurological conditions are a common indication for non-obstetric surgeries in pregnant women and are often performed under general anaesthesia [2]. A variety of factors determine foetal monitoring recommendations, including the woman's preferences, gestational age of the pregnancy and situational specific risks [18]. The main aim for intrapartum foetal monitoring is to predict significant foetal hypoxia and institute timely intervention to avoid foetal injury [19]. Therefore, a multidisciplinary approach is necessary both pre, intra and post operative patient care.

Conclusion

Even in a resource limited setting, we advocate for craniotomy to excise meningiomas particularly when the patient is symptomatic and gestational age is at or above second trimester. A similar approach protocol can be used for other conditions such as traumatic brain injury [20] or hemmorhagic stroke in pregnant women where emergency surgery is unavoidable. Protocols relating to antenatal neuroimaging should be discussed especially among risk groups. It is possible to have positive outcome for both the mother and child if care is taken both during pre, intra and post operative preparation. A multidisciplinary approach is necessary to ensure the best possible outcome. An investment in intraoperative obstetric monitoring is crucial to observe and intervene for good perinatal outcome.
