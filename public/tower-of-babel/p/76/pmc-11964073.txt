Hypertension and Ventilatory Responses During Exercise in the Fitness Registry and the Importance of Exercise National Database (FRIEND)
Authors: Joseph C Watso, Austin T Robinson, Ross Arena, Matthew P Harber, Leonard A Kaminsky, Jonathan Myers
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA high minute ventilation/rate of carbon dioxide production (V̇E/V̇co 2) slope during exercise is prognostic for cardiovascular death. Recent data indicate that adults with either controlled or untreated primary hypertension, but not those with uncontrolled hypertension, exhibit a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise. However, the sample sizes were modest. Therefore, we used the Fitness Registry and the Importance of Exercise National Database to determine whether adults with hypertension, particularly those with controlled or untreated hypertension, exhibit higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slopes compared with adults without hypertension.Methods and ResultsUsing the Fitness Registry and the Importance of Exercise National Database, we isolated primary hypertension by excluding those with any disease other than hypertension or taking any medications other than antihypertension medications. We also excluded current smokers and those with obesity. The V̇E/V̇co 2 slope was determined during a peak cycling exercise test. All data are presented as median [interquartile range]. We compared groups using linear regression adjusted for age, male/female, and body mass index. The characteristics of the entire sample (n=4109) were age, 42 [18] years; 48% women; body mass index, 26 [4] kg/m2. The V̇E/V̇co 2 slope did not differ between adults with hypertension (n=1940; 24.7 [3.7]) compared with those without hypertension (n=2169; 24.9 [3.8]) (hypertension versus no hypertension, P=0.31; overall model: R 2=0.07, F4,4104=73.0; P<0.001). Further, the V̇E/V̇co 2 slope did not differ between adults with medication‐controlled hypertension (n=107; 24.4 [3.0]), untreated hypertension (n=1626; 24.8 [3.9]), uncontrolled hypertension (n=207; 24.8 [3.0]), or those without hypertension (n=2169; 24.9 [3.8]) (hypertension subgroup versus no hypertension, P≥0.06; overall model: R 2=0.07, F6,4102=49.6, P<0.001).ConclusionsPrimary hypertension is not associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in the Fitness Registry and the Importance of Exercise National Database.Keywords: cardiopulmonary exercise testing, cardiorespiratory fitness, cardiovascular disease, respiratorySubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Exercise, Pathophysiology, Exercise Testing, Blood Pressure

Research Perspective

What Is New?

In the largest cohort published to date (n=4109), primary hypertension was not associated with greater ventilation to carbon dioxide production slope during cardiopulmonary exercise testing on an upright cycle; further, neither resting systolic nor diastolic blood pressure was associated with the ventilation to carbon dioxide production slope.

Exercise testing provides prognostic value for disease risk stratification. 1 The rate of carbon dioxide production (V̇co 2) and pulmonary minute ventilation (V̇E) increase in an exercise intensity–dependent manner. The rise in V̇E over the rise in V̇co 2 throughout exercise (V̇E/V̇co 2 slope 1 ) provides predictive information for disease risk in certain populations. An augmented V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in patients with heart failure was first reported in 1982 2 and confirmed in subsequent publications. 3 , 4 This ventilation‐to‐perfusion mismatch (ie, disproportionately elevated ventilation for a given metabolic demand) is thought to be related to dysfunction of the lungs, pulmonary resistance arteries, or pulmonary capillary bed. 4 Regarding prognostic value, a progressively greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope is associated with higher cardiovascular hospitalization/death and all‐cause death in adults with heart failure 5 , 6 , 7 and pulmonary arterial hypertension. 8 To summarize, abnormal ventilatory responses during exercise are present in multiple clinical populations, are related to exercise intolerance, and are prognostic for future mortality risk. However, knowledge gaps remain regarding which conditions influence this prognostic variable.

Adults with systemic arterial hypertension have an elevated risk for future risk of heart failure, cardiovascular death, and all‐cause death. 9 However, limited information is available on whether hypertension is associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. Further, it is unclear whether certain hypertension subgroups exhibit greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. For example, adults with uncontrolled hypertension have the greatest all‐cause and cardiovascular death when compared with adults with untreated hypertension (second‐highest risk group), controlled hypertension (third‐highest risk group), and no hypertension (index group). 10

Individuals with hypertension may exhibit a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise due to several interrelated factors. For example, increased sympathetic activity may enhance respiratory drive. 11 Early manifestations of pulmonary vasculature dysfunction and left ventricular dysfunction 12 , 13 may impair gas exchange efficiency, necessitating greater ventilation. Along these lines, recently published data suggest that adults with either controlled or untreated primary hypertension, but not those with uncontrolled hypertension, exhibit a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during cycling exercise. 14 However, an important limitation of this recent report is that the sample sizes were modest (13–22 adults per hypertension subgroup). Thus, there is a need for larger studies to determine whether hypertension and specific subgroups of hypertension are associated with a greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. Therefore, we sought to test the primary hypothesis that adults with hypertension have higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slopes during cycling exercise compared with adults without hypertension in a larger cohort. Additionally, we sought to test the secondary hypothesis that adults with controlled or untreated, but not uncontrolled, hypertension have higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slopes compared with adults without hypertension on the basis of past work. 14 Such information could inform us on which, if any, hypertension phenotypes (eg, controlled versus uncontrolled) exhibit impaired ventilatory responses during exercise to guide exercise training prescription and risk stratification.

Methods

The data supporting this study's findings are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Database

A multi‐institution collaboration called the Fitness Registry and the Importance of Exercise National Database (FRIEND) was established in 2014 by the Cardiorespiratory Fitness Advisory Board to establish normative values for physiological responses during exercise testing across the adult life span. 1 Briefly, laboratories experienced in cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) with valid and reliable testing procedures joined the FRIEND. This analysis includes tests from 1992 to 2021. Although there were variations in equipment, protocols, and procedures, all participating laboratories completed testing consistent with exercise testing recommendations. 15 , 16 , 17 Each contributing laboratory obtained local research ethics board approval before submitting deidentified, coded data to the data coordinating center at Ball State University, which has institutional review board approval for maintaining the database as a core laboratory. Depending on the data collection site, consent was provided or waived for each participant.

Participants

Participants self‐reported their age, male/female, race/ethnicity, the presence of chronic disease, smoking status, and current medication usage. For the present analysis, we excluded those with current smoking status, those aged <18 years, those with a pathological exercise test indication (eg, dyspnea upon exertion), and those reporting any medication usage other than antihypertensive medications. We also excluded those with coronary artery disease, stroke, peripheral artery disease, heart failure, any cardiomyopathies, valvular disease, hyperlipidemia, cancer, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, endocrine conditions, liver disease, neurological disease, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and any pulmonary restrictive disease. Before testing, participant body mass and height were measured to calculate body mass index (BMI). We excluded those with a BMI >30 kg/m2 because obesity independently affects the V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. 18 Resting blood pressure was measured in the seated position in the laboratory (ie, clinical) setting.

Exercise Mode

We selected upright cycling as the exercise mode for this analysis because (1) the physiological responses between cycling and treadmill CPETs vary; (2) the only other publication examining hypertension and V̇E/V̇co 2 slope used cycling exercise 14 ; (3) there are already 2 published reports from the FRIEND that generated age‐dependent reference standards for the V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during treadmill exercise testing among adults without hypertension 19 , 20 ; (4) the FRIEND had considerably more cycling tests available for analysis than treadmill tests after filtering on the basis of inclusion criteria; and (5) the test protocols on cycle ergometers progressively increase wattage, whereas treadmill test protocols manipulate grade and velocity to varying extents, which could have added unnecessary variability to exercise responses that are not a result of hypertension.

Exercise Testing

During exercise testing, V̇E and expired gases (eg, O2 and CO2) were measured during a CPET on an upright cycle ergometer. For the present analysis, we excluded those with a peak respiratory exchange ratio (RER; ie, V̇co 2/rate of oxygen consumption) <1.00. Peak rate of oxygen consumption was determined from the last 30 or 60 seconds of testing. As previously done, 19 V̇E and V̇co 2 data from the initiation of exercise to peak exercise were used to calculate the V̇E/V̇co 2 slope via least squares linear regression (y=mx+b; m = slope).

Data and Statistical Analysis

The a priori power analysis using G*Power version 3.1.9.4 to test the difference between 2 independent groups (hypertension versus nonhypertension) with Cohen's d of 0.85 (based on a past report 14 ), 1β of 0.99, and α of 0.05 required a minimum total sample size of 104 participants. For the present manuscript, we employed nonparametric testing for all analyses because the data in at least 1 group of interest for each variable were not normally distributed (Shapiro–Wilk test of normality P<0.05). Accordingly, we present data as median (interquartile range).

We split groups dichotomously into those with or without hypertension for the first classification structure. Adults were classified as having hypertension if they had a systolic blood pressure of ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mm Hg, reported a current diagnosis of hypertension, or reported current use of an antihypertensive medication consistent with the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Task Force on Clinical Practice Guidelines. 21 We compared group characteristics using Mann–Whitney U tests (rank biserial correlation was the effect size estimate) and χ2 tests (Cramer's V was the effect size estimate).

To address our primary hypothesis, we compared the V̇E/V̇co 2 slope between adults with and without hypertension. We also used linear regression to examine the association between the hypertension group and V̇E/V̇co 2 slope with the covariates age, male/female, and BMI (see the Covariate Selection section).

For the second classification structure, we retained a group without hypertension and split the hypertension group into 3 subgroups, for a total of 4 groups. The hypertension subgroups included those with controlled hypertension, untreated hypertension, and uncontrolled hypertension. We included adults using at least 1 antihypertensive medication with a blood pressure <130/80 mm Hg in the controlled hypertension group. We included adults reporting a present diagnosis of hypertension or having blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg and without current antihypertensive medication usage in the untreated hypertension group. We included adults using antihypertensive medication with a blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg in the uncontrolled hypertension group. We compared group characteristics using the Kruskal–Wallis tests (ε2 was the effect size estimate) with Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner post hoc pairwise comparisons and χ2 tests.

To address our secondary hypothesis, we compared the V̇E/V̇co 2 slope between adults without hypertension and hypertension subgroups using Kruskal–Wallis tests with Dwass‐Steel‐Critchlow‐Fligner post hoc pairwise comparisons. We also used linear regression to examine the association between the subgroups and V̇E/V̇co 2 slope with covariate adjustment. We also used a χ2 test to compare group proportions of V̇E/V̇co 2 values >45 based on previous work suggesting that this cutoff is a strong prognostic indicator for mortality risk in patients with heart failure. 5 On an exploratory basis, we used linear regression to examine the association between each medication (eg, β blocker, calcium channel blocker) and V̇E/V̇co 2 slope with covariate adjustment. Finally, we repeated this analysis using all possible medication combinations (eg, no medication, β blocker only, β blocker and calcium channel blocker).

Additionally, we used Spearman's rank partial correlation to examine the relation between resting and peak blood pressure versus V̇E/V̇co 2 slope among (1) adults using antihypertensive medication(s); and (2) adults not using antihypertensive medication(s) regardless of the presence of hypertension in unadjusted and adjusted models.

Throughout the results, we calculated effect sizes to aid with interpretation in addition to the calculated P values. 22 , 23 We interpreted rank‐biserial correlation effect sizes as small (0–0.19), medium (0.20–0.29), large (0.30–0.39), and very large (0.40–1). 24 We interpreted Cramer's V effect sizes as small, medium, and large depending on the degree of freedom and Cramer's V value. 25 We interpreted ε2 effect sizes as negligible (<0.01), weak (≥0.01 and <0.04), moderate (≥0.04 and <0.16), relatively strong (≥0.16 and <0.36), strong (≥0.36 and <0.64), and very strong (≥0.64). 26 We analyzed data using Jamovi (an internationally developed open‐source project 27 ) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.1 for Windows; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Covariate Selection

We included age as a covariate because blood pressure increases with age 28 and aging is related to increased V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. 20 , 29 We included the male/female ratio as a covariate because it is associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. 19 , 20 , 29 We included BMI as a covariate because it is related to V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. 19 , 20 We considered cardiorespiratory fitness as a covariate because it is related to V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. 19 , 20 , 29 , 30

Results

Participants

The characteristics of the entire sample (n=4109) were age, 42 [18] years old; 48% female; cardiorespiratory fitness, 28.7 [11.3] mLO2/kg per min; and BMI, 26 [4] kg/m2. The testing locations were as follows: 7% (n=293) from Canada, <1% (n=3) unknown, 6% (n=237) from Switzerland, 86% (n=3523) from the United States, and 1% (n=53) from the US Virgin Islands.

Hypertension and V̇E/V̇co 2 Slope

In an unadjusted analysis, adults with hypertension had a lower V̇E/V̇co 2 slope than adults without hypertension (Figure [A]), but the effect size was small. Further, the V̇E/V̇co 2 slope did not differ between adults without hypertension and the 3 hypertension subgroups in unadjusted analysis (Figure [B]). The moderate/medium effect sizes between groups for age and BMI (Table 1; 2‐group comparisons) and medium/moderate effect sizes between groups for age, male/female ratio, and cardiorespiratory fitness (Table 1; 4‐group comparisons) supported our decision to include them as covariates in adjusted models. However, we removed cardiorespiratory fitness because it was not a significant covariate (P=0.23 in the 2‐group analysis and P=0.39 in the 4‐group analysis). Further, due to concerns about multicollinearity (body mass: variance inflation factor=2.68 in the 2‐group analysis and 1.64 in the 4‐group analysis), we did not include body mass as a covariate because “kg” was already included in the model as a component of BMI (kg/m2). In a covariate‐adjusted analysis, neither the presence of hypertension (Table 2) nor any specific hypertension subgroup (Table 3) was significantly associated with a higher or lower V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. Finally, the proportion of adults with a V̇E/V̇co 2 slope >45 did not differ between hypertension subgroups and those without hypertension (0%–0.4% prevalence per group; χ2=1.48, P=0.69, Cramer's V=0.02).

V̇E/V̇co 2 Slope Versus Measured Blood Pressure

Neither resting systolic nor diastolic blood pressure was significantly correlated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in unadjusted or covariate‐adjusted analyses among those with or without current use of antihypertensive medication(s) (ρ=−0.02 to 0.10, P=0.07–0.86). Neither peak systolic nor diastolic blood pressure was significantly correlated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in unadjusted or covariate‐adjusted analyses among those with current use of antihypertensive medication(s) (ρ=−0.04 to 0.04, P=0.43 to 0.59). Peak systolic (ρ=−0.04, P=0.02) and diastolic (ρ=0.08, P<0.01) blood pressure were negligibly correlated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in unadjusted analyses among those without current use of antihypertensive medication(s). While systolic (ρ=−0.02, P=0.18) was not, peak diastolic blood pressure (ρ=0.06, P<0.01) remained significantly, yet negligibly, correlated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope after covariate adjustment.

Exploring Effects of Antihypertensive Medication(s)

The V̇E/V̇CO2 slope did not differ between adults using different medication categories (P>0.13 for all 4 medication categories available; overall model: R2=0.07, F7,4100=42.7, P<0.001). Likewise, the V̇E/V̇CO2 slope did not differ between adults using all possible medication combinations (P>0.08 for all 13 medication combinations observed in the cohort; overall model: R 2=0.07, F16,4092=18.9, P<0.001).

Discussion

There are several findings from this analysis of >4000 upright cycling CPETs. First, primary hypertension was not associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise. Second, adults with controlled hypertension, untreated hypertension, or uncontrolled hypertension did not have a greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope when compared with adults without hypertension. Third, the prevalence of V̇E/V̇co 2 slope values >45 did not differ between hypertension subgroups and those without hypertension. Fourth, neither resting systolic nor diastolic blood pressure was significantly associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in those using or not using antihypertensive medication(s). Fifth, peak exercise diastolic, but not systolic, blood pressure was negligibly positively associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in those not using antihypertensive medication(s). Sixth, our exploratory analyses suggested that no specific antihypertensive medication or combination of antihypertensive medication(s) was associated with a greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. To summarize, data from this international‐based FRIEND suggest that primary hypertension of any form is not associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope.

Discordance From Published Data in Hypertension

We found that hypertension was not associated with a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in the FRIEND using data from 4109 adults who completed CPETs on an upright cycle ergometer. This finding contradicts our hypothesis, which was based on a prior publication that examined the associations between hypertension and a greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise on an upright cycle ergometer in 79 adults. 14 While participants were matched for age, male/female, BMI, fitness, and peak RER, it is unclear whether individuals were excluded if they had any diagnosis other than hypertension, any current non‐antihypertensive medication use, a BMI >30 kg/m2, or a peak RER below any specific cutoff (eg, 1.00) for peak exercise test validity. Further, while a P value of 0.13 was observed when comparing the group means for BMI, the associated effect size (Cohen's d=0.5) would be considered moderate. 31 Therefore, it is possible that BMI in this past report partly confounded the results despite attempting to match BMI in those without hypertension (n=24) and with hypertension (n=55). The potential confounders that could explain the discordant results include BMI, existing cardiopulmonary disease, other medication use, including adults with obesity, and adults with a peak RER <1.00.

Notably, while we did not attempt to match groups with and without hypertension for any variable, we did exclude those with any existing cardiopulmonary (or other) disease, those with any other medication use, and those with a peak RER <1.00. Additionally, we included age, male/female ratio, and BMI as covariates in our statistical analyses. Moreover, the present analysis had more than sufficient statistical power to detect a difference in V̇E/V̇co 2 slope between those with and those without hypertension. Thus, the fact that V̇E/V̇co 2 slope was not associated with hypertension in covariate‐adjusted models when examined as a categorical yes/no variable, as a continuous variable (ie, absolute systolic and diastolic blood pressure), or in regression analyses provides strong confidence in the present outcomes. Another methodological difference between the present study and published work 14 was how blood pressure was assessed. In the present work, blood pressure was measured in a laboratory setting. In contrast, the past report used ambulatory blood pressure monitoring during the day, 14 which could be viewed as more rigorous and ecologically valid. While out‐of‐office measurements are recommended to confirm the diagnosis of hypertension, following standard guidelines (eg, the participant is relaxed) is sufficient to obtain accurate data and diagnose hypertension. 21 Additionally, the present cohort was primarily based in North America (94%), whereas the existing publication was based solely in the United Kingdom. 14 Therefore, based on various factors, we cannot rule out the possibility of regional differences in the relation between hypertension and V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. Thus, given the prognostic value of an abnormal V̇E/V̇co 2 slope among various clinical populations, 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 there is sufficient rationale for others to confirm the present findings in other cohorts of adults with hypertension.

Discordance From Published Data in Hypertension Subgroups

We found that neither controlled hypertension, untreated hypertension, nor uncontrolled hypertension was associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. This finding was counter to our hypothesis that was based on a prior publication that reported a greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope in adults with either controlled or untreated, but not uncontrolled, hypertension. 14 Many possible reasons for the discrepancy in the present findings from this past report were discussed in the Discordance From Published Data in Hypertension section. Similar to the general 2‐group analysis, the present work had subgroups (n=107–1626) that were larger than the past report (n=13–22). 14 Additionally, while the peak rate of oxygen consumption (a variable that did not significantly affect our covariate‐adjusted statistical outcomes) did not vary between hypertension subgroups in the past work, 14 whether age, male/female ratio, or BMI varied between hypertension subgroups is unknown. Thus, based on the information provided in the Covariate Selection section, the past hypertension subgroup results could have been partly confounded by these or other variables. We initially found it counterintuitive that adults with uncontrolled hypertension in past work were the only subgroup without a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. Our skepticism was because uncontrolled hypertension is associated with the highest risk for future cardiovascular and all‐cause death. 10 Thus, if a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope is associated with a high mortality risk and uncontrolled hypertension is associated with a high mortality risk, we would expect that adults with uncontrolled hypertension, in particular, would be at risk for greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise. However, neither the past study 14 nor the present data support this idea.

Potential Physiological Links Between Hypertension and V̇E/V̇co 2 Slope

Adults with hypertension have an elevated risk for future risk of heart failure (a condition strongly associated with a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope), cardiovascular death, and all‐cause death. 9 Further, adults with hypertension exhibit endothelial dysfunction in peripheral vascular arteries. 32 While not assessed in the present study, endothelial dysfunction in the pulmonary resistance arteries (eg, patients with heart failure or pulmonary arterial hypertension) is associated with a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. 2 , 3 , 4 , 33 , 34 , 35 Thus, we reasoned that it is plausible that those with hypertension could have decrements in pulmonary artery endothelial function and blood flow regulation and subsequently lower pulmonary capillary perfusion and greater physiological dead space to increase ventilation relative to metabolic demands. Therefore, despite the lack of an association between primary hypertension and V̇E/V̇co 2 slope, there may be a rationale to address this mechanistic knowledge gap in future research.

Limitations

As already alluded to, the strongest characterization of an individual's blood pressure would be derived from awake ambulatory monitoring to confirm hypertension. This, in combination with their medical history, could increase the rigor of hypertension diagnosis and hypertension subgroup assignment. The presence of any chronic disease (eg, hypertension) and the current use of medication (eg, diuretic) is limited to self‐report at a single time point. Thus, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility of inaccurate categorization when not dependent on measured blood pressure (eg, if blood pressure was normal, then they would be placed in the hypertension group on the basis of self‐reported information) nor how long they have had hypertension. However, this limitation occurs in some human‐based research when the patient's medical records are inaccessible to researchers. Regarding our secondary hypothesis in hypertension subgroups, the sample sizes in the present analysis ranged widely, from 107 to 1626. Thus, having groups more similar in size could potentially influence the strength of associations with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope.

Perspectives and Significance

Systemic arterial hypertension is a leading risk factor for developing heart failure. Heart failure is strongly associated with an elevated V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise, which is prognostic for greater future mortality risk. Therefore, understanding whether adults with hypertension exhibit a higher V̇E/V̇co 2 slope could have important clinical implications. For example, if specific hypertension phenotypes exhibit elevated V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise, this would suggest a potentially greater future mortality risk and warrant earlier and more aggressive interventions (eg, exercise training) to reduce risk in such groups. The limited data available suggest that those with primary hypertension, mainly controlled and untreated forms, have a greater V̇E/V̇co 2 slope. However, the present covariate‐adjusted analyses from a large, international database suggest that primary hypertension of any form is not associated with V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise among otherwise apparently healthy adults. In the largest cohort published to date, we did not detect any significant associations between systemic arterial hypertension and V̇E/V̇co 2 slope during exercise.
