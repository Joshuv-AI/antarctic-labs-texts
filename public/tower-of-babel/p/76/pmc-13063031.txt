Three-stage anterior lumbar interbody fusion revision surgery for transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion cage retrieval: surgical steps and preliminary results
Authors: Francesco Caiazzo, Daniel Alveal-Mellado, Lucas Capo, Paula Lavezzolo, Judith Salat-Batlle, Beatriz Iguácel-Duarte, Juan Bago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRevising failed transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) presents significant surgical challenges, primarily due to the presence of dense scar tissue and pre-existing cage placement. While anterior surgical approaches offer superior visualization for cage retrieval, existing posterior instrumentation often complicates adequate intervertebral distraction, which is crucial for both comprehensive cage removal and effective deformity correction. Here, we detail a three-stage surgical strategy (back-front-back) adopted in Instituto Quirúrgico Spanò to overcome these inherent limitations. Stage 1 involves the careful removal of existing posterior rods and release of the posterior tension band to re-establish segmental mobility. Stage 2 proceeds with an anterior approach to facilitate the precise removal of the failed interbody cage, meticulous preparation of the endplates, and the subsequent implantation of a new, hyperlordotic anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) cage, essential for optimal restoration of sagittal alignment. Finally, stage 3 focuses on definitive posterior stabilization using new pedicle screws and rods. To assess the safety and efficacy of this technique, we conducted a retrospective review of 11 consecutive patients who underwent revision surgery, presenting with conditions such as pseudarthrosis, cage subsidence, or sagittal malalignment. The three-stage procedure was technically successful in all 11 patients, with no major complications reported at the one-year follow-up. The mean surgical time recorded was 258 minutes, with an average estimated blood loss of 493 mL. Postoperative imaging studies consistently confirmed satisfactory cage placement in all treated cases, demonstrating the viability and promising outcomes of this comprehensive surgical approach.Keywords: Transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF), revision surgery, anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF)

• Considering an initial posterior-release approach could enhance the safety of TLIF cage extraction by mitigating excessive torsional forces on the posterior column while it remains instrumented.

Introduction

Transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) is a common surgical procedure for spinal fixation and fusion, utilized to treat various lumbar conditions, including symptomatic degenerative disc disease and spondylolisthesis (1,2). While literature reports a high fusion rate after surgery (3,4), TLIF can lead to complications such as pseudarthrosis, cage migration or protrusion, spinal deformity due to loss of lumbar lordosis, infection, and delayed wound healing (5,6). In such instances, revision surgery involving cage removal is strongly advised.

Revision surgery poses significant challenges. These stem from technical complexities related to cage removal, including restricted access due to scar tissue from previous surgeries and difficulties in cage extraction caused by subsidence or migration (7). Thus, while a posterior approach is a potential reintervention strategy, it has been associated with complications such as dural tears and suboptimal sagittal alignment improvement (8-10), largely due to the restricted surgical field it provides. As a result, alternative strategies like the lateral and anterior approaches have gained significant favor (11,12). These methods offer broader access for cage retrieval, thereby minimizing tissue damage.

The anterior-posterior approach, which involves anterior cage retrieval followed by posterior instrumentation, is of particular interest due to its association with optimal radiographic and clinical outcomes (9,13). Nevertheless, pre-existing posterior instrumentation or solid fusion can present challenges, as they restrict the necessary anterior interbody distraction required for inserting a new, appropriately sized cage. In such cases, a preliminary posterior release may facilitate cage removal and minimize tissue damage.

Despite the potential benefit of posterior release, few reports have adopted this strategy, and its effectiveness remains unproven. We contend that a detailed account of the planning and maneuvers for reintervention after failed TLIF surgery would help reduce complications, standardize procedures, and enhance reproducibility.

The aim of this work is to describe a three-stage (back-front-back) open surgical technique for the removal of a failed TLIF cage, featuring preliminary posterior instrumentation loosening to facilitate the subsequent anterior approach. Additionally, the safety of the procedure is evaluated by identifying major complications in a cohort of individuals who underwent the procedure in Instituto Quirúrgico Spanò. We present this article in accordance with the SUPER reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-160/rc).

Preoperative preparations and requirements

This three-stage technique for removing failed TLIF cages in revision scenarios was developed and performed in the operating rooms of Instituto Quirúrgico Spanò at Sagrada Familia Clinic, Barcelona, Spain. Indications include mechanical failure of TLIF constructs such as cage migration, screw loosening, rod breakage, pseudarthrosis, or persistent pain associated with non-union.

Individual assessment of general and relative contraindications for this procedure is essential and may vary based on surgeon experience. However, conditions that render anterior access technically unfeasible, such as vascular deficiency, a history of respiratory failure, or previous radiation therapy in the aorto-iliac lymph node chains, must unequivocally contraindicate the surgery (14).

Preoperative imaging is mandatory to guide surgical planning. Computed tomography (CT) is essential to evaluate the condition of the existing instrumentation, presence of bony fusion or screw loosening, and the relationship of the cage with the vertebral endplates. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is required to assess the neural elements and detect possible injury or compromise of adjacent segments. In addition, sagittal full-body standing radiographs provide valuable information on sagittal alignment, crucial for reconstructive planning following cage retrieval.

Efficient intraoperative planning necessitates careful consideration of position changes between surgical stages, optimizing both efficiency and sterility. In Instituto Quirúrgico Spanò, we use two L-configured auxiliary tables and a consistently sterile mayo stand (Figure 1). The first auxiliary table maintains sterility throughout positional changes and contains non-commercially provided instruments. The second table is initially prepared with instruments for hardware removal and the anterior approach, including navigation instruments if pedicle screw placement is anticipated, to minimize delays. The mayo stand consistently holds essential sterile instruments like suction tubing, a scalpel, and bipolar forceps. During subsequent position changes, anterior approach instruments are removed from auxiliary table 1, and, if not already done, navigation sets, screws, and rods are prepared. Readily available patient protection materials are crucial for safe and rapid repositioning. Effective interdisciplinary collaboration among all operating room personnel, including surgeons, scrub nurses, anesthesiologists, and neurophysiologists is paramount to ensuring patient safety and minimizing downtime during transitions.

Step-by-step description

Stage 1

With the patient positioned prone on a radiolucent table, the main surgeon and assistant stand on opposite sides of the patient, providing optimal bilateral access and visualization of the surgical field. A midline incision is made along the previous scar, followed by subperiosteal dissection to expose the existing posterior instrumentation at the index level (Figure 2). After removal of the connecting rods, the previously inserted pedicle screws are carefully inspected. Stable screws are preserved, whereas loose or lytic screws are extracted. This stage focuses on releasing the posterior tension band, ensuring adequate segmental mobility at the index level. In cases of a solid fusion mass, an osteotomy of the fused segment is performed to restore motion (Figure 2). The stage concludes with irrigation and a standard layered closure over the exposed or newly inserted screw heads.

Stage 1 posterior release for TLIF cage retrieval. (A,B) Subperiosteal dissection is performed to fully expose the existing posterior instrumentation. (C) An osteotomy of the fusion mass is performed to re-establish segmental mobility, a necessary step when a solid fusion is present. TLIF, transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion.

Stage 2

The patient is carefully repositioned into the supine position for the anterior phase. A retroperitoneal approach to the anterior lumbar spine is utilized via a midline infraumbilical incision, a technique previously described (15). Once self-retaining retractors expose the operative segment, a wide anterior annulotomy is performed.

The failed TLIF cage is then meticulously exposed and removed (Figure 3). In doing so, progressive distraction of the segment is crucial to prevent vertebral endplate fracture or damage. For this, we employ wedge-shaped distractors, known as ‘bullets’, which have increasing heights (Figure 4). These are positioned using an inserter handle. Thus, a gradual, controlled distraction of the segment is carried out with minimal endplate stress.

The failed TLIF cage is retrieved using a slap hammer (D,E). A trial ALIF cage (F) is then inserted to verify fit and alignment before implantation of the definitive ALIF cage. ALIF, anterior lumbar interbody fusion; TLIF, transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion.

Surgical instruments used during TLIF cage revision surgery. T-handle inserter with interchangeable bullet spacers (A). Slap hammer for implant removal (B). Magnified view of bullet spacers showing different dimensions and angulations (C). All instruments provided by Globus Medical. TLIF, transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion.

Unilateral distraction isolates the TLIF cage, facilitating access to release surrounding fibrosis using a Cobb elevator and chisels.

Once mobilized, the cage is extracted with a forceful, single-motion maneuver using a slap hammer. This maneuver enables direct and safe decompression of the spinal canal and neural foramina from the anterior corridor. After thorough preparation of the vertebral endplates, a trial cage is tested and then a new anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) cage with pre-planned dimensions and lordotic angle is securely implanted to restore disc height. Then, the new cage position is evaluated through C-arm fluoroscopy (Cios Spin, Siemens, Germany).

The stage concludes after meticulous hemostasis, copious irrigation with saline solution mixed with vancomycin, and standard layered wound closure.

Stage 3

For definitive posterior stabilization, the patient is repositioned prone. The posterior wound is reopened, and appropriately sized pedicle screws are inserted into decorticated tracts to promote healing (if not removed in stage 1). A CT-based navigation system is used to improve accuracy of pedicle screw placement (Figure 5). To facilitate a solid arthrodesis, bone graft chips are carefully packed medially and laterally to the pedicle screw heads, building the fusion mass. Custom-contoured rods matching the re-established lumbar lordosis are then locked to the screws with appropriate compression applied across the construct. Rods and cage position are verified through sagittal and anteroposterior fluoroscopy (Figure 6). Finally, after ensuring hemostasis, bilateral closed-suction drains are placed, and the wound is closed in layers, typically with surgical staples for the skin.

Postoperative considerations and tasks

Following surgery, the patient remains in the recovery unit for approximately 2 hours and is then transferred to the ward once fully awake. Oral intake is resumed 6 hours after awakening, unless intraoperative peritoneal rupture or intestinal manipulation has occurred, in which case tolerance is delayed until clinically safe.

Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin is initiated on the first postoperative day. While the duration is tailored to individual patient risk factors, Instituto Quirúrgico Spanò’s standard is to maintain prophylaxis for up to 1 week after hospital discharge.

Mobilization should begin early, typically within 24 hours, with assisted standing and ambulation once hemodynamic stability is confirmed.

Patients are instructed to wear a lumbar brace for 6 weeks, serving primarily as a proprioceptive reminder to limit excessive trunk motion and strenuous activity during the early postoperative period.

Postoperative radiological follow-up involves an immediate standing full spine radiograph during hospitalization, followed by another at 6 weeks post-surgery. Dynamic lumbar radiographs are performed at 3 months to assess implant integrity and rule out early signs of hardware failure or instability. Subsequently, full-spine radiographs are taken at 6 and 12 months. At the 1-year mark, both CT and MRI scans are conducted to evaluate fusion, alignment, and the condition of adjacent segments.

To evaluate the safety of the procedure, we conducted a retrospective analysis of Instituto Quirúrgico Spanò’s database, spanning from October 2021 to July 2024, to identify patients who underwent TLIF revision surgery via a 3-stage procedure. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital de Mataró/Consorci Sanitari del Maresme (No. CEIm-51/23). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants included in the study. Eleven patients met the inclusion criteria, with 8 (72.7%) being female and the mean age being 50.9 years.

The primary indications for surgery were symptomatic pseudarthrosis (90.9%), screw loosening (36.4%), cage subsidence (54.5%), and cage migration (18.1%) (Table 1). Surgical parameters showed an average operation time of 258.2±51.7 minutes, blood loss of 492.7±391.3 mL, and hospital stay of 4±2.4 days.

Discussion

This technical note describes a staged, three-position surgical strategy for the salvage of a failed TLIF. The foundational principle of this technique is the release of the posterior tension band to achieve better vertebral body distraction and surgical maneuverability during the subsequent anterior cage retrieval. Our preliminary results suggest this approach is a safe and effective method, with no major complications.

Patients requiring revision surgery for conditions such as pseudarthrosis or cage migration are frequently compromised by damaged vertebral endplates (16). This anatomical vulnerability requires wide surgical exposure and controlled intervertebral distraction during the anterior approach to minimize the risk of further injury. While the principle of anterior distraction is recognized (17) and combined anterior-posterior approaches are well-documented (9,18-20), these strategies typically contend with the resistance of an instrumented posterior column. The unique contribution of our technique is the strategic releasing of this posterior constraint before the anterior procedure—a critical preparatory step that, to our knowledge, has not been formally described for this indication.

The concept of a three-stage “back-front-back” procedure is mentioned by Derman and Singh (12) as a theoretical option for posteriorly located implants, though they cited prolonged operative time and patient repositioning as significant drawbacks. The Instituto Quirúrgico Spanò’s experience directly addresses this concern. In our series of 11 consecutive cases, the mean surgical time was 258 (±51) minutes, a duration comparable to that reported for standard two-stage or single-position ALIF revisions (7). Thus, with a refined and efficient institutional protocol, the logistical challenges of a three-stage procedure can be effectively overcome.

Beyond simply exchanging cages, a key aim of this revision strategy is to enhance spinopelvic parameters. Our protocol therefore includes thorough preoperative planning, involving an analysis of spinopelvic parameters and the Global Alignment and Proportion (GAP) score (21), to tailor surgical goals. This detailed planning is not just theoretical; it directly guides the choice of a hyperlordotic ALIF cage designed to achieve the desired correction. Our initial findings indicate that this planning-led method significantly improves sagittal alignment (Table 2). This aligns with extensive research showing a correlation between restoring sagittal harmony and long-term clinical success (7,22,23).

Intervertebral distraction with bullet-tipped trial spacers achieved desired correction in all cases, avoiding aggressive maneuvers like partial corpectomies, which other authors have described to mobilize failed implants (12). This suggests less aggressive instrumentation can be effective in a properly liberated spinal segment, potentially reducing iatrogenic endplate injury risk.

Although detailed, this descriptive, retrospective study has limitations due to its small sample size, hindering robust statistical analysis and generalizability. The 1-year follow-up is insufficient for assessing long-term outcomes like fusion rates and correction durability. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to validate this technique’s safety and efficacy.

Conclusions

The three-stage revision approach effectively addresses failed TLIFs by safely and efficiently correcting spinal malalignment through a careful, staged posterior-to-anterior procedure. This method, supported by rigorous planning and efficient workflow, is a vital tool for complex revision spine surgery.
