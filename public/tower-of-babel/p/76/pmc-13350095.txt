Intestinal muscularis in health and disease
Authors: Wuyan Yang, Jing Yang, Qiman Zou, Haijun Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The intestinal muscularis is a multifunctional tissue that coordinates motility, immune responses, and tissue homeostasis through interactions among enteric neurons, smooth muscle cells (SMCs), and immune cells. Beyond its classical role in peristalsis, emerging evidence identifies the intestinal muscularis as an active regulatory hub integrating neural, immune, and microbial signals. Here, we review the anatomical organization, physiological functions, and pathological alterations in inflammatory bowel diseases, irritable bowel syndrome, infection, and Hirschsprung disease. We further highlight how disruption of integrated neuromuscular and immune networks drives pathological remodeling and discuss emerging concepts and future directions in muscularis biology.

Introduction

The gut wall is organized into four concentric layers with distinct yet interdependent functions. The mucosa mediates nutrient absorption and barrier defense1,2, the submucosa provides vascular and neural support3, the muscularis externa drives motility via smooth muscle layers and the myenteric plexus4,5, and the serosa provides structural protection6. Together, these layers integrate digestive, neural, and immune activities to maintain intestinal homeostasis7. Within this organization, the intestinal muscularis has emerged as a critical functional unit that not only generates motility but also actively participates in immune regulation through cell-cell interactions8,9.

The intestinal muscularis coordinated contractions generate peristalsis and segmental movements required for luminal transport10. Beyond its mechanical role, the muscularis is increasingly recognized as a site of dynamic cellular interactions involving enteric neurons, smooth muscle cells (SMCs), enteric glial cells (EGCs), interstitial cells of Cajal (ICC), stromal cells, and a limited number of immune cells (Fig. 1). Accumulating evidence indicates that enteric neurons modulate immune cell distribution and function within the intestinal wall primarily through cytokine-mediated neuro-immune signaling, influencing macrophages, dendritic cells and lymphocytes11–13. In parallel, immune-derived cytokines and chemokines feed back to regulate smooth muscle contractility14 and promote sensor neurons repair15. Importantly, the enteric nervous system (ENS) operates in conjunction with extrinsic innervation from sympathetic and parasympathetic branches of the autonomic nervous system16,17, which provide top-down modulation of gut physiology, including the regulation of intestinal motility18, epithelial secretion19, and visceral sensation20. Given this complexity, the intestinal muscularis should be viewed as an integrated regulatory hub rather than a purely contractile structure. Disruption of neuro-immune-muscle interactions within this compartment contributes to the pathogenesis of a range of intestinal disorders. In this review, we first outline the anatomical organization and cellular composition of the intestinal muscularis, emphasizing its crucial role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis. We then discuss the mechanistic basis of neuro-immune-muscle crosstalk, and finally examine how dysregulation of these interactions drives disease processes, including inflammatory and functional gastrointestinal disorders. By integrating recent research and clinical insights, this review seeks to offer an in-depth understanding of the vital roles of the intestinal muscularis and neuroimmune interactions in the health of the gastrointestinal system and the pathogenesis of diseases.

Anatomy of the intestinal muscularis

The intestinal muscularis is composed of two smooth muscle layers-an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer-separated by the myenteric plexus, which is positioned between them and integrates neural regulation with muscle function5. The circular muscle layer, arranged circumferentially around the gut lumen, mediates segmental contractions that facilitate mixing of luminal contents22, whereas the longitudinal layer, aligned parallel to the length of the intestine, contributes to propulsion through coordinated shortening of the gut23. The integration of these orthogonal muscle layers enables peristalsis and ensures efficient transport of intestinal contents24,25.

Embedded between these layers, the myenteric plexus (Auerbach’s plexus) represents the principal neural network regulating gastrointestinal motility26. As a major component of the ENS, it forms a continuous chain of densely interconnected ganglia distributed along the entire gastrointestinal tract and provides extensive innervation to the smooth muscle layers27,28. In contrast, the submucosal plexus (Meissner’s plexus) is located within the submucosa29 and primarily controls epithelial secretion and absorption30,31. This anatomical distinction underlies functional specialization, with the myenteric plexus predominantly controlling motility and the submucosal plexus modulating mucosal physiology. Within the myenteric plexus, enteric neurons are organized into functional classes, including excitatory and inhibitory motor neurons, interneurons, and intrinsic primary afferent neurons (IPANs)32,33. In contrast, the submucosal plexus contains comparatively fewer motor neurons and is enriched in IPANs, secretomotor and vasodilatory neurons3,34. These distinct plexuses therefore exert region-specific functions within different layers of the intestinal wall.

Beyond neurons, the muscularis contains multiple specialized cell types that contribute to its integrated function (Table 1). ICC are widely distributed within the muscularis externa35,36, where they closely interact with enteric neurons of the myenteric plexus and serve as pacemakers that coordinate rhythmic smooth muscle contractions37. Additionally, EGCs surround neuronal cell bodies, providing metabolic support, modulating synaptic activity, and participating in immune regulation38,39. EGCs share developmental lineage with Schwann cells40 and exhibit morphological and molecular characteristics reminiscent of astrocytes41. However, they do not possess myelinating functions, although they show partial transcriptional overlap with myelinating glial populations42. Cell subtypes and functions of the intestinal muscularis layer

Importantly, the muscularis also harbors resident immune cells, particularly macrophages, which share transcriptional profiles with microglia (high expression of Trem2, Tmem119, and P2ry12)44,45. Moreover, these macrophages are functionally programmed by enteric neuron-derived TGF-β signaling, which drives the acquisition of a neuro-supportive phenotype of resident macrophages in the adult muscularis externa46. Together with stromal and vascular elements, these diverse cell types form a tightly coordinated microenvironment in which mechanical activity, neural signaling, and immune responses are integrated.

Consequently, the anatomy and structure of the intestinal muscularis represent a dynamic, multicellular interface in which smooth muscle, neural circuits, and immune components converge to regulate gastrointestinal function. Continued research efforts are essential to unraveling the complexities of muscularis organization and function, advancing our understanding of digestive physiology, and developing innovative therapies for gastrointestinal disorders.

Physiological functions of the intestinal muscularis

A primary physiological function of the intestinal muscularis is the generation of coordinated motility patterns, including peristalsis and segmentation47,48. This process is mediated by the synchronized activity of circular and longitudinal smooth muscle layers under the control of the ENS49. In parallel, segmentation involves rhythmic, localized contractions of the circular muscle layer that partition the intestinal lumen50, promoting mixing of chyme with digestive secretions and enhancing nutrient absorption by increasing mucosal contact51–53. Beyond its role in propulsion, recent studies using gut-on-a-chip platforms and live imaging approaches further demonstrate that peristaltic-like mechanical forces can influence host-pathogen interactions by modulating epithelial exposure and susceptibility to the invasion of enteric pathogens such as Shigella flexneri and Entamoeba histolytica54.

These motility patterns are tightly regulated by the ENS, which precisely controls the contractions of the smooth muscle through balanced excitatory and inhibitory signaling55,56. Cholinergic motor neurons release acetylcholine to stimulate smooth muscle contraction, whereas inhibitory neurons, including nitrergic neurons, induce relaxation via nitric oxide signaling57–59. In addition, serotonin involved in motility regulation is primarily derived from enterochromaffin cells and modulates ENS activity rather than serving as a principal neurotransmitter released by enteric neurons60,61. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates that microbiota-dependent pathways, such as aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) signaling in enteric neurons, further regulate intestinal peristalsis and luminal environment62, highlighting the integration of microbial and neural inputs in the control of motility.

While the muscularis externa is primarily responsible for motility, it should be distinguished from the muscularis mucosae, a separate thin smooth muscle layer within the mucosa that regulates localized epithelial movements related to absorption and secretion63. In addition to mechanical and neural functions, the muscularis plays a critical role in maintaining tissue homeostasis through resident immune populations. Muscularis macrophages represent a specialized and heterogeneous population that helps to maintain the gut microenvironment and support neuronal survival8. These cells exhibit long-lived and self-sustaining properties45. Functionally, they support neuronal survival and integrity, as depletion of specific long-lived macrophage leads to enteric neuron loss, vascular leakiness, reduced secretion, and decreased gut motility64,65. Interestingly, macrophages regulated by NLRP3 contribute to the survival of excitatory motor neurons, specifically ChAT+ and VGLUT2+ populations in the muscularis, but not inhibitory nNOS+ neurons66. A recent single-cell transcriptome investigation discovered muscularis macrophages in the human intestine with high expression of the cell adhesion molecule PMP22, suggesting a potential capacity to interact with neuronal axons67. However, the precise mechanisms underlying the crosstalk between muscularis macrophages and other resident cells, particularly enteric neurons, remain insufficiently characterized in the human intestine. A deeper understanding of their subset-specific interactions and regulatory functions will be critical for defining how neuro-immune networks maintain intestinal homeostasis.

Pathological roles of the muscularis in intestinal diseases

Inflammatory bowel disease

The intestinal muscularis, a critical component of gastrointestinal motility, undergoes significant pathophysiological changes in various diseases, profoundly impacting digestive function and clinical outcomes. In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), chronic inflammation extends beyond the mucosa and can affect the muscularis externa68–71.

In CD, the muscularis externa undergoes significant smooth muscle hypertrophy and is infiltrated by leukocytes within and around the enteric ganglia72. These alterations lead to neuronal damage73 and enteric glia activation74, ultimately impairing intestinal motility in patients with CD. In addition, activated SMCs secrete TGF-β, promoting fibronectin-mediated recruitment and differentiation of mesenteric preadipocytes, thereby driving creeping fat formation, a typical feature of CD75–77. Importantly, emerging single-cell transcriptomic evidence further shows that creeping fat-derived CTHRC1⁺ fibroblasts accumulate at the creeping fat-bowel interface and promote extracellular matrix deposition in a YAP/TAZ-dependent manner, indicating that mesenteric adipose tissue directly contributes to intestinal fibrosis and stricture progression78. Together, these findings highlight a mechanistic link between mesenteric adipose tissue and intestinal fibrosis, positioning creeping fat as a critical driver of muscularis remodeling and stricture progression in CD.

In UC, inflammation is primarily restricted to the mucosa, however, persistent inflammatory and oxidative stress signals can propagate to deeper layers and indirectly affect the muscularis79–83. At the molecular level, dysregulated cytokine signaling networks are characterized by key nodes such as TL1A, IL-23A, and IL-22, which drive coordinated inflammatory responses through JAK-dependent pathways, thereby amplifying downstream effects on non-epithelial compartments84. These cytokine signals influence the muscularis through multiple mechanisms. Pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-17A enhance SMCs responsiveness to growth factors by upregulating PDGFRβ expression, thereby promoting SMC proliferation and contributing to intestinal wall thickening and fibrosis85. In parallel, chronic inflammation is associated with fibroblast activation and collagen deposition between muscle layers, leading to altered mechanical properties of the intestinal wall86. In severe cases, fibrosis spreads into the muscularis propria and even the myenteric plexus, accompanied by disruption of a unique kind of stromal cell-telocyte networks surrounding smooth muscle bundles and ganglia87.

Beyond structural remodeling, neuro-immune interactions further contribute to muscularis dysfunction. Recent evidence indicates that microbiota-derived metabolites, such as succinate from pathogenic bacteria, can drive macrophage metabolic reprogramming and sustained IL-1β production, which in turn activates neuronal inflammasome pathways and promotes enteric neuron loss88. Together, these findings suggest that UC-associated muscularis dysfunction arises from integrated cytokine signaling, smooth muscle remodeling, and neuro-immune mechanisms, although the precise pathways linking mucosal inflammation to deep-layer pathology remain incompletely defined.

Irritable bowel syndrome

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is currently classified as a disorder of gut-brain interaction and affects approximately 5–10% of the global population89. Although traditionally considered a motility disorder, IBS is now understood as a systems-level condition involving dysregulated brain-gut interactions, including alterations in central neural circuits and ENS function90,91. Within this framework, intestinal muscularis dysfunction represents a key downstream effector, characterized by disrupted neuromuscular signaling and abnormal motility patterns.

A wide range of biological abnormalities have been reported in IBS, including alterations in the gut epithelium92, immune activation93,94, brain structure and function95, and the gut microbiome96. Despite this complexity, increasing evidence from both clinical observations and experimental models suggests that SMCs and the ENS, as core components of the intestinal muscularis, represent key sites where these upstream disturbances converge to regulate motility and visceral sensitivity97–100.

At the cellular level, increased smooth muscle contractility has been linked to upregulation of Ca2+ channels and enhanced sensitivity of contractile signaling pathways, including Rho kinase and protein kinase C, in a rat model of IBS101. In addition to these intrinsic alterations, emerging evidence indicates that smooth muscle activity is tightly regulated by neuro-immune-microbial interactions. For example, stress-induced metabolic changes in the gut microbiota can modulate immune signaling pathways, such as interferon responses, which in turn regulate colonic smooth muscle contractility, thereby linking psychological stress to altered motility in IBS with diarrhea102. These alterations are accompanied by imbalances in excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, including changes in acetylcholine release and activation of myenteric neuronal circuits100. At the same time, ion channel–mediated regulation of smooth muscle excitability plays a critical role, as exemplified by the modulation of Ca²⁺-permeable TRPC4 channels, which directly influence colonic contractility and represent targets of pharmacological interventions103. Importantly, abdominal pain and visceral hypersensitivity in IBS are not solely mediated by intrinsic enteric circuits but critically depend on extrinsic sensory innervation104,105. Nociceptive signaling is primarily conveyed by extrinsic afferent fibers innervating the intestinal muscularis, where transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1)-expressing nociceptors are abundantly localized106. Mechanistically, TRPV1-lineage nociceptors exhibit mechanosensitivity through stretch-activated Piezo2 channels, which drive visceral afferent firing and pain responses to colorectal distension under both physiological and pathological conditions, including IBS-like states107. In addition, serotonin, primarily derived from enterochromaffin cells, modulates enteric neuronal activity and contributes to altered motility and visceral hypersensitivity, often in association with increased mast cell activity108–110.

Beyond neuronal activity, emerging evidence highlights the importance of enteric glia-neuron interactions in shaping ENS function111. Molecules involved in synaptic organization, such as cell adhesion proteins CADM2, regulate communication between enteric glia and neurons, and their disruption may contribute to dysfunctional ENS circuits and visceral pain in IBS112. It is worth noting that CADM2 is related to a wide range of common psychological and neurological characteristics in IBS patients, which further suggests the upstream regulation by the gut-brain axis further shapes neuromuscular function in IBS113. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that microbial metabolites act as key mediators linking gut microbiota to both central and enteric neural circuits. For instance, alterations in tryptophan metabolism and reduced production of microbiota-derived indole have been shown to modulate brain activity via AHR signaling, thereby contributing to affective symptoms frequently observed in IBS114. Together, disruption of these integrated pathways contributes to abnormal smooth muscle activity, visceral hypersensitivity, and symptom generation in IBS.

Helminth infection

Helminth infection induces striking alterations in the intestinal muscularis, largely mediated by type 2 immune response115. Parasite-derived signals activate type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s), leading to the release of IL-4 and IL-13116. These cytokines regulate intestinal motility through both smooth muscle and neuron-dependent mechanisms117,118. Specifically, IL-4Rα signaling in intestinal SMCs has been shown to be essential for IL-4-driven hypercontractility and efficient expulsion of Schistosoma mansoni eggs. Loss of smooth muscle IL-4Rα responsiveness impaired worm clearance and increased host susceptibility, highlighting the protective role of muscular hypercontractility in schistosomiasis117. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that helminth-induced hypermotility can be predominantly myogenic rather than neurogenic, as increased smooth muscle thickness and contractility occur in the absence of major structural or functional alterations in the ENS, and independently of eosinophils119. This distinguishes helminth infection from other pathogen-induced dysmotility, which more commonly involves ENS disruption.

In parallel, IL-13 exerts stronger Stat6-dependent effects on neural and acetylcholine-mediated responses, underscoring its dominant role in nematode expulsion118. Furthermore, helminth infection engages bidirectional neuro-immune feedback within the muscularis. A subset of enteric neurons, including IL-13R⁺ IPANs, directly senses IL-13 and responds by releasing neuropeptides such as β-CGRP, which in turn suppress ILC2 activation120. This feedback loop constrains excessive type 2 inflammation while preserving effective parasite clearance. Collectively, these findings support a model in which helminth infection activates an integrated neuro-immune-muscle axis. Smooth muscle hypercontractility, ENS modulation, and macrophage-mediated neuroprotection are coordinated to promote parasite expulsion while maintaining tissue integrity.

Intestinal virus infection

In contrast to helminth infection, viral infections primarily disrupt the intestinal muscularis through neuroimmune-mediated tissue injury121,122. Neurotropic viruses, including herpes simplex virus and varicella-zoster virus, can directly invade the ENS via retrograde, hematogenous, or central routes, leading to neuronal dysfunction and gastrointestinal dysmotility. Experimental evidence demonstrates that flavivirus infection induces enteric neuronal injury and death, accompanied by inflammation, bowel dilation, and delayed intestinal transit, with CD8⁺ T cells playing a central pathogenic role123. Beyond acute infection, persistent Herpes Simplex Virus Type 1 infection in enteric neurons elicits sustained CD8⁺ T cell responses and macrophage recruitment, resulting in reactive oxygen and nitrogen species-mediated neuronal damage and neuromuscular dysfunction124,125. However, muscularis macrophages also exhibit a protective dimension. In West Nile virus infection, resident macrophages regulate enteric glial responses and limit excessive recruitment of cytotoxic immune cells. Disruption of this macrophage-glia regulatory axis amplifies inflammation and worsens neuronal injury126, highlighting a dual role for macrophages in both promoting and constraining neuroinflammation.

In addition to neuron-centered mechanisms, emerging evidence highlights the importance of epithelial-neural crosstalk in virus-induced dysfunction. SARS-CoV-2-induced epithelial stress triggers the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, which stimulate enteric neurons to secrete vasoactive intestinal peptide, thereby impairing electrolyte absorption and contributing to diarrhea127. Together, these findings highlight a pathogenic neuro-immune cascade in which immune responses to viral infection inadvertently impair ENS integrity, resulting in persistent gastrointestinal dysmotility. Understanding how these pathways interact may provide new therapeutic opportunities aimed at limiting immune-mediated neuronal injury while preserving antiviral defense.

Microbial infection

Microbial infection elicits a more complex and context-dependent response in the intestinal muscularis, characterized by both pathogen-induced neuronal injury and host-adaptive tissue protection. Pathogenic species, such as Salmonella Typhimurium, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis can trigger infections that extend beyond the mucosa128.

In the early phase of infection, a common feature across invasive is inflammation-associated neuronal injury. For example, microbial pathogens like Salmonella Typhimurium, elicit sustained mild inflammatory responses, accompanied by abnormal gut motility, which coincide with an early but enduring reduction in enteric neurons, suggesting that neuronal loss is a central feature of pathogen-induced intestinal dysfunction129. Conversely, microbial infections can also induce states of disease tolerance in the gut. For instance, primary Yersinia pseudotuberculosis infection triggers muscularis macrophages to adopt a neuroprotective phenotype via β2-adrenergic receptor signaling and Arg1-dependent polyamine production130. This adaptation preserves enteric neurons and maintains motility during subsequent infections, highlighting the capacity of the muscularis to balance immune activation with tissue protection, independent of direct effects on pathogen load.

In addition to these protective effects, mouse enteric serotonergic neurons also regulate gut immunity by promoting the activation and gut-homing of plasmacytoid dendritic cells, thereby enhancing IgA responses and mucosal defense against oral Salmonella Typhimurium infection via a serotonin-5-HT receptor 7 (HTR7) signaling axis131. Together with muscularis macrophages, these neurons regulate immune tolerance and host defense through bidirectional neuro-immune signaling, integrating neuronal activity with local immune responses in the gut microenvironment.

Hirschsprung disease

Hirschsprung disease (HSCR) is a congenital disorder characterized by the failure of enteric neural crest-derived progenitors to fully colonize the distal gut during development, leading to the absence of enteric neurons and disruption of coordinated excitatory and inhibitory signaling required for peristalsis132. While this mechanism has been established through human pathological studies and supported by experimental models, recent analyses of patient-derived tissues have further shown that HSCR is accompanied by increased extracellular matrix deposition, expansion, and early activation of fibroblast and myofibroblast populations, and upregulation of profibrotic regulators, indicating a pronounced fibrotic phenotype within the muscularis133. In parallel, studies in genetically modified mouse models demonstrate that smooth muscle and progenitor cell populations exhibit altered transcriptional states and functional heterogeneity, supporting the concept that intrinsic muscular dysfunction contributes to disease pathology134.

Beyond neuronal and muscular alterations, HSCR involves broader disruption of the intestinal cellular microenvironment. Single-cell analyses of pediatric patient tissues, together with functional studies in zebrafish models, have identified distinct enteric glial subtypes, with Schwann-like glia persisting in aganglionic segments and representing a potential target for functional restoration135. In addition, combined investigations using human HSCR samples and endothelin receptor B-deficient mice have demonstrated impaired epithelial regeneration and disrupted stromal-epithelial signaling, characterized by depletion of LGR5⁺ stem cells and a shift toward proinflammatory stromal populations, which may contribute to Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis136. Notably, recent transplantation studies using human pluripotent stem cell-derived ENS progenitors have shown that reintroduction of neuronal and glial components into patient-derived colonic tissue can restore contractile activity, while complementary in vivo experiments in mouse models demonstrate engraftment and partial functional rescue137. Together, these findings indicate that HSCR is a multicellular disorder involving coordinated disruption of neural, muscular, stromal, and epithelial compartments, underscoring the intestinal muscularis as an integrated and neuro-dependent functional unit.

Overall, the intestinal muscularis sustains peristaltic function through coordinated interactions among enteric neurons, SMCs, and immune cells under homeostatic conditions. The effects of microbiota on the intestinal muscularis

Gut microbiota is critical regulator of host physiology, comprising a complex ecosystem of bacteria, archaea, viruses, and eukaryotic microorganisms that extensively colonizes the gastrointestinal tract and contributes to digestion, nutrient assimilation, metabolic regulation, and the maintenance of immune homeostasis138–140. Beyond these classical functions, emerging evidence highlights a pivotal role of microbiota in shaping the structure and function of the intestinal muscularis141,142.

The intestinal muscularis relies on microbial signals for proper maturation and functional maintenance141. Microbial-derived metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as butyrate and acetate, serve as key mediators linking microbiota to both neural and immune functions143. Mechanistically, SCFAs regulate intestinal motility through both indirect and direct pathways. They stimulate enteroendocrine cells to release serotonin, thereby indirectly promoting peristalsis144, and can also act directly on intestinal smooth muscle function via yes-associated protein or cAMP/PKA pathway145,146. These findings indicate that microbial metabolites can modulate smooth muscle excitability independently of neural input.

Beyond smooth muscle regulation, microbiota exert profound influences on shaping ENS development, plasticity, and function. Accumulating evidence indicates that the gut microbiota is indispensable for the maturation and maintenance of the ENS147,148. Colonization of germ-free mice restores ENS structure and function, enhancing intestinal transit through microbiota-induced serotonin release and activation of 5-HT4 receptors, which promote neurogenesis and neuronal survival149. Conversely, antibiotic-induced microbial depletion disrupts ENS integrity and neuromuscular coordination, further supporting a causal role of microbiota in maintaining enteric neuronal networks. Among microbial metabolites, SCFAs have emerged as central regulators of ENS integrity and plasticity, linking microbial dysbiosis to motility disorders and enteric neuropathies150.

In parallel, microbiota critically shape the phenotype and function of muscularis-resident immune cells, particularly muscularis macrophages, which act as key intermediaries in neuro-immune communication45,151. Microbial signals regulate macrophage polarization and functional states, thereby influencing neuronal survival and motility control142. For example, SCFAs promote anti-inflammatory M2-like polarization by fueling fatty acid oxidation, which play a critical role in anti-helminth immunity152. In addition, tryptophan-derived metabolites, including indoles and kynurenines, further modulate macrophage polarization and cytokine production via AhR signaling, thereby integrating microbial metabolism with immune and neural regulation153–155.

Collectively, these findings position the gut microbiota as a central upstream regulator of intestinal muscularis function, integrating metabolic, neural, and immune pathways. The intercellular communication of muscularis

The intestinal muscularis is not simply a motor apparatus but also represents a dynamic communication hub where neurons, SMCs, and macrophages form a highly integrated network116,156. This network is plastic, shaped by developmental programs, microbial cues, and inflammatory insults. Beyond simple ligand-receptor signaling, these interactions involve spatiotemporal organization, bidirectional feedback loops, and context-dependent functional reprogramming, which together maintain motility and immune balance.

Neuro-immune interaction

The ENS and innate immune systems are both equipped to swiftly detect and react to danger cues, forming a tightly coupled neuro-immune regulatory network within the muscularis. Single-cell RNA sequencing analyses of CD45⁺ cells in a postoperative ileus mouse model reveal a complex immune landscape composed of resident and infiltrating macrophages, but also dendritic cells, neutrophils, and lymphocytes157. Within this heterogeneous immune network, macrophages represent the predominant and most extensively studied population, while additional immune subsets exhibit region-specific distribution patterns158,159.

Within this multicellular framework, enteric neurons act as central regulators of immune function, extending beyond macrophage-focused interactions. It should be noted that several neuron-immune pathways have been characterized primarily within the mucosal and submucosal compartments, where enteric neurons influence adaptive and innate immune responses that indirectly affect intestinal physiology. For example, serotonergic neurons promote mucosal immune defense by enhancing IgA production through a 5-HT receptor-dependent pathway that facilitates plasmacytoid dendritic cell activation and B cell differentiation131, highlighting a direct role of neuronal signals in shaping adaptive immunity. In parallel, dendritic cells control regulatory T cell differentiation and intestinal tolerance, suggesting that neuron-dendritic cell interactions may contribute to immune homeostasis160. Although these studies are not restricted to the muscularis, they illustrate the broader capacity of enteric neurons to coordinate intestinal immunity.

Within the muscularis itself, neuro-immune communication is mediated by resident and infiltrating immune populations located in or adjacent to the myenteric plexus. Neuronal regulation also extends to innate and effector immune populations that influence intestinal motility and tissue integrity. Activation of distinct neuronal subsets in mouse models has been shown to differentially modulate immune cell composition, including suppression of neutrophils and RORγ⁺ T cells, or expansion of mononuclear phagocytes, indicating that neuron subtype–specific signaling can shape the immune landscape in a context-dependent manner161. Consistently, neutrophils have been identified as critical effectors during infection, where their recruitment and production of reactive oxygen species contribute to pathogen clearance but may also influence tissue damage and neuromuscular function162. Similarly, innate lymphoid cells are tightly regulated by neuronal inputs. GABAergic neuron-derived signals suppress ILC3 activation and IL-17 production, whereas reciprocal signaling between ILC2s and enteric neurons via IL-13 and neuropeptides such as CGRP establishes bidirectional feedback circuits that modulate type 2 immunity163.

A central component of the neuro-immune network within the muscularis is the interaction between enteric neurons and muscularis macrophages, which forms a conserved and functionally critical regulatory circuit. During postnatal development, muscularis macrophages contribute to ENS maturation by regulating neuronal and synaptic density, partly through complement C1q signaling46,164. In adulthood, these macrophages adopt a neuron-instructed, tissue-protective phenotype driven by signals such as TGF-β, further enhancing BMP2 expression in macrophage, thereby supporting neuronal survival and maintaining motility46,165,166. Depletion of muscularis macrophages lead to enteric neuronal loss and impaired gut function, highlighting their essential neuro-supportive role167.

Under pathological conditions, this neuro-immune crosstalk is dynamically reprogrammed. In postoperative ileus, surgical manipulation activates these macrophages, triggering leukocyte recruitment and the release of proinflammatory mediators that impair gut motility, with macrophage depletion preserving transit168–170. In metabolic disorders such as diabetes, loss of neuro-supportive macrophage phenotypes, including CD206+HO-1+ cells, contributes to neuronal degeneration, ICC damage, and dysmotility171–173. In IBD, particularly CD, macrophages infiltrate the myenteric plexus during plexitis, promoting neurodegeneration, glial activation, and altered motility174. Conversely, neuronal activity can restrain macrophage-driven inflammation, as demonstrated by cholinergic neuron-mediated suppression of macrophage activation in colitis model174.

Beyond the neuron-macrophage axis, neuro-immune communication within the muscularis also involves enteric glia and infiltrating immune cells that accumulate around the myenteric plexus during inflammation and infection120,126,175. For instance, interferon-γ-mediated pathways involving T cells and stromal cells can restrict smooth muscle remodeling and prevent dysmotility, while macrophage-glia interactions modulate T cell and neutrophil recruitment to protect enteric neurons175.

Together, these findings establish the intestinal muscularis as a site of highly integrated neuro-immune communication, in which enteric neurons orchestrate immune composition and function through both direct and indirect mechanisms (Table 2). Immune cell populations and neuro-immune interactions in the intestinal muscularis

Smooth muscle-immune interaction

SMCs have long been recognized for their role in facilitating contraction in various tissues, such as the vasculature and gastrointestinal tract. SMCs located in different anatomical positions have different physiological tasks. Gastrointestinal SMCs can be broadly classified into four major types, which work together in coordination176 (Fig. 3). Emerging evidence further indicates that SMCs participate in local immune regulation within the muscularis through their interactions with resident muscularis macrophages, thereby contributing critically to innate immunity and tissue repair164,177.

Recent work demonstrates that muscularis macrophages express the transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) channel, which promotes the release of PGE2, and directly induces smooth muscle contraction through prostaglandin E receptor independently of ENS input178. This finding highlights a neuron-independent pathway through which immune cells can directly regulate motility within the muscularis.

Conversely, SMCs contribute to tissue repair and immune responses under pathological conditions. Following tissue damage, SMCs migrate to the injury site and proliferate to aid in repair179. Furthermore, ultrastructural analyses reveal direct and extended cell-to-cell interactions between muscularis macrophages and SMCs, as well as with telocytes/PDGFRα⁺ cells, but not with ICCs or enteric neurons180. These findings suggest that muscularis macrophages form selective cellular partnerships within the muscularis microenvironment, with the SMC-macrophage interaction emerging as a key axis for modulating gut function in health and disease.

SMCs also participate in immune responses during infection. Within the muscularis, SMCs also participate in type 2 immune responses, wherein IL-4 and IL-13-driven alternatively activated M2 macrophages promote smooth muscle hypercontractility and hypertrophy via arginase-dependent mechanisms, facilitating effective worm expulsion181. SMC function is also modulated under inflammatory conditions. LPS stimulation drives muscularis macrophages toward a proinflammatory phenotype, which diminishes smooth muscle contractility182,183. In addition, blockade of monocyte recruitment through monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 signaling reduces monocyte infiltration but paradoxically results in impaired intestinal smooth muscle function184. Despite growing evidence linking muscularis macrophages to the regulation of smooth muscle activity, direct evidence for macrophage-SMC interaction in the gastrointestinal tract remains limited. Future studies should move beyond correlative observations to dissect the molecular cues and bidirectional communication that may enable SMCs and macrophages to jointly regulate motility, repair, and immune responses.

Overall, these findings highlight the muscularis as an integrated communication hub where neurons, immune cells, and SMCs dynamically interact to coordinate motility and immunity (Fig. 4). Despite these advances, the molecular basis of SMC-immune communication remains incompletely defined. Conclusion

The intestinal muscularis is increasingly recognized not merely as a contractile apparatus, but as an integrated neuro-immune-muscle interface that coordinates gastrointestinal motility, tissue homeostasis, and host defense. Through tightly coupled interactions among SMCs, enteric neurons, enteric glia, and diverse immune populations, the muscularis operates as a dynamic regulatory unit in which mechanical activity, neural signaling, and immune responses are functionally interdependent. This integrated framework provides a unifying perspective to understand how local cellular interactions are translated into system-level control of gut physiology. In our review, we comprehensively clarify the anatomy and physiological activities of the intestinal muscularis in maintaining gut health. We further discuss its involvement in pathophysiological alterations such as IBD and IBS. Across these conditions, a recurring theme is the disruption of coordinated neuro-immune-muscle communication, rather than isolated cellular dysfunction, underlies altered motility, visceral hypersensitivity, and tissue remodeling. This conceptual shift from single-cell pathology to network-level dysregulation offers a more comprehensive framework for interpreting disease mechanisms.

Despite these advances, key mechanistic questions remain unresolved. A major challenge is to define how distinct immune cell subsets, including macrophages, innate lymphoid cells, and T cell populations, selectively modulate smooth muscle contractility and enteric neuronal functions in a context-dependent manner. Equally important is to elucidate the molecular logic governing bidirectional communication within the muscularis niche, including neurotransmitter signaling, cytokine networks, and metabolite-mediated pathways that integrate microbial, neural, and immune inputs. Addressing these questions will require combining single-cell and spatial transcriptomics with functional approaches to capture the spatiotemporal dynamics of these interactions in vivo.

Another emerging frontier lies in redefining the role of SMCs beyond contractility. Increasing evidence suggests that SMCs participate in immune modulation and tissue repair, yet whether they possess conserved, cell-intrinsic immune functions or primarily act as responders to immune cues remains unclear. Deciphering SMCs identity switching between contractile, synthetic, and immunomodulatory states, and how these transitions are regulated during inflammation and repair, will be critical for understanding muscularis remodeling in chronic disease.

From a translational perspective, these insights point toward a paradigm in which targeting intercellular communication, rather than individual cell types, may offer more effective therapeutic strategies. Modulating neuro-immune circuits, restoring SMC-immune homeostasis, or reprogramming microbial-metabolic signaling pathways could provide new avenues for treating motility disorders and inflammatory diseases of the gut. In summary, the intestinal muscularis should be viewed as a dynamic, multicellular regulatory network that integrates neural, immune, and mechanical signals. A deeper mechanistic understanding of this system will be essential for bridging basic biology with clinical intervention, and for developing next-generation therapies aimed at restoring intestinal homeostasis.
