Comparative analysis of embodied carbon in modular and conventional construction methods in Hong Kong
Authors: Jiaying Wei, Bin Ge, Ying Zhong, Tik Long Lee, Yi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAddressing the rise in global temperatures and the associated increase in greenhouse gases, particularly carbon dioxide, is a critical challenge necessitating innovative approaches within the building sector, a significant contributor to worldwide carbon emissions. While previous studies have demonstrated the prefabrication’s potential in reducing emissions, comprehensive assessments using actual project data for buildings constructed entirely with modular methods in Hong Kong are lacking. This study bridges this gap by evaluating the modular integrated construction (MiC) method through an embodied carbon assessment of the Kai Tak Community Isolation Facility. Using comprehensive project data from China State Construction (HK) Limited, the research conducts a comparative analysis between the actual emissions of the MiC method and those of a hypothetical conventional construction approach. Quantitative analysis reveals that MiC achieves a 20.7% reduction in embodied carbon, primarily due to shortened construction timelines, decreased waste generation, and optimized material usage. This significant reduction suggests substantial potential for decreasing the construction industry’s carbon footprint. The study provides empirical evidence supporting the environmental benefits of MiC in Hong Kong construction industry, promoting its broader adoption of MiC as a strategy for achieving carbon reduction targets. The findings align with Hong Kong’s carbon neutrality goals and contribute to the global initiative to mitigate the effects of climate change.Keywords: Embodied carbon emissions, Modular construction, Comparative carbon assessment, Carbon reductionSubject terms: Civil engineering, Energy infrastructure

Introduction

At the Paris climate conference in 2015, 197 countries signed the Paris Agreement, committing to limit the global temperature rise to 2 °C while making concerted efforts to keep it below 1.5 °C. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) emphasized in 2018 that to prevent catastrophic impacts of climate change, global warming must not exceed 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels1. There is a wealth of evidence demonstrating that the increase in greenhouse gases (GHG) is altering the global climate system, with carbon dioxide being the most significant human-made GHG2. To address this issue, the IPCC stated that GHG emissions need to be cut by half by 2030 and reach net-zero by 20503. In alignment with these objectives, the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region Government has pledged to achieve carbon neutrality by or before 20504. As of 2020, Hong Kong’s total greenhouse gas emissions were about 33.3 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent, with per capita emissions of about 4.46 tonnes5. To reach its 2050 goal, Hong Kong has set interim targets, including reducing carbon emissions by 50% before 2035 compared to 2005 levels, and ceasing the use of coal for daily electricity generation by 2035. The government has outlined four major decarbonisation strategies in its “Climate Action Plan”: net-zero electricity generation, energy saving and green buildings, green transport, and waste reduction4.

Given the critical role of infrastructure in global GHG emissions, the building sector represents a significant area for potential reduction in carbon output. Reports show that buildings are major contributors, accounting for almost one-third of the world’s total energy consumption and carbon emissions6,7. This global trend is reflected in local contexts, where the construction industry plays a crucial role in the economy. In Hong Kong, the construction sector’s industry value added, which measures its contribution to Hong Kong’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP), amounted to HK$121.8 billion in 2022. The sector comprised about 26,600 establishments and directly engaged approximately 186,900 persons8. Buildings account for about 90% of the city’s electricity use and 60% of its carbon emissions4. Given the construction industry’s significant contribution to Hong Kong’s economy and carbon emissions, evaluating sustainable construction methods is crucial for achieving the city’s carbon neutrality goals.

To comprehensively assess the environmental impact of buildings, life cycle assessment (LCA) is widely used. LCA evaluates the environmental effects of a product or system throughout its entire life cycle, from raw material extraction to disposal9. In the context of buildings, LCA typically considers carbon emissions in two main categories: embodied carbon (associated with material production, transportation and construction) and operational carbon (generated by energy consumption during buildings’ service life, including space cooling and heating, ventilation, lighting, etc.). While the use phase is widely recognized as a major contributor to a building’s total emissions – accounting for approximately 70–98% of them compared to the 2–26% from the production and construction phases10–15 – advancements in efficient technologies, design strategies, and environmentally friendly materials, along with the utilization of renewable energy sources like wind and solar have made buildings more efficient during the operational phase. Consequently, the relative significance of emissions from other life cycle phases is increasing16,17.

Modular integrated construction (MiC), commonly known as modular construction in other parts of the world, is an advanced form of prefabrication involving factory-made, fully furnished units. MiC is on the rise in Hong Kong to mitigate labor shortages and expedite construction projects while enhancing its quality and safety. As Hong Kong strives to meet its carbon reduction targets, assessing the potential of MiC to reduce embodied carbon compared to conventional construction methods becomes increasingly important. Studies have also demonstrated the potential of prefabrication in mitigating embodied carbon emissions. Lopez-Mesa et al.18 reported that precast concrete produced 12.2% less carbon than cast-in-situ slabs. Mao et al.19 compared cast-in-situ construction with semi-prefabricated construction and found a 13.62% reduction in GHG emissions. Dong et al.20 revealed that carbon emissions of prefabricated concrete per cubic meter were 10% lower than those of in-situ cast concrete. Ji et al.21 reported a 3.1% carbon reduction through the adoption of prefabrication compared to conventional construction. Teng et al.22 reviewed several empirical studies and found that prefabricated buildings resulted in an average reduction of 15.6% in embodied carbon. Du et al.23 employed a life-cycle assessment to calculate carbon emissions from cradle to site and found that buildings with prefabrication rates of 21%, 42%, and 83% can achieve carbon reductions of 16.93%, 20.99%, and 6.09% during construction, respectively. Han et al.24 found a 5.12% reduction in carbon emissions for buildings with a prefabrication rate of 46.98%. Mo et al.25 demonstrated notable reductions in carbon emissions for transportation (4.8 kg/m2 of CO2) and on-site construction activities (5.7 kg/m2 of CO2) for buildings with a high prefabrication rate of 96.8%. Loo et al.26 compared two hostel projects built with MiC and cast-in-situ methods and concluded that MiC resulted in a 17.6% reduction in total carbon emissions. In the above cases, the majority of carbon emission reductions in prefabricated buildings are attributed to the efficient use of materials, lessened waste generation, reuse of structural components, and streamlined on-site construction processes.

Despite the recognized environmental benefits of MiC, there are significant gaps in the existing literature. Many studies rely heavily on hypothetical scenarios or involve numerous assumptions due to limited data availability, which may not accurately reflect real-world performance. The scope of research is often restricted to certain structural components, such as precast slabs or staircases, rather than examining entire buildings. Furthermore, existing research frequently focuses on different levels of prefabrication, with few studies addressing fully modular constructed projects. The geographical coverage of existing studies is also limited, with a particularly shortage of research on MiC projects in high-density urban environments like Hong Kong.

To address these gaps, comparative studies assessing MiC buildings against conventional construction using the same design parameters are needed. Case studies are particularly valuable in this context, as they allow researchers to capture and analyze the complexity of construction processes and site-specific factors that influence environmental performance. This approach is crucial for MiC projects, where performance can vary significantly based on factors such as the availability of low-carbon materials and energy sources, as well as local construction practices. In Hong Kong’s dense urban context, where fully modular construction projects are rare, comprehensive studies using actual project data throughout the entire building lifecycle are essential to provide accurate insights into MiC’s environmental performance in challenging settings.

This study aims to fill these knowledge gaps by quantifying the extent to which MiC can reduce embodied carbon emissions compared to conventional construction methods in Hong Kong’s high-density urban environment. It provides a comprehensive embodied carbon assessment of a completed local project, the Kai Tak Community Isolation Facility (Kai Tak CIF). The Kai Tak CIF, featuring multiple steel building blocks constructed using the MiC approach, commenced operation in August 2022. Utilizing abundant data provided by the project team from the main contractor, China State Construction (HK) Limited, this study quantifies the carbon emissions associated with the production and construction stages of the Kai Tak CIF – using real project data – with those of a hypothetical conventional construction approach, for which an equivalent design has been proposed solely for this comparative analysis. Focusing specifically on structural components, this assessment excludes non-structural elements to enable a direct comparison between the two construction methods. By examining the environmental performance within the boundary of cradle to end-of-construction, this study provides valuable insights into the potential benefits of MiC and contributes to the development of more sustainable construction practices in high-density urban environments, supporting Hong Kong’s carbon neutrality goals.

Project overview and assessment methodology

Project description

The Kai Tak Community Isolation Facility (Kai Tak CIF) is located adjacent to the Kai Tak Cruise Terminal in Hong Kong (Fig. 1). Established in response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the facility commenced operations on August 19th, 2022. Covering an extensive area of over 110,000 square meters, the facility houses more than 2,700 units, providing over 6,000 beds. Designed primarily as four-storey structures, each building is equipped with lifts to ensure accessibility for patients and staff. The project is a testament to the efficiency of modular integrated construction (MiC), utilizing high-strength steel as the main structural component. The MiC method employs prefabrication technique, where modules are produced in a factory setting and then transported to the construction site for rapid assembly and connection to essential services, such as water, electricity, and sewage (Fig. 2). This approach significantly shortens the construction timeline, with the Kai Tak CIF being completed in just four months.

This project was selected as a case study due to its unique position as one of the few buildings in Hong Kong entirely constructed using MiC, as well as the authors’ access to comprehensive project data. The scale and recent completion of the project provide valuable insights into current MiC practices and their environmental impact.

Scope and boundary

The life cycle of a construction project can be subdivided into specific modules, as shown in Fig. 3, which is adapted from the Institution of Structural Engineers’ (IstructE) “How to Calculate Embodied Carbon” guide27. This guide is based on the internationally recognized standards BS EN 15978:2011 and BS EN 15804:201228,29. Following these guidelines, the embodied carbon (also known as ‘upfront carbon’) can be assessed by analyzing the product and construction process stages (modules A1 to A5).

Lifecycle stages and modules27.

Modules A1 to A3, known collectively as ‘cradle-to-gate’, define the product stage. These encompass the environmental impacts arising from the extraction and refinement of raw materials (A1), their transportation to manufacturing facilities (A2), and the production processes that yield finished building materials and products (A3). The embodied carbon from modules A1 through A3 typically accounts for a significant share of a building’s total embodied carbon footprint. To assess embodied carbon, the quantity of each material or product used is multiplied by a corresponding carbon factor, which is closely tied to the material’s features, including its composition and manufacturing process. Therefore, it is imperative to employ carbon factors that accurately reflect local production realities and methodologies.

Modules A4 and A5 constitute the construction process stage. Module A4 captures the environmental impact from transporting materials and products from the factory to the construction site. This typically accounts for less than 10% of a structure’s total embodied carbon. Module A5 covers the on-site construction activities, such as assembly and installation. The emissions within A5, which can vary based on construction practices, material selection, and site management, are further subdivided into two categories: A5a and A5w. A5a emissions, arising from site activities, such as the operation of construction machinery and site offices, are calculated from the consumption of fuel and electricity on-site and must be closely tracked during construction for an accurate as-built embodied carbon calculation. A5w emissions cover the carbon footprint resulting from several processes: the production of materials that end up as scrap; the transportation of these unused materials to site; their subsequent removal from site; and their final processing and disposal.

This study focuses specifically on stages A1-A5 due to the availability of comprehensive, verifiable data for these stages and their particular relevance to comparing MiC with conventional construction methods. These stages represent the critical phases where the choice of construction method has the most significant and immediate impact on embodied carbon emissions.

Assessment methodology

The assessment in this study encompasses a comprehensive approach to quantify and compare the embodied carbon emissions of MiC and conventional construction methods. The methodology includes the following key components:

Scope definition

Define the focus on embodied carbon emissions associated with product and construction process stages (A1-A5) of the building’s life cycle. Categorize assessment into two phases: A1-A4 (‘cradle-to-site’) and A5 (on-site construction stage).

Project overview and comparative framework

Identify Kai Tak CIF’s MiC construction method, which features a framework primarily composed of structural steel. Conceptualize a hypothetical counterpart using conventional construction methods with reinforced concrete structural design for comparative analysis.

Data collection

Collect relevant data through site investigation, review of drawings, specifications, bills of quantities, project documents, and interviews with project team members.

Emission quantification approach

Quantify GHG emissions in terms of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e), which includes the following types of GHGs: carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), perfluoro-carbons (PFCs) and sulphur hexafluoride (SF6)30. Use Global Warming Potentials (GWPs) from the IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) over a 100-year period31. Source energy conversion factors from databases like the International Energy Agency (IEA)32.

Calculation framework

Utilize the Construction Industry Council’s Carbon Assessment Tool (CIC CAT)33 as the primary framework, which provides standardized emission factors and calculation methods for the Hong Kong construction industry. For specific emissions, such as those related to energy consumption, the guidelines from the Electrical and Mechanical Services Department (EMSD) were followed34,35. Waste disposal and processing calculations were based on factors from the IStructE guide27. In cases where standard factors were not available, such as for welding processes, calculations were based on chemical equations derived from established scientific principles. Carbon emission assessment

MiC approach

Construction materials consumption (A1-A4)

The assessment of material usage in the Kai Tak CIF project, which was constructed using the MiC method, was based on detailed as-built architectural and structural design drawings. These documents provided essential details regarding the facility’s layout, structural element sizes, and materials specifications for an accurate calculation of material consumption for each module type. The Kai Tak CIF project is comprised of 54 four-storey buildings designed for patient accommodation, with each building consisting of 2 identical blocks (108 blocks in total). Each block consists of 7 typical units and a corridor. In the subsequent study, a block is considered the fundamental unit in the calculation for all 4-storey buildings. Additionally, there are a total number of 402 single-storey blocks serving various functions, including CAS sub-stations, DH medical posts, CAS management offices, CAS logistic offices, and connecting corridors. To calculate the total material consumption, the quantities of materials needed per block were multiplied by the number of blocks in the entire project. For simplicity, non-structural components were excluded from the assessment.

The emission factors of materials and the methodology for calculation were derived from a local database, adhering to the guidelines established by the Construction Industry Council’s Carbon Assessment Tool (CIC CAT)33. The CAT is a tool developed by the CIC in 2019 to help the construction industry in Hong Kong reduce its carbon footprint. Based on the Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) framework, the CAT aims to create a common platform to evaluate the carbon performance of buildings and civil infrastructure in Hong Kong from raw material extraction to the end of construction (A1 to A5). The assessment encompasses permanent structures, temporary works, and site impact, and currently covers over 200 construction materials.

In the analysis of four-storey steel MiC blocks, three distinct module types were identified: Module Type A1 (with bracing, on the ground and first floors), Module Type A2 (without bracing, on the second and third floors), and the Corridor Module (Fig. 4). Initial simulations (Fig. 5) were conducted using the structural design software YJK on these typical modules to determine the corresponding material usage. The foundation system for four-storey blocks consists of 375 mm thick raft footings.

On-site construction activities (A5a)

For the assessment of embodied carbon within construction activities (A5a), a comprehensive collection of data was necessary. This included information on fuel and refrigerant use, welding activities, tree removal and planting, energy purchasing and any existing renewable energy systems. This data was categorized by discipline to enable precise calculation of various emission types, such as those from stationary sources, mobile sources, and welding operations. A master programme of the project was obtained from the project team, which provided insight into the project’s schedule, allowing for assumptions to be made regarding the embodied carbon emissions from construction activities.

The construction of Kai Tak CIF commenced on February 19, 2022, with all major construction completed by June 30, 2022. In rare cases of the absence of actual data for estimating construction activities, a normalization method was utilized. On-site activities emissions (MiC).

Stationary emissions from the construction stage were exclusively from the consumption of B5 biodiesel fuel used in on-site generators and other industrial equipment for energy provision. According to site records, over the construction period, a total of 2,227,090 L of biodiesel were consumed, with consumption varying monthly based on construction activities. The highest consumption was observed in April and May, coinciding with peak construction activities.

Mobile emissions resulted from two main sources: petrol used in passenger cars and diesel used in private bus services for staff transportation. Petrol consumption data were obtained from refueling bill records, with some estimates made for missing data (first half of March) based on travel distances and fuel efficiency rates.

The assessment of private bus services was based on the bus service schedule, which included four routes from various locations to the construction site, specifically from Diamond Hill, Kowloon Bay, Tuen Mun, and Whampoa, with respective travel distances of 6 km, 4.2 km, 36 km, and 8.8 km. The bus schedule underwent several adjustments during the construction period, affecting daily fuel consumption. The estimated fuel efficiency for a single-deck private bus, taken to be 23.4 L/100 km as per the EMSD Energy Utilization Index34, was used to calculate the diesel consumption for these bus services. The carbon emissions from welding activities were determined using the acetylene storage records provided by the project team. According to the records, a total of 276 kg acetylene was consumed over the construction period, with consumption varying based on the intensity of welding activities at different stages of construction.

The assessment also considered potential emission from fugitive sources, tree removal and planting, purchased electricity, and renewable energy generation. However, these sources were found to have no net impact on the total embodied carbon for this project. Specifically, there were not reported refrigerant refilling or fire incidents, eliminating any fugitive emissions. Regarding tree management, the project involved the removal of 48 trees, which were subsequently replanted in alternative locations, resulting in a neutral carbon impact. The project relied entirely on on-site electricity generation using biodiesel generators, negating the need for purchased electricity and its associated emissions. Lastly, no renewable energy generation systems were implemented on the project site, precluding any potential emission offsets from this source.

On-site construction waste (A5w)

A5w covers the embodied carbon associated with the extraction, processing, manufacture, transportation and end-of-life processing of materials that end up as waste on the construction site. To thoroughly assess the on-site construction waste for the Kai Tak CIF project, a detailed data collection initiative was undertaken to determine the total volume of waste generated and the logistics involved in its transportation, disposal, and processing. The embodied carbon of A5w is divided into three main components: embodied carbon from the generated material waste, embodied carbon from transportation of on-site waste away from the site, and embodied carbon from the disposal and processing of on-site waste.

The embodied carbon assessment for on-site construction waste in the MiC approach excludes waste generated from temporary works, in line with the IstructE guidelines27. This assumption is based on the fact that the MiC method requires significantly fewer temporary works than conventional construction methods. Nevertheless, waste resulting from the excavation processes is included in the measurement of embodied carbon associated with the transportation of waste away from the construction site.

Material waste

According to the material list and structural plans for the Kai Tak CIF project, the weight of each material used in different module types and the total amount of material required for construction have been calculated in Sect. "Construction materials consumption (A1-A4)" for MiC. A waste factor is applied to the amount of material used in the structure. For this analysis, a waste factor of 0.01 is used for the materials prefabricated off-site according to BSEN15978:201128, which represents the worst-case scenario for prefabrication. For the reinforced concrete foundation that is constructed on-site using the conventional approach, a waste factor of 0.053 is adopted27.

Carbon factors for concrete, reinforcement, and structural steel are adopted from the same sources as specified in Sect. Construction materials consumption (A1-A4)33 for MiC. The embodied carbon from material waste is calculated by multiplying the weight of each material type by its respective waste factor and carbon factor.

Total fuel consumption for waste transport is determined by multiplying the fuel efficiency by the travel distance from the construction site to the waste processing center or landfill. The embodied carbon emissions are then calculated by multiplying the fuel consumption by the emission factor for diesel consumption.

Conventional construction approach

In this study, an equivalent reinforced concrete (RC) structural design was developed as a benchmark for evaluating carbon emissions. This RC design was chosen because it is compatible with conventional construction practices, aligns with the project’s architectural features (including regular building shapes and a medium-span column grid), and is cost-effective in the local industry. The design ensures equivalence to the MiC approach in key aspects, providing identical usable floor area, resisting the same design loads, and achieving similar capacity utilization ratios. Both designs utilize comparable structural systems, meet the same serviceability requirements, and employ raft footings for foundations. The RC design maintains equivalent functionality in terms of room layouts and building services integration while adhering to all relevant detailing requirements. By comparing the carbon emissions of the RC design to those of the innovative MiC approach, we can highlight the environmental impact disparities between the two construction methods.

On-site construction activities (A5a)

The duration of construction is the primary factor influencing the embodied carbon of on-site construction activities. In this study, the estimated duration for conventional construction of Kai Tak CIF is based on the following assumptions:

1. The working hours for conventional construction are assumed to be the same as for the MiC approach, operating 24 h a day;

2. An extended construction period of an additional 182 days is assumed for conventional construction, according to the project team’s planning;

3. The transportation of bus services is presumed to remain unchanged;

4. The use of purchased electricity is excluded, consistent with the MiC approach.

To determine the construction duration of Kai Tak CIF when built with conventional construction methods, a thorough analysis of the master programme provided by the project team was conducted. In the MiC approach, the fabrication of modules comprising the building structure took 91 days, while 131 days were allocated for on-site construction, with both activities taking place concurrently. For the purpose of this analysis, the authors collaborated with the project team to develop a construction programme for the same project, assuming it were to be constructed using conventional construction methods and reinforced concrete. Taking into account factors such as the lower efficiency of on-site works compared to off-site MiC fabrication, the time required for concrete curing and strength development, and potential weather-related delays, the project team estimated that the total construction period for the conventionally constructed project would be 313 days.

The emission estimates for on-site construction using the conventional approach were based on the calculations performed for the MiC approach in Sect. "On-site construction activities (A5a)" for MiC. Fuel and material consumption rates during MiC construction were normalized on a per-day basis and then extrapolated to account for the increased duration of conventional construction. Embodied carbon emissions were then calculated using the previously mentioned carbon emission factors and methods, thereby determining the total embodied carbon for on-site activities in the conventional construction scenario. Transportation of on-site waste away from site

In this study, the embodied carbon associated with waste transportation away from the construction site for conventional construction is assumed to be identical to the MiC approach. This assumption is necessitated by the absence of specific data on the waste transportation for the hypothetical conventional construction project. It is important to note that this assumption is conservative, implying that it is likely to underestimate the embodied carbon of conventional construction in comparison to MiC. The rationale behind this assertion is that conventional construction typically involves a longer construction period and extensive temporary works, which leads to increased waste generation. By assuming equal embodied carbon values for waste transportation in both methods, we ensure that the carbon assessment is not biased in favor of MiC.

Disposal and processing of on-site waste

The embodied carbon emissions resulting from the disposal and processing of on-site waste are assumed to be equivalent for both MiC and conventional construction methods. This assumption is based on the same rationale as described in Sect. The data presented reveals that the total embodied carbon for the construction of the Kai Tak CIF using the MiC method is quantified at 46,024 tCO2e, while the conventional construction method results in emissions of 58,005 tCO2e. This indicates a substantial emission reduction of approximately 20.7% when employing the MiC approach compared to the conventional method.

A detailed analysis of the individual construction stages reveals that the consumption of construction materials (A1 to A4) accounts for over 70% of the total carbon output during construction. The MiC method demonstrates a 6.1% reduction in emissions compared to conventional methods, indicating more carbon-efficient material utilization. This efficiency gain is primarily attributed to the material choices: MiC predominantly employs structural steel – a highly industry-processed material – with a comparatively minor inclusion of reinforced concrete for floor slabs and raft footing foundations. Conversely, the conventional approach utilizes RC in both the foundation and superstructure. The modest 6.1% reduction in material-related emissions for MiC is noteworthy, considering that structural steel typically has higher embodied carbon than reinforced concrete due to its energy-intensive production process. This suggests that the design efficiencies of MiC, such as the use of smaller-sized members due to steel’s superior strength-to-density ratio, are effectively offsetting the higher initial carbon footprint of steel. Importantly, this leads to both a lighter structure and, consequently, a slimmer raft foundation, contributing to additional material savings and carbon reductions.

However, the potential for further emission reductions in MiC is constrained by factors specific to high-density urban environments like Hong Kong. The need for dense support structures and limitations on module sizes due to transportation and installation constraints in urban areas restrict design flexibility. For example, the column grid of modules B, C, and D is constrained to a predefined span of 3 m due to limitations set by module sizes, which are restricted by transportation and site installation constraints. Conventional construction methods, on the other hand, can accommodate larger spans of 6 m for column grids, which can reduce the number of columns required for the same floor area. Despite these constraints, the MiC method manages to achieve a modest reduction in the carbon footprint of structural materials in the current study. However, there is potential for further improvements. If transportation constraints were relaxed, such as through the use of cargo ships for the entire route where feasible, MiC structures could potentially achieve greater material efficiency, further reducing embodied carbon. This highlights an area where future innovations in logistics and design could yield additional environmental benefits in MiC projects, particularly in high-density urban environments like Hong Kong.

In stage A5a, which pertains to carbon emissions from construction activities, the MiC method realizes significant reductions, generating 5,707 tCO2e in emissions, a 58.1% decrease compared to the 13,636 tCO2e generated by the conventional approach. This represents one of the most significant advantages of MiC over conventional methods in this study. This dramatic decrease is primarily attributed to the shortened construction period, which has implications beyond just carbon emissions. Reduced construction time in urban areas can lead to less disruption to local communities, lower noise pollution, and potentially reduced costs. The reductions in all fuel and material types are consistent, as they are based on the same assumption regarding the length of the construction period. However, it is important to note that the calculation of conventional approach assumes a linear relationship between construction time and emissions, which may not always hold true in practice.

Regarding emissions from on-site waste (A5w), the majority of the savings are attributed to the reduction in material waste from stages A1 to A4, which sees a 74.8% decrease. As discussed in the previous sections, the waste factor is significantly lower with the MiC approach due to more precise fabrication processes that reduce waste generation, a key benefit of prefabrication in construction. For waste transportation, disposal and processing, the carbon emissions are assumed to be identical for both the MiC and conventional approaches. However, it is expected that more savings can be realized from these areas because the assumption of equal carbon emissions for waste transportation, disposal, and processing is only a very conservative estimate. In reality, the longer construction period and extensive temporary works associated with conventional construction would likely lead to higher waste generation and, consequently, increased embodied carbon emissions from waste handling activities compared to MiC. Therefore, the actual carbon savings from MiC in these areas may be even more significant than the results suggest.

Conclusion

The comprehensive analysis conducted in this study of the Kai Tak CIF project has illuminated the environmental benefits of utilizing the MiC method over conventional construction approaches from cradle to end-of-construction. Based on the actual project data and comparison with a hypothetical conventional construction counterpart, this study offers concrete evidence of MiC’s environmental advantages.

The findings revealed that the MiC method resulted in 46,024 tCO2e in embodied carbon, a significant 20.7% decrease compared to the 58,005 tCO2e from conventional construction. This reduction is attributed to several factors: a 6.1% decrease in emissions related to the consumption of construction materials (A1-A4), primarily due to the optimized use of structural steel; a 58.1% reduction in emissions from construction activities (A5a), achieved through shortened construction timelines; and a 74.8% reduction in material waste, resulting in a 39.5% decrease in the total embodied carbon from on-site waste (A5w).

The environmental benefits demonstrated in this study are likely conservative, as they do not account for potential advantages in the end-of-life stage. MiC methods show promise for additional environmental benefits through material recycling and reuse. A recent local project, Nam Cheong 220, demonstrates the practical feasibility of relocating and reusing MiC structures, suggesting that including the end-of-life considerations could further reduce the need for virgin materials and landfill waste, thus enhancing the overall environmental performance of MiC beyond what this study has quantified.

In conclusion, the findings from Kai Tak CIF demonstrate that the MiC approach is not only a viable alternative to conventional construction but also a necessary step forward towards sustainable construction practices. This data-driven case study presents clear evidence of MiC’s environmental advantages, particularly in reducing embodied carbon emissions. As the construction industry continues to evolve, the incorporation of MiC could play a significant role in reducing the sector’s carbon footprint and fostering more ecologically responsible approaches.

While this study primarily focuses on embodied carbon emissions, it has limitations in scope, particularly regarding off-site construction impacts and long-term material recyclability. Future research should address these aspects, along with optimizing design for reduced embodied carbon, selecting sustainable materials, and improving waste management strategies. By investigating these areas, the construction industry can further advance sustainable MiC practices and reduce its environmental impact.
