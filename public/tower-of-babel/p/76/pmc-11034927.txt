Mitf is a Schwann cell sensor of axonal integrity that drives nerve repair
Authors: Lydia Daboussi, Giancarlo Costaguta, Miriam Gullo, Nicole Jasinski, Veronica Pessino, Brendan O’Leary, Karen Lettieri, Shawn Driscoll, Samuel L. Pfaff
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYSchwann cells respond to acute axon damage by transiently transdifferentiating into specialized repair cells that restore sensorimotor function. However, the molecular systems controlling repair cell formation and function are not well defined, and consequently, it is unclear whether this form of cellular plasticity has a role in peripheral neuropathies. Here, we identify Mitf as a transcriptional sensor of axon damage under the control of Nrg-ErbB-PI3K-PI5K-mTorc2 signaling. Mitf regulates a core transcriptional program for generating functional repair Schwann cells following injury and during peripheral neuropathies caused by CMT4J and CMT4D. In the absence of Mitf, core genes for epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, metabolism, and dedifferentiation are misexpressed, and nerve repair is disrupted. Our findings demonstrate that Schwann cells monitor axonal health using a phosphoinositide signaling system that controls Mitf nuclear localization, which is critical for activating cellular plasticity and counteracting neural disease.

INTRODUCTION

In response to injury, many tissues undergo adaptive cellular reprogramming to generate specialized repair cells for wound healing.1,2 Intuitively, this transdifferentiation process could be considered developmental regression involving factors from embryogenesis; however, repair cell generation appears to engage separate pathways dedicated uniquely to injury.1 While the central nervous system has a limited regenerative capacity, the peripheral nervous system possesses an astonishing capability for self-repair. Axonal damage caused by trauma induces mature Schwann cells (SCs), a type of glia within peripheral nerves, to transiently convert into repair cells that restore nerve function.3,4 While it is established that SC plasticity is fundamental for healing and repair, the molecular sensors within SCs that detect axonal damage and safely program mature SCs into repair cells with temporal and spatial precision are not well characterized.

Developmental studies have revealed many cellular steps and molecular factors involved in neural crest development into melanoblasts and SCs (Figure 1A).5 Mature SCs ensheathe peripheral axons as either myelinating or non-myelinating (Remak) cells.6 Upon peripheral nerve injury, loss of axonal contact causes myelinating and Remak SCs to be reprogrammed into repair SCs (Figure 1A).3 The transformation of mature but plastic SCs into repair cells requires reprogramming of the transcriptome, leading to expression of genes associated with stemness (i.e., genes associated with self-renewal), in addition to factors controlling an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), thereby promoting tissue remodeling and injury repair.7–10 These repair cells perform specialized functions not carried out by either mature SCs or SC precursors, including the clearance of axonal debris, activation of innate immune responses, and formation of Büngner bands, which serve as a substrate for the re-growth of axons to peripheral targets.11 Some of the factors controlling repair cell function have begun to be identified, including c-Jun, necessary for myelin clearance, and Stat3, involved in maintaining repair cell survival.12–17

Beyond physical injuries, nerve function is also compromised in hereditary peripheral neuropathies, including many forms of Charcot-Marie-Tooth (CMT) disease. Genetic studies have identified more than 10 recessive genes for CMT type IV (CMT4) alone, affecting axon myelination, SC-axon interactions, and, ultimately, neuronal survival. Thus, CMT4 patients often experience muscle weakness and diminished touch and thermal sensitivity in their distal limbs.18 Candidate factors revealed from human genetics include the phosphoinositide 5-phosphatase encoded by the Fig4 gene mutated in CMT4J, the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) inhibitor Ndrg1 mutated in CMT4D,19,20 the Rab11 effector Sh3tc2 mutated in CMT4C,21,22 and the PI(3)P phosphatase Mtmr2 mutated in CMT4B.23–25 Taken together, the genes linked to CMT4 suggest that second messenger signaling via lipid phosphoinositides may control how SCs respond to extracellular cues.26,27 While it is clear that plasticity is critical for repair following injury, the role of plasticity in neural disease remains enigmatic.

We investigated whether repair SCs are generated and modify disease progression in different types of CMT4 mouse models. To identify how the contact between axons and SCs is sensed, we characterized the transcriptome of nerves from CMT4J (Fig4plt) mice, in which the close apposition of SCs to motor and sensory axons is disrupted.28 Bioinformatics predicted that target genes of the Mit/Tfe family of basic-helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors were altered in CMT4J, suggesting that Mit/Tfe factors are linked to the disease. Mitf, a member of this family, was genetically identified eight decades ago from selection of naturally occurring Mitf-mutant mice that displayed altered pigmentation and coat color.29 The subsequent cloning and characterization of Mitf have revealed that this transcription factor is necessary for the embryonic development of neural crest-derived cell types, including melanocytes (Figure 1A).30–32 We discovered that Mitf is also expressed by mature SCs and that disrupting axon-SC interactions by nerve injury induces the transcription factor to translocate from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. We found that axon damage triggers Mitf nuclear localization by disrupting Nrg1-ErbB-PI3K-PI5K-mTorc2 signaling. Furthermore, we discovered that SCs induce a shared transcriptomic response that mediates repair in injury and disease. Mitf regulates this core transcriptional program in injury and disease-derived repair cells controlling EMT, metabolic and trophic factors, and SC dedifferentiation. Consequently, when Mitf is mutated, nerve repair is disrupted, and peripheral neuropathies such as CMT4D are more severe. Our findings indicate that Mitf controls a core network of genes linked to SC plasticity and repair cell function that modifies how peripheral neuropathies manifest.

RESULTS

Network analysis links the Mit/Tfe bHLH family to CMT4J

Mutations in the lipid phosphatase encoded by the Fig4 gene cause a demyelinating form of CMT disease, termed CMT4J, that leads to muscle weakness in humans. To identify SC responses to CMT4J disease, we sequenced sciatic nerve RNA from the “pale tremor” mouse line, which carries a transposon insertion that disrupts Fig4 (Fig4plt) expression during early onset (post-natal day 4 [P4]) and mid-disease (P14)28,33 (Figures 1B–1E and S1A–S1D; Tables S1 and S2). We used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) to identify transcription factor (TF) binding motifs enriched in the promoter regions of differentially expressed genes in Fig4plt. We found motif-enrichment of 93 TFs in the control and 68 in the Fig4plt mutant during mid-disease stages. Among the candidates, we detected AP-1 motif enrichment in the differentially expressed transcriptome of Fig4plt, suggesting that c-Jun may function during CMT4J (Figures S1E and S1F).12 At early-stage disease, GSEA revealed motif enrichment of 11 TFs in Fig4plt and 4 TFs in WT (Figures 1F and 1G). Tfeb-family bHLH TFs (Tfeb, Tfe3, Tfec, and Mitf) showed the highest enrichment in the differentially expressed genes in sciatic nerves from Fig4plt mutants (Figure 1G). Interestingly, TFEB promoter elements were also enriched in the differential gene expression at later disease time points (Figure S1F), indicating that one or more members of this gene family may regulate several stages of disease response in CMT4J.

Mitf is enriched in SCs

We investigated the role that Mitf, a Mit/Tfe family member, plays in SCs because (1) there are pigmentation defects in Fig4plt mutants, and Mitf is a master regulator of melanocyte development, and (2) Mitf has been shown to directly regulate several of the differentially expressed genes in Fig4plt at P4, including Pmel, Ptgds, and Tryp134,35 (Figures 1C and 1D). Previous studies have shown that Mitf is transiently expressed in the neural crest of the developing embryo and is down-regulated as the melanoblast-glia progenitor cells differentiate into SCs.32,36 However, the role of Mitf in SCs is complicated by the observation that this factor is necessary for the generation of some neural crest-derived cell types, such as retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells and melanocytes in mice.31 This leaves open the question as to whether there is a specific role for Mitf in SCs or only an early function in the neural crest-derived precursors of these glia. To address this, we first generated Mitf-specific antibodies and conducted a time course of protein expression in the sciatic nerve. Low levels of Mitf were detected in the peripheral nerve at birth, followed by an increase at P14 that was sustained through adulthood (Figures 1H–1J). Thus, Mitf has a biphasic expression during SC development: first in the neural crest and then in mature SCs (Figure 1A). In the adult nerve, our analysis revealed that Mitf is expressed by GFAP+ cells (nonmyelinating, Remak) and GFAP− (myelinating SCs) (Figures 1K–1N‴). While these data reveal that the TF Mitf is expressed in mature SCs, we unexpectedly found that it is enriched in the cytoplasm rather than nuclei (Figures 1M–1M‴).

Generation of conditional Mitf alleles

Constitutive mutations of Mitf have revealed its early role in neural crest development, but this obscures the possible function in mature SCs.37 Therefore, we generated a conditional Mitf allele. First, a Mitf knock-in mouse (MitfKI) was made using “knockout-first” targeting constructs (Figure 1L). Homozygous animals for this KI allele (MitfKI/KI) lack Mitf protein expression and are therefore equivalent to a constitutive knockout (Figure S1I). Consistent with previous Mitf loss-of-function mutations, MitfKI/KI mice were albino, had a reduced startle response, and had smaller eyes (microphthalmia) compared with littermate controls38,39 (Figures S1G and S1H). Despite these changes, MitfKI/KI mice did not exhibit signs of peripheral nerve dysfunction and displayed normal locomotor activity, consistent with previous descriptions of Mitf mutants.40

Next, we converted the MitfKI allele to a conditional flox allele (Mitffl), encoding wild-type protein by crossing to a mouse line with germline Flp expression (Figure 1L). To test the conditional nature of the Mitffl/fl allele, we used CDX2:CRE to selectively delete the Mitf gene from the caudal region of developing embryos (MitfCDX2; Figures S1J and S1K; Table S1). As expected, MitfCDX2 animals display caudal-selective albinism because of deletion of Mitf during caudal melanocyte development, thereby confirming the effective design of this Mitf allele for conditional targeting.

Mitf is dispensable for SC development

While hypopigmentation defects arise in mice and humans with Mitf mutations, peripheral nerve-related phenotypes have not been described.30 However, this did not preclude a subtle role of Mitf in SCs exposed to specific physiological conditions. Crossing the Mitffl/fl line to the pan-SC line Krox20:CRE41 (MitfKrox20) selectively and efficiently ablated Mitf expression in peripheral nerve SCs (Figures 1L–1N‴). We examined the sciatic nerves of MitfKrox20 and wild-type (WT) mice at P7 and 3 months of age using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). At both time points, Mitf mutants exhibited normal myelin thickness (G-ratio), axon diameter, Remak bundles, and extracellular matrix (ECM) organization (Figures 2A–2E and S2A–S2E). MitfKrox20 animals had normal grip strength at 3, 6, and 12 months of age and normal rotarod motor performance in adults (Figures 2F, 2G, and S2F–S2J).

To identify molecular changes that might be more subtle than behavioral alterations, we performed RNA sequencing on sciatic nerves from MitfKrox20 and littermate controls. We found only 14 differentially expressed transcripts, 6 of which represented protein-coding mRNAs (Figures 2H and S2M; Table S1). We independently confirmed these findings by deleting Mitf from SCs using DHH:CRE (MitfDhh) and identified 12 differentially expressed genes (Figures S2K–S2L). However, none of the transcripts from the outbred, mixed genetic backgrounds comprising MitfDhh overlapped with MitfKrox20, suggesting that the loss of Mitf in mature SCs leads to few, if any, detectable RNA expression changes. Taken together, the similarities in molecular, cellular, and functional characteristics between WT and conditional Mitf mutant mice suggest that this TF is dispensable for the normal development and function of mature SCs.

Axon transection induces nuclear Mitf translocation

Although Mitf is expressed in SCs, it is localized to the cytoplasm, and the absence of phenotypes in Mitf mutants suggests that it has no function outside of the nucleus. This prompted us to test whether Mitf functions in SCs under non-homeostatic conditions, such as those induced by nerve injury. First, we examined whether Mitf localization changes following nerve transection using an ex vivo preparation that prevents axonal sprouting and lymphocyte infiltration. Similar to the uninjured nerve, on day 1, Mitf was localized to the cytoplasm, whereas by day 3, 89% of SC nuclei had enriched staining for Mitf (Figures 2L–2Q‴). Next, we examined Mitf protein levels in vivo following nerve crush injury. With kinetics similar to the nuclear localization of Mitf ex vivo, 3 days post injury we observed a 3.6-fold increase in protein in vivo (Figures 2I–2K). Interestingly, the injury-induced increase in Mitf protein was not associated with increased RNA, indicating that Mitf protein levels are post-transcriptionally regulated (Figure S2N). These results indicate that axon integrity dynamically influences the protein concentration and localization of Mitf, likely through a post-translational mechanism.

Mitf is required for peripheral nerve regeneration

The increase in Mitf protein and nuclear localization in SCs following axon transection prompted us to investigate whether it has a specific role in nerve regeneration following injury. To identify the time points when loss of Mitf might impact nerve recovery, we conducted a histological time course at 3, 5, 21, 42, and 90 days after peripheral nerve crush (Figure 3A). At 3 and 5 days post-injury (dpi), we found that nerves from MitfKrox20 mice undergo faster axonal degeneration (i.e., Wallerian degeneration), as evidenced by fewer myelinated axons (Figures 3B–3F and S3A–S3D). These findings were striking because the rapid degeneration observed in Mitf mutants is in direct contrast to the slow Wallerian degeneration observed in WldS and c-Jun cKO mouse models12,42. At 21 dpi, Remak SCs were less tightly wrapped around small-caliber axons (Figures S3E–S3F′). These early perturbations result in a series of downstream consequences. At 42 dpi, a striking collection of SC defects were observed in MitfKrox20 animals and included ectopic myelination of small-caliber axons, supernumerary wrapping of small unmyelinated axons, SC wrapping of collagen, enduring Büngner SCs, and axons misdirected through the perineurium (Figures 3I–3K and S3G–S3G‴). At 90 dpi in Mitf mutants, SCs wrapped around collagen fibrils and had lingering SC processes present throughout the ECM (Figures S3H–S3I′). While myelination is normal during development of Mitf mutants, remyelination following injury is altered. Mitf mutants recover from nerve crush with thicker myelin relative to axon diameter (smaller G-ratio), and after regeneration, axon diameter was smaller (Figures 3L–3P). These data suggest that Mitf function is necessary for proper nerve repair.

After transection injuries, which create a gap in the nerve, unlike a crush, repair SCs take on increased mesenchymal characteristics as they invade the gap between the distal and proximal stump and guide axons through the nerve bridge.9 Interestingly, an additional layer of nerve disorganization was revealed after nerve transection in Mitf mutants. 21 days after sciatic nerve transection, we found disorganized, patchy perineurial fibroblast organization, the existence of an inappropriately infiltrating fibroblast cell type, and unmyelinated axons (Figures 3G–3H′). After injury, repair SCs facilitate nerve reconstruction in part through signals to connective tissue.43 Our results indicate that this process is disrupted in Mitf mutants.

We investigated whether the cellular changes we observed in Mitf mutants manifested as behavioral alterations in sensorimotor function following nerve crush. Prior to sciatic nerve injury, WT and MitfKrox20 mice responded with similar latencies to noxious heat on their hind paws. At 28 through 60 dpi, MitfKrox20 animals had significantly longer latencies to this stimulus compared with controls, indicating that recovery of nociceptive sensory function was impaired (Figure 3R). Next, we tested hindlimb grip strength. Prior to injury, WT and MitfKrox20 mice displayed similar grip strength, whereas at 60 dpi the grip strength recovery of injured Mitf mutants was significantly lower than that of the controls (Figure 3Q). These deficits in recovery of sensorimotor function following injury indicate that Mitf is required for effective repair of damaged nerves in vivo.

Mitf regulates a dynamic gene network in nerves undergoing repair

To investigate the molecular basis by which Mitf mediates repair, we conducted a whole-genome RNA sequencing time course following sciatic nerve transection at 3, 7, and 14 dpi (Figure 4A). After injury, mature SCs transition into repair SCs by undergoing EMT and acquiring a phagocytic phenotype8,9 (Figure 4B). EMT is mediated by downregulation of adhesion pathways involving WNT and transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) signaling components.8,9,44 At 3 dpi, WT SCs differentially expressed 74 genes relative to Mitfmutants (34 upregulated, 40 downregulated) (Figure 4C; Tables S1 and S2). Then, at 7 dpi, we detected 345 differentially expressed transcripts (244 upregulated and 101 downregulated) in WT nerves relative to Mitf mutants (Figure 4D). Although Mitf has been linked to lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy, neither of these pathways were significantly affected45–48 (Figures S4E and S4F). Rather, MitfKrox20 animals exhibited dysregulation of genes linked to cellular adhesion and ECM organization (key processes involved in EMT, including Ctnna1, Nfasc, Pecam1, Bcam, Ceacam1, Cadm2, Cadm3, and Itgb4), metabolism and glucose/lactate production (e.g., Pck1, Insr, and Srebf1), signaling molecules (including TGF-β and WNT signaling components), and elevated mature SC markers but not the injury marker Jun (Figures 4D, 4F, S4A, S4B, and S4D; Tables S1 and S2). Taken together, these data suggest that Mitf mutants lack proper SC dedifferentiation, leading to altered EMT and energy metabolism, which are early processes essential for the function of repair SCs.

To determine whether Mitf is required for EMT, we designed a functional cellular test to monitor migratory capacity, a key component of mesenchymal behavior (Figure 4G). We found that SCs lacking Mitf migrated significantly less than WT controls (Figures 4J–4M and 4O). Our data showed that α-catenin (ctnna1) is upregulated in Mitf mutants following injury (Figures 4D and S4B); α-catenin is a marker of focal adhesions and indirectly interacts with Paxillin.49 Following injury, downregulation of Paxillin+ focal adhesions and rearrangement of the actin cytoskeleton are critical for the morphological flexibility and migratory capacity of mesenchymal cells. We detected an increase in the number of Paxillin+ focal adhesions and elevated filamentous actin in Mitf mutant SCs (Figures 4H–4I and 4N). Thus, Mitf mutant SCs are less migratory and have increased adhesion properties, which is counterproductive for EMT and cell migration—key properties of wound healing.

Our sequencing analysis identified transcriptomic changes that impact the dedifferentiation of SCs following injury in Mitf mutants. To determine how these early defects impact SCs at later stages of repair, we sequenced nerves at 14 dpi, a time point of nascent axonal regrowth and subsequent repair cell redifferentiation into mature SCs. Chromatin rearrangement after injury has been shown to be a powerful factor in generating the repair cell state, in particular H3K4me3 marks injury-related genes, such as SHH signaling components.29,50 At 14 dpi, we identified 214 differentially expressed transcripts between WT and MitfKrox20 nerves (157 upregulated and 51 downregulated) (Figure 4E; Tables S1 and S2). MitfKrox20 mutants had elevated injury-related transcripts, including those for histone methylation H3K4me3 and SHH signaling components (Figures 4F and S4C).

Our molecular and cellular analysis following injury reveals defects related to repair cell function and plasticity. First, Mitf mutants exhibit accelerated axonal degeneration, possibly from a disruption of metabolic support (Figures 3B–3F, S3A–S3D, and 4F).51 Second, there are early morphological phenotypes of SCs, arising from changes in adhesion properties that disturb membrane flexibility and migratory capacity (Figures 4F–4O). Third, these early disruptions of SC dedifferentiation disrupt the subsequent redifferentiation process of repair SCs into mature SCs, as evidenced by prolonged injury-related chromatin marks and gene expression (H3K4me3, SHH pathway; Figures 4F and S4C), the persistence of Büngner SCs, excess SC processes in the ECM, ectopic myelination of small-caliber axons, and wrapping of collagen (Figures 3K and S3G–S3I′). Fourth, these defects in SC transformation following injury diminish axon re-growth and the recovery of sensorimotor function of mice in vivo (Figures 3Q and 3R).

mTorc2 controls Mitf localization in SCs

Several kinases have been shown to post-translationally regulate the subcellular localization and transcriptional activity of Mit/Tfe in cell lines (Figure 5A).52 Therefore, we investigated whether Mitf localization was regulated in SCs. To characterize the signaling pathways that control Mitf localization, we established an in vitro primary SC culture system (Figures 5B and S5A–S5B”). Antibody staining of endogenous Mitf revealed that it is detected in the nucleus and cytoplasm of primary SCs (Figures 5C, 5C′, 5O, and S5C). To determine whether kinases known to target the Mit/Tfe family alter the localization of Mitf in SCs, we tested antagonists for ERK, PKCβ, GSK3, AKT, mTorc1/2, and mTorc1.52 Inhibitors of ERK1/2 (Sch772984) and its upstream kinase MEK (trametinib) as well as inhibitors for Akt (MK-2206), PKCα/β/γ/ε (enzastaurin), and GSK3β (BIO) did not alter the subcellular distribution of Mitf (Figures 5A–5H′, 5O, and S5C). Conversely, treatment of cells with Torin, an mTorc1/2 inhibitor, induced translocation of Mitf from the cytoplasm into the nucleus (Figures 5I, 5I′, 5O, and S5C). Treatment with a selective mTorc1 inhibitor, rapamycin, did not alter Mitf localization relative to the control (Figures 5N, 5N′, and S5C). These data indicate that mTorc2 kinase controls Mitf localization. Interestingly, the Mit/Tfe family member TFEB is activated by mTorc1.45 Previous studies have shown that mTorc2 kinase activity is stimulated at the plasma membrane through the production of PI(3,4,5)P3.53,54 To test whether this lipid pathway regulates the localization of Mitf, we inhibited lipid kinases that directly or indirectly contribute to the generation of PI(3,4,5)P3. We inhibited Pikfyve (YM201636) to block the generation of PI(3,5)P2 and Vps34 (VPS34-IN1) to block the generation of PI(3)P. Inhibition of both lipid kinases induced localization of Mitf into the nucleus (Figures 5J–5K′, 5O, and S5C). These data reveal a lipid kinase signaling cascade that culminates with mTorc2 regulation of Mitf localization (Figure 5P).

Nrg is an axon-to-SC signal regulating Mitf

ErbB receptors are expressed by SCs and activate PI3K signaling;55 therefore, we tested whether Nrg1 influenced the localization of Mitf. Addition of the ErbB ligand hNrg-1 increased Mitf cytoplasmic retention (Figures 5M, 5M′, 5O, and S5C). Thus, Nrg1 from healthy axons likely activates SC-ErbB receptors and PI3K signaling that suppresses Mitf function by preventing nuclear translocation (Figure 5P). Our findings predict that axonal damage disrupts Nrg1-ErbB signaling, thereby increasing nuclear Mitf.6,56,57 To test this model, we used the pan-ErbB inhibitor canertinib and found that it induced nuclear enrichment of Mitf, similar to inhibition of PI3K and mTOR (Figures 5L, 5L′, 5O, and S5C). These data indicate that Nrg-ErbB signaling plays a key role in regulating the subcellular distribution of Mitf in SCs (Figures 5P and 5Q).

Peripheral neuropathy activates Mitf

In many forms of CMT, the physical interaction between SCs and axons is disrupted, raising the question of whether repair cells are formed and influence disease progression. To investigate Mitf and repair cell function, we created a “toolbox” of conditional CMT mouse models by targeting genes linked to diseases that cause myelination and axonal degeneration defects. Gene targeting was used to make floxed alleles of Mtmr2fl (CMT4B), Sh3tc2fl (CMT4C), and Ndrg1fl (CMT4D) (Figure 6A). CMT4B/Mtmr2 is a dysmyelinating peripheral neuropathy characterized by myelin outfoldings, without major signs of axonal loss23,58 (Figures 6B and 6C). CMT4C/Sh3tc2 is a hypomyelinating disorder with mild, progressive axonal loss,59 and CMT4D/Ndrg1 exhibits hypomyelination that leads to severe axonal atrophy20 (Figures 6D and 6E). We found that selective deletion of these genes from SCs in mice using DHH:CRE closely mimicked the human disease pathology of each respective disorder. Namely, Mtmr2Dhh displayed excessive myelin outfoldings of axons, Sh3tc2Dhh displayed hypomyelination, and Ndrg1Dhh had hypomyelination and axonal loss (Figures S6A–S6C). Importantly, these conditional gene mutations demonstrate that each factor has SC-autonomous functions that are required for peripheral nerve integrity.

Although Mtmr2Dhh animals exhibited significant hindlimb and forelimb weakness (Figures S6D and S6E), Mitf levels and subcellular localization in SCs were normal (Figures 6F–6H and S6I). Sh3tc2Dhh animals exhibit hypomyelination and display significant hindlimb and forelimb grip strength weakness (Figures S6F and S6G); Mitf is modestly upregulated in Sh3tc2Dhh animals (1.3-fold increase, p < 0.05; Figures 6I–6K and S6J). Ndrg1Dhh animals exhibit severe axonal loss and hypomyelination and are significantly weaker than littermate controls (Figures S7G and S7J). Mitf protein is elevated in Ndrg1Dhh animals and was enriched in Sox10+ nuclei compared with controls (4.6-fold increase, p < 0.05; Figures 6L–6N, S6H, and S6K–S6M”) but not F4/80+ macrophages (Figures S6N–S6O”). Thus, while Mitf activation is not linked to all forms of peripheral neuropathy, it does correlate with the breakdown of axon-SC interaction in the context of disease. The most extreme form of axonal loss is observed in Ndrg1Dhh (CMT4D), correlating with the most dramatic increase in the overall levels and nuclear localization of Mitf.

Shared transcriptomic response in injury and chronic disease

The increase in Mitf protein concentration and nuclear localization in Ndrg1Dhh (CMT4D) is reminiscent of Mitf dynamics following nerve injury (compare Figure 6N with Figure 2J). We therefore compared the transcriptomic changes of the nerve from Ndrg1Dhh mice with transected nerves from WT mice. Intriguingly, we detected overlapping patterns of differential gene expression in injury and Ndrg1Dhh (Figures 6O–6Q and S6P–S6R; Tables S1 and S2). Up- and down-regulated genes were strongly cross-correlated between nerve injury and Ndrg1Dhh, and Gene Ontology (GO) term analysis revealed coordinate alterations of cellular metabolism, cytoskeleton, and cell junction organization (Table S2). Similarly, these pathways were also perturbed in Fig4plt (CMT4J) nerves (Figure S7A).

Given the remarkable degree of shared differential gene expression between injury, Ndrg1Dhh, and Fig4plt, we examined whether there was a core transcriptomic program that was shared across these conditions. We found that a core group of 571 genes was coordinately differentially expressed in injury (3 dpi), CMT4D (Ndrg1Dhh), and CMT4J (Fig4plt) (Figures 7A and 7B). Several themes emerged from the GO term analysis of these shared genes: (1) regulation of cell migration, adhesion, and cell morphogenesis, hallmarks of the required EMT that mature SCs undergo to form functional repair cells; (2) changes in glial cell differentiation and nervous system development consistent with SC transdifferentiation into a repair cell; and (3) changes to the lipid, sterol, and alcohol biosynthetic process consistent with changes in cellular metabolism as mature SCs transform into repair cells (Figure 7C). These data suggest the existence of a “core” 571-gene transcriptional response of the peripheral nerve that is shared between injury and chronic disease (CMT4D and CMT4J).

Mitf regulation of core programs activated by nerve injury and disease

To identify potential regulators of the 571 “core” genes in injury and disease (CMT4D/Ndrg1Dhh and CMT4J/Fig4plt), we identified TF binding motifs enriched in the core gene promoter regions. GSEA revealed motif enrichment of three TF families represented by PSMB5, PBX1, and TFEB (the Mit/Tfe family, which includes Mitf) (Figures 7D and 7E). Next, we compared the “core” pathways predicted to be regulated by Mit/Tfe family members with the pathways misregulated in the Mitf mutants following injury. We found that the genes and pathways Mitf regulates following nerve injury overlap with those altered by disease (Figures 7F, 7G, and S7L). These findings indicate that Mitf may control a core gene set whose pattern of expression is similar following injury and CMT4D/J.

Mitf attenuates CMT4D in vivo

Because Mitf was predicted to regulate a core gene program for nerve repair, we sought to determine whether this TF is functionally active in CMT4D disease. We performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on sciatic nerves from WT, MitfDhh, Ndrg1Dhh, and Ndrg1Dhh MitfDhh mice. As expected, the transcriptomes of WT and uninjured MitfDhh nerves were similar (only 14 upregulated and 29 downregulated genes; Figures 7H and S7B; Tables S1 and S2). In contrast, comparison of the sciatic nerve transcriptome from Ndrg1Dhh animals with Ndrg1Dhh MitfDhh double mutants identified 222 upregulated and 85 downregulated genes (Figures 7I and S7C; Tables S1 and S2). GO term analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed dysregulation of pathways linked to adhesion, cell migration, and homeostasis in Ndrg1Dhh MitfDhh nerves (Figures S7D–S7F).

While MitfDhh animals do not exhibit grip strength phenotypes, we discovered that Ndrg1Dhh MitfDhh double mutants were significantly weaker than single Ndrg1Dhh mutants (Figures 7J and S7G). We next assayed motor coordination using an irregular ladder beam. Uninjured WT and MitfDhh animals were coordinated and rarely missed rungs, whereas Ndrg1Dhh mice had detectable missteps. The Ndrg1Dhh MitfDhh animals performed significantly worse than the Ndrg1Dhh mutants (Figures 7K and S7H–S7K; Videos S1, S2, S3, and S4). To identify potential cellular defects in these mutants, we analyzed TEM of the sciatic nerve. The organization of SCs and axon size distribution was similar between WT controls and (uninjured) MitfDhh mutants. However, we found that Ndrg1Dhh MitfDhh animals had many fewer large-caliber axons compared with Ndrg1Dhh single mutants, and axons had thicker myelin than normal (Figures 7L–7O). Given that uninjured MitfDhh animals were indistinguishable from WT, the behavioral and cellular phenotypes arising from NdrgDhh MitfDhh double mutants are not simply additive of those in the single mutants. These data reveal that Mitf has a compensatory function that attenuates the natural progression of CMT4D in vivo and is a key regulator of gene expression induced by this disease.

DISCUSSION

Physical damage to peripheral nerves is efficiently repaired by SCs, which detect axonal damage and transiently transform into repair cells that re-establish myelination and enable neuronal connections with their targets. Together, these important steps in repair restore sensorimotor function. In this study, we identify the TF Mitf as a critical regulator of SC transdifferentiation and repair cell function whose subcellular localization is coupled to the Nrg1 signaling pathway. An important discovery that emerged from the molecular characterization of SC plasticity was uncovering the role Mitf and repair cells play in limiting the severity of inherited peripheral neuropathies such as CMT4D. We discuss how SCs detect axonal damage leading to activation of a core genetic program controlled by Mitf that counteracts nerve damage from injuries and diseases.

Mitf is a sensor of axonal damage

Although Mitf is constitutively expressed by mature SCs, its transcriptional activity appears to be linked to post-translational modifications that regulate its subcellular localization. Our findings suggest mature SCs are readily poised to switch their gene expression in response to nerve damage by activating a cytoplasmic-to-nuclear redistribution of Mitf. To understand how Mitf localization is gated, we probed a variety of signaling pathways using pharmacological agents. We employed a pharmacological approach because many of the kinases shown previously to influence Mit/TFE family localization and activity are pleiotropically required for the development of several tissues and cell types. Small molecular inhibitors therefore allowed us to temporally inhibit each kinase specifically in purified populations of SCs. Furthermore, peripheral neuropathies are a wide-spread health concern that currently lacks robust therapeutic options; these experiments represent a first step toward identifying targetable intracellular molecules that may facilitate nerve repair. We found that the Nrg1 ligand, expressed by sensory and motor neurons, activates ErbB receptors on SCs, and this inhibits Mitf. Thus, the interaction between SCs and healthy axons triggers Nrg1-ErbB signaling that holds Mitf in a transcriptional “off” state within the cytoplasm of SCs (Figure 5P). Consistent with previous characterization of Nrg1-ErbB downstream signaling, the cytoplasmic retention of Mitf is dependent on a phosphoinositide signaling cascade regulated by PI3K, PI5K, and the mTORC2 variant of the mTOR serine/threonine kinase complex in SCs (Figure 5P). Consequently, axon damage is predicted to reduce Nrg1-ErbB signaling, leading to downregulation of mTORC2 activity and nuclear translocation of Mitf (Figure 5Q). Interestingly, Mitf protein levels increase following nerve injury, whereas mRNA levels remain constant. Thus, Nrg1 signaling likely controls the localization and the half-life of Mitf protein.

Mitf is a member of the Mit/Tfe family of TFs, which includes Tfeb, Tfec, Tfe3, and Mitf, expressed in varying combinations across many cell types.52 For example, Tfeb is enriched in oligodendrocytes, the myelinating glia of the central nervous system that serve as the counterpart to SCs in the peripheral nervous system.60 While it is tempting to compare Tfeb and Mitf functions, Tfeb regulates oligodendrocyte cell number during development, whereas we found that Mitf is dispensable for SC development but required for nerve repair. Interestingly, Mit/Tfe TFs have been linked to the regulation of lysosomal function and autophagy,45,47,52,61 but neither Tfeb in oligodendrocytes60 nor Mitf in SCs was found to be required for these functions. In the context of SCs, this apparent discrepancy could be due to transcriptional redundancy because Tfeb, Mitf, and other family members are co-expressed and may therefore cooperatively regulate autophagy. The disruption of autophagy following nerve injury in calcineurin mutants indicates that this phosphatase may contribute to the activation of multiple Mit/Tfe family members in SCs.62

Mitf’s role in SC plasticity

Peripheral nerve recovery from injury is dependent on the ability of SCs to transform into repair SCs.1 The functions of repair cells include metabolic support of axons, macrophage recruitment, clearance of cellular debris, and formation of Büngner scaffolds.5 After axons regrow, repair cells revert back into mature SCs that remyelinate axons and thereby restore efficient nerve conduction necessary for sensory and motor function.6 Interestingly, some of these repair steps can be dissociated with genetics. The mutation of Mitf causes accelerated degradation of injured axons and aberrant remyelination of new axons, but myelin debris is removed normally. In contrast, SCs lacking c-Jun fail to clear myelin debris following nerve injury.12 These genetically separable functions support the view that the transformation of SCs into repair cells followed by the retransformation of repair cells back into mature SCs is a tightly choreographed sequential process. However, it also remains possible that mature myelinating and non-myelinating Remak SCs, for example, transform into separate subpopulations of repair cells following injury that have distinct functions specified by different genetic pathways.

In the absence of Mitf, we found that damaged axons degenerate more rapidly, possibly because the glycolytic activity of Mitf-deficient repair SCs is compromised.51,63 In addition, Mitf mutants displayed striking alterations in remyelination following injury. It is not known why remyelination is defective; however, we found that chromatin organization genes became dysregulated in Mitf mutants, and this might impair the gene expression necessary for conversion of repair cells back to myelinating SCs. Taken together, our studies indicate that Mitf is necessary for two critical aspects of SC plasticity: the transformation of mature SCs into functional repair cells that support axonal integrity and the redifferentiation of repair cells into functional myelinating SCs that restore normal nerve conduction.

Nerve homeostasis and core genetic programs

Peripheral neuropathies arise from gene mutations in Fig4 (CMT4J), Ndrg1 (CMT4D), Sh3tc2 (CMT4C), and Mtmr2 (CMT4B). These genes directly encode and/or regulate phosphatases, kinases, and GTPases involved in cellular signaling and membrane trafficking. We found that selectively deleting the Ndrg1 and Sh3tc2 genes in SCs led to sensorimotor defects (i.e., peripheral neuropathies), indicating that each of these factors functions intrinsically within SCs and is required for maintaining healthy nerves. When we compared the transcriptional changes in nerves from Fig4(plt) and Ndrg1 cKO mutants with the gene expression in injured nerves, we discovered a core set of 571 genes shared across each condition. The predicted functions of this core set of common genes were cell dedifferentiation, metabolism, and migration. Thus, the gene expression alterations arising from chronic degenerative conditions such as CMT4J (Fig4(plt)) and CMT4D (Ndrg1 cKO) have striking similarities to the genes activated by acute nerve injury. The promoters of this core gene set are enriched for Mit/Tfe binding sites, suggesting that Mitf may contribute to their regulation. This was further supported by the finding that mutation of Mitf caused sensorimotor deficits to become more severe in CMT4D (Ndrg1 cKO). Taken together, the expression of this core gene set represents a beneficial transcriptional response for promoting injury repair and offsetting disease progression.

Previously, there have not been clear markers for repair cells. This study reveals genetic signatures for this cell population that are shared between injury and disease, which include nuclear localization of Mitf and the activation of the core gene set it controls. We found that Mitf is necessary for the generation of functional repair cells and that this TF attenuates neurodegeneration in CMT4D (Ndrg1 cKO). Thus, it is likely that repair cells are actively generated and provide a functional benefit during the course of this disease. Moreover, these findings raise the possibility that SC transdifferentiation is a process that is also needed to maintain healthy nerves by replacing support cells and repairing myelin as needed. Thus, the core gene program controlled by Mitf, including factors involved in dedifferentiation, metabolism, and migration, may represent targets that counteract the effects of aging, injury, and disease on peripheral nerve function.

Limitations of the study

The approach used in this study to identify the regulation of Mitf intracellular localization in primary SCs relies on the use of small-molecule kinase inhibitors, which may have off-target effects. We sought to mitigate these concerns through the construction of an internally consistent signaling pathway. Future studies using new mouse lines of Mitf phosphomutants will be needed to fully explore the post-translational regulation of Mitf in detail.

Western blots

Sciatic nerves were quickly dissected after euthanasia and placed on dry ice prior to lysis or preservation at −80°C. Protein lysates were generated by nerve homogenization in 50mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 300mM NaCl, 1% Tx-100 with 0.5% Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sigma #P8340). Lysates were centrifuged at 4°C at 15,000 RPM for 15 min in a table top centrifuge (Eppendorf 5424R). The supernatant’s protein concentration was measured using Bradford’s assay (Bio-Rad #5000202), and adjusted to 1 μg/μL with lysis buffer. Samples were denatured in Laemmli Sample Buffer (5X: 10% SDS, 50% Glycerol, 312mM Tris-HCL pH 6.8, 2% β-Mercaptoethanol) and heated to 100°C for 5 min. 5–10 μg of total protein was analyzed by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) followed by immunoblot with in-house rabbit anti-Mitf antibody (1:250) and mouse anti-GAPDH (1:5000) followed by DyLight or Alexa-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10000) and scanning on an Odyssey CLX scanner (Li-Cor Biosciences). Image quantification was performed on Odyssey software (Li-Cor Biosciences).

Surgical procedures

A blend of fentanyl (0.05 mg/kg), Midazolam (5 mg/kg), Medetomidin/Dormitor (0.5 mg/kg) were used as anesthesia for all procedures. The unilateral or bilateral nerve crush was conducted by crushing the sciatic nerve below the sciatic notch using cooled forceps and marking the crush site with carbon. Nerve transections were cut at the sciatic notch. After transection, the distal stump was positioned 1–2 mm away from the proximal nerve so that axons could regrow through the distal stump. For analgesia, 0.1 mg/kg buprenorphine SR was injected intraperitoneally immediately following the surgery, before the animals were revived with a blend of Flumaxenil/Anexate (0.5 mg/kg) and Antisedan (2.5 mg/kg). Ibuprofen was administered (0.112 mg/mL) in the water for up to 7 days following surgery. Animals were monitored in accordance with our IACUC protocol.

Electron microscopy

Sciatic nerves were freshly dissected after euthanasia and immersed in Karnovsky’s fixative (2.5% glutaraldehyde and 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1M Cacodylate buffer) and incubated overnight at 4°C. The next day samples were washed 3 times in 0.1M Sodium Cacodylate, pH7.4 for 15 min. Samples were then incubated in 2% osmium tetraoxide, 0.1M Sodium Cacodylate, for 1 h at room temperature (RT) in the dark followed by 3 washes 15 min each with molecular biology grade water at RT, and stained with 2% Uranylacetate for 1 h in the dark at RT. Samples were serially dehydrated from 25% ethanol to 100% ethanol and then submerged in 100% acetone for 15 min, followed by immersion in a mixture of 2:1 Acetone:EPON for 1–2 h at RT. The samples are left overnight in 1:1 Acetone:EPON, followed the next day by incubation in fresh 100% EPON for 4 h at RT and finally embedded overnight in EPON at 65°C. Fixatives and embedding chemicals are from Electron Microscopy Sciences. EPON resin was mixed according to the manufacturer specifications.

Morphological analysis

Semi-thin sections (500 nm) were stained with 1% toluidine blue and used for myelinated axon counts. Ultrathin sections (45–50 nm) were imaged with an FEI Tecnai Spirit G2 BioTWIN Transmission Electron Microscope equipped with an Eagle 16 megapixel camera. Random images were acquired for analysis. To calculate G-Ratio (i.e., the ratio between axon diameter and axon plus myelin diameter), the axon diameter and fiber diameters were measured manually using FIJI (ImageJ). At least 100 fibers per nerve were analyzed.

Animal behavior

The Hargreaves Assay was conducted using mice habituated in the testing environment for 2 h before the day of the assay, and 2 h on the day of the assay. Animals were then placed on a standard Hargreaves glass platform, where they were set in single isolation. The right (injured) hind paw was measured once every 5 min. Animals were only measured if they were inactive before the test began. Lamp power was set to 30%. Weekly measurements were conducted before nerve crush, and then each week for 8 weeks after nerve crush. The Rotarod Assay was conducted using a standard rotarod program accelerating from 0 to 30 rotations per minute over 5 min; the latency time for each animal was measured over 4 trials. The Grip Strength Assay: Both hindlimbs or both forelimbs were tested together using a standard grip strength apparatus measured using ‘Grams of Force’ [GF]. For some animals (Figure 3) either the right (injured) or the left (uninjured) hindlimb was tested for strength individually. The Ladder Beam Assay was performed by a blind observer who acclimated the mice to the ladder by allowing them to walk across with all prongs inserted. Designated ladder rungs were then removed, and mice were allowed to walk across the ladder 3 times. Simultaneous video capture of both the side and belly profiles were obtained. Slips were counted when an animal’s hindlimb completely missed a ladder rung, such that the entire leg falls below the level of the ladder; cumulative missteps were recorded. Male and female mice were used for all behavioral assays.

Primary Schwann cell culture

To prepare primary cultures of mouse Schwann cells, sciatic nerves were dissected at P4 from ICR wild type animals and cultured to ~50% confluence, typically taking 2–4 days. The nerves were placed into warm Dissection Media (Low Glucose-DMEM (Life Technologies), 0.025M HEPES (Life Technologies), Penicillin/Streptomycin (Life Technologies), and antibiotic/antimyotic (Life Technologies). Nerves were then treated with 1.25 mg/mL trypsin and 2 mg/mL collagenase for 20 min at 37°C. Nerves were washed in Feeding Media (10% FBS (Life Technologies), Low Glucose-DMEM (Life Technologies), 0.025M HEPES (Life Technologies), Penicillin/Streptomycin (Life Technologies), and antibiotic/antimyotic (Life Technologies)). Nerves were then triturated sequentially through an 18-gauge and then a 21-gauge needle. Epineurium and debris were removed with sterile tweezers and then remaining cells were plated onto PDL-coated coverslips in 50 μL droplets. Cells were allowed to adhere to the coverslip for 1–2 h and then additional Feeding Media was added to submerge the cells. Primary Schwann cells were tested for purirty by antibody staining with S100, Sox10, GFAP and by using Td-tomato reporters (Ai75) with Dhh:Cre and Krox20:Cre. Agonist/Antagonist Assay: Typically, cells grew for 3–5 days before the addition of drugs. Agonists and Inhibitors were added at 50% confluency for 18 h, except for BIO (GSK3 inhibitor) which was incubated for 5 h. All inhibitors were tested by a titration assay at 1X, 10X, 100X, 1000X (or up to the highest concentration before LD50). In no cases did higher concentrations of inhibitor impact Mitf localization differently than the established concentrations analyzed in this study. Scratch Assay: Schwann cells were grown to 100% confluency as determined by light microscopy. A pipette was then applied to the coverslip to remove cells from the center of the coverslip. Cells were incubated for 24 h, washed with 1x PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in 1x PBS and stained with 66 μM phalloidin-488 at 1:500 for 1 h at RT in the dark.

Nerve explants

Distal Sciatic Nerve segments from Krox20:Cre, Ai75 (Lox-Stop- Loxnuclear Td-tomato reporter) mice were quickly dissected and either immediately fixed (time point 0, uninjured condition) or transferred to warm Feeding Media and cultured in 5% CO2 at 37°C. Nerve Dissections for RNA-sequencing

Sciatic nerves were rapidly dissected after euthanasia, and quick-frozen on dry ice. Sciatic nerves were lysed with a pellet pestle in RLT buffer (Qiagen) with β-mercaptoethanol. Total RNA was isolated using Qiagen RNAeasy Plus Micro kit (# 74034). The Agilent Bio-analyzer (Tape Station) was used to determine RNA integrity (RIN) prior to library preparation. Stranded mRNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit according to manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina). Libraries were then quantified, pooled, and sequenced at paired end 75 (PE75) base-pair using the Illumina NexSeq (150 cycle, high output) at the Salk Institute for Biological Studies Next Generation Sequencing Core (Figures 1, 4, S1, and S4; Fig4 developmental and Mitf injury data) or the University of California, San Diego Genomic Sequencing Core (Figures 7 and S7 Mitf and Ndrg1 data).

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

RNA-seq analysis

Single and paired-end RNA-Seq reads were quantified using Kallisto against the Gencode (M17) transcript database based on the mm10 mouse genome. Estimated counts and TPM values for transcripts were imported into R for downstream analysis. Prior to further analysis the expression and count values for transcripts were summed into gene level values. Differential expression testing was performed with DESeq2 using default options. Genes were marked as significantly differentially expressed with post-hoc adjusted p values less than 0.05. VENN Diagrams: The VENN diagrams in Figures 7A and S6J show the shared significant and same fold change direction genes. Genes are shared between conditions if they have post-hoc corrected p values <0.05 and they are also both enriched or depleted relative to the control conditions. Figure 7F shows the intersection of two separate GO-term enrichment tests where the two lists of significant terms are intersected. Gene Ontology: Functional annotation enrichment was performed for significantly mis-regulated genes with the MSIGDB annotation database66 using the hypergeometric test. Each functional annotation term is tested for enrichment in significantly up-regulated and down-regulated genes. The gene set resulting in the lowest p value is considered to be the set enriched in the annotation term. Figures 1F and 1G: We used the GSEA method67 to discover transcription factor targets correlated with the differential expression phenotype. This method has been shown to discover biologically relevant annotation with phenotypes despite not having a large number of DE genes. Clustering: Functional annotation term clustering is performed by constructing a symmetrical matrix of all pairwise Jaccard index values based on shared associated genes between terms. This matrix is then dimension reduced with principal component analysis (PCA). Significant components are then used to cluster the terms by way of a k-nearest neighbors (k-NN) graph with k approximately equal to the square root of the number of terms being clustered. The graph is then partitioned with the Louvain algorithm (‘cluster_louvain’ function, igraph package, R). This partitioning algorithm is non-deterministic and may not produce an optimal clustering solution with default settings so we run many iterations across a range of ‘resolution’ values (typically 0.2 to 3) and evaluate each resulting clustering’s silhouette scores. We then fit the resulting silhouette scores against the number of clusters in each solution using local regression (‘loess’ function, R) to find an optimal solution with the best possible partitioning of the terms. Typically this means finding the highest peak in the regression curve. The clustering solution with the best silhouette score at the found peak was used as the final clustering solution.

Heatmaps

Following clustering of the annotation terms, we selected the top to most significantly affected terms from each cluster for representation in the heatmap. The selection is based on p value. We show the ‘escore’ of the terms in the heatmap which is simply −log10(p value).

Gene Ontology

Functional annotation enrichment was performed for significantly mis-regulated genes against the background of all expressed genes in the samples using the hypergeometric test. Significantly enriched terms were clustered by constructing a nearest neighbors graph where edges were defined by shared genes associated with the terms being clustered. Terms that share more genes are considered “closer” to one another in the clustering compared to terms that share fewer or no genes at all. We used 10 nearest neighbors in the graph construction. The resulting networks were clustered using the igraph R package’s community detection function ‘cluster_infomap’ with default options.

Except for RNA-sequencing analysis, all statistical analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1. Appropriate tests are described in the corresponding figure legends. Significance was assigned according to Graphpad as: ns = p ≥ 0.05, (*) = 0.01 ≤ p < 0.05, (**) = 0.001 ≤ p < 0.01, (***) = 0.0001 ≤ p < 0.001, (****) = p < 0.0001. All error bars correspond to standard error of the mean (S.E.M.).
