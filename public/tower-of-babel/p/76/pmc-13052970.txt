Effectiveness of the Implementation of a Bundle Protocol for the Reduction of Postmastectomy Reconstruction Failures
Authors: Celeste M. Guillén Rodríguez, Alberto Jiménez Puente, Francisco Rivas Ruiz, Alejandro J. Rohde Calleja, Antonio Rueda Domínguez, Beatriz Sánchez Jiménez, José C. Maañón Di Leo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Immediate breast reconstruction with implants after mastectomy offers significant clinical and psychological benefits. However, implant loss due to infection or complications remains a major concern. Variability in surgical practices may contribute to this issue. This study aims to evaluate whether implementing a standardized protocol (bundle) of intra- and postoperative measures reduces the rate of reconstruction failures within 12 months of surgery.Methods:A retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at a single institution, including 102 patients who underwent 119 implant-based breast reconstructions between January 2022 and May 2024. Outcomes were compared before and after implementation of a surgical bundle designed to minimize infection and complications. The bundle included measures such as intraoperative sterility practices, implant handling protocols, and prolonged antibiotic prophylaxis. The primary endpoint was the rate of implant loss and moderate-to-major complications. Multivariate logistic regression was used to assess associations between bundle implementation and outcomes.Results:Patients in the postbundle group had significantly fewer moderate-to-major complications (6.8% versus 24.6%, P = 0.008) and lower rates of implant loss (3.4% versus 14.8%). Bundle implementation was associated with a 6.4-fold reduction in the odds of experiencing a moderate or major complication (odds ratio, 6.40; 95% confidence interval, 1.81–22.62). No significant differences were found in baseline clinical characteristics between groups.Conclusions:Implementing a standardized protocol significantly reduced complications and implant loss in immediate postmastectomy reconstruction. These findings support adopting surgical safety measures to improve reconstructive outcomes. Further multicenter studies are needed to confirm long-term effectiveness.

Takeaways

Question: Can the implementation of a structured intra- and postoperative “bundle” protocol reduce moderate-to-severe complications and implant loss rates in immediate postmastectomy breast reconstruction?

Findings: In the retrospective cohort study (119 reconstructions, 102 patients), outcomes were compared before and after surgical bundle implementation. The postbundle group showed significantly fewer moderate-to-severe complications (6.8% versus 24.6%; P = 0.008) and lower implant loss rates (3.4% versus 14.8%), with an adjusted risk reduction (odds ratio 6.40).

Meaning: A standardized surgical safety bundle markedly decreases complications and implant losses following immediate postmastectomy breast reconstruction, enhancing patient safety and clinical outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Immediate postmastectomy prosthetic reconstruction in women with breast cancer provides excellent clinical, cosmetic, and psychological outcomes. It is also a more cost-effective option than delayed reconstruction.1–4

In recent years, the evolution of postmastectomy reconstruction has expanded reconstructive techniques to include hybrid approaches and single-stage immediate reconstruction, performed in either the subpectoral or prepectoral plane. In our institution, polyurethane-coated prostheses—alone or combined with biological meshes—are commonly used for their potential to reduce capsular contracture and improve implant stability.5 Internationally, immediate implant-based reconstruction represents more than 50% of cases,6 and according to the American Society of Plastic Surgeons, 82% of reconstructions in 2019 used direct implants or tissue expanders.

Despite its advantages, this technique inevitably carries risks, especially implant failure and explantation. This is an undesirable situation for the healthcare system, patients, and their body image. A recent iBRA (Implant-Based Reconstruction Outcomes) study showed that about 9% of implants are lost.7

The use of perioperative antibiotics to reduce surgical wound infections is well established. Cefazolin is typically used, with vancomycin or clindamycin as alternatives for patients with penicillin allergy. The role of prolonged postoperative prophylaxis in prosthesis-based postmastectomy reconstruction remains controversial. Although some studies have reported a reduction in surgical site infection rates with courses extending beyond 7 days, others have found no significant benefit compared with a 24-hour regimen. In our setting, and in line with protocols that have achieved low implant loss rates, prolonged antibiotic prophylaxis was included in the standardized protocol.8,9 Several factors are associated with an increased risk of reconstructive failure, including smoking, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, and hypothermia.9,10

Surgeons performing postmastectomy reconstruction use various infection-reduction measures based on their experience. A survey of the American Society of Plastic Surgeons found “considerable heterogeneity” in how surgeons apply these measures, including implant placement technique, prosthetic pocket irrigation, and perioperative antibiotics.11

Consequently, a standardized protocol for surgical care (bundle) has been introduced to minimize infection and reduce reconstructive failure in patients undergoing postmastectomy prosthetic reconstruction surgery. This protocol is defined as a set of strategies implemented during preoperative, intraoperative, or perioperative care routines.

However, the long-term impact of applying a standardized protocol remains unclear. A previous study by the same team, with a minimum follow-up period of 9 months, identified a 22.6% failure rate in reconstructions performed between 2002 and 2009. The most common cause of reconstruction failure was infection, occurring on average 5.3 (SD 7.6) months after the procedure, whereas the actual failure typically happened around 8.5 (SD 7.2) months postoperatively.12 Therefore, the main objective of this study is to analyze the effectiveness of implementing a standardized protocol of intra- and postoperative measures with a 12-month follow-up period to evaluate its impact on reducing the rate of infection-related prosthetic loss.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

A retrospective observational cohort study was conducted to compare the outcomes of patients undergoing breast reconstruction with prostheses after mastectomy, before and after the implementation of a standardized protocol (bundle) of intraoperative and postoperative measures. It was approved by the institutional ethics committee in February 2025 (protocol 005_feb25—Bundle, CEI Costa del Sol). As this was a retrospective study without direct intervention or additional personal data collection, a waiver of informed consent was granted. The study analyzed all patients who underwent postmastectomy reconstruction at Hospital Universitario Costa del Sol in Marbella (Spain), from January 1, 2022, to May 31, 2024.

The study period was divided into 2 time frames: pre- and postimplementation of the bundle. It was limited to intraoperative and postoperative practices aimed at reducing postoperative complications13,14 (Table 1). Fifty patients were included in the prebundle period, and 52 patients in the postbundle period. Because some patients underwent bilateral surgery, there were a total of 119 reconstructions (61 in the pregroup and 58 in the postgroup).

In both study periods, prepectoral reconstructions were predominantly performed using micropolyurethane-coated, silicone-filled implants, whereas subpectoral reconstructions typically used microtextured, silicone-filled implants. Implant selection was based on surgical plane, aiming to optimize stability, minimize capsular contracture, and achieve favorable aesthetic outcomes. Data were extracted from the Hospital Universitario Costa del Sol clinical information system, including surgical records, electronic medical records, and postoperative follow-up reports. The exclusion criteria included the use of preoperative radiotherapy and stage T4b breast carcinoma. The endpoint was short-term implant loss within 90 days and 12 months after surgery.

The sample size was calculated with an alpha risk of 0.05 and a power greater than 0.8 for a 2-sided test. A total of 120 subjects (60 in each group) are required to detect a statistically significant difference between 2 proportions, expected to be 0.22 for group 1 and 0.05 for group 2. A loss to follow-up rate of 5% was estimated. The following predictor variables were considered: age, tobacco use, diabetes, BMI, neoadjuvant chemotherapy and adjuvant radiation therapy (ART), reconstruction technique, and implant volume.

Complications were classified into 3 categories, following clinical relevance and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention surgical site infection classification principles:

Mild complications: self-limited events not requiring readmission, reoperation, or prolonged hospitalization (eg, seroma, superficial skin necrosis, small wound dehiscence).

A descriptive analysis was performed using measures of central tendency, dispersion, and position for quantitative variables, as well as frequency distributions for qualitative variables. These were segmented by the study period, and the database was filtered according to patient- and intervention-related variables. A Student t test (or a Mann-Whitney U test for nonnormal distribution) was used to evaluate differences between study periods for quantitative variables. A χ2 test (or a Fisher exact test for expected frequencies of less than 5) was used for qualitative variables. A logistic regression model was constructed to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (95% CI) for moderate or major complications according to the study period. No unbalanced variables were identified. SPSS v28 software was used.

A significant change in reconstructive techniques was observed when the protocol was implemented (P < 0.001). During the postbundle period, the use of subpectoral expanders decreased (from 78.7% to 41.4%), whereas reconstruction with direct prepectoral prostheses increased considerably (from 6.6% to 46.6%). This change likely reflects the evolution of reconstructive techniques rather than the direct effect of the bundle. Implementation of the bundle coincided with a significant reduction in moderate-to-major complication rates (Fig. 1).

A graph that shows analysis of complications.

Before implementation, 24.6% of patients experienced moderate-to-major complications; after implementation, this figure decreased to 6.8%. This difference was statistically significant (P = 0.008 in the Pearson χ2 test and P = 0.017 with continuity correction), suggesting a positive impact on surgical safety. Additionally, a detailed analysis of the complications shown in Figure 1 revealed that mild complications remained stable (18% prebundle versus 19% postbundle), whereas the bundle was associated with a 72% relative risk reduction (P = 0.017), decreasing the rate of moderate complications or reconstruction failure from 24.6% to 6.8%. The analysis of moderate complication causes revealed 6 cases in the prebundle period (3 due to hematoma and 3 due to necrosis), whereas the postbundle period saw a reduction to 2 cases, both due to necrosis.

In the prebundle period, 9 reconstruction failures were recorded, 8 from surgical wound infection and 1 from pain due to capsular contracture. After implementing the bundle, failures decreased to 2 cases, both related to surgical wound infection.

The multivariate logistic regression model revealed that the prebundle period was significantly associated with an increased risk of moderate or major complications (OR, 6.40; 95% CI, 1.81–22.62; P = 0.004). This means that patients who underwent surgery before the bundle were 6.4 times more likely to experience a moderate or major complication than those in the later period (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Our findings suggest that postmastectomy reconstructive losses can be significantly reduced by implementing a structured set of measures presented as a checklist or bundle. During the prebundle period, we experienced a 14.8% reconstructive loss rate, within the published range,15 but above the accepted 5%–9% standard.16 Large multicenter datasets, such as the Dutch Breast Implant Registry, report a 6.7% loss rate in more than 5000 immediate reconstructions, with published series ranging from 1.8% to 16.9%. Although higher than the registry average, our prebundle rate remains within the upper limit of published data and is comparable to outcomes from some high-volume centers before adopting standardized infection-prevention measures.17

Previous studies have reported that bundle protocols can reduce prosthetic losses to very low levels at 3-month follow-up, although they do not provide information on other types of complications.14 In contrast, our study has a median follow-up period of 18 months and classifies complications as mild, moderate, or major (implant loss). In our experience, there were 2 reconstructive losses (3.4%) in the postbundle period, both secondary to surgical wound infection. Implementing this protocol was associated with a significant relative reduction in moderate or major complications, decreasing complication events by 72%, from 24.6% to 6.8%.

We analyzed risk factors related to complications and reconstructive losses, such as chemotherapy,12 ART, BMI, presence of diabetes,18 smoking, and prosthetic volume.15 Implementing the protocol did not affect patient selection or associated risk factors. The 2 study groups showed no significant differences that could account for the results.

We generally use β-lactam antibiotics for infection prophylaxis in breast surgery because of their effectiveness against Staphylococcus aureus, Gram-positive cocci, and anaerobes. However, within our institutional bundle, postoperative doxycycline 100 mg twice daily was maintained until drain removal (maximum 10 d) due to its broad coverage—including methicillin-resistant S. aureus, methicillin-susceptible S. aureus, Enterococcus faecalis, and anaerobes—and its excellent oral bioavailability, long half-life (18–22 h), favorable safety profile in β-lactam–allergic patients, affordability, and not needing dose adjustment in renal or hepatic impairment.

Evidence regarding the optimal duration of postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis in implant-based breast reconstruction remains inconclusive. Some protocols report lower infection and implant loss rates with extended antibiotic courses,14,19 whereas others find no significant benefit beyond 24 hours.9,18 Despite diverging from standard guidelines recommending prophylaxis of 24 hours or less,20 immediate implant-based reconstruction with indwelling drains represents a higher risk environment for bacterial colonization and biofilm formation.21 For these reasons, doxycycline was included as part of a multifactorial infection-reduction strategy rather than as an isolated intervention. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the ongoing controversy and agree that prospective studies are needed to determine the optimal duration and clinical benefit of postoperative prophylaxis.

It is necessary to evaluate whether modifying the reconstructive technique influences the obtained results.22–25 Following bundle implementation, we observed a significant increase in direct prepectoral reconstructions, reaching 46%.

To assess whether the change toward prepectoral reconstruction could explain the observed reduction in complications, we performed an additional multivariate logistic regression model including the surgical technique variable (expander versus direct-to-implant). The adjusted model maintained the bundle’s independent protective effect (OR, 5.6; 95% CI, 1.7–21.4), suggesting that the improvement in outcomes cannot be solely attributed to the shift in reconstructive technique.

Recent large-cohort data report higher rates of infection, seroma, and implant loss in prepectoral compared with subpectoral reconstructions.17,26–28 Despite this, infection and explantation rates in large prepectoral direct-to-implant series—ranging from 10% to 18%14,18,19—remain substantially higher than after bundle implementation (3.4%), reinforcing the likely contribution of the structured perioperative protocol. Although the shift toward prepectoral reconstruction may influence outcomes, the bundle remained independently associated with fewer moderate-to-major complications, acknowledging that residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Prepectoral implants offer advantages over subpectoral implants, such as reduced animation deformity, pain, muscle weakness, and capsular contracture after radiation. However, overall clinical outcomes of prepectoral reconstruction remain debated.27,28

Postmastectomy radiotherapy (PMRT) with implants has been associated with poorer cosmetic outcomes and higher complication rates. Recent evidence suggests that muscle coverage may protect against PMRT-related complications.29 Surgical outcomes were compared in patients undergoing immediate prepectoral versus subpectoral reconstruction during PMRT, concluding that the plane of prosthesis placement was not predictive of complication rates.

In our study, the number of direct postbundle subpectoral reconstructions did not change substantially with the plane of prosthesis placement. Additionally, the percentage of patients receiving radiotherapy remained consistent throughout both study periods.

This study has several limitations. First, it is quasi-experimental. Although prospective randomized studies provide the highest level of evidence, conducting such trials to evaluate the long-term outcomes of clinically validated infection-reduction bundles may be ethically challenging. Therefore, observational cohort studies remain the most practical and informative approach to assess these interventions in routine practice. Second, it analyzes the protocol’s effectiveness as a whole rather than individual practices. Finally, it is difficult to accurately diagnose cases of postsurgical infection. The Hawthorne effect may also have contributed to complication reduction, as bundle implementation increased surgeons’ awareness and adherence to basic infection-prevention measures, independent of the protocol’s specific components. We also acknowledge that mastectomy flap perfusion—although not directly measured—may confound outcomes; however, implant volume did not differ significantly between periods (Table 3), and the bundle remained independently associated with fewer moderate-to-major complications. However, our results align with published evidence and demonstrate the protocol’s significant impact on patient morbidity, cosmetic outcomes, and healthcare costs in daily practice. A multicenter randomized study with long-term follow-up is needed to validate these findings across hospital settings and assess the protocol’s long-term benefits, especially for patients undergoing PMRT.

Implementing the bundle protocol in immediate postmastectomy reconstruction demonstrated a significant reduction in moderate complications and reconstructive failures, decreasing complications by 93.1%. Although the shift to the prepectoral technique was significant, the results suggest that the improvement is mainly due to the care protocol rather than surgical modification. This study underscored the importance of a culture of quality and safety within surgical teams,30 contributing to improved clinical outcomes and patient satisfaction. Future multicenter research with long-term follow-up is needed to validate these findings in diverse settings and assess the sustainability of benefits.
