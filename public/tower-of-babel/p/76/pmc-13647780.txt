Cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination among assigned female at birth transgender and gender-diverse individuals in South Korea: findings from the KITE cohort
Authors: Jung-won Yoon, Gaeun Kim, Eun Sil Lee, Sun Young Lee, Ssirai Kim, Kuylhee Kim, Na-hyun Hwang, Yehoon Choi, Hyein Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited evidence exists regarding sexual and reproductive health (SRH) utilization among transgender and gender diverse (TGD) populations in South Korea. This study examined cervical cancer screening and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination uptake among assigned female at birth (AFAB) TGD individuals and explored associated factors.MethodsData were derived from the Korean Initiative for Transgender Health Cohort Study (KITE), which recruited TGD participants from transgender-affirming healthcare facilities. Cervical cancer screening followed the Korean National Cancer Screening Program guideline (age ≥ 20), and HPV vaccination was defined as receipt of ≥ 1 dose.ResultsAmong 302 AFAB TGD participants, 295 were eligible for the National Cervical Cancer Screening Program (age ≥ 20). The guideline-concordant screening rate within the past two years was 17.6%. After indirect age standardization, this corresponded to 29% of the screening rate observed in the general population. Screening rates were lower among those with exclusively cisgender woman partners, while a history of non-consensual sexual contact was associated with higher uptake.The overall HPV vaccination rate was 25.5%. Among the HPV National Immunization Program recipients aged 18–20, vaccination rates reached 69.2%, comparable to cisgender counterparts. Vaccination was positively associated with younger age, college education, and Seoul residence, but negatively associated with longer hormone therapy duration and exclusively cisgender woman partners.ConclusionsDespite the existence of national screening and vaccination programs, AFAB TGD individuals in the KITE cohort experience substantial gaps in preventive SRH services. Structural assumptions that link cervical cancer risk primarily to heterosexual intercourse and cisgender women may contribute to reduced screening uptake among TGD populations. Anatomically based screening recommendations and gender-neutral immunization policies are needed to ensure equitable access to preventive care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28224-0.

Background

Transgender and gender diverse (TGD) individuals experience substantial health inequities driven by structural exclusion and social stigma, which contribute to markedly lower utilization of preventive and reproductive healthcare services [1–3]. In this context, ‘assigned female at birth’ (AFAB) refers to individuals whose sex was recorded or assigned as female at birth, typically based on sex characteristics observed at birth, regardless of their current gender identity. Among AFAB TGD individuals – which includes transmen (those who identify as men) and non-binary individuals (those whose gender identity falls outside binary categories of male or female) – cervical cancer screening rates are particularly low [4], despite global and U.S. clinical recommendations that all individuals with a cervix should be screened regardless of gender identity [2, 5]. While the risk of human papillomavirus (HPV)-related cancers does not decrease in TGD individuals [6–8], HPV vaccination uptake remains underexplored, partly because most national immunization programs fail to account for gender diversity and anatomical needs [4].

In South Korea, the TGD population is becoming increasingly visible [9], and research has begun to address mental health, stigma, and access to gender-affirming care (GAC) [10]. However, the studies examining comprehensive sexual and reproductive health (SRH) utilization – particularly cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination – remains extremely limited. This gap is notable given the structure of South Korea’s national preventive health system.

South Korea operates a highly centralized, population-based National Cancer Screening Program (NCSP), which provides low-cost or fully subsidized screening for major cancers [11]. Screening eligibility is defined primarily by legal sex, not anatomical status, contrasting with the global shift toward anatomy-based screening. For cervical cancer specifically, only individuals whose legal gender is female are invited for clinician-collected Papanicolaou (PAP) tests every two years beginning at age 20, regardless of whether they currently have a cervix [12]. Under this system, the lifetime screening uptake rate for cisgender women was 83.4% in 2023 [11]. Similarly, the HPV National Immunization Program (NIP) was introduced in 2016, providing free HPV vaccination to 12-year-old girls, and has recently been expanded to include boys [13]. These sex-based eligibility rules create structural barriers, leaving AFAB TGD individuals without equitable access to essential preventive care.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the uptake of cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination among AFAB TGD individuals in South Korea and to identify the factors associated with their utilization.

Methods

Study design

Korean Initiative for Transgender Health Cohort Study (KITE)

The KITE study is the first medical institution-based multicenter TGD cohort study in South Korea. Eight transgender-friendly facilities in Seoul and Daejeon participated in the study. This is a prospective study which comprehensively investigates subjective health, mental health, the incidence of chronic disease, SRH, health behaviors, quality of life (QoL) and the utility and satisfaction of GAC. The study also analyzes changes in health status according to GAC through a follow-up survey conducted up to 3 years.

The inclusion criteria for KITE study were as follows: (1) Aged 18 years or older; (2) Citizens of South Korea; (3) Self-identifying as TGD; (4) Having undergone at least one GAC, including hormone therapy or surgery at the study institution; (5) Voluntarily agreeing to participate in the study.

Individuals who were not able to comprehend Korean, were under the age of 18, or did not possess Korean nationality were excluded from the study. Participants recruitment was initiated after the approval of the Institutional Review Board (IRB) (IRB No. 2311-119-1485) and the analysis of the collected data was subsequently approved by the IRB (IRB No. E2441/004–010).

Study population

This study is based on the primary survey results of the KITE study, which was conducted from January 2024 to October 2024. The broader KITE study is a comprehensive cohort that includes both assigned male at birth (AMAB) and AFAB TGD individuals (n = 844 in total). For the current analysis, we specifically extracted data from the AFAB participants. A total of 302 AFAB TGD individuals participated in the survey, and as all participants met the inclusion criteria and provided complete data for the variables of interest, no individuals were excluded. Consequently, the final analytical sample consisted of 302 individuals.

Study variables

All data were collected using the self-report online structured questionnaires of the KITE study.

Data were collected on the age, gender identity – non-binary identities or transmen – and legal gender marker, recorded as female or male, at the time of the survey. Sexual behavior-related variables included sexual behavior within the previous year, the number of partners engaged in the sexual activities, and the gender of the partners. Health behavior and mental health variables encompassed diagnosis of major depressive disorder (MDD), history of non-consensual sexual contact and body mass index (BMI). Obesity was defined as BMI greater than 25.0 [14]. The duration of hormone therapy and the experience of gender-affirming surgery (GAS) were included in the GAC-related variables. The regulatory and social environment variables are as follows: accessibility to medical institutions, residential area, health insurance coverage status, level of education, income. Monthly personal income was categorized into four brackets for analysis: no income (0), low income (≤ 1 million Korean won [KRW]), middle income (1–3 million KRW), and high income (> 3 million KRW).

Dependent variables were SRH service utilization status: cervical cancer screening uptake and HPV vaccination status. Cervical cancer screening uptake was defined as having undergone a PAP smear test. Participants were asked whether they had ever received at least one screening in their lifetime, and whether they had been screened within the past two years. HPV vaccination status was categorized into completed vaccination and partial vaccination. Complete vaccination was defined as receiving the full recommended series–two doses for individuals younger than 15 years and three doses for those aged 15 years or older [15]. Partial vaccination referred to one or more doses but fewer than the full number recommended for the age group.

Statistical analysis

The frequency and percentage of sociodemographic characteristics and the rates of cervical cancer screening uptake and HPV vaccination were calculated. Continuous variables were presented as the mean and standard deviation (SD), or the median and interquartile range (IQR).

The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the independent t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables were utilized to evaluate the disparities in sociodemographic characteristics and health behavior variables in relation to cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination rates. In addition, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with SRH care utilization. Variables which were statistically significant in univariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model and represented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with values greater than 5 indicating potential collinearity concerns. All variables in the final model had VIF values below this threshold, confirming the absence of significant multicollinearity.

The cervical cancer screening uptake and HPV vaccination rates in the cohort were reorganized to facilitate comparison with the most recent data from the general population – specifically, cervical cancer screening rates reported in the Annual Statistical Report on National Health Screening by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) [16] and the HPV vaccination rate statistics from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA) [17]. Because the age distribution of the KITE cohort differed substantially from that of the general population and the sample size was relatively small, indirect age standardization was used to obtain a more stable comparison.

R (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) was used for statistical analysis; P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding sexual behaviors, 165 individuals (54.6%) reported sexual activity within the past year, among whom 15.6% had more than two sexual partners. Additionally, based on a multiple-response question, 16.6% reported having at least one partner whose gender was other than cisgender female. The median duration of hormone therapy was 3.7 years (IQR: 1.3–7.0) and 50% of participants had undergone hysterectomy. A total of 70 individuals (23.2%) reported to have history of non-consensual sexual contact.

Majority of participants resided in metropolitan areas and were covered by National Health Insurance. In terms of education, 71.5% of participants had completed higher education or above. Regarding income level, 45.4% were categorized in the middle-income bracket.

Cervical cancer screening

Among the 302 AFAB TGD individuals, 295 were eligible for the National Cervical Cancer Screening Program, which targets individuals aged 20 and older [12]. The remaining seven individuals were considered ineligible because they were aged 18–19 years, at the time of the survey, thus having not yet reached the minimum age for the screening program.

In South Korea, screening invitations are administratively issued to all individuals within the target age range, regardless of prior medical history. Thus, to maintain comparability with Korea general population screening statistics, the overall uptake rate was calculated for the entire cohort. A total of 73 individuals (24.7%) had ever undergone cervical cancer screening. Only 17.6% had been screened within the past two years per guideline recommendation [18], substantially lower than the 60.3% observed in the general population [16]. After indirect age standardization, the screening rate of AFAB TGD remained at 29% of that in the general population (Additional file 1).

A univariate comparative analysis was performed between the individuals who underwent cervical cancer screening and those who did not (Table 2). There was a significant difference in screening uptake according to gender identity, with higher rates observed in non-binary individuals compared to the transmen group (44.2% vs. 21.4%, p = 0.003). Legal gender status was significantly associated with screening rates, with individuals who had legally changed their gender to male exhibiting lower cancer screening rates compared to those retaining legal female status (15.8% vs. 32.7%, p = 0.001). The duration of hormone therapy showed an inverse association with screening, as the median duration was shorter among screened participants (2.9 years) than among those not screened (4.4 years, p = 0.018). Sexual activity was another significant factor. Participants who had been sexually active within the past year were more likely to have undergone screening than those without any sexual partners (p = 0.012). Additionally, a higher number of sexual partners (p = 0.005) and having sexual partners other than cisgender female (p < 0.001) were also positively associated with screening uptake. Individuals who had undergone hysterectomy were significantly less likely to be screened compared with those who retained their uterus (15.9% vs. 34.0%, p = 0.001). A history of non-consensual sexual contact was also associated with higher screening participation (40.3% vs. 20.2%, p = 0.001), while depressive symptoms were not significantly related. Participants in the screened group had a higher median BMI than those unscreened (p = 0.026), while variables such as travel time to healthcare facilities, residential area, insurance type, and education did not show significant associations with screening uptake.

In the multivariate logistic regression model including smoking status and obesity as covariates (Table 3), the composition of sexual partners remained a strong predictor of cervical cancer screening. Compared with participants who had partners of other genders, those who had no sexual partners (OR = 0.12, 95% CI = 0.04–0.34, p < 0.001) and those with only cisgender female partners (OR = 0.25, 95% CI = 0.10–0.58, p = 0.002) were significantly less likely to undergo screening. A history of sexual assault was positively associated with screening participation (OR = 2.19, 95% CI = 1.10–4.31, p = 0.023). Gender identity, legal gender status, number of sexual partners, hormone therapy duration, smoking, and obesity were not significantly associated with screening uptake.

A subgroup analysis was conducted among individuals who had not undergone hysterectomy, and thus retained their uterus (Additional file 2). Of the 144 individuals in this subgroup, only 34.0% (n = 49) had ever undergone cervical cancer screening. The cervical cancer screening rate within the past two years, in accordance with current guideline recommendations [18], was 27.1% (n = 39). Univariate and logistic multivariate result of this subgroup demonstrated that female partner composition and non-consensual sexual contact were positively associated with cancer screening uptake, consistent with findings observed in the overall AFAB TGD cohort.

HPV vaccination

Among the 302 AFAB TGD participants, 2.3% (n = 7) had received partial HPV vaccination, while 23.2% (n = 70) had completed the full vaccination series.

A univariate comparative analysis was conducted between individuals who had never received the HPV vaccine and those who had received at least one dose (Table 4). HPV vaccination rates differed significantly by age group (p < 0.001), with the majority of vaccinated individuals being in their 20s and 30s. Gender identity was associated with vaccination uptake, with higher vaccination rates among non-binary than transmen group (38.6% vs. 23.3%, p = 0.048). Legal gender change was significantly associated with HPV vaccination, with 35.6% of legal females and 13.7% of legal males having received at least one dose of the vaccine (p < 0.001). Individuals who reported having sexual partners other than cisgender females had significantly higher HPV vaccination rates compared to those who had only cisgender female partners (p < 0.001). HPV vaccination uptake decreased with greater progression in gender transition, as indicated by longer duration of hormone therapy and a history of hysterectomy (p < 0.001). A higher proportion of individuals who had received vaccine reported a history of non-consensual sexual contact (40.0% vs. 21.1%, p = 0.003). Moreover, the social environment variables – including travel time to general healthcare facilities, residential area, income – were related to the HPV vaccination rates.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study examined the factors associated with cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccine uptake among AFAB TGD individuals in South Korea, identifying factors associated with their utilization. Despite high educational level and urban residence, both preventive health indicators were markedly lower than those in the general population. Cervical cancer screening was primarily associated with gender identity-related factors and sexual behaviors, while HPV vaccination uptake was influenced by a broader spectrum of determinants, including age, education, residential area, and duration of hormone therapy. These findings demonstrate that gynecologic preventive care among AFAB TGD individuals is jointly shaped by identity-based and structural barriers, reflecting the persistent influence of binary and heteronormative healthcare systems.

Interpretation in context

Characteristics of included AFAB TGD participants

The study population was predominantly young (median age 29 years) and lived in urban areas, with cisgender women as the most common sexual partners. The elevated prevalence of MDD (31.1%) and history of non-consensual sexual contact (23.2% - three times higher than the general population [19]) highlights substantial mental health burden and vulnerability to sexual violence. Despite urban residence, median travel time to gender-affirming facilities (60 min) was six times longer than to general healthcare, revealing persistent geographic barriers to specialized care.

Cervical cancer screening

Among AFAB TGD individuals aged 20 years or older, only 17.6% had been screened within the past two years, corresponding to approximately 29% of the age-adjusted general population rate. Screening uptake was significantly lower among those who reported exclusively cisgender female sexual partners – consistent with previous reports of under-screening among sexual minority women [20], suggesting persistent heteronormative assumptions about HPV risk. Notably, traditional socioeconomic variables–income, education, insurance status, and geographic proximity–did not significantly predict screening uptake. This finding implies that screening uptake is more strongly influenced by personal perceptions of SRH-related risks and emotional context than by structural accessibility.

The positive association between non-consensual sexual contact history and screening uptake contrasts with literature showing trauma-related healthcare avoidance [21]. This unexpected finding may reflect complex help-seeking behaviors and warrants further investigation, including potential roles of specialized sexual violence response services.

HPV vaccination

HPV vaccination, in contrast, displayed stronger associations with structural and socioeconomic factors. The 25.5% overall vaccination rate improved substantially among those aged 18–20 (69.2%), matching general population rates [17] and demonstrating that universal access programs effectively reduce vaccination disparities. This is supported by the fact that the 18–20 age group was eligible for the NIP introduced in 2016. Although administrative barriers can exist for TGD individuals, legal gender change in South Korea is generally restricted to those aged 19 years or older; thus, AFAB TGD individuals in this group would have legally registered as female during their NIP eligibility window, ensuring access without self-payment. Vaccination showed strong associations with education and geographic location, while screening did not. This divergence reflects differences in program structure and financial accessibility. Cervical cancer screening is provided free of charge to all eligible individuals through the NCSP, while HPV vaccination outside the age-restricted NIP requires out-of-pocket payment, creating socioeconomic gradients in access. This suggests financial barriers significantly impact vaccination when programs are not universally available.

Transition-related patterns

Both cervical screening and HPV vaccination declined with progression in medical and legal gender transition. Individuals with longer hormone therapy duration, history of hysterectomy, or legal male gender marker were less likely to participate in gynecologic preventive care. This pattern suggests that as masculine identity becomes more consolidated through medical transition and social affirmation, gynecologic preventive care may be perceived as less relevant or may evoke gender dysphoria, leading to avoidance. Similarly, participants with no sexual partners or exclusively cisgender female partners were less likely to be screened or vaccinated, indicating that HPV prevention continues to be perceived through a heterosexual framework. However, this perception may overlook documented HPV transmission routes and the anatomical risks. These converging patterns highlight a fundamental tension in TGD healthcare: providing anatomy-based preventive care while affirming gender identity. As transition progresses and lived gender becomes more aligned with identity, anatomically-indicated care may feel increasingly incongruent, creating acceptance barriers even when services are accessible.

Policy and practice implications

The findings support the need for policy reform toward gender-inclusive SRH frameworks in South Korea. Current national guidelines that tie screening eligibility and immunization access to legal gender markers create systematic barriers for TDG individuals. We recommend three key policy changes: First, extend the NIP to all individuals within target age ranges including boys to achieve a gender-neutral and universal HPV vaccination program – while ensuring access remains inclusive of all individuals regardless of gender identity or legal gender marker, such as AFAB TGD individuals. Second, revise cervical cancer screening guidelines to explicitly include all individuals with a cervix, regardless of legal gender marker. International guidelines from the World Health Organization (WHO) and other public health authorities already endorse such anatomy-based approaches [22]. Third, National health surveys must also incorporate gender identity and sexual orientation items to enable population-level monitoring of SRH disparities.

At a practical level, transitional measures are equally critical. Healthcare providers involved in transgender and gender-affirming care should receive education and training on appropriate cervical screening and HPV vaccination guidelines for AFAB TGD individuals, ensuring that both clinicians and patients are aware of preventive recommendations. For TGD individuals preparing for legal gender change, information should be provided proactively so that necessary gynecologic screening and vaccination can be completed prior to gender marker change. In addition, the regional disparity in access to LGBTQ+-affirming healthcare facilities must be addressed through public support for decentralized and inclusive SRH services. Strengthening provider competency and promoting gender-affirming communication at every level of care will help bridge current gaps while longer-term structural reforms are implemented.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides the first comprehensive data on cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccine uptake among AFAB TGD individuals in the TGD population. By recruiting participants through medical facilities, we improved data reliability relative to online surveys, and the inclusion of both sexual behavioral and social structural variables enabled multidimensional analysis of healthcare utilization.

However, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot capture changes in SRH need across different life stages or transition stages. For instance, we cannot determine whether individuals reduced screening after beginning hormone therapy or whether those who initiated hormone therapy earlier were already less likely to engage in screening. Self-reported measures of screening and vaccination may be subject to recall bias. The study did not assess potentially important mediating factors such as health literacy specific to SRH, knowledge about HPV transmission and prevention, experiences of procedural discomfort during gynecologic examinations, or direct experiences of provider stigma or discrimination. Finally, recruitment limited to specific trans-affirming institutions in urban areas may have led to overrepresentation of individuals who are already engaged in healthcare, selection bias cannot be excluded when comparing screening rates with those of the general population.

Conclusions

This study provides one of the first quantitative assessments of cervical cancer screening and HPV vaccination among AFAB TGD individuals in South Korea. Despite the presence of a national screening system and publicly funded HPV vaccination program, cervical cancer screening participation remained substantially lower than that of the general population. Notably, the KITE cohort was recruited from transgender-affirming healthcare settings and represents a relatively medically engaged population, yet screening uptake remained markedly low.

These findings suggest that, in addition to established socioeconomic determinants of preventive healthcare utilization, preventive care systems structured around legal gender categories – and longstanding assumptions linking cervical cancer risk primarily to heterosexual intercourse among cisgender women – may create additional barriers for TGD populations. Integrating anatomically based screening recommendations and gender-neutral vaccination policies could improve equitable access to preventive SRH services.
