Temporal trends, cardiovascular complications, and outcomes in patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy: a 6-year nationwide analysis
Authors: Karnav Modi, Himil Mahadevia, Raj Shah, Deepthi Vodnala, Talal Asif, Taiyeb Khumri, Furha Cossor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has transformed the treatment of relapsed or refractory hematologic malignancies. However, real-world data describing cardiovascular complications with CAR T-cell therapy remain limited.MethodsUsing the National Inpatient Sample, we identified hospitalizations involving CAR T-cell therapy from 2017 to 2022. Temporal trends, in-hospital mortality, cardiovascular complications, and other clinical outcomes were evaluated using survey-weighted regression models.ResultsAmong 13,370 weighted hospitalizations, CAR T-cell use increased from 70 cases in 2017 to 4,335 in 2022. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 3.4%. Cardiovascular complications were observed in a subset of patients, including arrhythmias in 20.2%, acute myocardial infarction in 0.5%, stroke in 1.0%, cardiogenic shock in 0.2%, and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in 4.5%. Atrial fibrillation was the most common arrhythmia (11.1%), followed by conduction abnormalities (3.9%). The prevalence of arrhythmias increased with advancing age, reaching 48.3% in patients older than 80 years. Acute kidney injury occurred in 14.4%, with 1.7% requiring renal replacement therapy. Cytokine release syndrome occurred in 61.0% in 2021 and 62.1% in 2022, while immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome was observed in 24.2% in 2022. Mean length of stay was 18.3 days, and inflation-adjusted hospitalization costs increased from $83,939 in 2017 to $378,507 in 2022.ConclusionsCAR T-cell therapy utilization increased substantially in the United States over the study period, with relatively low in-hospital mortality but notable cardiovascular complications, particularly arrhythmias. These findings highlight the importance of cardiovascular risk assessment and monitoring in patients undergoing CAR T-cell therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40959-026-00471-5.

Introduction

Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy has transformed the management of relapsed and refractory hematologic malignancies, particularly B-cell lymphomas and leukemias, and, more recently, multiple myeloma, offering durable responses in patients with limited therapeutic options [1–4]. Since the first FDA approval in 2017, the clinical use of CAR T-cell therapy has expanded rapidly across the United States, supported by robust efficacy data from pivotal clinical trials [5].

Clinical trials have demonstrated meaningful survival benefits with CAR T-cell therapy. In diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) demonstrated a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 5.9 months and an overall survival (OS) of 25.8 months in long-term follow-up. Similarly, tisagenlecleucel (tisa-cel) showed a 12-month PFS of 33% and OS of 49% in adult patients with relapsed/refractory DLBCL [6]. In pediatric and young adult B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), the 5-year OS rate approaches 55% [7]. For multiple myeloma (MM), in the KarMMa trial [8], Idecabtagene vicleucel (ide-cel) demonstrated an overall response rate (ORR) of 73% and a median PFS of 8.8 months. In the CARTITUDE-1 trial [9], ciltacabtagene autoleucel (cilta-cel) achieved an ORR of 98% with a median PFS of 34.9 months. These outcomes reflect the potential for durable remissions in heavily pretreated populations.

Despite its therapeutic promise, CAR T-cell therapy is associated with significant toxicities, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), which contribute to morbidity, prolonged hospitalizations, and increased costs [10–12]. In this study, we utilized the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) to evaluate temporal trends in utilization, cardiovascular complications, and other inpatient outcomes among patients receiving CAR T-cell therapy in the United States from 2017 to 2022.

Methods

Data overview/source

We used the NIS datasets from 2017 to 2022 for our study. The NIS is maintained and made available by the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), a program of the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). It is the largest publicly available all-payer inpatient healthcare database in the United States, containing data from over 4,400 hospitals across 47 states. It includes data on approximately 20% of all U.S. hospital discharges, which are weighted to generate national estimates, about 35 million discharges annually. It utilizes the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) and Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS). Since all datasets are de-identified and publicly available, our study did not require Institutional Review Board permission.

Data selection and study population

Using appropriate ICD-10-PCS procedure codes (Supplementary Table 1), we identified all patients who underwent CAR T-cell Therapy between 2017 and 2022. Demographic, hospital, and patient-level variables were identified using NIS variables. Comorbidities were assessed with Elixhauser software [13], which generates binary variables from ICD-10-CM codes; additional comorbidities were identified using secondary diagnosis codes (Supplementary Table 2).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital all-cause mortality. We evaluated the prevalence and temporal trends of lymphoma, multiple myeloma (MM), and leukemia using relevant ICD-10-CM codes across all diagnosis fields. All outcomes (Table 2) were identified using appropriate ICD-10-CM or ICD-10-PCS codes in secondary diagnosis fields (Supplementary Table 1). Cardiovascular complications included cardiac arrhythmias, conduction disorders (including heart block), acute myocardial infarction (AMI), stroke, and cardiogenic shock. Major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) were defined as a composite of in-hospital death, AMI, and stroke. Infectious complications included pneumonia, gram-negative infections or bacteremia, sepsis, and septic shock, along with the use of non-invasive ventilation (NIV) and mechanical ventilation (MV). Acute kidney injury (AKI), renal replacement therapy (RRT), and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) were also evaluated. To further characterize patients who developed cardiac arrhythmias, additional analyses were performed comparing baseline demographic characteristics and comorbidities between CAR T-cell recipients with and without arrhythmias (Table 3). A multivariable regression analysis was subsequently conducted to identify independent predictors of arrhythmias among CAR T-cell recipients (Supplementary Table 4). Individual arrhythmia subtypes were analyzed using specific ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes (Table 4; Supplementary Table 1). Age-stratified analyses with multivariable regression were also performed to assess differences across predefined age groups (Table 4). For cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell–associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), dedicated ICD-10-CM codes were introduced in October 2021 and 2022 [14, 15]. Therefore, we evaluated related clinical manifestations such as fever, hypotension, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), and hypoxia as indicators of systemic toxicity, and toxic encephalopathy as a marker of neurotoxicity, following the methodology of prior studies [16]. Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate CRS for the years 2021–2022 and ICANS for the year 2022 (Table 4).

The NIS reports total hospital charges, which reflects the amount billed by hospitals but excludes professional physician fees and non-covered charges. These charges differ from actual costs, which represent the resources used to deliver care. To estimate true inpatient costs, HCUP Cost-to-Charge Ratio (CCR) files were applied and then inflation-adjusted to 2024 U.S. dollars using the Consumer Price Index (CPI) for Medical Care, as published by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (bls.gov) (Supplementary Table 3).

Statistical analysis

Temporal trends in outcomes and disease prevalence were assessed using survey-weighted logistic or linear regression models, as appropriate. To calculate adjusted p-trend values, each outcome was modeled as a function of calendar year (treated as a continuous independent variable), adjusting for age, sex, race, hospital bed size, and hospital location/teaching status. All analyses used Stata 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In the NIS datasets, a total of 13,370 patients received CAR T-cell therapy between 2017 and 2022, increasing markedly from 70 cases in 2017 to 4,335 in 2022 (Fig. 1). The most significant year-over-year increases were observed between 2020 and 2022, with 2240 recipients in 2020, 3155 recipients in 2021, and 4335 recipients in 2022.

The mean age at admission was 56.9 years (95% CI: 55.9–57.8). Males comprised 61.8%. The majority identified as White (72.3%), followed by Hispanic (13.2%), and African Americans (7.0%). Most patients were insured through private payers (49.4%) or Medicare (37.5%). The majority of CAR T-cell therapy admissions occurred at large bed-sized hospitals (73.3%). Nearly all patients were treated at urban teaching hospitals (99.6%). Regionally, the South accounted for the highest proportion of CAR T-cell hospitalizations (28.5%), followed by the Northeast (25.7%), West (23.8%), and Midwest (22.0%). The most prevalent comorbidities were uncomplicated hypertension (HTN) (31.8%), dyslipidemia (24.0%), tobacco use (21.7%), hypothyroidism (11.5%), depression (11.1%), obesity (9.6%), and chronic pulmonary disease (9.2%).

Malignancies

Lymphoma comprised 65.7% of cases overall, followed by MM (16.6%) and leukemia (14.6%). The proportion of lymphoma cases increased from 64.3% in 2017 to a peak of 73.0% in 2019 and 2020, followed by a modest decline to 59.9% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend < 0.001). MM showed a significant overall increase, starting at 21.4% in 2017, declining to 13.1%, 8.8%, and 8.9% in 2018–2020, and then rising again to 23.4% by 2022 (adjusted p-trend < 0.001). In contrast, the proportion of leukemia cases was highest in 2017 (28.6%) but remained relatively stable from 2018 to 2022 (range: 13.8%–16.7%) (p-trend = 0.952).

The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 3.4%. Mortality peaked in 2018 at 5.2%, was 2.9% in 2019, 3.8% in 2020, and declined to 3.5% and 2.9% in 2021 and 2022, respectively (adjusted p-trend = 0.458).

Fever occurred in 47.6%, acute hypoxic respiratory failure (AHRF) in 6.0%, and hypotension in 21.5%. SIRS occurred in 6.7% and toxic encephalopathy, suggestive of neurotoxicity, was observed in 23.3%. HLH occurred in 1.5% of patients overall, peaking at 2.7% in 2020 and declining to 1.5% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.416). AMI occurred in 0.5% overall, ranging from 0.0% in 2017 to 1.1% in 2020, and 0.3% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.298). Stroke occurred in 1.0% overall, peaking at 1.6% in 2020, and then declining to 1.0% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.909). MACCE events were reported in 4.5% overall (adjusted p-trend = 0.486). Cardiogenic shock was rare (0.2% overall). Arrhythmias or heart block were noted in 20.2% of patients, increasing from 14.3% in 2017 to 22.0% in 2021, and 21.2% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.613). AKI occurred in 14.4%, ranging from 13.5% to 15.9% (adjusted p-trend = 0.587). RRT was required in 1.7%, peaking at 2.3% in 2018 (adjusted p-trend = 0.685). Use of NIV was in 1.9% overall, ranging from 1.4% to 2.5% between 2018 and 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.775). Use of MV was in 4.1%, with the highest rate in 2018 (6.5%) and a significant decline to 2.9% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.005). Pneumonia affected 5.9%, peaking at 7.8% in 2019 and decreasing to 4.3% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.001). Gram-negative infections or bacteremia were reported in 1.4%, with no clear temporal pattern (0-2.5%) (adjusted p-trend = 0.816). Sepsis occurred in 6.5%, highest in 2018 (9.2%) and a decline to 5% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.018). Septic shock was reported in 2.4%, with a peak in 2019 (3.7%) and a decline to 1.3% in 2022 (adjusted p-trend = 0.045).

Discussion

This study, to our knowledge, represents one of the largest nationwide real-world analyses of hospitalized CAR T-cell therapy recipients in the United States, evaluating six-year trends in utilization, mortality, cardiovascular complications with a particular focus on arrhythmias, other inpatient outcomes, and healthcare resource utilization. Unlike clinical trials or single-center reports, it provides a broad real-world snapshot of evolving outcomes and practice patterns following the adoption of CAR T-cell therapy, helping address important gaps in understanding population-level outcomes since FDA approval.

The use of CAR T-cell therapy grew substantially over the six-year study period. This rapid expansion parallels the timeline of FDA approvals—including axi-cel and tisa-cel in 2017, lisocabtagene maraleucel and ide-cel in 2021, and cilta-cel in 2022—as well as increasing institutional capacity and broader payer coverage. These trends align with the exponential year-over-year growth reported in the Center for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research (CIBMTR) annual report in 2025 [17]. Our study further identifies shifting disease indications over time. Initially dominated by lymphoma and leukemia, the proportion of MM increased from 8.9% in 2020 to 23.4% in 2022, reflecting the integration of ide-cel and cilta-cel into practice following the KarMMa and CARTITUDE-1 trials [8, 9].

The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 3.4%, similar to data from previous studies. In ZUMA-1, the 30-day mortality rate was ~ 3.7% [18]. In JULIET, the 30-day mortality was 3.2% [3]. Sharma et al. reported a mortality of 4.1% from the National Readmission Database [16]. Importantly, in our study, in-hospital mortality declined over time, from 5.2% in 2018 to 2.9% in 2022, suggesting improvements in patient selection, toxicity management, and post-infusion care protocols. It is important to note that clinical trials typically report mortality at set intervals, such as 30, 90 days, or 1 year, and differentiate between relapse-related and non-relapse-related deaths. In contrast, the NIS captures all-cause mortality only during the index hospitalization, making direct comparisons difficult due to differences in reporting and follow-up.

Quantifying the true incidence of CRS and ICANS in CAR T-cell recipients is challenging due to limitations inherent to administrative coding, including the lack of specific ICD-10-CM codes until recently. In our subgroup analysis using the newly available codes, CRS was identified in 61.0% of patients in 2021 and 62.1% in 2022, while ICANS was documented in 24.2% of patients in 2022. CRS rates were lower, while ICANS rates were comparable to those reported in clinical trials—ZUMA-1 [2] (CRS 93%, ICANS 64%), KarMMa [8] (CRS 84%, ICANS 18%), and CARTITUDE-1 [9] (CRS 95%, ICANS 21%). While improvements in toxicity management may contribute, underreporting and variability in documentation likely explain these discrepancies.

In our study, arrhythmias were observed in 20.2% of CAR T-cell recipients. Atrial arrhythmias, particularly AF (11.1%), represented the most common subtype, followed by conduction blocks (3.9%), whereas ventricular arrhythmias were relatively uncommon. These findings are consistent with prior observational studies showing that atrial arrhythmias account for the majority of cardiovascular events after CAR T-cell therapy. Shouval et al. reported nearly a fourfold increase in atrial arrhythmias following CAR T therapy, frequently occurring in the setting of CRS [19]. Similarly, Lee et al. observed AF in 11.1% of patients after CAR T therapy [20]. Alvi et al. reported that 12% experienced CV events, including arrhythmias, HF, or CV death, over a median follow-up of 10 months [21]. A meta-analysis by Koeckerling et al. demonstrated ventricular arrhythmias in 0.7%, and supraventricular arrhythmias in 7.8% [22]. Using NIS data, Thotamgari et al. reported an overall arrhythmia incidence of 15%, including AF in 8.1%, VT in 4.7%, atrial tachycardia in 2.7%, and atrial flutter in 0.3% [23]. Our observed rate of 20.2% aligns with these findings, as it includes a broader spectrum of conduction disorders, tachyarrhythmias, and bradyarrhythmias.

Our analysis also demonstrated a strong association between arrhythmias and traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Patients with arrhythmias were older and had a higher burden of comorbidities. Consistent with established epidemiologic patterns in the general population, age, complicated HTN, PVD, dyslipidemia, and obesity remained independent predictors of arrhythmias [24]. Age showed a particularly strong association, with arrhythmia prevalence increasing across age strata and reaching nearly half of patients older than 80 years, similar to trends observed in population-based studies and prior cardio-oncology cohorts [19, 20].

In addition to arrhythmias, we observed relatively low rates of AMI (0.5%), stroke (1.0%), MACCE (4.5%), and cardiogenic shock (0.2%) during the index hospitalization. These findings are consistent with prior reports suggesting that while electrical complications are relatively common after CAR T-cell therapy, ischemic and hemodynamic events are less frequent. Several observational studies have reported varying rates of myocardial injury and HF following CAR T-cell therapy. Alvi et al. demonstrated that 54% of patients had troponin elevation, 28% developed new LV dysfunction, and 4.4% experienced decompensated HF [21]. In a meta-analysis, Koeckerling et al. reported LV dysfunction in 8.7%, HF events in 3.9%, and MI in 0.6% [22]. Similarly, Lee et al. observed cardiomyopathy in 1.1% of patients, while Lefebvre et al. reported MACCE in 21.4%, including CV death, symptomatic HF, acute coronary syndrome, ischemic stroke, and arrhythmias [20, 25]. The relatively lower incidence of cardiac events in our study likely reflects the focus on complications occurring only during the index hospitalization.

Collectively, these studies suggest that cardiac complications after CAR T-cell therapy exist along a spectrum, ranging from arrhythmias and troponin elevation to transient LV dysfunction, HF, and less commonly ischemic events or cardiogenic shock. A shared pathophysiologic mechanism likely underlies these manifestations. CRS, characterized by elevated inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, and interferon-γ, can lead to myocardial injury, autonomic dysfunction, and electrical instability [26]. This inflammatory state may cause myocardial stunning, vasodilation, capillary leak, and hypotension, resulting in demand-mediated myocardial injury rather than primary plaque rupture. Hemodynamic stress, hypoxia, electrolyte disturbances, and catecholamine surges during CRS or sepsis may further exacerbate arrhythmic risk and precipitate transient LV dysfunction or HF, particularly in patients with underlying structural heart disease. Prior studies have consistently demonstrated that higher grades of CRS are strongly associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events, including arrhythmias, troponin elevation, and LV dysfunction [25, 27, 28].

From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment before CAR T-cell therapy, especially in older patients and those with established cardiovascular disease. Although ischemic and shock-related complications are uncommon, their occurrence carries important prognostic implications. Close hemodynamic monitoring during CRS, early recognition of myocardial injury, and prompt management of arrhythmias and HF may help prevent progression to cardiogenic shock. As CAR T-cell therapy expands to older and higher-risk populations, integration of cardio-oncology evaluation into pre-treatment assessment and post-infusion monitoring may help mitigate cardiovascular complications and improve outcomes.

A notable observation in our study is the significant decline in mechanical ventilation (MV) use, from 6.5% in 2018 to 2.9% in 2022, despite the relatively stable use of non-invasive ventilation (NIV) over the same period. The JULIET trial [3] reported an intubation rate of 7%, comparable to our findings. The decline in use of MV in our study parallels the observed decrease in pneumonia (from 7.8% in 2019 to 4.3% in 2022), sepsis (from 9.2% in 2018 to 5.0% in 2022), and septic shock (from 3.7% in 2019 to 1.3% in 2022). These trends may suggest improvements in ICU care, early identification of complications, antimicrobial stewardship, and post-infusion supportive management, particularly in high-risk patients.

Our findings also compare favorably with infection rates reported in prior clinical trials. The JULIET trial [3] reported infections in 20% of patients, KarMMa [8] reported 69%, and CARTITUDE [9] reported 58%. CARTITUDE [9] also reported pneumonia and sepsis in 8% and 4% of patients, respectively, These findings broadly align with our results, although our analysis was limited to infections occurring during the index hospitalization. Additionally, infection-related complications observed in our study may have been influenced by the COVID-19 period. The incidence of gram-negative infections and bacteremia remained stable over time (1.4%, p-trend = 0.816), suggesting an underlying risk independent of hospital practices, potentially driven by factors such as prolonged neutropenia, mucosal barrier injury from lymphodepletion, or individual patient vulnerability. Importantly, the implementation of evidence-based infection prevention strategies, including tailored antimicrobial prophylaxis, close monitoring during cytopenias, and IVIG replacement for hypogammaglobulinemia, is now standard in clinical practice and may be contributing to the observed decline in infectious complications among CAR T-cell recipients [29].

Immune Effector Cell–associated Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis-like Syndrome (IEC-HS) is a rare but potentially life-threatening hyperinflammatory complication of CAR T-cell therapy [30]. In our study, HLH was identified in 1.5%. Despite its severity, data on HLH in CAR T-cell recipients remain limited. This is partly due to diagnostic challenges, as HLH often presents with clinical features that overlap with high-grade CRS, leading to underdiagnosis or misclassification. Early pivotal trials such as ZUMA-1 and JULIET did not systematically report HLH, likely due to the absence of formal diagnostic criteria at the time. Subsequently, the CARTITUDE trial [9] reported one HLH-related death among 97 patients, and Locke et al. [18] noted one HLH-related death in their long-term follow-up of the ZUMA-1 cohort. Although the incidence observed in our study is lower, likely due to the absence of longitudinal follow-up beyond the index hospitalization, it provides one of the first large-scale real-world estimates of HLH following CAR T-cell therapy across a six-year period among hospitalized patients.

Strengths and limitations

This study utilizes the strengths of the NIS to provide the largest nationwide, real-world analysis of hospitalized CAR T-cell therapy recipients in the U.S., capturing over 13,000 cases across six years and evaluating a broad range of clinically relevant outcomes. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the NIS lacks longitudinal follow-up, restricting its assessment to outcomes that occur during the index hospitalization. Second, the use of administrative codes may lead to misclassification or underreporting of complications, especially for syndromes like CRS, ICANS, and HLH, for which specific ICD-10 codes were only recently introduced. Additionally, detailed clinical variables (e.g., laboratory data, disease burden, CAR construct) and treatment responses are not available in the NIS, limiting risk stratification. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal data to assess long-term outcomes, identify high-risk phenotypes, and evaluate predictors of toxicity and resource use.
