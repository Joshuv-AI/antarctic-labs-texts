SERS surgical navigation with postsurgical immunotherapy of local microtumors and distant metastases for improved anticancer outcomes
Authors: Yu Wen, Ruoxuan Liu, Yangcenzi Xie, Xinyu Liu, Ming Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The standard of clinical care of most malignant solid cancers is surgery, followed by postsurgical adjuvant therapy, but microtumor lesions left behind after surgery and invisible distant metastases are the major reasons for treatment failure. Here, we report an integrated strategy combining surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) surgical navigation with postsurgical immunotherapy elicited by near-infrared II photothermal treatment and programmed death-1 antibody. The SERS surgical navigation is principally based on the multifunctional optical probes (namely, MATRA probes) integrating with T1-weighted magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, photothermal effect and Raman spectroscopic detection. We demonstrate in a 4T1 breast tumor mouse model that the pre-surgical MR/SERS dual-modal imaging is capable of providing comprehensive tumor information, and intraoperative SERS detection allows accurately delineating the tumor margins and guiding the surgical resection in real time with the least residual microscopic foci. We verify that the postsurgical immunotherapy effectively eradicates those local microtumor lesions and invisible distant metastases, greatly inhibiting the postsurgical cancer recurrence and distant metastasis.

INTRODUCTION

Surgical resection is now the mainstay of cancer treatment clinically and often remains the only curative option for most malignant solid cancers (1, 2). Approximately 19.3 million people were diagnosed with cancer in 2020, 80% of which could choose surgery to treat cancer (3, 4). However, postsurgical local recurrence and distant metastasis often occur in clinical practice, which are the important causes of surgical treatment failure of cancer (5–7). Studies have shown that cancer recurrence and metastasis are tightly associated with (i) residual microtumors unavoidably left behind after surgery and (ii) surgery-induced tissue injury and local inflammation in a surgical bed (8, 9). Ideal clinical care of cancer is the complete surgical resection of the entire tumor mass without adverse influence on blood vessels and vital organs and the unnecessary removal of adjacent healthy tissues, followed by effective perioperative treatments (e.g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy, thermal ablation, and immunotherapy) to remove those naked-eye invisible microscopic residual foci and unresectable distant metastases. Accurate preoperative and intraoperative detection provides important information on the presence, location, size, number, and stages of tumors for surgeons’ clinical decision-making. In clinical practice, the surgical workflow typically starts with preoperative medical imaging to precisely localize the tumors and visualize the tumor margins for surgical planning. The prevailing clinical approaches including computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography, magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, and ultrasound imaging are widely used for noninvasive preoperative detection and guiding surgical resection of tumors (10). Nevertheless, these approaches suffer from high costs, large instrument footprints, limited spatial resolution, poor sensitivity, long operation time, as well as the incongruency between the preoperatively determined tumor location and the intraoperatively actual location due to the soft tissue shift during surgery (10–12). Moreover, these techniques face dilemma to detect residual microscopic lesions (typically tens of tumor cells, <1 mm) after surgery and precisely define the full extent of the tumor margins with tumor cells infiltrating into the adjacent normal tissues. Recently, optical imaging techniques such as photoacoustic (PA) imaging and fluorescence imaging have been explored for surgical navigation to guide surgical resection of tumors (13–18). Despite the large penetration depth and high spatial resolution, PA imaging shows the inabilities to detect microscopic lesions due to its insufficient sensitivity and to intraoperatively guide the real-time surgical resection of tumors (13, 14). Near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging presents high sensitivity and excellent molecular specificity with the absence of ionizing radiation for surgical navigation that can localize the tumors, clearly visualize the tumor margins, and guide the real-time surgical resection of tumors, but its clinical translation is restricted by the inabilities to visualize the infiltrated tumor foci at the tumor boundary, and the photobleaching and the tissue autofluorescence interference adversely affecting the long-time surgical operation (15–18). Therefore, the development of effective surgical navigation strategies is highly demanded for preoperative tumor localization, tumor margin delineation, and guiding complete removal of microtumors in real time.

Recently, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) nanoparticles (NPs) have attracted considerable interest as versatile optical contrasts for next-generation in vivo biosensing and bioimaging, integrating with multiple functionalities of SERS detection, CT and PA imaging, photothermal ablation, and sometimes drug carriers (19–25). SERS presents high sensitivity down to single-molecule level, excellent molecular specificity, powerful multiplexing detection capabilities, negligible photobleaching, and excellent anti-interference ability against complex biological matrices, overcoming those issues typically existing in conventional clinical medical techniques and optical imaging techniques (e.g., PA and fluorescence imaging). With these prominent features, SERS NPs could serve as ideal “all-in-one” versatile imaging contrast agents for multimodal imaging applications, for instance, accurate tumor margin delineation and intraoperative SERS-guided surgery for removal of tumors including microtumors and infiltrated tumor cells at the tumor boundary that are the major reasons for poor surgical outcomes (12, 14, 22–24, 26). Over the past decades, considerable efforts have been devoted to innovating high-performance SERS platforms for biosensing and bioimaging, demonstrating their great promises for practical biomedical applications (12, 14, 22–24, 26). Moreover, our group and others recently reported the in vivo SERS imaging of the tumors and realized the intraoperative detection for real-time eradiation of microscopic lesions in a surgical bed, achieving obvious improvement of surgical outcomes (12, 14, 24, 26).

After surgical resection, postsurgical adjuvant treatments for patients with cancer are also essential because surgery typically cannot treat metastatic tumors, and those invisible residual microtumors tend to induce local cancer recurrence and distant metastasis (27–29). At present, the clinical postsurgical cancer treatment mainly uses adjuvant radiotherapy, oral chemotherapy, or thermal ablation, but they rarely treat those metastatic tumors and are often associated with poor clinical outcomes and severe side effects (27–29). Moreover, these postsurgical adjuvant treatments are usually administrated after a few weeks of primary surgical resection for recovery of patients’ health from surgery. This long postsurgical recovery period may miss the best time for the treatment of microtumors in the surgical bed that potentially trigger local cancer recurrence and distant metastasis. As an alternative, recent studies have shown that cancer immunotherapy can arouse patients’ innate and adaptive immunity to inhibit local cancer recurrence and also treat those distant metastases (30–34). Cancer immunotherapy has been demonstrated with exciting therapeutic outcomes for the treatment of a range of advanced cancers, such as breast cancer, brain cancer, lung cancer, and melanoma (35, 36). Therefore, we infer that postsurgical immunotherapy is a promising adjuvant strategy for effective treatment of postsurgical microtumors and distant metastases.

In this work, we report an integrated diagnostic and therapeutic strategy combining SERS surgical navigation and postsurgical cancer immunotherapy, which allows presurgical determination of the tumor location and size, intraoperative SERS-guided surgical resection of the primary tumor, and immunotherapeutic eradication of local residual microtumors and distant metastases. Our strategy is principally based on multifunctional optical probes (namely, MATRA probes) integrating with T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging, photothermal effect, and Raman spectroscopic detection. Our MATRA probes are made of p-nitrothiophenol (p-NTP)–encoded Au nanostar (AuSts) embedded within a magnetic Gd3+-doped silica layer, which are further decorated with PEGylated Au@Cu2−xSe (GCS) core/shell NPs of NIR-II plasmonic activity (Fig. 1A). Using the MATRA probes, the surgical workflow begins for surgical planning with presurgical MR imaging and SERS detection to identify the tumors (Fig. 1, B to D); the ultrasensitive SERS detection allows real-time intraoperative visualization of the tumor margins and microtumors in the surgical bed. After surgical resection, the adjuvant immunotherapy combining the NIR-II photothermal effect of MATRA probes and programmed death-1 antibody (anti–PD-1) is carried out. Our results show substantial improvement of surgical outcomes using the present MATRA probes through presurgical MR/SERS imaging and intraoperative SERS-guided surgical resection. The subsequent immunotherapy further eradiates those local residual microtumors and distant metastases and thereby effectively inhibits the cancer recurrence and cancer metastasis, greatly improving the anticancer outcomes in a 4T1 breast tumor model.

RESULTS

Synthesis and characterization of MATRA probes

The MATRA probes consist of p-NTP–encoded plasmonic AuSt core encapsulated within a silica layer with the Gd3+ doping, GCS NP decoration, and surface PEGylation (Fig. 1A), whose synthetic procedure is illustrated in fig. S1. First, SERS NPs (AuSt@p-NTP@SiO2 NPs) were synthesized using a previously reported method in our laboratory (26, 37, 38), which were doped by Gd3+ in the presence of GdCl3 to yield Gd3+-SERS NPs. Then, Au@Gd3+-SERS NPs were made by decorating Gd3+-SERS NPs with Au NPs via direct reduction of HAuCl4 by NaBH4. Afterward, Au@Gd3+-SERS NPs were converted into GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs by a selenium-mediated two-step synthetic approach well established in our laboratory (39–41), in which the loading of GCS NPs could be adjusted by the SeO2 amount added. After that, GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs were functionalized with poly(ethylene glycol) monomethyl ether thiol (mPEG-SH) to improve the aqueous dispersibility and biocompatibility (42), yielding PEGylated GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs (named MATRA probes). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images in Fig. 2A and fig. S2 show that the GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs comprise a GCS-decorated silica shell surrounding an AuSt core, suggesting the obvious core-shell structure. The core-shell structure of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs was also verified by the energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping (Fig. 2B). It is obvious that the GCS loading in the GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs increases as the SeO2 concentration increases from 0.5 to 2.0 mM (fig. S2). The synthesis of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs was followed by optical extinction spectroscopy. The plasmon resonance wavelength is at 742 nm for as-synthesized AuSts and red-shifts to 776 nm for Gd3+-SERS NPs (Fig. 2C). In addition to the AuSt-associated plasmon band, Au@Gd3+-SERS NPs display a new plasmon band at 559 nm, assigned to the Au NPs deposited on the silica surface. An intense NIR-II plasmon band localized at 1051 nm is obviously seen in the extinction spectrum of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs synthesized with 1.0 mM SeO2, which is assigned to the plasmon resonance absorption of GCS NPs (39–41). We also observed that the plasmon resonance absorption of the NIR-II plasmon band is intensified with the elevated SeO2 concentration from 0.5 to 2.0 mM (fig. S3). The powder x-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs shows characteristic 2θ peaks at 26.7°, 44.6°, 52.9°, and 65.0° for the cubic berzelianite structure (JCPDS, no. 06-0680) and at 38.3° and 77.5° for the cubic Au nanostructure (JCPDS, no. 01-1172), verifying the successful decoration of GCS NPs on Gd3+-SERS NPs (Fig. 2D). The characteristic XRD peaks of the GCS component become more dominant over the Au components relative to those of the Au component with more loadings of GCS NPs in MATRA probes (fig. S4). The zeta potential of Gd3+-SERS NPs is −34 (±0.2) mV and gradually increases to −30 (±2.1) mV for Au@Gd3+-SERS NPs and −23.3 (±0.7) mV for GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs. The hydrodynamic size is 185.3 (±0.4) nm, 189.3 (±0.4) nm, and 213.6 (±0.9) nm for GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs synthesized with 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM SeO2, respectively (fig. S5). The results demonstrate the successful synthesis of MATRA probes.

Because GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs exhibit remarkable NIR-II plasmon resonance absorption, we next investigated the NIR-II photothermal conversion ability. Upon the 1064-nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2), we can apparently see a concentration-dependent temperature rise (ΔT) for all these GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs within 10 min (Fig. 2E and fig. S6). Notably, the maximum ΔT values of aqueous suspensions of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs (400 μg/ml) are 42.5°, 43.5°, and 44.5°C for GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs synthesized with 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM SeO2, respectively, while negligible ΔT (≈2°C) takes place for phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The NIR-II photothermal conversion efficiency (η) is determined to be 55.2, 64.7, and 68.4% for GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs synthesized with 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM SeO2, respectively (fig. S7). In addition, negligible variation of the steady-state temperature can be seen during the consecutive five on/off laser cycling irradiation (fig. S6). These results demonstrate the remarkable NIR-II photothermal conversion ability and photostability of the present GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs.

Then, we tested the NIR SERS performance of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs synthesized with varied SeO2 concentrations (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM). Agarose phantoms containing various concentrations of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs (6.25 to 400 μg/ml) were made for the SERS measurements under NIR 785-nm laser (Fig. 2F and fig. S8). All these agarose phantoms show characteristic Raman peaks of p-NTP, and the SERS images become brighter with the elevated concentration of GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs. Notably, we found that the SERS intensity decreased with more GCS loading in GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs, which is attributed to the optical shielding effect of the GCS component with strong optical absorption at the laser wavelength (Fig. 2F and fig. S8). The nonlinear increase of the SERS intensity at higher concentrations of SERS probes could be attributed to the decreased laser exposure along the propagation pathway, signal saturation, and the low collection efficiency far-away from the sample upper surface. The limit of detection is estimated to be 2.39, 4.26, and 6.84 μg/ml for GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs synthesized with 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM SeO2, respectively. Thus, the GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs synthesized with 1.0 mM SeO2 were used to create the MATRA probes for following experiments, which has a Gd3+ loading of 20% determined by inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES). We observed insignificant variations of the hydrodynamic size of MATRA probes after incubation in either 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) or cell culture medium [Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM)] and also no obvious deterioration of the SERS intensity after continuous exposure to the 785-nm laser (9.2 mW) for 1 hour (fig. S9). These results further demonstrate the excellent colloidal stability and photostability of the present MATRA probes. Because Gd3+ is the most widely used contrast agent in clinic for T1-weighted MR imaging, we assessed the MR performance of MATRA probes. As shown in Fig. 2G, the T1-weighted MR images become brighter with the increasing concentration of MATRA probes, and the relaxivity r1 is measured through linear regression of the relaxation data versus the Gd3+ concentration ([Gd3+]) using the formula (43): 1/T1 = r1 × [Gd3+] + b, where T1 is the relaxation time, r1 is the relaxivity, and b is the intercept. The relaxivity r1 is determined to be 11.02 mM−1·s−1, much higher than that ≈4 mM−1·s−1 (at 3 T) of the commercial Gd3+-1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (44). Overall, these results suggest that our MATRA probes could be applied as a “three-in-one” multifunctional contrast agent for MR imaging, sensitive SERS detection, and NIR-II photothermal ablation.

In vitro immunostimulatory effects and SERS delineation of live cancer cells

Given the excellent NIR-II photothermal conversion ability of our MATRA probes, we performed a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay to evaluate their cytotoxicity and photothermal killing ability against 4T1 cells. The results shown in Fig. 3A (i) demonstrate a high cell viability of >92% even after incubation for 12 hours at a high concentration of up to 400 μg/ml. However, when the cells were treated with MATRA probes plus 1064-nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 5 min), the cell viability of 4T1 cells exhibits a dose-dependent decrease with only 24% survival of the treated cells at 400 μg/ml, indicative of the impressive photothermal killing ability of the MATRA probes. Similar results were also observed in the LIVE/DEAD cell costaining assay (Fig. 3A, ii. The temporal cellular uptake was examined using Cy3 dye-labeled MATRA probes. The red fluorescence in the treated 4T1 cells becomes stronger with prolonging incubation, indicating the increasing cellular uptake of Cy3 dye-labeled MATRA probes after longer incubation (fig. S10). The MATRA probes are nonuniformly localized in the cytoplasm, verified by the cellular fluorescence imaging of Cy3 dye-labeled MATRA probe–treated 4T1 cells (fig. S11).

Photothermal treatment can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) with the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) in dying cancer cells and the subsequent immune system activation (45, 46). Thus, we next assessed the expression of two critical DAMPs—calreticulin (CRT) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in 4T1 cells treated with MATRA probes plus 1064-nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 5 min). The treatments with either PBS plus 1064-nm laser irradiation or MATRA probes were conducted for control. The immunofluorescence images show that the CRT expression in the plasma membrane of 4T1 cells is the highest when treated with MATRA probes plus laser while negligible in 4T1 cells treated in the control groups (Fig. 3B). Similarly, we obviously see the lowest expression of HMGB1 in the cell nuclei after the combination treatment with MATRA probes plus laser irradiation, indicating the highest HMGB1 secretion in the MATRA probes + laser group (Fig. 3C). This suggests that the combination treatment with MATRA probes plus laser irradiation induces much stronger ICD of 4T1 cells than the treatment with either PBS plus laser or the MATRA probes alone.

To study the capability of MATRA probes for targeted SERS imaging of live cancer cells, 4T1 cells were incubated with MATRA probes (120 μg/ml) for 12 hours and then washed with PBS to remove excess MATRA probes, which were subsequently subjected to the SERS measurement. As presented in Fig. 3D, the SERS image of the live cells can depict the contour of the cells. Furthermore, strong SERS signals characteristic of p-NTP can be observed inside the cell, whereas negligible SERS signals appear outside the cell. Thus, the present MATRA probes can detect cancer cells with high sensitivity and high specificity, suggesting their potential for precisely delineating the tumor margins and identifying the microtumors in a surgical bed.

Image-guided surgical resection of the primary tumor

After verifying the remarkable SERS detection performances of MATRA probes at the cellular level, we performed longitudinal SERS imaging of the tumors in a 4T1 breast tumor–bearing mouse model intravenously administrated with MATRA probes (2.4 mg/ml, 200 μl in PBS). As shown fig. S12, the SERS images of the tumor become brighter with prolonging time, indicating the time-dependent tumor accumulation of MATRA probes. Afterward, we investigated their potential in guiding surgical resection of the primary tumor in a 4T1 breast tumor mouse model (Fig. 4A). We injected 4T1 tumor-bearing mice through tail vein with MATRA probes and presurgically performed whole-body MR imaging and in vivo SERS imaging after 1 day of the injection. For comparison, we also took the MR image of 4T1 tumor-bearing mice before injection of MATRA probes. As shown in Fig. 4B, the MR image taken after injection of MATRA probes shows much clearer visualization of the tumor than that without injection of MATRA probes, demonstrating the enhanced localization capability of the tumors by the treatment with MATRA probes. After naked eye–guided surgical resection, no visible tumor foci are seen in the MR image of the surgical bed. To examine whether the tumor lesions are completely removed by surgery, we analyzed the surgical beds from 4T1 tumor-bearing mice using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and SERS detection (n = 22). Figure 4 (C and D) presents representative H&E-stained images of the tissue sections in these surgical beds and SERS images of the surgical beds after the surgical resection of the primary tumors. It can be seen that there exist multiple microtumor foci in the surgical bed in Fig. 4C, while no observable microtumor foci in the surgical bed shown in Fig. 4D and figs. S13 and S14. We observed a 2.5-fold increase of the MR contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) at the tumor sites after treatment with MATRA probes (Fig. 4E, i). The H&E staining analysis shows that 77% of the mice present tumor-positive tissues after surgery with an average microtumor area percentage of 4.3 (±3.5)%, which is defined as the tumor-positive area divided by the total area of the surgical bed (Fig. 4E, ii). We obviously observed strong SERS characteristics of MATRA probes at these tumor-positive sites with no obvious SERS signals in the tumor-negative sites of the surgical bed, consistent with the results from H&E analysis. Threshold segmentation was exploited to discriminate between the tumor-positive and tumor-negative SERS characteristics, creating the segmentation maps with the intensity of the 1340-cm−1 SERS peak [v(NO2)] [Fig. 4, C (iii) and D (iii)]. The quantitative analysis from the segmentation maps shows that ≈6.7 (±5.0)% of the total area of the surgical bed is tumor positive (Fig. 4E, iii). Compared with the H&E analysis, the much higher tumor-positive area percentage from the SERS measurement is attributed to the high sensitivity of the SERS detection in our MATRA probes. Thus, we demonstrate that using the MATRA probe, the SERS detection enables more sensitive detection of microtumors in the surgical bed, which could achieve improved surgical outcomes owing to more complete removal of microtumors.

Next, image-guided surgical resection of the primary tumor was carried out, schematically illustrated in Fig. 5. First, after 24 hours of intravenous injection of MATRA probes, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were anesthetized and subjected to the whole-body MR imaging to localize the tumors and subsequently in vivo SERS imaging to delineate the tumor margins. As shown in Fig. 5A, the SERS image well depicts the contour of the primary tumor and delineates the tumor margins with strong SERS signals inside the tumor and weak or negligible SERS signals outside the tumor. Then, the primary tumor was removed sequentially by surgical resection under the guidance of the SERS images. We intraoperatively collected high-resolution SERS images of the surgical bed and the resected tumor tissues after each resection step, which are correlated with their optical images (Fig. 5, B and C). With each resection, the SERS images of the surgical bed well depict the contour of the tumors left behind after the resection, and the SERS images of the resected tumors follow their visual shapes as well. The resected tissues were further examined by H&E staining analysis, verifying that the resected tissues were cancerous without the obvious presence of normal tissues. The resection seems to be complete with no visual inspection of tumor foci in the surgical bed, but the SERS image of the surgical bed reveals obvious microtumors. In addition, the histological analysis also confirms the presence of microtumors at the tumor boundary owing to the infiltration of tumor cells into the adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 5D). Therefore, we demonstrate that the intraoperative SERS detection using our MATRA probes is able to guide the real-time surgical resection and identify those microtumors invisible by naked eyes and clinical MR imaging.

In vivo therapeutic effects of image-guided surgery with postsurgical immunotherapy

The aforementioned results have unambiguously demonstrated that multiple residual tumor foci are still retained in the surgical bed after SERS-guided surgical resection, verified by the intraoperative SERS detection. In addition, the surgical resection cannot treat distant metastases. In recent years, cancer immunotherapy has been demonstrated with great potential for cancer treatments (35, 36). In view of this, we next studied whether the combination of image-guided surgery with postsurgical adjuvant immunotherapy could improve the treatment outcomes with elimination of local residual microtumors and distant metastases. Anti–PD-1 immunotherapy has been demonstrated to be a promising treatment strategy for a diversity of cancers, achieving substantial improvement of therapeutic outcomes for metastatic cancers (47, 48). The timeline and experimental design of the combined image-guided surgery with postsurgical anti–PD-1 immunotherapy are presented in Fig. 6A. To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic effects, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6), which are intravenously injected with: (i) intravenous injection of 200 μl of PBS (control group), (ii) SERS-guided cancer surgery (surgery group), (iii) SERS-guided cancer surgery plus anti–PD-1 immunotherapy (surgery + PD-1 group), (iv) SERS-guided cancer surgery plus laser irradiation (surgery + laser group), and (v) SERS-guided cancer surgery plus laser irradiation and PD-1 antibody immunotherapy (surgery + laser + PD-1 group). The 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with PBS (200 μl) or MATRA probes (2.4 mg/ml, 200 μl), and whole-body MR imaging and in vivo SERS imaging were performed after 24 hours of the injection (day 0) to guide the surgical resection of the primary tumor. After surgical resection of the primary tumor, we conducted the SERS imaging over the entire surgical bed to examine whether there exist residual microtumors. Once SERS-positive sites were found, these sites were exposed to a 1064-nm laser at 1.0 W/cm2 for 5 min for tumor site–selective photothermal ablation. We examined the photothermal temperature of the surgical bed after the resection of the primary tumor, as shown in Fig. 6B. It can be seen that the temperature of the surgical bed progressively increases to a steady-state temperature of ≈42.5°C within 5 min in the MATRA probes + laser group, much higher than that (38.6°C) in the PBS + laser group (Fig. 6B). This demonstrates the considerable in vivo photothermal effect of MATRA probes. Afterward, the distant metastatic mouse model was created by inoculating 4T1 cells into the right abdominal of the treated mice, followed by adjuvant immunotherapy with three consecutive injections of anti–PD-1 at days 1, 4, and 8 after surgery. The biodistribution analysis of the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and tumors shows that the injected MATRA probes are accumulated in the tumor tissues with a high percentage of about 5.3% only lower than those in the liver and spleen, indicating effective accumulation in the tumors (Fig. 6C).

According to the results (Fig. 6, D and E), the surgical treatment is effective to remove the primary tumor, which slightly relapses over time. The combination treatment of surgical resection, laser irradiation, and anti–PD-1 immunotherapy completely removes the primary tumor without observable postsurgical local recurrence and also inhibits the distant metastases, more effective than the other groups. We also assessed the therapeutic effect of the regular surgical treatment guided by naked eyes. As displayed in fig. S15, after the naked eye–guided surgical treatment, three of the six mice show no obvious tumor growth due to the complete removal of the tumors, while obvious tumor growth is observed in one mouse and slight tumor growth takes place in two mice. The inhibition rates of the primary tumors and distant metastases are 100 and 80% in the surgery + laser + PD-1 group, respectively (Fig. 6F). Although substantial inhibition of recurrence of the primary tumor takes place in the groups such as control, surgery, surgery + PD-1, and surgery + laser, we can see obvious recurrence of the distant tumor in these groups. The mice receiving the combination treatment of surgery plus laser irradiation plus anti–PD-1 immunotherapy showed extended survival (Fig. 6G). The results suggest that the adjuvant immunological treatment from anti–PD-1 and laser irradiation plays essential roles in the suppression of the local tumor recurrence and distant metastasis. Furthermore, no obvious changes of the body weight of the mice are observed among various treatment groups, confirming the good biosafety of MATRA probes (fig. S16). The H&E histological analysis of the slices of the major organs harvested from the treated mice shows negligible histological abnormalities, indicating no obvious systemic toxicity of MATRA probes (fig. S17). The hematological and biochemical analysis of blood serum samples reveals no noticeable difference between the PBS-treated mice and MATRA probe–treated mice (2.4 mg/ml) (fig. S18), suggesting no obvious adverse effects on the liver/kidney functions and immune response.

Immunoinhibitory effects against postsurgical local residual microtumors and lung metastases

The immune response in spleen tissues was next studied using flow cytometry. Specifically, the spleen was harvested from 4T1 tumor-bearing mice at day 15 after various treatments and analyzed using flow cytometry. The local presentation of tumor antigens in dendritic cells could trigger the increase of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells (48). As presented in Fig. 7A, flow cytometric analysis displays a notable increase in CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells in spleen tissues from the surgery + laser + PD-1 group, relative to those in other groups. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining also demonstrates an obvious increase in CD8+ T cells and CD4+ T cells in the distant metastases from the surgery + laser + PD-1 group, much higher than those in other groups (Fig. 7A). These findings verify that the immune response is effectively triggered by the injected MATRA probes and the NIR-II laser irradiation as well as anti–PD-1 immunotherapy, resulting in effective suppression of postsurgical tumor recurrence and distant metastasis. We further revealed notably elevated levels of multiple cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) in sera of 4T1 tumor-bearing mice in surgery + laser, surgery + PD-1, and surgery + laser + PD-1 groups (Fig. 7B). The surgery + laser + PD-1 group presents the highest levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ, which is attributed to more effective activation of the anticancer immune system through the combination treatment with surgical resection, NIR-II photothermal ablation, and anti–PD-1 immunotherapy. Further, we examined the lung metastasis in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice after various treatments. The lung tissues were harvested at day 31 after treatment and stained with India ink, showing that the number of the metastatic nodules in the lungs follows: control > surgery > surgery plus laser > surgery plus PD-1 > surgery plus laser plus PD-1, with no observable metastatic nodules in the surgery + laser + PD-1 group, further verified by the H&E histological analysis (Fig. 7C). Thus, we conclude that the combination treatment with surgical resection, NIR-II photothermal ablation, and anti–PD-1 immunotherapy could effectively suppress the postsurgical lung metastasis.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have reported a strategy combining SERS-guided surgical resection with postsurgical immunotherapeutic treatment of local microscopic tumor foci left behind after surgery and distant metastases for improved treatment outcomes. This strategy is principally based on the multifunctional MATRA probes integrating with MR imaging, sensitive SERS detection, and excellent NIR-II photothermal effects, followed by anti–PD-1 immunotherapy. In vivo studies in a 4T1 breast tumor mouse model demonstrate that the intravenously administrated MATRA probes enable (i) presurgical MR/SERS dual-modal imaging to determine the specific tumor location, size, and shape; (ii) intraoperative SERS imaging to accurately delineate the tumor margins, overcoming the shift issue of soft tissues during the surgery, and (iii) real-time intraoperative SERS-guided surgical resection of the primary tumor to achieve the least retention of microscopic tumor foci in the surgical bed and allow tumor foci-selective photothermal elimination. These prominent performances are attributed to the high sensitivity, molecular specificity, and negligible tissue fluorescence interference of the SERS detection, as well as long-term retention of MATRA probes in tumor tissues. The sensitive SERS detection allows for visualization of tiny tumor lesions for tumor site–selective photothermal ablation, effectively overcoming the drawbacks of poor resolution of the surgeon’s naked eyes. We verify the improved surgical outcomes with SERS-guided surgery in 4T1 breast tumor–bearing mice through intravenous administration of MATRA probes. Moreover, it is shown that postsurgical anti–PD-1 adjuvant immunotherapy effectively eliminates the local residual microscopic foci in the surgical bed and distant metastases, greatly inhibiting local cancer recurrence and distant metastasis. We would like to point out that MR imaging is a widely used clinical technique with the ability of whole-body imaging, and the SERS technique has great advantage for microarea detection due to its high sensitivity. We expected that the combination of the MR imaging and SERS technique integrated in the present multifunctional MATRA probes has great potential for surgical navigation in human subjects with optimized parameters (e.g., dosage, spatial resolution, and laser exposure). Overall, this study provides a general strategy combining SERS surgical navigation with postsurgical immunotherapy to improve treatment outcomes of malignant solid cancers.

Characterization

TEM images were recorded on a FEI Tecnai G2 F20 S-TWIN TMP transmission electron microscope at 200 kV. High angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) microscopy and EDX elemental analysis were carried out using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200X scanning/transmission electron microscope at 200 kV. Optical extinction spectra were taken using an Agilent Cary 5000 UV-visible NIR spectrophotometer. The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential measurements were conducted on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZSE analyzer. Powder XRD patterns were taken using a Rigaku SmartLab3kW x-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 30 mA). The CCK-8 viability assay was carried out through a Tecan Spark microplate reader. Fluorescence imaging was conducted on either a Zeiss LSM880 Airyscan confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) or a Zeiss Axio Vert. A1 inverted fluorescence microscope. Quantitative compositional analysis was performed with either an Agilent 5100 ICP-OES or an Agilent 7900 ICP–mass spectrometer. MR imaging was performed using a clinic 3.0-T MR scanner (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens, Germany).

Synthesis of MATRA probes

First, AuSts were synthesized following the seed-mediated growth method well established in our laboratory (49, 50). Then, AuSts were used as the plasmonic substrates for the SERS enhancer to prepare SERS NPs (37, 38). Briefly, an aqueous solution of AuSts (2 mg/ml) was added with 10 μM p-NTP. After magnetically stirring for 30 min at room temperature, the reaction mixture was added with 20 μl of MPTMS and 140 μl of 14.7 M (25%) NH3·H2O. The reaction mixture was stirred for 3 min at 1200 rpm and kept standing at 30°C for 12 hours, followed by centrifugation at 4500 rpm and washing three times with ultrapure water to obtain AuSt@p-NTP@SiO2 NPs (denoted as SERS NPs hereafter).

Subsequently, Gd3+-SERS NPs were prepared following the protocol reported by the Zhao group with slight modification (51). Briefly, a Gd3+ stock solution was first made by mixing 100 μl of 1.0 M trisodium citrate solution, 50 μl of 1.0 M GdCl3, and 150 μl of 1.5 M NH3·H2O. Then, 40 μl of the Gd3+ stock solution was added to 10 ml of the aqueous solution of SERS NPs (0.6 mg/ml). After continuously stirring for 24 hours at 40°C, Gd3+-SERS NPs were purified by centrifugation and successively washing twice with ethanol and once with water and stored in ultrapure water for the following use. The mass percentage of the Gd loading in Gd3+-SERS NPs was determined to be 20% by ICP-OES. Subsequently, 5 ml of 0.1 mM HAuCl4 aqueous solution was added with 100 pM Gd3+-SERS NPs, and the pH value of which was adjusted to 9.0 with NaOH aqueous solution (10 mM). After stirring for 15 min at room temperature, 600 μl of 10 mM NaBH4 was added. The reaction proceeded for 6 hours under magnetic stirring, and the reaction mixture was centrifuged at 4500 rpm and washed twice with ultrapure water, producing Au NP–decorated Gd3+-SERS NPs (denoted Au@Gd3+-SERS NPs). Next, a selenium-mediated two-step synthetic approach developed in our laboratory was adopted for synthesis of Au@Cu2−xSe core/shell NP-decorated Gd3+-SERS NPs (denoted as GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs) (39–41). Specifically, Au@Gd3+-SERS NPs were added at 150 pM to 15 ml of PVP aqueous solution (5.0 mg/ml), along with the addition of SeO2 at varied concentrations (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mM). After stirring for 10 min, 250 μl of 100 mM AA was added. The reaction mixture was stirred for another 15 min and added with CuSO4 aqueous solution (1.0, 2.0, and 4.0 mM) and 250 μl of 100 mM AA. After proceeding for 24 hours, the reaction solution was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min and washed three times with ultrapure water. The resulting precipitates were dispersed in 1.0 ml of ethanol, followed by the addition of 10 mg of mPEG-SH. After proceeding for another 24 hours, PEGylated GCS@Gd3+-SERS NPs (denoted as MATRA probes hereafter) were obtained and washed with ultrapure water and stored in ultrapure water at 1.0 mg/ml for following experiments.

Evaluation of NIR-II photothermal conversion performance of MATRA probes

In situ infrared thermal images and temperature of aqueous solutions of MATRA probes were recorded in real time using an FLIR A35 infrared thermal imaging camera. Cell culture and cytotoxicity test

The 4T1 mouse breast cancer cell line was provided by the American Type Culture Collection. The 4T1 cells used in this work is not listed in the misidentified cell line registry curated by the International Cell Line Authentication Committee. The 4T1 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented by 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO2-humidified incubator.

The cytotoxicity was evaluated against 4T1 cells using the standard CCK-8 assay. In a brief, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 8 × 103 cells per well and cultured overnight for cell adhesion. The culture medium was removed and replenished with 100 μl of fresh culture medium containing various concentrations of MATRA probes [PBS (0), 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 μg/ml]. After 12-hour incubation, the treated cells in laser irradiation (+ laser) groups were exposed to a 1064-nm laser for 5 min at a power density of 1.0 W/cm2. Then, the culture medium was replaced with 100 μl of fresh culture medium containing 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent. After incubation for another 60 min, the absorbance at 450 nm of each well was measured using a Tecan Spark microplate reader for determination of cell viabilities. In addition, the treated cells were also examined by fluorescence microscopy after LIVE/DEAD cell costaining with calcein-AM (green) and PI (red).

Animal model

Female BALB/c mice (4 weeks, 13 to 16 g) were ordered from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. All animal experiments in this work were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Central South University (approval no. CSU-2022-0111). The 4T1 primary orthotopic breast tumor model was established by inoculating 25 μl of cell suspension containing 2.5 × 106 4T1 cells dispersed in PBS into the left second breast of each mouse. Once the primary tumor reached to ≈250 mm3, the 4T1 primary tumor models were established for use in animal-related experiments. To establish the distant metastatic tumor model, the 4T1 primary orthotopic breast tumor models were first established following the method described above. After the primary tumors were surgically resected, 25 μl of cell suspension containing 5 × 105 4T1 cells dispersed in PBS was inoculated into the right abdominal of each mouse. The tumor size, weight, and survival of the mice were monitored every other day. The tumor volume (V) was calculated by the following formula: V = (L × W2)/2, where L and W represent the length and width of the tumors, respectively.

SERS measurements

Both spontaneous Raman and SERS measurements were carried out using a Renishaw inVia confocal Raman spectrometer with a Leica DM2700M Ren RL/TL microscope and an air-cooled charged-coupled device detector in conjunction with a holographic grating of 1200 lines/mm. The NIR 785-nm diode laser with a maximum power output of 300 mW was used as the excitation source. All Raman and SERS spectra were preprocessed with the baseline subtraction and the cosmic spike removal using the integrated WiRE 5.3 software. To test the SERS performance of MATRA probes, agarose phantoms containing various concentrations of MATRA probes were made, and the SERS images were conducted using a 50× objective lens with a laser power of 9.2 mW on the sample and 2-s integration time at a step size of 300 μm. The SERS images were created with the peak intensity of the 1340-cm−1 SERS band.

For cellular SERS imaging, 4T1 cells (5 × 105 cells per well) were seeded into a quartz slide placed in a well of the six-well plate. After 12-hour culture for cell adhesion, the culture medium was removed and replenished with fresh culture medium containing MATRA probes (120 μg/ml). After culture for 12 hours, the cells were washed with PBS and cultured in fresh culture medium, which were subjected to the SERS imaging with a 50× objective lens (numerical aperture = 0.5) (12-mW laser power on the sample, 0.5-s integration time, and 1.0-μm step size). The SERS images were created with the peak intensity of the 1340-cm−1 SERS band.

For ex vivo and in vivo SERS imaging of the tumors, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with 200 μl of MATRA probes (2.4 mg/ml) dispersed in PBS. The ex vivo SERS imaging of the tumors was performed on the tumor resected at day 1 after injection, and the in vivo SERS imaging was performed on the tumor or the surgical bed over an area of 1 cm by 1 cm, both using 5× objective lens with 75.7-mW laser power, 3-s integration time, and 300-μm step size. The mice were anesthetized initially with 100 μl of 1% pentobarbital solution and then maintained in anesthesia status with 50 μL 1% pentobarbital solution for a 1–2 h period during the surgery. The multivariate curve resolution with alternating least squares algorithm was used to discriminate between the tumor-positive and tumor-negative SERS signals and thereby convert the SERS images into the corresponding segmentation maps.

In vitro and in vivo MR imaging

In vitro MR imaging was conducted by dispersing various concentrations (0, 0.0156, 0.0313, 0.0625, and 0.125 mM, Gd basis) of MATRA probes in water placed in a microcentrifuge tube. T1-weighted MR images were acquired, and the T1 relaxation rate was achieved for various concentrations to calculate the r1 relaxivity through the liner fitting of the 1/T1 relaxation time as a function of the Gd concentration. For in vivo MR imaging, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice (n = 3) were intravenously injected at 24 hours after with 200 μl of MATRA probes (2.4 mg/ml), which were anesthetized with 100 μl of 2% pentobarbital solution for MR imaging on a clinic 3.0-T MR scanner.

Detection of ICD biomarkers

The CRT and HMGB1 expressions in 4T1 cells after various treatments were assessed by immunofluorescence assay. In a brief, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a cell density of 8 × 103 cells per well and incubated in either culture medium or culture medium containing MATRA probes (120 μg/ml) for 12 hours. Afterward, the cells were washed three times with PBS, followed by exposure to a 1064-nm laser (1.0 W/cm2, 5 min) for the laser irradiation (+ laser) group. Then, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and further blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution for 1 hour at 37°C and then treated with either anti-CRT Ab or anti-HMGB1 Ab at 4°C for 12 hours, followed by staining with Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody at 37°C for 2 hours. The cells were further stained with Hoechst 33342 (nuclei, blue) at room temperature for 15 min and subjected to immediate observation by CLSM.

Evaluation of in vivo therapeutic effects

To study in vivo therapeutic effects of the combined image-guided surgery with postsurgical immunotherapy, 4T1 breast tumor–bearing mice were randomly divided into five groups for various treatments (n = 6): (i) intravenous injection of 200 μl of PBS (control), (ii) SERS-guided cancer surgery (surgery), (iii) SERS-guided cancer surgery plus PD-1 antibody immunotherapy (surgery + PD-1), (iv) SERS-guided cancer surgery plus 1064-nm laser irradiation (surgery + laser), and (v) SERS-guided cancer surgery plus 1064-nm laser irradiation and PD-1 antibody immunotherapy (surgery + laser + PD-1). For SERS-guided cancer surgery, 4T1 breast tumor–bearing mice were intravenously injected with 200 μl of MATRA probes (2.4 mg/ml) dispersed in PBS, and after 1 day of the intravenous injection, MR and SERS imaging were carried out to localize the primary tumor and delineate the tumor margins. For laser irradiation groups, the surgical bed was exposed to a 1064-nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 5 min immediately after SERS-guided surgical resection of the primary tumor. For the immunotherapeutic treatment, the 4T1 breast tumor–bearing mice after the combined treatment of surgical resection plus laser irradiation were inoculated with 25 μl of cell suspension containing 5 × 105 4T1 cells dispersed in PBS in the right abdominal of each mouse. Afterward, PD-1 antibody was intraperitoneally injected at days 1, 4, and 8 at a dosage of 75 μg per mouse. Body weights and tumor volumes were monitored and recorded every other day over a 15-day period. To evaluate the anticancer outcomes, the survival of the treated mice was monitored over a 31-day period after surgery. The treated mice were etuhanized at day 31 after surgery, and major organs (heart, spleen, liver, lung, and kidney) and tumors were harvested, followed by H&E staining analysis. The lungs were harvested at day 31 after various treatments and stained with India ink for counting of the number of tumor nodules to analyze the lung metastasis.

Cytokine analysis

On the day 15 after various treatments, blood was taken from the eyeballs of 4T1 tumor-bearing mice and the serum was separated. The levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ in the serum samples were determined using the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instruction.

Flow cytometric analysis of the spleen tissues

The spleen tissues of the 4T1 tumor-bearing mice after various treatments were retrieved for flow cytometric analysis. The spleen tissues were harvested and homogenized in PBS to obtain the single-cell suspensions and washed twice with erythrocyte lysis buffer to remove erythrocyte. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and then treated with 2% BSA solution to block the nonspecific binding sites for another 30 min. To analyze tumor-infiltrating T cells, the cell suspensions of tumors were stained by APC–anti-CD3e mAb, FITC–anti-CD4 mAb, and PE–anti-CD8a mAb at 4°C for 12 hours following the manufacturers’ instruction and lastly analyzed by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence analysis of distant metastases

To determine the tumor-infiltrating T cells, the distant metastases were harvested from 4T1 tumor-bearing mice after various treatments and fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by sequentially cut and dewaxing of 4-μm-thick paraffin sections, antigen repair, and then incubation in PBS buffer containing 3% BSA for 1 hour at 37°C. The primary antibodies were then incubated at 4°C overnight. In this experiment, antibodies against CD4 and CD8 were used. Afterward, the sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies, followed by staining with Hoechst 33342 in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Last, the treated tissue sections were observed by CLSM.

In vivo biosafety assessment

4T1 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with either 200 μl of PBS or 200 μl of MATRA probes (5 mg/ml) dispersed in PBS (n = 3). In laser irradiation groups, the 1064-nm laser was irradiated at a power density of 1.0 W/cm2 for 5 min. The treated mice were euthanized at day 1 after various treatments, and blood samples were collected for blood routine analysis and blood biochemistry analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as means ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software. Statistical significance between multiple groups was determined using Student’s t test. Survival data were determined for each group by the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by the log-rank test. Significance differences were indicated as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.0001.
