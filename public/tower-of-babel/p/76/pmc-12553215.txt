Computerized cognitive training enhances cognitive function in Alzheimer’s disease by downregulating Ruminococcus-TMAO pathway
Authors: Wenbo Zhang, Jiaqi Song, Fuxin Zhong, Wuhan Yu, Zhangjin Qin, Ziying Yang, Jiarui Liu, Xiaoqin Wang, Lihua Chen, Wenqi Lü, Dianxia Xing, Junjin Liu, Chengcheng Huang, Jiani Wu, Xintong Liu, Weihua Yu, Yang Lü
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe microbiota-gut-brain (MGB) axis is implicated in Alzheimer’s disease (AD), but evidence for interventional strategies targeting this axis remains limited.MethodsIn a 24-week, single-blind, randomized controlled trial, 84 individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild AD received either computerized cognitive training (CCT) or treatment as usual (TAU). Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-cognitive subscale (ADAS-cog) was the primary outcome. We also assessed functional connectivity (fNIRS), plasma trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) levels, and gut microbiota at baseline and 24 weeks.ResultsSeventy-four participants completed the study. The CCT group showed significant improvement in ADAS-cog scores compared to controls (Cohen’s d = 1.57 by week 24). Notably, CCT also induced a distinct reorganization of prefrontal functional connectivity and significantly reduced plasma TMAO levels. Microbiome analysis revealed that CCT mitigated the expansion of Ruminococcus torques group (R.torques), which was observed in the control group. Crucially, R.torques was the only genus significantly correlated with improvements in cognition (ADAS-cog, r = 0.407), neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPI, r = 0.395), TMAO reduction (r = 0.443), and functional connectivity changes (r = 0.449).ConclusionA 24-week CCT program improves cognitive function in MCI and mild AD, potentially through downregulating the R.torques-TMAO pathway within the MGB axis. This pathway represents a promising novel target for multi-domain intervention in AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07209-4.

Introduction

The relationship between gut microbiota and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) has gained significant attention in recent years as a potential pathway for understanding AD pathogenesis and identifying therapeutic targets [1]. Emerging evidence suggests that specific gut bacteria may play either pathogenic or beneficial roles in AD patients [2, 3], influencing cognitive function through complex biochemical pathways. However, most studies to date have been cross-sectional, often exhibiting substantial variability in the bacterial taxa identified across AD populations [4–6]. This highlights the need for longitudinal studies to establish causal relationships and identify specific bacterial species that may influence AD progression.

A promising area of research focuses on gut microbiota-derived metabolites, particularly Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), which is elevated in the plasma and cerebrospinal fluid of AD patients [7, 8]. TMAO has been identified as a key microbiota-derived metabolite associated with AD biomarkers and has a notable potential to promote the aggregation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and tau proteins [9, 10]. However, the precise mechanisms through which TMAO contributes to cognitive decline in AD remain unclear.

The bidirectional regulation within the microbiota-gut-brain (MGB) axis offers a unique framework for understanding how gut microbiota may influence brain function in AD and other neural disorders [11, 12]. This axis facilitates complex metabolic, immune, neuronal, and endocrine communication between the gut and brain, which may significantly impact neurological health. Previous studies have highlighted that key metabolites produced by gut microbiota, such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) like acetate and butyrate, can profoundly influence brain function and behavior through G-protein-coupled receptors [13]. Additionally, gut-derived cytokines can activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and neurotransmitters produced by gut bacteria may interact directly with the brain via the vagus nerve, further emphasizing the multifaceted nature of the MGB axis [1, 12].

Despite the potential of the MGB axis, most interventions aimed at modulating it have focused on bottom-up approaches, such as gut-based treatments [14, 15], often overlooking the potential of brain-based (top-down) interventions to induce changes within the axis. This study proposes that non-pharmacological, brain-centered interventions–specifically computerized cognitive training (CCT) [16, 17]-may positively influence cognitive function, neural plasticity, and biomarkers within the MGB axis in individuals with AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI).

To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted a 24-week randomized controlled trial to examine the impact of CCT on cognitive abilities, neural plasticity, plasma TMAO levels, and gut microbiota composition in MCI and mild AD. This study aims to bridge the gap in understanding the role of MGB axis in AD by determining whether CCT-driven cognitive improvements are associated with shifts in gut microbiota composition and enhanced brain functional connectivity, as measured by functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS). The findings from this approach may offer insights into AD pathogenesis and present potential therapeutic targets within the MGB axis for multidimensional intervention in AD.

Methods

Study design and registrations

The design is a single-blind, randomized, controlled clinical trial with two-treatment arm (CCT over 24 weeks vs. treatment as usual [TAU]) (Fig. 1). The trial adhered to the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration, the CONSORT statement, and the CONSORT statement for nonpharmacologic treatment [18, 19]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University. Participants were recruited from Memory Clinic of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, and all provided written informed consent. The trail was registered at chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2100051362).

Participants

All participants underwent a comprenhensive medical history review, a neuropsychological battery, structural magnetic resonance imaging (sMRI), and routine blood tests. MCI and AD diagnoses were made according to 2011 criteria from the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA/AA) [20, 21]. Inclusion criteria included: (1) Aged between 55 and 90 years; (2) Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0.5 or 1; (3) At least three months of stable dosing of antidementia or mood-stabilizing medication for participants with mild AD; (4) Primary school education and higher; (5) A consistent caregiver (>5 days/week); (6) Signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Conditions that might hinder completion of assessments; (2) Severe psychiatric illness or antidepressants use; (3) Condition that would interfere with training and follow-up assessments; (4) Other disorders that would impact cognition.

Sample size

Sample size calculation were performed using G*Power 3.1.9, with cognition (measured by the Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-cognitive subscale, ADAS-cog) as the primary outcome, a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.35 [22] for CCT was targeted. With 85% power and a two-tailed α-level of 0.05, 68 participants were initially required. Anticipating a 20% attrition rate, the target recruitment was increased to 84 participants to ensure robust results.

Randomization and blinding

Participants with MCI or mild AD were stratified and randomly assigned to CCT or Control (TAU) groups in a 1:1 ratio, creating four subgroups: MCI control (C-MCI), MCI CCT (T-MCI), mild AD control (C-AD), and mild AD CCT (T-AD), each with 21 participants. Randomization was performed using a random number table generated by MATLAB (R2013b) by an independent statistician. While participant blinding was not feasible due to the nature of the interventions, single blinding was applied for researchers conducting measurements to minimize assessor biases. Blinding was maintained for data management, outcome assessment, and analysis.

Procedures

After recruitment and initial assessments, participants with MCI and mild AD were randomly allocated to the study subgroups. The CCT group received a computerized multidomain cognitive training program (www.66nao.com), with participants required to complete at least 30 min of training per day (3 cycle of 5 2-min tasks), 5 days a week for 24 weeks. Task difficulty automatically increase once accuracy exceeded 80%. Adherence was supervised monitored based on training days and hours per day. TAU comprised routine clinical care (regular pharmacotherapy as prescribed) and online basic health education sessions every two weeks (medication adherence, diet, physical activity, sleep hygiene) (online education web: https://www.xyxun.top/course/explore/LNBK).

Neuropsychological assessments were conducted at baseline, 4, 12, and 24 weeks. fNIRS data, plasma and fecal samples were collected at baseline and at the 24-week follow-up.

Primary outcome and neuropsychological measurements

A comprehensive neuropsychological battery was employed to assess various cognitive domains, with ADAS-Cog serving as the primary measure. The Mini-mental State Examination (MMSE) was used for baseline cognitive screening. Learning and memory were assessed by the Free and Cued Selective Reminding Test (FCSRT) and the Auditory Verbal Learning Test (AVLT). Attention, executive and visuospatial function were assessed using the Trail Making Test (TMT), Digital Span Test (DST), and Clock Drawing Test (CDT). Verbal ability was evaluated with the Boston Naming Test (BNT) and Verbal Frequency Test (VFT). Depressive symptoms were rated using the 30-Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-30). Neuropsychiatric symptoms were assessed using the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI). and activities of daily living were measured using the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living scale (IADL). Cognitive impairment severity was evaluated using the Clinical Dementia Rating scale (CDR).

Plasma samples and ELISA detection for TMAO

Five milliliters of venous blood were collected from participants into anticoagulant vacuum tube. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C within 60 min. The supernatant was collected, transferred to a clean EP tube, and immediately stored at − 80 °C.

Plasma TMAO levels was detected using Human TMAO ELISA Kit (Cat. No.: CKE-13641, Shanghai Yan Hui Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) following the standardized procedures.

Microbiome data processing

Fecal sample collection

Fecal samples were collected from participants in standard 300 mL sterilin tubes and frozen immediately at -80 °C. Participants were instructed to provide the first-morning sample for consistency and to avoid alcohol consumption within the prior 24 h.

DNA extraction 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 mg of fecal samples, and the V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA was amplified using primers Fwd. 5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3’ and Rev. 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’. A sequence library of the V3–V4 region of the 16sRNA was prepared. The purified PCR products were mixed in equal proportions and sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq system (Illumina Inc., USA).

The procedure for PCR amplification, sequencing of PCR amplicons and quality control of raw data was performed according to a previous study [23]. Briefly, PCR reactions were performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min, 25 cycles of 95 ℃ for 30 s, 55 ℃ for another 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 30 s, followed by a final extension at 72 ℃ for 5 min. Amplicons from each sample were indexed, pooled in equimolar amounts, and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq instrument using 2 × 300 bp paired-end chemistry. Negative controls, namely extraction blanks and PCR no-template controls, were processed alongside and showed no amplification.

Bioinformatic analysis of 16 S rRNA sequencing data

The high-throughput 16 S rRNA sequencing data were analyzed following a reported workflow [24]. First, the quality check, filtering and cropping of fastq files were performed by the dada2 package in nf-core. Trimmed the sequence to remove ambiguous bases (max N = 0), amplicons with an average mass score less than 30, greater than 260 base pairs, and amplicons less than 200 base pairs. Secondly, after filtering the data, the silva_nr_v132_train_set based on the naive Bayes classifier was used as the training set to infer high-resolution amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) [25]. Then, the two ends of the sequence were merged, the chimera was removed, and the sequence table was composed. Samples yielding fewer than 20,000 reads after filtering were omitted, and the remaining samples were rarefied to 20,000 reads per sample to normalize sequencing depth for diversity analyses. Species abundance and diversity (α-diversity), sample clustering (β-diversity), and bacterial composition at the genus level were analyzed by Qiime v1.8.0.

Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (fNIRS)

A total of 48 channels covering the bilateral prefrontal cortex and temporal cortices (Figure S1) was used for the 8-min resting state fNIRS acquisition. Data was measured and analysed using NirScan and NirSpark v1.7.3 (Danyang Huichuang Medical Equipment Co. Ltd, China). We centered the middle probe at Fpz in the international 10–20 system. The array comprised 31 optodes (15 sources, 16 detectors) spaced 3 cm apart (source–detector distance) to cover the bilateral prefrontal and temporal cortices, with the lowest row aligned to the Fp1–Fp2 line. The localization methodology is consistent with our previous work [22]. Regions of interests (ROIs) were selected as the Brodmann Area (BA) 9 and 46 (overlapping the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, DLPFC), left BA 6 (overlapping the pre-motor and supplementary motor cortex, PM-SMC), and right BA 45 (overlapping the pars triangularis Broca’s area). Functional connectivity (FC) was analyzed by Spearman’s correlation between the time series of each ROI-to-ROI pair [22]. Furthermore, we additionally mapped 48 channels to BA-defined ROIs, applied standard preprocessing, and fitted subject-level multivariate autoregressive models. Directed effects were tested with Granger-causality F-tests across all ordered ROI pairs, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR (q = 0.05) controlled multiplicity, and model order, residual whiteness, and stationarity were formally checked to yield a binarized significant adjacency matrix per subject. At the group level, reproducibility was quantified as adjacency rate and networks were visualized at a threshold of ≥ 0.95, longitudinal change was summarized as Δrate = follow-up - baseline, and we computed node metrics, namely out-degree, in-degree, net outflow, reciprocity, together with an a priori frontal-temporal pathway aggregation to support hypothesis testing. Detailed methodology presented in Supplementary Material 4.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS ver. 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA) and Python (3.8.10). Figures and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9, www.figdraw.com, www.omicstudio.cn, and Photoshop. Linear mixed-effect models were used to examine the effects of CCT on neuropsychological scores, gut microbiota diversity, and abundance. Time was included as the repeated variable. Group, time, and group-by-time interaction were incorporated as fixed effects. Changes in the neuropsychological scores, gut microbiota diversity and composition, and fNIRS data from baseline to follow-up timepoints were analyzed. fNIRS data was analyzed using pairwise comparisons, with statistical results corrected for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR). A statistically significant difference (two-tailed, P < 0.05) for the primary outcome (ADAS-cog) at the end of the intervention would be considered as preliminary evidence of efficacy. Pearson’s correlation coefficient were calculated between changes in brain functions, gut microbiota diversity, composition of, and neuropsychological scores.

Results

Participants’ characteristics and intervention adherence

Participants were enrolled between September 30, 2021, to February 14, 2022, with the final follow-up completed on August 19, 2022. The participant flow is shown in Fig. 1. A total of 231 individuals were assessed for eligibility, of which 84 were enrolled in the study and randomly assigned to either the CCT or control groups. Ultimately, 74 participants completed the trial. Of the 10 participants who withdrew (11.9%), 3 cited health issues and 7 withdrew due to time constraints and personal reasons. As detailed in Table 1, there were no significant differences between groups in age, sex, or years of education. Neuropsychological scores, except for VFT, were matched between the two groups. All participants in the CCT group completed over 95% of the training sessions (Table 1). Additionally, only one participant in the T-MCI subgroup reported mild anxiety at week 12 of the follow-up.

Neuropsychological outcomes

Significant improvements the primary outcome measure, ADAS-cog, were observed in the CCT group compared to the control group from baseline to each follow-up timepoint (Fig. 2, Table S1 to S3). Notably, improvements in ADAS-cog scores were evident as early as 4 weeks, with a mean difference (MD) of 2.300 (95% CI: 0.717 to 3.884, P = 0.004) and an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.674 (95% CI: 0.203 to 1.140), and continued to increase throughout the 24-week intervention.

Additional continuous improvements were observed in FCSRT-word (IFR and ISC), AVLT (Delayed Recall and Cued Recall), DST-Forward, BNT, VFT, NPI, IADL, and CDR, with effect sizes rising from moderate (mean Cohen’s d: 0.671) at week 4 to large (mean Cohen’s d: 1.281) at week 24. These results indicated cognitive enhancements in learning, memory, attention, language, and neuropsychiatric symptoms and daily functioning, and severity of cognitive impairment. Executive function improvements were observed at week 24, with moderate to large effect sizes for group and time interactions in DST-Backward (Cohen’s d: -0.844 [-1.317, -0.365]) and TMT-B (Cohen’s d: -0.786 [-1.257, -0.310]).

Subgroup analysis revealed that a shorter duration of CCT intervention required to improve global cognition in mild AD compared to MCI. Linguistic function improvements were also more prominent in the AD subgroups (Figures S2 to S3, Tables S4 to S6).

Brain network outcomes

A total of 35 participants in the CCT group (18 T-MCI and 17 T-AD) and 33 patients in the control group (16 C-MCI and 17 C-AD) completed fNIRS data collection at baseline and week 24. At baseline, the mean FC among ROIs was lower in MCI compared to AD patients (MCI vs. AD: 0.284 [0.271, 0.297] vs. 0.311 [0.298, 0.324], t = 2.860, P = 0.005) (Fig. 3A). After intervention, significant changes in FC were observed in the CCT group, including a decrease in FC between the BA45.R and BA46.R (mean SD, baseline vs. 24 weeks: 0.327 ± 0.203 vs. 0.180 ± 0.287, t = 3.523, P = 0.006, FDR corrected) and increases between BA9.R and BA46.L (mean SD, baseline vs. 24 weeks: 0.339 ± 0.203 vs. 0.482 ± 0.1197, t=-3.7747, P = 0.005, FDR corrected), as well as between BA9.L and BA6.L (mean ± SD, baseline vs. 24 weeks: 0.134 ± 0.209 vs. 0.283 ± 0.208, t=-3.775, P = 0.005, FDR corrected) (Fig. 3B-F). In contrast, no significant changes in FC were observed in the control group (Figure S4A). Furthermore, an analysis of resting-state fNIRS data from 45 cognitively normal older adults (NC) indicated that the FC between the BA45.R and BA46.R, BA9.R and BA46.L, and BA9.L and BA6.L in the CCT group at 24-week was closer to that in the NC group, suggesting that CCT may restore the impaired brain network back to a state similar to normal cognition (Fig. 3G).

Subgroup analysis showed that the changes in FC observed in the T-MCI subgroup were consistent with the overall CCT group findings (Figure S4B). Whereas the T-AD subgroup only showed a decrease between BA45.R and BA46.R and an increase between BA9.R and BA46.L (Figure S4C). No significant changes in FC were observed in the C-MCI and C-AD subgroups.

Using a stringent stability criterion (adjacency rate ≥ 0.95), the control cohort displayed a dense pattern of fronto-frontal and fronto-temporal effective connectivity at baseline that remained largely unchanged after 24 weeks (Fig. 4A and B), and the corresponding Δrate map showed only small, spatially limited reweighting (Fig. 4C), and node-level averages, namely out-degree, in-degree, net outflow, and reciprocity, were essentially stable (Fig. 4G). In contrast, the CCT cohort exhibited a sparser baseline network that, after 24 weeks of training, underwent selective reconfiguration rather than global densification (Fig. 4D and E). The Δrate map highlighted increases within frontal circuitry, including strengthened influences linking inferior and dorsolateral prefrontal regions, and reinforced projections from frontal cortex toward temporal association areas, alongside a few decreases involving premotor targets (Fig. 4F). Node-level means changed little, but along the pre-specified frontal-temporal pathway the balance shifted toward greater frontal outflow, with out-degree rising and in-degree falling so that net outflow from frontal to temporal regions became positive at follow-up. Taken together, these findings indicate that CCT remapped directed information flow, preferentially stabilizing specific frontal-frontal and frontal-temporal connections, while the control network remained test-retest stable.

TMAO outcomes

All 74 participants provided venous blood samples at baseline and the 24-week follow-up. At baseline, plasma TMAO levels were significantly higher in AD patients (mean: 17.732 µmol/L) compared to MCI patients (mean: 15.042 µmol/L) (t = 2.799, P = 0.007) (Fig. 5A). A linear mixed-effects model, controlling for demographic and lifestyle factors (age, sex, education, meat consumption, probiotic foods, and medications), revealed a significant interaction effect between group and time on TMAO levels. Following the CCT intervention, TMAO levels in the CCT group were significantly reduced compared to the control group (P = 0.037) (Fig. 5B), with a mean difference of 2.520 µmol/L (95% CI: 0.444–4.595). The effect size was moderate (Cohen’s d = 0.563, 95% CI: 0.096–1.026), indicating a meaningful reduction in plasma TMAO levels as a result of the CCT intervention.

No significant interaction effect between group and time was observed for TMAO levels in the MCI subgroup (P = 0.277) (Figure S5A). However, a significant group-by-time interaction effect was found in the AD subgroup (P = 0.014) (Figure S5B), indicating that the CCT intervention was particularly effective in reducing TMAO levels among AD patients.

Gut microbial outcomes

A total of 68 participants provided fecal samples at both baseline and the final follow-up. After rarefaction and clustering, 130 fecal samples from 36 participants in the CCT group (20 T-MCI and 16 T-AD) and 29 in the control group (14 C-MCI and 15 C-AD) were included in the analysis. Across these 130 included samples, the median raw reads were 123,624 per sample (IQR: 117,138 − 127,878), and after DADA2 denoising and chimera removal, the average usable reads per sample was 47,598 (95% CI: 45,203 − 49,993). A total of 5,151 ASVs were detected in the dataset. Rarefaction analysis indicated a plateau in observed ASVs beyond 20,000 reads, which was chosen as the rarefaction threshold (Figure S6A-B). Negative controls showed no amplification and were excluded from analysis (Figure S6C).

The effects of CCT on microbial alpha-diversity, including Chao1 richness, Simpson evenness, and Shannon diversity, were assessed (Table S3). Compared to the control group, the CCT group showed a significant smaller decrease in Chao1(MD: -58.528 [-112.460, -4.595], P = 0.033, Cohen’s d: -0.541 [-1.037, -0.041]) and significant increases in Shannon diversity (MD: -0.555 [-0.939, -0.171], P = 0.003, and Cohen’s d: -0.721 [-1.233, -0.213]) and Simpson evenness (MD: -0.025 [-0.048, -0.003], P = 0.016, and Cohen’s d: -0.562 [-1.059, -0.061]). Analysis of beta diversity using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed no significant differences in the MCI and AD subgroups from baseline to 24 weeks of follow-up (Figure S7).

At the genus level, a significant group-by-time interaction was observed for the relative abundance of Ruminococcus_torques_group (R.torques), which remained stable in the CCT group throughout the intervention period, while it increased in the control group (Fig. 5C). Roseburia and Eubacterium_ruminantioum_group showed slight increases in the CCT group, while decreasing in the control group (Fig. 5D-E). Subgroup analysis revealed significant group-by-time effects in 12 genera within MCI subgroups. Specifically, Roseburia, Lachnospira, Butyricicoccus, and other taxa showed increased relative abundance in the T-MCI subgroup and decreased relative abundance in the C-MCI subgroup (Figure S8). In the AD subgroups, only Roseburia exhibited increased relative abundance in the T-AD subgroup, and decreased relative abundance in the C-AD subgroup after 24 weeks.

MGB axis correlation outcomes

Pearson’s correlation analyses were performed to investigate relationships between changes in neuropsychological outcomes, FC between ROIs, TMAO levels, and gut microbiota characteristics following the CCT intervention. Changes in alpha diversity indexes (Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson) showed the strongest correlations with multiple outcomes (Figs. 5F-G). Specifically, changes in ADAS-cog scores were positively correlated with the genera R.torques (r = 0.407, P = 0.012), Tyzzerella (r = 0.412, P = 0.011), and Subdoligranulum (r = 0.489, P = 0.002), and negatively correlated with Anaerotruncus (r=-0.488, P = 0.002). No significant correlations were found between changes in ADAS-cog scores and other neuropsychological outcomes or FC between ROIs.

The decrease in FC between BA45.R and BA46.R was associated with changes in neuropsychological scores (AVLT-immediate, TMT-A, CDT, BNT, VFT) and with the relative abundance of specific genera, including R.torques, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, and others. Changes in FC between BA9.R and BA46.L, as well as BA9.L and BA6.L, were correlated solely with changes in gut bacterial genera.

Plasma TMAO level reduction was positively correlated with changes in ADAS-cog, NPI scores, and with the decrease in FC between BA46.R and BA45.R (ADAS-cog: r = 0.549, P < 0.001; NPI: r = 0.419, P = 0.009; FC between BA46.R and BA45.R: r = 0.355, P = 0.036) (Fig. 5H-J). At the genus level, six bacterial genera were significantly correlated with changes in TMAO levels (Fig. 5G). Among these, Roseburia (r = -0.443, P = 0.006), Lachnospiraceae_UCG-004 (r = -0.392, P = 0.016), and Lactobacillus (r = -0.480, P = 0.003) exhibited negative correlations, while R.torques (r = 0.443, P = 0.007), Fusobacterium (r = 0.484, P = 0.002), and Herbasporillum (r = 0.365, P = 0.026) exhibited positive correlations.

Among all 94 bacterial genera analyzed, R.torques was uniquely and significantly correlated with changes in ADAS-cog scores, NPI (r = 0.395, P = 0.016), Shannon diversity (r = 0.416, P = 0.012), Simpson evenness (r = 0.425, P = 0.010), and FC between BA45.R and BA46.R (r = 0.449, P = 0.008) (Figs. 5L-N), as well as changes of TMAO levels (Fig. 5K).

Discussion

This study represents a pioneering investigation into the effects of a 24-week adaptive, multi-domain CCT program on both cognitive and non-cognitive functions in individuals with MCI and mild AD. Our findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying cognitive enhancement through CCT, particularly emphasizing the roles of neural plasticity and dynamic alterations in gut microbiota, specifically through the modulation of the R.torques–TMAO axis within the MGB pathway. These insights not only advance our understanding of CCT’s potential but also highlight new avenues for clinical interventions targeting AD.

The MGB axis has gained attention in recent years for its role in neurological health, with studies often focusing on hormones (e.g., corticotropin-releasing hormone in the HPA axis), neurotransmitters (e.g., serotonin, dopamine, and GABA), and microbial metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acid (SCFAs) [26]. Emerging research has also highlighted the importance of TMAO as a key player in AD. Elevated TMAO levels are associated with AD biomarkers and have been implicated in the stabilization and aggregation of Aβ and tau proteins, which may contribute to AD pathology [8, 27]. However, the exact mechanisms by which TMAO influences cognition remained unclear. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating that reductions in plasma TMAO levels following CCT intervention are significantly correlated with improvements in ADAS-cog and NPI scores, as well as reduced FC between specific brain regions (e.g., right DLPFC and right Broca’s area). These findings suggest that CCT may induce cognitive benefits and alleviate neuropsychiatric symptoms by modulating TMAO levels. We hypothesized that CCT might stimulate brain regions with initially reduced neural activity, thereby reducing the need for compensatory hyperactivity in interconnected regions, improving cognitive efficiency, and mitigating neuropsychiatric symptoms. Additionally, our results suggest that changes in TMAO levels may be partially mediated by alterations in gut microbiota, particularly the downregulation of R. torques, suggesting a potential pathway through which CCT influences MGB interactions.

The role of R.torques-TMAO pathway in the MGB axis emerged as a notable finding in our study. R.torques, a mucin-degrading, pro-inflammatory taxon on the intestinal mucosa [28, 29], was the only genus significantly correlated with changes in cognition, NPS, alpha diversity, brain network connectivity, and TMAO. Although R.torques is not a dominant producer of trimethylamine (TMA), the microbial precursor of TMAO, its expansion tends to co-occur with microbial or dietary milieus that favor TMA generation and thus higher systemic TMAO [30], and it has been observed at increased relative abundance in cognitive impairment [31]. In our trial, CCT lowered plasma TMAO, and the magnitude of TMAO reduction correlated with ADAS-cog improvement, while R.torques varied positively with TMAO and negatively with beneficial genera, consistent with a dysbiosis-TMAO pathway. Preclinical evidence supports pathogenicity of elevated TMAO by crossing and impairing the blood-brain barrier, downregulating tight-junction and vascular proteins (e.g., ZO-1, Occludin, PDGFR-β), amplifying neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, and disrupting synaptic and metabolic homeostasis, whereas TMAO inhibition improves cognition [32, 33]. Together with reports of fluctuating Ruminococcus in AD [6, 34] and its links to inflammatory disease [35], these findings position the R.torques-TMAO axis as a plausible mediator of gut-brain effects. We propose that CCT exerts top-down benefits partly by dampening this axis through reducing R.torques-associated dysbiosis and TMAO, thereby alleviating neuroinflammatory and vascular mechanisms that undermine cognition, with the observed post-CCT decline in R.torques also consistent with reduced systemic inflammation and slower cognitive decline [11, 36]. Our findings propose that R.torques-TMAO pathway could serve as a novel biomarker within the MGB axis for future AD studies, with its downregulation representing a potential therapeutic effect.

Regarding neuroplasticity, CCT appeared to normalize FC patterns between specific brain regions. Post-intervention FC architecture resembled the connectivity observed in healthy cognitive functioning, indicating a possible restoration or enhancement of neural pathways. Increased FC between certain ROIs might reflect the reactivation of previously underutilized areas, while decreased FC could signify reduced compensatory engagement of atypical brain regions, suggesting a shift towards more efficient neural processing [37]. This balancing effect highlights the role of extended CCT in inducing durable neuroplastic changes in AD, underscoring the need for sustained, adaptive CCT to achieve broad cognitive benefits.

Our findings also help clarify the debate surrounding the optimal duration and intensity of CCT. The adaptive, multi-domain approach used in this study, which included personalized difficulty adjustments, resulted in significant improvements across various cognitive domains, supporting the effectiveness of intensive, long-term CCT [38, 39]. In particular, executive functions required longer intervention durations to show significant improvement, consistent with findings that durations under 12 weeks are typically insufficient for enhancing executive function [40, 41]. Our results suggest that CCT protocols tailored to cognitive impairment severity could optimize cognitive outcomes, with at least 12 weeks for initial improvements and 24 weeks for full-domain enhancement. Therefore, to maximize the effects of CCT for individuals with MCI and mild AD, we advocate for a regimen exceeding 30 min per day, five days a week. Using a multi-domain and difficulty-adaptive approach ensures comprehensive cognitive engagement across various domains.

The role of the MGB axis in AD is a promising but underexplored avenue for cognitive training research. Cognitive training could influence cognition by directly promoting neuroplasticity and remodulating compensatory neural networks, while simultaneously impacting the gut microbiome and altering neuroactive molecule production, including serotonin, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA), neuropeptides, and SCFAs, which communicate with the brain via diverse pathways [2]. Our findings provide robust support for this dual mechanism. The 24-week CCT intervention was associated with distinct changes in FC, gut microbiota composition, and cognitive outcomes. For example, reduced FC between Broca’s area and the right DLPFC correlated with improvements in multiple cognitive domains, while increased FC in other brain areas was associated with changes in microbiota composition, independent of cognitive improvements. These findings suggest that cognitive training impacts both cognitive and non-cognitive functions via separate pathways, mediated by neuroplasticity and gut microbiota interactions. The gut microbiota may influence brain health through neural, immune, endocrine, and metabolic signaling, providing a foundation for new AD treatment strategies.

Our FC analysis relied on pairwise Spearman correlation among ROI-averaged fNIRS signals to infer connectivity changes induced by CCT. While this method is robust and widely used in resting-state neuroimaging, including fNIRS, it is limited in capturing dynamic, nonlinear, and temporally multiscale nature of brain activity. Recent advances in brain network modeling have highlighted the value of temporal–frequency domain analyses and attention-based mechanisms. For example, deep learning frameworks combining wavelet transforms and attention modules have been shown to detect transient, nonlinear coupling patterns in fMRI or EEG data [42, 43]. Similarly, graph neural networks and self-attention-based factorization models can uncover meaningful latent structure in high-dimensional signals that static FC measures overlook [44, 45]. Although these models improve detection sensitivity and classification accuracy, they also present challenges such as high sample requirements, interpretability concerns, and computational complexity. Nevertheless, their utility in modeling nonlinear dynamics in psychiatric and neurodegenerative disorders is increasingly recognized.

Future CCT studies should incorporate such approaches to elucidate training-induced reorganization of oscillatory brain networks. Specifically, frequency-resolved graph representations and interpretable attention-based pipelines may uncover latent mechanisms that cannot be captured by traditional FC. This aligns with the growing literature advocating multi-scale, dynamic, and explainable AI-based connectivity modeling for fNIRS, EEG, and fMRI studies [46].

The study has several limitations. This single-center randomized trial, although supported by robust statistics and high adherence, limits generalizability, and the absence of post-intervention follow-up prevents firm conclusions about durability of benefit. Because all CCT participants received adaptive training while the control arm received treatment-as-usual without a time-matched, computer-based interactive activity, the between-group differences may partly reflect non-specific factors (novelty, engagement, contact time, expectancy, and social stimulation) rather than the specific contribution of algorithmic adaptivity or training content. These influences can inflate apparent efficacy relative to treatment-as-usual and can obscure which ingredient, namely adaptivity, generic stimulation, or social interaction, drives the observed changes in cognition, network physiology, and the microbiota-metabolite profile. Accordingly, the present findings are best interpreted as the effect of the overall CCT exposure rather than the isolated impact of its adaptive algorithm. Furthermore, our fNIRS montage focused on prefrontal and anterior temporal regions. This limited montage may weaken the generalizability of our conclusions regarding whole-brain network dynamics [42–44, 47]. Lastly, the study also lacked animal-based behavioral phenotyping for causal validation, and short-read sequencing of a single 16 S region limited species/strain-level resolution and functional inference.

Therefore, for future studies, in order to isolate CCT-specific mechanisms and enhance external validity, they should implement a multi-center, time- and attention-matched three-arm randomized trial that compares adaptive CCT, non-adaptive CCT with identical content and contact, and a social-interaction or education control matched for frequency, duration, interface engagement, and staff contact. In parallel, imaging should include multiple network models via appropriate montages. Causal inference should be strengthened with behavior-anchored fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) studies in AD mouse models, and microbiome resolution should be increased using full-length (V1-V9) 16 S or shotgun metagenomics for species and strain-level and functional profiling.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a 24-week adaptive, multi-domain CCT program enhances cognitive and non-cognitive functions in MCI and mild AD patients by modulating the MGB axis, with key contributions from brain network reorganization and gut microbiota alterations. Specifically, the downregulation of the R. torques–TMAO axis emerges as a critical mechanism linking gut microbiota to cognitive function. These findings present novel targets within the MGB axis for managing AD and related cognitive disorders.
