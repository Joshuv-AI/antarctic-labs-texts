Nurses’ knowledge and self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines during in-hospital cardiac arrest in a referral hospital in Ghana
Authors: Gyimaa Nti –Darkwah, Susana Erica Somuah, Amos Prince Otuah, Mary Agongo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAdherence to Advanced Life Support guidelines is vital for improving patient survival during a cardiac arrest. Nurses are often the first responders during inhospital cardiac arrests. This study assessed nurses’ knowledge of advanced life support guidelines and their self-reported adherence to recommended practices during in-hospital cardiac arrest.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional design was employed. Using simple random sampling, 138 registered nurses were recruited from the New Tafo Government Hospital in Ghana. A structured questionnaire was used to collect relevant data on the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge on and self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines. Descriptive and inferential statistics were used for analysis.ResultsLess than half (39.9%) of nurses demonstrated good knowledge of advanced life support guidelines, with a similar proportion (34.8%) showing high self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines in simulated scenarios, and confidence level (39.8%) in responding to a cardiac arrest. Most respondents (65.2%) had never performed CPR in a clinical setting, and only 22.5% had participated in a simulation exercise. Recent advanced life support training (within the past 12 months) and greater years of clinical experience were significantly associated with higher knowledge levels.DiscussionNurses’ knowledge of and self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines, as well as confidence level in responding to a cardiac arrest situation were low. Regular, structured advanced life support refresher training incorporating simulation and practical drills is essential to enhance nurses’ readiness and performance during cardiac emergencies

Introduction

Cardiac arrest is a serious medical emergency, occurring both within and outside hospital settings, and contributing significantly to global cardiovascular-related morbidity and mortality [1]. Globally, an estimated two million individuals experience sudden cardiac arrest annually [2], yet survival rates remain alarmingly low [3]. In Africa, and Ghana in particular, effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), which is a cornerstone of Advanced Life Support (ALS), is challenged by gaps in knowledge, limited training, and systemic inefficiencies [4]. A study in Ghana has reported that, 45 % of healthcare professionals possess inadequate CPR knowledge, raising serious concerns about the readiness of front-line staff to effectively respond to cardiac emergencies [5]. Interventions such as high-quality CPR, timely defibrillation, and adherence to advanced life support guidelines are essential to improving patient survival during in-hospital cardiac arrest. However, there is often a gap between guideline recommendations and the actual quality of CPR delivered in clinical practice [6,7]. Successful ALS requires that nurses not only understand the cognitive and technical aspects of resuscitation but also demonstrate effective teamwork and coordination [8,9]. Delays in medication administration and interruptions in chest compressions are indicators of poor adherence to ALS protocols, which in turn contribute to suboptimal patient outcomes [3]. At the New Tafo Government Hospital in Ghana, the 2021 annual report showed total hospital death rose from 28 in 2020 to 54 in 2021, and cardiac arrest ranked as number one cause, accounting for 7.4 % of all mortality. Given the national rise in the incidence of cardiovascular disease, the burden of cardiac arrest within hospitals in Ghana is expected to increase [10]. Despite the critical role nurses play during patient resuscitation, to the best of our knowledge, there is a paucity of quantitative local evidence on their knowledge of and adherence to advanced life support guidelines in clinical settings. However, a recent qualitative study in Ghana highlighted nurses’ perceived skills gaps and challenges with CPR delivery, underscoring the need for more rigorous, quantitative investigations [11]. Although the American Heart Association (AHA) updated its Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) guidelines in 2020, it is important to clarify that this study does not assess formal ACLS certification training but rather evaluates nurses’ knowledge and adherence to resuscitation protocols informed by these guidelines.

This study therefore seeks to assess nurses’ knowledge of and adherence to advanced life support guidelines while responding to in-hospital cardiac arrests at a municipal referral hospital in Ghana. The findings aim to inform clinical training, policy, and quality improvement strategies to enhance the effectiveness of advanced life support interventions and ultimately improve patient survival outcomes.

Methods

Setting

This study was conducted at the New Tafo Government Hospital, which is an 83-bed capacity municipal referral hospital, located in the Eastern Region, Ghana. The hospital serves as the primary referral centre for approximately 91,297 residents. At the time of the study, New Tafo Government Hospital did not have a formally documented advanced life support protocol, although resuscitation practices were generally informed by the American Heart Association ACLS guidelines.

Study design

A descriptive, cross-sectional study design was employed to assess nurses’ knowledge of and adherence to advanced life support guidelines (informed by the 2020 AHA ACLS recommendations, but not formal ACLS certification training) while responding to in-hospital cardiac arrest. The study population consisted of all registered professionals working as nurses and midwives at the New Tafo Government Hospital. As of March 2023, there were 217 nurses and midwives employed at the facility. Included in the study were all registered nurses and midwives with at least one year of clinical experience. Nurses on study leave, internship, or undergoing post-qualification orientation were excluded.

A simple random sampling technique was used. A complete list of eligible nurses and midwives was obtained from the hospital’s human resource department to form the sampling frame. Each individual was assigned a unique code, written on identical slips of paper, mixed thoroughly in the container and drawn randomly without replacement until the sample size was reached.

Data collection and analysis

Data was collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire (N material, Appendix 1) was derived from literature review [12,13] and adapted from the 2020 American Heart Association (AHA) ACLS guidelines [3] to reflect advanced life support protocols. Importantly, the tool did not assess proprietary ACLS certification, but rather guideline-informed knowledge and practices relevant to in-hospital cardiac arrest. The questionnaire was comprised of three sections. Section A asked about sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, years of experience; Section B measured nurses’ knowledge on advanced life support guidelines and had 15 multiple-choice questions (MCQs), adapted from the AHA recommendations. A correct response to each item was scored 1 while an incorrect response was scored 0, with a total possible score of 15. Section C assessed nurses’ self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines using 4 clinical scenario-based multiple-choice questions, alongside 1 confidence-rating item. The 4 scenario questions were designed to evaluate how nurses would respond in key advanced life support situations. The fifth item in this section assessed the participant’s confidence in managing a cardiac arrest, rated on a 10-point scale (1 = not confident at all; 10 = very confident).

Face and content validity were assessed by two emergency physicians and three senior nurse educators, leading to revisions for clarity and contextual relevance. A pilot study with 15 nurses at Hawa Memorial Hospital informed further item refinement. Reliability analysis showed acceptable internal consistency: knowledge (α = 0.76) and self-reported adherence (α = 0.81).

Data collection took place over a two-week period in April 2023. Two trained research assistants, who were degree-qualified nurses on post-qualification rotation (not participants), assisted with the process. Participants were approached individually during shift breaks. The purpose, procedures, and confidentiality measures of the study were explained. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. No personal identifiers were collected, and participants were informed they could withdraw at any time without consequences. The questionnaires were self-administered, and participants completed them in a private area to reduce response bias.

Data was entered and analysed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive statistics was used to summarize socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge and self-reported adherence. Based on scores, knowledge was classified as good (≥75 %; ≥11/15), moderate (50–74 %; 8–10/15), or poor (<50 %; ≤7/15).

Self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines was scored as 1 point for correct and 0 for incorrect responses. Adherence levels were categorized as high (4/4), moderate (2–3/4), and low (0–1/4). A separate 10-point rating scale assessed confidence in managing cardiac arrest. Associations between knowledge and background characteristics were tested using logistic regression with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Data was securely stored, with access limited to the research team.

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ghana Health Service Ethics Review Committee (GHS-ERC 040/03/23). In addition, approval was secured from the Ghana College of Nurses and Midwives Research Committee and the management of the New Tafo Government Hospital. All participants provided written informed consent after being informed about the study’s objectives, their rights, potential risks, and benefits. Participation was voluntary, and participants were assured of anonymity and confidentiality.

Results

A total of 141 questionnaires were distributed, and 138 were completed and returned, resulting in a response rate of 97.9 %.

Socio-demographic characteristics

The majority of respondents were aged 30–39 years (63 %, n = 87), female (73.9 %, n = 102), and registered as general nurses (97.8 %, n = 135). More than half (51.4 %, n = 71) held a diploma in nursing/midwifery, and most (60.8 %, n = 84) had between 1 and 5 years of clinical experience. Knowledge of advanced life support guidelines

With respect to advanced life support guidelines, 89.9 % answered that adrenalin is recommended for shockable cardiac rhythms and 78.3 % were correct in saying that Glasgow coma scale is required by the team transitioning to advanced airway management during an arrest and subsequent patient intubation. Also, 95 out of 138 (68.8 %) of the nurses knew how to correctly identify an adult patient who needs life support. Significant 81.2 % of the nurses gave a wrong response relating to how often the team practicing the CPR would switch positions or check to see if a patient had a pulse. Out of the 138 participants studied, 27 (19.7 %) knew that an ideal body temperature of 32 °C to 36 °C had to be maintained in the post cardiac arrest care phase of management.

Summative knowledge scores on advanced life support guidelines

The mean score of correct answers among the 138 nurses was approximately 7.94 out of 15, indicating a moderate level of knowledge on advanced life support guidelines. While some questions showed high correctness rates (e.g., use of adrenaline, Glasgow Coma Scale), others such as oropharyngeal airway sizing and CPR rotation frequency were incorrectly answered by majority of respondents. This suggests uneven knowledge across advanced life support domains, highlighting the need for targeted refresher training in specific guideline areas. The mean knowledge score was 7.94 (SD ±5.57), with scores ranging from 2 to 12. Based on this scoring, 39.9 % of respondents had good knowledge of advanced life support guidelines. (Supplementary material, Appendix 2)

Self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines

From Table 4, the majority of participants (n = 132, 95.7 %) correctly indicated that CPR should be continued until spontaneous circulation is regained or death is confirmed. In response to a patient who is unresponsive, pulseless, and not breathing, 68.8 % (n = 95) correctly selected “start CPR,” while 21.0 % (n = 29) indicated they would call a doctor, a delay not aligned with advanced life support recommendations. Regarding the correct management of a patient who is not breathing but still has a pulse, only 40.6 % (n = 56) selected “give rescue breath,” while 44.9 % (n = 62) incorrectly indicated they would begin chest compressions. Furthermore, half of the respondents (n = 69, 50 %) reported that initiating CPR was the standard immediate action in their ward during a cardiac arrest. Based on a composite adherence score derived from these four items, 34.8 % (n = 48) of respondents demonstrated high adherence (4/4 correct responses), 42.0 % (n = 58) showed moderate adherence (2–3 correct responses), and 23.2 % (n = 32) exhibited low adherence (0–1 correct response). Self-confidence in responding to cardiac arrest

Majority of participants (48.6 %, n = 67) rated their confidence in responding to cardiac arrest as 5 on a scale of 1–10, followed by ratings between 6 and 8 (34.0 %, n = 47). Only 5.8 % (n = 8) rated themselves at the highest confidence levels [[9], [10]], while 11.6 % (n = 16) reported very low confidence [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. (Supplementary material, Appendix 4)

Factors associated with knowledge of advanced life support guidelines

Bivariate analysis using logistic regression was conducted to assess the association between background characteristics and nurses’ knowledge of advanced life support guidelines. For the purposes of regression analysis, the knowledge outcome variable, originally classified into good, moderate, and poor was collapsed into a binary outcome: good knowledge (scores 9–15) and poor knowledge (scores 0–8). This transformation was performed to enhance statistical power and meet the assumptions of binary logistic regression.

The multivariable logistic regression results revealed that nurses who had received CPR or advanced life support training within the past 6 to 12 months were significantly more likely to demonstrate good knowledge compared to those trained more than a year ago (AOR = 2.76; 95 % CI: 1.40–5.42; p = 0.023). Additionally, years of experience showed a significant positive association with knowledge, as nurses with 6–10 years of experience had significantly higher odds of good knowledge compared to those with over 10 years of experience (AOR = 5.11; 95 % CI: 2.74–7.55; p = 0.011). Although nurses with 1–5 years of experience also had elevated odds of good knowledge (AOR = 5.01), this result was not statistically significant (p = 0.097).

While general nurses had somewhat higher odds of good knowledge compared to those in other specialties (AOR = 2.33; 95 % CI: 1.22–5.11; p = 0.452), and junior nurses compared to senior nurses (p = 0.999), these differences were not statistically significant.

These findings underscore the potential impact of recent training and mid-level work experience on nurses’ knowledge of advanced life support guidelines. Future analyses are encouraged to explore how these demographic and institutional factors influence self-reported confidence and adherence to advanced life support guidelines, which are critical to improving clinical outcomes and guiding policy decisions. Discussion

The study found that less than half (39.9 %) of the nurses demonstrated good knowledge of the advanced life support guidelines, suggesting that while some foundational understanding exists, overall mastery remains limited. This agrees with findings from a study in Ethiopia by Senbeta et al. [12]. which found 39.5 % of participants to have good knowledge of advanced life support guidelines. Findings from the present study, however, contrasts somehow with a study by Sajjad et al. [13]. which found over 51 % of nurses in Pakistan to have good knowledge on advanced life support guidelines.

Importantly, the present study identified critical knowledge gaps in key areas of advanced life support protocol. For instance, less than 20 % of the respondents understood the advanced life support guidelines recommendations as it relates to essential components of maintaining high quality compression (i.e. rotating compression roles/ checking pulse) and optimal temperature range required for post resuscitation care, highlighting insufficient awareness of post-resuscitation protocols. These results underscore the urgent need for comprehensive in-service training programs aimed at improving overall knowledge of advanced life support guidelines, encompassing both core resuscitation skills and post-arrest care principles.

The study also found that nurses who had undergone training based on advanced life support guidelines within the past 12 months were significantly more knowledgeable than those who had their last training more than 1 year prior to the study (p = 0.023). This supports findings by Jeong & Moon [14], who reported that recent training was associated with higher knowledge in advanced life support performance. It also reinforces evidence from international studies showing that CPR and advanced life support skills decline significantly within 6–12 months of training unless refreshed through simulation or hands-on practice [15].

In addition, years of experience showed a significant positive association with knowledge, as nurses with 6–10 years of experience had significantly higher odds of good knowledge compared to those with over 10 years of experience (AOR = 5.11; 95 % CI: 2.74–7.55; p = 0.011). This aligns with a study by Veettil et al. [16], which found that more experienced nurses retained and applied advanced life support guidelines knowledge more effectively.

In this study, while 34.8 % of participants reported high adherence level by selecting all four guideline-consistent responses, a considerable proportion (23.2 %) returned responses which turned out to be low adherence, correctly answering only one or none of the scenarios. These findings suggest that despite baseline familiarity with advanced life support principles, practical application during emergencies may be inconsistent.

The observed gaps in adherence may be linked to limited exposure to cardiac arrest cases. Notably, 65.2 % of respondents had never performed CPR in a clinical setting, which may reflect either a low incidence of cardiac arrests or limited opportunities for nurses to actively participate in emergency interventions due to role delegation, lack of confidence, or inadequate training. Furthermore, only 22.5 % had ever participated in a simulation exercise. This lack of hands-on practice and structured simulation training likely contributes to diminished procedural confidence and skill retention.

Participants’self-reported confidence in managing cardiac arrest incidence which was measured separately using a 10-point scale revealed that the majority (60.2 %) rated themselves within the low to moderate confidence range[[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]], while 39.8 % expressed good to high confidence[[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. This is concerning, as confidence has been positively associated with both competence and willingness to initiate resuscitation [17]. The current findings contrast the findings by Scipião et al.’s [18] study, which reported that healthcare professionals who recently attended advanced life support training exhibited significantly higher levels of confidence.

These results reinforce the critical importance of ongoing simulation-based training. Lin et al. [19] emphasize that simulation with real-time feedback not only improves technical performance but also enhances team dynamics and guideline adherence. Although cardiac arrests occur in resource-limited settings, recognition and active participation by nurses may be limited due to inadequate monitoring, staffing shortages, and gaps in training or resuscitation protocols [[20], [21], [22]]. In such contexts, simulation provides a practical approach to maintain readiness, assess response systems, and improve team dynamics during resuscitation.

The infrequent practice of CPR, and the vast majority never or only occasionally engaging in it, highlights a well-documented issue of skills decay. Studies [14,15] have shown that resuscitation knowledge and competence begin to decline within 6–12 months after training. This underscores the need for structured and routine refresher programs to sustain advanced life support proficiency among nurses.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that nurses’ knowledge of and self-reported adherence to advanced life support guidelines, as well as confidence in responding to cardiac arrest in emergency situations were low. To improve nurses’ readiness and response during in-hospital cardiac arrests, it is essential that the hospital implements regular, mandatory training programs that incorporate simulation-based learning and hands-on practical sessions. Such interventions will help improve knowledge and adherence to guideline-based resuscitation-protocols, reinforce clinical confidence, and ultimately enhance the quality of emergency cardiac care.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, it relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Second, the study was conducted in a single municipal hospital, which limits the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings in Ghana. Third, the cross-sectional design does not allow for establishing causal relationships between training exposure and knowledge or adherence outcomes. Fourth, although the questionnaire was adapted from AHA ACLS guidelines and relevant literature, it may not fully capture the breadth of advanced life support knowledge and practices. Finally, because no respondents had formal ACLS certification, the findings reflect knowledge and practice based on guideline-informed training rather than proprietary ACLS training.

Dissemination of results

The study and its findings were presented to management of the hospital. A clinical meeting presentation has been scheduled for the entire body of nurses and midwives in the hospital, to discuss the findings and strategies for improvement in the practice of ACLS.
