Distinction between primary and metastatic mucinous ovarian carcinoma from histopathology images using deep learning
Authors: Ming-Yi Zhang, Bin Liu, Zhao-Juan Qin, Yue-Di Zhang, Zhi-Qian Li, Rui-Zhi Liu, Zhi-Xiang Xia, Qiong-Xian Long, Jia Xu, Xiao-Li Mou, Lian-Sha Tang, Hong-Shuai Li, Wen-Jun Meng, Ai Zheng, Yang-Mei Shen, Ji-Yan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately distinguishing between primary and gastrointestinal metastatic mucinous ovarian carcinoma (MOC) is crucial but remains highly challenging. We developed and validated the MOC Origin Prediction Model (MOCOPM), the deep learning model specifically designed to predict the origin of MOC from histopathology images. Cases of primary or gastrointestinal metastatic MOC were collected from three hospitals and divided into internal and external cohorts. Three neural networks were trained using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) as the primary performance metric. After five-fold cross-validation on the internal cohort, the best-performing model was selected to construct MOCOPM, which was then externally validated. In total, 167 MOC patients were included. MOCOPM achieved an average AUROC of 0.91 internally and 0.96 externally. This model offers a promising tool to support clinical decision-making in MOC diagnosis.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Mucinous ovarian carcinoma (MOC) is an important histologic subtype of ovarian cancer. For patients diagnosed with MOC, accurately distinguishing whether the primary cancer is ovarian or metastatic from other sites (predominantly the gastrointestinal tract) is crucial, as the treatments and prognoses for these two conditions differ significantly. However, it is well known that a significant proportion of cases diagnosed as primary MOC in the past were actually metastatic MOC1–3. Despite increased attention to distinguishing between primary and metastatic MOC, this remains one of the most challenging areas in gynecological pathology and surgery today, especially when extra-ovarian metastatic foci are subtle or clinically occult4–6.

Histopathological assessment of surgical resection specimens remains the standard and most crucial approach for diagnosing primary and metastatic MOC. To improve accuracy, immunohistochemical, clinical, and imaging data should also be integrated for a comprehensive evaluation4. This process requires considerable expertise, time, and effort, but the results are often unsatisfactory. Although numerous studies have proposed various clinicopathological features to help distinguish between primary and metastatic MOC, the ability of some metastatic tumors to present clinically and morphologically as ovarian primaries renders these parameters often inconclusive7–9. More critically, some of these features, such as invasive growth patterns, are usually semiquantitative and subjective, frequently affected by interobserver variability.

On the other hand, deep learning, a subset of artificial intelligence (AI), has recently demonstrated significant potential in medical image analysis. Constructed using various neural networks, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or graph neural networks (GNNs), deep learning can automatically learn and extract complex and subtle patterns from cancer histopathology images. These features may contain a wealth of clinically relevant information that has not yet been fully exploited, allowing deep learning to potentially outperform human expertise in some tasks such as tissue classification, cancer detection, and patient prognosis10–15.

Emerging deep learning architectures continue to enhance this field, with the Structure-Aware Hierarchical Graph-Based Multiple Instance Learning Framework (SGMF) being a recently proposed architecture within the GNN category. This framework effectively organizes information from multi-magnification images using a structure-aware hierarchical graph and captures critical spatial features through a hierarchical attention-based graph representation network, enabling precise identification of tumor infiltration patterns and significantly improving staging accuracy16.

To date, deep learning approaches have been applied to ovarian cancer classification, histological subtyping, and tissue-of-origin prediction in broader oncological contexts, including cancers of unknown primary11,17. However, these studies have not specifically addressed the clinically challenging distinction between primary and gastrointestinal metastatic MOC, a task that often remains difficult even with comprehensive histopathological assessment. In this study, we developed and validated the MOC Origin Prediction Model (MOCOPM), a fully automated AI model to address this diagnostic challenge.

Results

Patients for MOCOPM

We collected 285 H&E-stained whole slide images (WSIs) from 131 MOC patients at West China Second Hospital to form the Internal-MOC, which included 58 primary cases and 73 gastrointestinal metastatic cases. Compared to metastatic MOC, primary MOC is more likely to be unilateral, with a tumor size of ≥ 10 cm and CK7 positivity on immunohistochemistry (Table 1). Additionally, 52 WSIs from 36 MOC patients were obtained from Nanchong Central Hospital and the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College to create the External-MOC, consisting of 17 primary and 19 gastrointestinal metastatic cases (Supplementary Table 1). Performance of MOCOPM

We investigated several neural network architectures, including two widely used CNNs: ResNet-18 and EfficientNet-B1, and a novel GNNs, SGMF, to evaluate their performance in distinguishing between primary and gastrointestinal metastatic MOC. SGMF demonstrated the best performance during five-fold cross-validation on the Internal-MOC. As shown in Fig. 2, SGMF achieved an average AUROC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.847–0.926) across all testing folds, with average accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall of 0.84, 0.86, 0.89, and 0.84, respectively. Therefore, SGMF was selected as the default backbone for MOCOPM. A detailed performance comparison of ResNet-18, EfficientNet-B1, and SGMF is provided in Table 2.

Generalization of MOCOPM

To further evaluate the generalization capacity of MOCOPM, an additional set from two different hospitals was collected for independent external testing. The tissue processing and staining at these two hospitals were conducted independently. MOCOPM also demonstrated good performance on this external dataset, achieving an average AUROC of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.924–0.972) across all folds, which was numerically slightly higher than the performance observed in the internal validation set (Fig. 3A). The average accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall of MOCOPM were 0.90, 0.91, 0.92, and 0.90, respectively (Fig. 3B). Moreover, we downloaded 41 WSIs of all 41 primary MOC cases from the UBC-OCEAN dataset, which includes histopathological images of five distinct ovarian cancer subtypes, to further evaluate the model’s robustness, and MOCOPM continued to perform well, achieving an average accuracy of 0.87 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Beyond discriminative performance, decision curve analysis demonstrated that MOCOPM provided a consistent net benefit over treat-all and treat-none strategies across a clinically relevant range of threshold probabilities in both the internal and external cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 2A and C). Reliability plots further showed reasonable agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies, indicating acceptable calibration without post hoc probability adjustment (Supplementary Fig. 2B and D).

Explainability of MOCOPM

To enhance our understanding and validation of MOCOPM’s predictions, as well as to boost confidence in its application, we employed GNNExplainer to perform explainability analyses and visualize the key features influencing its predictions. GNNExplainer heatmaps were generated for all correctly classified cases in Internal-MOC. For systematic evaluation, 50% of these cases (n = 56) were randomly selected and their corresponding heatmaps independently reviewed by experienced gynecological pathologists.

In 45 of the 56 evaluated cases, the highlighted high-attention regions overlapped with at least one established histopathological feature relevant to distinguishing primary from gastrointestinal metastatic MOC, including clusters of signet ring cells, infiltration patterns, and the coexistence of benign, borderline, and malignant components. Figure 4 presents representative heatmaps generated by GNNExplainer when MOCOPM identifies primary or gastrointestinal metastatic MOC, with red indicating regions of high attention and blue indicating regions of low attention. The concordance between highlighted regions and recognized diagnostic features may suggest that the model’s predictions are informed by histopathologically meaningful patterns rather than spurious visual cues.

Discussion

Accurately distinguishing between primary and gastrointestinal MOC is often highly challenging. Misdiagnoses in clinical practice may lead to severe consequences for patients. In contrast, deep learning has recently demonstrated considerable promise in medical image analysis. Therefore, we explored deep learning techniques for the first time to address this challenge in gynecological pathology and surgery.

As mentioned earlier, distinguishing between primary and metastatic MOC is often difficult when relying on single, manually extracted clinicopathological features. Published literature frequently employs algorithms that combine these features4,18–20. For instance, Khunamorpong et al. utilized an algorithm that incorporated tumor diameter, laterality, and the presence of signet ring cells in 68 MOC patients (including 16 primary and 52 metastatic cases), achieving an accuracy of 0.84, sensitivity of 0.81, and specificity of 0.8420. Similarly, Hu et al. employed an approach using tumor diameter, laterality, and PAX8 immunohistochemical staining in 65 MOC patients (including 47 primary and 18 metastatic cases), resulting in an accuracy of 0.86, sensitivity of 0.91, but a specificity of only 0.719. In comparison, our MOCOPM demonstrates comparable, if not slightly enhanced, differentiation performance. Specifically, MOCOPM achieved an average AUROC of 0.91, with average accuracy, F1 score, precision, and recall of 0.84, 0.86, 0.89, and 0.84, respectively, during five-fold cross-validation. Notably, these performance metrics were derived from a dataset with a relatively larger sample size (131 patients) and a more balanced distribution of primary and metastatic MOC cases (58 primary and 73 metastatic), which bolsters the reliability of MOCOPM’s predictive performance. In contrast, when the previously mentioned algorithms are applied to larger and more balanced datasets, their accuracy only reaches 76.6-77.2%21. Furthermore, MOCOPM’s performance was validated using an independent external cohort from two additional hospitals, as well as in the publicly available UBC-OCEAN dataset. The performance observed on the external validation cohorts was numerically higher than that obtained from internal cross-validation. This finding should be interpreted with caution. The internal cohort was derived from a regional tertiary referral center, which is more likely to include diagnostically challenging and ambiguous cases, and was evaluated using multi-fold cross-validation, representing a more stringent assessment. In contrast, the external cohorts were collected from local hospitals with generally clearer clinicopathological presentations and were relatively smaller in size, where performance estimates are inherently associated with greater variability. Accordingly, the external validation results should be regarded as supportive evidence of robustness rather than superior diagnostic accuracy.

Additionally, in clinical practice, diagnostic efficiency and cost are also important. MOCOPM requires only routine H&E-stained slides to deliver rapid and automatic diagnoses. In contrast, traditional methods often necessitate experienced pathologists to perform detailed microscopic examinations and additional tests, such as immunohistochemical staining. These conventional approaches are highly subjective, time-consuming, labor-intensive, and increase costs. Moreover, deep learning models like MOCOPM offer greater scalability. We have established a demonstration website (http://www.aislide.clinic) where users can upload H&E-stained slides, such as those from public databases, to obtain example classification from MOCOPM. This platform is provided solely as a proof-of-concept for research and educational purposes and is not intended for clinical diagnostic use.

Deep learning models are frequently associated with limited transparency in their decision-making processes. To address this, we employed GNNExplainer to analyze and explain MOCOPM’s predictions and visualize the key features influencing its decisions. The generated heatmaps for some slides highlighted regions corresponding to known histological features used by pathologists to differentiate between primary and metastatic MOC, including clusters of signet ring cells, infiltration patterns, and the coexistence of benign, borderline, and malignant components. This alignment between the MOCOPM’s focus and established diagnostic criteria enhances trust in MOCOPM, provides transparency in its decision-making process, and may inform future integration into clinical workflows.

During the development of MOCOPM, we observed that the latest GNN architecture, SGMF, significantly outperformed the two CNNs, ResNet-18 and EfficientNet-B1. SGMF is inspired by pathologists’ practice of using low, medium, and high magnification microscopy to assess tumor infiltration for pT staging. By employing the structure-aware hierarchical graph and hierarchical attention graph representation networks, SGMF integrates multi-magnification image information to accurately capture tumor infiltration patterns, thereby enhancing staging accuracy16. Unlike traditional CNNs that typically process single-scale local features, SGMF captures cross-scale spatial relationships, greatly improving the recognition of tumor infiltration patterns. Coincidentally, although the assessment of invasive growth patterns lacks objective evaluation metrics, the presence of such patterns is considered crucial in distinguishing metastatic MOC from primary MOC. SGMF’s proficiency in extracting and recognizing tumor infiltration patterns may explain why it performed better and was selected as the default backbone of MOCOPM.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that MOCOPM is not intended to replace expert pathological diagnosis or existing standards of care. The model should be regarded as a decision-support tool rather than a standalone diagnostic system. Its potential clinical value lies in assisting pathologists by highlighting regions of interest that may warrant closer review, as well as by providing supportive input in diagnostically challenging cases or resource-limited settings. Determining whether such systems can provide incremental benefit beyond standard care will require prospective studies incorporating head-to-head comparison with pathologists and evaluation of human-AI collaboration.

Although MOCOPM has shown promising results, our study has several limitations. First, the relative rarity of MOC limited the sample size. Additional validation from other medical centers is necessary before MOCOPM can be widely adopted in clinical practice. Second, we did not include metastatic MOC from non-gastrointestinal origins, such as the pancreas or bile ducts, during model development due to their extreme rarity. This may impact MOCOPM’s generalizability and accuracy in distinguishing these less common metastatic sources. Third, although we have used intuitive visualization methods to provide explainability of MOCOPM’s predictions, understanding the specific reasons behind the model’s predictions remains challenging.

Methods

Dataset curation

This was a retrospective study and approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Second Hospital / West China Women’s and Children’s Hospital (ethics approval number: [2023125]). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized histopathological data. To develop MOCOPM, we utilized MOC pathological images from three hospitals: images from West China Second Hospital served as the internal dataset (Internal-MOC) for training and internal validation, while images from Nanchong Central Hospital and the Affiliated Hospital of North Sichuan Medical College constituted the external dataset (External-MOC) for independent validation to assess generalization. The inclusion criteria were: (1) patients who underwent oophorectomy with a pathological diagnosis of primary or gastrointestinal metastatic MOC between 2015 and 2023, and (2) availability of clinical data and H&E-stained tumor slides. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients who received preoperative therapy (such as neoadjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy), and (2) patients with incomplete clinical information or substandard slides. Two experienced gynecological pathologists independently reviewed and confirmed all included specimens. The pathologists were blinded to all deep learning model outputs during case review. For gastrointestinal metastatic MOC, a clear history of gastric or colorectal cancer was required before the pathological diagnosis. In contrast, primary MOC cases had no history or evidence of extra-ovarian metastases before the pathological diagnosis and during at least 1.5 years of follow-up afterwards. The majority of cases received concordant diagnoses from the two senior pathologists. A small number of cases with diagnostic discrepancies or quality issues, such as tissue folds or tears, were excluded from the development of MOCOPM. One or three representative H&E-stained tumor slides from each patient’s resection were scanned at ×40 magnification using a KF-PRO-400-HI scanner (Ningbo Jiangfeng Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd, China) to generate WSIs in SVS format.

Additionally, the UBC-OCEAN dataset, a significant resource for gynecological histopathological research encompassing five distinct ovarian cancer subtypes, was also utilized17,22. We downloaded all WSIs of primary MOC cases from this dataset via https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/UBC-OCEAN/overview.

Development of the MOCOPM

To develop MOCOPM, we employed three neural networks: two CNNs, ResNet-18 and EfficientNet-B1, and one GNNs, SGMF.

Initially, we used the OpenSlide library to read WSIs and obtain their dimensions and downsampling levels, ensuring the accuracy of subsequent processing. After acquisition, we utilized HSV color space and median filtering for tissue segmentation, generating a binary mask using the Otsu method to identify tissue regions. Then, we applied morphological operations to optimize the mask and remove small holes, thereby enhancing segmentation precision. To ensure the relevance of the actual tissue, contour filtering was applied using an area threshold, effectively excluding irrelevant contours and thereby focusing the downstream analysis exclusively on pertinent tissue structures. Finally, we obtained non-overlapping patches for subsequent models to use. All deep learning models were developed and evaluated using histopathology images alone, without access to clinical information or reference standard results.

ResNet-18 and EfficientNet-B1

ResNet-18 has been widely adopted in histopathology image analysis due to its lightweight architecture and robustness, whereas EfficientNet-B1, a more recent CNN family, achieves high accuracy with fewer parameters and has also been successfully applied in computational pathology (we included both for fair benchmarking)10,23,24. We first adopted a multiple instance learning (MIL) framework based on these CNNs. Each WSI was treated as a bag, and the 1024×1024 patches derived from the same WSI were considered instances within that bag. Next, we input these patches into the ResNet-18 or EfficientNet-B1 networks for feature extraction and aggregated the extracted features into bag representations through the MIL pooling layer. Finally, bag-level classifications were generated, which allowed us to classify the WSIs.

SGMF

In addition to the CNN-based approaches above, we applied SGMF for classification. This architecture was inspired by the need for pathologists to observe H&E-stained slides at different magnifications during the diagnostic process16. Specifically, patches extracted from WSIs at magnifications of 5×, 10×, and 20× were used to construct three separate graphs using the eight-nearest-neighbor algorithm. Additionally, based on the subordinate relationships among patches corresponding to the 5× magnification WSI and those for the other two magnifications, we created cross-scale connections between the graphs composed of patches from different magnifications. Then, we fed the constructed structure-aware hierarchical graphs into a hierarchical attention-based graph representation network to capture key features, ultimately providing the classification for the WSIs.

Training and optimization

In the training of the above three neural networks, a five-fold cross-validation strategy was employed. Specifically, the entire dataset was randomly divided into five independent subsets, with each subset segmented according to patient IDs rather than WSI IDs. In each iteration, four subsets were used for model training, while the remaining subset was utilized for validation. This process was repeated five times.

All neural networks were trained using the Adam optimization algorithm to minimize the cross-entropy loss between the bag-level outputs and the true labels, with a threshold of 0.5 applied for classification. When the predicted probability of the patient’s WSI was less than 0.5, the patient was classified as primary MOC; otherwise, as metastatic MOC.

Evaluation of the MOCOPM

We used the Internal-MOC and External-MOC mentioned above for internal and external evaluations respectively. For internal evaluation, the aforementioned five-fold cross-validation strategy was employed to assess the performance on the Internal-MOC. For the external testing, the five models trained during the five-fold cross-validation process were used to make predictions on the External-MOC. Additionally, we further validated the model using all WSIs of primary MOC cases from the UBC-OCEAN dataset.

In evaluating the performance of the models, we employed various metrics, including AUROC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score. The calculation of AUROC involves two main steps: first, adjusting the classifier’s threshold to compute the True Positive Rate (TPR) and False Positive Rate (FPR) at various thresholds, and plotting these points to form the ROC curve; second, calculating the area under this curve, which gives the AUROC value. AUROC ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating stronger classification performance across different thresholds. Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly predicted samples, while precision represents the ratio of true positive samples to all samples predicted as positive. Recall assesses the proportion of correctly identified positive samples out of all actual positive samples. The F1 score is calculated as the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 were computed using the same 0.5 probability threshold applied during classification.

To assess potential clinical utility, decision curve analysis was performed across a range of threshold probabilities, estimating net benefit relative to treat-all and treat-none strategies. Model calibration was evaluated using reliability plots, which examined the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcome frequencies by comparing mean predicted probabilities with empirical event rates. These analyses were conducted without post-hoc probability calibration.

Explainability analysis of the MOCOPM

We applied the GNNExplainer method to analyze and explain the Graph-based MOCOPM. GNNExplainer is a general-purpose tool designed to uncover the prediction mechanisms of GNNs25. Specifically, we used this method to perform a local analysis of the input graph, employing an optimization algorithm to identify nodes, edges, and features closely related to the prediction. This process generated concise subgraph explanations that revealed the core graph elements and their relationships on which the model relied when handling complex graph structures. GNNExplainer applies to a variety of graph-based tasks, including the graph classification problem discussed in this paper.

Statistical analysis

The baseline characteristics between primary and gastrointestinal metastatic MOC were compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS statistical software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).
