Percutaneous Rescue With Sequential Second-Device Usage for Left Atrial Appendage Perforation During Amulet Implantation
Authors: Masanori Yamamoto, Ryo Yamaguchi, Tatsuya Tsunaki, Mitsuru Sago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft atrial appendage closure (LAAC) prevents stroke in nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, while device-related left atrial appendage (LAA) perforation may lead to cardiac tamponade.Case SummaryA man in his early 80s underwent percutaneous LAAC using the Amulet device. During deployment, sustained counterclockwise torque in a ball-shaped configuration resulted in perforation of the LAA, with device extrusion and progressive pericardial effusion. To avoid bleeding upon extraction, we executed a sequential second-device rescue: The first device was left in situ to tamponade the defect, while a downsized Amulet was positioned at the orifice; the first device was then withdrawn, and the second device was released immediately, sealing the LAA. Ultimately, the patient was discharged uneventfully.DiscussionSheath rotation in a ball configuration is generally considered safe, while excessive torque can make this maneuver risky.Take-Home MessageLAAC itself can treat acute perforation by sealing both the defect and the LAA before considering surgery.Key words: left atrial appendage closure, left atrial appendage perforation, bailout strategy

Sequential Second-Device Exchange to Treat LAA Perforation During Amulet Implantation

Preprocedural CT, TEE, and angiography were used to evaluate LAA anatomy. A 22-mm Amplatzer Amulet device was initially selected, but noncoaxial alignment resulted in inadequate posterior lobe coverage. During partial advancement to a ball-shaped configuration with sustained counterclockwise sheath torque, the device extruded outside the LAA, causing acute perforation with pericardial effusion. To avoid bleeding with immediate extraction, the first device was left in situ to tamponade the defect while a second, downsized 20-mm Amulet was positioned at the LAA orifice; the first device was then recaptured and the second device was released immediately, achieving complete LAA sealing without residual flow. Pericardiocentesis stabilized hemodynamics, and follow-up imaging confirmed no recurrent effusion or peridevice leak. CT = computed tomography; LAA = left atrial appendage; LAAC = left atrial appendage closure; TEE = transesophageal echocardiography.

History of Presentation

A man in his early 80s with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation was referred for elective left atrial appendage closure (LAAC) given high thromboembolic risk and bleeding concerns. He was asymptomatic at presentation (no chest pain, presyncope, or heart failure exacerbation).

Past Medical History

Comorbidities included hypertension, chronic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, prior ischemic stroke without residual deficit, and stable angina on single antiplatelet therapy long-term per cardiology. He had recurrent epistaxis on oral anticoagulant therapy requiring outpatient cauterization 2 times in the past 12 months. The CHA2DS2-VASc score was 5 (heart failure, hypertension, prior stroke, vascular disease); and HAS-BLED score was 3 (hypertension, prior bleeding [epistaxis], concomitant antiplatelet).

Investigations

Computed tomography (CT) angiography demonstrated a chicken-wing morphology of the left atrial appendage (LAA) with prominent pectinate muscles; no thrombus was identified (Figure 1A). Preprocedural 2-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated an elliptical LAA ostium. In the same 2-dimensional views (0°, 45°, 90°, and 135°), the ostial diameters were 20.3, 18.8, 21.5, and 24.1 mm, respectively, and the diameters measured 10 mm distal to the ostium were 17.8, 14.7, 14.4, and 19.2 mm, respectively (Figure 1B).

Management

The LAAC procedure was performed under general anesthesia and TEE guidance. Transseptal puncture was performed at the inferoposterior portion of the fossa ovalis. Left atrial pressure was confirmed to be ≥12 mm Hg, and a dedicated 14-F delivery sheath was advanced. In combination with a 5-F pigtail catheter, LAA angiography was performed (Figure 2A). Based on the TEE findings, a 22-mm Amplatzer Amulet device (Abbott) was selected. Deployment was difficult owing to noncoaxial alignment between the sheath and LAA (Figure 2B), resulting in inadequate posterior lobe coverage (Figures 2C and 2D). To correct the orientation, the device was partially advanced to a ball-shaped configuration while continuous counterclockwise torque was applied (Figure 2E). After the sheath was rotated, the alignment seemed to be corrected (Figure 2F). However, TEE demonstrated complete extrusion of the device outside the LAA, with new pericardial effusion (Figures 2G and 2H).

Given the risk of torrential bleeding with extraction, the first device was kept in situ to tamponade the perforation. Additional femoral venous access was obtained, and a second transseptal puncture was performed (Figure 3A). The initially selected device also had axis misalignment, and its large size caused posterior lobe protrusion. Therefore, a downsized 20-mm Amulet was prepared. The first device was withdrawn into the sheath, then the second device was immediately deployed at the LAA orifice (Figure 3B). TEE confirmed device release criteria (Figure 3C), and angiography showed no residual flow (Figure 3D). Pericardiocentesis followed (≈100 mL serosanguineous drainage), with rapid hemodynamic stabilization (Figure 3E).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's hemodynamics stabilized after deployment of the second device. The pericardial drain was removed on postprocedure day 1 without re-accumulation. The patient was discharged on day 3. Follow-up CT at 3 months confirmed a stable device without peridevice leak or effusion (Figure 3F).

Discussion

This case highlights several important lessons. Excessive rotational force during LAAC can lead to catastrophic complications such as LAA perforation. In general practice, once the Amulet is configured into a ball shape, operators are advised that the sheath may be rotated clockwise or counterclockwise to align with the LAA axis, and this maneuver is often recommended to achieve coaxial alignment. However, in our case, maintaining counterclockwise rotation was associated with excessive stress, and the entire device in ball configuration was forced outside the LAA. This observation indicates that even maneuvers considered safe may become hazardous.

In terms of bailout, prior reports have described surgical repair or the use of an alternative catheter occlusion device.1,2 Our case demonstrates a sequential exchange strategy using a second Amulet device, which successfully sealed the acute perforation. For this strategy, attention must be paid to sheath handling to avoid interference, and it is crucial that retrieval of the first device and delivery of the second device are performed in a smooth, uninterrupted sequence. Such an approach may be feasible only in hemodynamically stable patients and under immediate surgical backup, but it illustrates that LAAC itself can occasionally serve as a definitive treatment for this rare and life-threatening complication. When hemodynamics are stable and surgical backup is immediate, a sequential second-device exchange may provide definitive percutaneous treatment by sealing both the perforation and the appendage.

An alternative bailout strategy was immediate recapture and redeployment of the initially selected device. In addition to the device axis being misaligned, the device seemed to be oversized. Redeployment of the 22-mm device could have left a posterior leak and provided inadequate sealing of the perforation site, with persistent bleeding. Therefore, selecting the smaller 20-mm device was judged an appropriate decision.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that although sheath rotation in ball configuration is generally considered a safe maneuver during LAAC using the Amulet device, excessive torque may cause LAA perforation with potentially catastrophic consequences. A novel sequential second-device exchange strategy allowed both the perforated site and the LAA to be sealed percutaneously, resulting in a favorable outcome.
