Bridging the Gap in Dementia Rehabilitation: Evaluating the Impact of the INCLUDE Package on Allied Health Professionals' Knowledge, Attitudes and Practice Change
Authors: Den‐Ching A. Lee, Catherine Devanny, Grant Russell, Claire M. C. O'Connor, Keith D. Hill, Kate Swaffer, Monica Cations, Kate Laver, Lee‐Fay Low, Barbara Barbosa Neves, Helen Skouteris, Michele L. Callisaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesDespite evidence and guidelines supporting rehabilitation, people with dementia experience limited access due to health professionals' attitudes, knowledge gaps, and systemic barriers. The INCLUDE package is an interdisciplinary online training programme and Community of Practice (CoP) designed to address these barriers. The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of the INCLUDE package on health professionals' knowledge, attitudes, confidence, advocacy, and practice change in dementia rehabilitation.MethodsA pre‐post longitudinal study involved two groups of health professionals across Australia. Group 1 (n = 103) completed the online training and an 8‐month CoP; Group 2 (n = 373) completed training only. Surveys administered at pre‐training (T1), post‐training (T2), and 10‐month follow‐up (T3; Group 1 only) assessed knowledge, attitudes and confidence towards dementia and dementia rehabilitation. Multilevel mixed‐effects regression models were used to examine changes over time. Content analysis was used to explore advocacy, practice changes, barriers, and sustainability.Results476 health professionals participated. The largest groups were physiotherapists (n = 121, 26.7%), occupational therapists (n = 120, 26.5%) and nurses (n = 37,13.6%). The Dementia Attitudes Scale (coefficient 10.4, 95% CI 9.4–11.4), Dementia Rehabilitation Questionnaire (3.8, 95% CI 3.0–4.5), and Confidence in Delivering Dementia Rehabilitation Scale (3.2, 95% CI 2.8–3.5) improved from T1 to T2. In Group 1, improvements in attitudes towards dementia and confidence in rehabilitation were sustained at T3, but knowledge and attitudes towards dementia rehabilitation declined from T2 to T3. Participants advocated for dementia rehabilitation and made changes in their workplace including revising rehabilitation access criteria, advertising dementia rehabilitation to referrers, and developing interdisciplinary programs.ConclusionsThe INCLUDE package improved health professionals' attitudes, knowledge and confidence in dementia rehabilitation. Although participants made changes in their workplace, barriers still existed. Organisational and system‐level changes are also required to improve access to dementia rehabilitation.Trial RegistrationThis study is registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry: ACTRN12623001029684

Introduction

Dementia is a major cause of disability, with increasing recognition of the need for effective rehabilitation to address dementia‐related disabilities. Various rehabilitation therapies, including physiotherapy [1], occupational therapy [2], speech pathology [3], exercise [4], and cognitive rehabilitation [5], have shown positive outcomes, particularly when initiated early in the condition. Importantly, the World Health Organisation (WHO) emphasises rehabilitation as a crucial component of care for chronic conditions such as dementia [6], and in 2023 the WHO launched a dementia specific ‘Package of Interventions for Rehabilitation’ [7]. Furthermore, global guidelines recognise the importance of rehabilitation for people with dementia, recommending that individuals should be referred to allied health professionals to optimise function [8, 9, 10].

This study is part of a larger project (The right to rehabilitation for people with dementia: tackling stigma and implementing evidence‐based interventions) which aimed to improve access to rehabilitation for people with dementia [11, 12]. Phase 1 of the project involved workshops with health professionals, people with dementia, care partners, and representatives from dementia support organisations to identify key barriers to accessing services and designing solutions to address these barriers [12].

The workshops revealed barriers to accessing rehabilitation at the individual, health professional and systems levels. For the person with dementia, barriers exist to perceiving, seeking, reaching, and engaging in rehabilitation care due to self‐stigma, delayed diagnosis, lack of knowledge about the benefit of rehabilitation, difficulty navigating or paying for services, lack of transport and lack of availability of a care partner [12]. At the health professional level, key barriers to an approachable, available, acceptable and appropriate service included widespread health professional stigma, therapeutic nihilism, lack of knowledge and confidence about dementia rehabilitation, as well as a lack of clear care pathways [12]. At the systems level, barriers included fragmented health, aged care and disability services, inequitable funding models for allied health and rehabilitation, limited publicly funded rehabilitation sessions, long wait times, rural service shortages, and policies that excluded people with dementia from rehabilitation programs [12].

These insights led to the co‐design of solutions for implementation and evaluation in Phase 2. The resulting “INCLUDE” package features online dementia rehabilitation training modules aimed at reducing stigma, improving interdisciplinary knowledge of dementia rehabilitation using a human rights‐based approach, and teaching participants to become advocates to improve access to dementia rehabilitation interventions in their workplaces. Additionally, the package included an asynchronous 8‐month community of practice (CoP) designed to foster interdisciplinary collaboration, further education, and support participants to advocate and make changes to improve access to dementia rehabilitation.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of the INCLUDE package on health professionals' (a) knowledge and attitudes towards dementia and dementia rehabilitation, (b) confidence in delivering dementia rehabilitation, and (c) the extent of advocacy efforts and practice changes within workplaces.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study used a longitudinal pre‐post design.

Participants

Participants included health professionals such as doctors, nurses, allied health professionals, dementia support specialists, aged care assessors, care support coordinators and allied health assistants. In Australia, dementia support specialists provide tailored support and care for individuals with dementia and their families, offering guidance on managing symptoms, navigating services, and improving quality of life. Aged care assessors and coordinators evaluate the needs of older adults to determine appropriate care services, including home care and coordinate the delivery of these services, ensuring that the care plans align with the person's needs and preferences.

There were two groups of participants. Group 1 were recruited between November 2023 and January 2024. Eligibility criteria included: (1) working in the Eastern Melbourne and South Eastern Melbourne Primary Health Network (PHN) areas in Victoria, Australia; (2) expected to remain in their place of work for at least 12 months and (3) predominantly working in a community setting. Group 2 were recruited between May 2024 and July 2024. For Group 2, eligibility criteria were expanded to include health professionals working anywhere in Australia and across community, hospitals and residential aged care settings. This change was made due to demand from health professionals across different care settings and to increase reach.

Human research ethics approval was obtained prior to participant recruitment, and all participants provided written informed consent. Ethics approvals were granted by Monash University (Project ID: 38423), Monash Health (HREC/100657/MonH‐2023‐386489 (v1), Local Reference: RES‐23‐0000534A), Eastern Health (HREC Reference Number: RES‐23‐0000534A, ERM Project ID: 100657, Reference Number: S23‐087‐100657), and Peninsula Health (HREC Reference Number: HREC/100657/MonH‐2023; SSA: SSA/100657/PH‐2023).

Recruitment

Recruitment strategies for both intakes included emails, phone calls, and letters to managers and individual clinicians in the area, as well as advertisements through researcher networks. Professional associations, such as the Australian Physiotherapy Association, Occupational Therapy Australia, and Speech Pathology Australia, promoted the study to their members via newsletters and direct contact. Additionally, a snowball recruitment approach was used, where participants were encouraged to share study recruitment materials with colleagues and professional contacts who met the inclusion criteria.

Intervention

Both groups completed the self‐paced online training. Group 1 additionally participated in an 8‐month CoP. Participation in both the training and CoP was provided free of charge. Group 2 was not offered the CoP due to the project timeline. Further details of the structure and delivery of the intervention is described below.

Online Training Modules

The online training was hosted on Moodle, an accessible online learning platform. There were nine modules of approximately 10 h in total over 4 weeks. Modules were co‐developed by clinicians, researchers, people with dementia and care partners to ensure content reflected real‐world perspectives. The modules (Figure 1) covered key areas related to dementia rehabilitation. The interdisciplinary course used a human rights‐based approach that aimed to change attitudes, increase knowledge on dementia rehabilitation and teach participants how they could advocate and take action in their workplace. Support was provided through weekly announcements and motivational emails. Content was interactive, including flip cards, embedded links, quizzes, and reflective activities.

Modules covered in dementia rehabilitation.

Secure logins to access the course were provided to Group 1 on January 29, 2024, and Group 2 on July 15, 2024. Participants were given 4 weeks to complete the modules, with an additional 4‐week extension provided to 38 participants on request.

Community of Practice (CoP)

Group 1 also participated in a CoP aimed to foster interdisciplinary education, professional connections, further education, and support workplace advocacy and action to improve access to dementia rehabilitation. Six workshops were held approximately six to 8 weeks apart from April to November 2024. Most workshops were conducted online (one‐hour sessions). The final session was a three‐hour in‐person event, with 1 hour recorded for online access. Each workshop featured content from experts with diverse backgrounds in research, clinical practice, and lived experience. Recordings were uploaded to Moodle for flexible access.

Outcome Measures

Structured surveys were used to record participants' demographics, and assess their knowledge, attitudes and confidence about dementia and dementia rehabilitation. The surveys comprised validated scales and a custom‐designed Dementia Rehabilitation Questionnaire (see Appendix A). Group 1 additionally completed four open‐ended questions (see below). For Group 1, surveys were undertaken pre (T1) and post (T2) the online training modules and again at the end of the CoP period (T3) after 10 months. For Group 2, the same surveys were collected only pre (T1) and post (T2) the online training modules.

It consists of two domains: (1) Dementia Knowledge: Understanding of dementia‐related issues and (2) Social Comfort: Comfort levels when interacting with individuals with dementia. Participants rated each item on a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = ‘strongly disagree’, 4 = ‘neutral’, 7 = ‘strongly agree’). The total score ranges from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes. Each domain score ranges from 10 to 70, with higher scores reflecting greater dementia knowledge and social comfort. Each item is scored 0 (denoting an incorrect response to a factually true or false statement) or 2 (correct response), with a total score ranging from 0 to 50. Higher scores indicate more comprehensive knowledge of dementia. Confidence in dementia rehabilitation: Measured by self‐ratings on a scale of 0–10, where 0 indicated “no confidence at all” and 10 indicated “very confident”. Participants rate their confidence in three areas: (1) providing rehabilitation, (2) using cognitive and communication support strategies, and (3) understanding the roles of allied health professionals in dementia care. Higher scores represented greater confidence across these domains. The Dementia Rehabilitation Questionnaire: Created by MLC and DCAL, and informed by current evidence [4, 15, 16, 17, 18], it is a 16‐item scale designed to assess knowledge and attitudes towards dementia rehabilitation (Appendix A). Participants rate each item on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = “strongly disagree, 3 = “Neither agree or disagree”, 5 = “strongly agree”). The total score ranges from 16 to 80, with higher scores indicating more positive knowledge and attitudes towards dementia rehabilitation.

Quantitative Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarise participants' demographic and work‐related clinical practice characteristics. We calculated mean (SD) scores for all survey measures at T1, T2, and T3.

To evaluate changes in continuous outcomes (DAS, DKAS, Dementia Rehabilitation Questionnaire), multilevel mixed‐effects linear regression models were employed. These models accounted for repeated measures within individuals using random intercepts and robust standard errors [19]. To evaluate changes in ordinal outcomes (self‐rated confidence in rehabilitation and dementia strategies), multilevel mixed‐effects ordinal logistic regression was used, accounting for clustering within individuals and including random intercepts with robust standard errors [19]. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata SE version 15.1 (StataCorp, 2017), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Results are presented as regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), indicating the estimated absolute change in outcome scores between time points. A positive coefficient reflects an increase in the outcome measure over time, while a negative coefficient reflects a decrease.

Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative content analysis was conducted on open‐text responses from the follow‐up survey of the first intake group [20]. A deductive approach was used, informed by the study aims and survey questions relating to advocacy, practice change, barriers, and sustainability. Two researchers (DCAL and CD) independently reviewed the responses, identified meaning units, and assigned initial codes to relevant text segments. The researchers then collaborated to group and refine codes into broader categories. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, with a third researcher (MLC) providing additional input to reach consensus. Results

Participants (n = 476) had a mean age of 41.5 years (SD = 11.5), most were female (91.2%) and worked an average of 32.3 h per week. Common professional roles included physiotherapists (n = 121, 26.7%), occupational therapists (n = 120, 26.5%), nurses (n = 61, 13.6%), and speech pathologists (n = 37, 8.2%). Participants worked primarily in hospitals (n = 133, 28.1%), community health (n = 65, 13.7%), and residential aged care (n = 47, 9.9%). Most were based in Victoria (n = 312, 65.8%). Over one‐third (n = 184, 38.7%) had treated more than 20 people with dementia in the past year, 85 (17.9%) had completed postgraduate dementia training, and 336 (70.9%) had referred people with dementia to allied health professionals. Quantitative Results

Dementia Attitudes Scale (DAS)

Participants' attitudes significantly improved from pre‐to post‐training across the combined group, with a regression coefficient of 10.4 (95% CI: 9.4–11.4, p < 0.001) on the DAS (Table 2). This was primarily driven by the Social Comfort subscale (coefficient = 6.9, 95% CI: 6.2–7.6, p < 0.001), with smaller gains in Dementia Knowledge (coefficient = 3.5, 95% CI: 3.0–3.9, p < 0.001). In Group 1, DAS scores remained significantly higher than baseline at 10‐month follow‐up (coefficient = 2.7, 95% CI: 0.1–5.3, p = 0.04), although a decline was observed between T2 and T3 (coefficient = −7.3, 95% CI: −13.6 to −1.1, p = 0.02) (Table 3). The sustained effect was mainly attributed to the Social Comfort subscale (coefficient = 2.5, 95% CI: 1.1–3.9, p = 0.001).

Dementia Knowledge Assessment Scale (DKAS)

Total DKAS scores showed no statistically significant change post‐training (coefficient = 0.3, 95% CI ‐0.2 to 0.8, p = 0.3; Table 2), with participants demonstrating already high baseline knowledge (mean = 44, SD = 5.4). However, small improvements were seen in the subscales: Causes and Characteristics (coefficient = 0.4, 95% CI 0.2–0.6, p = 0.001), Communication and Behaviour (coefficient = 0.3, 95% CI 0.1–0.5, p = 0.002), and Risk and Health Promotion (coefficient = 0.5, 95% CI 0.3–0.7, p < 0.001). In contrast, Care Considerations declined post‐training (coefficient = −0.9, 95% CI ‐1.1 to −0.7, p < 0.001). Table 3 (Group 1) shows total DKAS scores were unchanged at follow‐up (p = 0.79). Care Considerations remained lower than baseline (coefficient = −0.4, 95% CI: −0.8 to −0.1, p = 0.01), while Risk and Health Promotion remained slightly improved (coefficient = 0.3, 95% CI: 0.0–0.6, p = 0.04).

Confidence in Dementia Rehabilitation

Table 2 shows significant improvements across all confidence domains: Providing dementia rehabilitation (coefficient = 3.2, 95% CI: 2.8–3.5, p < 0.001), using cognitive and communication strategies (coefficient = 3.0, 95% CI: 2.6–3.4, p < 0.001) and understanding allied health roles: (coefficient = 3.3, 95% CI: 2.9–3.7, p < 0.001). Table 3 (Group 1) shows these improvements were sustained at 10‐month follow‐up: Providing rehabilitation (coefficient = 1.2, 95% CI: 0.9–1.6, p < 0.001), cognitive and communication strategies (coefficient = 1.4, 95% CI: 1.0–1.8, p < 0.001), understanding roles (coefficient = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.4–2.3, p < 0.001). No significant changes were observed between T2 and T3.

Dementia Rehabilitation Questionnaire

Knowledge and attitudes towards dementia rehabilitation improved post‐training in the combined group (coefficient = 3.8, 95% CI: 3.0–4.5, p < 0.001; Table 2). No significant change was found across the three time points in Group 1 (p = 0.6), but there was a decline between T2 and T3 (coefficient = −5.8, 95% CI: −9.6 to −1.9, p = 0.003) (Table 3).

Qualitative Results

Of the 51 Group 1 participants who completed the follow‐up survey, 24 provided at least one open‐text response across the four qualitative questions, although not all participants responded to every question. Participants contributing qualitative data represented a range of disciplines and workplace settings. Occupational therapists comprised the largest professional group (n = 9, 37.5%), followed by physiotherapists (n = 6, 25.0%) and speech pathologists (n = 3, 12.5%). Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of the INCLUDE online training modules and CoP on health professionals' dementia knowledge, attitudes, confidence, advocacy and practice change. The online training completion rate of 77% was high compared to other online dementia courses [21]. The findings demonstrate that interdisciplinary online training, combined with peer‐supported learning, can positively influence professional attitudes, knowledge and confidence, as well as facilitate both clinical practice and service change. However, a number of barriers remained in implementing dementia rehabilitation. Clearer pathways, greater funding and broadening the reach of the education across disciplines and services were recommended to ensure people with dementia can access rehabilitation.

Dementia knowledge as measured by the DKAS did not change overall, possibly due to ceiling effect but the ‘Causes and Characteristics’, ‘Communication and Behaviour’ and ‘Risk and Health Promotion’ subscales did show modest improvements. The small changes are not surprising as the course focussed on rehabilitation strategies and therapies, rather than general dementia knowledge. Of note, the ‘Care Considerations’ domain of the DKAS declined after completing the training. This may reflect the wording of the DKAS questions, or the publication of new evidence that is contradictory to the answers in the scale. For example, question 15 states Movement is generally affected in the later stages of dementia with the answer in the scale ‘true’. However, recent findings demonstrate gait changes can occur in the pre‐dementia phase [22], and this was included in the training. Interestingly, although knowledge and attitudes towards dementia rehabilitation improved from T1 to T2, these improvements were not maintained at T3. One possible explanation is that participants developed a more nuanced understanding of dementia rehabilitation over time, leading to more critical self‐appraisal at follow‐up. In contrast, confidence in delivering dementia rehabilitation remained stable, possibly reflecting continued clinical exposure and reinforcement through the CoP. Organisational and system‐level supports may also be important for sustaining gains over time [11, 23].

Participants showed significant improvements in attitudes towards dementia following the training. These improvements were largely maintained at 10‐month follow‐up, reflecting durable attitudinal shifts. The content of modules was carefully designed to address negative attitudes and stigma, teaching responsibility in professional roles, providing reflection activities, and supporting interdisciplinary collaboration to make workplace change [24, 25]. Module 1 included information on the different types of stigma that exist, including internalised stigma, structural stigma and particularly the incorrect belief that people with dementia can't engage with rehabilitation or improve [26, 27]. Module 2 focused on dementia as a major cause of disability which requires rehabilitation. This was then framed as a human right, as outlined in the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and the World Health Organisation Rehabilitation 2030 initiative [6], that health professionals should uphold [28]. Furthermore, the inclusion of people with dementia in the modules, webinars and face to face sessions may have also contributed to changing attitudes and reducing stigma [24, 25]. This is in line with the ‘Time for Dementia’ education programme, where health care students visit people with dementia and their family, resulting in improved knowledge, attitudes and confidence in delivering person‐centred care [29].

Confidence in delivering dementia rehabilitation also improved after training, and this was maintained at T3. Previous research showed that values‐based and experiential learning can positively influence attitudes and person‐centred care approaches in dementia care [30, 31]. The modules provided practical, applicable skills to use in clinical practice, and the opportunity to communicate with people with dementia during the CoP. Active reflection, an important component of experiential learning [32], was encouraged throughout the modules by asking participants to reflect on their own attitudes, experiences, and learnings, and then to apply this into practice. Importantly, confidence in using cognitive and communication strategies improved, and was maintained over time. This is encouraging, as this is an area that health professionals have found particularly difficult in working with people with dementia [26]. Confidence is also a known driver of behaviour change in healthcare [33, 34], and these gains are encouraging in the context of historically low engagement with dementia rehabilitation among health professionals.

Improvements in attitudes and confidence may have contributed to the practice changes reported by participants, including advocacy efforts and service‐level initiatives. Participants described delivering professional development, improving referral processes, removing exclusion criteria for people with dementia, enacting interdisciplinary programme development and advertising dementia rehabilitation services to referrers. These reports suggest that some participants applied learnings from the training and CoP to advocacy and practice change efforts within their workplaces. Throughout the training, participants were provided with real life examples of health professionals who had implemented dementia rehabilitation in their workplace. They were further provided with ideas for changes they could make at different levels depending on their experience or role. It is encouraging that many of these examples were trialled. In addition, participants offered forward‐looking strategies to improve access to dementia rehabilitation and sustain change. These included scaling up training to reach broader professional audiences (e.g., aged care staff), more practical content, and continuing CoP activities. These recommendations align with broader calls to embed dementia training into workforce development strategies and to adopt whole‐of‐organisation approaches to dementia capability [35].

Implications for Practice and Policy

The results provide support for integrating dementia rehabilitation training into national and organisational professional development frameworks. Sustained improvements in attitudes and confidence, combined with evidence of workplace advocacy and practical service changes, underscore the potential of educational initiatives like INCLUDE to shift practice and culture. The findings also address a critical gap in workforce education, particularly for the diverse allied health and aged care workforce. Although some participants had undertaken postgraduate dementia training, only 17.9% reported this background. Dementia rehabilitation content remains inconsistently represented in many entry‐level allied health programs [36]. Embedding dementia rehabilitation in foundational training would represent a significant and much‐needed advancement.

At a policy level, improving access to dementia rehabilitation requires investment in workforce development, service redesign, and system navigation supports. Participants' advocacy efforts were often constrained by unclear referral pathways, funding, workforce shortages, and a lack of dementia‐specific services—highlighting the need for clearer service models and better integration across primary, community, and aged care systems. These barriers are consistent with known access issues in dementia rehabilitation identified in national and international literature [12, 35, 37]. Reducing stigma, supporting interdisciplinary collaboration, and embedding dementia rehabilitation in routine care pathways will be critical to realising the goals of national dementia strategies and the WHO's global rehabilitation agenda.

Strengths and Limitations

This study offers several strengths. It used a mixed‐methods, longitudinal design, allowing examination of both immediate and sustained effects of the INCLUDE intervention. The sample included a diverse range of health professionals from different disciplines and care settings, enhancing the generalisability of findings. The intervention was co‐designed with clinicians, researchers, and people with lived experience, ensuring relevance to real‐world practice. Importantly, the integration of both training and CoP activities enabled examination of the value of ongoing peer engagement.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. Participation was voluntary, introducing potential self‐selection bias among those already motivated or interested in dementia care. Attrition at follow‐up (35%) may have further limited the representativeness of the results. In addition, demographic information was not systematically collected for participants who did not complete the training modules, limiting our ability to determine whether non‐completion was associated with specific professional or demographic characteristics. While open‐ended survey responses offered valuable insights, they lacked the detail and probing of in‐depth interviews or focus groups. In addition, the Dementia Rehabilitation Questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and, although grounded in existing literature [4, 15, 16, 17, 18] and co‐design processes [12], has not undergone formal psychometric validation. As such, findings related to this measure should be interpreted cautiously. Furthermore, our evaluation of changes in practice were based on self‐report. Auditing the practice of a subset of participants before and after training may have been a useful way to determine whether participation translated to improved dementia rehabilitation awareness or practice in clinical or aged care settings. Previous initiatives in dementia care, such as a quality improvement collaborative, have been shown to improve adherence to dementia guidelines by allied health practitioners [38]. Finally, this was not a randomised controlled trial, and the absence of a control group limits the ability to attribute observed changes solely to the intervention.

Conclusion

Interdisciplinary dementia rehabilitation online training and a CoP can improve knowledge, attitudes and confidence, and facilitate workplace practice change. Future efforts must address systemic constraints, embed training within organisational structures, improve undergraduate dementia training and provide sustained opportunities for peer learning and implementation support.
