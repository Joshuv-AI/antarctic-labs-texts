Guselkumab in Biologic-Naïve Patients with Active Psoriatic Arthritis in Russia: A Post Hoc Analysis of the DISCOVER-1 and -2 Randomized Clinical Trials
Authors: Philip Mease, Tatiana Korotaeva, Pavel Shesternya, Muza Kokhan, Anton Rukavitsyn, Dmitry Vasilchenkov, Mohamed Sharaf, Frédéric Lavie, Atul Deodhar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThere are limited data on the use of advanced therapies to treat psoriatic arthritis (PsA) in Russia. Guselkumab, an interleukin (IL)-23p19-subunit inhibitor, demonstrated efficacy in patients with PsA in the phase 3 DISCOVER-1 and -2, and COSMOS trials. This analysis evaluated the efficacy and safety of guselkumab in patients with PsA in Russia.MethodsThis post hoc analysis of DISCOVER-1 and -2 included 1002 biologic-naïve patients with active PsA from Russia (n = 317) and the rest of the world (RoW; n = 685). Patients received guselkumab 100 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W), or at week 0 and 4 then Q8W, or placebo then guselkumab Q4W at week 24 (Russian: n = 119, 88, and 110, respectively; RoW: n = 216, 246, and 223, respectively). Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? There is currently a lack of data on the response of patients from Russia with psoriatic arthritis (PsA) to biologic therapies.The phase 3 DISCOVER-1 and -2 trials recruited a large number of patients from various sites in Russia. This enabled this post hoc analysis, which aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of guselkumab in biologic-naïve patients with active PsA from Russia compared with the rest of the world (RoW). What was learned from the study? Patients from Russia treated with guselkumab generally experienced improvements across all PsA and psoriasis domains, and quality of life measures, sustained through week 100. Responses were consistent with those of patients from the RoW.No cases of tuberculosis (TB) were reported, which supports the safety of guselkumab in Russia, where the incidence of TB is considered high compared with countries in the European Union and European Economic Area.Patients from Russia had higher use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs vs. the RoW cohort. The use of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs was lower in the Russian cohort vs. the RoW cohort; however, the use of methotrexate was similar in both cohorts.This post hoc analysis demonstrates similar efficacy and safety trends in patients with PsA from Russia and the RoW when treated with guselkumab.

Introduction

Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) is a systemic, chronic inflammatory condition characterized by heterogeneous signs and symptoms across disease domains, including peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis, axial involvement, and skin and nail psoriasis [1]. PsA has a significant negative impact on patients’ health-related quality of life and can impact work productivity and disability outcomes if poorly controlled [2].

Interleukin (IL)-23 is a regulatory cytokine involved in the pathogenesis of PsA by mediating the differentiation of pro-inflammatory T helper 17 cells and release of inflammatory cytokines. Elevated levels of IL-23 and of the IL-23 receptor have been detected in psoriatic skin and synovial fluid from patients with PsA [3]. Guselkumab is a fully human immunoglobulin G1 λ monoclonal antibody that prevents the interaction of IL-23 with its receptor and, thus, inhibits the initiation of the IL-23 pathway and the subsequent release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [4, 5].

The sustained efficacy of guselkumab across disease domains and in patient-reported outcomes has been established in patients with PsA who are naïve to biologics and in those with prior inadequate response or intolerance to treatment with tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFis) [6–10]. Consistency of response to guselkumab has also been demonstrated across baseline demographic and clinical characteristics [11]. While baseline characteristics have not shown differences in efficacy across patients with PsA, data concerning response to guselkumab in patients from specific geographic locations and genetic backgrounds are limited.

The prevalence of PsA in Russia was estimated to be approximately 12.4 cases per 100,000 people in 2013, although large differences exist in the incidence of PsA between different regions within the country [12]. The global prevalence of PsA reported in 2018 was notably higher, i.e., 133 per 100,000 people [13]. Additionally, one-third of Russian patients with psoriasis were found to report joint pain, while only 8% were diagnosed with PsA [12].

Data on the use of advanced therapies, including biologics, to treat active PsA in Russia are limited. This post hoc analysis pooled data from DISCOVER-1 and -2, assessed the efficacy and safety of guselkumab in biologic-naïve patients with active PsA residing in Russia, and compared these data with data from patients in the rest of the world (RoW). This post hoc analysis was possible owing to the large number of patients recruited into the DISCOVER-1 and -2 trials from sites in Russia.

Methods

Study Design

Full details of the phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicenter DISCOVER clinical trials of guselkumab in patients with active PsA have been published previously [6, 7]. DISCOVER-1 (NCT03162796) allowed for about 30% of enrolled patients to have previously received one or two TNFis, with the remaining study population being biologic-naïve patients, while DISCOVER-2 (NCT03158285) evaluated only biologic-naïve patients. This analysis included only biologic-naïve patients from both DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2.

Patients either received guselkumab 100 mg every 4 weeks (guselkumab Q4W), guselkumab 100 mg at week 0, week 4, then every 8 weeks (guselkumab Q8W), or placebo (PBO) through week 24 followed by crossover to guselkumab 100 mg Q4W (PBO → Q4W). Patients in the crossover group received guselkumab through week 48 in DISCOVER-1 and up to week 100 in DISCOVER-2 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Participants

DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2 inclusion and exclusion criteria have been previously reported [6, 7]. In brief, patients were eligible if they were ≥ 18 years of age, had active PsA despite conventional therapy, and had a current or documented history of plaque psoriasis. Active PsA was defined by swollen joint count (SJC) ≥ 3 and tender joint count (TJC) ≥ 3 at screening with C-reactive protein (CRP) ≥ 0.3 mg/dL in DISCOVER-1, and by SJC ≥ 5 and TJC ≥ 5 at screening with CRP ≥ 0.6 mg/dL in DISCOVER-2.

Assessments

Joints were evaluated for tenderness (0–68) and swelling (0–66, excluding hips), enthesitis (Leeds Enthesitis Index 0–6), and dactylitis (0–3 per digit; total score 0–60) by an independent joint assessor. Samples were collected for determination of serum CRP levels (mg/dL). Patients reported their pain level (Patient Global Assessment of Disease Activity [0–10 cm visual analogue scale (VAS)]), and physical function (Health Assessment Questionnaire–Disability Index [HAQ-DI] 0–3).

The extent and severity of skin psoriasis was assessed using the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI; scored as 0–72), which considers the body surface area (BSA) affected by psoriasis, and the degree of redness, scaling, and induration.

Symptoms of axial involvement in patients with imaging-confirmed sacroiliitis (investigator-identified) were assessed using the Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score (ASDAS) and the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI). The ASDAS (score 0 = no disease activity; no maximum score) considers peripheral and back pain, CRP levels, morning stiffness, and patient-reported disease activity [14]. The BASDAI (0–10) is a patient-reported composite score that considers fatigue, spinal pain, peripheral joint involvement, entheseal pain, and morning stiffness [15]. A modified BASDAI (mBASDAI; 0–10) score was calculated by excluding the question related to peripheral joint pain to assess axial skeleton disease activity.

Outcomes

The outcomes assessed in this post hoc analysis were as follows: ≥ 20% and ≥ 50% improvement in the American College of Rheumatology criteria (ACR20 and ACR50, respectively) response; resolution of enthesitis (in patients with enthesitis at baseline) and dactylitis (in patients with dactylitis at baseline); clinically meaningful improvement (≥ 0.35) in HAQ-DI score among patients with a baseline HAQ-DI score of ≥ 0.35; the proportion of participants with a ≥ 90% improvement in PASI score (PASI90) in patients with ≥ 3% BSA involvement and Investigator’s Global Assessment (IGA) score ≥ 2 at baseline; achievement of Disease Activity in Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA) low disease activity (LDA; ≤ 14) or remission (≤ 4); and minimal disease activity (MDA). Additional outcomes were assessed in patients with axial PsA, defined as patients with investigator-confirmed axial PsA and evidence of sacroiliitis on either a previous radiograph or magnetic resonance imaging of sacroiliac joints (DISCOVER-1 and DISCOVER-2), or pelvic radiograph at screening (DISCOVER-2 only) [16]. These outcomes included changes from baseline in ASDAS and in BASDAI, mBASDAI, and patient-reported spinal pain (BASDAI question 2; 0–10) scores. Safety outcomes included adverse events (AEs); serious AEs; AEs leading to discontinuation of study drug; and AEs of interest including infections, serious infections, deaths, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), malignancies, opportunistic infections, tuberculosis (TB), and inflammatory bowel disease.

Statistical Analysis

Data from the biologic-naïve patients in DISCOVER-1 (1-year study) and DISCOVER-2 (2-year study) were pooled through week 52. Data at week 100 derive only from DISCOVER-2 participants.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize data in the Russian and RoW cohorts. Data are presented as means and standard deviation or number and proportion of patients. Baseline characteristics were compared between the cohorts of patients living in Russia and the RoW using the independent samples t test and chi-squared test. Significance was defined as nominal P ≤ 0.05.

Clinical response outcomes were also summarized by geographic location, i.e., for the Russian and RoW cohorts. Non-responder imputation was employed for all binary efficacy endpoints.

For all continuous efficacy endpoints, two mixed models with repeated measures (MMRM) were developed, with each including randomized treatment group, baseline use of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), and the interaction of treatment group with time as fixed effects, and respective baseline levels as a covariate. The first model was applied to pooled data from DISCOVER-1 and -2 to evaluate data through week 52. The second model was applied to DISCOVER-2 data to evaluate week 100 data. Missing data were not imputed prior to the input of data into the model, since under an MMRM model, missing data are predicted by observed data via the model for the conditional mean.

AEs are summarized by Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities system organ class/preferred term and described as the number and proportion of patients experiencing an AE and the number of events per 100 patient-years of follow-up (100 PY).

Compliance with Ethics Guidelines

DISCOVER-1 and -2 were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. The study protocols were approved by ethics committees at each site. All participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Patient Disposition and Baseline Characteristics

The Russian and RoW cohorts comprised 317 (Q4W, n = 119; Q8W, n = 88; PBO → Q4W, n = 110) and 685 (Q4W, n = 216; Q8W, n = 246; PBO → Q4W, n = 223) patients, respectively. Of these, four patients in the Russian cohort (Q4W, n = 3; Q8W, n = 1) and 34 patients in the RoW cohort (Q4W, n = 24; Q8W, n = 10) discontinued between weeks 24 and 52, and a further nine (Q4W, n = 7; Q8W, n = 2) and 28 (Q4W, n = 19; Q8W, n = 9) patients from the Russian and RoW cohorts, respectively, discontinued between weeks 52 and 100 (Fig. 1).

Although baseline patient demographic and disease characteristics were generally consistent between the geographic cohorts, several nominally significant differences were noted. The Russian cohort was somewhat younger (44.9 vs. 46.8 years), had higher CRP levels (1.4 vs. 0.8 mg/dL), and had more severe skin disease (mean PASI score, 12.4 vs. 8.1) at baseline than the RoW cohort. The Russian cohort was also characterized by lower rates of corticosteroid use (12.0% vs. 20.1%) and higher rates of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use (82.6% vs. 56.8%) at baseline (Table 1). Of the 685 patients in the RoW cohort, 82% were recruited from sites in Eastern Europe (Bulgaria, Czech Republic, Estonia, Hungary, Lithuania, Latvia, Poland, and Ukraine).

Efficacy

ACR20 and ACR50 Response

In the Russian cohort, higher proportions of patients receiving guselkumab achieved an ACR20 response at week 24 (Q4W, 60%; Q8W, 51%) vs. patients receiving PBO (30%). ACR20 response rates increased through week 52 (range across guselkumab-randomized patients, 69–78%); at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), ACR20 response rates ranged from 77% to 80% and were consistent between the crossover and guselkumab-randomized groups. Response patterns for the Russian cohort were consistent with the RoW cohort across all timepoints (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 2).

Data for weeks 24 and 52 are from the pooled populations of the DISCOVER-1 and -2 trials. Data for week 100 are only from patients from DISCOVER-2. Data are reported using non-responder imputation. ACR20 ≥ 20% improvement in the American College of Rheumatology criteria, ACR50 ≥ 50% improvement in the American College of Rheumatology criteria, Q4W every 4 weeks, Q8W every 8 weeks, RoW rest of the world

Additionally, trends in ACR50 response were similar to those observed for ACR20 for both cohorts across all timepoints (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 2).

PASI90 Response

In the Russian cohort, rates of PASI90 response in patients with ≥ 3% BSA and IGA ≥ 2 were higher with guselkumab (Q4W, 69%; Q8W, 56%) vs. PBO (7%) at week 24 and rates increased through week 52 (range 72–75%); at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), the rate of PASI90 response was 62–74% across the treatment groups. Response rates in the crossover group were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients. Response patterns for the Russian cohort were consistent with the RoW cohort across all timepoints (Supplementary Table 1).

Resolution of Enthesitis and Dactylitis (in Patients with Domain Involvement at Baseline)

In the Russian cohort, higher proportions of patients receiving guselkumab achieved complete resolution of enthesitis and dactylitis at week 24 vs. patients receiving PBO. Between weeks 24 and 52, the rates of enthesitis and dactylitis resolution increased across all treatment groups; at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), the proportion of guselkumab-randomized patients with enthesitis resolution was 59–67% and with dactylitis resolution was 74–91%. At week 100, rates of dactylitis resolution were higher in the Russian vs. the RoW cohort; resolution of enthesitis and dactylitis was generally consistent between the Russian and RoW cohorts at all other timepoints. In both cohorts, response rates in the crossover groups were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 3).

Patient-Reported Outcomes

HAQ-DI Scores

In the Russian cohort with baseline HAQ-DI ≥ 0.35, the proportion of patients who achieved a clinically meaningful improvement of ≥ 0.35 in HAQ-DI score was higher with guselkumab (Q4W, 51%; Q8W, 46%) vs. PBO (33%) at week 24. These proportions increased through week 52 (range 49–60%); at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), the proportion of guselkumab-randomized patients achieving an improvement of ≥ 0.35 in HAQ-DI score generally improved from week 52. Response rates in the crossover group were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients. Response patterns in the Russian cohort were consistent with the RoW cohort at all timepoints (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 4).

Patient-Reported Pain

In the Russian cohort, improvements in the least squares mean changes in patient pain VAS scores from baseline were larger for patients receiving guselkumab (Q4W, − 4.49; Q8W, − 6.28) vs. PBO (− 1.56) at week 4. Improvements continued through week 52 in guselkumab-randomized patients (range − 26.98 to − 27.69); at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), least squares mean changes in patient pain VAS scores from baseline ranged from − 34.53 to − 35.30. Response rates in the crossover group were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients. Changes in patient-reported pain in the Russian cohort were consistent with those in the RoW cohort through week 100 (Supplementary Table 1).

Composite Measures of Disease Activity

DAPSA LDA/Remission

In the Russian cohort, higher proportions of patients receiving guselkumab achieved DAPSA LDA/remission at week 24 (Q4W, 31%; Q8W, 33%) vs. patients receiving PBO (19%). These proportions increased through week 52 (52% for both guselkumab-randomized groups); at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), the proportion of guselkumab-randomized patients achieving DAPSA LDA/remission was 60–65%. Response rates in the crossover group were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients. Rates of DAPSA LDA/remission were generally lower in the Russian vs. the RoW cohort through week 52 and were consistent across the two cohorts at week 100 (Supplementary Table 1).

Least squares mean changes in all individual DAPSA component scores, including CRP levels, improved over time through week 100 (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Minimal Disease Activity

In the Russian cohort, higher proportions of guselkumab-treated patients achieved MDA at week 24 (Q4W, 18%; Q8W, 19%) vs. patients receiving PBO (6%). These proportions increased through week 52 (range 31–34%); at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), the rate of MDA was 39–44% across all treatment groups. Response rates in the crossover group were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients. Response patterns in the Russian cohort were consistent with the RoW cohort at all timepoints (Supplementary Table 1, Fig. 5).

Symptoms of Axial Involvement

In the Russian cohort, improvement in BASDAI scores in patients with imaging-confirmed axial involvement at baseline was higher with guselkumab (Q4W, − 1.96; Q8W, − 1.98) vs. PBO (− 0.62) at week 24; at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), changes in the guselkumab-randomized patients ranged from − 2.32 to − 2.53. Response rates in the crossover group were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients. Changes in BASDAI scores were smaller in the Russian cohort vs. the RoW cohort across all timepoints (Supplementary Table 1; Fig. 6), and similar trends were observed in mBASDAI and ASDAS scores through week 100 (Supplementary Fig. S3).

In the Russian cohort, patient-reported spinal pain also improved (measured by least squares mean changes in patient-reported spinal pain) through week 24 in patients with axial PsA (Q4W, − 1.65; Q8W, − 2.45; PBO, − 0.24). Improvements continued through week 52 (range − 1.88 to − 2.36); at week 100 (DISCOVER-2), changes in patient-reported spinal pain were − 2.42 to − 2.37 for guselkumab-randomized patients. Response rates in the crossover group were consistent with those in guselkumab-randomized patients. Changes in patient-reported spinal pain were smaller in the Russian cohort vs. the RoW cohort across all timepoints (Supplementary Table 1).

Safety

Rates of AEs with guselkumab were similar to those with PBO through week 24 and similar across all treatment groups in the Russian cohort (Table 2). The rate of AE occurrence at week 24 was 146.9 per 100 PYs (n = 83, 40.1%) in patients treated with guselkumab, vs. 162.4 per 100 PYs (n = 47, 42.7%) in patients receiving PBO. The rate of patients experiencing MACE was 1.0 per 100 PYs (n = 1, 0.5%) in patients treated with guselkumab. Among patients receiving guselkumab, the rate of patients experiencing serious AEs was 3.1 per 100 PYs (n = 3, 1.4%) and the rate of patients experiencing an AE leading to drug discontinuation was 3.1 per 100 PYs (n = 3, 1.4%). Amongst patients receiving PBO, these rates were 5.9 per 100 PYs (n = 3, 2.7%) and 3.9 per 100 PYs (n = 2, 1.8%), respectively. No increase in time-adjusted incidence rates was observed through week 52.

Through week 112 in DISCOVER-2, the rate of AE occurrence among patients treated with guselkumab was 89.7 per 100 PYs (n = 170, 62.7%). Among patients receiving guselkumab, the rate of patients experiencing serious AEs was 4.5 per 100 PYs (n = 21, 7.7%) and the rate of patients experiencing an AE leading to drug discontinuation was 0.9 per 100 PYs (n = 5, 1.8%).

No deaths or malignancies occurred, and no cases of inflammatory bowel disease, opportunistic infection, or active TB were reported.

Discussion

This is the first analysis to evaluate the long-term efficacy of guselkumab treatment across PsA disease domains in a Russian cohort.

Across PsA domains and patient-reported outcomes, broadly similar short- and long-term efficacy with guselkumab vs. PBO was demonstrated in the Russian and RoW biologic-naïve cohorts in this post hoc analysis. Findings were consistent with those seen in the overall DISCOVER-1 and -2 populations [6, 7]. Consistent safety findings were also observed for the Russian cohort and the overall population of DISCOVER-1 and -2 [6, 7], as well as with findings of a pooled analysis of four randomized clinical trials in PsA and of 11 randomized clinical trials in both psoriasis and PsA [17, 18].

The proportion of patients with axial involvement and baseline BASDAI scores were similar between the Russian and RoW cohorts. The Russian cohort had a higher proportion of male patients, lower mean age, and higher baseline CRP levels vs. the RoW cohort, all of which have been associated with an improved response to biologic therapy [19, 20]. As such, it is unexpected that the Russian cohort experienced smaller improvements in axial disease outcomes than the RoW cohort. Further investigation is needed to explore the reasons behind this.

Of note, there were no reported cases of TB in this analysis. Russia is known to have a high incidence rate of TB (47 cases per 100,000 people in 2021 vs. 10.6 cases per 100,000 people in the European Union and European Economic Area) [21, 22]. Given this, the absence of any cases of TB in the Russian population supports the safety of guselkumab in regions of endemic TB.

The use of NSAIDs was higher, and the use of corticosteroids was lower, in the Russian cohort than in the RoW cohort of this post hoc analysis. The analysis population was biologic-naïve and so no insights into the potential use of biologic therapy in Russian clinical practice could be obtained. Data from the Russian healthcare system reported that the mean duration of disease for patients with PsA before biologics were prescribed was 12.5 years [23]. While these data suggest a delay before advanced therapies are employed, this could neither be confirmed nor compared with the situation in the RoW because of limited supporting clinical and real-world data. The ongoing registry, RU-PsART, may provide important insights into Russian treatment and management practices for patients with PsA in the future; to date, analyses from this registry have predominantly focused on patient clinical characteristics, including the presence of nail psoriasis, PsA duration, quality of life, and work impairment, rather than types of or response to treatments received [24–26].

In the absence of Russian PsA management guidelines, data on the efficacy and safety of different therapies in patients with PsA in Russia are needed to inform clinical decision-making. At present, data for biologic therapies are lacking; to our knowledge, only one such analysis has been conducted examining patients’ responses to treatment with secukinumab in clinical trials [27]. Current international guidelines such as the Group for Research and Assessment of Psoriasis and Psoriatic Arthritis emphasize a multi-domain approach in the assessment and treatment of PsA [28]. As such, it is important that therapies show efficacy across the range of clinical outcomes.

Strengths of this analysis include the use of validated outcome measures and conservative analyses applying non-responder imputation to a large Russian population from a randomized clinical trial. Additionally, the responses of patients to other biologic DMARDs have not been analyzed in patients with PsA from Russia in a clinical trial setting.

Limitations of this analysis include the post hoc nature and the high proportion of patients in the RoW population who were enrolled in Eastern Europe (82%), which may contribute to the consistency in response between the Russian and RoW cohorts.

Conclusion

This post hoc analysis demonstrates that the safety profile and efficacy of guselkumab treatment across PsA domains in patients enrolled in Russia are consistent with those seen in patients from the RoW. Taken together, these data affirm that patients in Russia respond in a similar way to patients from the RoW when treated with guselkumab.
