Economic and Resource Use–Related Guidance in US Medical Societies’ Clinical Practice Guidelines
Authors: Anand R. Habib, Omar Qureshi, Reshma Ramachandran, Joseph S. Ross
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIn an era emphasizing value-based care, what proportion of recent clinical practice guideline recommendations include economic or resource use–related guidance?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 309 clinical practice guidelines and 7582 recommendations, 14 of 23 large US-based medical societies explicitly suggested that economic or resource use–related evidence be considered when developing guidelines, 22.5% of recommendations issued from 2019 to 2023 narratively discussed economic or resource use–related evidence, and 3.8% had an upfront economic or resource use–related statement.MeaningThese findings suggest that more high-quality, health-economic evidence and clarification by societies as to how they appraise such evidence may allow clinical practice guidelines to better inform value-based care.

Introduction

US health care expenditures in 2024 exceeded $5.3 trillion, a 7.2% year-over-year increase.1 The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) anticipate that health care spending growth will continue to outpace growth in gross domestic product through 2033, further straining household and government budgets.2 Private and public payors have responded by embracing value-based care through alternative payment mechanisms (eg, accountable care organizations, episode-based bundled payments). In contrast to fee-for-service reimbursement, value-based care compels health care organizations and clinicians to consider economic data or resource use relative to anticipated marginal health benefits when providing patients with diagnostic and therapeutic services.3 For example, CMS’ merit-based incentive payment system (MIPS) ties claims reimbursement partly to the assessed costs and quality of care provided to Medicare beneficiaries.4 With rising out-of-pocket expenses and resultant health care-related financial toxicity, patients are also increasingly expecting their health care clinicians to practice value-based care.5,6

Recognizing these demands, medical specialty societies (ie, professional organizations that issue clinical practice guidelines [CPGs], provide continuing medical education, and advocate for their clinician members’ interests regarding reimbursement rates and health care policies) have launched efforts to promote value-based care. For instance, medical societies educate members about essential practice-affecting components of value-based care,7 inform the development and selection of reimbursement-related MIPS quality measures,8 and convene stakeholders to further solutions to patients’ medical debt.9 Additionally, from 2012 to 2023, more than 80 medical societies participated in the American Board of Internal Medicine’s Choosing Wisely campaign, through which they identified examples of low-value clinical services.10

Despite a decade of medical societies’ positioning themselves as leaders in shaping value-based care, little is known about whether these organizations systematically address economic or resource use–related considerations in their CPGs. Examining CPGs offers insights into how societies normatively view economic or resource use–related considerations in clinical care and whether they are providing guidance that goes well beyond the 700 services highlighted through the Choosing Wisely campaign. By scrutinizing CPGs issued by a diverse set of US-based medical specialty societies, we therefore sought to determine the proportion of CPG recommendations that include economic or resource use–related guidance and assess factors associated with recommendations’ incorporation of their considerations.

Methods

This cross-sectional study was prepared according to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.11 This study used publicly available data and was not human participant research and did not require ethics review or informed consent, per the Common Rule.

Data Sources and Sampling of Medical Specialty Societies and CPG Documents

Informed by prior literature12 and data regarding medical specialties with the greatest number of practicing physicians in the US,13 we identified 24, US-based medical specialty societies with at least 10 000 dues-paying members. We used the Emergency Care Research Institute’s (ECRI) Guidelines Trust database to identify all CPGs issued by these societies from January 1, 2019, through December 31, 2023.14 This database includes English-language CPG documents produced by medical specialty societies or similar organizations, predicated on a rigorous, systematic evidence review, and that provide recommendation statements with a narrative or tabular synthesis of relevant evidence.15 These criteria align with the Institute of Medicine’s (IOM) 2011 CPG development standards.16

We reviewed documents to determine that all were CPGs; documents establishing appropriate use criteria were excluded. For feasibility, we limited our analysis to no more than 25 randomly selected CPG documents per society. To remove duplicates, CPGs jointly developed by more than 1 medical society were attributed to either the society in whose official journal the CPG document was published (eg, American College of Physicians for consensus guidelines published in Annals of Internal Medicine) or based on the specialty affiliation of the CPG document’s first author.

Categorization of How Societies Suggested Economic or Resource Use–Related Evidence Be Considered in CPGs

Our main explanatory variable was whether a society suggested that economic or resource use–related evidence be considered when formulating recommendations. For this, we searched each society’s website for the most recently published manual outlining the methods its guideline-writing committees are instructed to follow in developing CPG recommendations. Adapting an approach from prior studies,12,17 we examined each society’s methods manual for language describing how the society suggested economic or resource use–related evidence be considered when developing recommendations. Two study team members (A.R.H. and O.Q.) independently reviewed and categorized each society as (1) explicitly suggesting economic or resource use–related should be considered, (2) ambiguously addressing how such evidence should be considered, (3) excluding such evidence, or (4) not addressing how economic or resource use–related evidence should be considered.

Identification of Economic or Resource Use–Related Considerations in Societies’ Recommendations

Our 2 outcomes of interest were the presence of a composite measure of economic or resource use–related considerations either in the upfront clinical practice recommendation summary statement (“upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statement”) or the narrative discussion summarizing evidence supporting the upfront recommendation statement (“narrative discussion of economic or resource use–related evidence”). If a recommendation had an upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statement, we considered it as also having a narrative discussion of economic or resource use–related evidence (ie, the outcome of upfront statements is nested within the outcome of narrative discussions). These outcomes could be considered different levels of visibility for economic or resource use–related guidance, with upfront statements potentially more actionable for CPG readers. Adapting a strategy used in prior studies,18,19 for each CPG document’s recommendations, we searched for economic or resource use–related terms including cost*, price, expen* (eg, expense, expensive, expenditure), afford* (eg, afford, affordable), financ* (eg, finance, financial), resource, dollar, money, value, and econom* (eg, economic, economical). These terms are not synonymous.20 However, as a composite measure, they offer a sensitive, but not specific, assessment of economic or resource use–related considerations in recommendations. For each relevant recommendation, authors (A.R.H. and/or O.Q.) manually confirmed that these terms’ usage signified an economic or resource use–related consideration (eg, value in economic terms rather than as connoting a preference, as in patients’ values). As we specifically sought to determine whether the presence of economic or resource use–related considerations were present for each clinical recommendation, we excluded mention of such considerations from general sections of CPG documents (eg, introduction, future research, references).

Other Recommendation-Level, Guideline-Level, and Society-Level Characteristics

We also extracted data related to recommendation-level, guideline-level, and society-level characteristics. At the recommendation level, we determined the intervention type (diagnostic, therapeutic, or both) and intervention setting (outpatient or ambulatory, inpatient, or both). At the CPG-document level, we noted the publication year, whether it was disease-focused (eg, myocardial infarction vs chest pain), pediatric-focused, COVID-19-focused, or developed by multisociety consensus, and its clinical emphasis (prevention, symptom or disease management, or both). At the society level, apart from how the society suggested whether economic or resource use–related evidence be considered, we characterized societies as representing primary care vs specialty care.

Two study team members (A.R.H. and O.Q.) independently extracted data from each society’s CPG methods manual and for recommendations from 140 (45%) CPG documents randomly selected across all societies. For these 140 documents, there was 91.5% agreement between the 2 reviewers across all extracted data elements. For the remaining 8.5% of data, authors resolved differences by negotiated consensus. The 2 reviewers divided the remaining 169 CPG documents and singly extracted data using the consensus approach. A separate analysis of the concordance between the strength of recommendation and level of evidence from this cohort of recommendations has been previously reported.21

Statistical Analyses

Using descriptive statistics, we report characteristics for societies, for recommendations, and for our 2 nested outcomes—upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statements and narrative economic or resource use–related evidence discussions. Next, we fit multilevel logistic regression models with fixed effects for recommendation characteristics and a random intercept for guideline document. Variables with a P < .20 in univariable analyses were included in a backward stepwise multilevel multivariable regression model, which used likelihood ratio tests comparing nested models to sequentially remove variables with a removal criterion of P > .05. We conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding recommendations produced by multisociety consensus to assess for the potential of bias based on our classification of which society primarily led the effort. We reported odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and considered 2-sided P ≤ .05 to be statistically significant. We conducted analyses using StataMP version 19 (StataCorp LP). Data were extracted between April and August 2024 and analyzed from September 2024 to April 2025.

Results

Our final sample consisted of 309 CPG documents issued by 23 medical societies (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). The median (IQR) number of CPG documents per society was 14 (8-17.5) documents. Each CPG document contained a median of 15 (8-27) recommendations.

Characteristics of Societies

Median (IQR) membership for the 23 medical societies was 25 000 (17 500-44 500) (Table 1). Three societies (13.0%) represented primary-care fields. Based on their methods manuals for CPG development, 14 societies (60.9%) explicitly suggested that economic or resource use–related evidence be considered when developing recommendations, 6 (26.1%) ambiguously addressed whether such evidence be considered, and 3 (13.0%) did not address consideration of such evidence (Table 2 and eTable 1 in Supplement 1).

Characteristics of Recommendations

Of 7582 recommendations overall, 5017 recommendations (66.2%) pertained to therapeutic interventions, and 4375 (57.7%) pertained to outpatient or ambulatory care (Table 3). There were 5672 disease-specific recommendations (74.8%), 6629 (87.4%) provided guidance on symptom and/or disease management, 561 (7.4%) were pediatric-focused, 268 (3.5%) were COVID-19-focused, and 4080 (53.8%) were developed by multisociety consensus. The 3 primary care societies issued 286 (3.8%) recommendations. Societies that explicitly suggested that economic or resource use–related evidence be considered issued 3536 recommendations (46.6%).

Frequency of Upfront Economic or Resource Use–Related Statements and Narrative Discussions of Economic or Resource Use–Related Evidence

At the CPG document-level, 71 of 309 documents (23.0%) had at least 1 recommendation with an upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statement, and 236 guideline documents (76.4%) had at least 1 recommendation with a narrative discussion of economic or resource-use related evidence. Table 2 and eTable 2 and 3 in Supplement 1 provide illustrative examples. The median (IQR) number of recommendations per document with upfront statements was 0 (0-0), whereas the median (IQR) number of recommendations per document with narrative discussions was 3 (1-7). Among the 7582 recommendations, 287 (3.8%) contained upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statements, and 1706 (22.5%) had narrative economic or resource use–related evidence discussions.

The presence of recommendation-level economic or resource use–related considerations varied by how societies suggested whether economic or resource use–related evidence be considered (eTable 4 and eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Of the 3536 recommendations published by the 14 societies that explicitly suggested economic or resource use–related evidence be considered, 158 (4.5%) contained upfront economic or resource use–related statements, and 1223 (34.6%) had narrative economic or resource use–related evidence discussions. In contrast, of the 740 recommendations published by the 3 societies that did not address how economic or resource use–related evidence be considered, 2 recommendations (0.3%) had upfront statements and 76 (10.3%) had narrative evidence discussions. The proportion of recommendations with upfront economic or resource use–related statements or related narrative discussions varied across societies (eFigure 3 in Supplement 1).

Factors Associated With Upfront Statements and Narrative Discussions With Economic or Resource Use–Related Considerations

In adjusted analyses examining the presence of an upfront economic or resource use–related statement (Table 4), recommendations that were treatment-related (196 of 5017 recommendations [3.9%]; adjusted OR, [aOR] 1.75; 95% CI, 1.18-2.59; P = .005) compared to diagnostic-related (74 of 2293 recommendations [3.2%]), and those issued by primary care societies (76 of 286 recommendations [26.6%]; aOR, 12.81; 95% CI, 3.37-48.70; P < .001) compared with specialty care societies (211 of 7296 recommendations [2.9%]) had higher odds of offering upfront economic or resource use–related statements, while multisociety recommendations (58 of 4080 recommendations [1.4%]) compared with single society recommendations (229 of 3502 recommendations [6.5%]) had lower odds (aOR, 0.18; 95% CI, 0.07-0.47; P < .001). How a society suggested whether economic or resource use–related evidence be considered was not a significant factor in presence of upfront economic or resource use–related statements.

Discussion

In this analysis of 7582 CPG recommendations issued by 23 large US-based medical societies from 2019 through 2023, only 22.5% of recommendations narratively discussed economic or resource use–related evidence, and 3.8% of recommendations provided an upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statement. Recommendations issued by societies that, according to their CPG methods manuals, explicitly suggested that economic or resource use–related evidence be considered had 6-fold greater odds of discussing such evidence but did not demonstrate greater odds of providing upfront economic or resource use–related statements. In contrast, although primary care societies’ recommendations did not have greater odds of discussing economic or resource use–related evidence, they had nearly 13-fold higher odds of providing upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statements.

With $100 billion in health care expenditures arising from low-value care and 4-in-5 physicians eager to follow value-based care recommendations within CPGs,22,23 societies may be increasingly recognizing the relevance of economic or resource use–related considerations. A 2013 cross-sectional study of 30 US-based medical societies reported that 3 societies excluded economic or resource use–related considerations when developing CPGs, and 6 never addressed how economic or resource use–related evidence be considered.12 In comparison, only 3 of the 23 societies we examined did not address how economic or resource use–related evidence be considered, and no societies excluded such evidence. Consequently, 76.4% of CPG documents examined contained at least 1 economic or resource use–related narrative discussion, compared with the 25.7% observed in a 2002 investigation.24

There are several potential reasons for the tension between the findings that three-fourths of CPG documents had at least 1 economic or resource use–related evidence discussion and that, overall, only 22.5% of individual recommendations discussed economic or resource use–related evidence. First, existing literature suggests a relative paucity of high-quality, economic or resource use–related evidence related to the type of clinical questions typically broached by CPGs and that such evidence may quickly become outdated (eg, the cost of a medication decreases upon becoming generic).25,26 Additionally, there is no established consensus as to whose perspective matters (eg, that of individual patients, society at-large, accountable care organizations, insurers, or governments) when adjudicating economic or resource use–related matters. Furthermore, the large societies that we examined produce CPGs whose intended audience is cross-national; the applicability of economic or resource use–related evidence may depend on the particularities of how a country’s delivery system is financed and organized (eg, single payer vs market-based).25 To preserve the generalizability and durability of their recommendations, CPG writing committees may either exclude or defer discussing economic or resource use–related evidence unless it is high quality and unambiguous. Even when reliable economic or resource use–related evidence exists, further methodological challenges may arise. Conducting a systemic review of economic or resource use–related evidence can be time and resource intensive, and not all guideline writing committees may have the expertise and comfort in appraising such evidence.25 Although more societies note grading quality of evidence based on a rubric (eg, Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) that acknowledges the relevance of economic or resource use–related evidence in formulating CPGs, prior research suggests the application of these frameworks to such evidence may be inconsistent or challenging.27

The case of primary care societies is potentially illustrative of the upsides to meeting these evidentiary and methodological barriers head-on. All three primary care societies we examined explicitly suggested that economic or resource use–related evidence be considered. To address methodological ambiguities in applying GRADE to economic or resource use–related evidence, the American College of Physicians (ACP) has even published its own framework for incorporating health economic evidence into CPGs.28 The commitment to systematically considering economic or resource use–related evidence has positioned primary care societies to be responsive to their primary care clinician members’ needs. Prior literature has suggested that primary care clinicians would need 27 hours per day to adhere to all guideline recommended care.29 This coupled with increasing attribution of patients to primary care clinicians within accountable care organizations with associated financial risk likely heightens the priority for primary care societies to offer legible, upfront economic or resource use–related recommendation statements for their member clinicians’ benefit. Moving from evidence to decision for recommendation statements is a subjective enterprise30 and not all societies may prioritize or feel confident providing such clear economic or resource use–related upfront statements. Still, given increasingly ubiquitous concerns regarding the sustainability and affordability of US health care spending, committing to more explicit, systematic appraisal of economic or resource use–related evidence may allow additional medical societies to more effectively inform discussions regarding value-based care.

Limitations

This study has limitations. First, we based our assessment of economic or resource use–related considerations at the recommendation level on the binary presence of 1 of several economic or resource use–related terms, aggregated into a composite measure of economic or resource use–related considerations. We recognize those terms are not synonymous20 but were deliberately generous in our assessment of economic or resource use–related considerations. A more conservative approach would likely find a lower proportion of recommendations containing economic or resource use–related guidance. Similarly, we did not appraise the interpretability or usability of identified considerations for CPG end-users, although, we hypothesize that economic or resource use–related guidance in upfront recommendation statements may be more actionable than when only included in narrative discussions. Future work clarifying best lexicographic practices in discussing economic or resource use considerations (eg, when to use the term value vs cost-effective) may help to meaningfully disaggregate this composite measure and improve the interpretability of such statements. Second, as we drew our sample from the ECRI Guidelines Trust (which limits entry to CPGs meeting IOM’s 2011 CPG standards), our findings may not generalize to all CPGs produced by the societies we examined if those CPGs did not meet the IOM standards or to other large, US-based medical societies or disease-oriented societies. Third, for the 3 societies (American Gastroenterological Association, American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and American Society of Clinical Oncology) that produced more than 25 CPG documents from 2019 to 2023 and for which we randomly sampled 25 documents per society, proportions of recommendations with the 2 outcomes may not generalize to recommendations contained in other CPG documents these societies published from 2019 to 2023.

Conclusions

Although an increasing proportion of US-based medical societies explicitly suggested that economic or resource use–related evidence be considered when developing CPGs, only 22.5% of recommendations discussed such evidence in this cross-sectional study. With higher quality health-economic evidence and societies clarifying how they appraise such evidence, medical societies’ CPGs could better inform value-based care amidst rising health care costs.
