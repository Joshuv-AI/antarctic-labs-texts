Delayed Diagnosis of Pyogenic Spondylitis After Impalement by Steel Rods: Case Report
Authors: Myoung Soo Kim, Seok Hwa Youn, Seonpyo Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPenetrating spine injuries associated with abdominal and thoracic injuries caused by steel bars are rare. We report a unique case of delayed spinal spondylitis after penetrating abdominal and thoracic injuries from multiple steel bars. A 65-year-old man fell from a 20-m high scaffolding onto vertical steel bars. Steel bars penetrated his scrotum, abdomen, thoracic cavity, thigh, calf, and left ankle. At the accident scene, a fellow worker removed multiple penetrating bars. The first operation included repairs to the liver, diaphragm, transverse colon, sigmoid colon, jejunum, scrotum, and the pelvic wound, as well as open reduction and internal fixation of the left ankle. In a second operation, surgeons resected the sigmoid colon with anastomosis, and then sutured and debrided the peritoneum, mesentery, and the right thigh and calf wounds. The patient complained of anorexia and melena a month and a half after the trauma. Chest computed tomography 57 days after the trauma demonstrated pyogenic spondylitis in the thoracic spine. Culture of a perivertebral abscess 2 months after trauma revealed Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Antibiotic treatment resolved the pyogenic spondylitis. High clinical suspicion and awareness of delayed pyogenic spondylitis support early diagnosis and improve outcomes when appropriate treatment is given.Keywords: Spondylitis, Spondylitis, Foreign body, Penetrating, Abdomen

INTRODUCTION

Non-missile penetrating injuries caused by foreign bodies, such as a steel bar, are infrequent. Due to the various locations of penetration, no standardized management strategy exists. However, prehospital care is critical to the survival of these patients. Care must be taken not to remove the penetrating object while the patient is being transported to a nearby hospital where definite treatment is to be provided.1,6) Early removal of the penetrating foreign body might result in fatal hemorrhage and overlooking possible organ injury.9)

Here, we report a patient with a delayed diagnosis of pyogenic spondylitis after penetration injuries by steel bars. In this patient, steel bars penetrated the abdominal viscera and the thoracic cavity. A fellow worker removed multiple impacting steel bars at the accident scene. We overlooked a thoracic vertebral fracture due to steel bar penetration, and a diagnosis of infective spondylitis was delayed. We discuss risk factors, diagnosis, and treatment for delayed infective spondylitis. This study had Institutional Review Board approval from National Medical Center (approval No. NMC 2025-09-036).

CASE REPORT

History

A 65-year-old man working at a construction site fell from 20-m high scaffolding. He was impaled on multiple reinforcement steel bars that penetrated his thigh, calf, scrotum, ankle, and abdominal wall. His coworkers removed the rods from his body immediately after the accident and transferred him to our hospital.

Examination at the emergency department

When we first examined the patient in the emergency room, he was drowsy, and his vital signs showed 60/40 mmHg (blood pressure), 80/minute (heart rate), 20/minute (respiration rate), and a temperature of 36.0°C. External wounds were observed in the right thigh, right calf, scrotum, and left side of anterior abdominal wall. His left ankle demonstrated an open fracture-dislocation. Chest X-ray demonstrated a right pneumothorax. After insertion of a 16 French chest tube, his vital signs were 81/58 mmHg, 80/minute, 34/minute, and 35.0°C, respectively. Rh-positive O blood transfusion was started. Computed tomography (CT) of the left ankle showed both dislocation of the left ankle joint and open fractures of the left distal tibia, talus, and fibula. CT of the abdomen and pelvis showed a right lobe hepatic laceration, probable small bowel perforation with panperitonitis, bilateral lower-lobe lung contusions, a left pleural effusion, and chest and abdominal wall emphysema. Chest CT showed acute fractures at the posterior left 9th and 10th ribs and the left T9 transverse process, with a small left hemothorax (FIGURE 1A & B). Sagittal CT of the right chest demonstrated liver laceration and right lung damage (FIGURE 1C). Sagittal and coronal CT of the left chest showed a suspicious diaphragm rupture (FIGURE 1D & E). We identified that four bars penetrated body of this patient. One bar penetrated from right calf to right thigh. Two bars damaged abdominal and thoracic organs. Last bar impacted left ankle.

Hospital course in the first admission

Multiple surgeries were performed. In the emergency operation, the liver, diaphragm, transverse colon, sigmoid colon, jejunum, scrotum, and pelvic wound were repaired. In the first operation, multiple perforations of the small intestine, its mesentery, the transverse colon, and the sigmoid colon were identified. One trajectory of penetrating injury extended from the scrotum, small bowel, transverse colon, liver, diaphragm, and right lung (FIGURE 2). While exploring a second trajectory from left side of abdominal wall, we identified a tract that involved the abdominal wall, sigmoid colon, and mesentery of the small intestine (FIGURE 2). In the retrogastric space, finger exploration of the trajectory tract could only be entered up to 5 cm. The surgeon thought that the second trajectory had stopped in the retrogastric space. Open reduction and internal fixation of the left ankle were undertaken. Postoperatively, tazolactam (piperacillin–tazobactam [4 g/0.5 g]; Ildong Pharmaceutical Co., Seoul, Korea) was administered for 10 days. In a second operation on the day following the first operation, surgeons resected the sigmoid colon and created an anastomosis, then sutured and debrided the peritoneum, mesentery, and wounds of the scrotum, right thigh, and calf. After diagnosis of pulmonary embolism using chest CT 3 days after the accident, the attending physician prescribed enoxaparin (Clexane; Sanofi-Aventis Korea, Seoul, Korea) 60 mg subcutaneous injection twice daily for 10 days and rivaroxaban (Xarelto; Bayer Korea, Seoul, Korea) 20 mg per os once daily. After being treated for peritonitis and hypotensive shock following the first hospitalization, the patient recovered enough to speak three days later. At this time, neurological examination revealed no additional muscle weakness other than limited movement due to penetrating wounds to the right thigh and calf and limited movement due to a fracture of the left ankle. The patient also did not complain of paresthesia. During hospitalization, the patient complained of severe pain around the left ankle. The patient was discharged 29 days after the trauma.

Hospital course in the second admission

The patient was readmitted for recurrent dislocation of the left ankle. Reoperation (open reduction and internal fixation) for the left ankle was undertaken 43 days after the trauma. The patient complained of anorexia since the second admission. At 57 days after the trauma, he was anemic (hemoglobin 6.6 g/dL); the white blood cell (WBC) count was 7,200/µL, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated (140.1 mg/L). On a detailed neurological examination, the patient did not complain of back pain or sensory abnormalities, and aside from left ankle pain and left ankle movement impairment, there was no reported muscle weakness. Before rivaroxaban was discontinued, a chest CT was obtained to evaluate for pulmonary embolism. This CT demonstrated pyogenic spondylitis in the thoracic spine (FIGURE 3). Thoracic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) 58 days after the trauma showed pyogenic spondylitis (FIGURE 4). Physicians discontinued rivaroxaban. A culture of percutaneous aspirate in the abscess 2 months after the trauma revealed Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Antibiotic treatment for P. aeruginosa including tazolactam administered for one month, a combination of ciprofloxacin (HK Inno.N, Cheongju, Korea) and ceftazidime (HK Inno.N) for 15 days, ciprofloxacin for 1 month, and ciprofloxacin hydrochloride (Cycin; Ildong Pharmaceutical Co.) for 18 days resolved the pyogenic spondylitis. WBC and CRP of the peripheral blood returned to within reference ranges after antibiotic treatment. At 102 days after the accident, he underwent a third operation (ankle fusion) for his left ankle.

DISCUSSION

Infective spondylitis development in penetrating injury

The most important risk factor for infection in penetrating abdominal injuries is hollow viscus injury; colonic perforation carries the highest risk of infection among intra-abdominal organ injuries.2) Sarica et al.8) reviewed several steel rod impalement injuries involving the spine. They reported that 5 of 10 cases were followed by infection. Tokushige et al.9) suggested that if the rod first penetrates the intestine and then the spine, it would result in an increased infection rate; whereas, if the rod penetrated the spine before entering the intra-abdominal spaces, no infection occurred.

With a foreign body in place, it is always easier to identify the injured organs, thus avoiding the oversight of any organ injury during treatment.1,4) Once a penetrating object is removed, it is difficult to identify the exact injury track, even by radiological examination, such as in our case.9) The right bar in FIGURE 2A clearly penetrated the scrotum, abdominal organs, right side of diaphragm, and right lung as seen in imaging findings and abdominal surgical findings. However, it is difficult to accurately determine the terminal portion of the penetration path of the left bar in FIGURE 2A.

During the first abdominal operation on our patient, the surgeon identified the distal tract produced by a left steel bar using finger exploration in the retrogastric area. The finger advanced about 5 cm along the tract in the retrogastric area, but could not be passed further. The surgeon judged that the left steel bar in FIGURE 2A had not perforated the left diaphragm or the thoracic spine. However, we identified rupture of left side of diaphragm via retrospective evaluation of chest CT after development of spondylitis (FIGURE 1D & E). Additionally, if the right and left bars were at the same height, it can be assumed that the left rebar is located at the thoracic spine.

We initially thought that acute fractures in the posterior of the left 9th and 10th ribs and the left transverse process of T9 were not penetrating injuries. Suspicion of penetrating injury to the thoracic spine and rib is most important. This patient had fallen from a 20-m height and landed with his trunk and extremities in a crouched posture. The tracts of penetration might not have been in a straight line while the patient was supine. Therefore, in patients with a penetrating injury caused by steel bars, all fractures and wounds discovered in the emergency department should be considered potential results of the penetrating injury. If steel bars had not been removed at the accident scene, we may have been able to identify the exact tracts of the penetrating injuries. If foreign bodies are too long to be transported, they must be cut to an appropriate length.6)

Diagnosis and treatment of infective spondylitis in penetrating injury

In non-missile penetrating spinal injuries, delayed neurological deficit may result from a retained weapon, infection, edema, or cerebrospinal fluid leak.5) The classic symptoms for spinal infection includes back pain, fever, and neurologic sign. In our patient, left ankle pain, multiple injuries, and multiple operations masked the symptoms of spine infection. During the initial admission, the focus was on treating peritonitis and hemorrhagic shock. Antibiotics were administered from the time of initial admission for 10 days post-surgery, resulting in a decrease in CRP and WBC levels. However, in patients with multiple trauma involving damage to the large or small intestine, the possibility of another infection must be considered even if CRP and WBC levels decrease to normal levels. In this patient, delayed spinal infection did not lead to any neurologic deficit, likely because the epidural abscess was small and there was no kyphotic deformity. However, he complained of anorexia related to the spondylitis, and laboratory testing demonstrated anemia.

Chest CT was obtained 12 days after the accident (FIGURE 3A & B), showing a slightly progressed lesion in the left paravertebral area of the thoracic spine. More attention to this change in the thoracic vertebra might have resulted in an earlier, more accurate diagnosis of probable pyogenic spondylitis. In suspicious penetrating spine lesions, imaging 2 weeks after the accident could demonstrate trauma-associated change.

Infection is an important complication when penetrating trauma introduces contaminated material, such as a steel bar or fecal matter. History, physical examination, and imaging support timely diagnosis and early treatment of associated spinal infection.10) Contrast-enhanced MRI has become the investigation of choice for spinal infection.7) Management of pyogenic spondylitis includes both surgical and antibiotic therapies.3) In this delayed presentation and diagnosis of pyogenic spondylitis, we used CT-guided percutaneous aspiration of perispinal abscess material. Antibiotic therapy led to a favorable clinical response.

CONCLUSION

An embedded steel bar should not be removed without proper exposure in the operating room, due to the risk of major bleeding and the need to identify the exact tract at surgery. In a situation where the colon and then spine are penetrated, clinicians must maintain high suspicion and awareness for delayed pyogenic spondylitis.
