Unveiling the hidden health challenges: malaria, helminths, STIs and other pathologies among street children, adolescents and young adults in Cameroon
Authors: Valerie Makoge, Derick Ntale, Rodrigue Roman D Nana, Gervais T Kamga, Jean-Patrick Molu, Vanessa O A Tangueu, Wilfred F Mbacham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe phenomenon of street children, adolescents and young adults (SCAYA) is a growing global challenge. In Africa, >10 million children reside in the streets, driven by poverty, abuse and family instability. In Cameroon, this phenomenon is particularly evident in major cities. Being on the street exposes SCAYA to unsanitary living conditions, sexual exploitation, drugs and health challenges. Despite significant health challenges faced by SCAYA, there are few studies on their health status.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in Yaoundé, Cameroon, from March to July 2022. A total of 147 male street children, adolescents and young adults were enrolled through convenience sampling and voluntary consent. Data collection included semi-structured questionnaires, medical examinations and the collection of blood and stool samples for screening infectious diseases. Fisher's exact test and binomial logistic regression were used to assess associations between participant characteristics and health conditions, including malaria, helminth infections, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and hepatitis B virus (HBV).ResultsOur results revealed a prevalence of malaria parasitaemia of 34.3% (47/137) among mostly asymptomatic participants. Helminth infections accounted 10.9% (16/147), HIV 3.6% (5/138) and HBV 8.7% (12/138). Other pathologies noted were scabies, ringworms, rhinobronchitis, oral infections, wounds and fractures. The logistic regression analysis did not show any statistically significant association between the presence of infectious diseases and the sociodemographic characteristics of SCAYA.ConclusionOur study is one of the first to shed light on the health status and prevalence of infectious diseases among SCAYA in Cameroon. The absence of significant associations with participant characteristics suggests multifactorial influences on the health of these individuals. Targeted healthcare and social interventions are essential to address SCAYA vulnerabilities and improve health outcomes.

Introduction

The phenomenon of children and youths living on the streets is a pressing challenge for many countries worldwide.1 In 2012, the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) estimated that approximately 150 million children worldwide were living on the streets and labelled them as ‘children under difficult circumstances’.1 Glauser et al.2 described street children as ‘Children for whom the streets more than their families have become their home’. In Africa, an increasing number of children find themselves on the streets due to family instability, household violence, abuse and poverty. Estimates suggest that >10 million children in Africa live on the streets.3 Despite the adoption of international frameworks, such as the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child and the African Charter on the Rights of the Child, which aim to safeguard children's rights,4 there is growing evidence that street children, adolescents and young adults (SCAYA) are often marginalized, especially in public and private healthcare systems, in developing countries such as Cameroon.1,5 Being on the street exposes these individuals to precarious, poor and unsanitary living conditions, sexual exploitation, drugs, health challenges and limited access to healthcare. Some of the common health challenges that have been reported in this group include oral diseases, respiratory and dermatological conditions,6 intestinal parasitic infections7,8 and sexually transmitted infections.9,10 Barriers such as limited financial resources and inadequate awareness of available health services further exacerbate their health risks. The dual burden of increased disease susceptibility and restricted access to care amplifies the vulnerability of SCAYA to both chronic and acute health problems.

In Cameroon, the issue of street-connected individuals is becoming increasingly visible, particularly in major cities such as Douala and Yaoundé,5 where these youths and young adults can be seen loitering, sleeping, begging or offering services on the streets. Their actual number is not known owing to challenges in data collection, given the transient/mobile nature of this population. Despite the significant health challenges faced by these children, adolescents and young adults, there is a notable lack of studies on their health status, both globally and particularly in Cameroon. To inform public health interventions and guide targeted strategies for improving the well-being of this vulnerable population, there is a need for comprehensive data on health status and disease prevalence among street-connected individuals in Cameroon. This study aimed to address this gap by investigating the health challenges faced by SCAYA through direct medical assessments, including interviews and disease screening. Specifically, the study focused on determining the prevalence of malaria, helminth infections, HIV and hepatitis B virus (HBV) in this population. These diseases pose significant public health threats, particularly in resource-limited individuals and settings. By shedding light on the burden of these infections and other pathologies, this study aims to inform targeted interventions and policies to improve the health outcomes of SCAYA.

Methods

Study design

This study was part of a larger mixed-method investigation titled ‘Investigating Malaria, Other Health Challenges, Coping Strategies, and Opportunities for a Coordinated Response for Street Children in Cameroon’. This cross-sectional study was conducted from March to July 2022 in Yaoundé, Cameroon, combining direct medical consultations (health assessments), biological analyses of blood and stool samples, semi-structured interviews and focus group discussions with street children. The qualitative research findings are presented elsewhere.

Study settings

Yaoundé, situated at 3.8617°N and 11.5202°E, is the administrative capital of Cameroon, a cosmopolitan city with approximately 2.5 million inhabitants. Also known as the City of Seven Hills, Yaoundé stands out for its diverse culture and hilly terrain. The city is divided into six health districts: Biyemassi, Cite-verte, Djoungolo, Efoulan, Nkolbisson and Nkolndongo. Participants in the study were recruited from neighbourhoods across these districts, including Mvan, Mokolo, Efoulan, Bastos, Gare Voyageur, Columbia and Poste-Centrale.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All participants who could give assent or consent and were registered under the Yaoundé Listening and Transit Center (CETY) and the Ministry of Social Affairs were eligible for participation. Children who were on the street during the day and went back home to their families in the evening did not meet the eligibility criteria and were not part of this study. Eligible participants included SCAYA who primarily lived and/or worked on the streets of Yaoundé. This study was conducted in collaboration with the Cameroon Ministry of Social Affairs (MINAS) through its regional delegation in Yaoundé. This regional delegation facilitated access to street children through the CETY. The CETY, a government institution dedicated to the reintegration of street children, connected the research team with the Organisation Camerounaise de Lutte Contre le Phénomène des Enfants de la Rue (OCALUCOPER), an association of former street children working to address issues affecting street-connected youth. Potential participants were approached during outreach sessions with the CETY and OCALUCUPER, informed about the study objectives and procedures and provided with opportunities to give informed assent or consent. Participation was strictly voluntary, with the possibility of discontinuing the study at any time, without any consequences. Street-connected individuals who agreed to participate were provided transportation to the CETY office premises under the supervision of OCALUCOPER. SCAYA are used to going to the CETY for activities organised by the MINAS for their benefit.

The study includes some participants <18 y of age, who are considered minors (<21 y in Cameroon). These street children are wards of the state. As such, SCAYA are officially registered with the CETY and under the MINAS, a key collaborator in this study. Signed informed consent was obtained from street young adults ≥21 y of age, while verbal assent was obtained from those <21 y of age (street children and adolescents). This aligns with the national ethical requirements in Cameroon. Additionally, officials from the MINAS actively accompanied the researchers throughout the study to ensure ethical compliance and participant welfare.

Study procedure: data collection methods

Medical consultations

Direct medical consultations (health assessments) were conducted by two qualified practicing medical doctors. Medical doctors assessed general health status (through physical examinations), mental and emotional health, health awareness and risky behaviours of SCAYA through tailored questions during the clinical consultations. This was because currently there are no universally accepted instrument exists for assessing the mental and emotional health of street individuals, especially in the African context. Given the unique characteristics of our study population—many of whom have limited formal education and experiences that differ significantly from non-street-dwelling peers—we opted for a context-specific approach.

Questionnaires

Semi-structured questionnaires were administered to gather data on the sociodemographic characteristics of SCAYA, including accommodation details, sources of income, duration of time spent on the street, exposure to drugs and substance use. Example of questions asked are: ‘How long have you been living on the street?’ and ‘Have you ever used drugs? If yes, why? If no, why not?’

In order to determine the prevalence of diseases, blood and stool samples were collected for laboratory analyses.

All participants received antihelminthic medication and any health issues identified by medical doctors during the consultation, including dental caries, sprains and wounds, were treated at no cost. Additionally, participants were provided with breakfast and lunch at the CETY facility.

Sample collection and examination

After medical assessment, blood samples were collected by a certified laboratory technician. Participants were also given sterile sample cups and were told how to collect stool samples and place them in containers.

For blood collection, 5 ml of venous blood was drawn from each participant to test for malaria, anaemia, HIV and HBV. Screening for HIV and HBV was performed using disease-specific kits. Anaemia was screened using a URIT-13 haemoglobinometer, which is professionally tailored to provide fast and accurate haemoglobin results. Blood samples were transported to the laboratory of the Biotechnology Centre of the University of Yaoundé I for malaria parasitaemia detection. Stool samples were collected in sterile containers and sodium azide was added to preserve the parasite eggs. These samples were analysed at the Epidemiology of Parasites and Clinical Research Laboratory of the Institute of Medical

Research and Medicinal Plant Studies (IMPM) by two certified laboratory technicians.

For parasitological examination of blood samples, thin and thick films were prepared, stained with Giemsa and examined microscopically at ×100 magnification. The presence of Plasmodium parasites was confirmed by identification of the asexual stages (trophozoites or schizonts) of the parasite or gametocytes. Parasite density was calculated by counting parasites against 200 white blood cells (WBC), assuming a standard count of 8000 WBC/μl. Negative results were confirmed after scanning 100 high-power fields. Malaria speciation enabled the identification and determination of the prevalence of different Plasmodium species. Stool samples were analysed using the Kato–Katz method to observe parasite eggs. This method requires that stool samples are pressed through a mesh to remove debris, placed unto a slide template, after which cellophane soaked in glycerol–malachite green solution was used to spread the sample to facilitate observation under a microscope. Prepared slides were examined at ×40 magnification by two experienced laboratory technologists. Infection intensity was expressed as eggs per gram of faeces.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) and SPSS version 24 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics including frequencies and means were calculated. Fisher's exact test and the χ2 test were used for categorical variables, while analysis of variance was used for continuous variables. Bivariate and multiple regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with health outcomes, with p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant. For variables with a small sample size, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare variables with three or more groups on the different dependent variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare differences between variables with two groups on the dependent variable. χ2 with the corresponding p-value was presented for Kruskal–Wallis analysis, while the z-value with corresponding p-value was presented for Mann–Whitney U test results.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the IMPM and the ethical review board of the Regional Delegation of Social Affairs of the MINAS. Participation in the study was strictly voluntary. We provided only minimal, non-coercive compensation (meals) to the participants without conditioning participation. Information about the study was first given orally during outreach sessions and again when the respondents arrived the CETY. The study objectives were explained in simple language to the SCAYA, emphasizing the voluntary nature of the study.

Informed consent was obtained from participants ≥21 y of age and assent was obtained from those <21 y of age. Informed assent was obtained orally. Codes were assigned to each participant to ensure confidentiality and anonymity. We did not collect personal information unless necessary. All data were stored in secure cabinets and used only by researchers for purposes of the study.

To ensure psychological well-being, we worked with the OCALUCOPER, who were in charge of the street children on a daily basis in the street and in whom the children had confidence. We worked in collaboration with trained social workers of the CETY. We had oversight from the Delegation of Social Affairs to ensure strict adherence to ethical standards. Also, to make sure the participants were comfortable we conducted the research in a safe, street children–friendly environment (the CETY premises) and not on the streets.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of study participants

A total of 147 male street children participated in this study. Of these, 96% were Cameroonian nationals, while the remaining 4% originated from neighbouring countries such as the Central African Republic, Niger and Nigeria. A total of 38% of participants were <15 y of age. The youngest participant was 10 y old. Most of the participants were <18 y of age (see Table 1). Regarding the education level of the participants, 25% reported no formal education, 41% attended primary school and 33.8% had some level of secondary education. Only 1.4% of the participants had received vocational training. Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants.

Sources of revenue

Almost all of the participants (90%) reported having a source of income. Income-generating activities included car washing (22%), carrying goods in markets (22%), working as scrap metal dealers (18%), washing dishes in restaurants (21%), begging (12%), ambulant vending (5%) and garbage collection (4%). Further investigation revealed that 70% of participants had two or more jobs, with some (4%) holding as many as four jobs at the same time. However, 11% mentioned that they did not have a source of income. The monthly earnings varied among the participants: 54% earned <20 000 Central African francs (XAF) (<US33), 30% earned 20 000–30 000 XAF (US33–50) and 7% earned >50 000 XAF (>US84).

Accommodation

Most (97%) of the participants slept in open spaces such as markets, train stations, bus terminals, abandoned cars and public stands. Only 3% reported sleeping in roofed spaces. A prominent settlement referred to as ‘Columbia’ in Yaoundé was identified as a hub for street children, street families and former street children.

Health status of street children in Yaoundé

Physical health assessment

Medical assessments revealed a range of pathologies among the participants. Skin conditions, including scabies, ringworms, abscesses and untreated wounds, were the most common. Poor oral hygiene was also observed in many SCAYA. Physical examination revealed an untreated sexually transmitted infection (STI). Approximately 7.3% of participants (n=9) presented with fever (37.8°C–38°C) on the day of examination.

Further assessments identified specific issues such as inguinoscrotal hernias, conjunctival jaundice, dental caries, gingivitis and musculoskeletal issues, such as back and neck pain, fractures and sprains caused by work-related activities, street fights or accidents. Respiratory conditions such as rhinobronchitis and reduced oxygen saturation levels (88–96%) were also noted. Street children, adolescents and young adults also presented with digestive tract issues, including dysenteric syndromes, and reported general complaints such as headaches, visual disturbances and pruritus.

Mental and emotional health

Medical doctors assessed the mental and emotional health of the SCAYA using tailored questions during the consultations. Medical consultations with the participants revealed varied levels of mental and emotional well-being. Many expressed resignation and acceptance of their street-connected lifestyle, although they acknowledged its challenges and hostility. Some participants showed a strong determination to adapt, while others revealed emotional distress when faced with health-related findings. Two children were visibly distressed when they were informed of their poor respiratory health, highlighting their concerns about well-being despite limited resources.

Health awareness and risk behaviours

The results from medical consultations indicated that while SCAYA demonstrated a general awareness of their health, they often lacked the necessary resources to maintain it. Risk behaviours, including substance abuse and unsafe sexual practices, were evident. A significant subset of participants showed signs of substance use, with altered states of consciousness attributed to narcotic consumption. A total of 62% of participants reported taking drugs. Marijuana was the most commonly consumed drug. The main reason for taking these drugs was to forget about their problems (22%). Other reasons included ‘peer company’ (18%) and ‘to get courage’ (14%). Some participants reported that they consciously avoided these behaviours to preserve their health and secure a better future.

Prevalence of infectious diseases

Laboratory analysis of the blood samples revealed a malaria parasitaemia prevalence of 34.3%. Plasmodium falciparum was the most common malaria species (76%), followed by Plasmodium malariae (21.3%) and one case of Plasmodium ovale (2.2%) (Figure 1). The average parasitaemia level was approximately 880 parasites/µl of blood.

HIV and HBV screening revealed prevalence rates of 3.6% (5/138) and 8.7% (12/138), respectively (Figure 2).

Laboratory analyses of stool samples revealed a geo-helminth prevalence of 10.9% (16/147). Schistosoma mansoni accounted for 68.8% (11/16) of the cases. Other identified species included Ascaris lumbricoides, Ankylostoma and Trichocephalus. Co-infections with Schistosoma and Ankylostoma were observed (1/16 [6.3%]). Microfilariae in thick blood smears were observed in 5.6% (6/114) of participants. We are unable to confirm which species of microfilariae were observed.

Our study also screened participants for anaemia. Results showed that haemoglobin (HGB) in the blood ranged from 9 to 15 g/dl of blood, with an average HGB level of 12.9 g/dl among the participants. HGB levels of most participants fell within normal ranges.

Logistic regression analysis could not identify potential factors associated with the presence of infectious diseases in this group, as none of the sociodemographic characteristics were significantly related to the presence of malaria, HIV or HBV (Supplementary Table 1). In the case of anaemia, univariate linear regression showed that having contact with drugs, drug consumption and the duration of time on the street (<1 y) were all significantly related to a decrease in haemoglobin. However, these previously mentioned factors lost statistical significance after adjustment in a multivariate model.

Discussion

Our study medically assessed the health status and infectious disease prevalence of 147 male SCAYA in Yaoundé, Cameroon, an aspect that has been largely underreported. The choice of only male participants was not deliberate but was the result of the researchers not finding female SCAYA during the cross-sectional study. Another study on street children in Cameroon was also unable to recruit female SCAYA.11 Other studies have included females in their reports (usually much fewer than males), but in our case it was revealed that once on the street, females are quickly taken under the custody of an adult male as a girlfriend or ‘wife’ and removed from the street to live in specialized hubs for street adults, such as Columbia, mentioned earlier. Further studies can investigate the path of female SCAYA from the street into the custody of males.

Drug use, accommodation, skin infections and injury

Our findings showed that not all SCAYA who had had contact with drugs, used them. This indicates a level of health awareness and preservation among some SCAYA. Drug use among SCAYA is widely reported.1,5,11 Our respondents reported that they used drugs as a means to forget their problems. This aspect of using drugs to forget problems seems to be common among street children, as this has also been reported in other studies.12 This showcases a SCAYA context-specific coping mechanism.

We reported that almost all participants lived in open spaces. This categorizes our study participants under the UNICEF umbrella of ‘children of the street’, different from street children who are in the streets during the day but go back home at night13 (i.e. ‘children on the street’). Our SCAYA participants had to fend for and defend themselves from the dangerous, risky and unpredictable nature of the street. From this background, it can be understood why they suffered from several pathologies and various forms of injury (sprains, fractures and wounds). We found that skin infections, poor oral health and injuries were most common. This reflects not only the poor hygiene and dangerous conditions of their living environments, but also poverty and a lack of parental or responsible adult supervision. These findings are in line with other studies14–16 reporting that skin infections and injuries are common among street children in Iran, Nigeria and Kathmandu.

The various forms of injury noted in our study were work related or as a result of street fights and accidents. Street fights may be a way of addressing power imbalances among this group or asserting oneself among peers.17 Many studies agree that street children and youths are more likely to witness an attack or be attacked themselves. It is evident that SCAYA have few resources or support systems to keep them safe at all times. Previous reports have indicated the disproportionate manner in which street children are affected by trauma and infectious diseases compared with their non-street peers.1,10,18 For this reason, our study screened for three diseases that have severe consequences for the well-being of SCAYA.

Prevalence of infectious diseases

The first disease screened for was malaria, which is a severe disease that causes great morbidity and mortality in many countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, where 90% of malaria deaths on the continent occur.19 It is endemic in Cameroon and is the main reason for hospitalizations and absences from school and work.19,20 To date, very few studies have reported on the prevalence of malaria among SCAYA. In fact, review reports pooling several studies indicated that malaria prevalence reports concerning street children were not found among the studies pooled from several databases.10,21,22 Studies reporting on malaria and street children are based on perceived malaria infections since the research did not include actual malaria screening. The added value of our study is that it is one of the very few studies highlighting the prevalence of malaria after screening SCAYA. Our results showed malaria parasitaemia in 34.3% of mostly asymptomatic SCAYA. Asymptomatic carriers are reservoirs for malaria transmission and have been known to maintain the disease in endemic countries, creating a hurdle for successful elimination and eradication of the disease.23 Notably, the reported prevalence of malaria in our study is higher than the national prevalence for malaria (26%) in the country reported in 2022.20 The insalubrious conditions of the living environments of SCAYA and the finding that 97% of SCAYA reported living in open spaces, which exposes them to high rates of mosquito bites, could contribute to this high prevalence. There is therefore a need for strategies to address the living conditions of this vulnerable group. Addressing the living conditions of SCAYA requires a comprehensive, multisectoral approach that tackles their immediate needs while providing long-term solutions for stability, safety and self-sufficiency. One way this can happen is through the provision of safe spaces for SCAYA where they can access food, hygiene and rest. Also, group homes, foster care programs or supervised independent living could be beneficial for SCAYA.

Unlike in the case of malaria, we found studies reporting the prevalence of HIV and HBV among street children. HIV and HBV are sexually transmittable and the exposure of SCAYA to sexual predation and abuse5,12 makes them vulnerable to these infectious diseases. Our study showed an HIV prevalence of 3.6%. This is not very different from results from Eldoret, Kenya, which was 5% in 2015 and 2.2% in 2019.24 However, there was a difference, in that the street children who participated in the Eldoret studies were aware of their status before the survey, while in our case there were not. It is important for regular screening to be carried out in this population to reduce the spread of the disease. In Togo, HBV prevalence among a similar group was 3.7%. In Cameroon, studies have reported that the HBV prevalence in the general population is 11.2%;25 among female sex workers in Douala it was 10%.26 These results in Cameroon differ slightly from our findings, which reported a prevalence of 8.7%. The reasons for these differences is unclear. However, high-risk sexual behaviours and vulnerability to abuse can explain the prevalence of these infectious diseases among this population. Further studies can delve deeper into risk factors contributing to these infectious diseases, as these would inform policy for targeted health promotion interventions towards this group.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is that it is does not provide information on girls or young women living on the streets in Cameroon as explained in the discussion session. However, we believe that the information presented in this article would be beneficial to some extent for females who find themselves in the street, even temporarily. Second, our sample size was small and this may affect the power of our study. Our small sample size reflects the mobile nature of this group, which makes it difficult to reach them, especially for disease-screening activities. It also highlights what can be termed collection bias, because not all children invited to participate in the study followed the OCALUCOPER to the CETY premises. Our results are based only on those who agreed to come to the CETY, where the study was actually being carried out. Our findings are particularly useful for the MINAS to make informed decisions on ways to reduce health disparities in this specific population. Third, we did not investigate the nutritional status of the street children. We invite further studies to investigate the nutritional behaviour, nutritional diversity and frequency of food intake of the SCAYA to see whether this has an effect on their general health status.

Conclusions

Our study is one of the first to shed light on the health status and prevalence of infectious diseases (malaria, HIV and HBV) among SCAYA in Cameroon. The findings are of significant public health importance as they provide evidence to inform policy and show areas for targeted interventions to improve the health and well-being of this population. Some interventions to consider include mobile healthcare units offering medical check-ups and vaccinations for SCAYA, rehabilitation programs for street children with substance abuse issues, family reunification through tracing and mediation and giving grants to families to prevent SCAYA from returning to the street. A strength of our study is that it was conducted in strong partnership with the MINAS, centres/institutions responsible for SCAYA and a community organisation (OCALUCPER) looking out for the needs of SCAYA. This study contributes towards evidence-based information for appropriate policy applications.
