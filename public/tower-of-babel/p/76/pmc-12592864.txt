Evaluation of Structural Characterization and Biocompatibility of Glass Ionomer Cement Enhanced with Nanocomposites of Chitosan, Titanium, Zirconium, and Hydroxyapatite: Assessment of Cell Viability and Adhesion through Gingival Fibroblast Cell Line

AbstractAimTo evaluate the structural characterization and biocompatibility of novel glass ionomer cement (GIC) enhanced with a nanocomposite of chitosan, titanium, zirconium, and hydroxyapatite.Materials and methodsThe experimental GIC was formulated by incorporating chitosan, titanium, zirconium, and hydroxyapatite nanoparticles into the conventional GIC matrix. Introduction

Glass ionomer cement (GIC) has been a cornerstone material in dental practice owing to its unique properties, including chemical adhesion to dental tissues, fluoride release, and biocompatibility.1 These characteristics have positioned GIC as a versatile material for restorative and preventive dental applications. However, inherent limitations such as brittleness, low wear resistance, and limited mechanical strength have spurred research into their modification to enhance performance. In recent decades, progress in material science has facilitated the integration of various additives to overcome these challenges, with nanotechnology offering transformative possibilities. Nanotechnology, a field defined by the manipulation of materials at the nanoscale, has significantly impacted dentistry by enabling the advancement of materials offering improved resilience, bioactivity, and functional versatility.2,3

One promising approach to enhancing the properties of GIC involves the incorporation of nanocomposites, which combine the benefits of multiple materials to achieve synergistic effects.4 In this context, incorporating chitosan, titanium, zirconium, and hydroxyapatite (HA) into GIC offers a novel strategy to overcome the shortcomings of traditional formulations. Chitosan, a naturally sourced biopolymer derived from chitin, is particularly valued for its excellent compatibility with living tissues, inherent antimicrobial activity, and capacity to support tissue repair.5,6 Its proven applications in areas such as wound healing and targeted drug delivery further highlight its ability to improve the biological performance and clinical effectiveness of dental restorative materials. When incorporated into GIC, chitosan can improve the material's flexibility, reduce bacterial adhesion, and potentially enhance bioactivity. These properties are particularly valuable in addressing challenges such as secondary caries and restoration longevity. Titanium and zirconium are well-recognized for their excellent tissue compatibility and resistance to degradation, making them valuable additions to dental restorative materials. The incorporation of titanium nanoparticles has been reported to enhance the strength and durability of GIC by improving its resistance to compressive forces and surface wear, while simultaneously imparting notable antibacterial effects.7 Similarly, zirconium nanoparticles enhance the toughness and durability of the material. Both metals, when used in their nano forms, offer a significant advantage by maintaining or improving the esthetic appearance of GIC due to their smaller particle size and higher dispersibility within the matrix. Hydroxyapatite, the principal inorganic constituent of human bone and dental tissues, has been extensively applied in both dentistry and orthopedics owing to its remarkable biocompatibility, bioactivity, and ability to support bone integration and regeneration. When integrated into GIC, nano-hydroxyapatite particles contribute to remineralization and strengthen the bond between the cement and the tooth structure. This incorporation mimics natural enamel and dentin, providing a biomimetic approach to restorative dentistry. Furthermore, hydroxyapatite can enhance the cement's overall mechanical properties and fluoride release, amplifying its therapeutic benefits. The combination of these materials into a single nanocomposite offers the potential to create a multifunctional restorative material. Chitosan provides antimicrobial and regenerative properties, titanium and zirconium improve mechanical performance and esthetics, and hydroxyapatite enhances bioactivity and remineralization.8–10 The resulting composite is expected to not only address the shortcomings of traditional GIC but also introduce new functionalities, such as enhanced antibacterial effects and tissue integration. Biocompatibility is a critical parameter for any dental material, as it directly influences the material's acceptance and functionality within the oral environment. The complex and dynamic oral cavity, characterized by its fluctuating pH, temperature, and microbial diversity, presents a unique challenge for restorative materials. The addition of a chitosan–titanium–zirconium–hydroxyapatite nanocomposite into GIC calls for an in-depth investigation of its biocompatibility, particularly with respect to its cytotoxicity, inflammatory potential, and ability to support cell adhesion and proliferation. Previous studies on the individual components of this composite have reported favorable biocompatibility, but their combined effects within a GIC matrix remain to be fully understood.11,12 Structural characterization is another essential aspect in evaluating the performance of this enhanced GIC. The integration of nanoparticles into the GIC matrix can alter its microstructure, influencing properties such as porosity, hardness, and fracture toughness. Cutting-edge analytical methods such as scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) offer a crucial understanding of the structural features and chemical interactions occurring between the incorporated nanocomposite and the GIC matrix. Such analyses are crucial for optimizing the formulation and ensuring the uniform distribution of nanoparticles within the cement. The integration of chitosan, titanium, zirconium, and hydroxyapatite-based nanocomposites into GIC represents a paradigm shift in restorative dentistry, offering the potential to create a material that is not only mechanically robust but also biologically active and therapeutically beneficial. This manuscript aims to explore the structural characterization and biocompatibility of this innovative composite, focusing on its potential to enhance the functional properties and overall effectiveness of GIC in clinical applications. In the following sections, we will detail the synthesis and characterization of the nanocomposite, assess its integration into the GIC matrix, and evaluate its mechanical and biological performance. This study endeavors to support the development of next-generation dental materials that correspond with the principles of biomimetics and sustainability, ultimately improving patient outcomes and advancing the field of restorative dentistry.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Sample Size Determination

An in vitro study on material characterization and cytotoxicity was conducted at a university dental hospital in Chennai, India, following ethical approval (SRB/SDC/UG-1901/23/PEDO/220). The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay sample size was set at 30 specimens, calculated using G*Power 3.1.9.3 with α = 0.05 and power = 0.90, corresponding to a large effect size (d ≈ 1.18–1.50).

Material Preparation

Plant extracts and powders were procured from Annai Aravindh Herbals, Chennai, and analytical-grade chemicals from Merck, Mumbai. Chitosan nanoparticles were synthesized by combining eucalyptus leaf extract with a chitosan–acetic acid solution, followed by crosslinking with sodium tripolyphosphate and incubation at 37°C.13 Titanium oxide nanoparticles were prepared by mixing neem leaf extract with a titanium oxide solution under similar conditions.14 Zirconium oxide nanoparticles were obtained by reacting aloe vera extract with zirconium oxychloride solution and incubating overnight.15 For hydroxyapatite nanoparticles, eggshell-derived CaO was combined with Moringa oleifera extract and orthophosphoric acid at a Ca/P ratio of 1.67, followed by aging.16 Finally, all synthesized nanoparticles were processed using a one-pot method (Rahman et al.),17 involving stirring at 80°C, reflux with ethanol, and freeze-drying at −92°C for 48 hours to yield nanoparticle powders.

Incorporation of Nanoparticles into GIC Specimens

The control group used unmodified GIC (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) prepared as per the manufacturer's guidelines. In the experimental groups, 5 wt% of chitosan, titanium, zirconia, and hydroxyapatite nanoparticles (15 mg) were blended with 285 mg of GIC powder and mixed with two drops of GIC liquid, maintaining the standard 1:1 powder-to-liquid ratio. The powders were thoroughly mixed before adding the liquid (polyacrylic acid) to prepare the restorative cement.

Structural Characterization

SEM

The nano Ch-Ti-Zr-HA GIC powder was analyzed by SEM (JEOL JSM IT-800, Germany) after gold coating with a Desk-II sputter coater (Denton Vacuum Inc., United States).

FTIR

FTIR spectra of nano Ch-Ti-Zr-HA GIC powder were recorded using a Nicolet iS10 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 500–3500 cm−1 with 4 cm−1 resolution to analyze chemical bonds.

XRD

X-ray diffraction (XGT 5200 WR, Horiba, Japan) was used to analyze specimen patterns against the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS) database, scanning 25°–60° with 0.02° step size and 2.5 seconds step time.

Biocompatibility Assay

Biocompatibility of GIC, nano-modified GIC, and control was tested on 3T3 fibroblast cells using the MTT assay. 17 Elutes from each material were incubated with cells in 96-well plates for 24 hours, followed by the addition of 10 μL MTT (10 mg/mL) and incubation at 37°C for 4 hours. Formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and absorbance was read at 570 nm (Synergy Hybrid Multi-Mode Reader, BioTek). Cell viability (%) was then calculated.

Cell Viability Assay

Live/dead staining was done 2 days postincubation with the formulated GIC using a Calcein-AM kit (Invitrogen, United States). Stem cells (1 × 10⁶/well) were seeded in 6-well plates, treated after 24 hours with Calcein-AM, incubated for 30 minutes, rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and imaged under an EVOS fluorescence microscope. Live cells fluoresced green. Cell counts, live/dead ratios, and aspect ratios were analyzed using Fiji's Analyze Particles tool.

Cell Adhesion Assay

Fibroblast cells (5 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded on GIC discs in 24-well plates, incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2, and fixed after 24 hours in 2.5% glutaraldehyde/2% paraformaldehyde. Samples were washed, ethanol-dehydrated, critically point dried, and examined for cell adhesion and morphology using SEM (JEOL, Japan).

Result

Structural Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy of nano-modified GIC showed concentration-dependent surface changes: Lower levels had granular, scattered nanoparticles, while higher levels displayed denser, uniform distribution with smoother morphology (Fig. 1A). Nano Ch-Ti and ZrO₂ filled gaps between HA rods, forming a compact Ch-Ti-Zr-HA powder. At 33,000×, rough HA rods were coated with fine TiO2 and ZrO2 residues.

Structural characterization of GIC enhanced with a nanocomposite: (A) SEM micrograph under magnification of 33,000×; (B) FTIR pattern; (C) XRD pattern

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy of modified GIC (Fig. 1B) showed peaks for OH stretching (3310.28 cm−1), carboxyl groups (1644.88 cm−1), and Ti–O/Zr–O bonds (1549.53, 1463.99 cm−1). A new peak at 581.54 cm−1 confirmed incorporation of nano Ch-Ti-Zr-HA into the GIC matrix.

X-ray diffraction analysis of the modified GIC showed multiple peaks, with crystalline content at 8.8% and an amorphous phase of 91.2% (Fig. 1C), confirming the successful incorporation of nanoparticles and changes in the material's crystallinity.

MTT Assay

The MTT assay assessed the biocompatibility of the formulated GIC disks on fibroblast cells. Results (mean ± SD) showed no significant difference from the control group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Both the newly formulated GIC-treated group and the control group showed a high number of viable cells, indicating that the GIC disk does not negatively impact fibroblast cell viability. Furthermore, the conventional GIC and the nano-modified GIC produced similar outcomes, confirming that the modified GIC is as biocompatible as the conventional version.

The biocompatibility effects of newly formulated GIC disk on fibroblast cells were evaluated by MTT assay

Morphological Evaluation Using Phase Contrast and Fluorescent Microscopy

The biocompatibility of the newly formulated GIC was assessed through morphological evaluations using phase contrast and fluorescent microscopy. Phase contrast microscopy showed that fibroblast cells exposed to the new GIC maintained their normal shape, with no signs of stress, shrinkage, or detachment, indicating no cytotoxicity (Fig. 3). Fluorescent microscopy further confirmed these findings, with most cells remaining viable and only a small number of dead cells, suggesting the material had minimal toxic effects. When compared to conventional GIC, the newly formulated GIC demonstrated superior results, with higher cell viability and preserved morphology, highlighting its enhanced biocompatibility.

The biocompatibility of newly formulated GIC was evaluated by morphological assessment using phase contrast and fluorescent microscopy

Cell Adhesion Assessment

The morphological assessment of cell adhesion was performed using SEM, which revealed that the newly formulated GIC exhibited superior cell adhesion compared to conventional GIC. SEM images showed fibroblast cells adhering well to the surface of the new GIC, with clear, well-defined cell morphology and strong interaction with the material (Fig. 4). In contrast, cells exposed to conventional GIC showed weaker adhesion and less effective spreading. The improved cell attachment and spreading on the newly formulated GIC suggest that it provides a more favorable surface for cell interaction, enhancing its biocompatibility and potential for tissue integration.

Discussion

The addition of chitosan, titanium, zirconium, and hydroxyapatite composite into GIC demonstrates significant potential in addressing the limitations of traditional GICs. This study highlights how structural and biocompatibility evaluations validate the innovative design of the enhanced GIC material, offering promising implications for its use in restorative dentistry. The results from the structural characterization reveal that the addition of the nanocomposite to the GIC matrix leads to improved microstructural properties. SEM revealed critical details about the morphology and microstructural organization of the modified GIC. The consistent embedding of nanoparticles throughout the matrix suggests effective synthesis and mixing techniques, which are crucial for achieving optimal mechanical and biological performance. The reduced porosity observed in the SEM images is particularly noteworthy, as it enhances the material's durability and resistance to degradation in the oral environment. FTIR results further validated the chemical interactions between the nanocomposite components and the GIC, indicating strong bonding and effective integration within the matrix. These interactions contribute to enhanced mechanical properties, as observed in previous studies,18–21 where similar nanocomposite additions improved the wear resistance and compressive resistance of GIC. The XRD analysis confirmed the successful incorporation of nano formulation into the GIC matrix, with clear evidence of phase stability and the formation of a homogeneous composite.

Biocompatibility is a fundamental requirement for any dental material, and the in vitro tests conducted in this study underscore the biological safety of the newly formulated GIC. The MTT assay results demonstrated accepted cell viability for fibroblast cells exposed to the nanocomposite-enhanced GIC compared to the control. This finding aligns with the known biocompatibility of the individual components, such as chitosan, titanium, zirconium, and hydroxyapatite. The absence of significant cytotoxicity highlights the potential of the composite to support cellular functions in the oral cavity. The live/dead staining assay provided complementary evidence, showing a higher proportion of viable cells in contact with the modified GIC. The green fluorescence observed in the Calcein-AM staining reflects the ability of the material to support cell survival, likely due to the antimicrobial and regenerative properties of chitosan and the bioactivity of hydroxyapatite. This enhancement in cell viability and proliferation is critical for reducing adverse biological responses and promoting tissue integration, particularly in applications involving close contact with dental pulp or periodontal tissues. Cell adhesion and morphology were assessed using SEM, revealing improved fibroblast attachment and spreading on the nanocomposite-enhanced GIC. This finding is attributed to the surface modifications introduced by the nanoparticles, which increase the surface energy and roughness of the material, thereby enhancing cell-material interactions.22,23 The critical point drying process further confirmed the presence of well-adhered fibroblast cells with distinct morphological features, indicating the biocompatibility and suitability of the modified GIC for clinical use. The enhanced cellular adhesion observed in this study has significant implications for restorative dentistry. Effective cell attachment is essential for the prolonged efficacy of restorative materials, as it facilitates the integration of the material with surrounding tissues and supports the healing process. The addition of chitosan likely plays a key role in this process, given its well-documented ability to promote cell proliferation and tissue regeneration.24,25

The multifunctional properties of the chitosan, titanium, zirconium, and hydroxyapatite nanocomposite offer a transformative approach to overcoming the limitations of conventional GIC. The enhanced mechanical properties, as evidenced by the structural characterization, address the brittleness and low wear resistance commonly associated with traditional GICs. The cumulative effect of the nanocomposite components, chitosan for flexibility and antimicrobial properties, titanium and zirconium for mechanical strength, and hydroxyapatite for bioactivity, results in a material that aligns with the principles of biomimetics.26–29 By mimicking the natural structure and function of enamel and dentin, this novel GIC composite has the potential to improve clinical outcomes and patient satisfaction. These findings suggest that the newly formulated GIC is a safe, effective, and biocompatible material for both medical and dental applications. It demonstrates superior cell adhesion compared to conventional GIC, indicating its potential to serve as a promising alternative with better biocompatibility. This makes the newly formulated GIC an excellent candidate for use in medical and dental fields, offering enhanced tissue integration and cell interaction. While this study demonstrates the promising properties of the modified GIC, further investigations are needed to validate its clinical efficacy. Long-term in vivo studies evaluating the material's performance under dynamic oral conditions, including masticatory forces and varying pH levels, will provide critical insights. Additionally, exploring the optimal ratios of the nanocomposite components and their interactions with different adhesive systems could further enhance the material's performance.

Conclusion

The structural characterization and biocompatibility assessments presented in this study demonstrate the potential of a chitosan-titanium-zirconium-hydroxyapatite nanocomposite to transform GICs into multifunctional restorative materials. The enhanced mechanical strength, biocompatibility, and bioactivity of the modified GIC offer a promising solution to the limitations of traditional GICs, paving the way for next-generation dental materials that prioritize both performance and sustainability.
