Semisupervised approach for dominant gene selection and classification
Authors: Reshma Rastogi, Mamta Bhattarai Lamsal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSemisupervised learning has attracted significant interest in gene expression analysis due to its ability to improve classification performance under limited labeled data, a common challenge arising from high dimensionality and small sample sizes. Existing semisupervised methods often rely on manifold learning to propagate label information; however, weak label connections in early learning stages can reduce their effectiveness.ResultsTo address these limitations, we propose a semisupervised approach for dominant gene selection and classification (SADGSC) that employs feature decomposition to identify essential features while suppressing noise and redundancy. The proposed method adopts hinge loss instead of square loss for label prediction and constructs a projection matrix based on confidence-weighted features, leading to improved classification accuracy and interpretability. Biological analysis of the top-ranked genes (e.g., PGR, KRT14, TOX3, and FGF10) reveals enrichment in hormone signaling epithelial-mesenchymal transition, apoptosis, and oxidative stress pathways, demonstrating the biological relevance of the selected genes. Experimental evaluations conducted on eleven datasets, primarily gene expression datasets, show that SADGSC achieves competitive performance compared with state-of-the-art methods.ConclusionsThe proposed SADGSC framework effectively addresses the challenges of limited labeled data and high dimensionality in gene expression analysis, providing strong classification performance and biologically meaningful gene selection. These findings highlight the potential of semisupervised learning for dominant gene discovery and robust classification in high-dimensional biological datasets.Keywords: Semisupervised learning, Gene selection, Feature decomposition, Dominant features, Noisy features

Introduction

Microarray gene expression analysis plays a crucial role in identifying disease associated biomarkers and understanding complex biological mechanisms. However, these datasets are inherently high dimensional, often containing tens of thousands of genes but only a few hundred samples. This imbalance between dimensionality and sample size poses significant challenges for supervised learning, as acquiring labeled samples through biological experiments is both costly and time consuming. In this work, we focus on feature selection in gene expression data, where the terms “gene selection” and “feature selection” are used interchangeably. Semisupervised learning (SSL) offers a promising solution using both labeled and unlabeled data to enhance classification and gene selection performance [1]. In this context, semisupervised feature selection has gained growing interest in improving model interpretability and generalization in small sample biological datasets [2, 3]. Despite its potential, the high dimensionality of microarray data introduces redundancy, noise, and overfitting, motivating the need for robust semisupervised feature selection techniques.

Existing approaches and limitations

Feature selection aims to identify the most relevant subset of genes to improve classification accuracy, reduce computational cost, and enhance interpretability [4]. Classical methods such as minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) [5], Laplacian score [6], and SVM-RFE [7] have been widely applied in gene expression analysis. While effective, these approaches often fail to model inter gene dependencies and suffer from scalability limitations in large scale datasets.

Graph based semisupervised feature selection methods address these challenges by modeling relationships among labeled and unlabeled samples [8, 9]. However, conventional static graphs (e.g., KNN or -ball graphs) may not accurately represent complex data structures [10, 11]. To overcome this limitation, adaptive graph learning techniques have been proposed [12, 13], which dynamically refine graph topology to better preserve manifold structures. Manifold based methods such as Laplacian score and its extensions [14] aim to capture local geometric relationships, but Laplacian regularization often struggles to maintain manifold curvature [15]. Hessian regularization [16] improves extrapolation by preserving the higher order structure of data manifolds.

The design of loss functions also plays a critical role in improving model robustness. Traditional least squares losses are highly sensitive to outliers, especially in partially labeled datasets. Hinge loss has emerged as a more reliable alternative [17, 18], maximizing classification margins and enhancing generalization in noisy environments [19].

Emerging deep semisupervised approaches

Recent advances in deep learning have introduced powerful generative and graph based SSL frameworks for biological data. Graph neural networks (GNNs) and Variational autoencoders (VAEs) are capable of capturing nonlinear dependencies and latent biological patterns from limited labeled data. For example, the class conditional Variational autoencoder (cVAE) [20] achieved nearly 98% accuracy in pan-cancer RNA-seq classification by generating synthetic expression profiles conditioned on tumor types. Similarly, SyntheVAEiser [21] demonstrated that VAE based synthetic augmentation can enhance traditional ML models, particularly to benefit rare cancers with limited data availability. While these models achieve outstanding performance in large scale omics datasets, they require substantial data and computational resources, making them less suitable for small sample microarray contexts. This motivates the need for interpretable and data efficient semisupervised feature selection methods.

Building upon these insights, we propose a novel Semi-supervised adaptive decomposed graph based sparse classification (SADGSC) framework that integrates hinge loss with confidence based feature decomposition. The model decomposes microarray data into confident and noisy components, constructs an adaptive graph based on the confident features, and learns a sparse projection matrix to identify discriminative genes. FME (Flexible manifold embedding: A framework for semisupervised and unsupervised dimension reduction) a unified manifold learning framework that employs a linear regression function to map new data points while integrating label fitness, manifold smoothness, and a flexible penalty term on the regression residue. This framework effectively utilizes label information from labeled data and the manifold structure from labeled and unlabeled data [26].

SFAM (Semisupervised multi-label feature selection with adaptive structure learning and manifold learning) combining adaptive global structure learning and manifold learning, effectively preserves global structures and explores label correlations, overcoming limitations of existing methods. To assess the biological relevance of the genes selected by our proposed SADGSC framework, we analyzed the top ten breast cancer relevant genes based on their selection frequency over 100 runs (Table 11). These genes are strongly implicated in key oncogenic processes, including hormone signaling, proliferation, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), immune modulation, and oxidative stress response.

PLA2G2D encodes a phospholipase A2 enzyme involved in lipid metabolism and immune regulation. Dysregulation of phospholipase A2 pathways has been linked to breast cancer inflammation and tumorigenesis [36].

KRT14 (Cytokeratin 14) is a basal epithelial marker, highly expressed in basal like and triple negative breast cancers, serving as a diagnostic biomarker for aggressive subtypes [37].

PGR (Progesterone Receptor) is a well established hormone receptor whose expression status guides prognosis and endocrine therapy decisions in breast cancer [38].

ACVR1C (Activin A Receptor Type IC) regulates TGF- signaling and EMT; its overexpression correlates with poor prognosis and increased invasiveness [39].

TOX3 plays a role in calcium-dependent transcription and chromatin remodeling, and GWAS have identified TOX3 polymorphisms as susceptibility loci for breast cancer risk [40].

TMEM45B encodes a transmembrane protein linked to hypoxia responses and drug resistance, contributing to poor therapeutic outcomes in breast cancer [41].

FGF10 (Fibroblast Growth Factor 10) promotes epithelial proliferation, and aberrant FGF10 signaling activates MAPK and PI3K pathways, driving tumor progression [42].

TNNT3 (Troponin T3) is aberrantly expressed in stromal tissue and has been associated with enhanced cancer cell motility and metastasis [43].

S100B, a calcium binding protein, modulates p53-dependent apoptosis; elevated S100B levels are linked to poor prognosis in breast cancer [44].

APOD (Apolipoprotein D) is involved in lipid transport and oxidative stress resistance. Its higher expression correlates with better survival and reduced proliferation rates [45].

These results demonstrate that SDSCGS effectively identifies biologically meaningful and disease relevant markers, highlighting its potential utility for breast cancer classification and interpretation.

Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed SADGSC, a semisupervised method for dominant gene selection and classification that separates informative features from noisy ones using a hinge-square loss, offering greater robustness than conventional least-squares approaches. Experiments on eleven datasets demonstrated consistently strong performance, highlighting the method’s adaptability for gene expression analysis.

While SADGSC achieves accurate and biologically interpretable results, its iterative optimization leads to longer runtimes, indicating the need for scalability improvements for ultra-high-dimensional omics data. Biological analysis further showed that the top-ranked genes (e.g., PGR, KRT14, TOX3, FGF10) were enriched in hormone signaling, EMT, apoptosis, and key cancer-related pathways, confirming the biological relevance of the selected features. SADGSC also shows potential for integration with multi-omics data and applications beyond bioinformatics, including medical imaging and high-dimensional data mining. The inclusion of non-gene datasets in the experimental evaluation further demonstrates the generalizability and robustness of the proposed framework across diverse scenarios.
