How much is enough? Assessing 30‐ versus 60‐day Timeline Followback stability in youth alcohol use patterns
Authors: Anna E. Kirkland, Pamela L. Ferguson, Tirza J. Alcala, Louise Mewton, Rachel L. Tomko, Lindsay M. Squeglia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe variability of youth alcohol use is thought to necessitate longer Timeline Followback (TLFB) reporting windows to accurately capture alcohol use patterns. However, longer TLFB windows can increase burden for participants and study staff. This study assessed youth alcohol use patterns across two different 30‐day periods (TLFB days 1–30 and 31–60) to inform future research protocols.MethodsParticipants (N = 481 with 504 visits, ages: 15–25) completed a shared intake to determine eligibility for numerous substance‐related studies. Alcohol consumption over the past 60 days was assessed using a modified TLFB that recorded total number of drinks, average drinks per day, average drinks per drinking day (DPDD), total number of drinking days, maximum number of drinks in a single day, and total number of binge drinking days (4+ drinks for females, 5+ drinks for males). TLFB data were divided into two 30‐day periods: the most recent (Days 1–30) and the more distal (Days 31–60). Analyses were conducted overall and within subgroups by sex, age (under 21 or 21+), presence of alcohol use disorder (AUD), and other substance use. Alcohol use patterns were assessed using Spearman correlations, Wilcoxon signed rank tests, and intraclass correlations (ICC) for test–retest reliability.ResultsAll alcohol outcomes were significantly correlated between reporting windows (p's < 0.001). Alcohol use was significantly higher during the more recent reporting window (TLFB Days 1–30; p's < 0.05), except for DPDD (p = 0.58). Overall, the differences between reporting windows were small (on average, less than three total drinks and <1 drinking day or binge drinking day) and suggested clinically similar use patterns. The ICC values suggest moderate‐to‐good stability between reporting windows. Similar patterns were noted in each subgroup analysis.ConclusionsA 30‐day TLFB window appears sufficient for youth alcohol use assessment, providing comparable and agreeable self‐reported alcohol use patterns, with reduced burden.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol continues to be the most commonly used substance in adolescents and young adults, together referred to as “youth” (Miech et al., 2023; Ryan et al., 2019). Recruiting participants and collecting data for youth alcohol and other substance use research studies can present several challenges, including needing rigorous screening criteria and methods to ensure the study samples are accurately reflecting the greater population of interest. However, assessing alcohol use in youth can be a complicated and resource‐intensive component of research and may require different methods depending on the stage of the research study. For example, alcohol use data can be collected daily using concurrent recall methods, such as Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA). While EMA provides detailed and time‐locked data, it can only be employed after a participant is already enrolled in a research study. Retrospective recall is the most commonly used method when screening participants for eligibility to enroll in a study, collecting baseline data, or collecting other data for observational (e.g., longitudinal or cross‐sectional designs) and interventional studies. Retrospective self‐report of substance use can be a complete and reliable assessment of substance use when collected under appropriate conditions, such as assured confidentiality, within a clinical setting, and when participants are not currently under the influence of any substances (Vakili et al., 2008). However, these measures do rely on accurate reporting from participants.

The Timeline Followback (TLFB) is the most commonly used assessment for retrospective self‐report of alcohol and substance use, and it is valid for youth substance use assessment (Levy et al., 2004; Sobell & Sobell, 1992a, 1992b). The TLFB is a structured assessment that uses a calendar with participant‐specific anchors (e.g., birthday, graduation, and other event) to guide alcohol and other substance use recall within a specific timeframe, usually ranging from 30 to 90 days but sometimes extending out to 6 or 12 months (Buu et al., 2014; Vakili et al., 2008). Patterns of alcohol use are known to be highly variable in youth. The variability is driven by several factors, including the limited availability of alcohol under the age of 21 in the United States that can lead to binge‐like drinking when accessible; the influence of the day of the week, with higher rates of alcohol use later in the week (Thursday to Sunday); and the school calendar leading to fluctuations of alcohol use for those in school (e.g., school breaks or holidays) (Carpenter & Merrill, 2021; Finlay et al., 2012; Lau‐Barraco et al., 2016; Wood et al., 2007). The variation in alcohol use can also change as a function of age, both on an individual and group level. Therefore, the duration of time chosen for the TLFB may be critical to accurately screen youth for inclusion in alcohol use research or capture alcohol use patterns within studies.

There is an important question to consider when designing youth alcohol use screening and other data collection processes: Can we streamline our procedures to accurately and rigorously collect appropriate data without overburdening the participant? The current study sought to assess the stability of alcohol use patterns between Days 1–30 and 31–60 on a modified TLFB in a large sample of youth participating in an intake process specific for substance use studies. Since the TLFB can be a particularly intensive piece of the screening and data collection process, researchers should be purposeful in the selected timeframe of interest to accurately capture alcohol use patterns in youth without being too cumbersome. The present work will be able to aid in such decisions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Participants were 481 youth with 504 visits ages 15 to 25 who were recruited for a shared youth substance use intake process for various studies being conducted at the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC; more details below). For this study, participants were included if they were under the age of 26 based on our definition of “youth,” reported lifetime alcohol use, and completed the full 60 days of TLFB procedures (details below). The last participant in this study completed the intake process on April 25, 2024. All participants over the age of 18 provided written informed consent, and participants under the age of 18 had parental consent and provided assent. Participants were recruited year‐round from the community using a variety of techniques, including social media, in‐person events, and flyers.

Entryway intake procedure

MUSC has several ongoing youth substance use studies; thus, a shared intake process, called “Entryway,” was created (Figure 1) (Davis et al., 2024). Entryway aids in rigorously screening the eligibility of participants for multiple studies that are concurrently enrolling a similar population, and participants consent to share their screening data with the study/studies in which they ultimately enroll. Entryway intake uses an IRB‐approved protocol which includes a battery of assessments, prioritizing PhenX Toolkit measures to promote data harmonization, including demographic, general and mental health, and substance use (e.g., modified TLFB) (Conway et al., 2014). It also limits “guessing” eligibility criteria (e.g., providing answers that one hopes will increase the chance of being enrolled, even if the answer is not completely accurate) and demand characteristics (e.g., reacting to cues in a research study setting that may potentially alter behavior), while providing infrastructure to ensure participants are not enrolled in multiple studies simultaneously.

Overview of the Entryway intake screening protocol. Participants first complete the multi‐modal intake screening process called “Entryway” before being triaged into a substance use study that fits their inclusion criteria. TLFB, Timeline Followback.

Modified Timeline Followback (TLFB)

The modified TLFB was used to collect detailed substance use histories over the past 60 days for all participants, with Day 1 being the day prior to the intake visit and Day 60 being 60 days prior to the intake visit (Sobell & Sobell, 1992a, 1992b). The assessment was administered by trained study staff, either in‐person (81.9%) or virtually (18.1%), depending on the format for the Entryway intake session. There were no significant differences in TLFB administration style, nor time of year (i.e., month of most distal TLFB reporting date) (Data S1). Prior to beginning the modified TLFB, study staff reviewed the definition of a US standard drink with participants to aid in the accuracy of reporting. Participants were also prompted to review their calendars, social media, or anything else that would help them recall their alcohol use over the past 60 days.

Alcohol variables were computed for two 30‐day periods, the most recent (Days 1–30) and the more distal (Days 31–60), and included the following for each time period: total number of drinks, average drinks per day (AVGDPD), average drinks per drinking day (DPDD), total number of drinking days, maximum number of drinks in a single day, and total number of binge drinking days. Binge drinking was defined as 4+ drinks for females or 5+ drinks for males on an occasion, with occasion defined as a day for this analysis. DPDD required at least 1 drinking day to be calculated.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, box plots, and Shapiro–Wilk normality tests were used to assess the distributions of the drinking variables. Correlations between Days 1–30 and Days 31–60 were assessed for each alcohol variable. Since none of the variables were normally distributed, Spearman's rank correlation was used. Wilcoxon signed rank tests were used to assess whether there were significant differences between the time periods for the median of each of the drinking variables. Intraclass correlations (ICC) were run to assess test–retest reliability.

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess for stability within sex (females and males), age (younger than 21, 21 or older), alcohol use disorder (AUD) status (criteria met, criteria not met), and other non‐alcohol substance use (yes, no). For subgroup analyses, sex was split into female and male, and age was split into younger (<21 years) and older (21 or older) groups, based on the legal drinking age in the United States, which affects access to alcohol. The Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) for AUD was used to create a bivariate variable for whether or not participants met AUD criteria during the past 12 months. Other substances assessed in the modified TLFB included tobacco/nicotine, cannabis, K2/Spice, other recreational drugs, heroin, and synthetic opioids. A bivariate variable was created based on any other substance use during the past 60 days versus none. Wilcoxon signed rank tests were used to assess differences in the median of drinking variables between reporting windows separately for each of the four covariates' two groups. Differences were considered significant at p‐values <0.05. ICC models were run using a two‐way model with random effects on single measurements with an outcome of absolute agreement (Shrout & Fleiss, 1979). Agreement was defined as poor (ICC < 0.50), moderate (0.50 < ICC < 0.75), good (0.75 < ICC < 0.90), or excellent (ICC > 0.90) (Koo & Li, 2016).

Five percent of the visits (n = 23) were repeat visits by a participant where TLFB reporting windows did not overlap, and due to the small percentage, they were treated as independent visits. Participants may have multiple intakes due to screening/enrolling into multiple studies sequentially. A sensitivity analysis was completed removing any repeat visits to assess for the effect of interdependent data. These results are presented in the Supplemental Materials. There were five visits in which 1 drinking day did not have the number of drinks entered, and one visit with two amounts (i.e., number of standard drinks) missing. This did not affect the total number of drinking days but may have affected the other drinking variables. Two participants were missing AUD results, so they were not included in that comparison.

RESULTS

Participants

Five hundred four visits had participants under the age of 26, with higher percentage of participant visits being female (64.1%) (Table 1). The sample was almost evenly split between participant visits under the age of 21 (50.4%) and 21 years or older (49.6%), with an average age of 21.3 (SD = 2.1). On average, participants completed high school and obtained some college education. In over half of the visits, participants met criteria for current AUD (59.9%), while an even higher percent met criteria for current cannabis use disorder (CUD; 68.8%); this is due to a historically higher number of cannabis use or CUD specific studies screening participants through the Entryway intake process. The most prevalent psychological disorder was lifetime Major Depressive Disorder (36.9%), followed by current Generalized Anxiety Disorder (15.9%), Lifetime Panic Disorder (15.5%), and current ADHD Combined Type (13.1%). Alcohol use associations, differences, and intraclass correlations between reporting windows

All variables were significantly correlated between Days 1–30 and Days 31–60 (Figure 2). Across the full sample, there were significant differences across the reporting windows (p's < 0.05) for all variables except for DPDD and binge drinking days. Specifically, more recent reporting (Days 1–30) corresponded with higher alcohol use compared with more distal reporting (Days 31–60) for all significant variables. Additionally, ICC values showed good agreement between reporting windows (ICC > 0.75) for all variables except for maximum number of drinks on a single drinking day which only showed moderate agreement (ICC = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.65–0.74; Table 3). We also examined the effects of sex (male, female), age (under 21, 21 or older), AUD status in the past year, and substance co‐use (yes, no) using subgroup analyses. Demographic‐based subgroups are presented in Table 4. There were no significant differences (p's ≥ 0.05) between reporting windows for DPDD and binge drinking days for all sex and age subgroups; maximum drinks on a single drinking day for females and individuals under the age of 21; total number of drinking days for males and individuals 21+ years old; or AVGDPD for individuals 21+ years old. In terms of agreement, all measures showed good agreement (ICC > 0.75) except for maximum drinks on a single drinking day across all subgroups (ICC range 0.63–0.74), binge drinking days for females (ICC = 0.71), and total number of drinking days for females (ICC = 0.74) which all showed moderate agreement.

DISCUSSION

The goal of this work was to assess the stability of alcohol use patterns in youth across recent (Days 1–30) and distal (Days 31–60) reporting windows of the modified TLFB to inform screening and alcohol data collection procedures for youth substance use studies. We found strong and significant correlations between the total number of drinks, average drinks per day (AVGDPD), drinks per drinking day (DPDD), total drinking days, maximum number of drinks on an occasion, and binge drinking days between the two reporting windows. However, across the full sample, we also found significant differences between the recent and distal reporting windows for these variables, reflecting higher levels of reported alcohol use in the recent window, except for DPDD, which did not differ. The ICC values indicated good agreement across each outcome, suggesting relatively stable use between reporting windows across the full sample. This pattern varied some within subgroups depending on demographic (sex and age) and other substance use. Overall, these results reflect fairly consistent patterns of alcohol use. While there were significant differences noted between reporting windows, the numerical differences were minimal (e.g., on average, less than three total drinks and less than 1 drinking day or binge drinking day in the full sample and for each subgroup) indicating clinically similar patterns.

Our findings are aligned with previous work in this area which compared the correspondence in alcohol use variables between different timeframes in adults. One study compared a web‐based, repeated 7‐day TLFB to an interviewer‐administered 30‐day TLFB in college students. Differences were noted in drinking patterns, where participants reported higher quantity and frequency of alcohol consumption using the 7‐day TLFB compared with the 30‐day TLFB; however, there was good overall correspondence between the summary indicators (Hoeppner et al., 2010). Similar to our findings, the discrepancy between the formats became more apparent for more distal days, indicating that longer recall periods may lead to less accurate reporting. Several other studies have assessed other windows of time across 90‐day TLFB (1–30 days, 31–60 days, vs. 61–90 days) and found similar patterns (Buu et al., 2014; Rice, 2007). Summary measures remained fairly stable across the window of interest; however, day‐to‐day agreement between outcomes varied greatly and the odds of reporting a drinking day decreased as the window was extended (Rice, 2007). Together with our work, it seems the shorter TLFB timeframes may provide finer details of alcohol use patterns compared with longer timeframes. This could be for several reasons. First, it may be a simple memory effect where participants are better able to accurately recall quantity and frequency of alcohol use for more recent timeframes. Second, depending on the order of the TLFB data collection (forward vs. backward in time), it may reflect increased burden leading to decreased reporting for longer timeframes. Individuals may rely on typical patterns of use for distal reporting (e.g., “I always go out on Thursday night and usually have 3 drinks”) compared with more recent reporting. These reasons support our argument for a shorter TLFB window during screening for youth substance use studies, as it is important that eligibility is assessed using the most accurate data possible.

This work should be interpreted within its strengths and weaknesses. We were able to recruit a large sample of youth with a highly variable range of alcohol and other substance use. As such, our results should generalize to most of the youth population who are screening for substance‐related research studies. We also used PhenX Toolkit assessments for the ability to harmonize and combine screening data across many different youth substance use studies that were recruiting similar demographics. In terms of weaknesses, we only had up to 60 days of TLFB data. Like previous work, it would be interesting to assess the stability of alcohol use patterns across longer windows of time (e.g., 90 days). We had a fairly homogenous sample demographically when considering education, race, and ethnicity, and it was a convenience sample. As such, our findings may not generalize to other populations. There were also overall lower ICC values and larger confidence intervals observed for participants who reported alcohol use with no other substance use. This is most likely due to the smaller sample size (n = 88, 17.5%) resulting in lower power for this subgroup analysis. Studies focused on alcohol use only will need to confirm these findings in a larger sample. Finally, less than 5% of the visits in the sample were second or third visits of the same participant, so there was a small amount of nonindependence in the cohort. However, all models were run without the repeat visits, and all results were confirmed to hold without the interdependent data.

CONCLUSIONS

The variability of youth alcohol use has been thought to necessitate longer TLFB reporting windows to accurately capture use patterns. This study assessed the stability of youth alcohol use across TLFB Days 1–30 and 31–60 to inform future research data collection procedures. Findings support using 30 days of TLFB data when recording data from generally healthy youth for substance use studies as there were strong correlations, good agreement, and any significant differences in use patterns reflected reduced reporting in distal periods. The higher alcohol use in the more recent 1–30 days may reflect more accurate reporting due to temporal proximity. Thus, a shorter TLFB reporting window seems appropriate for youth substance use studies and may aid in decreasing burden for the participant and study staff without sacrificing data quality.
