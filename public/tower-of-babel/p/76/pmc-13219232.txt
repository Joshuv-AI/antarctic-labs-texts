Measuring capacities to advance global health security and bioeconomic development on the African continent
Authors: Margaret M Dunne, Yenew Kebede, Wilmot James, Ali Asy, Peter Ahabwe Babigumira, Donewell Bangure, Ian Peter Busuulwa, Rym Benkhalifa, Joseph Bitilinyu-Bangoh, Elizabeth E Cameron, Carly Gasca, Christopher R Isaac, Issa Makumbi, Talkmore Maruta, Zibusiso Masuku, Natalie Mayet, Diane Meyer, Geoffrey Namara, Jaures Noumedem, Tolbert Nyenswah, Chinwe Lucia Ochu, Donald Ibe Ofili, Paul Okwalinga, Judith Omumbo, Augustin Mouinga Ondeme, Nathan A Paxton, Samira Senouci, Hayley Severance, Lizeka Tandwa, Jaime M Yassif, Elly Vaughan, Jennifer B Nuzzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Health security capacities are crucial to reduce the risks from infectious diseases.•African countries demonstrate a strong commitment to improving health security.•The biosafety and biosecurity capacities of African countries need strengthening.•Enhancing health security boosts preparedness and supports Africa’s bioeconomy.Keywords: Global health, Health security, Infectious diseases, One Health, Epidemiology

Background

The African continent continues to face a disproportionately high burden of infectious disease outbreaks [1]. While the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (Africa CDC) presided over a coordinated effort to respond to the COVID-19 pandemic, inequities in the global response led to delays in accessing crucial medical products, especially vaccines [2]. Despite initially appearing to have fared comparably better than other regions during COVID-19, subsequent analyses adjusting for differences in age structure and mortality reporting suggest higher than previously appreciated pandemic mortality on the continent [3]. In addition to COVID-19, the continent continues to face other health threats, including outbreaks of mpox, measles, cholera, and viral hemorrhagic fevers [4]. Moving forward, climate change, the effects of which the continent is likely to be disproportionately impacted by, urbanization, and growing migration all have the potential to impact the frequency, severity, and range of infectious diseases [5].

To reduce the impacts of these events on human health, it is imperative that the continent strengthen health security capacities to improve outbreak prevention, detection, and response. Importantly, health security capacities needed to address public health emergencies also have relevance for economic development on the African continent. The bioeconomy, the part of the economy that translates biological materials into products, has great potential to support continued economic growth on the continent [6]. This could be through job creation, increased food security, and the development of biotechnology products for use both on the continent and around the world [[6], [7], [8]]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, leading African and global public health institutions provided large investments into the continent’s bioeconomy, particularly for scaling up the production of medical countermeasures (MCMs), including diagnostics and vaccines [2,9,10]. Investments to strengthen health security capacities, such as biosafety and biosecurity, will support the growing bioeconomy. In turn, harnessing the bioeconomy for the development of MCMs like vaccines will improve health security.

To decrease reliance on external actors during health emergencies such as infectious disease outbreaks, in 2021, the Africa CDC launched “Africa’s New Public Health Order”—a strategy focused on increasing the capacities of public health institutions, local manufacturing for MCMs, and increasing domestic investment in health security [2]. Additional continent-wide activities related to strengthening health security in Africa include the launch of the African Medicines Agency (AMA) in 2025 and the second iteration of the Africa Pathogen Genomics Initiative, which aims to increase next-generation sequencing capacity [11,12].

Describing the status of health security on the continent is critical to understanding existing strengths and weaknesses and can assist policymakers in determining where to invest limited resources. It can also provide a framework for measuring the impacts of these investments over the short and long-term, including how best to capitalize on the intersections of health security capacities and the bioeconomy.

To understand current health security capacities in the African continent, we analyzed data from the most recent Global Health Security Index (GHS Index), which includes data from 2019 to 2021. The GHS Index assesses the health security capacities of all 195 countries that are States Parties to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) International Health Regulations (IHR) across six categories—prevention, detection and reporting, rapid response, health systems, commitments to international norms, and the risk environment [13,14]. Developed with the input of an international panel of experts and validated before publication, the GHS Index aggregates publicly available information on capacities to prevent and respond to high-consequence biological events and is designed to enable cross-country comparisons [14]. While the GHS Index incorporates elements of other international tools, including the WHO’s voluntary Joint External Evaluation (JEE) and the World Organization for Animal Health’s Performance of Veterinary Services (PVS) pathway, it includes additional dimensions of health security, including health security financing and the risk environment. The GHS Index can be used as a benchmarking tool to assist governments and regional organizations in determining where to allocate resources to strengthen health security capacities and can also help track progress over time [13]. Although there were initial concerns raised about the validity of the GHS Index as a metric for preparedness during the COVID-19 pandemic, subsequent analyses have provided compelling evidence that better prepared countries had lower excess mortality [3,15,16].

To describe the status of health security on the African continent and highlight strengths and opportunities, we selected GHS Index indicators across five of the six categories in consultation with an expert advisory group of African public health leaders during a roundtable meeting in October 2024. These indicators are aligned with ongoing programmatic priorities on the continent as described by “Africa’s New Public Health Order” [2]. Table 1 illustrates how the GHS Index indicators chosen for presentation in the main analysis correspond to these five pillars. Data on all indicators can be found in the appendix (Supplemental Table S1). While category six (risk environment) remains an important determinant of countries’ ability to leverage capacities during a public health emergency, it is not under the direct control of health authorities and therefore is not discussed here. GHS Index data were extracted using the publicly available data model from GHSIndex.org [17]. Analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.4.1, R Core Team 2024).

Prevention

As the adage “an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure” suggests, stopping biological threats from occurring is the most effective way to mitigate their effects. Despite the benefits of investing in prevention, this category of the GHS Index had the lowest average scores globally and on the African continent in both 2019 and 2021.

Biosecurity, including preventing the unauthorized access or misuse of pathogens, is lacking around the world [19]. In both the 2019 and 2021 indices, the global average score for the biosecurity indicator—which included metrics such as the presence of biosecurity training and practices and transportation security—was less than 20 points of a possible 100, while the average score for the African continent was less than five points. Over half of the countries on the continent had none of the five capacities measured by the biosecurity indicator. According to the 2021 GHS Index, the only biosecurity capacities present on the continent were 1) the capacity to conduct tests for anthrax and/or Ebola without culturing live pathogens and 2) the presence of national regulations on transportation security of infectious substances (Table 2).

Similarly, capacities for biosafety must be improved globally. In both 2019 and 2021, fewer than half of the countries in the world had national biosafety legislation or regulations in place or an agency responsible for the enforcement of these regulations. On the continent, only South Africa had these capacities as of the 2021 GHS Index. In both 2019 and 2021, fewer than 10% of countries globally had requirements in place for standardized biosafety training for individuals handling especially dangerous pathogens. Kenya is the only country on the continent with these requirements in place as of the 2021 GHS Index (Table 2). Of all indicators measured by the GHS Index, the fewest number of countries have capacities for oversight of dual-use research, defined as research using dangerous pathogens or toxins that could be misused and cause harm [20]. Globally, only 11 countries had evidence of legislation requiring oversight of dual-use research. As of 2021, no country on the continent had demonstrated oversight of research with especially dangerous pathogens or implemented legislation or regulations requiring screening for providers of genetic material (Table 2).

To strengthen these capacities, in 2019, Africa CDC launched a continental initiative on biosecurity and biosafety that includes the development of a legislative framework that enables countries to implement biosafety and biosecurity interventions nationally, training, biocontainment assessment, national coordination, and technical support to national public health institutes [21]. Data collection for the 2021 GHS Index occurred between August 2020 and June 2021. Due to the time it takes for such initiatives to build capacities, Africa’s scores for biosecurity and biosafety may have improved in the interim. In addition, most of the questions that measure biosecurity and biosafety in the GHS Index implement a binary scoring structure, making it challenging to capture interim progress. A review of JEE reports published since 2021 demonstrates advancements in biosecurity and biosafety at the country level. For example, Benin and Nigeria have biosecurity and biosafety legislation in place, and Guinea and Sierra Leone have consolidated dangerous infectious agents to a minimum number of facilities (Supplemental Table S2) [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. A new Africa CDC Biosafety and Biosecurity Strategy 2026-2030 builds on the earlier initiative and aims to strengthen systems bolstering safe scientific practice on the continent by enhancing multisectoral working groups and promoting innovation and emerging technologies [26].

Detection and reporting

Early detection and reporting of infectious disease outbreaks, including those of continental and international concern, is key to enabling countries to muster resources and implement a plan to contain or, if possible, stop an outbreak.

While some countries on the African continent, like Nigeria, have strong infectious disease surveillance systems, most need strengthening. In 2021, Nigeria demonstrated capacities across all indicators for real-time surveillance and reporting in the GHS Index, including conducting event-based surveillance and analysis, reporting emergencies, and having interoperable, interconnected, and electronic real-time reporting. According to the 2021 GHS Index, 28% of countries on the continent had evidence of ongoing event-based surveillance for infectious diseases, compared to 46% globally (Table 2). Based on data presented in the 2021 GHS Index, even in countries with ongoing event-based surveillance, only Kenya and Nigeria analyze this data daily.

To build surveillance capacity on the continent, in 1998, the WHO’s Africa Regional Office introduced the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) system [27]. The wide range of scores for the real-time surveillance GHS Index indicator on the continent demonstrates that countries are at different phases of implementing the IDSR, now in its third edition. In the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic, multiple initiatives were created to improve surveillance. WHO’s Hub for Pandemic and Epidemic Intelligence is supporting African countries in developing collaborative surveillance plans with collaboration among diverse partners at the center [28]. The Health Security Partnership in Africa (HSPA) is building event-based and genomic surveillance in Tunisia, Morocco, the Gambia, Mali, South Africa, and Namibia [29]. The impacts of these newer initiatives were not captured by the 2021 GHS Index, as they were implemented after its publication, but demonstrate a strong commitment to strengthening surveillance capacities on the continent. Additionally, JEE reports published since 2021 detail progress in advancing surveillance systems at the country level. These reports indicate improvements in event-based surveillance capacities in Benin, Guinea, Tanzania, and Togo [22,24,30,31]. Based on their most recent JEE reports, Algeria, Tanzania, and Togo operate electronic reporting surveillance systems at the national and sub-national levels [[30], [31], [32]]. In addition, the surveillance systems in Benin, Togo, Chad, and Sierra Leone have the capacity to collect laboratory data (Supplemental Table S2) [22,25,31,33].

A strong epidemiology workforce is crucial to rapidly respond to outbreaks as soon as they occur, as they conduct outbreak investigations, support contact tracing initiatives, and implement surveillance programs. African countries are successfully working to strengthen their epidemiology workforce. Over 75% of countries on the continent have programs to provide interested citizens with robust training in field epidemiology either in-country or abroad. Between the 2019 and 2021 GHS Index, the continent improved its epidemiology workforce capacity. While there is a dearth of field epidemiologists globally, the proportion of countries on the continent with at least one trained field epidemiologist per 200,000 people increased from 26% in 2019 to 28% in 2021—higher than the global average (Table 2). In addition to continued growth, countries should also ensure that there is a workforce strategy that ensures the appropriate distribution and deployment of trained epidemiologists.

Leveraging the momentum to strengthen surveillance systems on the African continent in the wake of COVID-19 and continuing to grow the epidemiology workforce should remain a priority as the threat of health emergencies is escalating amid a changing climate, urbanization, and migration [5].

Rapid response

Rapidly responding to outbreaks can save lives by stopping their spread. The GHS Index’s emergency preparedness and response planning indicator measures the extent to which countries have national public health emergency response plans in place. Globally, scores increased between 2019 and 2021 from 21.1 to 30.4. The African average score dramatically improved for this indicator– nearly tripling from 5.3 in 2019 to 15.6 in 2021. The largest driver boosting this indicator’s score was the increase in countries with a plan in place to implement nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during an epidemic or pandemic. In 2019, only three countries on the continent had a plan in place for the implementation of NPIs. By the 2021 GHS Index, this number had increased to 32 countries (Table 2). However, as of the 2021 GHS Index, only Rwanda, Nigeria, and Uganda had plans in place for more than one disease. Since 2021, the JEE reports for Benin, Tanzania, Nigeria, and Sierra Leone indicate the presence of multihazard emergency preparedness and response plans that include infectious diseases (Supplemental Table S2) [22,23,25,30].

Though many African countries made great progress between 2019 and 2021 on emergency preparedness and response planning, additional efforts to strengthen these capacities will enable countries on the continent to more quickly respond to future outbreaks.

Health system

Strong health systems enable countries to treat both individuals affected by a biological event and those who need routine care while also protecting healthcare workers. Countries on the African continent improved health capacity in clinics, hospitals, and community care centers between 2019 and 2021. In 2019, only one country (Nigeria) had either demonstrated the ability to or had a plan in place to expand isolation capacity in response to an infectious disease. By 2021, 22 countries on the continent had this capacity (Table 2). Since then, Tanzania and Sierra Leone also have the capacity to isolate patients based on their JEE reports that were published after 2021 (Supplemental Table S2) [25], [30].

The capacity to test and approve MCMs is critical to ensuring access to novel, effective treatments during an outbreak. Scores on the continent for this indicator remained unchanged between 2019 and 2021. As of the 2021 GHS Index, the global average score was 45.1, while Africa’s average score was 34.7. While Ethiopia is the only country on the continent with a national requirement for ethical review before beginning a clinical trial, an expedited process for approving clinical trials for unregistered MCMs, a government agency responsible for approving new MCMs for humans, and an expedited process for approving MCMs for human use during public health emergencies, other countries on the continent also have strong capacities. Ghana, Guinea, Kenya, Liberia, Mozambique, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and South Africa all have three of the four MCMs capacities measured by this GHS Index indicator. Many continent-wide efforts are in progress to strengthen the regulatory environment on the continent. The African Development Bank is working with the African Union/New Partnership for Africa’s Development (NEPAD) to support the AMA, a regulatory agency launched in November 2025, charged with improving access to high-quality, safe, effective products across the continent [11].

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated the importance of having a plan in place for dispensing MCMs for national use during a public health emergency and a system for receiving health personnel from other countries to respond to a public health emergency. These capacities are lacking around the world. The global average score for the MCMs and personnel deployment GHS Index indicators remained stable between 2019 and 2021, with 9% of countries having a plan in place for dispensing MCMs during a public health emergency and 11% of countries having a plan for receiving foreign health personnel during a public health emergency. On the continent, no country had either of these capacities in 2019 or 2021. Sierra Leone’s 2023 JEE indicates that the country now has a plan in place for dispensing MCMs in a public health emergency (Supplemental Table S2) [25]. Given the inequities in access to MCMs on the continent, African countries understandably have prioritized procuring these products over building out plans to dispense them. However, with the implementation of continental initiatives to support local manufacturing of MCMs, it will be important to develop systems for dispensing these products during a public health emergency.

Between 2019 and 2021, many African countries increased their facilities’ capacity to respond to infectious disease outbreaks. Continuing to expand capacities to isolate patients, testing and approving new MCMs, and deploying these MCMs and personnel will strengthen preparedness on the continent.

Commitments to international norms

The interconnected world calls for coordinated public health responses to biological threats. The African continent is a leader in demonstrating a commitment to global norms that promote coordination and collective action in addressing health threats (Supplemental Table S1). In the 2021 GHS Index, the continent performed better than the global average for the completion of the JEE and PVS. As of the 2021 GHS Index, 94% of African countries had completed and published a full JEE report in the past 5 years, compared to only 53% of countries globally. African countries are also leaders in creating National Action Plans for Health Security (NAPHS) to address gaps identified in the JEE. As of the 2021 GHS Index, 8 of the 15 total countries that had created NAPHS within the past 5 years were on the African continent. With respect to capacity for veterinary services, 22% of countries on the continent had completed and published a PVS report in the past 5 years, compared to only 11% globally (Table 2). African countries’ strong commitment to international norms and engagement with international evaluation processes demonstrates the continent’s leadership role in advancing global health security capacities.

Health security and Africa’s growing bioeconomy

Efforts to continue growing Africa’s bioeconomy will necessitate similar capacities as those identified as priorities in efforts to strengthen the capacities needed to prevent, detect, and respond to infectious disease threats. Biosafety and biosecurity frameworks, strong laboratory capacities, robust surveillance systems, and a trained workforce will bolster both the bioeconomy and defenses against disease threats. In turn, the bioeconomy can be leveraged to increase health and economic security through the development of medical countermeasures, such as diagnostic tests and vaccines, drought-resilient livestock, disease-resistant crops, and conservation of endangered species [34].

Global inequity in access to COVID-19 and mpox vaccines has demonstrated the importance of continuing to grow the bioeconomy on the African continent from a health perspective [2,35]. By November 2022, almost 2 years after high-income countries began vaccinating their citizens, only 25% of the population on the African continent had been fully vaccinated against COVID-19 [36]. Currently, there are 25 active vaccine manufacturers on the continent, but only 1% of vaccines are produced locally [37]. To address this inequity in future pandemics, the African Union has set a goal of locally manufacturing 60% of vaccines needed on the continent by 2040 [38]. Reaching this target will require additional expansions of the bioeconomy to support mechanisms such as Africa CDC’s Platform for Harmonized African Health Products Manufacturing [38]. Challenges to increasing activities that fall within the bioeconomy include a lack of strategic plans, limited biosafety regulations, and workforce constraints [39]. Currently, just 12 countries in Africa have bioeconomy strategies: South Africa, Ethiopia, Senegal, Nigeria, Ghana, Mali, Kenya, Mauritius, Mozambique, Namibia, Tanzania, and Uganda [34]. These plans can be leveraged for workforce development, sustainable land use, and resilience against the threats of climate change and infectious diseases [6,34]. A case study modeling the impact of a proposed project to locally sequence 1000 Moroccan genomes found that the initiative would have an over 3-fold return on investment and demonstrated value to agriculture, fishing, research and development, and education with large economic contributions [34].

Sustaining momentum to increase health security capacities will ensure the continent is equipped for recurring infectious disease emergencies, climate change, and other threats in an ever-changing world. Strengthening capacities such as biosecurity, biosafety, surveillance systems to identify biological threats, and MCM development, testing, and approval will also support the continent’s expanding bioeconomy.

Limitations

In this analysis, we discussed indicators that are aligned with ongoing priorities on the continent to provide an overview of the health security status of African countries. From a review of GHS Index data along with recent JEE reports, we found improvements in health security capacities in epidemiology workforce capacity and plans for the implementation of nonpharmaceutical interventions. Gaps remain, especially in areas of biosecurity, biosafety, and dual-use research of concern—though some countries demonstrated recent improvement in these areas.

This work is strengthened by an exploration of the connection between health security capacities and the burgeoning African bioeconomy. Additionally, this description can be utilized by policymakers to help determine where to invest resources. This study was nevertheless subject to limitations. The most recent GHS Index update was published in 2021. Much has changed related to health security since that time, and a new iteration of the GHS Index would capture this progress. We aimed to address this by reviewing JEE reports published on the WHO website that were conducted since 2021. However, countries that have not done a JEE exercise since 2021 may also have improved their health security capacities since the 2021 GHS Index was published. We also only included JEE reports since 2021 that are published on the WHO website—there are, however, countries that have conducted a JEE since 2021 whose reports are not yet publicly available. The indicators presented in the main analysis were selected thematically and through unstructured consultations with public health leaders on the African continent. This is not to say that the other capacities measured by the GHS Index are less important. Results for all indicators can be found in the supplemental materials. Additionally, as one of the goals of the GHS Index is to further transparency and accountability, it is dependent on publicly available documentation [14]. Countries may have plans in place that are not publicly available—these are not captured in the GHS Index. Because the Index relied on publicly available information, countries can increase their scores both through capacity gains and through improving public access to information about their health security capacities. Preliminary GHS Index results are shared with each country through official diplomatic channels to give countries an opportunity to review and comment. However, the data may not always reach the technical experts who would be best positioned to review the findings [14]. The GHS Index is a tool that countries can use for benchmarking and resource allocation, but it is not meant to be a predictive measure. Even if capacities are in place, it does not necessarily mean that they will be implemented effectively in an emergency [3]. Lastly, the GHS Index measures national capacity, but there are a number of continent-wide efforts underway, many coordinated by Africa CDC, to improve health security [2,11,26]. Nonetheless, the GHS Index is a good starting point for assessing progress towards health security and serves as a complementary tool for the JEE as countries fulfill their IHR requirements.

Conclusions

Countries on the African continent have been making great strides in improving health security since the implementation of the IHR in 2005. Characterizing the current state of preparedness for biological threats on the continent can support leaders in effectively allocating resources and enacting policies that will support the expansion of the bioeconomy and continue bolstering preparedness. Doing so will require multi-sectoral collaboration and new partnerships. In an era of increasing pandemic threats, continuing to improve health security capacities is critical to safeguarding economic and development achievements accomplished in the past two decades and building a world that is safer from threats to health.
