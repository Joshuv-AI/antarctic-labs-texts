Reprocessable Disulfide‐Based Vitrimers with Adhesive Properties
Authors: Sasan Moradi, Osman Konuray, Javier G Valverde‐Bastidas, Xavier Fernández‐Francos, Xavier Ramis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA family of disulfide (S–S) containing thiol–vinyl–epoxy dynamic networks are presented. The networks are obtained through a dual‐curing mechanism wherein a photoinduced thiol–ene reaction at room temperature is followed by a thermally activated, tertiary amine catalyzed thiol–epoxy curing stage, enabling controlled network development. Cured materials show reprocessability thanks to heat‐triggered disulfide bond exchange. The influence of catalyst structure on curing behavior, thermomechanical properties, stress relaxation, and this reprocessability is systematically investigated. These solvent‐free materials with a unique ternary composition exhibit efficient network rearrangement and successful thermal reprocessing, demonstrating their potential as sustainable materials.Keywords: covalent adaptable networks, disulfide exchange, dual curing, reprocessability, stress relaxation, thiol–ene, thiol–epoxy, vitrimers

Introduction

Thermosetting polymers are extensively used because of their high stiffness, dimensional stability, and resistance to heat and chemicals. However, their permanent crosslinked structure prevents melting and significantly limits recycling possibilities after curing. As a consequence, most thermoset wastes are disposed of by landfill or incineration, raising increasing environmental and economic concerns. In contrast to thermoplastics, which can be reprocessed by simple melting, conventional thermosets lack any mechanism for network rearrangement once cured [1, 2]. In recent years, dynamic covalent chemistry has enabled the development of covalent adaptable networks (CANs), in which exchangeable covalent bonds allow topology rearrangement under external stimuli while maintaining the integrity of the polymer network. These materials combine the mechanical robustness and chemical resistance of thermosets with the reprocessability associated typically with thermoplastics. By incorporating reversible bond exchange reactions within a permanently crosslinked structure, CANs enable stress relaxation, reshaping, self‐healing, and recycling without complete depolymerization of the network.

Vitrimers constitute a subclass of associative CANs where bond exchange occurs without loss of crosslink density, enabling viscoelastic flow at elevated temperatures while preserving the network architecture [3, 4, 5, 6]. Their viscosity decreases in an Arrhenius‐like manner upon heating, allowing thermoforming without destroying network connectivity. Their behavior is generally described by two characteristic temperatures: the glass transition temperature (T g), and the topology freezing temperature (T v), above which exchange reactions become sufficiently rapid to enable macroscopic flow [4]. In most vitrimer systems, T g lies below T v, resulting in Arrhenius‐type viscosity behavior dominated by exchange kinetics. In less common systems where T v is lower than T g, viscosity may follow WLF behavior near T g before transitioning to Arrhenius behavior at higher temperature [5, 6]. The relative position of T g and T v therefore governs the balance between dimensional stability during service and reprocessability at elevated temperatures.

A wide range of dynamic chemistries have been explored to design CANs and vitrimers, including transesterification [7, 8, 9], transamination, transcarbamoylation [10], transalkylation, boronic ester exchange, siloxane equilibrium, imine exchange [11, 12, 13] and disulfide exchange [14, 15, 16, 17]. These exchange reactions provide a versatile platform for tuning relaxation kinetics, activation energies, and long‐term mechanical stability. Importantly, theoretical and experimental analyses have shown that the dynamic behavior of such networks strongly depends on the fraction of exchangeable bonds and the presence of an underlying “permanent network” structure. When the dynamic bond content falls below a critical threshold, complete stress relaxation may be hindered, whereas above this threshold the network can rearrange efficiently. This concept, rooted in gelation theory and statistically‐based network build‐up modeling, highlights the importance of compositional control in vitrimer design [18].

Among dynamic covalent chemistries, disulfide bonds have attracted particular interest due to their relatively low activation energy for exchange and their ability to promote stress relaxation and reprocessability under moderate thermal conditions [19, 20]. The relatively weak S–S bond enables exchange even at comparatively low temperatures and, in some systems, without additional catalysts. In addition, the presence of thiol functionalities may further accelerate exchange through thiol–disulfide mechanisms [20]. Disulfide‐containing networks have demonstrated fast stress relaxation and efficient recyclability, and in certain formulations the combination of disulfide exchange with additional mechanisms such as transesterification has been shown to broaden the dynamic temperature window. Furthermore, disulfide‐based dynamic networks have also shown promise in adhesive applications. For example, adaptable thermoset adhesives incorporating disulfide bonds were shown to significantly enhance lap shear strength after thermal annealing due to the ability of the network to rearrange, relax internal stresses generated during curing, and improve interfacial wetting between substrates [20]. These results highlight the potential of disulfide dynamic chemistry not only for enabling recyclability but also for improving adhesion performance in crosslinked polymer systems. However, increasing dynamic bond concentration may also enhance creep at ambient temperatures, which represents a critical limitation for structural and adhesive applications. Therefore, optimizing the balance between dynamic adaptability and mechanical stability remains a central challenge.

A complementary strategy to improve processability is sequential dual curing, in which two distinct reactions are activated independently. In thiol–ene/thiol–epoxy systems, UV irradiation rapidly generates an intermediate network via thiol–ene photopolymerization, while a second thermally activated stage consumes remaining thiol groups through thiol–epoxy reactions, completing network formation [1, 2]. This separation allows intermediate materials to be handled or shaped before final curing and is particularly advantageous for adhesive applications. Moreover, dual‐curing systems provide additional flexibility to tailor network architecture, crosslink density, and the distribution of functional groups that may participate in dynamic exchange reactions. Several authors have developed thiol‐ene/thiol‐epoxy hybrids intended for dental restorative materials to achieve high crosslinking density while minimizing shrinkage and internal stress. These materials were prepared via a sequential curing process, consisting of UV photocuring followed by thermal treatment. However, the resulting materials lacked adaptable covalent bonds, limiting their reprocessability [21, 22].

In the present work, disulfide‐containing thiol–vinyl–epoxy formulations were designed to obtain recyclable vitrimer‐like thermosetting materials through a dual‐curing strategy. To this end, an S‐S‐containing monomer was synthesized through a facile synthetic route using commercially available reagents. By incorporating disulfide linkages within a sequentially cured network and systematically varying catalyst chemistry, the interplay between curing kinetics, network structure, and dynamic exchange processes was investigated. Particular attention was devoted to understanding how catalyst type influences thermomechanical performance, stress relaxation behavior, topology rearrangement capability, and recyclability. Through this approach, the study aims to establish structure–property relationships that enable the design of reprocessable thermosets with controlled relaxation behavior and minimized creep while preserving mechanical integrity. The development of vitrimeric adhesives capable of enabling substrate recovery under moderate temperature conditions represents a significant advantage of this system. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of a dual thiol–ene/thiol–epoxy system incorporating dynamic disulfide bonds.

Experimental Sections

Reagents and Preparation of Materials

The following chemicals were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Madrid, Spain): 4,4′‐dithiodibutyric acid (DTBA, 95%), allyl 2,3‐epoxypropyl ether (AGE), triethanolamine (TEA, ≥99.0%), trimethylolpropane tris(3‐mercaptopropionate) (S3, ≥95.0%), and diphenyl(2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (TPO, 97%). All reagents were used as received without further purification. Bisphenol A diglycidyl ether (DG, Mw = 374 g mol− 1), kindly supplied by Po.Int.Er Srl. (Valfenera, Italy), was used as the epoxy monomer.

To catalyze the thiol–epoxy reaction and disulfide exchange, five catalysts, purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Madrid, Spain) were evaluated: dibutylamine (DBA, 129.2 g mol−1), 1‐methylimidazole (1MI, 82.11 g mol−1), 1,5‐diazabicyclo[4.3.0]non‐5‐ene (DBN, 124.18 g mol−1), imidazole (IMZ, 68.08 g mol−1), and 1‐allyl‐1H‐imidazole (ALIMZ, 108.14 g mol−1).

A diallyl monomer containing disulfide bonds, denoted as DAG (Mw = 466.5 g mol− 1), was synthesized through an epoxy–acid reaction between 4,4′‐dithiodibutyric acid (DTBA) and allyl 2,3‐epoxypropyl ether (AGE), following a procedure previously reported in our earlier work [15, 23]. The synthetic route is illustrated in Figure S1.

The investigated materials were prepared through a dual‐curing strategy consisting of two consecutive reactions (Figure 1). In the first stage, the liquid formulation underwent UV‐induced thiol–ene photopolymerization at room temperature. For all formulations, the reaction was initiated using 2 phr of TPO as photoinitiator (relative to the total formulation mass). Depending on the monomer feed ratio, this step yields a stable intermediate material that may remain as a highly viscous liquid or become a partially gelled solid. We selected composition in which half of the thiol groups will react with the DAG component in the 1st stage, while the remaining groups will react with DG. This stoichiometry was selected to prevent complete gelation at this stage, enabling the formation of processable intermediate materials (see Supporting Information for details).

(a) Chemical structures of the monomers used in this study: S3, DAG, and DG. (b)Schematic illustration of the dual‐curing process involving UV‐induced thiol–ene photopolymerization followed by a thermally activated thiol–epoxy reaction, leading from a soluble monomer mixture to a fully crosslinked, insoluble polymer network.

In the second stage, thermal curing proceeds through a base‐catalyzed thiol–epoxy click reaction between the remaining thiol and epoxy groups [24, 25]. This well‐established reaction leads to the formation of a fully cross‐linked network with enhanced thermal and mechanical properties. Minor compositional adjustments were made depending on the catalyst used. Dynamic Bond Exchange Mechanisms

The disulfide exchange, shown in Figure S2a for this system, is generally accepted to proceed via a dissociative pathway; however, several studies have suggested that in the presence of basic catalysts it may follow an associative mechanism [26]. In fact, disulfide exchange reactions can occur even in the absence of a catalyst, particularly when the disulfide bonds are connected to aromatic structures. In contrast, for aliphatic disulfides such as those used in this work, the use of a catalyst is recommended to reduce recycling temperatures and times. Tertiary amines are commonly employed for this purpose [19]. In the present system, tertiary amines are particularly suitable since they also catalyze the thiol–epoxy reaction involved in the second curing stage. Dynamicity would be further enhanced by thiol–disulfide exchange reactions that may occur in the system. These S–S/S–H exchanges typically proceed at significantly higher rates than disulfide–disulfide exchanges [19, 20]. Another possible exchange mechanism in the studied formulations is transesterification. During the thiol–epoxy curing stage, hydroxyl groups are formed, and in combination with the ester groups already present in the thiol crosslinker, can enable transesterification reactions within the network. Previous work from our group demonstrated that these transesterification reactions can enable complete stress relaxation and full recyclability of the materials, provided that a permanent network is not formed [27, 28]. However, these reactions generally occur at relatively high temperatures and therefore are not expected to significantly contribute to stress relaxation within the temperature range where S–S exchange reactions are active. Figure S2 summarizes the two potential exchange mechanisms present in the thiol–ene–epoxy formulations investigated in this work.

Results and Discussion

Curing Conditions

Photo‐DSC experiments were performed at 30 °C to quantify the reaction enthalpy of the photo‐curing stage and estimate the conversion. The heat flow signal was integrated after baseline correction to obtain the reaction enthalpy Δh1 (J/g). For improved comparability, the heat was also normalized per double bond, Δh1 (kJ per C = C). Figure S3 shows the heat flow as a function of time for DBA system, as an example. The exothermic process starts upon switching on the UV lamp (t ≈ 60 s), displays a sharp peak, and rapidly decays. From the same data set, the cumulative conversion was calculated by integrating the heat flow signal. As shown in Figure S3, the conversion exceeds 95% within a few minutes. The heat values normalized per double bond close to 70 kJ per C = C, which agrees well with literature values and indicates an almost complete conversion of the double bonds [29].

According to the FTIR spectroscopic assignments, photo‐curing stage is dominated by the thiol–ene reaction involving the consumption of C = C and S–H groups, whereas the epoxy contribution is negligible at this stage. This confirms the sequential nature of the dual‐curing process: first the thiol–ene reaction during UV irradiation, followed by the thiol–epoxy reaction during the thermal stage (see Figures S4–S7). In previous studies, similar sequential behavior was reported when photocuring preceded thermal curing. In contrast, when the curing sequence was reversed, epoxy homopolymerization could occur during the first stage, leaving unreacted thiol groups [21, 30]. FTIR also confirms that near‐complete curing is achieved in both stages under the selected curing conditions. Details of this analysis is presented in the Supporting Information.

After the photo‐curing step, the second curing stage was monitored by dynamic DSC heating until complete conversion. The observed exotherm in Figure 2a is attributed to the epoxy ring‐opening reaction; therefore, the reaction enthalpies were also reported per epoxy equivalent. The heat flow curves (dh/dt vs temperature) reveal differences in reaction rate depending on the catalyst employed. The systems containing ALIMZ, and 1MI are activated at lower temperatures and show relatively sharp peaks. DBN and IMZ exhibit intermediate behavior, whereas DBA requires higher temperatures and displays a broader peak with a tail, indicating slower reaction kinetics. This behavior is consistent with the catalytic mechanism of DBA, since the active tertiary amine species must first be generated by reaction between the secondary amine of DBA and an epoxy group.

The reaction enthalpies of the thermal stage (Δh2 ), expressed in J/g and kJ per epoxy equivalent (kJ/ee), are summarized in Table 2. These values fall within a narrow range and are consistent with literature data [31], indicating that the epoxy reaction proceeds to completion in all systems despite the kinetic differences.

The conversion profiles obtained from integration of the calorimetric signal are shown in Figure 2b. These show a sigmoidal dependence on temperature and reach a plateau close to full conversion for all formulations. The relative reaction rates follow the same order observed in the heat flow curves. In particular, the DBA‐catalyzed system requires higher temperatures to initiate the reaction and to reach the same conversion as the other catalysts. In addition, a double sigmoidal behavior is observed for DBA: the first stage at lower temperature is attributed to the formation of the active catalytic species, while the second stage at higher temperature corresponds to the actual curing reaction.

To further illustrate the influence of the catalyst and temperature on the curing kinetics, the conversion profiles determined by isoconversional kinetic analysis simulation for the 1MI system at 90 °C and the DBA system at 90 °C and 120 °C are shown in Figure 2c. The results clearly demonstrate the faster catalytic activity of 1MI, with conversions exceeding 90% before 30 min at 90°C. In contrast, the DBA‐catalyzed system exhibits significantly slower conversion at the same temperature. However, increasing the curing temperature to 120°C markedly accelerates the reaction, shifting the conversion curve toward shorter times and confirming the strong temperature dependence of the DBA‐catalyzed thiol–epoxy reaction. These observations are consistent with the dynamic DSC results presented in Figure 2a,b, where DBA is seen to require higher temperatures to achieve comparable reaction rates. Details of the kinetic analysis linked to the simulation method is presented in the Supporting Information. The same simulation method was also used to demonstrate the high storage stability of the materials after photocuring. Specifically, at 25°C, 1MI requires 4 h and DBA 15 h to initiate the second curing stage, remaining workable for significantly longer periods.

Based on these results, together with the FTIR analysis, the first curing stage was carried out at room temperature under UV irradiation using a Photopol UV chamber with an exposure time of 10 min. The second curing stage was subsequently performed in a Memmert natural convection oven. Isothermal curing was conducted for 2 h at 90°C for all systems, except for the DBA‐containing formulation, which required 2 h at 120°C to achieve complete curing. The resulting materials exhibited mean glass transition temperatures of −33°C for the intermediate state and −1°C for the fully cured network.

Thermogravimetric Analysis

To evaluate the thermal stability of the cured materials, dynamic thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed up to 600°C under an inert atmosphere. The curves shown in Figure S8 exhibit a single main degradation step at relatively high temperatures, with a final residue of approximately 10% at 600°C. Only moderate differences among formulations are observed, indicating that all systems are thermally stable within the typical processing range (< 300 °C) and suitable for reprocessing without significant weight loss.

Isothermal TGA was then performed at 90–110°C for 6 h under nitrogen. The results at 90°C (Figure 3) show very slight mass losses (1%–2% after several hours), which nevertheless allow differentiation between formulations. 1MI and DBA exhibit the highest stability, with minimal mass drift, while ALIMZ also shows good stability. DBN and IMZ display intermediate behavior, with moderate mass loss during the isothermal treatment.

Isothermal TGA at 90°C showing remaining weight (%) vs time (a). Dynamic DSC at 10 K min−1 showing the evolution of T g with time at 90°C of the DBA‐based system (b).

The observed mass losses are attributed to moisture desorption and/or volatilization of residual low‐molecular‐weight species not incorporated into the network. Overall, 1MI and DBA are the most suitable candidates for processing at 90°C, combining good isothermal stability with complete conversion.

The temporal stability of fully cured samples was further assessed by holding 1MI and DBA formulations at 90°C for different times (0–60 min; 2–12 h), followed by DSC analysis. For DBA, the T g remains essentially unchanged over 12 h (from −0.5°C to +0.5°C, Figure 3b), while 1MI shows only a slight increase (see Figure S9). These results indicate that no significant post‐curing or loss of volatile species occurs at 90°C.

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis (DMA)

The stress relaxation ability of the network at 100°C was evaluated tensile mode. A small strain step was applied and the modulus E(t) was monitored. Additional details regarding the stress relaxation test configuration are provided in the Supporting Information. The evolution of the normalized modulus E/E0 as a function of time is presented in Figure 4. The dashed line indicates E/E0 = 1/e, which is used to define the characteristic relaxation time τ*.

Stress relaxation curves of the networks at 100°C, showing the normalized modulus E/E0 versus time for different catalysts. The dashed line (E/E 0 = 1/e) indicates the characteristic relaxation time τ*.

The results indicate clear differences among the catalysts. The order, from fastest to slowest relaxation, is: DBN > 1MI > DBA > ALIMZ ≫ IMZ. At 100°C, IMZ catalyzes a very slow relaxation, with E/E0 decreasing over timescales of tens of minutes, making it unsuitable for rapid reprocessing. In contrast, DBN is the most efficient catalyst, facilitating a rapid decrease in E/E0 within a few minutes. 1MI and DBA provides a good balance between relaxation rate and performance. ALIMZ exhibits a slightly lower relaxation rate than 1MI and DBA. The adjustments made to the formulations for the different catalysts are not expected to significantly affect the relative ranking of the relaxation processes, since all formulations contain the same catalyst equivalents per sample weight (see Table S2). DBN represents the only exception, exhibiting the fastest relaxation despite containing fewer catalyst equivalents per sample weight.

After the screening at 100°C, the cycle‐to‐cycle stabilities of selected catalysts were investigated. The protocol consisted of applying several relaxation cycles (using the same initial strain and the same recording time) and comparing the E/E 0 vs time curves between cycles. The overlap of the curves indicates a stable network, whereas separation or a progressive shift from one curve to the next suggests an evolution of the network (e.g., post‐curing, bond exchange or scission, volatilization, etc.).

For the DBA system (Figure 5a) at 100°C, the E/E0 –time curves show a gradual shift toward longer relaxation times during the first relaxation cycles. From cycle 1 to cycle 4, the characteristic relaxation time τ* (defined at E/E 0 = 1/e, corresponding to the time required for significant stress relaxation and commonly used as a practical criterion for network reconfiguration). However, after the fourth cycle the curves become nearly superimposed, suggesting that the network reaches a stable state. For DBN (Figure 5b), the successive relaxation cycles (same strain and recording time) show that the E/E 0–time curve progressively shifts to longer times: the characteristic time at E/E 0 = 1/e (τ*, dashed line) increases from cycle 1 to cycle 5. This behavior indicates that the network relaxes progressively more slowly, which is consistent with a loss of catalytic activity. The low molecular weight of DBN, coupled with the fact that it remains unbound to the network, suggests that its volatilization may be responsible for the observed behavior. For this reason, DBN is not considered suitable for repeated cycles or for long‐term recycling processes. ALIMZ and 1MI exhibit a similar behavior to DBN.

Stress relaxation behavior (E/E 0 vs relaxation time) at 100°C of samples containing DBA (a), and DBN (b). The dashed line indicates the E/E 0 = 1/e threshold used to determine the characteristic relaxation time (τ*).

Among the studied formulations, those containing DBA exhibited the most stable relaxation behavior. After reaching this stabilized state, the material was mechanically recycled and subjected again to relaxation measurements. The recycled sample displayed relaxation curves very similar to those of the stabilized network, confirming that the dynamic network structure remains stable and that the material retains its relaxation capability after reprocessing. The reaction between the secondary amine of DBA and epoxy groups yields covalently network‐bound tertiary amines. This immobilization suppresses volatilization and is presumably responsible for the enhanced stability observed in the DBA‐based systems. Despite all studied catalysts allowing for complete material relaxation, DBA was considered the most suitable candidate for an in‐depth study of relaxation and recycling.

The temperature dependence of stress relaxation was further evaluated for DBA by analyzing E/E 0 as a function of time in the temperature range of 80–110°C (Figure 6). As the temperature increases, the curves shift clearly toward shorter times. At 110°C, the E/E 0 = 1/e threshold (dashed line) is reached within minutes, whereas at 100°C several tens of minutes are required (up to nearly one hour). At 90°C, relaxation requires several hours, and at 80°C many hours. The Arrhenius plot of the relaxation time (logarithmic scale) as a function of 1000/T for the DBA system is shown in Figure 6. A good linear relationship is observed (R2 ≈ 0.99), indicating a well‐defined thermally activated relaxation process. Within the temperature range of 80–110°C, the relaxation times required to reach the threshold follow the expected temperature dependence of the network reconfiguration process.

Adhesion Test

Lap shear tests were performed on DBA formulations to evaluate their adhesive performance. The force–displacement curves obtained at room temperature and at 80°C are shown in Figure 7. At room temperature, the joint exhibits a significantly higher maximum stress (τmax = 1.89 ± 0.11 MPa) and a gradual increase in force up to failure, indicating strong adhesion between the aluminum substrates. The lap shear test results are similar to those of other elastic adhesives used in highly flexible structural joints [33]. In addition, the dual‐curing approach enables the formation of an intermediate material that improves interfacial contact with the substrate prior to final curing, contributing to the observed adhesion performance.

Force–displacement curves from lap shear tests of the DBA adhesive joints at room temperature (RT) and 80°C.

In contrast, testing at moderate temperature of 80°C results in a noticeably lower maximum stress (τmax = 0.68 ± 0.16 MPa) and earlier failure. The decrease in shear strength at elevated temperature is consistent with the thermally activated dynamic exchange reactions within the dynamic network, which increase chain mobility and reduce the effective stiffness of the adhesive layer. Importantly, the reduced strength at higher temperature facilitates debonding of the joint, enabling the separation and recovery of the aluminum substrates upon heating. These results confirm the balance between adequate adhesion at service conditions and the possibility of controlled disassembly, which is a key feature of vitrimer‐based recyclable adhesive systems. Experimental details of the lap shear test configuration and additional information are provided in the Supporting Information.

Recycling and Reprocessability

To evaluate recyclability, the cured samples were ground, recompacted in a press, and subsequently reprocessed at 100°C. After recycling, macroscopic welding, the glass transition temperature (T g, DSC), and the relaxation capability (E/E 0–time), which serves as the functional criterion for network reconfigurability, were evaluated. Both pristine and recycled samples were visually examined, confirming the absence of significant entrapped air bubbles.

For the DBA system, tensile tests were performed on several specimens of the original material to assess reproducibility, and the results were compared with those of the recycled sample. As shown in Figure 8, the stress–strain curves of the original material display consistent mechanical behavior, while the recycled sample exhibits a similar initial slope. Accordingly, the Young's modulus remains practically unchanged after recycling (E ≈ 4.35 MPa). However, the recycled sample fails at lower strain, which is consistent with the presence of interfacial minor defects between the reprocessed fragments which are visible from the photograph given as inset of Figure 8. From the same photograph the relatively uniform appearance and good color stability after recycling can be appreciated. These are in agreement with the high thermal stability discussed earlier (See Figure 3a). The glass transition temperature of the material remains unchanged during the cycling process.

Stress–strain curves of DBA before and after recycling (100°C, 45 min), showing similar Young's modulus, while the recycled sample fails at lower strain. Inset: Photographs showing the results of the hot press molding of DBA under 10 MPa pressure for 45 min at 100°C.

Stress relaxation measurements at 100°C further confirm that the recycled DBA network remains capable of dynamic reconfiguration (See Figure 5a), although the relaxation occurs at a slower rate compared to that of the original material. The E/E0 –time curves shift toward longer times but still cross the relaxation threshold within practical time scales, confirming that the material can be efficiently reprocessed under the studied conditions. A plausible explanation is that the original material may contain residual thiol groups that accelerate thiol–disulfide exchange reactions. During repeated relaxation cycles and recycling, these S–H groups may be consumed or oxidized, causing the relaxation process to become dominated by disulfide exchange (S–S), which is intrinsically slower [19].

Conclusions

Disulfide‐containing thiol–vinyl–epoxy networks were successfully developed through a sequential dual‐curing strategy, enabling controlled formation of intermediate materials followed by final network consolidation. This approach provides processing advantages, particularly for adhesive applications, by improving interfacial contact with the substrate prior to full curing.

DSC and FTIR analyses confirmed that both curing stages proceed to completion. The intermediate materials exhibit a glass transition temperature of approximately −33°C, making them suitable for adhesive applications in which the final properties are developed during the second thermal curing stage. The fully cured materials, with glass transition temperatures slightly below room temperature, showed strong potential for elastomeric applications. When higher mechanical performance is required, the properties can be tailored through the same dual‐curing strategy by employing formulations containing more rigid or higher‐functionality monomers.

The incorporation of disulfide bonds introduces dynamic covalent exchange, allowing efficient stress relaxation and vitrimer‐like behavior at moderate temperatures. Catalyst structure plays a key role in governing both curing kinetics and relaxation behavior, with DBA showing the best balance between stability and efficient reprocessability, due to its covalent incorporation into the network during the second curing stage.

The materials exhibit good thermal stability, effective adhesion, and efficient recyclability without significant loss of properties. Overall, this work demonstrates that combining dual‐curing strategies with dynamic disulfide chemistry is an effective route to design recyclable thermosetting materials with potential in adhesive applications.
