Understanding food addiction in obesity: a genetic perspective
Authors: Elvira Anna Carbone, Marianna Rania, Federica Catalano, Ettore D’Onofrio, Renato de Filippis, Daria Quirino, Matteo Aloi, Marta Letizia Hribal, Teresa Vanessa Fiorentino, Cristina Segura-Garcia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFood addiction (FA) has gained more scientific attention but needs deeper understanding. Data indicates that the central melanocortin (MC) system through the MC4 receptor (MC4R) and its polymorphisms play a crucial role in the regulation of eating behaviour and in the motivation for the rewarding properties of food potentially leading to obesity. This may also contribute to the emergence of altered reward-related behaviors such as FA. The study aims to evaluate the genetic contribution of rs17782313, rs12970134, rs10871777, rs6567160, rs17700144 MC4R polymorphisms to the development of FA and to assess the association between these MC4R variations and clinical features.MethodsEating (EDE-Q, BES, NEQ, GQ) and general psychopathology (BDI-II, STAI-S, DERS) were evaluated in patients with obesity with and without FA. Y-FAS 2.0 was used to assess FA. A blood sample was collected from all patients for the genotyping of MC4R polymorphisms.ResultsAll the polymorphisms were equally distributed between groups except for rs17782313. A direct association between rs17782313 with FA was evident. Patients with FA and with C allele showed higher risk of FA compared to group without FA. There was a significant effect of rs17782313 on psychopathological variables in patients with FA. Allele C carriers exhibited higher anxiety and depression than T carriers.ConclusionsThe rs17782313 of the MC4R showed an association with FA. A significant direct influence of C allele on anxiety and depression emerged in the group with FA but not in patients without FA.Keywords: Food addiction, Genetic, Melanocortin system, MC4R, Obesity

Background

Food addiction (FA) was first proffered in 1956 as “a common pattern of symptoms descriptively similar to those of other addictive processes” [1], referring to the conceptualization that foods, particularly highly palatable ones, may be the subject of addiction. In this light, the consumption of palatable foods and the addictive psychological and behavioral symptoms are characteristics of FA, including cravings, tolerance, limited control of substance intake and “withdrawal-like” physiological effects if food intake is stopped or reduced [2]. The concept of FA has been widely discussed and debated in recent years [3–5]. It has recently gained more scientific attention as researchers aim to better define, classify and understand its underlined mechanisms and clinical relevance [3, 6, 7].

FA is highly prevalent in individuals with overweight or obesity (OB) and others eating disorders (EDs) [8] such as binge eating disorder (BED) [9, 10].

Studies have reported a direct relationship between the severity of FA and the associated eating and general psychopathology symptoms in patients with both BED and obesity [11–13]. Patients reporting FA have higher depressive symptoms, negative affect, stress, emotion dysregulation, lower self-esteem [14–16] as well as altered eating behaviors, including binge eating, grazing and night eating [12, 14, 17–21].

On the other hand, FA seems associated with lower rates of full remission, higher rate of dropout [22], poorer weight loss, altered eating, and lifestyle behaviors in patients undergoing bariatric surgery [23–25].

Even though FA may seem similar to BED or OB and these conditions share common neurobiological features [26, 27], it is important to remark that while BED and OB are classified and diagnosable disorders, FA remains a controversial concept which has not yet been recognized as a diagnostic entity. Thus, the need of deeper understanding of FA.

The melanocortin (MC) system was studied for its involvement in the food intake, body weight and energy balance regulation [28, 29] but also in the control of appetite, and of the motivation for the rewarding properties of food [30, 31]. Anatomical and pharmacological data suggest that satiety, hunger and rewarding aspects associated with feeding are regulated in different brain sites where the MC receptors (MCRs) are located [32]. Five MCRs have been identified explaining different effects: while MC1R, MC2R and MC5R are primarily found in the periphery, MC3R and MC4R are predominantly expressed in the brain [33]. The MC3R is predominantly expressed in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus and moderately localized in the limbic system, in ventral tegmental area, in the nucleus of raphe, in the nucleus of the septum and in the nucleus of the thalamus [34, 35]. In contrast with MC3R, the MC4R has the highest level of expression in the central nervous system [36] in particular in hypothalamic sites (i.e., paraventricular nucleus, the medial preoptic area, anterior hypothalamic nucleus, ventromedial nucleus of the hypothalamus, dorsomedial nucleus of the hypothalamus, tuberomammillary nucleus and other several hypothalamic areas), in the brainstem and in the cortex, hippocampus, corpus striatum, amygdala, thalamus, spinal cord and also identified in the peripheral nervous system [34, 37, 38].

Considering that MC4R is widely expressed in the hypothalamus, brainstem, and other brain regions of the mesolimbic system [38, 39], it is also the most explored in preclinical and clinical studies. Supporting findings demonstrated that MC4R contributes to regulate energy balance, weight gain [34, 38, 40, 41] and appetite by modulating the brain reward circuitry [29, 31]. Recent investigations into the MC4R have unveiled its involvement in reward-related behaviors, particularly in response to the hedonic properties of highly palatable food, a potent natural reinforcer [30–32]. In addition, MC4R plays a role in stress response, negative emotions, anxiety and depressive symptoms, keys factors triggering altered eating behaviors [42, 43].

The MC4R gene is located at position 21.32 on the long arm (q) of chromosome 18 and consists of one exon with a total length of 1.9 kb. Different polymorphisms of the MC4R affect gene function participating in the MC4R expression and the regulation of the interaction between the genes of the reward system and the MC4R being responsible for increased binge eating, altered eating behavior and increased Body Mass index (BMI) [44]. In a recent GWAS, various single-nucleotide-polymorphisms (SNPs) within the MC4R gene were identified. These SNPs were found to be associated with an elevated BMI and an increased risk of developing obesity [45]. Notably, one of these SNPs, namely rs17782313, has gained significance in the context of obesity [46] and altered eating behaviors [47]. In a recent meta-analysis, C allele has been linked with higher risk of obesity compared to T ancestral allele [48]. The rs17782313 variant of the MC4R and the rs9939609 are correlated with weight gain and with sweet eating, emotional eating, food craving [49, 50] and binge eating [51]. Very recently, the rs17782313 MC4R polymorphism was associated with the presence of severe binge eating [48] and pinches [52]. Rs17782313-C allele, the risk allele, may modulate appetite-related phenotypes in both children and adults, supporting a regulatory role of this genetic variant of the MC4R gene [52]. Other studies have reported an association between rs17782313 C allele and eating behaviors (i.e., grazing, hyperphagia) but results were incoherent [53]. It was demonstrated that C allele carriers of rs17782313 show altered food intake as well as reduced levels of IL-6 and high levels of ghrelin [48]. Finally, it was suggested a possible genetic interaction between the genes of the reward system and the MC4R supporting the possible link between dopamine and melanocortin pathways as responsible for increased binge eating and subsequent altered eating behaviour [48], food craving, elevated BMI, and depressive symptomatology in carriers of rs17782313 [44]. Among other polymorphisms, recent but limited data show that the A risk allele of rs12970134 is associated with increased risk of obesity [54–56], insulin resistance [57] and altered lipid metabolism [58]. Rs10871777, rs657160 and rs17700144 showed an association with BMI and waist circumference in large cohort [59, 60] and risk of obesity [61].

Due to these considerations, the MC system has been suggested to play a crucial role not only in obesity [62, 63], but also in several dysfunctional eating habits [33, 50] that may contribute to obesity, overseeing the motivation associated with the pleasurable aspects of food [64]. Dysfunction in this system could be linked to a disruption of normal regulatory processes and increased susceptibility to loss of control in food intake, potentially resulting in altered eating pattern such as FA.

Thus, the study aims to evaluate the genetic contribution of rs17782313, rs12970134, rs10871777, rs6567160, rs17700144 MC4R polymorphisms to the development of FA and to assess the association between the MC4R variations and clinical features.

Methods

Participants

Individuals seeking weight loss treatment for obesity were consecutively admitted to the diagnostic and therapeutic network of care for obesity (PDTA Obesità) of the University Hospital “Renato Dulbecco” of Catanzaro (Italy) and were screened for eligibility and invited to participate in the study. Data collection ran between May 2021 and December 2023. This PDTA refers to an outpatient program that includes multidisciplinary specialized visits (i.e., internal medicine, psychiatry, and surgery, as appropriate) for the assessment and management of people suffering from obesity.

The inclusion criteria for participation in the studies were individuals aged between 18 and 65 years, with a BMI equal or greater than 30.0 kg/m2, the ability to respond to self-report questionnaires, consent to genetic analysis, and valid informed consent to participate.

Severe medical conditions, (i.e. epilepsy, dementia) and major psychiatric diagnoses (i.e., neurodevelopmental, schizophrenia spectrum, bipolar disorder, neurocognitive and current substance-related disorders (with the exception of occasional alcohol consumption and tobacco smoking) that could alter the ability to complete psychometric assessment or that could influence eating habits, pregnancy and/or breastfeeding over the previous 12 months were considered exclusion criteria. All medical conditions were adequately documented through appropriate medical evidence. Eligible candidates were provided with comprehensive information regarding the aims and procedures of the study, underlining the voluntary and anonymous nature of participation and clarifying that no fees would be provided for their involvement. Participants were also informed on the protocol for data management and storage. Prior to proceeding with the enrolment, each participant signed a written informed consent, affirming their understanding and agreement to participate in the research and a formally consent to genetic analysis.

A total of 211 eligible patients agreed to participate in this study. Out of the initial sample, 41 participants consented but did not show up at the psychiatric examination, 29 did not fulfil psychometric questionnaires, and 15 did not agree to the genetic evaluation. The study included 126 participants with obesity that completed all the assessments (interviews, questionnaires and genetic evaluation). All participants were Caucasian.

Measures

Participants answered the following tests:

Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire 6.0 (EDE-Q 6.0).

Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire 6.0 (EDE-Q 6.0).

The EDE-Q 6.0 [65, 66] is a 36-item self-report questionnaire assessing ED symptoms over the previous 28 days, rated 0–6. The EDE-Q provides four subscales: “restraint” to assess attempts to restrict food intake to influence shape or weight, “eating concern” to evaluate preoccupation with food, eating or guilt about eating, “shape concern” to measure dissatisfaction or distress related to body shape, and “weight concern” to assess worries and dissatisfaction about body weight. In addition, a global mean score is provided to evaluate the overall ED psychopathology. Higher scores indicate more impaired eating behaviors. The EDE-Q total score was used for this study. Cronbach’s alpha in the present research was 0.889.

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI).

State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI).

The STAI [67] is a self-administered questionnaire made up of 40 items that assess two distinct dimensions of anxiety: state anxiety (STAI-S) which refers to transient emotional condition characterized by subjective feelings of tension and apprehension, and trait anxiety (STAI-T), which reflects a stable tendency to experience anxiety across a variety of situations. Total scores for each subscale range from 20 to 80 with higher scores indicating grater anxiety levels [68]. Cronbach’s alpha in the present research was 0.870.

Binge Eating Scale (BES).

Binge Eating Scale (BES).

The BES is a self-administered questionnaire that measures binge eating severity through 16 items [69]. The items include both behavioural (e.g., eating large amounts of food) and associated emotional or cognitive components (e.g., feeling of guilt, loss of control). A total BES score of < 17, 17–27 and > 27 indicates, respectively, unlikely, possible, and probable BED. In the present study, the threshold was set at 17. Reliability in this research was 0.915.

Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ).

Night Eating Questionnaire (NEQ).

The NEQ [70] is a psychometric tool that evaluates severity of symptoms associate with night eating syndrome. It consists of fourteen likert-type items, scoring from 0 to 4, and yields four subscales: “morning anorexia” (e.g. lack of appetite in the morning), “evening hyperphagia” (e.g., consuming a significant portion of daily food intake after dinner), “mood/sleep disturbances” (e.g., sleep difficulties or use of food to fall asleep), “nocturnal ingestions” (e.g., waking uo to eat during night) and a total score. Higher scores indicate greater severity of night eating related symptoms. Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.759.

Grazing Questionnaire (GQ).

Grazing Questionnaire (GQ).

The GQ [71] is composed of eight items corresponding to two dimensions grazing behavior (e.g. the frequency and persistence of grazing episodes) and controllability (e.g., the degree of perceived control over grazing behavior). Each item is rated on a Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (always) with higher scores reflecting higher severity or frequency of grazing behavior and cognitions. Cronbach’s alpha in the present study was 0.911.

Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II).

Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II).

The BDI-II [72] is a self-report questionnaire assessing the presence and severity of depressive symptoms through 21 items. Each item describes a specific symptom of depression (e.g., sadness, pessimism, fatigue, sleep disturbance, change in appetite). Scores of < 10, 10–16, 17–29 and 30 respectively indicate minimum, mild, moderate, and severe depression. A total score > 16 is considered the clinical cut-off. Reliability in this research was 0.917.

Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS).

Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS).

The DERS consists of 36 items and assesses emotional dysregulation. It evaluates multiple dimensions of emotion regulation difficulties that are commonly associated with psychological distress and maladaptive behaviors. It yields a total score and six subscales: non-acceptance of emotional responses, difficulties engaging in goal-directed behaviour, impulse control difficulties, lack of emotional awareness, limited access to emotion regulation strategies, lack of emotional clarity [72]. The DERS total score was used in the study. Cronbach’s alpha in the present research was 0.794.

Procedures

Assessment at first registration included a clinical interview performed by trained psychiatrists (40–50 min alike), semi-structured interviews, self-report questionnaires and genetic evaluation. Psychiatric diagnoses were confirmed or excluded during the clinical interview by trained psychiatrists with expertise in the field of EDs through the Structured Clinical Interview for the DSM-5 (SCID-5-CV) [73] and the Eating Disorder Examination (EDE 17.0D) [66]. The Yale Food Addiction Scale (YFAS 2.0) [74] was used to assess the presence and severity of FA. The scale is a self-report tool that comprises 35 items. Each item is scored on an 8-point Likert-type scale (ranging from 0: never to 7: every day). According to the item specific cut-off, each item is dichotomized (0 for “non-endorsed” vs. 1 for “endorsed”) to obtain the final score. The presence or absence of each criterion is calculated via these thresholds [74]. Two different scores are provided. The first one is the symptom count score: number of FA symptoms experienced in the previous 12 months, ranging from 0 to 11; impairment/distress criterion should not be counted. The second one is the diagnostic score: FA is diagnosed when the participant reports 2 or more symptoms plus a clinically significant impairment/distress. Based on the revised DSM-5 taxonomy, it is possible to establish FA severity cut-offs: mild (i.e., 2 or 3 symptoms and clinically significant impairment/distress), moderate (i.e., 4 or 5 symptoms and significant impairment/distress), or severe (i.e., 6 or more symptoms and significant impairment/distress).

Then, participants individually answered to the battery of psychometric questionnaires above described and a blood sample from patients was collected for the genotyping of the MC4R polymorphisms.

Data on physical health, anthropometric data (i.e., height, weight, and BMI), and socio-demographics were collected for each participant with an ad-hoc form. Within the anthropometrical evaluation (waist circumference, height, and weight), BMI (kg/m2) was calculated, controlling for light clothing and no shoes.

Patients genotyping analysis

Genomic DNA was isolated and extracted from a 200 µL peripheral whole blood sample by a commercial DNA isolation kit (Promega, Madison, WI). DNA samples were stored at -20 °C until use. The real-time PCR systems were used for analysing the MC4R polymorphisms. Genotyping of the MC4R variants rs17782313 (assay ID: C_ 32667060_10), rs12970134 (assay ID: C_3058722_10), rs10871777 (assay ID: C_11499875_10), rs6567160 (assay ID: C_3058649_20), and rs17700144 (assay ID: C_32667090_10) was performed using TaqMan allelic discrimination assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Template DNA was amplified, and fluorescence was detected on a BioRad CFX-96 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for socio-demographic and clinical characteristics and other relevant assessment instruments. The distributions of the variables were checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data showed a non-normal distribution. Data are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) or frequencies and percentages (%), as appropriate. Groups were compared in the demographic and clinical variables of interest through chi-squared test for categorical variables (i.e., sex and BED diagnosis) and Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables (i.e., age, psychopathological measures). Cramer’s V was calculated to assess the strength of association between categorical variables. Effect size for Mann-Whitney are reported as Rank-Biserial Correlation. A rank-based analysis of covariance (Rank ANCOVA) model, adjusted for FA, BED diagnosis, BMI, sex and age, was run to control for potential confounders.

Genotypes and allelic frequencies were calculated within our cohort population to determine the significant differences between groups. The chi-squared test was used to observe the difference between SNPs within the study groups. Post-hoc comparison was performed using Bonferroni test. Differences between SNPs and continuous variables were tested using the Kruskal–Wallis’s test. Additionally, Rank ANCOVA models adjusted for potential confounders including BED diagnosis, BMI, sex, age and rs17782313 polymorphism were conducted. For significant results the effect size (ŋ2 values of 0.2, 0.6, 1.2 and > 1.2 considered, respectively, slight, small, moderate, and large effect) [74] and Bonferroni post-hoc analyses were run. To account for multiple comparisons, significant p values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate balancing the type I error control and statistical power.

The level of statistical significance was set at a nominal value of p ≤ 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed by using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences Version 26 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA).

Genotype and allele frequencies in groups

Furthermore, patients with FA and with C allele showed higher risk of FA compared to group without FA (Odd ratio = 1,558). The MC4R rs17782313 genotypes (CC, CT and TT) frequencies were calculated for patients with FA as 17.4, 36.2 and 46.4%, respectively, whereas for the group without FA as 5.3, 54.4 and 40.3%, respectively. C and T allele frequencies were estimated as 35.5 and 64.5%, respectively, for the group with FA and 32.5 and 67.5%, respectively, for the group without FA.

There were significant differences between rs17782313 genotypes of the MC4R in patients with FA according to psychopathological variables (Table 5and Fig. 2). There was a significant effect of rs17782313 on both STAI-S and BDI-II score. Discussion

In this study we evaluated the genetic contribution of the MC system to FA assessed with the YFAS 2.0 via MC4R polymorphisms in a clinical population. The comparison of the groups confirmed data previously reported in the literature. In our sample, a significantly higher percentage of female patients and a diagnosis of BED were evident in the group with FA than in the group without FA. This result is in line with community studies that directly compared individuals with obesity and FA with individuals with obesity but without FA [76]. As noted, BED and FA often overlap and are common in individuals with high body weight [9, 77]. Patients with FA, particularly those with high-functioning addiction and fully addiction profile, exhibited more anxiety, depressive symptoms, altered eating behaviors and emotion dysregulation [78]. Numerous studies found that people who meet the criteria for both BED and FA are likely to report more frequent bingeing episodes, depressive symptoms, intense food cravings more than those with BED alone [11, 76] suggesting that this comorbidity may represent a different phenotype of BED, characterized by not only a greater and more severe psychopathology [14, 15, 18, 26] but also serious health consequences such as impaired metabolic response [79]. Recently, a study involving 502 individuals found that 8.5% met the criteria for BED, 16.6% met the criteria for FA, 10.1% met the criteria for both BED + FA, and 64.8% met neither (controls) [77]. All the three groups (FA, BED and FA + BED) scored significantly higher on eating disorder psychopathology, impulsivity, self-control than controls, while patients with FA and FA + BED groups reported significantly higher depression scores than the control group. Emotion dysregulation and deficits in interoceptive awareness can also contribute to FA leading people with FA to consume certain foods in an attempt to regulate their emotions [80]. In our sample, BED diagnosis had a significant effect on depression in FA group. It was demonstrated that the desire for certain food can also be elicited by negative emotional states and food can be used to regulate negative mood [80]. On the other hand, negative emotions can promote a craving for highly palatable foods preceding binge eating episodes [81, 82].

Although previous studies have examined the link between MC4R variants and eating-related behaviors or obesity-related phenotypes [44, 83–86], the most important and innovative finding of this study is the association between the MC4R polymorphisms, and the presence of FA as assessed with the YFAS 2.0 in our sample that, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated.

In this study we evaluated the genetic contribution of the MC system to FA assessed with the YFAS 2.0 via MC4R polymorphisms in a clinical population.

In our sample the genotype and the allelic frequencies are superimposable to that of general population for all the MC4R variants allowing us to generalize our results. The rs17782313 of the MC4R showed an association with the FA and the CC genotype was more prevalent in patients with FA than the group without FA.

The rs17782313 is one of the SNPs of the MC4R gene most studied. This SNP has two alleles, T and C, where the T allele is the ancestral one and the C allele is the risk allele. The rs17782313 has been linked with increased BMI, increased waist circumference [87], insulin resistance [88], diabetes mellitus type 2 and lipid intake [89]. In particular, the C allele has already been demonstrated to give a higher risk for weight gain than the T allele [90, 91] and it was related to food intake, overeating, severe binge eating [44, 48], pinches or snacking [52], emotional eating [86], lower satiety responsiveness and higher uncontrolled eating scores compared to non-carriers [83]. Few studies have reported an association between rs17782313 C allele and other eating behaviors (i.e., grazing, hyperphagia) with mixed and unclear results [53]. It was observed that the MC4R play a role in energy intake and dietary high-fat/high-sucrose preference [92] and that rs17782313 variant is associated with higher energy intake, especially higher percentage of fat energy [93]. The MC4R rs17782313 polymorphism has been shown to decrease proopiomelanocortin (POMC) expression and to be related with morbid obesity, since POMC can influence regulatory mechanisms that stimulate satiety [94]. A possible explanation of this association may be due to the MC4R involvement in the homeostatic and non-homeostatic mechanisms of food intake. Indeed, the MC4R is co-expressed with dopamine receptors in hypothalamic area [95] and is highly expressed in the mesolimbic system [38] where can modulate dopamine release [30] and consequently food intake and the reinforcing value of food [96]. Additionally, some preclinical studies suggest that melanocortin signaling regulates food reward [38, 39] and food addiction behaviors [31] through their genetic interaction [48]. The C allele of rs17782313 may contribute to the satiety responsiveness thus patients may eat in the absence of hunger [97]. In this light, having two “non-functioning” MC4R alleles, such as CC allele, had probably compromised satiety response significantly, suggesting that the variant rs17782313 could decrease satiety responsiveness with a greater alteration in homozygous than heterozygous individuals [53]. Thus, in the presence of a hypofunction of the MC4R due to the presence of CC genotype, the result is a decreased POMC stimulation with altered energy homeostasis and reward system regulation via the alteration of the mesolimbic dopaminergic and the opioid systems, and a consequent increase of food intake and altered eating behaviors such as FA. Indeed, patients show a high responsiveness to reward perception when exposed to relevant signals (i.e., highly palatable, and intensely processed foods) and may engage in compulsive behaviors to satisfy these intense desires. While the reward system is generally hyporeactive, it becomes hyperreactive to stimuli associated with addiction thus compelling individuals to eat even when not driven by homeostatic signals (i.e., hunger), solely to experience the hedonic and pleasurable effects of consuming highly palatable foods [98].

We observed a significant influence of the rs17782313 polymorphism on psychopathological variables in the group of patients with FA but not in the group without FA. Our analyses suggest that patients with FA and C allele carrier have higher anxiety and depression than controls. This finding aligns with previous research showing that individuals with CC genotype are more likely to consume unhealthy foods (e.g., processed food, energy dense drinks, high fat food) and to experience significantly depressive symptoms [99]. Furthermore, suggestive findings demonstrated that the MC system has a pivotal role in stress and negative emotional states response, suggesting that it could be a potential treatment target [42, 43]. The distribution of the MC4R in the brain suggests a significant involvement of this receptor in the promotion of negative emotional states [42, 43]. The MC4R can mediate stress responses and a possible involvement in mood regulation, due to the interaction of the MC4R with the serotoninergic and dopaminergic pathways, has also been suggested [100]. The MC4R is prominently present also in the hypothalamus, where it is supposed to regulate the activity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis through arginine vasopressin and corticotropin-releasing factor neurons [42, 43]. It has been reported that melanocortin elicits anxiogenic-like effects: stimulation of the MC4R may cause anxiety-like behaviors, while the MC4R antagonists may have anxiolytic potential [42]. Furthermore, the MC4R signaling may play a role in initiating stress-induced synaptic adaptations in the nucleus accumbens. This brain region is linked to reward processing and characterized by a high expression of dopamine [101].

Strengths and limitations

Some limitations need to be acknowledged, and results should be interpreted cautiously. First, the cross-sectional design precludes us to infer about causality regarding the genetic contribution of the MC system polymorphisms to FA due to the heterogeneity of genetic variations. Further studies with larger sample size and longitudinal design would provide more robust data to explore causality and generalize findings. Second, self-report questionnaires, although validated tools, may be affected by recall bias, leading to underestimation of eating behaviours or affective symptoms. Indeed, psychiatric comorbid symptoms could have affected psychopathology expression because people with obesity, BED and other comorbid major psychiatric disorders exhibit worse eating psychopathology than those without [102–104]. The presence of FA was assessed only using the YFAS 2.0. Although the YFAS 2.0 as a valid tool to evaluate addictive-like eating behaviors [3], present results surely need contextualizing the diagnostic limits. Additionally, findings can be interpreted as applying to individuals with obesity, but it is known that FA also occurs among individuals with a healthy or lower BMI [11].

Furthermore, the study evaluated the effect only of the MC system and the MC4R polymorphisms, and clarifying whether these gene variations have a direct relationship with eating behaviors such as FA is not possible. Other genes (e.g., MC3R, those involved in dopamine regulation or food reward pathways) might also play a role in FA and further research should explore this area over time.

Even if both sexes were investigated, male participants were underrepresented in our sample potentially hindering the detection of any sex-related effects. While studies provide some findings for sex-specific effects [83, 105, 106], more research is required to establish a definitive link between the MC4R rs17782313 and sex-specific FA risk. Also, as the causal factors of genetic variants can manifest differently across diverse populations, our findings shed light only on the Caucasian population, which, to the best of our knowledge, was not investigated regarding the effect of the MC4R variations and FA. Importantly, future studies should include more comprehensive behavioural and metabolic measures to better clarify the specificity of the association between MC system polymorphisms and FA, as well as the intricate interactions among genetic, metabolic, and behavioural factors in the aetiology of FA.

Despite these limitations, this study has some noteworthy strengths. It explored the genetic features associated with FA in patients with obesity reporting an association between the MC system variations and the construct of FA, evaluating psychopathological variables, and employing a control group of patients with obesity but without FA.

Conclusions

The current findings suggest that, when present, FA may represent a peculiar and more specific phenotype of patients with obesity and/or BED potentially requiring targeted therapies. Results of this study support the notion that obesity is not synonymous either with FA or BED and demand to routinely assess the presence of FA and evaluate more accurately patients with obesity and/or BED. Moreover, the findings of the present work may contribute not only to enhance our understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying the food intake regulation but also shed light on the hedonic and behavioural features of the FA.

In conclusion, while FA is not yet incorporated in the current diagnostic systems (i.e., DSM-5 or ICD-11), and lacks well-validated diagnostic criteria, the biological and genetic suggestive findings of this work may support the existence of FA as a distinct clinical phenomenon.

These results underlie the need to considering FA not only for prevention of its long-term complications but also to mitigating and treating excessive eating motivation, high palatable food intake and consequently the weight gain.
