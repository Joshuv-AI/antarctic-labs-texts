First case of Aureobasidium pullulans in a dog after sterilization surgery in Chile
Authors: Ailén Dumont-Viollaz, Belén Rivera Gómez-Barris, Gabriel Velozo, Leslye Camila del Río, Byron Guzmán-Marín, Pamela Thomson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn November 2024, a female Chilean Chihuahua presented with a limb lesion that did not heal after spaying. The diagnosis indicated Aureobasidium pullulans infection. The antifungigram showed susceptibility to itraconazole, treatment with this drug together with bilateral amputation resulted in a complete recovery. The phylogenetic tree of the ITS gene showed differences with other sequences of the same species from animals and lichens. This case highlights this environmental fungus as a potential pathogen in dogs.Keywords: Antifungigram, Canine, Itraconazole, Mycosis, Opportunistic fungus

Introduction

Fungi of the genus Aureobasidium are recognized as rare pathogens [1], however, when infections occur, they are usually caused by traumatic implantation of the fungus from plants and soil [2,3]. Aureobasidium pullulans ((de Bary) G. Arnaud (1918)) is a dematiaceous fungus, associated with infections in immunocompromised and immunosuppressed humans [[4], [5], [6]].

In animals, infections caused by A. pullulans are uncommon. Worldwide, cases have been reported in a porcupine [7], a cat [8], a freshwater fish [9], a Neotropical deer [10], and several cases in dogs [[11], [12], [13]]. In addition, a case of ocular coinfection between A. pullulans and Rhodosporidium sp. has been reported in brown shrimp [14]. It is noteworthy that this fungus has been also isolated from Amazonian anurans [15], from the milk of cows with mastitis [16], and from the hair of dogs, horses, cats, cows, guinea pigs, and lesser pandas [17].

Cases of A. pullulans mycosis in dogs include otitis [12], disseminated infections [11], and subcutaneous phaeohyphomycosis [13] in at least two countries, the USA and Brazil. In this report we describe the first case of infection by A. pullulans in a dog in Chile.

Case

An 8-year-old spayed female Chihuahua was referred for a third opinion on November 12, 2024, with a two-month history of nonhealing lesions on the distal hindlimbs. The owner reports that this began after spay surgery, where the patient received amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (15 mg/kg BID) and pentoxifylline 400 mg (20 mg/kg BID) for 14 days.

On the day of consultation (day 0), a clinical evaluation revealed lethargy and apathy. Physical examination revealed tenderness, deep lesions on both hindlimbs with necrosis of the skin from the metatarsal to the distal area, and exposed bone with no signs of vital tissue (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, debridement of the devitalized tissue revealed no blood flow to the affected area. On the same day, a cytology swab was performed, which showed neutrophilic inflammation, degenerated neutrophils, and oval fungal structures (Fig. 1B). A complete blood count and biochemical profile were requested, and a biopsy was scheduled for the following day.

A. Necrosis of skin tissue from the metatarsus to the distal area of the hind limbs, bone exposure. B. Cytology shown fungal structures.

On day +1, a biopsy was performed from the affected area of each extremity, and the tissues were sent to the microbiology and microbiome laboratory in saline solution for fungal culture. While awaiting the results, the patient was administered a single dose of cefovecin 8 mg/kg (Zoetis, Las Condes, Chile) and prescribed itraconazole 5 mg/kg SID (Laboratorio Bioformula, Providencia, Chile) and pentoxifylline 25 mg/kg BID (Laboratorio Chile, Ñuñoa, Chile), both for 30 days.

The complete blood count results revealed lymphopenia, degenerated neutrophils, and a left shift. Considering the characteristics of the lesions, after 10 days of treatment (day +11), a bilateral hindlimb amputation was performed by disarticulation of the first and second tarsal joints, preserving the calcaneus. The bone stump was preserved, and the skin was accessed for traditional tension-free closure.

Direct examination of both samples revealed unicellular oval structures. They were plated on potato dextrose agar with chloramphenicol and cycloheximide and incubated at 25 °C and 37 °C for 15 days. Initially, smooth, whitish, creamy-looking colonies were observed (Fig. 2A), which later turned black with irregular edges (Fig. 2B). Microscopic analysis prepared with lactophenol cotton blue showed hyaline and dematiaceous hyphae and numerous chlamydospores (Fig. 2C) consistent with Aureobasidium spp.

Antifungal susceptibility testing performed using the broth microdilution method according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) document M38-A2 showed the following minimum inhibitory concentrations: voriconazole and posaconazole 0.125 μg/mL, itraconazole 0.5 μg/mL, fluconazole 32 μg/mL, amphotericin B 2 μg/mL, and caspofungin 0.25 μg/mL.

DNA extraction from a fresh colony was performed using the Quick-DNA Fungal/Bacterial Miniprep Kit (ZYMO Research, Irvine, CA, USA), and DNA quantification was performed using a Nanodrop 2000c nanospectrophotometer (ThermoScientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Subsequently, 10 ng of the extracted DNA was used for PCR amplification of the core region of the ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS), using primers ITS4 5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′ and ITS5 5′-GGAAGTAAAAGTCGTAACAAGG-3′ [18]. The sequences obtained from the amplified regions were compared with those available in GenBank using the basic local alignment search tool (BLAST). The species was confirmed with an identity and coverage percentage greater than 95 %, validating the presence of A. pullulans, whose accession code in GenBank is PX361306.

A phylogenetic analysis was performed based on the obtained ITS sequences, calculating genetic distance and creating a maximum likelihood tree construction were performed using MEGA version 11. For this purpose, 1) the ITS sequence obtained in this study (PX361306), 2) freely available A. pullulans ITS sequences related to animal mycoses obtained from GenBank (PP580138.1, MG547431.1, MG595273.1), and 3) freely available A. pullulans ITS sequences from Chile obtained from Genbank (PP664496.1, PP664494.1, PP664499.1, PP664497.1, PP664495.1, PP664500.1) were used. The tree and neighbor-joining map were visualized with Microreact version 282. As shown in Fig. 3, phylogenetic analysis of the ITS gene sequences did not reveal clustering by sampling country. The sequence from the present study showed the greatest genetic distance from the other sequences, even from the other dog sequence extracted from Bressan Waller et al. [13]. Five of the six sequences extracted from lichens clustered together and then grouped with the deer sequence and the remaining lichen sequence.

On day +36 (25 days after surgery), the patient showed complete improvement in both hindlimb stumps. A new complete blood count and biochemical profile showed normal parameters. Treatment with itraconazole was continued until completing two months of therapy. Eight months after surgery, the patient remains relapse-free.

Discussion

Overall, A. pullulans has been the most studied species of its genus, and it is currently considered effective and safe for various agricultural applications [19]. Regarding clinical forms, it is usually associated with immunocompromised and immunosuppressed humans, and various antifungal drugs are used, including amphotericin B, itraconazole, fluconazole, ketoconazole, and flucytosine, and whose treatment can last between 4 and 8 weeks [6,[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. However, animal cases of A. pullulans are usually rare, but at least three cases of canine mycosis due to A. pullulans are known in the literature.

These cases include otitis caused by A. pullulans [12], a study that sought to understand the fungal flora of healthy and diseased canine ears but did not include any treatment followed in the sick animals. In this study, various fungi were isolated from the ears of dogs; however, A. pullulans was only isolated in one of the 97 dogs sampled, and it was also a coinfection with Penicillium spp. On the other hand, the study by Perkins et al. [11] showed a case of fungal pneumonia with dissemination to hilar lymph nodes, spleen, meninges, and brain, caused by A. pullulans in a German shepherd. The animal received treatment with antiprotozoals and corticosteroids prior to diagnosis; however, this was ineffective, so euthanasia was performed. Finally, Bressan Waller et al. [13] reported a case of subcutaneous phaeohyphomycosis in a female Labrador caused by a subcutaneous lesion on the right hind limb that appeared after the animal's castration. The authors point out that postoperative stress could have played an important role in the development of the disease, and that the source of infection would be traumatic inoculation related to contact with the environment, given the animal's outdoor behavior. The patient began daily topical applications of rifamycin prior to diagnosis, which were discontinued after the negative result of the bacteriological analysis. When A. pullulans infection was confirmed, veterinarians recommended oral itraconazole for four weeks; however, for economic reasons, the guardian decided not to treat the patient. Five weeks later, spontaneous remission of the lesion was observed. This last case is very reminiscent of the report of the present work, in which a female Chihuahua presented injuries in the distal area of hind limbs that did not heal after sterilization surgery. In both cases, the female dog presented lesions following sterilization, which, combined with the knowledge that this fungus tends to behave as an environmental infection, could indicate that it is a secondary infection. However, unlike the work of Bressan Waller et al. [13], the patient presented here was treated with cefovecin, pentoxifylline, and itraconazole, achieving clinical cure 25 days after surgery.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that, at least for the ITS sequences, no clustering by sampling site was observed; furthermore, the two sequences obtained from dogs differed from each other. In this regard, it would be interesting to obtain new isolates A. pullulans, especially those from fungal infections, to understand whether there is any phylogenetic pattern in this gene.

In this work we present the first case of A. pullulans in a dog in Chile, and as other authors mentioned previously, we highlight the importance of considering this environmental fungus as a potential pathogen and suggest its inclusion in the differential diagnosis of skin lesions in dogs [13]. In addition, it is suggested to consider good post-operative cleaning practices, which allow minimizing the risk of infection.
