MARCKS Effector Domain, a reversible lipid ligand, illuminates late stages of membrane fusion
Authors: Amy Orr, William Wickner
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Yeast vacuolar HOPS tethers membranes, catalyzes trans-SNARE assembly between R- and Q-SNAREs, and shepherds SNAREs past early inhibition by Sec17. After partial SNARE zippering, fusion is driven slowly by either completion of SNARE zippering or by Sec17/Sec18, but rapid fusion needs zippering and Sec17/Sec18. Using reconstituted-vacuolar fusion, we find that MARCKS Effector Domain (MED) peptide, a lipid ligand, blocks fusion reversibly at a late reaction stage. The MED fusion blockade is overcome by either salt extraction, inactivation with the MED ligand calmodulin, or addition of Sec17/Sec18. During incubation with MED, SNAREs assemble stable complexes in trans and fusion becomes resistant to antibody to the Qa SNARE. When Q-SNAREs are preassembled, a synthetic tether can replace HOPS for fusion. With a synthetic tether, fusion needs both complete SNARE zippering and Sec17/Sec18 to overcome a MED block. In contrast, when SNARE domains are only two-third zippered, only HOPS will support Sec17/Sec18 driven fusion without needing complete zippering. HOPS thus remains engaged with SNAREs during zippering. MED facilitates the study of distinct fusion stages: tethering, initial trans-SNARE assembly and its sensitivity to Sec17, SNARE zippering, Sec17/Sec18 engagement, and lipid and lumenal mixing.

INTRODUCTION

Membrane fusion during exocytic and endocytic vesicular trafficking is essential for subcellular protein delivery. Fusion occurs by mechanisms which are conserved between organelles and among organisms from yeast to humans, catalyzed by several families of proteins. Rab family GTPases act as switches to regulate membrane tethering (Hutagalung and Novick, 2011; Baker and Hughson, 2016). Tethering allows catalyzed assembly of SNARE proteins, anchored to each fusion-partner membrane (Zick and Wickner, 2014; Baker and Hughson, 2016; Song and Wickner, 2019). SNAREs (Jahn and Scheller, 2006) have N-domains, SNARE domains with heptad repeat apolar amino acids, short but crucial juxtamembrane regions, and (for many SNAREs) C-terminal apolar transmembrane anchor domains. There is an arginyl or glutaminyl residue instead of the apolar residue at the central 0-layer of each SNARE. SNAREs are in conserved R, Qa, Qb, and Qc subfamilies (Fasshauer et al., 1998). Individual SNARE domains are random coil, but they become alpha-helical as four SNAREs, an R-SNARE anchored in one membrane and Qa, Qb, and Qc SNAREs from its fusion partner membrane, zipper together in the N to C direction to form an RQaQbQc 4-SNARE complex (Sorensen et al., 2006). SNARE complexes are in cis if anchored to one membrane or in trans if anchored to apposed tethered membranes before fusion. SNARE-complex assembly is catalyzed by SM (Sec1/Munc18) family proteins (Parisotto et al., 2014; Baker et al., 2015; Jiao et al., 2018; Orr et al., 2017; Stepien et al., 2022), and is initiated well before fusion occurs (Ungermann et al., 1998). ATP-dependent cis-SNARE complex disassembly is catalyzed by Sec18/NSF and Sec17/SNAP (Söllner et al., 1993; Mayer et al., 1996; Cipriano et al., 2013). Sec17 bound to SNAREs can bind Sec18, conveying energy from the ATP-dependent Sec18 conformational change to disassemble the SNAREs (Söllner et al., 1993; Mayer et al., 1996; Cipriano et al., 2013).

We study membrane-fusion mechanisms with yeast vacuoles. Vacuoles undergo frequent fission and fusion. Mutants with blocked fusion and continuing fission exhibit numerous small vacuoles, the vac phenotype (Wada et al., 1992). The 9 VAC gene-encoded proteins, as well as the proteins required for many fusion events (Sec17, Sec18, and the Vti1 Qb-SNARE), constitute the basic catalysts of vacuole homotypic fusion. Vacuole fusion needs the Rab Ypt7 (Haas et al., 1995), Sec17 and Sec18 (Haas and Wickner, 1996), 4 SNAREs (the R-SNARE Nyv1, the Qa-SNARE Vam3, the Qb-SNARE Vti1, and the Qc-SNARE Vam7, hereafter referred to as R, Qa, Qb, and Qc) and HOPS, a large hexameric protein of Vps11, 16, 18, 33, 39, and 41 subunits (Seals et al., 2000; Wurmser et al., 2000; Stroupe et al., 2006). HOPS fulfills several functions. Its Vps39 and Vps41 subunits bind Ypt7 on 2 membranes to tether them together (Brett et al., 2008; Hickey and Wickner, 2010; Bröcker et al., 2012). HOPS binds each of the four vacuolar SNAREs (Song et al., 2020) and catalyzes the initial steps of 4-SNARE assembly (Baker et al., 2015; Orr et al., 2017; Jiao et al., 2018). The HOPS Vps33 subunit, the vacuolar SM protein, has specific sites which bind the SNARE domains of R and Qa, in parallel and in register (Baker et al., 2015). HOPS shepherds early stages of fusion past inhibition by Sec17 (Xu et al., 2010). HOPS is allosterically activated by its associations with phosphatidylinositol, phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate, Ypt7, and R on one membrane to catalyze the initial incorporation of the Q-SNAREs from the apposed tethered membrane into a trans 4-SNARE complex (Torng et al., 2020; Torng and Wickner, 2021). It has been unclear whether HOPS also acts later in the fusion pathway. Fusion can be driven by either of two pathways. While relatively slow fusion can be driven by either completion of SNARE zippering or by the combined action of Sec17 and Sec18 which have assembled on a platform of partially-zippered SNAREs without needing complete SNARE zippering, rapid fusion needs both complete SNARE zippering and Sec17/Sec18 (Song et al., 2017, 2021). As revealed in the classical 20s structure of synaptic SNARE domains, NSF, and SNAP (Zhao et al., 2015), each Sec18/NSF hexamer can bind several copies of Sec17/SNAP. Sec18 and several Sec18-bound Sec17s will associate with membranes through the apolar N-domain loops of each Sec17, enhancing the concentration of Sec17 at the membrane and thereby lowering the required concentration of Sec17 for fusion (Orr and Wickner, 2022). Several Sec17 molecules with their bound Sec18 assemble around a core of SNAREs which are at least partially zippered.

Sec17 is a major constituent of trans-SNARE complexes (Xu et al., 2010). Sec17 and HOPS are not found in the same SNARE complexes (Collins et al., 2005), and Sec17 may displace HOPS from SNAREs (Collins et al., 2005; Schwartz et al., 2017). The relationship between Sec17 and HOPS during the final steps of fusion remains unclear. Membrane fusion is blocked when SNARE zippering is arrested through deletion of several C-terminal heptad repeats from any of the Q-SNAREs, but this block is bypassed by Sec17/Sec18 (Schwartz and Merz, 2009; Zick et al., 2015; Song et al., 2021). Might several Sec17 molecules, which assemble with the aid of Sec18 into an oligomer around the SNARE complex (Zhao et al., 2015), simply provide a protein tunnel to entrap and colocalize the rest of the R and Qa SNARE and juxtamembrane domains? In this model, SNARE zippering or Sec17/Sec18 entrapment of R and Qa would have the same function, the colocalization of juxtamembrane (Jx) and transmembrane regions of R and Qa. Were that true, mutants in the Jx regions or in the transmembrane anchoring of Q-SNAREs would have the same effect on fusion reactions that were either mediated by SNARE zippering alone or by Sec17/Sec18 without SNARE zippering. However, mutants in the R and Qa Jx region or deletion of the membrane anchor of Qb which block zippering-dependent fusion are bypassed by Sec17/Sec18 (Orr and Wickner, 2022; Wickner et al., 2023), distinguishing the function of Sec17/Sec18 from that of complete SNARE domain zippering. While Sec17 and Sec18 have positive roles in fusion, an excess of Sec17 can block fusion (Wang et al., 2000), especially in the absence of HOPS (Mima et al., 2008; Song and Wickner, 2019; Jun and Wickner, 2019). HOPS needs a functional Vps33 (SM) subunit to confer resistance to Sec17 (Baker et al., 2015).

Understanding the interplay between Sec17/Sec18 and HOPS, the role(s) of HOPS after catalyzing the initial trans 4-SNARE assembly, and whether Sec17/Sec18-triggered fusion is independent of complete SNARE domain zippering will require specific inhibitors of each stage of the fusion pathway. While the early steps of cis-SNARE complex disassembly and membrane tethering have been dissected with the help of several well-understood reversible inhibitors (Thorngren et al., 2004), understanding the late steps of fusion has been hindered by the lack of a reversible late blocker. We now report that MARCKS effector domain (MED), the 25 amino acyl peptide KKKKKRFSFKKSFKLSGFSFKKNKK (Harlan et al., 1991) which can inhibit the fusion of purified vacuoles (Xu et al., 2010), is a potent and reversible late-stage inhibitor of proteoliposome fusion as reconstituted with purified proteins. The MED region of MARCKS mediates its membrane association by binding phosphoinositides and other acidic lipids (Bähr et al., 1998). MARCKS can be displaced from membranes by calmodulin (Yamauchi et al., 2003; Chen et al., 2021), and we find that calmodulin reverses MED inhibition of reconstituted proteoliposome fusion. We now show that MED acts late in the fusion pathway as a potent inhibitor of fusion between reconstituted proteoliposomes bearing Ypt7 and R-SNARE and fusion-complementary proteoliposomes with Ypt7 and Q-SNAREs. Stable trans-SNARE complex assembly is undiminished during MED inhibition, and fusion incubations with MED progress past sensitivity to antibody to Qa or to high levels of Sec17. There is no close MARCKS homolog in yeast; the MED peptide is employed here as a late-acting reversible-fusion inhibitor.

We have exploited this late-acting fusion inhibitor to investigate whether HOPS only functions during the initial stages of docking, or whether its functions continue throughout SNARE zippering. Without any block to SNARE zippering, the inhibition by MED is bypassed by Sec17/Sec18. MED inhibition is not limited to the HOPS tether, as MED also blocks fusion when HOPS is replaced by a synthetic tether. Fusion is restored by Sec17/Sec18 with either tether, but fusion with a synthetic tether requires both completed SNARE zippering and Sec17/Sec18. SNARE-bearing membranes tethered by either HOPS or a synthetic tether undergo zippering, but only HOPS allows Sec17/Sec18 to bypass the need for complete zippering. HOPS therefore remains engaged with fusion-active trans-SNARE complexes well into their zippering. MED inhibition allows resolution of distinct stages late in the fusion pathway.

RESULTS

Fusion was assayed between proteoliposomes which had been prepared by dialysis of mixed-micellar solutions of protein, lipid, and detergent. The Rab Ypt7, R- or Q-SNAREs, β-octylglucoside, and vacuolar composition mimic lipids (VML) were mixed with either of two soluble fluorescent proteins, Cy5-streptavidin or biotinylated phycoerythrin. After dialysis, proteoliposomes with fluorescent proteins entrapped within their lumens were isolated by floatation. In mixtures of these proteoliposomes, the two fluorophores are initially separated by at least the thickness of two bilayers, too far for FRET (fluorescence resonance energy transfer). Upon fusion and its attendant-lumenal mixing, the tight binding of biotin to streptavidin brings the attached Cy5 and phycoerythrin fluorophores into intimate proximity, yielding a strong FRET signal (Zucchi and Zick, 2011). Fusion assays are performed with a large molar excess of external nonfluorescent streptavidin to block any FRET due to lysis. Fusion between proteoliposomes bearing Ypt7 and R and those bearing Ypt7 and either Qa, QaQb, or QaQbQc is supported by the HOPS SM/tethering complex and any “soluble” Q-SNAREs (recombinant forms without their membrane anchors) not on the proteoliposomes (Figure 1; lanes a, d, and g). MED inhibits fusion without regard to the initial state of assembly of the Q-SNAREs (lanes c, e, f, h, i). Ensuing studies sought agents which can reverse a MED block, determined which steps of fusion occur during MED-mediated fusion arrest, and assayed the requirements for fusion after reversal of the MED block.

Reversibility of the MED block

A late block of fusion by a lipid-ligand such as MED is most useful if it can be reversed, showing that arrested intermediates had not been shunted onto dead-end pathways. HOPS-mediated fusion between Ypt7/R and Ypt7/3Q proteoliposomes (Figure 2A; blue circles and squares) is partially inhibited by an extra 150 mM NaCl (blue triangles) but more fully blocked by MED (black circles and squares). A mixture of the extra salt and MED only inhibited fusion to the extent seen for the salt (black triangles). This suggests that salt relieved the MED inhibition, which is consistent with the largely ionic interaction of the basic MED with acidic phospholipids (Bähr et al., 1998). After 15 min of incubation with MED, the addition of salt restored fusion (open black squares), indicating a relief of the MED block. With Q-SNAREs preassembled, fusion can also be supported by a synthetic-tethering agent, the inherently dimeric protein glutathione-S-transferase (GST) fused to a C1b domain which binds to diacylglycerol on each membrane (Song and Wickner, 2019; Orr and Wickner, 2023). Fusion with the GST-C1b synthetic tether (Figure 2B; blue circles and squares) was strongly inhibited by either 150 mM additional NaCl (blue triangles) or by MED (black circles and squares) or by MED in the presence of salt (black triangles). After 15 min incubation with MED, the block to fusion was relieved by salt addition (open black squares), showing that the MED block and its relief are not specific to HOPS.

MED blocks after stable SNARE assembly

Proteoliposomes can form rapid-fusion intermediates with partially assembled SNARE complexes which exhibit labile Qc-SNARE association (Song et al., 2020). We therefore asked whether stable SNARE assembly occurs when all three Q-SNAREs are present but fusion is blocked by MED. Fusion between Ypt7/R and Ypt7/QaQb proteoliposomes with HOPS and 50 nM Qc (Figure 3A; curve 1, blue circles) is blocked by either a large molar excess of Qc3Δ with its C-terminally truncated SNARE domain to block zippering (curve 2, blue triangles) or by the presence of MED (curves 3 and 4, black circles and diamonds). MED-fusion inhibition is relieved by calmodulin (curve 3, open black circles), a characterized ligand of MED (Yamauchi et al., 2003), and this relief is insensitive to adding a large molar excess of Qc3Δ along with the calmodulin (curve 4, open black diamonds). Qc had engaged for fusion in a stable and functional manner during the MED block. Conversely, when proteoliposomes are initially blocked from fusion through incubation with 50 nM Qc3Δ and MED (Figure 3B; curve 4, black circles), fusion is not restored by the later addition of calmodulin and 1 μM full-length Qc (Figure 3B; curve 4, open black circles), though premixed 50 nM Qc3Δ and 1 μM Qc supports fusion, whether added from t = 0 (blue triangles, curve 2) or after 20 min (curve 3). Thus, the truncated Qc3Δ had stably engaged with the other SNAREs. During MED block, a stable trans-SNARE complex assembles on the pathway to fusion.

Overcoming the MED block with Sec17 and Sec18

As well as salt and calmodulin, Sec17 and Sec18 provide a third, independent means of overcoming the MED fusion blockade. We assayed the fusion of Ypt7/R proteoliposomes with Ypt7/QaQb proteoliposomes in incubations with HOPS, Qc, and the indicated level of MED (Figure 5). Fusion inhibition by MED (3 µM) was relieved by Sec17, Sec18, and ATPγS (black bars). Sec17/Sec18/ATPγS prevented complete blockage by even high concentrations of MED.

MED-staged fusion reveals that HOPS functions throughout SNARE zippering

Does HOPS only function at the start of docking, perhaps leaving the trans-SNARE complex shortly after the start of zippering? HOPS has three known functions: 1) membrane tethering, 2) catalysis of the initiation of SNARE zippering, and 3) conferring resistance of the initial stage of SNARE associations in trans to Sec17. Each of these might be fulfilled at the start of SNARE zippering. Furthermore, the surfaces of the SNARE domains of R and Qa, which lie in pockets on the HOPS subunit Vps33 (Baker et al., 2015), must turn to face each other within the core of the assembling 4-SNARE bundle as zippering proceeds, which may weaken HOPS:SNARE affinities. Sec17 may aid displacement of HOPS from SNAREs (Collins et al., 2005; Schwartz et al., 2017), and Sec17 and HOPS are associated with distinct sets of SNAREs (Collins et al., 2005). For each of these reasons, it seemed possible that HOPS only functions at the start of docking. To test whether HOPS functions throughout zippering, we compared its functions to a synthetic tether, dimeric GST-C1b, which tethers by its affinity for diacylglyerol on each membrane (Orr and Wickner, 2023) but lacks binding sites for Ypt7 or SNAREs. When the three Q-SNAREs, including wild-type Qc, are preassembled during proteoliposome preparation, GST-C1b and HOPS support comparable fusion (Figure 6; open circles and diamonds), as reported (Song and Wickner, 2019; Orr and Wickner, 2023). With either tether, partially zippered structures may be accumulating, because the addition of Sec17 and Sec18 at 20 min (arrow) caused an abrupt increase in fusion. With the GST-C1b tether, fusion is blocked by either MED (open triangles), or by Sec17/Sec18 added from the beginning of the reaction (open squares). After 20 min with MED, the fusion reaction with GST-C1b had progressed past sensitivity to Sec17/Sec18, and Sec17/Sec18 addition relieved the fusion blockage by MED (open triangles). The support of fusion by Sec17/Sec18 is thus not a unique property of HOPS. SNARE zippering is arrested with only two-third completion by the Qc3Δ mutant protein which lacks the four C-terminal heptads of the Qc SNARE domain (Schwartz and Merz, 2009). With Qc3∆, fusion with the GST-C1b tether is not triggered by Sec17/Sec18, whether in the presence or absence of MED (filled triangles and diamonds). In contrast, with HOPS-dependent SNARE zippering arrested by Qc3Δ, full and rapid fusion is restored to the arrested fusion intermediate by addition of Sec17/Sec18 (Figure 6; filled circles). Because HOPS and GST-C1b both rely on SNARE assembly and N- to C-zippering, and because after full zippering Sec17/Sec18 will restore fusion to incubations with either HOPS or GST-C1b, HOPS is responsible after two-third of SNARE zippering for the ability of Sec17/Sec18 to bypass the need for zippering completion. The unique capacity of HOPS to rescue fusion without complete zippering shows that HOPS remains engaged with SNAREs even after two-third of the SNARE domains have zippered.

With either HOPS or GST-C1b and complete zippering, fusion is triggered by the addition of Sec17/Sec18, but with only two-third zippering, Sec17/Sec18-triggered fusion requires HOPS, showing that HOPS remains engaged this far into zippering. Fusion incubations with Ypt7/R and Ypt7/QaQbQc proteoliposomes (with wild-type Qc or Qc3∆) had HOPS (50 nM) or GST-C1b (16 μM), and MED (9 μM) where indicated. MED (9 μM) or Sec17 (100 nM), Sec18 (300 nM), and 1 mM MgATPγS were added at either t = 0 or t = 20 min as 2 μl of a 10-fold concentrate. At the times of these additions, other incubations received Rb150 buffer. Statistical analysis of triplicate assays can be found in Supplemental Figure S6.

DISCUSSION

MED blocks a late stage of fusion, allowing temporal resolution of components which act earlier during docking (shown schematically in Figure 8). This block is removed by salt (Figure 2) or by the MED ligand calmodulin (Figure 3; green arrows in Figure 8). Further studies will be needed to determine whether MED blocks other intracellular membrane-fusion reactions. HOPS tethers membranes (Stroupe et al., 2006; Hickey and Wickner, 2010), catalyzes trans-SNARE assembly (Baker et al., 2015; Orr et al., 2017; Jiao et al., 2018), and allows trans-SNARE assembly despite the presence of the SNARE-ligand Sec17 (Figure 7B). HOPS initially engages R and catalyzes the assembly of this R with the three Q-SNAREs in trans (Torng et al., 2020). In contrast, GST-C1b, a lipid-targeted membrane tether, allows Sec17/Sec18 to block trans-SNARE association (Figure 7B) and the ensuing fusion (Figure 7A). When fusion with HOPS is blocked by the lipidic ligand MED, intermediates accumulate which are trans-SNARE paired (Figure 7B). These intermediates fuse rapidly upon Sec17/Sec18 addition, bypassing the MED block (Figure 6). With a synthetic tether and complete zippering, Sec17/Sec18 will also trigger fusion after an MED block, showing that Sec17/Sec18- triggered fusion is not specific for HOPS (Figure 6). Fusion with a synthetic tether needs both Sec17/Sec18 and completion of SNARE zippering to overcome the MED lipidic block, as it is prevented by C-terminal truncation of the Qc-SNARE (Figure 6). In contrast, HOPS supports Sec17/Sec18-driven fusion despite a block to complete zippering (Figure 6), showing the continued functional involvement of HOPS after the four SNARE domains have zippered for two-third of their length.

Conceptual model: HOPS remains functionally engaged well into the SNARE-zippering stage of docking. Agents acting as inhibitors at a particular step are shown in red, those which promote or bypass the step in green, and the synthetic tether GST-C1b in purple. Qc3Δ blocks fusion after two-third of the SNARE domains have zippered (Schwartz and Merz, 2009) with membranes tethered by HOPS or by GST-C1b, but only HOPS allows Sec17/Sec18 to bypass the need for energy from zippering for fusion (Figure 6).

The fusion blockade by MED has remarkable features. Its action is reversible in three distinct ways, through bypass by addition of Sec17/Sec18 on a platform of SNAREs or through MED release from membranes by salt or by calcium/calmodulin. The MED block is at an advanced stage of docking, as it is past sensitivity to antibody to Qa. The target of MED blockage is not simply HOPS, because MED also blocks fusion with GST-C1b instead of HOPS (Figure 2B).

We suggest that the lipid ligand MED inhibits the bilayer rearrangements that constitute the essence of two apposed membranes fusing without lysis. Vacuole fusion is highly dependent on lipid composition and properties. A screen of the yeast nonessential gene-knockout library showed that several enzymes of lipid metabolism are needed for normal-vacuole fusion (Seeley et al., 2002). Phosphoinositides and acidic lipids are critical for the membrane association and activities of HOPS (Stroupe et al., 2006) and of Qc (Boeddinghaus et al., 2002; Zick and Wickner, 2013; Zick and Wickner, 2014) and for the allosteric activation of HOPS (Torng and Wickner, 2021). Small headgroup lipids such as sterol and phosphatidylethanolamine are especially prone to nonbilayer structures such as will form during fusion, and these small headgroup lipids are required for in vitro fusion (Zick et al., 2014; Torng and Wickner, 2021). Inhibition by MED may reflect its ligation of phosphoinositides, diminishing lipid mobility as MED forms noncovalent crosslinks between lipid headgroups, or other, unanticipated modes of membrane interaction.

Many conditions in which fusion is blocked by alterations in SNAREs are rescued by Sec17/Sec18. These include C-terminal truncation of any of the Q-SNAREs (Song et al., 2021), replacement of apolar residues which drive SNARE zippering by polar-amino acids (Song et al., 2021), trans-membrane anchoring of only the Qb-SNARE and absence of the integral membrane anchor from the Qa SNARE (Wickner et al., 2023), swap of the juxtamembrane domains between R- and Qa-SNARE (Orr and Wickner, 2022), and altered lipid headgroup composition or fatty acyl chains (Zick et al., 2015). The Sec17/Sec18 relief from blockage by MED is seen with all wild-type SNAREs and vacuolar mimic lipid composition and is readily reversible by three means. MED does not impede the trans-association of the SNAREs (Figure 7B) and allows docking to proceed past several signposts, including early sensitivity to salt (Figure 2), sensitivity of trans-SNARE association to Sec17 (Figure 6), stable transassociation among SNAREs (Figure 7B), and sensitivity to inhibitory antibody to Qa (Figure 4). Our working model is that each MED molecule binds to several lipid headgroups, and this membrane-surface mesh increases the energy barrier to the lipid rearrangements of fusion.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

ATPγS was from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO), SpectraPor dialysis tubing with 25K cutoff and 12-mm width was from Repligen Corp (Waltham, MA), Cy5-streptavidin was from Seracare (Milford, MA), and biotinylated phycoerythrin was from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Carlsbad, CA). Lipids were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL), Echelon Biosciences (Salt Lake City, UT), and Sigma-Aldrich. Biobeads SM2 were from Bio-Rad (Hercules, CA), Histodenz was from Sigma-Aldrich, and β-octylglucoside was from Anatrace (Maumee, OH). MED peptide was synthetized by Vivitide (Gardner, MA).

Proteins

The vacuolar SNAREs Nyv1 (R), Vam3 (Qa), Vti1 (Qb), Vam7 (Qc), and Qc3∆ were prepared as described (Mima et al., 2008; Schwartz and Merz, 2009; Song et al., 2020). MBP-soluble Qb, used in Figure 1, was purified as described in Zick and Wickner, 2013, and cleaved to remove its tag before use. The purifications of HOPS (Zick and Wickner, 2013), GST-Nyv1, and Ypt7-anchored by a C-terminal recombinant transmembrane anchor (Song et al., 2020), Sec17 (Schwartz and Merz, 2009), Sec18 (Haas and Wickner, 1996), and GST-C1b (Johnson et al., 2000; Fratti et al., 2004) were also as described. Yeast calmodulin was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified essentially as described by Peters and Mayer (1998).

Proteoliposomes

Proteoliposomes were prepared as described in detail (Orr and Wickner, 2022). Briefly, β-octylglucoside in methanol was mixed with chloroform solutions of the vacuole-mimic lipids in proportions PC (46 mol %), PE (18%), PI (18%), PS (4.4%), PA (2%), ergosterol (8%), diacylglycerol (1%), and phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate (1%). Solvent was removed under nitrogen and in a Speedvac. Lipids were rehydrated by nutation at room temperature, then stored frozen at –80°C until use. Thawed lipid stocks were mixed in Rb150 (20 mM HEPES/NaOH, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol) plus 1 mM MgCl2 with SNARE proteins and with Ypt7 bearing a C-terminal transmembrane anchor at a 1:8000 M ratio of Ypt7 to lipids and 1:16,000 M ratios of SNAREs to lipids, unless otherwise noted. The mixed-micellar solution also contained either Cy5-streptavidin or biotinylated phycoerythrin. Portions of 1 ml were dialyzed at 4°C for 16 h with stirring in the dark against 250 ml of Rb150 with 1 mM MgCl2 and with 1 gm Biobeads SM2. Each was harvested, mixed in a SW60 tube with 1 ml of 70% Histodenz in iso-osmolar Rb150 + MgCl2, then overlaid with 1.6 ml of 25% Histodenz in this same buffer and finally 450 µl of Rb150 + MgCl2 before centrifugation (90 min, 3°C, SW60, 55,000 rpm). Floated proteoliposomes were harvested from the upper interface, assayed for lipid phosphorus, diluted to 2 mM lipid phosphorus with Rb150 + 1 mM MgCl2, frozen in 30 μl aliquots in PCR strips by immersion in liquid nitrogen, and stored in liquid nitrogen.

Fusion assay

Fusion assays were performed as described (Orr and Wickner, 2023). Briefly, proteoliposomes bearing Ypt7, R, and lumenal biotinylated phycoerythrin were mixed with proteoliposomes with Ypt7, the indicated Q-SNAREs, and lumenal Cy5-streptavidin. These mixed proteoliposomes were preincubated for 10 min at 27°C with nonfluorescent streptavidin, EDTA, and GTP, then chilled on ice, given MgCl2 to complete GTP loading onto Ypt7, and 10 μl portions loaded into wells of a 384 well plate (Corning, Item 4514; Kennebunk, ME). Mixtures of soluble-assay components such as HOPS or GST-C1b, soluble Q-SNAREs, adenine nucleotide, MED, Sec17, and Sec18 were loaded into other wells. After 10 min at 27°C, 10 μl of the soluble-component mixture was added to wells with the proteoliposomes and the FRET between the two fluorophores was recorded (Spectramax Gemini XPS, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) as a measure of fusion, defined as the lumenal mixing of the fluorescent proteins while remaining protected from an excess of extralumenal nonfluorescent streptavidin. All assays were performed in triplicate, and wherever kinetic assays are shown, averages and SDs can be found as Supplemental Figures.
