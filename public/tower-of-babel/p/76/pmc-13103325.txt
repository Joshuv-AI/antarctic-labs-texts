Sexual dimorphism in the programming effects of prenatal manganese multi-media biomarker on temporal cognition in school-aged children
Authors: Jamil M. Lane, Shelley H. Liu, Deborah A. Cory-Slechta, Vishal Midya, Shoshannah Eggers, Cecilia Alcala, Gabriela Gil-Martínez, Martha M. Téllez Rojo, Robert O. Wright
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There is limited knowledge about the sexually dimorphic effects of manganese (Mn) on children’s temporal cognition (i.e., time perception), an important component of executive function, and whether impacts differ by sex. We proposed that the effects of prenatal Mn exposure on children’s ability to perceive time could be best assessed using a multi-media biomarker (MMB) to estimate cumulative exposure. Children aged 6–7 from Mexico City were administered a temporal response differentiation task, an operant test in which a reward is provided if they correctly hold a lever down between 10 and 14 s (s). Short (< 10s) and long holds (> 14s) were incorrect and not rewarded. Blood and urinary Mn levels were assessed from mothers in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters. A covariate-adjusted generalized weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression estimated the effect of a Mn MMB index (combining blood and urine biomarkers into a single metric) on temporal cognitive performance. Sex-stratified models were also assessed. Higher prenatal Mn MMB index, representing higher exposure levels, was significantly associated with impaired performance across multiple cognitive timing measures. When examining Mn exposure in sex-stratified analyses, girls exhibited significantly poorer performance than boys. Among girls, higher prenatal Mn MMB index was associated with fewer timing holds (β = -2.14; 95%CI: -3.57, -0.72), fewer total holds (β = -2.11; 95%CI: -4.18, -0.04), and lower timing accuracy, reflected in total holds excluding durations of 2s or less (β = -1.29; 95%CI: -1.49, -1.09), and greater variability in lever hold durations for total holds (β = 0.67; 95%CI: 0.09, 1.24). High Mn exposure impacts temporal assessment pathways with greater impacts in girls. Our results also highlight the necessity of assessing sex as a biological factor in prenatal Mn exposure, as the sex specificity of results was clear in the stratified models. Results may reflect sex-specific susceptibility via dopamine signaling, neurodevelopmental timing, and/or hormonal influences, warranting further research on these mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44007-4.

Introduction

Manganese (Mn) is an omnipresent, nutritionally essential trace element that is found naturally in the environment. The general population is often exposed to Mn through inhalation (e.g., air pollution), dietary intake (e.g., green leafy vegetables), and drinking water (e.g., municipal and groundwater sources)1. Mn is a micronutrient with essential properties necessary for human growth and development and is fundamental for brain development. Normal concentration levels for adults range from 4 to 15 µg/L in blood and 1–8 µg/L in urine2, although levels vary by age and life stage3,4. For pregnant women, the Food and Nutrition Institute of Medicine recommends a daily adequate intake of 2 mg of Mn, which is considered a safe level of consumption5. Mn has multiple functions and is integral to neurotransmitter metabolism and protects against oxidative stress; however, imbalanced Mn metabolism -both deficient and excessive Mn levels- can be detrimental to cognitive maturation6–8. Chronic low exposure may impair Mn metabolic pathways required for optimal cognitive function. In contrast, Mn is a transition element and can catalyze oxidative stress reactions. Chronic high Mn levels are neurotoxic, with Mn accumulating in mitochondrial tissues and penetrating the blood-brain barrier7,9–12, which impacts the prefrontal cortex, the epicenter for regulating executive functions13–15, with extensive connections to other regulatory brain regions16. Early brain development is susceptible to unbalanced Mn levels, with evidence showing that prenatal exposure causes children to develop more gradually or to exhibit maladaptive cognitive trajectories than children with balanced exposures17–19. Prenatal studies show that Mn neurotoxicity can impair motor, psychomotor, and behavioral foundations20–23 in infants and children. In addition, prenatal Mn exposure can lead to delayed verbal abilities, impaired inhibitory control, weaker working memory, lagged language development, and neurobehavioral dysfunctions20,24–28.

The neurotoxicity of excess prenatal Mn exposure on general cognitive function is now well-established18. However, its effect on time perception (also referred to as temporal cognition), the cognitive ability to perceive time, remains limited and largely unknown. Time perception is regulated by a cooperative network of complex cortical and subcortical regions (i.e., the hippocampus, basal ganglia, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, inferior parietal cortex, and cerebellum)29–32. It is required for various human activities, including verbal time estimation, time production, time orientation, and time management. Time perception is an under-appreciated function crucial to several complex cognitive and behavioral functions that rely on time-based information and temporal control33, such as memory, motor performance, language development, attention span, and executive function34–36. Proficient time perception, particularly during the school-age years, enables children to strategize (e.g., “I will study for my science exam in an hour”) and complete timing tasks efficiently (e.g., “I will finish my test in 10 minutes”). Notably, time perception is foundational for higher-order cognitive faculties, knowledge formation, and sensory input and emotional response systems37,38. For children to grasp and interpret the passage of time, they need to develop a sense of expectancy, which involves the ability to foresee and mentally prepare for upcoming events. Key cognitive processes such as organizing actions, launching tasks, and formulating plans are inherently linked to temporal awareness, as these processes require an understanding of and adherence to specific time constraints39. Given this background, we conducted a study of the impact of variable prenatal Mn exposure on time perception, using a reward-based operant test protocol in school-age children in a prospective, longitudinal study design.

There has been a growing interest in studying the differences in chemical exposure effects on neurodevelopment between boys and girls, commonly referred to as sexually dimorphic effects. We note that sexually dimorphic effects may occur via different toxicodynamics (i.e., sex hormones may impact effects) or at different steps in toxicokinetics, such as exposure (i.e., Mn absorption rates may differ by sex) or may be based on phenotype (i.e., the domain tested may differ by sex at baseline, with Mn exposure then exacerbating differences). All these mechanisms would affect the dose-response curve. However, further research is needed to elucidate the nature and scope of these differences, especially for cognitive timing skills among children, as this area has not been extensively studied in neurotoxicology research.

Furthermore, unlike lead exposure, for which blood levels are considered a gold standard exposure biomarker, no single biomarker media has emerged for Mn, and studies have used blood, tooth, urine, or hair levels. Most epidemiologic research has focused on investigating a single Mn biomarker media for studies of cognitive measures20,40–44, but this approach does not capture cumulative exposure. The absence of a definitive gold standard for measuring Mn heightens the need for an alternative approach that combines biomarker media without requiring the selection of a specific one. This is precisely the tactic employed in the multi-media biomarker (MMB) approach described by Levin-Schwartz et al.45, which creates a cumulative measure of Mn exposure by integrating two or more exposure biomarkers. The MMB employs supervised statistical mixture approaches, such as Weighted Quantile Sum (WQS) regression, to combine biomarkers into a single index or vector. Thus, the present study leverages the WQS approach to investigate the programming effects of an MMB index of prenatal Mn on time perception performance in a cohort of school-age children living in Mexico City and assess whether these relationships exist in a sexually dimorphic manner. Since Mn traverses the placenta and can accumulate in the fetal developing brain, prenatal exposure may induce lasting neurotoxic effects on neurodevelopment that continue into later childhood. Accordingly, we hypothesize that Mn exposures during the prenatal period, as captured by the MMB index, disrupts tightly regulated Mn homeostasis during critical windows of brain development, leading to dysfunctions in time perception programming with sexually dimorphic effects in children.

Methods

Participants

The Programming Research in Obesity, GRowth, Environment and Social Stressors study (PROGRESS) is a longitudinal birth cohort with initial enrollment in pregnancy managed in partnership with the National Institute of Public Health and the National Institute of Perinatology in Mexico, following mothers and their children longitudinally from pregnancy through childhood. From 2007 to 2011, the study enrolled pregnant women from the Mexican Social Security Institute’s primary care clinics in Mexico City during the 2nd trimester. Inclusion criteria ensured that participants were healthy women over 18 years old, within 20 weeks of pregnancy, without cardiovascular or kidney disease, with access to a phone, and intending to reside in Mexico City for at least 3 years. Further details on the inclusion criteria have been previously described46. Following birth, mother-child pairs underwent follow-up assessments, including neurodevelopmental and neurobehavioral tests, as well as biological sample collection. The children (N = 563; See Supplementary Figure S1) included in the study were those who completed the time perception measures at the age of 6–7 years.

Time perception battery

A trained psychologist administered the temporal response differentiation (TRD) test, an operant test (Fig. 1) that assesses time perception. The TRD task is sensitive to a range of toxicological and pharmacological agents and has been widely used as a reliable measure of time perception, in both animals and humans, including children48–52. During the tasks, the child was seated across from a testing panel installed in a wooden cabinet (Fig. 1). The testing panel had circular press-plates and response levers that the child could press, stimulus lights that could indicate a correct or incorrect response, and a row of 6 lights that could indicate which lever to press for one of the behavioral tasks. Located at the bottom of the apparatus was a container where the apparatus delivered nickels following a correct response. After completing the study visit, children traded the earned nickels for a toy of their choice.

Before each task, children watched video instructions in Spanish. The following verbal instructions were presented on the television monitor: “You will notice on the panel in front of you [the narrator points to the row of response levers on the panel], that at the bottom there are four levers. In this game, the lever on your far left, here [the narrator points to and touches the response lever on the far left], will come out of the machine [the response lever on the far left extends from the machine]. To receive a nickel, you must hold the lever down like this [the narrator presses down the extended response lever], for at least 10 seconds (s), but not any longer than 14 s. When you think that it has been at least 10 s, but not more than 14, let the lever up. If you are correct, you will receive a nickel [the narrator releases the response lever and receives a nickel]. This is a very hard game [the narrator presses down the extended response lever again], so don’t give up if you don’t do well right at first. Keep trying and you’ll get it right. Remember, hold the lever down for at least 10 s, but not anymore then 14, and then let up [the narrator releases the response lever and receives a nickel]. We hope you do well at this game. Remember it’s a hard game, so don’t give up.”

After the children acknowledged that they understood the video instructions, the psychologist left the study room and continuously monitored the children’s behavior through a one-way mirror. Children ages 6–7 years completed the TRD test in which they repeatedly held a lever down between 10 and 14 s (Fig. 2). Correctly timed lever holds produced a nickel as a reward. Short (< 10s) and long holds (> 14s) were considered incorrect and not rewarded. Once the child released the response lever, whether the response was correct or incorrect, they could promptly initiate a subsequent trial. The task persisted until either 30 correct responses were achieved or 10 min had passed. The duration of the lever hold is divided into timing holds (holds greater than or equal to 2 s in duration) and bursts (holds less than 2 s in duration). Statistical analysis

RStudio 4.0.3 software was used to perform all statistical analyses. The interquartile range (IQR) method was applied to identify significant outliers in the time perception variables, with limits of 1.5 IQR from the first and third quartiles. Data points falling beyond these limits were subsequently removed. Missingness among exposure variables, particularly in the 3rd trimester, was moderate (approximately 18%), whereas all covariates had complete data (0% missing), and missingness for TRD outcome measures ranged from 0.9% to 8.4%. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations [SDs], frequencies, and percentages) were calculated for all study variables (Table 3).

A series of covariate-adjusted generalized WQS regressions was conducted to estimate the integrated effects of an MMB index of prenatal Mn on time perception performance, accounting for the multiplex correlational structure and timing of measurements for each Mn biomarker media. Mn biomarkers, measured in both blood and urine at the 2nd and 3rd trimesters, were incorporated into the mixture model to create a unified exposure metric (i.e., MMB index). The advantage of the MMB is that users do not have to choose among different exposure biomarkers but can instead integrate them into a single cumulative exposure index. Detailed descriptions of the WQS regression approach are available in prior publications53,54. The WQS implementation proceeded in two steps: (1) a weighted index was derived to quantify the contribution of each trace metal biomarker to the outcome, using 200 bootstrap samples, and (2) this weighted index was entered into a linear regression model to estimate its association with the time perception outcomes. To preserve exposure resolution, biomarker concentrations were ranked into deciles, including values below the limit of detection (< LOD). Given that less than 1% of observations fell below the LOD, the first decile contained both < LOD and detectable values, rather than consisting solely of < LOD observations. All WQS models were constrained to assume a negative direction of effect, and analyses were stratified by sex. Table 4 presents the component weights (expressed as percentages) for each Mn MMB mixture component measured at different gestational time points, indicating their relative contributions to time perception performance. Higher weights reflect greater influence, providing insight into potential windows of susceptibility.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted to implement adjusted generalized linear regression models, enabling the independent examination of the linear relationship between prenatal Mn biomarkers and time perception performance while controlling for covariates. Subsequently, regression models with a quadratic term for each prenatal Mn biomarker were performed to assess nonlinear relationships with performance on each time perception task.

Covariates

Prior research suggests several covariates are associated with neurodevelopmental outcomes in children. All models were adjusted for child age at time of testing, maternal educational attainment (< high school, high school, and > high school), body mass index z scores, and socioeconomic status (SES) index. Our SES index was calculated based on an index created by the Asociación Mexicana de Agencias de Investigación de Mercados y Opinión Pública (AMAI). We used 13 variables derived from a questionnaire to classify participant families into six levels, which we simplified into a relative three-level index of low, medium, and high SES55.

Results

Demographic statistics

The sample consisted of 563 children, including 283 boys and 280 girls, with a mean age of almost 7 years (Table 3). Approximately 40% of mothers had less than a high school diploma, 36% had a high school diploma, and nearly 24% had more than a high school diploma, with a little over half (53.5%) being from low SES backgrounds. The mean ± SD for blood Mn in the 2nd trimester and 3rd trimester in the total sample were 1.41 µg/L ± 0.43 and 1.85 µg/L ± 0.54, respectively. In the 2nd and 3rd trimesters, the means for urinary Mn were 1.28 µg/L ± 0.41 and 1.23 µg/L ± 0.46, respectively. Table 3 also presents the average performance on each of the time perception tasks. Participants retained in the analytic sample did not differ significantly from those lost to follow-up in baseline BMI, maternal education, SES, child sex, or age at assessment (all p > .05).

MMB mixture effect of prenatal Mn on time perception performance

In the total sample (Table 4), a higher prenatal Mn MMB index was significantly associated with impaired performance across multiple cognitive timing tasks, implying reduced temporal control and less precise or sustained response timing. Figure 3 shows that a one-decile increase in the prenatal Mn MMB index exposure was associated with fewer timing holds (β = -1.62; 95%CI: -2.76, -0.48), lower timing accuracy, reflected in total holds excluding durations of 2s or less (β = -0.81; 95%CI: -0.93, -0.69), and greater variability in lever hold durations for total holds (β = 0.56; 95%CI: 0.15, 0.97). In contrast, a one-decile increase in the prenatal MMB index was associated with longer average timing holds (β = 0.47; 95%CI: 0.06, 0.88) on the TRD task. Across all other models, the prenatal Mn MMB index was not significantly associated with percent of tasks completed, average hold, average latency, average latency (SD), total holds, total hold time (SD), and total holds (timing accuracy).

Sex-stratified analysis of the MMB mixture effect of prenatal Mn and time perception performance

In the sex-stratified models (Table 4), girls exhibited significantly poorer time perception performance across several cognitive timing tasks compared to boys, suggesting reduced temporal processing efficiency and diminished precision in timing control, potentially reflecting less stable internal clock mechanisms critical for sustaining accurate response execution. In Fig. 3, girls with a one-decile increase in the prenatal Mn MMB index was associated with fewer timing holds (β = -2.14; 95%CI: -3.57, -0.72), fewer total holds (β = -2.11; 95%CI: -4.18, -0.04), and lower timing accuracy, reflected in total holds excluding durations of 2s or less (β = -1.29; 95%CI: -1.49, -1.09). A one-decile increase in the prenatal Mn MMB index was associated with greater variability in lever hold durations for total holds (β = 0.67; 95%CI: 0.09, 1.24), indicating poorer performance. In boys, the only significant association identified was between a one-decile increase in the prenatal Mn MMB index and reduced timing accuracy, as measured by total holds excluding durations of 2 s or less (β = -0.11; 95% CI: -0.18, -0.04). This association indicated poorer performance relative to higher exposures, although boys overall performed better than girls. No significant associations were observed between the prenatal Mn MMB index and other time perception tasks.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses revealed significant, independent associations between prenatal Mn biomarkers during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters and performance across several cognitive timing tasks, both in total and sex-stratified models. Only a limited number of models exhibited significant quadratic effects, ultimately suggesting predominantly linear exposure–outcome patterns (see Supplementary Materials for full model results).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the prenatal programming effects of Mn on temporal cognition using an MMB to quantify exposure and assessed the sexually dimorphic effects of Mn. The MMB consisted of jointly modeling blood and urinary Mn within a mixture framework to capture an integrated prenatal Mn exposure construct across biological compartments and exposure windows, rather than to imply biological equivalence or a single toxicokinetic pathway. Children with higher prenatal MMB index levels exhibited poorer performance across multiple timing-based cognitive tasks, indicating diminished temporal regulation and less accurate or sustained response timing. Our primary findings revealed a sexually dimorphic pattern, indicating that girls exhibited reduced temporal cognitive performance, characterized by poorer temporal precision, impaired interval timing mechanisms, and less efficient calibration of internal chronometric systems, when exposed to a higher MMB index of prenatal Mn compared to boys. In sensitivity analyses examining individual Mn biomarkers in relation to TRD outcomes, boys consistently exhibited larger effect estimates than girls, particularly for the quadratic terms. This sex-specific pattern was most pronounced in models using blood Mn, a more stable biomarker of exposure, highlighting meaningful sex differences at the level of single biomarkers. In contrast, within the mixture models, stronger associations were observed among girls, motivating our interpretation of heightened susceptibility at the level of the integrated exposure index. Our results are unique, as limited studies have investigated timing-related cognitive abilities, such as time perception, specifically using operant testing in children within a neurotoxicological paradigm. In our study, school-aged children earned nickels as rewards, which they later exchanged for prizes. This reward-based approach differentiates our methodology from many computer-based cognitive tasks that lack tangible incentives and is intended to enhance engagement and sustain motivation during task performance. By increasing motivation, incentivization may support task learning and performance consistency, rather than redefining the outcome as a direct measure of associative learning15.

Our findings are consistent with earlier neurotoxicology studies linking prenatal Mn exposure to decrements in neurodevelopment and neurobehavioral performance in children. For instance, several studies have found that elevated blood Mn levels during pregnancy may alter cognitive development, resulting in maladaptive performance in executive functions, such as working memory, mental flexibility, inhibitory control, and latent domains related to problem-solving, academic performance, and neuromotor skills41,56–59. More specifically, our prior work has revealed that prenatal exposure to Mn and other metals such as Pb was linked to performance deficits across various operant testing domains, particularly in motivation, working memory, and learning processes15,60–63. As previously mentioned, neurotoxicology research in children rarely assesses the involvement of reward reinforcement. Many studies use neurodevelopmental and neurobehavioral assessments derived from global survey-based instruments (e.g., Bayley Scales of Infant Development, Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children – Revised, McCarty Scales of Children’s Abilities) rather than domain-specific testing20,42,64,65. Mn is an essential micronutrient, exhibiting a biphasic effect with neuroprotective properties at balanced levels but neurotoxic at both deficient and excessive concentrations, depending on developmental timing and exposure dose66. Its impacts may be domain-specific, requiring researchers to use diverse cognitive batteries to detect Mn-induced dysfunction in distinct neurocognitive and neurobehavioral systems. Consequently, global cognitive assessments (e.g., IQ tests or broad screening batteries) may overlook or average out these more subtle, domain-specific impairments induced by neurotoxicant substances67. Operant testing, by contrast, offers a more targeted and specialized approach capable of capturing both global and domain-specific effects of neurotoxicants, while also distinguishing learning and behavioral processes from other cognitive mechanisms67.

Additionally, some evidence suggests that boys and girls may exhibit differences in time perception at baseline, with girls presenting greater accuracy in the perception of timing order than boys68,69. Understanding baseline phenotype differences by sex will aid in the interpretation of toxicologic data. Several studies in children suggest sexually dimorphic responses to prenatal Mn exposure40,41,70, potentially ingrained in its nutritional role and influence on brain development, as reflected across various neurodevelopmental and neurobehavioral assessments. Although many studies report stronger associations between prenatal Mn exposure and neurodevelopmental outcomes in girls compared to boys, findings remain inconsistent, with some studies showing adverse, beneficial, or null divergent effects.

Further, what is unique about our study is that these children are pre-puberty, which means metabolic differences due to sex hormones are not as different as in post-pubertal adolescents or adults. The mechanism of action for this effect is therefore present even early in life. To date, studies have shown that higher prenatal Mn has been linked to poorer working memory and neuromotor performance in girls relative to boys41,70, whereas other studies report improved verbal, quantitative, and general cognitive abilities in girls20. In our previous work, which the current study extends, we found that girls performed better than boys on operant tasks assessing spatial memory and motivation15. Importantly, in the current study, girls showed reduced performance on timing-based cognitive tasks, perhaps suggesting that brain systems involved in temporal perception, such as the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus71, basal ganglia72, and cerebellum73,74, may be more vulnerable to Mn-induced neurotoxicity in girls than in boys. These inconsistencies in the literature possibly reflect the inherent complexity of toxicological and epidemiological research, where cohort-specific factors, methodological variability, Mn biomarker selection, and differences in exposure timing and dose-response relationships all influence cognitive outcomes25,47. From a neurobiological and human developmental perspective, the brain growth and maturation trajectories of boys and girls diverge significantly, both structurally and functionally, which may trigger sex-specific susceptibilities to early-life Mn-induced neurodevelopmental effects. Specifically, sex hormones (e.g., estrogen and testosterone) play a crucial role in the sex dimorphic patterns of development of fundamental brain regions, including the timing and trajectory of prefrontal cortex maturation75,76, which has been shown to be a sensitive target for Mn during prenatal and early-life development77,78. Lastly, boy- and girl-specific differences in Mn transporters, oxidative stress response, and dopaminergic signaling may contribute to differential susceptibility to Mn-related neurobehavioral toxicity vulnerability79–82. Together, these biological differences may help explain why the observed associations differed between boys and girls in this study.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess sexually dimorphic effects of prenatal Mn exposure on time perception performance, which is itself a very novel, underappreciated, and important neurophenotype. This represents a novel conceptual advance in the field, as accounting for sex-specificity is critical to fully understanding the differentiated relationship between prenatal Mn exposure and time perception. As mentioned, time perception is a fundamental yet sophisticated cognitive activity that enables children to experience the dynamic temporal structure of everyday events and actions, from temporal-related cognitive, behavioral, and social functions. A primary strength of our study is that the MMB design, combined with WQS implementation, adds the ability to assess the dose-response relations between multi-media Mn, which can provide greater insight into how components of the MMB drive time perception performance. Because the MMB approach integrates biomarkers measured in different media, it has been shown to increase statistical power and precision compared to single biomarkers45. This approach reduces the bias inherent in selecting one biomarker media (e.g., blood vs. urine) to represent exposure when another biomarker could provide complementary exposure data. Our focus on the MMB of Mn also addresses the issue of the lack of a gold standard media for measuring Mn. Further, rather than use child sex as a covariate, the role of child sex was applied as an effect modifier, enabling the detection of whether a sexually dimorphic effect exists and potentially uncover sex-specific protections and regulations relating to oxidative stress.

One potential limitation of our study is that its generalizability to other populations may be limited. This is because the PROGRESS cohort, we analyzed is relatively homogeneous and is located in an urban environment with higher levels of air pollution. We can also speculate that cultural differences in time perception may influence the distribution of the phenotype across populations. However, relying on a single task to measure time perception could introduce limitations and potential measurement error. Because time perception draws on multiple cognitive domains, the lack of adjustment for additional cognitive measures in the WQS and GLM models may have introduced residual confounding and should be considered when interpreting these findings. Additionally, since blood and urinary Mn reflect distinct exposure windows and physiological processes, the composite MMB index may mask matrix-specific effects, accentuating the need for future studies with repeated measures and physiologically based toxicokinetic modeling. Although outcome missingness was low and covariate data were complete, moderate missingness in exposure measures, particularly during later pregnancy, may have reduced statistical power and should be considered when interpreting the findings. We must acknowledge that measurement errors may occur in the exposure variables, another limitation of our study. Another possible limitation is that residual confounding by co-occurring environmental exposures and aspects of perinatal factors, such as child-rearing environment, cannot be dismissed and may have influenced the associations between prenatal Mn exposures and time perception outcomes. Selection bias cannot be entirely ruled out and represents a potential limitation of this study due to participant attrition over time. Individuals who remained in the cohort may differ systematically from those lost to follow-up, which could affect the generalizability of our findings. Finally, future studies should integrate multiple Mn biomarkers across biological media (e.g., hair, teeth, and toenails) to better capture cumulative exposure and incorporate MRI/fMRI methodologies to examine neural substrates of temporal cognition with greater spatial and functional resolution.

Conclusions

Our findings show that the cognitive ability to perceive and estimate time is programmed and impaired by prenatal Mn exposure, with downstream effects on global cognitive function, and that these effects exist in a sexually dimorphic manner, particularly among girls who appear more susceptible than boys. Time perception is a fundamental cognitive process that serves as a foundation for a range of higher-order cognitive functions, allowing children to effectively coordinate behavior, maintain attention and memory, plan actions, and process temporal information across tasks. More importantly, we advance the current literature by using an MMB approach that does not force choices of biomarker media but instead combines biomarker data on the same chemical into an integrated index. This index predicted temporal cognition. This study provides a translational bridge for neurobehavioral toxicology research between human and non-human studies by employing an operant test to measure time perception in children and its sensitivity to prenatal Mn levels.
