Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease symptom subtypes and endoscopy findings: a retrospective, cross-sectional study from Southwestern Nigeria
Authors: E Odeghe, A Itam-Eyo, A Oluyemi, O Owoseni, A Nnadi, J Uchechukwu, O Onodingene, S Tamunodukobipi, O Adeniyi, M Lawal, G Oyeleke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) presents with either oesophageal or extra-oesophageal symptoms, or both. The association between symptom subtypes and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (UGIE) findings has been rarely studied in Nigeria. Our study aimed to determine the pattern of symptom subtypes and UGIE abnormalities, as well as their association in patients with GORD symptoms in Nigeria.MethodsA retrospective, cross-sectional study of UGIE findings in patients with GORD from two endoscopy suites in southwest Nigeria, conducted from January 2022 to December 2023. Symptoms were categorised as oesophageal-only (OO), extra-oesophageal only (EO), or oesophageal plus extra-oesophageal (O + E). Demographic and UGIE data were compared using ANOVA, Pearson’s chi-square, or Fisher’s exact tests using R software. Analyses were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.017.ResultsThere were 411 patients, 180 (43.8%) of whom were male, with a mean age of 45.3 years. The most frequent symptoms were chest pain (131, 31.9%) and heartburn (97, 23.6%). Overall, 280 (68.1%) of them presented with EO, 99 (24.1%) with OO, and 32 (7.8%) with O + E symptoms. Patients with O + E were younger (mean age 37.7 years) than those with OO (44.6 years) or EO (46.4 years), p = 0.008. On UGIE, gastroduodenal erosions were seen in 250 (60.8%), GORD-associated oesophageal injury in 183 (44.5%), peptic ulcer in 43 (10.5%), candidal oesophagitis in 38 (9.3%), tumour in 6 (1.5%), and normal findings in 34 (8.3%). Only gastroduodenal erosions were associated with symptom subtype.ConclusionMost patients with GORD presented with extra-oesophageal symptoms. While fewer than half had reflux-associated oesophageal injury, non-oesophageal lesions were present in the majority of patients, highlighting the importance of endoscopic evaluation in patients with GORD symptoms.Keywords: Gastro-oesophageal reflux disorder, Oesophageal, Extra-oesophageal, Endoscopy

Background

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is a condition in which the retrograde flow of gastric contents into the oesophagus results in symptoms and/or complications [1]. It is prevalent worldwide, with one meta-analysis reporting a pooled global prevalence of 13.3%, although with striking variations among countries due to a lack of standardised definitions [2]. In the United States (US), it is one of the most common gastrointestinal diagnoses [3]. An Ethiopian study found a prevalence of 32% among university students, with 39% experiencing sleep disturbance due to their symptoms [4]. Studies from different regions of Nigeria report prevalence rates ranging from 7.6% to 33%, with up to 3% of affected individuals reporting a significantly impaired quality of life [5–7]. A few prospective studies have investigated the association between GORD and mortality in the general population.

A study from the United Kingdom (UK) showed a slight (1.16-fold) increase in mortality associated with GORD in individuals without oesophagitis or Barrett’s oesophagus, and only part of this association was attributed to oesophageal cancer [8]. In the UK, the estimated cost of healthcare and work absenteeism due to GORD is £760 million, whereas the cost is $24 billion in the US [9]. A 2006 study in Germany reported that the direct cost (including physician visits, drug costs, test costs, and hospitalisation) of GORD per patient per year was approximately €342 [10]. It has been found that being obese/overweight and GORD-specific symptoms and endoscopic features are related, and weight loss improves GORD endoscopic manifestations significantly [11]. The role of dietary behaviour, mainly consumption of specific nutritional components, has not been well delineated. Mild routine physical activity, in conjunction with dietary modifications, such as a high-fibre diet, is considered advisable for preventing and alleviating reflux symptoms [12]. Determining modifiable risk factors for this disease and its symptoms is essential for effective dietary prevention and treatment of GORD [12].

The definition of GORD is, importantly, patient symptom-centred, and the disease is classified into oesophageal and extra-oesophageal syndromes on clinical grounds as well [1]. Its classic symptoms are heartburn and regurgitation (so-called typical or oesophageal symptoms). A host of other, more atypical, or extra-oesophageal symptoms can also be present, including chest pain, chronic cough, asthma, hoarseness, and dental erosions [1].

Although a diagnosis of GORD is clinical and independent of endoscopic findings, some patients may have evidence of oesophageal mucosal injury on upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (UGIE), such as oesophagitis, oesophageal strictures, oesophageal ulcers, or Barrett’s oesophagus [13, 14]. In contrast, others may not have any evidence of mucosal injury. The latter group of patients is usually diagnosed as having non-erosive reflux disease, NERD [1]. A study from Nigeria found that NERD is the predominant form of GORD, occurring in 74.5% of patients, which is consistent with findings from around the world [10, 15].

Previous studies have detailed the prevalence and patterns of UGIE findings in patients with GORD and the associations with presenting symptoms in Caucasian patients [16]. However, despite the high frequency of GORD in Nigeria, there is a dearth of studies examining the patterns of endoscopic findings in these patients and the association between GORD symptom subtypes and upper gastrointestinal endoscopic findings. This study aimed to determine the pattern of symptoms and UGIE abnormalities in GORD and the association of symptom subtype with UGIE abnormalities, closely examining the association of symptom subtype with GORD-associated oesophageal injury.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective, cross-sectional study of the UGIE records of patients with GORD who presented to the endoscopy suites of two centres in southwest Nigeria (Lagos University Teaching Hospital, Surulere, Lagos, and ReMay Consultancy and Endoscopy Centre, Ikeja, Lagos) from January 2022 to December 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (i) adults (≥ 18 years); (ii) presence of GORD symptoms, whether oesophageal (regurgitation or heartburn) or extra-oesophageal (cough, hoarseness, asthma, chest pain, recurrent sore throat, frequent throat clearing, bloating, belching, halitosis, lump in the throat, dysphagia, vomiting and dental erosions), while the exclusion criteria were: (i) previous UGIE; (ii) incomplete procedures. No sample size calculation was used, and data from all UGIE procedures performed during the study period were analysed if they fulfilled the eligibility criteria. All endoscopy procedures were performed using conventional white-light video endoscopes. Ethical approval was obtained before the commencement of the study.

Data collection

The following data were retrieved from the endoscopy records of eligible patients: age, sex, GORD symptoms, and UGIE findings. Oesophageal GORD symptoms were defined as any of the following: regurgitation or heartburn, while extra-oesophageal GORD symptoms were defined as any of the following: cough, hoarseness, asthma, chest pain, recurrent sore throat, frequent throat clearing, bloating, belching, halitosis, lump in the throat, dysphagia, vomiting, and dental erosions. Patients were classified as having oesophageal symptoms only (OO), extra-oesophageal symptoms only (EO), or a combination of oesophageal and extra-oesophageal symptoms (O + E). Endoscopic GORD-associated oesophageal injury was defined as any of the following: reflux oesophagitis (Los Angeles (LA) Grades B, C, or D oesophagitis), oesophageal strictures, or Barrett’s oesophagus [13, 14]. LA Grade A was not classified under GORD-associated oesophageal injury according to the Lyon Updated Guidelines, 2024 (Lyon) [14]. Other UGIE findings, such as hiatus hernia, gastric and duodenal erosions, ulcers, and tumours, were also noted.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using R version 4.3.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Baseline characteristics were described using mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables, such as age, and counts and percentages for categorical variables, such as sex. Clinical characteristics and UGIE findings were compared between patient groups (OO, EO, O + E) using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and the χ2 test (or Fisher’s exact test) for categorical variables, as appropriate. To control for Type I error due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied, and the significance threshold was set at p < 0.017 (0.05/3). There was no missing data.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 1737 upper gastrointestinal endoscopy procedures were performed during the study period, of which 411 met criteria and were included in the study, while 1326 were excluded due to incomplete procedures/missing data in 18, age less than 18 years in 24, repeat procedures in 43, and no GORD symptoms in 1241. Data collected from 411 patients eligible for analysis showed a mean age of 45.3 ± 15.1 years, with 180 (43.8%) males and 231 (56.2%) females. In total, 280 (68.1%) presented with EO, 99 (24.1%) with OO, and 32 (7.8%) with O + E. The most frequent symptoms were chest pain (131, 31.9%) and heartburn (97, 23.6%). Endoscopy findings in the study patients

Endoscopy revealed abnormalities in 377 patients (91.7%) and was normal in 34 (8.3%), resulting in a diagnostic yield of 91.7%. The endoscopy findings were gastroduodenal erosions (250, 60.8%), GORD-associated oesophageal injury (183, 44.5%), peptic ulcers (43, 10.5%), candidal oesophagitis (38, 9.3%), and tumours (6, 1.5%), as shown in Fig. 2. Clinical and endoscopy findings based on the GORD symptom subtypes

Upon comparison of the clinical and endoscopy characteristics in the three GORD symptom subtypes, age was found to be associated with symptom subtype. Patients with O + E were significantly younger (mean age, 37.7 years) than those with either OO (44.6 years) or EO (46.4 years), p = 0.008. Sex distribution was similar among the three subtypes (p = 0.6). GORD-associated oesophageal mucosal injury

In total, 183 patients (44.5%) had endoscopy evidence of GORD-associated oesophageal mucosal injury, while 228 (55.5%) did not have. The GORD-associated oesophageal mucosal injuries seen were reflux oesophagitis in 178 (97.3%), Barrett’s oesophagus in four (2.1%), and oesophageal stricture in one (0.5%).

Discussion

In this multicentre study from southwestern Nigeria, we aimed to describe the pattern of symptoms and endoscopy findings among patients with GORD symptoms and to examine the association between symptom subtype and endoscopy abnormalities. The mean age of our patients is similar to that of a study by Mengistie et al. from Ethiopia [17] still, older than that of another Nigerian study by Sadiq et al. [18]. Our finding of a female preponderance is consistent with other studies [16, 18], but in contrast with others that showed a male preponderance [17, 19].

More than two-thirds of our patients presented with only extra-oesophageal symptoms rather than the typical GORD symptoms of heartburn and/or regurgitation. Chest pain was the most frequently reported symptom. Similar to our findings, a study from Ethiopia reported that less than half of their patients with GORD presented with heartburn or regurgitation [17]. These findings differ from reports from Europe and America, where more than 80% of patients with GORD presented with either heartburn or regurgitation [16, 19]. This difference may be attributed to the superior ability of the Reflux Disease Questionnaire (RDQ) to identify patients with heartburn or regurgitation compared to physician assessment [20], and since our study was based on physician diagnosis and not the RDQ, some patients with heartburn or regurgitation may have been missed [19]. However, our study, being retrospective in design, did not lend itself to the use of the RDQ or other validated GORD questionnaires [21, 22]. Heartburn and regurgitation, although typical symptoms of GORD, may not be as reliable as one might believe due to their moderate sensitivity and specificity for diagnosis [1, 23]. Another reason may be that many Nigerian languages do not have a word for the more specific term “heartburn” in the context of GORD. Therefore, our patients are more likely to articulate the more diffuse term of “chest pain” as their felt symptom. In addition, the association of chest symptoms with heart disease and other ailments that are considered more critical than GORD is perhaps another reason why patients widely use chest pain. Thus, medical practitioners must inquire what non-native English-speaking patients mean when they present with “chest pain” indicative of GORD.

Endoscopy, though helpful in evaluating upper gastrointestinal symptoms, is not recommended in all patients with GORD symptoms [13]. However, due to a lack of minimally invasive tests, such as oesophageal pH monitoring, it is commonly performed in many patients with GORD symptoms in our locality. It is one of the common indications for endoscopy in Nigeria and many regions in Africa [24–26]. In this study, the yield of abnormal findings at UGIE was very high at 92%, with only 8% of our patients having normal findings at endoscopy. This yield is higher than reported by others [18, 27]. Our high yield, a consequence of the preponderance of gastroduodenal mucosal lesions (erosions and ulcers) in our patients, may be a result of the high background prevalence of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection in Nigeria, as said lesions are typically associated with this infection [28, 29]. Gastroduodenal erosions, ulcers, and other lesions, including features of portal hypertension, have also been reported in patients with GORD from different studies [17].

In patients undergoing endoscopy for evaluation of GORD symptoms, endoscopy evidence of GORD includes LA Grades B, C, and D oesophagitis, biopsy-proven Barrett’s oesophagus, and peptic stricture [14]. We found endoscopic evidence of GORD-associated oesophageal injury in fewer than half of our patients. This is similar to reports by Nwokediuko et al. from southeastern Nigeria and Choi et al. from Korea [15, 30]. However, these findings are in contrast with those of Sadiq et al. from northern Nigeria and Vakil et al. from Europe, who found that reflux oesophagitis was the predominant finding in their patients with GORD [18, 19]. The most frequent oesophageal injury seen in our study was reflux oesophagitis, as also reported by Weissman et al. from the US [16]. Chronic exposure of the distal oesophagus to gastric acid, leading to inflammation and increased expression of inflammatory markers, is thought to be responsible for oesophageal injury [31]. Other endoscopic oesophageal injury features, such as Barrett’s oesophagus and peptic strictures, though infrequent in our study, have been reported [16, 19]. Barrett’s oesophagus, a premalignant condition of the oesophagus, has been rarely reported in African patients with GORD [17, 27]. Risk factors for Barrett’s oesophagus include male sex, increasing age, obesity, smoking, and being Caucasian [13, 32].

In this study, symptom subtype was associated with age, but not with sex or GORD-associated oesophageal injury. Our patients with extra-oesophageal symptoms were older than those with either oesophageal only symptoms or a combination of oesophageal and extra-oesophageal symptoms. In the study by Weissman et al. [16]., age and sex were associated with symptom subtype, even though the participants in our study were almost a decade younger. Our findings are in contrast with those reported in the study by Zhong et al., where symptom subtype was associated with sex and the presence of reflux oesophagitis on endoscopy, but not with age [33]. However, in this latter study, a different symptom classification (typical reflux syndrome, reflux chest pain syndrome, and extra-oesophageal syndrome) was used. This, along with differences in study design and other factors, may explain these discrepancies. Our finding of a lack of association between symptom subtype and GORD-associated oesophageal injury may be due to the presence of other upper gastrointestinal symptoms in these patients, even though GORD symptoms were the predominant complaint.

This study’s retrospective design imposes several limitations, including the lack of validated symptom assessment tools, which may affect symptom classification and study findings, and limit comparability with other studies. Additionally, the study is limited by the unavailability of H. pylori test results, a notable cause of gastroduodenal lesions in our environment. Indeed, our high yield of endoscopic abnormalities, resulting from a preponderance of gastroduodenal mucosal lesions, may be a consequence of H. pylori infection, the results of which were unavailable to us for further evaluation. There are yet to be established links between H. pylori infection and gastropathy on the one hand, and GORD on the other. This intriguing interplay is beyond the scope of this research and has been extensively reviewed by Scida et al. [34].

Also, the authors acknowledge that oesophageal chest pain (typically described as retrosternal) is classified as a typical symptom of the disorder [1], however we have chosen to classify it as “atypical” in this paper as we acknowledge the local languages of Nigeria’s many tribes do not hold specific terms for such pain and thus, the specificity of said term in our parlance is questionable. The retrospective nature of this work did not allow for the exploration of further descriptive terms that might be closer in meaning to “chest pain” in the context of GORD-specific definitions. Being a retrospective study that relied on the symptoms and diagnoses as documented by the requesting doctors, it is possible that chest pain from other causes was not ruled out or accounted for. Thus, the diagnosis of GORD might have been misclassified in some patients. Additionally, key data such as symptom duration, treatment with proton pump inhibitor, and patient’s response, lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol), and body mass index were not available, hindering the identification of GORD risk factors and disease progression.

The authors note that various endoscopists conducted the endoscopy procedures in the two centres. This fact also introduces inter-observer variability, which the retrospective nature of this study could not correct for. The study is further constrained by its small sample size, lack of a formal sample size calculation, and geographic focus on southwestern Nigeria, which limits the generalisability of the findings. These limitations highlight areas for future research improvement.

In conclusion, many of our patients with GORD presented with extra-oesophageal symptoms. In addition to reflux-associated oesophageal injury, gastroduodenal lesions were present in the majority of patients, highlighting the importance of endoscopic evaluation in patients with GORD symptoms in our locality.
