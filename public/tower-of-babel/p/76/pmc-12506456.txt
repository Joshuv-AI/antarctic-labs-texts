Programme evaluation of Strategies to Embrace Living with Lupus Fearlessly (SELF): an evidence-based digital programme to build and improve lupus self-management skills
Authors: Sarah D Gilman, Sara Johnson, Katherine Carpenter, Melissa French, Patricia Castle, S Sam Lim, Charmayne Dunlop-Thomas, Cristina Drenkard, Maria Dall’Era, Emily C Somers, Suzanna Zick, Melicent Miller, Daniel J Wallace, Victoria P Werth, Mary Crimmings
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThere is increasing recognition of the need for patient-centred lupus self-management (SM) programmes to improve outcomes. The purpose of this manuscript is to describe the underlying methodology and initial pilot results of Strategies to Embrace Living with Lupus Fearlessly (SELF): an evidence-based digital SM intervention designed for individuals with lupus based on the Transtheoretical Model of Behaviour Change.MethodsWe describe pilot testing and initial feasibility and acceptability results of SELF among individuals with lupus. Participants for the 90-day pilot test were recruited through three Centers for Disease Control & Prevention-funded lupus registry sites across the USA and the Lupus Foundation of America’s constituency, including a diverse sociodemographic lupus population, using varied sources. Feasibility, acceptability and preliminary impact were assessed using data on enrolment, retention, ease of utilisation and satisfaction with SELF’s features, changes in readiness for SM behaviours, impact on patient-reported outcomes (eg, fatigue interference) and user feedback.ResultsNearly 80% of participants had moderate or high skill gaps at programme intake (ie, were in an early stage of change for a key SM skill), underscoring the importance of digital SM programmes like SELF. Among those who completed the programme, almost 60% of participants closed a skill gap by mastering one or more SM skills. Significant effects on patient–provider interactions and fatigue interference were also noted, highlighting a need for future investigation. Qualitative data were mostly positive in terms of feasibility and acceptability, with specific recommendations for future improvement.ConclusionsSELF utilisation and pilot data indicate that SELF is generally feasible and acceptable, particularly among those needing to build lupus SM skills. Future work will explore ways to improve the digital SM intervention and reduce barriers to initial and ongoing engagement.

Introduction

Despite medical advances, the unpredictable nature of lupus still creates challenges for patients, particularly those living in poverty and those who belong to racial and ethnic minoritised groups.13 Self-management education programmes—which teach people the daily mental, physical and social tasks needed to manage chronic conditions—may help reduce these challenges.4 5 Among patients with lupus, self-management programmes have led to positive results including improved self-efficacy, physical function and increased skills.6 7

In 2022, the Lupus Foundation of America (LFA) completed a 5-year cooperative agreement with the Centers for Disease Control & Prevention (CDC) to develop and disseminate ‘Strategies to Embrace Living with Lupus Fearlessly (SELF)’. SELF is a digital self-management programme based on the Transtheoretical Model of Behavior Change (TTM) and codesigned by patients, behaviour change scientists, lupus researchers, social scientists and implementers at the LFA.8 The purpose of this article is to describe the results of the initial SELF programme evaluation in terms of feasibility, acceptability and impact on patient-reported outcomes.

Methods

Conceptual framework

The TTM undergirds SELF, supporting the programme’s structure through an approach rooted in psychology and behaviour change science.9 The TTM defines behaviour change as a process that unfolds through five stages: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance.

Programme development

The SELF programme is a digital self-management intervention for individuals with lupus developed between 2018 and 2020.10 Development involved a landscape analysis of the existing resources, targeted literature searches, interviews with patients and experts, a survey to operationalise a stage-of-change approach, storyboarding and usability testing with a patient committee (see onlinesupplemental files 13 for formative research details). Through this work, four key self-management strategies were identified: managing symptoms, managing stress, managing medications and working with your doctor. SELF is accessible and optimised across any internet-enabled device (smartphone, computer and tablet). See online supplemental file 4 for additional information on SELF’s structure and images.

Programme evaluation

In June 2020, LFA partnered with three academic institutions with CDC-funded lupus registries: Emory University, the University of California at San Francisco and the University of Michigan10 to obtain feedback on SELF from patients and to ensure a representative sample in terms of sociodemographics and disease spectrum. Participants from the academic medical centres and the LFA’s constituency were invited to try SELF for 90 days. Invitations included flyers, postcards and emails.

Participants were invited to complete an online acceptability survey. Those who completed the initial session but not the 90-day assessment received a 39-item survey (see online supplemental file 5 for all surveys). Those who completed the 90-day assessment received a 30-item version survey. ‘Enrolment disengagers’ (those who didn't complete onboarding in the initial session) received a 14-item survey about barriers and suggested improvements. Participants who completed onboarding received up to six email and five text reminders while those who did not received up to 10 email reminders. Survey completers were entered into drawings for Amazon gift cards: US$100 cards for disengagers, and US$250 or US$25 cards for SELF users.

After completing the surveys, seven in-depth interviews were conducted for participants who completed the 90-day assessment (n=4) and those who did not (n=3) (see online supplemental file 6 for discussion guides). Participants were selected from a pool of adults who had previously enrolled in SELF. Due to constraints related to project timelines and available resources, the number of interviews was limited to seven. Interviews lasted 1 hour and were recorded. Interviewers independently coded emerging themes, compared findings and merged their data into overall themes. Data on enrolment, retention, usability, changes in readiness for self-management behaviours and feedback from SELF users were analysed to assess feasibility and acceptability. The onboarding and 90-day assessment administered several patient-reported outcome measures (outcomes and references are in online supplemental file 7), including PROMIS physical/mental health, the Lupus Impact Tracker, PROMIS Pain Interference, Self-Efficacy for Managing Chronic Disease, Perceived Stress, Medication Adherence, PROMIS Fatigue and Doctor-Patient Communication. Pre and postchanges in outcomes were compared among those who completed the 90-day assessment using paired t-tests. Effect size was calculated using Cohen’s d. The Revised Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE V.2.0) were used to report findings.

Patient involvement

Participants played a critical role in shaping SELF, through feedback on key self-management behaviours, operational definitions of these behaviours, perspectives on SELF’s development through a patient committee, feedback on the SELF pilot in in-depth interviews and suggested future improvements.

Ethical considerations

This programme evaluation was classified by CDC as a programme evaluation and a non-research public health practice activity.11 The IRB at each academic institution confirmed that these activities did not constitute research activities and were exempt from IRB approval. All activities with participants met ethical standards for consent, privacy, confidentiality and data protection.

Approximately 1300 invitations were issued for SELF (it is difficult to ascertain an exact number due to the wide scope of recruitment channels used). Individuals in the first four stages of readiness to acquire a lupus self-management strategy (ie, Precontemplation–Action) were classified as having a skill gap (see online supplemental file 8). At onboarding, 20.6% (n=32) of SELF users were classified as having low skill gaps (ie, 0–5 gaps); 41.9% (n=65) as having moderate skill gaps (6–10 gaps) and 37.4% (n=58) as having high skill gaps (11–17 skill gaps). As skill gaps increased, self-reported physical health, mental health, overall health and functioning declined while pain interference increased, underscoring the importance of closing gaps (all p<=0.01).

Among the 42 SELF users who completed the 90-day reassessment, 57.1% (n=24) mastered at least one additional key self-management skill compared with their initial session. In addition to the impact of SELF on skill improvement (ie, promoting behaviour change for key self-management strategies), SELF also reduced patient fatigue interference (mean difference=1.5, p<0.01, d=0.33) and improved doctor–patient communication (mean difference=−2, p<0.05, d=0.27). No differences were found in other outcome measures.

Acceptability survey data were captured from 98 of the 240 SELF enrolees and 24 of the 85 (28.2%) enrolment disengagers. Among the 113 who did not complete a 90-day reassessment, 41 (36%) completed the online acceptability survey, as did 33 of the 42 users (78.6%) who completed a 90-day assessment.

Like the overall SELF user base, acceptability survey respondents were most likely to select Managing Symptoms and Managing Stress as their primary focus area. Survey results showed that SELF users had predominantly positive initial impressions (see figure 2).

In response to questions about opportunities for improvement, respondents shared specific suggestions (eg, ensure text messages do not end, shorter assessments, offer an app version of SELF). Participants also commented on the need for content for those diagnosed with SLE for longer.

During interviews, several key themes arose, including SELF’s positive impact on patients, the perceived usefulness of SELF’s resources, suggested improvements to SELF, barriers to participation and differences in utilisation between those newly diagnosed versus those diagnosed for a longer time. Discussion

This manuscript describes the evaluation of SELF–a web-based, digital self-management intervention for individuals with lupus. Despite challenges with long-term participation, pilot data suggest that it is feasible and acceptable, especially so for those with low self-management skills. While this pilot was not designed or powered to capture impact on health outcomes, significant improvements in fatigue interference, doctor–patient communication and behaviour change (ie, skill mastery) were seen among those who completed the 90-day reassessment. Five main themes also emerged from the interview data: participants reported positive impacts from using SELF, found the resources useful, offered suggestions for improvement, identified barriers that limited participation, and demonstrated different usage patterns depending on time since diagnosis. These findings provide early support for existing evidence that digital self-management programmes may be feasible and acceptable to lupus patients.12 13 Considering documented barriers to in-person self-management programme participation, digital platforms like SELF may complement traditional programmes or serve as standalone interventions for developing self-management skills.14 15

Pilot strengths included participation of under-represented sociodemographic groups, heavy reliance on participatory design, geographic diversity across the USA, reliance on a robust theoretical model to guide the programme’s development and a multidisciplinary team with expertise ranging from rheumatology (SSL, CD-T, CD, MD, ECS, DJW, VPW) to lupus self-management education (KC, SDG, MF, MC, MM), implementation science (SDG), epidemiology (SZ) and psychology (SJ, PC).

The pilot was limited by several factors including lack of a randomised, two-group design, insufficient power to detect differences and a small convenience sample. Accessibility may also be limited for those with low literacy, vision challenges or limited access to technology. Additionally, recruiting participants based on employment status may have introduced significant selection bias by potentially excluding those with higher fatigue levels. This limits our ability to draw conclusions beyond the scope of this specific self-management programme.

Conclusion

Pilot data suggest that SELF is generally feasible and acceptable, especially for those with low self-management skills. Future work will improve SELF by reducing intake length, adding content for those with longer disease duration and measuring health outcomes with larger sample sizes and a control. Additional studies have taken place to explore and validate these findings further; findings are forthcoming.
