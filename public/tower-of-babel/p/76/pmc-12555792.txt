Alternative splicing immune signature for detecting HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma in peripheral blood mononuclear cells
Authors: Noah J. Daniels, Courtney E. Hershberger, Daniel M. Rotroff
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) exhibit disease-specific alternative splicing changes that may serve as robust biomarkers for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Nearly half of the 800,000 annual HCC cases arise from chronic HBV, which affects 254 million people globally, underscoring the urgent need for reliable, non-invasive diagnostic tools. We conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis of PBMC transcriptomes, identifying reproducible immune-related alternative splicing events (ASEs) altered in HBV-related HCC. We identified shifts in inferred immune cell composition and ASEs associated with HBV-related HCC and tumor stage. Using eight immune-related ASEs, we trained a random forest classifier on 120 samples from six independent studies to distinguish HBV-related HCC from chronic HBV, non-HBV liver disease, and healthy controls. The model showed excellent performance in cross-validation (N = 120, AUC: 0.97), internal validation on withheld test samples (N = 54, AUC: 0.99), and external validation with two independent cohorts (N = 27, AUC: 1.00). These findings support PBMC splicing signatures as promising non-invasive biomarkers for early detection and surveillance of HBV-related HCC.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents a global health issue and is currently the fourth most common cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Liver cancer is the second leading cause of years of life lost from cancer and its incidence is projected to rise by more than 50 % with an estimated annual 1.3 million deaths globally by 2040 [[2], [3], [4]]. Notably, HCC accounts for nearly 90 % of primary liver cancers [5,6].

Hepatitis B infection (HBV) is the most prominent risk factor for HCC, contributing to approximately 50 % of cases with other major risk factors including metabolic-dysfunction associated steatohepatitis (MASH), metabolic-dysfunction associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), cirrhosis, as well as other viral infections, such as hepatitis C virus (HCV) [7]. In 2022, the World Health Organization estimated 254 million people living with chronic HBV globally and 1.2 million new infections every year [8]. The contribution of HBV to HCC is multi-faceted, beginning with a combination of pro-neoplastic intracellular mechanisms and modulation of the liver microenvironment, leading to chronic inflammation which is conducive to development of cirrhosis and HCC [9]. Although cirrhosis is common at presentation, HBV has also been shown to increase the chance of developing HCC without cirrhosis.

Given the prevalence of HBV and its close association with HCC, surveillance programs have been instituted in endemic regions [10,11]. However, early-stage detection remains challenging. Ultrasound is the most common imaging diagnostic used for HCC surveillance and has been reported with sensitivities ranging from 58 to 89 % [12] but drop to only 63 % when detecting early-stage HCC [13]. Surveillance approaches that combined biomarker serum alpha fetoprotein (AFP) with ultrasound reported only an additional 8 % detection of cases not previously identified by ultrasonography [14,15]. Furthermore, only 10–20 % of early-stage HCC patients exhibit elevated circulating AFP levels [15]. The importance of early-stage detection is highlighted by the stark contrast in survival rate between early and later stages. Early diagnosis leads to survival rates of more than 70 % at five years, while advanced stages have less than 20 % survival rate during the same period [16]. This is mainly due to the availability of potentially curative options at early stages such as local ablation, surgical resection, and liver transplantation, emphasizing the need for sensitive biomarkers to detect early HCC.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) constitute a significant proportion of circulating immune cells that can provide valuable information about the response of the immune system to cancer. In fact, prior studies have provided evidence for changes in immune-related gene expression and methylation patterns in this compartment for various cancers, including breast, lung, and liver cancer [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. However, other intracellular mechanisms, such as alternative splicing, which governs mRNA isoform production, have not been studied regarding cancer specific immune response.

In this work, we explored alternative splicing of immune-related genes in PBMCs as potential biomarkers for HCC (Fig. 1). We characterized alternative splicing events (ASEs) within circulating immune populations for HBV-related HCC, chronic HBV without HCC, non-HBV liver disease, and healthy individuals. Splicing meta-analysis revealed hundreds of significantly altered ASEs across separate studies, which were overrepresented in pathways involved in immune cell activation. Deconvolution of bulk RNA-seq data provided evidence of significant differences in immune cell populations and ultimately, these data highlighted a stark contrast in immune activity among diagnoses. We leveraged this information to generate a predictive model utilizing ASEs in immune-related genes of circulating immune cells, providing reliable disease classification at all HCC stages and in non-HCC liver disease across eight separate studies.Fig. 1Study Workflow. Results

HBV-related HCC patients have a Unique Alternative Splicing Phenotype

To gain insights into the alternative splicing landscape in PBMCs and whole blood isolated from HCC, chronic-HBV, or healthy individuals, we acquired RNA-Seq data from 120 samples across six separate Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) cohorts, with a majority of HCC cases being HBV-related (Table 1: 67 HCC, 4 HBV, and 49 Healthy) (see Methods, ‘Alternative Splicing Meta-Analysis’) [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. Percent spliced in (PSI) was calculated for 52,522 detected ASEs and clustering revealed three distinct clusters with minimal separation among sample diagnoses (Fig. S1). We identified 1715 annotated ASEs significantly dysregulated across studies (FDR P < .05, |ΔPSI| ≥ 0.05) (Fig. 2A, Table S1). This included 149 ASEs being SE, 59 A3SS, 29 A5SS, 15 MXE, and 1463 RI (Fig. 2B). Of the 1715 significant ASEs, 264 were located within immune-related genes that were consistently dysregulated across cohorts (Fig. 2D and E). Interestingly, 77 % (30/39) of significantly dysregulated exonic ASEs were found to be in annotated functional protein domains (Fig. 2F) with a putative impact on immune response.

ASEs are Over-Represented in Pathways Associated in Immune Response

Gene ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) over-representation analysis were performed on all genes of significantly dysregulated ASEs (N = 1715) identified in HCC vs non-HCC PBMCs (FDR P < .05, |ΔPSI| ≥ 0.05) (Tables S2 and S3). Most notably, we identified a large group of pathways involved in leukocyte activation and immune response. This included activation of innate immune response, regulation of adaptive immune response, B cell activation, NF-κB signaling, and lymphocyte, B Cell, and T cell differentiation (Fig. 3A). Moreover, KEGG analysis showed an abundance of ASEs in immune response pathways such as Hepatitis B, PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer, T cell receptor signaling, as well as NOD–like receptor signaling (Fig. 3B, Fig. S2). HBV-related HCC PBMCs have a Distinct Immune Cell Composition

PBMCs are primarily composed of different populations of lymphocytes that play a crucial role in the immune system. To explore immune cell composition across disease states, we estimated relative immune cell proportions and performed unsupervised hierarchical clustering and dimensionality reduction (Table S4). We further examined pairwise associations for each cell type and diagnosis by performing logistic regressions. A total of 40 statistically significant differences in 12 cell types were identified (FDR P < .05), reflecting substantial variation in inferred immune cell populations among diagnoses (Fig. S4). Among the most prominent changes were shifts in CD4 memory T cell subsets. Resting CD4 memory T cells were significantly reduced in HBV and non-HBV liver disease compared to HCC and healthy controls, suggesting a possible depletion or altered differentiation state in chronic HBV or non-neoplastic late-stage liver disease. In contrast, activated CD4 memory T cells were elevated in both HBV and Non-HBV liver disease compared to either HCC or healthy, indicating immune activation or chronic stimulation. In addition to CD4 memory subsets, other immune populations such as monocytes, mast cells, NK cells, and M0 macrophages also showed significant variation, particularly between HBV or HCC and the other groups. For example, M0 macrophages were elevated in all diagnoses groups compared to healthy controls.

Immune-related ASEs are cell type-specific and associated with HCC progression

Analysis of the cell type specificity of ASEs was performed for all immune-related exonic ASEs (N = 3511) and immune cell types (N = 18) that passed near non-zero variance filtering (Table S5). This analysis revealed 2962 ASEs located within 783 unique genes significantly correlated with at least one cell type (Fig. S5A). Notably, we identified a substantial shift in 925 ASEs within 450 unique genes that had significant opposite correlations in memory resting CD4 T cells and memory activated CD4 T cells, indicating that these ASEs may play a role in CD4 T cell activation (Fig. S5B, Table S6).

We investigated whether immune-specific ASEs were associated with HCC stage and found that increased PSIs for three SEs in HLA-E, FYN, and CCND3 were associated with higher HCC staging (FDR P < .1) (Table S7). Similarly, we assessed associations between HCC stage and immune cell type proportions (Table S8). Increased proportions in M0 macrophages, monocytes, and plasma cells were associated with higher HCC stage, whereas M2 macrophages, CD8 T cells, and naïve B cells were enriched in earlier HCC stages.

Immune cell-type composition offers modest predictive insights for HBV-related HCC

We showed that there are significant differences in inferred immune cell type proportions in PBMCs based on diagnosis (Fig.4, Fig.S3-4). We subsequently generated a random forest (RF) model to determine if immune cell type proportions can distinguish HBV-related HCC from non-HCC (Chronic HBV, Non-HBV liver disease, and healthy). The balanced accuracies for HCC vs non-HCC classification were 68 %, 71 %, and 17 % with AUCs of 0.78, 0.79, and 0.93 for the training (leave-one-out cross-validation, LOOCV), internal validation, and external validation cohorts, respectively (Fig. S6). Cell type proportions demonstrated similar performance for distinguishing the three groups: HBV-related HCC, chronic HBV, and healthy/non-HBV liver disease (Fig. S7). Overall, the model demonstrated satisfactory classification for intermediate and late-stage HCC but underperformed when predicting early-stage HCC (2 out of 16 cases, BCLC: A0 or A, TNM: I) and classification of our external validation cohort (Fig. S6D).

ASEs in immune-related genes can predict HBV-related HCC

Given the significant changes observed in immune-related ASEs, we hypothesized that circulating immune cells that interact liver tumors may experience alternative splicing that provides a predictive signature that can be used to distinguish HBV-related HCC from chronic HBV, non-HBV liver disease, or healthy individuals. The mean LOOCV OOB error rate was plotted for each iteration and the model with lowest mean OOB (18.1 %) included eight ASEs (A3SS, A5SS, or SE) in the following genes: KLF6, PSPC1, FTH1, PMAIP1, TCIRG1, FYB1, HCLS1, and PTPRC (Fig. 5A, Fig. S8B–C). The LOOCV procedure with these eight features provided classification metrics for the training set with the following balanced accuracies and AUCs: HCC - 91 % and 0.975, HBV - 89 % and 0.98, Healthy/non-HBV liver disease - 86 % and 0.95 (Fig. 5B and C). A 3-class RF classifier was trained using these eight features to predict HBV-related HCC and tested on the withheld internal validation, and external validation cohorts. This resulted in balanced accuracies and AUCs for the two validation cohorts as follows: For detecting HCC, internal validation achieved 96 % and an AUC of 0.99, while external validation attained 98 % and an AUC of 0.98. Similarly, for detecting HBV, internal validation achieved 91 % accuracy with an AUC of 0.98, and external validation achieved 50 % accuracy with an AUC of 0.7. For detecting healthy/non-HBV liver disease, internal validation achieved 86 % accuracy with an AUC of 0.95, while the external validation achieved 75 % accuracy with an AUC of 0.63. Of note, all misclassified HBV samples in the external validation cohort were predicted to be in the healthy/non-HBV liver disease group, and none were misclassified as HCC. To assess the predictive contribution of each ASE, we trained separate random forest classifiers using each feature individually and evaluated performance across the training, internal validation, and external validation datasets (Table S9, Fig. S9A). Violin plots illustrate the PSI distributions per diagnosis group for each ASE (Fig. S9B).

Individual AFP measurements were available for two studies (SRP330954 and SRP332585). Only a single HCC case was misclassified by our ASE-based model, and this case was AFP-negative (Fig. S10). We also repeated the predictive modeling pipeline including MXEs, which yielded a 13-feature model that incorporated all eight features from the primary model plus five additional ASEs, including a MXE in IRF1. Although the expanded model performed well (Fig. S11), it did not exceed the accuracy of the original 8-feature model.

Of note, our internal and external validation cohorts contained a group of patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) (HBV-related: N = 20, non-HBV-related: N = 8) or non-HBV acutely decompensated cirrhosis (LC) (N = 5) from two separate PBMC GEO studies. ACLF is defined as sudden hepatic decompensation due to a background of chronic liver disease with or without cirrhosis and is associated with one or more organ failures. This process is initiated by injury and death of hepatocytes leading to systemic inflammation. Our 2-class model correctly classified 32/33 (97 %) samples (HBV-ACLF: 19/20, non-HBV ACLF: 8/8, LC 5/5) (Fig. 6D). The 3-class model correctly classified 30/33 (91 %) with only one HBV-ACLF classified as HCC and two HBV-ACLF classified as healthy/non-HBV liver disease (Fig. 5D). Of note, twenty of the ACLF samples had a background of HBV, whereas the other eight ACLF were caused by another precipitating event other than HBV [28].

To evaluate whether combining cell type proportion information and ASEs would boost prediction accuracies, we generated a RF model that incorporated both sets of features (Fig. 5A, Fig. S8B–C). The combined models for HBV-related HCC vs non-HCC or HBV-related HCC vs HBV vs healthy/non-HBV liver disease did not perform better than the model including only ASEs (Figs. S12–13). In addition, the ASEs tended to have the highest Gini scores for indicating they were more informative features than the cell type composition information (Figs. S12A and S13A). Overall, the model demonstrated robust performance as demonstrated on withheld test samples from six studies as well as on a separate independent study that not used in feature selection.

Discussion

HCC continues to be one of the significant causes of cancer mortality and has shown a rapidly increasing incidence. HBV remains a leading cause of HCC worldwide, but there are no accurate non-invasive screening tools in clinical use. Here, we identified 1701 consistently dysregulated ASEs in HBV-Related PBMCs across six studies when compared to PBMCs from healthy individuals or chronic HBV. Prior studies have shown that alternative splicing of key immune genes occurs during an immune response for many leukocyte lineages, such as macrophages, dendritic cells, B-cells, and T-cells [29,30]. Of note, many of these ASEs were over-represented in immune-related pathways such as activation of immune response, immune cell differentiation, as well as the PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer. Furthermore, the immune cell composition was significantly distinct among PBMCs from patients with HBV-related HCC, HBV, non-HBV liver disease, and healthy patients.

We delineated cell type specificity for our immune-related ASEs based on the correlations between PSI and cell type proportions from deconvoluted bulk RNA-seq data—identifying numerous ASEs significantly associated with specific immune cell types. Notably, 31 % (886/2850) of these ASEs showed significant opposing correlations between memory resting CD4 T cells and activated CD4 T cells, switching from positive to negative, or vice versa, upon activation. This suggests that immune-related ASEs capture activation-state transitions in distinct immune populations, which aligns with previous research demonstrating the role of alternative splicing in regulating T cell function [31,32].

We also explored the relationship between ASEs and disease progression, identifying specific cell types and ASEs associated with HCC staging. Skipped exons in HLA-E, FYN, and CCND3 were associated with more advanced HCC stages. Notably, previous studies have shown alternative splicing of CCND3 in exhausted T cells was linked to poorer prognosis in HCC patients, reinforcing the potential impact of splicing regulation in immune-mediated tumor progression [33].

We used the inherent differences in ASEs and immune cell composition between these groups to build a minimally invasive diagnostic biomarker utilizing ASEs in immune related pathways in the PBMCs of these patients with HBV-related HCC. To our knowledge, this is the first predictive model to utilize ASEs within PBMCs as a potential biomarker for HCC detection. We were able to build a robust model using eight ASE features that performed well in our training cohort (Sensitivity: 90 %, Specificity: 96 %, AUC: 0.971, N = 120) and internal validation cohort (Sensitivity: 100 %, Specificity: 93 %, AUC: 0.992, N = 54), built from six separate GEO studies using LOOCV. Furthermore, our model only misclassified one healthy sample in our external validation cohort consisting of two independent GEO studies (sensitivity: 100 %, specificity: 95 %, AUC: 1, N = 27). Of note, one of these GEO datasets (SRP330954) was derived from whole blood and not PBMCs, unlike the data the model was trained on. Considering that whole blood samples are comprised of a large proportion of white blood cells, it demonstrates robust model performance for detecting these events in more heterogeneous sample types. This has important clinical implications, as the use of whole blood could eliminate the need for PBMC isolation and simplify implementation in real-world surveillance. However, validation across larger whole blood cohorts will be needed to determine whether model performance is comparable to the PBMC-based assay.

In addition to correctly classifying HBV-related HCC from healthy subjects, our model distinguished HBV-related HCC from chronic liver disease cases with and without chronic HBV and varying levels of liver dysfunction. This provides evidence that the model is detecting a signature that is HCC specific rather than an immune response related to the HBV infection, severity of liver dysfunction, or systemic inflammation. Notably, our HBV group was heterogeneous, collected by two separate studies, with different stages of liver dysfunction, and varying levels of circulating HBV DNA. Out of the 53 HBV samples, at least 9 had cirrhosis (AFP μg/L: 2.2 [1.7, 2.9]; INR: 1.0 ± 0.0; HBV DNA level (IU/mL): ≤2 × 102 N = 8, 2 × 102 - 2 × 106 N = 1), 10 were diagnosed with acute-on-chronic hepatic dysfunction, defined as a group of patients with more severe liver dysfunction (AFP μg/L: 4.5 [2.5, 278.3]; INR 1.4 ± 0.4; HBV DNA level (IU/mL): ≤2 × 102 N = 4, 2 × 102 - 2 × 106 N = 6), and 20 were diagnosed with ACLF, defined as late stage liver failure with systemic inflammation (AFP μg/L: 138.7 [58.0, 222.7]; INR 2.3 ± 0.6; HBV DNA level (IU/mL): ≤2 × 102 N = 2, 2 × 102 - 2 × 106 N = 16, >2 × 106 N = 2) [27].

We found that the model could differentiate samples from both HCC and HBV from those whose disease was triggered by causes other than HBV using an independent cohort of non-HBV ACLF samples. This result suggests that our model captures a signature specific to HCC or chronic HBV infection [28]. Overall, the varying levels of liver damage and dysfunction in the HBV group suggest that it is unlikely that our model is separating samples based on a signature for liver damage in our HCC group. This highlights the possibility for the expansion of immune-related ASE diagnostic biomarkers for detection of HCC with different etiologies. While our analyses indicate that the ASE signature primarily reflects HCC-specific splicing alterations rather than HBV-related immune dysfunction, its performance in non-HBV HCC remains to be determined and will require future validation in well-characterized cohorts with alcohol, NAFLD, or HCV-related HCC.

Our ASE predictive model optimized at eight features, including ASEs in the following genes: KLF6, PSPC1, FTH1, PMAIP1, TCIRG1, FYB1, HCLS1, and PTPRC. Although identified as being important for prediction, only SEs in KLF6 and FYB1 reached statistical significance for differentially splicing across studies in our meta-analysis (|ΔPSI| > 0.05, FDR P < .05). Interestingly, KLF6 and FYB1 mRNA were not significantly differentially expressed across studies (FDR P > .05), suggesting that these ASEs provide unique sources of information not directly captured by overall mRNA expression.

The most important predictive feature based on Gini scores across all models was a SE in KLF6 (FDR P = 1.0 x 10−7, ΔPSI = 0.08). This gene is a transcription factor that has been implicated in macrophage polarization and B cell growth and development [34,35]. Interestingly, KLF6 is also a member of the GO pathways: lymphocyte differentiation (GO:0030098), mononuclear cell division (GO:1903131), and B cell activation (GO:0042113), all of which were over enriched for significant ASEs in HCC vs non-HCC PBMCs. Prior work has shown KLF6 is a novel transcription regulator of canonical macrophage polarization and is dysregulated in various diseases such as IBD, hepatic fibrosis, hepatic steatosis, and cancer [34,36,37]. Recent work has provided evidence of KLF6 involvement in the phenotypic switch of tumor-associated macrophages, ultimately altering cancer cell proliferation and migration [38]. The SE in KLF6 encodes for a C2H2 zinc finger domain and produces an annotated isoform, KLF6-SV3 [39]. Although little is known about the role of this splice variant, its large contribution to our model warrants further investigation in future research efforts.

A SE in FYB1, also known as ADAP, was a key feature in our models and was significantly dysregulated across studies (FDR P = 1.6 x 10−6, ΔPSI = 0.15). FYB1 is expressed in T cells, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, and myeloid cells [40]. Interestingly, FYB1 is also a member of the GO pathways: activation of immune response (GO:0002253), immune response-regulating signaling pathway (GO:0002764), and immune response-activating signaling pathway (GO:0002757), all of which were over enriched for significant ASEs in PBMCs from patients with HCC vs non-HCC. Furthermore, this SE in FYB1 was significantly negatively correlated with CD4 memory resting T cells (R = –0.57, FDR P = 7.7e−15) and positively correlated with CD4 activated memory T cells (R = 0.41, FDR P = 2.2e−7), suggesting a shift in exon incorporation during CD4 T cell activation. FYB1 acts as a pivotal protein in T cell receptor activation by serving as a crucial mediator in the FYN-LCP2 signaling cascade [41]. Interaction, of FYB1 and SLP76 regulate T cell activation, adhesion, and differentiation [41,42]. In CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, FYB1 was shown to increase PD-1 expression, resulting in a decreased cytotoxic response against cancerous cells [43]. Other work has described alternative splicing of FYB1 in effector T cells from patients with HCC [33]. FYB1 plays a functional role in other immune cell types such as activation of natural killer cells, and IL2 simulated lymphocytes, subsequently enhancing an anti-tumor response [44]. Furthermore, it has been shown to play a role in migration, proliferation, stimulation, and cytokine production [[45], [46], [47]].

The final predictive model also includes features not significant in the meta-analysis, reflecting their potential biological relevance. PSPC1, a core paraspeckle scaffold, contributes to innate immune regulation through activation of the cGAS–STING pathway and downstream interferon responses [48,49]. In HCC, it promotes epithelial–mesenchymal transition, stemness, and metastasis via TGF-β and Wnt/β-catenin signaling [48,50]. FTH1 encodes the heavy chain of ferritin, which stores and regulates intracellular iron, thereby protecting cells from iron-induced oxidative damage and ferroptosis [51]. In cancer, FTH1 expression has been associated with tumor immune infiltration by macrophages, neutrophils, and T cells [51,52]. PMAIP1, also known as Noxa, encodes a BH3-only protein that selectively antagonizes the anti-apoptotic factor MCL-1 through proteasomal degradation to promote apoptosis, and its expression is regulated by p53 as well as by T-cell receptor–driven PKC signaling [[53], [54], [55]]. Splice variants of Noxa that lack the BH3 domain are unable to induce apoptosis [56]. The TCIRG1 gene encodes multiple protein isoforms, with the full-length isoform functioning as the V-ATPase a3 subunit essential for lysosomal acidification, while the shorter isoform encodes a T-cell–specific membrane protein that regulates immune activation by modulating CTLA-4 expression and T- and B-cell responses [57]. TCIRG1 expression has also been correlated with immune infiltration in tumors [58,59]. HCLS1, also known as HS1, is an actin-regulatory adaptor required for T and B cell receptor signaling, immunological synapse formation, and NK cell cytotoxicity [60,61]. PTPRC (CD45) is a tyrosine phosphatase expressed on all nucleated hematopoietic cells. It regulates T and B-cell receptor signaling, and influences T-cell differentiation and activation states [62,63]. In HCC, CD45+ T cells display altered activation profiles that are linked to distinct patterns of immune infiltration [33,64].

Even with WHO-initiated advances in HBV vaccine implementation around the world, developing and maintaining immunity to HBV continues to be a challenging task, necessitating accessible, sensitive, and minimally invasive biomarkers [65]. Current screening tools for HCC in the healthcare setting are limited. These include ultrasound of the liver and measuring serum AFP. Although the screening techniques are effective for detecting later stage HCC, they have been shown to miss nearly 40 % of early-stage diagnoses when the cancer can be most successfully treated [66]. Our HBV-HCC vs non-HCC model only misclassified 5 of 67 HCC cases across all cohorts and detected 13/16 early-stage HCC cases (BCLC: A0 or A, TNM: I) and all intermediate stages (15/15, BCLC: B, TNM: II). Furthermore, only 7 out of 134 non-HCC samples (chronic HBV, non-HBV liver disease, and healthy) from eight separate studies were misclassified as HCC, providing both high sensitivity and specificity. The heterogeneity of our samples reflects the diversity of real-world clinical populations, enhancing the generalizability of our predictive model. Additionally, by incorporating multiple independent datasets and using raw PSI values, we eliminate the need for batch normalization and facilitate integration of future datasets.

This model holds promise as a minimally invasive biomarker signature for surveillance of HCC in the chronic HBV population and future studies will focus on validating these results in a larger cohort of patients with a more comprehensive set of clinical characteristics. While our analysis demonstrates the strong potential of ASE-based classifiers, future work is needed to evaluate their feasibility for clinical implementation. Because only eight ASEs are required, this approach could be adapted to targeted multiplexed assays, which may support eventual cost-effective, minimally invasive applications in real-world surveillance. Ultimately, a more sensitive diagnostic could help guide physicians to next steps in the care for their patients, such as additional screening tools or potential treatment options. In summary, we have shown that ASEs in PBMCs have substantial predictive power to be used as a non-invasive biomarker for HBV-related HCC.

Limitations

As with any study, there are important limitations that should be considered when interpreting these results and present opportunities for future studies. Because we were limited to the available data previously published with these cohorts, some clinical characteristics may have been missing that introduced unforeseen biases. Since the samples were sourced from publicly available GEO datasets, certain clinical characteristics, such as HBV subtypes, co-existing liver diseases, and other potential confounders were unavailable at the individual sample level for certain studies, potentially limiting the interpretability of our model. Additionally, while our study includes 201 samples from eight separate datasets, further validation in larger and more diverse cohorts is necessary to confirm the robustness and generalizability of our findings. The heterogeneity of HCC, particularly the influence of HBV subtypes on disease progression and immune-related splicing events, remains an important area for further investigation. Future studies incorporating well-annotated datasets with a standardized staging system, HBV genotypic information and detailed clinical profiles will be essential to elucidate subtype-specific splicing signatures and their potential diagnostic or prognostic value.

We used raw PSI values for classifier construction to preserve generalizability across heterogeneous datasets and to avoid cohort-specific normalization, allowing our model to be applied directly to future datasets without recalibration. PSI values represent a ratio between isoforms, which accounts for differences in sequencing depth, unlike raw gene expression counts. However, it is possible that other subtle batch effects are present among these datasets. The consistent performance across eight independent cohorts suggests robustness to batch effects, but future prospective studies using standardized sample processing will be important to evaluate the impact of technical variability.

Current HCC surveillance relies on AFP and ultrasound, but both have limited sensitivity, particularly for early-stage disease. We were unable to perform a direct comparison of ASE performance with AFP or ultrasound due to limited and inconsistent AFP data across cohorts and lack of imaging data. While our small AFP-linked cohorts suggest the ASE signature is agnostic of AFP levels, larger well-annotated datasets will be required to formally evaluate the potential added value of combining ASE with AFP and ultrasound for surveillance.

An important question that remains unanswered is whether ASE-based signatures can be used for surveillance and early prediction of HCC prior to radiographic diagnosis. Our current study design did not include longitudinal blood samples, and thus we cannot directly address this point. However, the observation that none of the cirrhotic HBV samples in our study were misclassified as HCC is notable, given that patients with HBV-related cirrhosis are known to develop HCC at an annual incidence of approximately 1–5 % [67]. This suggests that the ASE classifier may capture splicing alterations that emerge very early in tumorigenesis, or immediately prior to overt HCC development. Future studies incorporating longitudinal surveillance cohorts with serial PBMC sampling will be essential to evaluate whether ASE signatures can identify high-risk patients before conventional diagnostic evidence of HCC is present.
