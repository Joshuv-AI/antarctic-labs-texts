Performance of shock index, lactate and physician’s gestalt in predicting adverse events among critically ill adult patients at an Emergency Department in Tanzania☆
Authors: Humphrey G Medarakini, Alphonce N Simbila, Ellen Weber, Zeinab B Hassan, Florian Vedasto, Jennifer Tesha, Rebecca N Tugara, Peter P Kunambi, Elizabeth Bernard, Said S Kilindimo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPhysicians working in Emergency Departments (ED) must be able to identify critically ill patients likely to deteriorate and prioritize their care. While shock index, point of lactate and physician gestalt are commonly used in high-income countries to predict adverse events their applicability has not been widely tested in low-middle income countries.MethodsA prospective cohort study of all adult patients (≥18 years) triaged to the resuscitation area of Muhimbili ED in Tanzania from 1st September - 31st December 2022. Shock index (SI) on arrival, initial point of care lactate and the physician’s estimates of the probability of an adverse event were recorded. The discriminatory ability of these index tests was compared for prediction of an adverse event in the ED, using DeLong’s test. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values were calculated. For SI, a standard cut off ≥ 1 was used and for lactate a standard cut off > 2 mmol/L was used. For physician gestalt, the optimum cut off of a 70 % probability of adverse event was determined based on Youdens’ index.ResultsA total of 320 critically ill patients were enrolled. SI (AUC = 0.734, 95 % CI 0.658 to 0.810) achieved a sensitivity of 59.3 % and a specificity of 83.1 %. Elevated lactate (AUC = 0.819, 95 % CI 0.76 to 0.88) achieved a sensitivity of 89.8 % and a specificity of 58.2 %. Physician gestalt had an AUC = 0.877, (95 % CI 0.833 to 0.922) using the optimal probability of ≥70 % and achieved a sensitivity of 88.1 % and a specificity of 76.2 %. The AUC of physician’s gestalt was significantly higher than that of SI (p = 0.0008), but was not significantly different from lactate (p = 0.067). The difference in AUC for lactate and SI was also not statistically significant (p = 0.10).DiscussionPhysicians gestalt in our setting demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to shock index and a comparable performance to lactate. This supports the use of gestalt as a low-resource risk stratification tool in emergency settings. However, accuracy of gestalt may depend on physician experience.Keywords: Physician gestalt, Shock index, Lactate, Critically ill patients, Adverse events, Emergency department

Introduction

It is estimated that over 90 % of worldwide trauma deaths, [1] maternal mortality, [2] and deaths from pneumonia, meningitis, and other infections [3] occur in low and middle-income countries. Emergency Departments (ED) in these areas are under-resourced, making it essential that the most critically ill patients are identified early to prevent impending adverse events such as need for vasopressors, initiation of mechanical ventilation, cardiac arrest and ED deaths.

A number of studies in high-income-countries have assessed the role of lactate and shock index in early prediction of adverse events. As a blood biomarker of inadequate organ perfusion, lactate levels have been shown to be a predictor of poor prognosis in the ED, particularly in sepsis[4,5] A lactate level >2 mmol/L has been associated with an increased risk of adverse events [6,7] while improved outcomes for critically ill patients have been linked to early lactate clearance [6] However, in low–middle income countries (LMIC) other than in tertiary hospitals, regional and lower level health facilities may lack point of care lactate testing, or if available this adds cost to the care of patients, most of whom are poor and pay for care out of pocket.

Shock index (SI) is defined as heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure (SI=HR/SBP) with normal values ranging from 0.5 to 0.7 [8] Easily calculated from vital signs that are routinely taken in the ED, SI does not incur additional costs, [9] thus addresses the limitations of using lactate in low resource areas. A systematic review in the United States found that a SI ≥1 predicted a greater likelihood of mortality, the need for vasopressor initiation, and the need for admission to the intensive care units among critically ill adult patients presenting to the ED [10]

Physician gestalt is the ability to use patient’s clinical history, physical examination findings and the clinician’s prior experience to assess a patient’s condition. In practice, it plays a significant role in decision-making for critically ill patients in determining need for fluid resuscitation and vasopressor initiation in septic patients [11] In a study conducted in the United States, ED physician judgment predicted a 28-day hospital mortality among critically ill emergency department patients with an area under the curve (AUC) of, 0.78 (95 % CI, 0.74–0.82) [12] However, in practice physician gestalt can be variable depending on the physicians level of education and clinical experience [13]

While SI, point of care (POC) lactate, and physician gestalt are widely used in high-income countries to guide early risk stratification at ED, their performance in predicting adverse events has not been well studied in LMIC settings. Limited resources, delayed access to POC tests, and high patient volumes create a context in which cost-effective and rapid decision-making tools are essential. This study aimed to determine the performance of SI, POC lactate and physician gestalt in predicting adverse events in critically ill ED patients. Our goal was to find a cost-effective, sensitive and specific marker to guide care in EDs in LMICs.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a prospective hospital-based diagnostic cohort study comparing the performance of shock index (SI), point of care (POC) lactate and physician gestalt in predicting adverse events among critically ill adult patients presenting to the Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) ED in Tanzania, a large urban national referral hospital. Approximately half of patients seen in the ED are referred from peripheral hospitals with fewer capabilities.

Participants

Patient enrollment occurred between 1st September - 31st December 2022. Inclusion criteria for the study were adults ≥ 18 years, triaged to the resuscitation rooms of the ED (considered “emergent” by 3-level triage system). Patients were excluded from enrollment if no informed consent was obtained from either the patient or relative, point of care lactate was not performed, they were already receiving vasoactive drugs, or invasive mechanical ventilation, were in sustained cardiac arrest or cardiopulmonary resuscitation was ongoing upon arrival at the ED. Although the ED has standard protocols for critically ill patients, investigations and treatment were performed at the discretion of the treating physician.

Study procedures

Research assistants were present to enroll patients throughout the week on both day and night shifts. Each patient was assessed for eligibility, consented, and enrolled shortly after arrival in the ED when the research assistant was present. For each patients a structured case report form was used. Prior to obtaining any lab results, and within 10 min of patient arrival, the research assistant asked the treating physician to estimate the likelihood (from 0 to 100 %) that this patient would have an adverse event in the ED. Physicians were not explicitly informed of SI at the time of recording their gestalt estimation although they had access to vital signs. After giving their estimate the research assistant asked the physician to select which parameters they used to inform their judgement including: chief complaint, vital signs, SI, clinical findings, general appearance of the patient, history of presenting illness, level of consciousness, referral letter notes, and physician’s age, level of training, and if a resident, year in training were recorded. The patients initial vital signs were also recorded and the results of a POC lactate levels if performed. SI was calculated by the investigators retrospectively from the initial vital signs. Ethical clearance was granted by MUHAS IRB (MUHAS-REC-10-2022-1410) and permission to collect data by MNH training and research unit.

Outcomes

The index tests were SI, POC lactate and physician gestalt, the reference standard was the occurrence of adverse events. These were defined as any of the following: mechanical ventilator support, vasoactive drug use, cardiac arrest, mortality at ED.

Analyses

Sample size estimation was based on sensitivity and specificity of physician gestalt in predicting adverse events among critically ill adult patients using the formula for estimating sensitivity and specificity as described by Buderer, which incorporates disease/event prevalence into the calculation [14,15] A study conducted in two urban academic EDs in the United States found that physician gestalt had a sensitivity of 92 % and a specificity of 72 %, with an observed adverse event rate of 9.9 % (16) Incorporating an additional 10 % for loss to follow up the estimated minimum sample size was 318 participants. The statistical analyses were performed using the statistical package SPSS, version 23. Descriptive statistics for patient demographic and clinical variables, as well as the qualifications of the physician attending the patients, are presented as either median (IQR) or frequency and percentage as appropriate.

Patients were excluded from analysis if they had missing initial vital signs or the physician did not order a POC lactate. We compared baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between patients with and without POC lactate using Mann-Whitney U test to determine if there were statistically significant differences among those included in the study. The discriminatory performance for the index tests were evaluated by comparing area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUROC) using DeLong’s test for correlated ROC curves, with significance set at p < 0.05. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values were calculated to evaluate each test’s ability: for SI, a threshold of ≥ 1 and for POC lactate > 2mmol/L were used based on prior literature. The optimum cut-off for gestalt probability was determined based on Youden’s index.

In a post-hoc analysis we also assessed the test characteristics of combining SI with physician gestalt for predicting adverse events. We analyzed the sensitivity and specificity of the combined index test, defined as a positive result when both SI and physician gestalt were positive for identifying adverse events among critically ill patients. The AUC of the combined index test could not be assessed as these were binary variables.

Results

A total of 528 adult patients were triaged to resuscitation rooms as “emergent” during the study period. After exclusions, 320 patients (61 %) were eligible and included in the analysis (Fig. 1). A total of 59 (18 %) patients had one or more adverse events at the ED: 32 (10 %) required vasoactive drugs due to hypotension, 31 (10 %) were intubated due to airway compromise, 5 (2 %) had cardiac arrest and 28 (8.8 %) died at ED before disposition.

Clinical profile and socio-demographic characteristics of study participants

Of the 320 patients included in the analysis, the median age was 54 years (38,70) with a nearly equal distribution of sexes (Table 1). Hypertension, malignancy, diabetes and chronic kidney disease were the most frequent comorbidities, while difficulty in breathing, altered mental status and vomiting were the commonest reported chief complaints. Compared to those without a POC lactate performed, patients who had lactate measured were significantly older, had higher heart rates and higher physician gestalt probability for risk of adverse events with no statistically significant differences observed for systolic blood pressure and shock index. Predicted adverse events

Using a standard cut off ≥1 for SI and > 2 mmol/L for lactate level, 79 (24.7 %) and 162 (50.6 %) patients were classified as having a high likelihood of developing adverse events respectively. For physician gestalt estimate of the probability of an adverse event, Youden index suggested the optimal cut off of ≥70 %. Using this cut-off, the physicians classified 114 (35.6 %) of patients as having a high probability of developing an adverse event. Physicians primarily relied on chief complaints, vital signs, examination findings, general appearance, and history of the presenting illness to assess the likelihood of adverse events in patients. Of note 11 (3.4 %) reported using SI to guide their gestalt prediction. (See supplemental Table 1)

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the performance of SI, POC lactate level and physician gestalt in predicting adverse events among critically ill adult patients. Gestalt and lactate both had an excellent discriminatory ability, but gestalt may be preferable in LMIC’s where point of care lactate might not be available or, adds extra costs to the patients.

In critically ill patients, early physiological compensation such as tachycardia maintaining blood pressure can mask underlying severity when vital signs are solely interpreted in isolation, SI accounts for this by integrating both heart rate and systolic blood pressure. In our study, SI had an acceptable discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.734, 95 % CI 0.658 to 0.810) with a sensitivity of 59.3 % and a specificity of 83.1 % for predicting adverse events. This finding contrast with a study of sepsis patients in Malaysia where sensitivity was higher 81 %, likely reflecting differences in patients population and timing of SI measurement, as their SI was calculated two hours after resuscitation rather than at presentation [17].

We used a cut-off for SI of ≥ 1 based on a prior systematic review [10] however other studies have found varying optimal cut-offs. A study done in Brazil found that adverse events occurred when SI was elevated >0.7 [18] and another study in the United States found that patients with a sustained elevation in SI >0.8 for at least 80 % of ED vital signs assessment had adverse events [11] The use of SI of ≥1 increased specificity but reduced sensitivity. Although this cut-off may under-detect some at risk patients, in resource limited settings like ours, the higher specificity may help prioritize scarce ICU resources by avoiding unnecessary admissions.

Serum lactate is a well-established marker of tissue hypoperfusion and has been shown to predict adverse events among critically ill adults irrespective of their infection status [4,5] In our study, serum lactate had an excellent discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.819, 95 % CI 0.76 to 0.88), with levels > 2 mmol/L yielding high sensitivity of 89.8 % but low specificity of 58.2 % for adverse events. In contrast a study done in an urban tertiary ED in Tanzania using a higher threshold of ≥ 3.6 mmol/L predicted the need for inotropes/vasopressors with a sensitivity of 80 % and specificity of 80 % and predicted occurrence of cardiac arrest at ED with a sensitivity of 78 % and specificity of 80 % [4] The lower specificity in our study reflects our lower cut-off value, which increases case recognition but risks over triage. Clinically, this trade-off underscores that lactate is most valuable as a screening tool to flag patients at risk of deterioration, but its low specificity means it should be interpreted alongside other clinical and physiological markers to guide critical care resources allocation in resource limited settings.

Physician gestalt demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability in our study (AUC = 0.877, 95 % CI 0.83 to 0.92), correctly identifying adverse events in 88.1 % of patients and ruling out these adverse events in 76.2 %. These results are better than a study in the US where the AUC = 0.78, (95 % CI, 0.74–0.82) [12] Despite its subjective nature, gestalt is a valuable tool in the early risk stratification of critically ill patients in resource-limited emergency settings highlighting the value of clinical experience and pattern recognition. Gestalt may be enhanced by using clinical decision rules, point of care testing or use of SI [11,16]: however our analysis showed that combining gestalt with SI increased specificity to 92 % but lowered sensitivity to 52.5 % as both parameters had to be positive to predict an adverse event. Although this approach risk missing some critically ill patients, the higher specificity could be advantageous in resource limited settings where prioritizing patients for limited ICU beds is critical.

Notably, positive predictive values (PPV) were relatively low (gestalt: 45.6 %, SI: 44.3 %, lactate: 32.7), reflecting the low prevalence of adverse events, 18 % in our study population with negative predictive values (NPV) consistently high across all three tests. This makes these tool particularly valuable in ruling out adverse events, allowing clinicians to identify patients at low risk and allocate scarce resources more effectively.

Our findings indicate that while lactate offers strong sensitivity and SI provides a simple, non-invasive marker, physician gestalt especially when applied with a clear cut-off and possibly combined with SI may serve as a practical and complementary tool within triage systems in resource-limited emergency departments. Both tools have potential for use in Tanzania and other LMICs, though further validation is required. Physician gestalt in particular may benefit from structured training and feedback to improve consistency, yet it remains uncertain how far it can be systematically taught. If validated, applying a defined gestalt cut-off (e.g., ≥70 %) as a triage trigger could support rapid, low-cost decision-making and improve prioritization of critically ill patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Being a single-center study may restrict generalizability, although Muhimbili ED receives patients from across Tanzania and is broadly representative of critically ill populations. Consecutive sampling was applied, but lack of 24/7 coverage could have led to missed patients despite alternating investigator shifts. Exclusion of 206 patients without POC lactate testing may have introduced spectrum bias by disproportionately excluding less sick individuals. In addition, the absence of standardized cutoffs for physician gestalt required us to derive one using Youden’s index, though other approaches may yield different trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity. We also did not assess whether accuracy of gestalt varied by physician experience, and it is possible that less experienced providers may have lower performance. Finally, although physicians were unaware of the study hypothesis, and very few mentioned using their gestalt estimates some may have incorporated SI, which could have influenced results.

Conclusion

Physician gestalt showed excellent discrimination and outperformed SI in identifying patients likely to have an adverse event. POC lactate also had an excellent discriminatory ability but requires additional testing and costs. These data suggest that in our setting, physicians can use their clinical judgement to identify critically ill patients needing prioritization and likely ICU care. Notably all three index tests had a low PPV and a high NPV hence are more useful for ruling out adverse event rather than confirming them. Future studies should explore serial SI and lactate measurements during the ED course to better predict outcomes and compare gestalt accuracy across different levels of clinician experience to determine whether some physicians should not solely rely on gestalt. Further validation in a large, unselected population should be done to confirm the generalizability of these preliminary findings.
