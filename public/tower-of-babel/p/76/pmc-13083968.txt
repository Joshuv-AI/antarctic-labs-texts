Estimated glucose disposal rate and cardiovascular risk in metabolically healthy adults: a nationwide prospective cohort study
Authors: Wenbo Li, Fang Yuan, Cong Gao, Wenting Li, Qirui Guo, Xiaoling Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To investigate the association of estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) with CVD, including stroke and heart disease, in metabolically healthy adults. All data were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, including participants aged ≥ 45 years who were free of metabolic syndrome (MetS) at baseline. Participants were enrolled in 2015 and followed up in 2018 and 2020. The association between eGDR and CVD was evaluated using multivariable Cox regression models and restricted cubic spline (RCS). Among the 4999 participants, the median age was 61 (IQR: 55–68) years, and 2838 (56.77%) were female. During the 5-year follow-up, 885 (17.70%) individuals experienced incident CVD events, comprising 322 (6.44%) cases of stroke and 653 (13.06%) of heart disease. After adjustment for other covariates, higher eGDR levels were independently associated with lower risks of CVD (HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.37–0.61), stroke (HR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.25–0.61), and heart disease (HR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.38–0.68). RCS analysis indicated a significant linear association of eGDR with CVD and its subtypes (all P for nonlinearity > 0.05). Higher levels of eGDR were significantly associated with a decreased risk of CVD and its subtypes in metabolically healthy individuals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41550-y.

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases remain leading causes of death and disability worldwide, contributing substantially to the global burden of disease1,2. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 report, the global prevalence of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) has increased steadily from 271 million in 1990 to 523 million in 2019, accompanied by parallel rises in mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs)3. Similarly, the 2021 Global Burden of Stroke Study estimated a stroke prevalence of 93.8 million (89.0–99.3 million) and 11.9 million incident stroke events (10.7–13.2 million). Although age-standardized rates of incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality have declined over recent decades, the absolute number of new stroke cases continues to increase4. Despite advances in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, the global burden of CVD and stroke remains substantial, highlighting the urgent need for reliable predictive markers and early identification of high-risk individuals5,6.

Insulin resistance (IR) is an insulin-mediated impairment of glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver7. Substantial evidence indicates that IR contributes meaningfully to cardiovascular risk8,9. In a prospective cohort study of 3168 young adults aged 18 to 30 years, persistently high IR trajectories were associated with markedly increased risks of CVD, stroke, and coronary heart disease (HR = 2.62, 95% CI 1.49–4.61; HR = 3.37, 95% CI 1.39–8.20; HR = 3.02, 95% CI 1.34–6.79, respectively)10. Although the euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp is the most accurate method for evaluating IR, its complexity, high cost, and invasiveness limit feasibility in population studies11,12. Consequently, the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), and the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index have gained increasing attention as practical tools for evaluating IR. These simplified indicators reflect IR and have been associated with the risk and prognosis of stroke and CVD13–16. Williams et al. first introduced eGDR, which combines waist circumference (WC), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and hypertension status to estimate IR in individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus (DM)11. Among various indicators of IR, eGDR has emerged as an important and valuable marker. Previous prospective cohort studies have found inverse associations between eGDR and incident CVD among individuals in stages 0–3 of cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, with eGDR outperforming other IR indices17. Consistently, Zhang et al. compared eight IR markers across normoglycemia, prediabetes, and diabetes and found that eGDR showed the strongest predictive performance for stroke and heart disease.18.

Although the association between eGDR and cardiovascular risk has been increasingly recognized, most prior studies have primarily focused on populations with existing metabolic abnormalities rather than those considered metabolically healthy19,20. Metabolic health status is commonly defined by the absence of major cardiometabolic risk factors, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, or DM. This definition, based on routinely used clinical indicators, is practical and widely applied in research21,22. Meanwhile, accumulating evidence indicates that CVD risk is also observed among individuals classified as metabolically healthy22,23.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association of eGDR with incident CVD (stroke or heart disease) among participants aged ≥ 45 years without metabolic syndrome (MetS), using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).

Methods

Study design and population

The CHARLS is a population-based longitudinal cohort study designed to monitor the health, social, and economic conditions of Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years. Baseline data was collected between June 2011 and March 2012 across 150 counties and districts in 28 provinces, enrolling 17,707 participants24. Follow-up assessments have been conducted biennially, leading to five waves of data collection in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 202017. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided informed consent. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

For this study, the 2015 (third) wave was designated as the baseline. A total of 21,111 individuals were initially considered. Individuals were excluded if they met any of the following exclusion criteria: (1) incomplete information data on any variable (N = 12,437); (2) age < 45 years (N = 34); (3) history of cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease (N = 1598); (4) non-fasting blood samples (N = 1044); (5) diagnosis of MetS, according to the criteria described below (N = 999). Finally, the study enrolled 4999 participants (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Data collection and definitions

Baseline clinical data were obtained through standardized questionnaires, physical examinations, and laboratory assessments25. The collected variables were categorized into four major domains: (1) Demographic and lifestyle characteristics: age, gender, residence, marital status, education level, smoking, and drinking status; (2) Physical examination measurements: systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), height, weight, and WC; (3) Medical history: hypertension, dyslipidemia, and DM; (4) Laboratory parameters: including total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fasting blood glucose (FBG), HbA1c, C-reactive protein (CRP), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and uric acid (UA).

Hypertension was defined as a mean SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg based on three consecutive measurements, current use of antihypertensive drugs, or a self-reported physician diagnosis. Normal glucose regulation (NGR) was characterized by both HbA1c < 5.7% and FBG < 100 mg/dL. Prediabetes mellitus (pre-DM) was defined as HbA1c 5.7%-6.4% and FBG 100–125 mg/dL. DM was defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, use of antidiabetic medication, or a self-reported physician diagnosis. Dyslipidemia was identified based on any of the following: TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, HDL-C < 40 mg/dL, use of lipid-lowering drugs, or a self-reported diagnosis by a physician. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2)26.

Assessment of exposure and outcomes

Metabolic health status was defined based on the absence of MetS23,27. MetS was diagnosed by the presence of ≥ 3 of the following: (1) WC ≥ 90 cm for males or ≥ 85 cm for females; (2) TG ≥ 1.70 mmol/L (150 mg/dL); (3) HDL-C<1.0 mmol/L (38.67 mg/dL) for males or<1.3 mmol/L (50.27 mg/dL) for females; (4) FBG>5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) or use of antidiabetic drugs; (5) SBP ≥ 130 mmHg, DBP ≥ 85 mmHg, or use of antihypertensive drugs28,29. The eGDR was calculated as: 21.158 – (0.09*WC)-(3.407*hypertension status[0 = no, 1 = yes]) − (0.551*HbA1c)30.

The primary outcome was incident CVD, comprising stroke and heart disease. The outcomes were identified through standardized questionnaires consistent with those used in previous CHARLS studies. In accordance with established CHARLS protocols, participants who reported heart disease or stroke during the follow-up were classified as having incident CVD. They were asked to answer the following standardized questions: “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor that you had any heart attack, including coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” or “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor that you had a stroke, including ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke?”31,32.

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and all continuous variables were found to be non-normally distributed. Accordingly, continuous variables were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Participants were categorized into eGDR quartiles: quartile (Q)1 < 7.23, Q2 7.23–9.79, Q3 9.80–10.74, and Q4 ≥ 10.75. Intergroup comparisons were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables.

Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the associations of eGDR (per 1-unit increase and by quartiles) with incident CVD, stroke, and heart disease. In quartile analyses, the lowest quartile (Q1) was used as the reference. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 was further adjusted for residence, marital status, education level, drinking and smoking status, SBP, DBP, BMI, dyslipidemia, pre-DM, DM, TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, FBG, CRP, Scr, UA, and BUN. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of eGDR were fitted within Model 3 to flexibly assess nonlinearity, and the median eGDR value was set as the reference (HR = 1).

Additionally, potential effect modification was evaluated by incorporating interaction terms into Model 3, with subgroups defined by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, BMI (< 25, 25 –< 30, ≥ 30 kg/m²), residence and marital status, education level, smoking and drinking status, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and glucose status (including NGR, pre-DM, and DM).

As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the multivariable Cox regression models in the participants who were strictly metabolically healthy, defined as having none of the five MetS components. All two-sided p-values < 0.05 were regarded as statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 4,999 metabolically healthy participants, the median age was 61 (IQR: 55–68) years, and 2,838 (56.8%) were female. The median level of eGDR was 9.80 (IQR: 7.23–10.75). Participants with higher levels of eGDR tended to be younger and have lower BMI, WC, SBP, and DBP. The proportion of females, hypertension, dyslipidemia, pre-DM, and DM also declined with higher eGDR levels. In contrast, the prevalence of rural residence, being married, current smoking or drinking, and NGR increased (all P<0.05).

Regarding laboratory parameters, higher eGDR levels were associated with lower concentrations of TC, TG, LDL-C, FBG, HbA1c, CRP, and UA, whereas HDL-C concentrations were higher (all P < 0.05). However, education level, BUN, and Scr concentrations did not differ significantly across the eGDR quartiles (Table 1).

eGDR levels and CVD

During the five-year follow-up, 885 (17.70%) incident CVD events, 653 (13.06%) incident heart disease, and 322 (6.44%) incident stroke cases were identified. Participants in the lowest eGDR quartile (Q1) exhibited substantially higher incidences of CVD, stroke, and heart disease compared with those in Q4 (Fig. 1).

In Cox proportional hazards models, eGDR showed an inverse association with the risks of CVD, stroke, and heart disease. The association remained significant after multivariable adjustment for potential confounders. Compared with participants in Q1, those in Q4 had significantly lower risks of CVD (HR = 0.47, 95% CI 0.37–0.61), stroke (HR = 0.39, 95% CI 0.25–0.61), and heart disease (HR = 0.51, 95% CI 0.38–0.68) (Table 2). Moreover, results of RCS analyses indicated significant linearity in the relationships of eGDR with CVD and each subtype (all P for nonlinearity > 0.05; Fig. 2).

In the subgroup of strictly metabolically healthy participants, higher eGDR levels remained inversely associated with the risk of CVD and stroke, but not with heart disease (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

In this population-based cohort study, higher eGDR levels were independently and inversely associated with the risk of CVD, stroke, and heart disease in metabolically healthy adults aged ≥ 45 years. These associations remained consistent across multiple subgroups, reinforcing the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, RCS analysis revealed significant linear associations of eGDR with the risks of CVD, stroke, and heart disease.

Our results are consistent with prior studies demonstrating an inverse association between eGDR and CVD across diverse populations19,25,33. A recent UK Biobank analysis involving participants without a history of CVD revealed that lower levels of eGDR and cumulative eGDR were significantly associated with increased risks of CVD (HR = 3.13, 95% CI 2.34–4.20; HR = 3.07, 95% CI 2.14–4.39). Consistent results have also been observed in CHARLS, supporting the robustness of this association31. Furthermore, a cohort study based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and UK Biobank revealed that lower eGDR levels were associated with incident CVD and all-cause mortality among participants with non-diabetic chronic kidney disease34. Han et al. found that lower eGDR levels were correlated with a higher incidence of all-cause mortality and stroke35. Similarly, Gou et al. reported a negative association between eGDR and long-term risks of both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in the general population36.

Although participants in this study were classified as metabolically healthy according to commonly used definitions in prior research and based on routinely available clinical indicators21,22, this classification does not imply completely normal metabolic function, as residual metabolic risk not captured by conventional criteria may still be present. From a mechanistic perspective, IR is widely recognized as a key contributor to the development of CVD.37,38 The eGDR serves as a surrogate indicator of IR, with lower eGDR values reflecting greater IR39. Its involvement in CVD development may occur through multiple mechanistic pathways. First, each of its three components—WC, hypertension, and HbA1c—has been closely associated with IR40–42. However, individual IR markers capture only limited aspects of metabolic risk. As a composite index, the eGDR may better reflect metabolic risk heterogeneity within populations classified as metabolically healthy. Second, given that biological markers may vary among individuals, an integrative metric encompassing multiple metabolic dimensions may more accurately reflect variations in IR. Overall, such composite indicators may improve the identification of populations at elevated cardiovascular risk if validated in future studies26.

In subgroup analysis, the inverse association between eGDR and heart disease was particularly evident in participants who were younger than 65 years. For stroke, the association between eGDR and stroke events varied by education level, suggesting effect modification across socioeconomic groups. These may be attributed to a higher prevalence of IR and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors within these populations41. In addition, these subgroups may exhibit greater sensitivity to changes in IR, which may enhance the value of eGDR for assessing the risk of CVD. These findings reveal the importance of identifying these high-risk populations, which may benefit from early screening and targeted interventions.

This study has a few strengths. It is the first large-scale analysis to evaluate the association between eGDR and CVD among metabolically healthy individuals. Moreover, the consistency of results across multiple subgroups enhances the robustness and reliability of our findings.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered. Firstly, although eGDR is a convenient and noninvasive surrogate of IR, it lacks the precision of the gold-standard euglycemic-hyperinsulinemic clamp technique. Secondly, while standardized protocol and structured questionnaires were employed in CHARLS to ensure data quality, and this study additionally incorporated information from exit interviews to ascertain incident cases of heart disease or stroke among participants lost to follow-up, the identification of CVD outcomes still relied primarily on self- or proxy-reported data, which may be susceptible to potential recall and reporting bias. Thirdly, this study was conducted among middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals. Therefore, the findings may have limited generalizability to younger individuals or other ethnic groups. Future studies in younger and ethnically diverse populations are needed to validate these associations and assess their broader applicability. Finally, the relatively short follow-up period (up to 5 years) may have limited the assessment of long-term cardiovascular outcomes, underscoring the need for future studies with extended follow-up to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

It is the first study to demonstrate an independent inverse association of eGDR with the risks of CVD, stroke, and heart disease among metabolically healthy populations. These findings suggest that eGDR may serve as a potentially useful marker for cardiovascular risk stratification in this population. Nevertheless, future prospective studies should be conducted to explore and validate these associations.
