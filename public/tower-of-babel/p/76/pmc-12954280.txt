Letter to the editor – Regarding “Reconsidering pancreatic autolysis in early postmortem interval estimation: Methodological reflections”
Authors: Preeti Lamba
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

To.

The Editor, Forensic Science International: Synergy.

Subject: Reappraising the Role of Pancreatic Autolysis in Early Postmortem Interval Estimation.

I read with great interest the recent article by Sripirom et al., “Postmortem pancreatic autolysis as a histological marker of early postmortem interval: a forensic autopsy study in a tropical setting” (Forensic Sci Int Synergy. 2026; 12:100659) [1]. The authors are to be commended for their systematic effort to quantify pancreatic autolysis and evaluate its correlation with early postmortem interval (PMI) in a tropical forensic context. Their attempt to translate a traditionally descriptive histological observation into a structured, statistically analyzable parameter represents a valuable contribution to forensic pathology.

However, after careful appraisal, several aspects merit further discussion.

First, while the reported correlation between increasing autolytic changes and elapsed PMI is statistically significant, the inherent variability in postmortem biochemical and enzymatic processes warrants closer examination. Pancreatic tissue is uniquely rich in proteolytic enzymes, and autolysis may be influenced not only by time but also by premortem metabolic states, agonal hypoxia, systemic infection, or pancreatic pathology [2]. Could the authors clarify whether comorbid conditions such as sepsis, diabetic ketoacidosis, or acute pancreatitis were systematically excluded or stratified in their analysis? Without controlling for these variables, the specificity of pancreatic autolysis as a temporal marker may be overestimated.

Second, environmental parameters—particularly ambient temperature, humidity, and body storage conditions—are known to significantly influence early postmortem tissue degradation [3]. While the study was conducted in a tropical setting, a more granular analysis of temperature-adjusted autolytic progression might strengthen interpretative reliability. Would incorporation of controlled temperature-based regression models enhance the predictive accuracy of pancreatic autolysis across different climatic contexts?

Third, histological grading systems inherently introduce interpretive subjectivity. Although the authors propose percentage-based estimation of autolytic involvement, inter-observer variability remains a potential limitation. Standardized digital morphometric analysis or blinded multi-observer validation could improve reproducibility. Future research incorporating artificial intelligence–assisted histopathological quantification may further reduce subjective bias and enhance forensic defensibility.

Finally, while pancreatic autolysis may serve as a useful adjunct in early PMI estimation, it should not be considered in isolation. Contemporary forensic practice increasingly emphasizes multimodal integration—combining histology, vitreous biochemistry, microbial succession profiling, and thermometric modelling [4]. A composite predictive framework may provide greater robustness than reliance on a single tissue marker.

In summary, Sripirom et al. provide an important step toward refining histopathological approaches in early PMI estimation. Nevertheless, broader validation across diverse populations, environmental conditions, and premortem clinical variables is essential before pancreatic autolysis can be incorporated into standardized forensic protocols. Continued interdisciplinary research integrating pathology, biochemistry, and computational modelling will be critical in strengthening the evidentiary foundation of time-since-death estimation.
