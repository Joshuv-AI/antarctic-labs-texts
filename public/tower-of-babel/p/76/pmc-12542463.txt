Effects of blood flow restriction training on patients with chronic ankle instability: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Meng Liu, Sha Pu Lu Bi, Yuhang Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically review the effects of blood flow restriction training (BFRT) on postural control and ankle function in patients with chronic ankle instability (CAI).MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, Embase, The Cochrane Library, Web of Science, EBSCO, and Scopus databases up to February 2, 2025. This study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025642187). The search aimed to collect randomized controlled trials examining the effects of BFRT on patients with CAI. Four outcomes including Y balance test reach distance, Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool scores, ankle muscle strength, and ankle muscle activation were selected. Quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool Version 1, and meta-analysis was performed with RevMan 5.3.ResultsA total of eight randomized controlled trials were included, involving 243 patients with CAI. Compared to the control group, BFRT significantly improved Y balance anterior reach distance (MD = 4.90, 95% CI: 1.73 to 8.08, P = 0.002), Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool scores (MD = 3.09, 95% CI: 1.59 to 4.59, P < 0.001), ankle dorsiflexor strength (SMD = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.03 to 1.77, P = 0.04), ankle plantar-flexor strength (SMD = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.20 to 2.04, P = 0.02), and tibialis anterior muscle activation (MD = 11.13, 95% CI: 4.12 to 18.14, P = 0.002).ConclusionCurrent evidence showed that BFRT significantly improved Y-balance anterior reach distance, CAIT scores, ankle dorsiflexor and plantarflexor strength, and tibialis anterior activation. These findings supported its effectiveness in improving postural control and ankle function in patients with CAI. However, some included studies are of low quality, requiring larger, high-quality studies for validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-09232-4.Keywords: Ankle function, Ankle sprain, Postural control, Self-reported function, Strength training

Introduction

Ankle ligament sprains are common musculoskeletal injuries [1]. Most ankle sprains typically affect the lateral ligaments, especially anterior talofibular ligament and/or calcaneofibular ligaments [2]. These injuries are often caused by rapid, unexpected inversion and internal rotation during foot contact [3]. Unfortunately, only a portion of individuals achieve full recovery without residual symptoms one year after a lateral ankle sprain [4]. Around 40% of individuals experience recurrent injuries, which can progress to chronic ankle instability (CAI) [5]. CAI involves a variety of sensory perceptual, pathological mechanical, and motor behavioral impairments, which develop independently or together after the initial sprain [6].

Decreased ankle muscle strength and neuromuscular control deficits are key factors contributing to “giving way” and recurrent lateral ankle sprains [6, 7]. The peroneus longus primarily everts the ankle, and its increased activation is crucial for controlling ankle inversion and preventing lateral ankle sprains [8]. Similarly, the tibialis anterior plays a crucial role in maintaining ankle stability. Weakness and/or reduced activation of the tibialis anterior may impair stability at the talonavicular joint, leading to increased internal rotation of foot and exposing lateral ankle structures to greater stress. This elevates the risk of lateral ankle sprain [8]. The tibialis anterior, as the primary dorsiflexor of the ankle, may not absorb joint loads adequately if its activation is insufficient. This ultimately expose the ligamentous structures to excessive forces. Therefore, how to effectively improving the muscle strength of patients with CAI to mitigate the risk of re-injury has emerged as an exceptionally challenging issue in sports medicine [9].

Studies indicated that high-load training can increase muscle strength and mass. American College of Sports Medicine recommended that resistance loads should reach 60–70% and 70–85% of one-repetition maximum, respectively, when training aimed at enhancing muscle strength and hypertrophy [10]. However, such high-load training may cause tissue damage and induce pain in patients with CAI, as the ankle joint may not tolerate the excessive mechanical stress during training [11].

Blood flow restriction training (BFRT) is the application of placing restrictive bands or a blood pressure cuff on proximal region of the limb and inflating them to limit venous return while allowing partial arterial blood flow during exercise [12, 13]. This creates an intramuscular hypoxic environment which is believed to induce vascular endothelial growth and high levels of metabolic stress [14], enhancing the recruitment of growth factors, satellite cells, transcription factors, reactive oxygen species, and type II muscle fibers [10, 14, 15], thereby promoting muscle hypertrophy and strength [16].

BFRT combined with low-load resistance training is considered to yield effects similar to high-load resistance training [17]. Previous studies have confirmed that BFRT enhances muscle hypertrophy and strength in both healthy individuals and those with musculoskeletal disorders [18–20]. Due to the reduction in joint stress, it also increases training tolerance and adherence [12]. Additionally, lower levels of pain and perceived fatigue have been observed during and after BFRT [12].

The aforementioned studies indicated that BFRT may be a safe and effective treatment for patients with CAI. However, there are still conflicting results in existing studies regarding the clinical efficacy of BFRT for patients with CAI, and a quantitative summary of its efficacy through meta-analysis is still lacking. Therefore, this study employed a meta-analysis to systematically assess the effects of BFRT on postural control and ankle function in patients with CAI, providing evidence-based support for its clinical application.

Methods

This meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42025642187). Scopus and Y balance test reach distance were added after registration to enhance review comprehensiveness, corresponding to protocol version 2.0.

Search strategy

PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, EBSCO, and Scopus databases were systematically searched from inception to February 2, 2025, with a language restriction to English. The search terms used included “Blood flow restriction training”, “Blood flow restriction”, “Occlusion training”, “Kaatsu”, “Therapeutic Occlusion”, “BFRT”, “Chronic ankle instability”, “CAI”, “Ankle instability”, “Functional ankle instability”, and “FAI”. This study also traced the references cited in the included studies to gather more relevant information. The search was conducted independently by two researchers following the search strategy.

Study selection

We included studies that met all of the following criteria: (1) the study type is randomized controlled trial; (2) the participants are patients with CAI; (3) the control (Con) group received functional training, while the BFRT group underwent functional training supplemented with BFRT and (4) including at least one of the following outcomes: Y balance test (YBT) reach distance, Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) scores, ankle dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion, and eversion muscle strength (measured by isokinetic or dynamometry), as well as tibialis anterior and fibularis longus muscle activation. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) duplicate publications; (2) absence of included outcomes; (3) non-randomized controlled trials; (4) reviews, conference abstracts, or meta-analyses; and (5) lacking of necessary data for the meta-analysis and failure to obtain data from the authors. Two researchers independently screened the studies, and any disagreements were resolved by consulting the third researcher.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool Version 1 for randomized trials. This tool evaluated the following seven domains: (1) random sequence generation; (2) allocation concealment; (3) blinding of participants and personnel; (4) blinding of outcome assessment; (5) incomplete outcome data; (6) selective reporting and (7) other bias. Each domain was rated as “low risk”,“high risk”, or “unclear”. Meanwhile, the certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology[21]. Two researchers independently assessed the studies, and any disagreements were resolved by consulting the third researcher.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently screened the literature and extracted data in a double-blind manner. In the literature screening process, an initial screening was conducted based on titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review to further assess eligibility according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. In case of disagreement, a third researcher was consulted for adjudication. Data extracted from the included studies encompassed first author’s name and publication year, sample size, age, intervention time, intervention methods, BFRT application parameters (pressure value/cuff width), and outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted using RevMan 5.3 software. For continuous outcome variables, mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were used if measurement methods and units were consistent; otherwise, standardized mean difference (SMD) and 95% CI were applied. Heterogeneity between studies was determined using the χ² test (α = 0.1). A P ≥ 0.1 and I² < 50% indicated low heterogeneity, and a fixed-effect model was applied. Conversely, P < 0.1 and I² ≥ 50% suggested high heterogeneity, and a random-effects model was used. Additionally, sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of each study on the overall combined estimate.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A preliminary search identified 169 articles. After removing 69 duplicates with EndNote X9.1, 100 articles remained. Further removal of 45 repeated articles left 55. Titles and abstracts were reviewed, excluding 32 irrelevant articles, leaving 23. After full-text review, 15 articles were excluded, and eight randomized controlled trials were included [22–29] (Fig. 1).

Literature screening flow chart

A total of 228 patients with CAI were enrolled in the eight included randomized controlled trials, comprising the BFRT group (n = 117) and Con group (n = 111). There were 104 males and 124 females, with participant ages ranging from 17 to 29 years in the BFRT group and 16–29 years in the Con group. Interventions lasted 4–6 weeks, 2–3 sessions weekly. The BFRT group received BFRT combined with RRT, while the Con group received RRT alone. Cuff pressure was measured via arterial occlusion pressure in seven studies and subjective pressure in one, with cuff widths ranging from 5 to 15 cm. Five studies [22, 23, 27–29] assessed the YBT reach distance, three studies [22–24] reported CAIT scores, five studies [24–28] measured ankle muscle strength, and three studies [22, 23, 29] examined ankle muscle activation (Table 1).

Risk of bias

Among the eight studies [22–29] included, seven studies [22–28] reported the specific methods for random sequence generation, while one study [29] only mentioned randomization. In terms of allocation concealment, five studies [22, 24–27] implemented allocation concealment. Regarding blinding, two studies [24, 27] blinded both the participants and the assessors, and four studies [23, 25, 26, 28] blinded only the participants. All eight studies [22–29] reported complete outcome data with three [22, 26, 27] having no selective reporting. None of the eight studies [22–29] mentioned specific other potential biases (Figs. 2 and 3).

Certainty of evidence for the outcomes

Y balance test distance

Five studies [22, 23, 27–29] reported the results of YBT test distance, with low heterogeneity between studies (I² = 20%, P = 0.23), and a fixed-effect model was used for analysis. Due to the consistency in measurement methods, MD was used to merge the results. The results indicated that BFRT significantly improved the Y balance test distance in patients with CAI (MD = 2.11, 95% CI: 0.64 to 3.59, P = 0.005) compared to Con group (Fig. 4). Subgroup analysis based on YBT directions revealed that BFRT significantly improved anterior reach distance (MD = 4.90, 95% CI: 1.73 to 8.08, P = 0.002). However, there were no significant effects on posteromedial reach distance (MD = 1.04, 95% CI: −2.94 to 5.03, P = 0.61), posterolateral reach distance (MD = 2.35, 95% CI: −1.00 to 5.70, P = 0.17), and composite scores (MD = 1.00, 95% CI: −1.20 to 3.20, P = 0.37) (Fig. 4).

CAIT scores

Three studies [22–24] reported the results of CAIT scores, with low heterogeneity between studies (I² = 0%, P = 0.39), and a fixed-effect model was utilized for analysis. Given the consistency in measurement methods, MD was employed to merge the results. The results indicated that BFRT significantly improved the CAIT score in patients with CAI compared to Con group (MD = 3.09, 95% CI: 1.59 to 4.59, P < 0.001) (Fig. 5).

Effect of BFRT on CAIT score in patients with CAI. Notes: BFRT, blood flow restriction training; CAI, chronic ankle instability

Ankle muscle strength

Five studies [24–28] reported the results of ankle muscle strength, with high heterogeneity between studies (I² = 81%, P < 0.001). As heterogeneity could not be reduced by the leave-one-out method, a random-effects model was applied for analysis. Due to varying measurement methods, SMD was used to merge the results. Results showed that BFRT significantly improved ankle muscle strength in patients with CAI compared to Con group (SMD = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.48 to 1.34, P < 0.001) (Fig. 6). Subgroup analysis based on tested muscles revealed significant improvements in dorsiflexor (SMD = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.03 to 1.77, P = 0.04) and plantar-flexor strength (SMD = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.20 to 2.04, P = 0.02) with BFRT. No significant effects were found for inversion (SMD = 0.58, 95% CI: −0.13 to 1.28, P = 0.11) or eversion strength (SMD = 1.11, 95% CI: −0.19 to 2.40, P = 0.09) (Fig. 6).

Effect of BFRT on ankle muscle strength in patients with CAI. Notes: BFRT, blood flow restriction training; CAI, chronic ankle instability

Ankle muscle activation

Three studies [22, 23, 29] reported the results of ankle muscle activation, with high heterogeneity between studies (I² = 69%, P = 0.006). As heterogeneity could not be reduced by the leave-one-out method, a random-effects model was used for analysis. Due to the consistency in measurement methods, MD was employed to merge the results. The results indicated that BFRT significantly improved ankle muscle activation in patients with CAI compared to Con group (MD = 8.32, 95% CI: 2.57–14.07, P = 0.005) (Fig. 7). Subgroup analysis based on tested muscles revealed that BFRT significantly improved tibialis anterior activation (MD = 11.13, 95% CI: 4.12–18.14, P = 0.002), while no significant effect was observed on fibularis longus activation (MD = 5.39, 95% CI: −3.33–14.12, P = 0.23) (Fig. 7).

Results of sensitivity analysis

To further investigate the sources of heterogeneity, sensitivity analysis was conducted for four outcome measures of CAIT scores, Y balance test distance, ankle muscle strength, and ankle muscle activation. Sequential removal of individual studies did not alter the overall effect size, suggesting the stability of meta-analysis results.

Discussion

This study included eight randomized controlled trials aimed at systematically assessing the effects of BFRT on postural control and ankle function in patients with CAI. The results demonstrated that BFRT significantly enhanced Y balance anterior reach distance, CAIT scores, ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion strength, and tibialis anterior muscle activation in patients with CAI.

Evidence summary

YBT is widely used to assess dynamic postural control and effectively detects functional deficits in patients with CAI [30]. This study found that BFRT significantly improved the Y balance anterior reach distance in patients with CAI, suggesting that BFRT has greater benefits in improving dynamic postural control ability. Liu et al. [23] demonstrated that BFRT effectively improves the perception of joint active position, which contributes to enhance joint stability and balance test performance. Some researchers suggested that BFRT improves balance test outcomes by increasing the activation of both proximal and distal muscles during movement. Recent studies indicated that BFRT not only increases the activation of distal muscles but also enhances the activation level of proximal muscles [31]. Theoretically, distal occlusion (calf muscles) may cause fatigue in the muscles below the cuff, along with the recruitment of synergistic proximal muscles (thigh and hip muscles) above the occlusion site, thereby contributing to pelvic stability and dynamic balance [32]. Notably, Burkhardt et al. [33] observed that the application of BFRT during dynamic balance tasks not only reduced the YBT distance in patients with CAI but also increased perceptions of postural instability and fatigue. These findings may be attributed to blood flow restriction being used as a testing condition rather than an intervention. The balance tests were performed under both blood flow restriction and non-restriction conditions. It is conceivable that blood flow restriction during testing introduces additional constraints on the motor system due to limb and arterial blood flow occlusion, thus decreasing sports performance. Subgroup analysis found improvement only in anterior reach, which may be explained by enhanced dorsiflexor strength and increased tibialis anterior activation. Ankle dorsiflexion range has been shown to correlate with anterior reach distance [34].

CAIT scores assess ankle stability and function during functional activities and can be used to screen individuals with CAI. Higher scores indicate better ankle function. International Ankle Consortium recommended a CAIT score of less than 24 to diagnose CAI in the Chinese population [35]. The results of this study indicated that BFRT significantly outperforms Con group in improving CAIT scores in patients with CAI. This provided clinical evidence supporting the positive impact of BFRT on the rehabilitation of ankle function in CAI patients.

The results of this study showed the positive effects of BFRT on dorsiflexion and plantarflexion muscle strength in patients with CAI. This finding aligns with a recent study examining the impact of BFRT in patients following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, which demonstrated that BFRT significantly surpasses Con group in enhancing lower extremity muscle strength [36]. Previous studies have confirmed that combining BFRT with low-load resistance training produces similar muscle functional adaptations to those from high-load resistance training [37, 38]. Otha et al. [39] found that BFRT with low-load resistance training improved muscle strength more than low-load resistance training alone. A study comparing BFRT and high-load resistance training for musculoskeletal pain showed that 25% of high-load resistance training participants withdrew due to exercise-related pain, while no adverse events were reported with BFRT [40]. Overall, these studies suggested that BFRT may help increase muscle strength. Additionally, its lower pain levels may promote better patient adherence compared to high-load resistance training. The precise mechanism by which BFRT induces muscle strength improvements remains unclear. However, most researchers currently believe that BFRT reduces oxygen delivery and the clearance of metabolic byproducts in muscle tissue. This reduction in blood flow creates a hypoxic intramuscular environment, which increases muscle activation via type III and IV afferent nerve fibers [41]. Furthermore, the cortical excitability of the primary control sites in occlusion zones of lower extremities may spill over to proximal control sites, leading to the recruitment of a larger muscle. The increase in the amplitude of corticospinal motor-evoked potentials during BFRT may influence the motor output in the distal blood flow occlusion zones [42]. The accumulation of metabolic products induced by BFRT could result in high levels of metabolic stress, thereby stimulating anabolic signaling and inflammatory responses [43]. This subsequently leads to enhanced muscle adaptation [43].

The tibialis anterior plays a critical role in the dynamic stability of the ankle joint. Its inhibition may compromise talus and navicular stability, reducing dynamic restraint in patients with CAI [44]. In this study, tibialis anterior activation in patients with CAI after BFRT was significantly higher than in Con group. This finding aligns with several studies showing that BFRT enhances muscle activation compared to functional training [45]. Killinger et al. [46] found that combining resistance eversion and dorsiflexion exercises with BFRT significantly increased tibialis anterior activation in patients with CAI. Increased activation of tibialis anterior aids in the restoration of normal posture control [47]. In conclusion, we believe that BFRT positively impacts ankle muscle activation and posture control in CAI patients.

Clinical summary

Although this study demonstrated the positive effects of BFRT on CAI, several factors should be considered for its clinical application:

Cuff width and pressure: studies on BFRT parameters proposed that cuff size affects the metabolic response provided by BFRT [48, 49]. A recent study found that wider cuffs (13/12 cm) may impede muscle growth, causing discomfort during exercise and lower safety, indicating that narrower cuffs can yield better training effects [48]. A review of 38 retrospective studies showed that BFRT with wider cuffs results in greater discomfort compared to narrower cuffs, leading to lower adherence to rehabilitation programs [50]. The included studies lacked descriptions of cuff size and adverse reactions, making it difficult to determine the role of cuff size in the rehabilitation of this population. However, previous evidence suggested that narrower cuffs are more beneficial and safer. Some researchers [51] have noted that neglecting cuff size in studies may not only compromise the intervention’s efficacy but also pose risks to patient safety and health. Furthermore, other researchers have emphasized the necessity of applying personalized pressure prescriptions to ensure adequate blood flow restriction, taking into account limb circumference for optimize performance during training [52]. For limbs with larger circumferences, higher pressures are required to achieve the same level of occlusion as those with smaller limbs [52].

Intervention duration: another important factor to consider is training duration. A study investigated the effects of 12 weeks of BFRT, high-load, and low-load resistance training on musculoskeletal pain patients, and found that BFRT and high-load resistance training had similar effects on muscle strength, both outperforming low-load resistance training [40]. In contrast, another included trial showed that 4 weeks of BFRT did not result in additional benefits compared to low-load resistance training [53]. One possible explanation is that BFRT is as effective as high-load resistance training, but a longer duration (more than 4 weeks) is needed to surpass low-load resistance training.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, varying CAI diagnostic criteria across studies may increase the heterogeneity of the results. Second, this study did not fully account for BFRT parameters, as different parameters may lead to varying treatment effects. However, subgroup analysis was not conducted due to the limited number of studies. Third, some studies had low quality and carried a certain risk of bias. Lastly, the small number of studies for certain outcomes may reduce evidence reliability.

Conclusions

Current evidence showed that BFRT significantly improved Y-balance anterior reach distance, CAIT scores, ankle dorsiflexor and plantarflexor strength, and tibialis anterior activation. These findings supported its effectiveness in improving postural control and ankle function in patients with CAI. However, some included studies are of low quality, requiring larger, high-quality studies for validation.
