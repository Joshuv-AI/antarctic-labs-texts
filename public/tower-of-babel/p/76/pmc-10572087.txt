Rapid progression of an outflow graft obstruction with a left ventricular assist device: images in cardiology
Authors: Satoru Takaesu, Tasuku Hada, Satsuki Fukushima, Yasumasa Tsukamoto, Diego Araiza-Garaygordobil, Michelle Feijen, Edin Begic
Published: 2023

Case description

Outflow graft obstruction (OGO) is a known long-term complication of left ventricular assist devices (LVADs).1 However, the speed of progression and triggers of OGO remain unclear. We report a case of a patient with an LVAD that rapidly developed an OGO.

A 38-year-old man with a HeartMate II (Abbott) implant for advanced heart failure caused by dilated cardiomyopathy for 4 years presented to our outpatient department with a 3-month history of frequent dizziness and headaches. Due to the worsening of his symptoms over the course of several months, he was admitted to our institution. Laboratory examination revealed no elevation of lactate dehydrogenase, suggesting the absence of haemolysis. The patient’s medications included aspirin (100 mg/day) and warfarin as antithrombotic agents. His prothrombin time-international normalized ratio was well controlled (range, 2.0–3.0) and an electrocardiogram showed no arrhythmia. Parameter analysis of his LVAD revealed an initial gradual decline in the pulsatility index within the last several months, followed by a recent abrupt decline in pump power and pump flow (Figure 1A).

Six months before admission, he was asymptomatic, and OGO was not observed on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CCT) (Figure 1B and C). However, CCT on admission revealed a rapidly progressive OGO, with minimal internal lumen due to twisting and extraluminal compression (Figure 1D and E). Considering the risk of sudden occlusion and stroke, an urgent pump replacement was performed. Intraoperative surgical findings revealed that a gelatinous fibrin mass, between the outflow graft and the bend relief, caused extraluminal compression. Pathological findings showed a seroma with predominantly fibrous tissue with fibrin thrombi (Figure 1F and G). After removing the mass and replacing the LVAD with a HeartMate 3, his symptoms improved.

Perigraft seroma is speculated to form when plasma components leaking from the polyester vascular outflow graft accumulate between the outflow graft and the polytetrafluoroethylene bend relief.1–3 This case highlights that even if no stenosis was observed in the initial CCT scan, symptomatic LVAD parameter abnormalities and suspicion of pump malfunction should prompt further investigation and additional CCT scans in such cases.
