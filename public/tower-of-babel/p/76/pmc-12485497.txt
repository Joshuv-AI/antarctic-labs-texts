Revascularization in Ischemic Left Ventricular Dysfunction: From Risk to Reality
Authors: Bjorn Redfors, Sandeep Jha, Elmir Omerovic
Published: 2025

Ischemic left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction in the setting of multivessel coronary artery disease presents a frequent and complex decision point in cardiovascular practice: Should the patient undergo coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)? This decision is often guided by a combination of anatomical and clinical risk factors, with tools like the EuroSCORE intended to capture surgical risk. Yet, despite its centrality to care, the optimal revascularization strategy for these patients remains uncertain.1

Two landmark trials have shaped much of the current thinking. The STICH trial (2002-2007) demonstrated a survival benefit with CABG compared with medical therapy in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy over long-term follow-up, establishing CABG as a cornerstone in eligible patients.2,3 Conversely, the REVIVED-BCIS2 trial (2013-2020) found no survival or LV function benefit from PCI over medical therapy in a seemingly similar population, albeit one selected for PCI candidacy.4 These divergent results highlight the need to reassess not only the procedures themselves but also the frameworks we use for decision making.

In this issue of JSCAI, Marquis-Gravel et al5 demonstrate in a pooled, post hoc analysis of STICH and REVIVED studies that surgical risk as assessed by EuroSCORE II did not modify the treatment effect in either trial.5 Thus, while there were substantial differences in the estimated surgical risk between patients in STICH and REVIVED trials, this does not appear to explain the lack of benefit from PCI in REVIVED. Whether other differences in the trial populations (STICH enrolled patients eligible for CABG, while REVIVED included those deemed PCI appropriate, often because CABG was declined or contraindicated) explain the lack of benefit of revascularization by PCI in REVIVED remains unknown.

The results provided by Marquis-Gravel et al5 matter. In the real-world, patients with ischemic LV dysfunction and multivessel disease often fall into a gray zone—technically eligible for both procedures, yet with no clear evidence to guide which strategy offers superior outcomes. Despite stronger evidence supporting CABG, there has been a steady increase in the use of PCI for these patients,6 often due to perceived lower procedural risk, faster recovery, or patient preference. This real-world trend underscores the disconnection between evidence and practice and further highlights the urgent need for definitive comparative data. The analysis by Marquis-Gravel et al,5 which pooled data from the only 2 randomized trials to date in this space, further underscores the depth of this clinical uncertainty. Despite surgical risk being a central determinant in real-world decision making, their findings revealed that risk—as quantified by EuroSCORE II—did not significantly influence outcomes in either STICH or REVIVED. This challenges one of the key clinical assumptions used to differentiate between PCI and CABG candidates and reinforces the critical need for a prospective, head-to-head trial in patients who are candidates for both strategies.

It is precisely this gap that the STICH 3 trial (NCT05761067) seeks to address. By directly comparing CABG and PCI in patients who are anatomically and clinically suitable for either, STICH 3 targets the heart of the revascularization dilemma.

The STICH 3 is a prospective, randomized, multicenter trial enrolling patients with ischemic LV dysfunction and multivessel coronary artery disease who are candidates for both CABG and PCI. The trial is being conducted across Canada, Denmark, France, the Netherlands, Sweden, and the United Kingdom as individual randomized controlled trails, using a harmonized trial design that enhances generalizability and targets a sufficiently large total sample size to evaluate the impact of revascularization modality on prognosis. It also aims to provide robust evidence regarding the differential effects across key anatomical and patient characteristics, thereby addressing the practical questions that clinicians face daily. The trial integrates contemporary guideline-directed medical therapy and standardized definitions of end points including all-cause mortality, heart failure hospitalization, and quality of life.

Until the results of STICH 3 are available, the choice between CABG and PCI for patients with reduced LV ejection fraction relies particularly heavily on navigating complex interdisciplinary dynamics that can influence patient care, with the risk that differing procedural perceptions and institutional cultures between cardiothoracic surgeons and interventional cardiologists concerning revascularization choices results in a “turf war.” Surgeons may emphasize the durability and completeness of revascularization achievable with CABG, while interventional cardiologists may highlight the reduced procedural risk and faster recovery with PCI. These professional perspectives each have valid elements but can create barriers to optimal patient care when they overshadow evidence-based decision making. The finding by Marquis-Gravel et al5 that surgical risk stratification does not modify treatment effects removes a potential source of interdisciplinary disagreement and redirects focus on the fundamental question of comparative effectiveness.

Beyond acquiring more data, optimal revascularization also demands clear communication that counters cognitive biases and fosters mutual respect, openly acknowledging the expertise and concerns of both surgical and interventional colleagues. Until the results of STICH 3 are available, clinicians must continue to weigh complex anatomical and clinical factors in their decision making with little direct supporting evidence. For patients eligible for both procedures, we lack randomized evidence directly comparing CABG and PCI. In this context, the analysis by Marquis-Gravel et al5 is both sobering and humbling. It challenges long-standing assumptions and reminds us of the limits of our current evidence base. The heart team must, therefore, make decisions based on their best assessment of clinical equipoise, balancing anatomical complexity, patient preferences, procedural risks, and comorbidities. All the while, it is essential to acknowledge that current data support CABG as the only revascularization strategy to demonstrate a long-term survival benefit in this population. Hopefully, STICH 3 will provide the critical evidence needed to resolve this dilemma and is well-positioned to inform future guidelines and everyday clinical practice.

In conclusion, the choice between CABG and PCI in patients with ischemic LV dysfunction and multivessel disease is consequential to a large number of patients. Marquis-Gravel et al5 nicely illustrate how STICH and REVIVED trials offered not only critical insights but also left key questions unanswered. STICH 3 represents the necessary next step—a direct comparison between CABG and PCI to inform evidence-based, individualized care for patients facing this difficult decision.
