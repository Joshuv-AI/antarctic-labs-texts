Comparative evaluation of physical methods for extracting leaf trichomes
Authors: Satinderpal Kaur, Devi Balakrishnan, Rupesh Kariyat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Trichomes are hairlike protuberances in plants that deter herbivores using both physical and chemical means, with pre- and post-ingestive effects. In addition, trichomes also serve as a protective layer, reducing transpiration loss, thereby improving abiotic stress tolerance. A common method for assessing the role of trichomes in defense is to extract and infuse trichomes into the artificial diet, and then examine the feeding, growth, and developmental parameters of herbivores. Although methods such as brushing, using dry ice, and shaving with a razor, have been commonly used, there is no consensus on the best and most efficient method for trichome extraction. In the current study, to optimize the ideal method to extract trichomes, we tested three common methods with some modifications, such as flash freezing and brushing, shaving using a scalpel, and vortexing followed by freezing. Here, we used representative species from both eudicots and monocots with significant trichome diversity. For eudicots, we selected three cultivated plant species: tomato, sunflower, and Mexican squash, and two wild plant species: wild sunflower and wild squash, and for monocots, we used the model plant, rice. The efficiency of each method was examined using a combination of light and scanning electron microscopy. The results suggest that freezing followed by brushing is the most efficient and ideal method for extracting trichomes and can be recommended for eudicots. However, none of the evaluated methods were effective for the monocot rice.

Introduction

Trichomes are fine epidermal hairs in plants that have been well known to act as the first line of defense [56] against both biotic [5, 25] and abiotic stressors [6, 13, 16]. Broadly, trichomes are of two types, glandular and non-glandular, based on the presence or absence of a glandular head [56], and can be further categorized based on their morphological characteristics [9, 26, 36, 53], with pre-and post-ingestive effects against herbivores [20, 21, 25, 48]. Among these, non-glandular trichomes act as a physical barrier, which can impede the movement, feeding, and oviposition of the herbivores, making them more vulnerable to predation [18–21, 23, 24, 35, 51, 52]. In addition, when ingested, they can rupture the peritrophic matrix [21, 24], leading to the mixing of hemolymph with gut contents. Similarly, glandular trichomes can also entrap the neonate caterpillars in their sticky exudates [45] along with mediating other chemical defenses [37]. Furthermore, previous studies have also shown the enhanced induction of trichomes on new leaves in response to herbivory, suggesting the counter-defensive role of trichomes against herbivores [18]. Hence, notably, trichomes play an integral role against herbivores that have been examined across various plant and insect species [31].

Most studies on plant trichome-herbivore interactions have focused on determining the effect of trichomes on the feeding, growth, and development of herbivores [11, 12, 19–22]. A commonly used method in laboratory conditions includes separating the trichomes from the leaf surface, followed by adding them to the artificial diet, with various extraction methods [33, 55][20, 21, 52]. Some of the common methods are brushing [14, 46, 50], shaving by scalpel [3, 20, 21, 52], using a vortex [17, 20, 34, 58], with powdered dry ice, using fixatives such as formaldehyde and glutaraldehyde [59], immersing the leaves in organic solvent followed by agitation [34], wiping the surface with cotton gauze saturated with a solvent [34, 38], shaking the leaves vigorously with liquid nitrogen and glass beads [37], and using adhesive carton sealing tape [43]. Besides having all these extraction methods, we lack a consensus on the best and easiest method that can extract the maximum amount of trichomes in a timely fashion. This is mainly due to the lack of any comparative studies that have tested methods using different plant species.

To address this, we selected three commonly used methods to remove trichomes with slight modifications: (a) freezing and brushing the trichomes with a common paint brush, (b) freezing and shaving the trichomes by using a surgeon-style scalpel, and (c) agitating the frozen leaf by using a vortex mixer. We tested these methods of trichome extractions from different plant species to determine the most efficient method, which can save time as well as resources. Finding such an idealmethod can help us in extracting an adequate amount of trichomes, which can increase the efficiency of the experiments investigating insect-plant defense interactions.

Materials and methods

Plant species

We used five eudicot species, out of which, three were field crops, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum); Mexican squash (Cucurbita pepo), sunflower (Helianthus annum) and two were wild plant species, wild squash (Cucurbita pepo); wild sunflower (Helianthus spp.) (Fig. 1) and one monocot species, rice, Oryza sativa L. Eudicot plant species were chosen based on the highest leaf trichome density as well as the presence of wide diversity of trichomes (Table 1; Fig. 1), whereas rice was chosen as the monocot model plant based on its unique characteristics and a few studies on trichomes [4, 27].

The seeds of each plant species were procured from different places: tomato variety, Sungold from Johnny’s selected seeds (Johnnyseeds.com); mexican squash, wild and cultivated sunflower from a farmer in Rio Grande valley; wild squash seeds from plants grown at the Rock Springs Agricultural Experiment Station at The Pennsylvania State University in Centre County, PA; rice variety, Diamond, from the University of Arkansas Foundation Seed Program. All plants were grown in controlled conditions in a greenhouse at 25–27 C temperature, and 16:8 day night cycle.

Methods of trichome extraction

All the methods that we tested include physical removal and isolation of frozen trichomes from the plant tissue. We used different types of objects to produce force at the point of connection of trichome with the tissue. We tested the following methods of trichome extraction:

Brushing: The collected leaves were immediately stored in -80 °C for 24 h and then the trichomes were carefully brushed off the leaf surface using a common paint brush (Size 4, Walmart Inc). The extracted trichomes can be added to a beaker with de-ionized water by rinsing the brush. It is extremely important to change the brush for each treatment as the trichomes tend to get tangled in the bristles of the brush and can contaminate the sample. It is recommended to remove any plant material from the de-ionized water by using a clean brush under a dissecting microscope (Figs. 2 and 3A).Shaving: The leaves were collected and stored in -80 °C for 24 h. A surgeon scalpel (No. 22, VWR®, Stainless Steel Scalpel) was used to shave the trichomes from leaf surface. It is extremely important to not to let the plant leaf thaw while shaving. Since the plant tissue after thawing becomes very soft, making the tissue (epidermis) immensely prone to shave off along with the trichomes itself. The extracted trichomes on the scalpel can be added into a beaker of de-ionized water and can also be stored in refrigerator for further use. As mentioned earlier, the scalpel may remove some of the plant tissue also, therefore, any visible leaf material should be carefully removed using a sterilized brush. Use of a dissecting microscope is recommended to remove the plant material more effectively (Figs. 2 and 3B).

3. Vortexing: The vortex method used in this study is a modified version of method demonstrated by Yerger et al.,1992, in which they used dry ice in the tube and then vortexed it for 1 min. This method is known to preserve the integrity of glandular trichomes. The collected leaves were stored in -80 °C in 15 ml of falcon tubes. The tubes were removed from the freezer and vortexed for 30–35 s by using Benchmark scientific Vornado Miniature Vortex Mixer. This is also a physical method where the force tends to break the point of connection of plant tissue and the trichome and the trichome fall off the leaves and get stuck to the wall of the falcon tube. The tube can then be rinsed with 5 ml of de-ionized water. This water is then collected in a beaker and any plant material if present, can be removed using a dissecting microscope (Figs. 2 and 3C).

Experimental design

For determining the trichome number on leaves, before and after trichome removal, stereomicroscope, Visual Dynamix Z80 was used for eudicots and AmScope LED-64 S was used for rice. For trichome characterization of all species and for visual representation of trichome extraction methods, we used scanning electron microscopy, including Desktop SEM (DSEM; SNE- 4500 Plus Tabletop Nanoimages LL, Pleasanton, CA, USA), Zeiss EVO LS 10 SEM and SNE- ALPHA Tabletop SEM (Fig. 5, S1 and S2). Due to technical limitations, SEM images of trichome extraction methods across all tested species couldn’t be obtained.

Samples for SEM were prepared by excising circular leaf discs from the samples using a hole punch. The discs were mounted on the aluminium stubs using a double-sided carbon tape and were placed inside the SEM for scanning and image processing [52]. Further details on DSEM working procedures and methodology, see [54] . For determining trichome density, the images were captured at 100X magnification. Each method was tested on all the six plant species by taking 10 leaf samples from 10 different plants of each species. After removing the trichomes by using all the methods, the trichome density of leaves was again determined to compare the efficiency of these methods to remove trichomes. The trichome removal efficiency of total number of trichomes, glandular trichomes and non-glandular trichomes were calculated.

Statistical analysis

To determine if the extraction method, the plant species and their interactions affect the trichome removal efficiency, we ran a two-way Anova with total trichomes, glandular trichomes and non-glandular trichomes removal efficiency as the response variables. The data was log transformed to meet Anova assumptions. All the Anova tests were followed by Tukey’s post hoc tests to do pairwise comparisons. All analyses were carried out in JMP Pro 16 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC) and the graphs were made using the tool, GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, version 9.0.0 for Mac, CA, USA) (Fig. 4).

Results

Total trichome removal efficiency

We found that in eudicots, brushing method extracts more trichomes significantly, followed by shaving and vortex (Two- way Anova, F = 15.11, P < 0.0001) (Table S1, Fig. 5A). Moreover, the plant species also vary in the removal efficiency of total trichomes with the highest efficiency in sunflower (F = 18.75, P < 0.0001) (Table S1, Fig. 5B). On the other hand, the interactions of plant species and method does not affect the efficiency (F = 1.75, P = 0.098) (Table S1, Fig. 5C). However, in monocot, we fail to extract trichomes with any of the tested methods due to the loss of brittleness and due to the low trichome density in rice.

Glandular trichome removal efficiency

For the glandular trichomes, we found that brushing method extracted significantly higher numbers as compared to vortexing and shaving in eudicots (Two- way Anova, F = 13.42, P < 0.0001) (Table S2, Fig. 6A). Moreover, the plants species (Two- way Anova, F = 11.44, P < 0.0001) (Table S2, Fig. 6B) as well as the interaction of plant species and method (Two- way Anova, F = 2.23, P = 0.03 (Table S2, Fig. 6C) affects the efficiency significantly. However, in rice, we fail to carry out the test due to the absence of glandular trichomes in the rice variety used for the study.

Non-glandular trichome removal efficiency

We found that the brushing and shaving methods extracted significantly higher number of non-glandular trichomes in eudicots, followed by vortex; however, no significant differences was observed between these two methods. (Two- way Anova, F = 10.79, P < 0.0001) (Table S3, Fig. 7A). Moreover, both the plant species (F = 15.85, P < 0.0001) (Table S3, Fig. 7B) as well as the interaction of species and method (F = 3.42, P = 0.0013) (Table S3, Fig. 7C) significantly affected the efficiency. We also found that shaving with scalpel removed more plant tissue along with trichomes than brushing and vortex and the vortex removed the least amount of plant material than other methods (visual observation). In monocot model plant, rice, even though we found non glandular trichomes, we fail to extract with any of the tested methods.

Discussion

Efficient extraction of trichomes is critical in plant-insect interaction studies for assessing their effect on herbivore growth and development, which is achieved by incorporating them into the artificial diet [21] [25]. Besides exploring the effects against herbivores, the trichome separation also has major implications in proteomics studies. This helps to decipher the biochemistry involved in the biosynthesis of proteins and metabolites in glandular trichomes, which have pharmaceutical and industrial importance [7, 8, 10, 40, 41, 49, 57].

In this study, we evaluated three simple and commonly used methods for isolating trichomes from the leaf surfaces with slight modifications: brushing, shaving with a scalpel, and vortexing, on five different eudicot and one monocot species. Our results demonstrated that in eudicots, the brushing of leaf samples after freezing them in -80 °C for 24 h can remove the highest number of trichomes irrespective of their type. Shaving the trichomes with a scalpel was the second most efficient method, followed by vortex; however, shaving was found to be equally efficient as brushing, particularly for extracting non-glandular trichomes. Furthermore, one of the major challenges in trichome extraction is to minimize the amount of leaf tissue while extracting them from the point of connection on the leaf surface.

Based on the visual observation, the removal of plant materials was highest in the scalpel, followed by the vortex method. The shaving method using a scalpel in the study is adapted from [20, 52], where they used a scalpel to extract non-glandular trichomes without freezing the leaves. Similarly, scraping plant tissue with a frozen spatula was also employed to extract trichomes in tomato [42]. The vortex method has been previously shown to remove the glandular trichomes efficiently while preserving their integrity [58]. This method was previously used by [20] for removing glandular trichomes of tomato and was successful in evaluating their role against tobacco hornworm, Manduca sexta. Taken together, in the eudicot species tested, the brushing method can be considered as the simple and most convenient method to extract trichomes compared to other methods. In contrast, in the monocot model plant, rice, we failed to extract trichomes using any of the methods. We speculate that this might be due to the leaf texture making it soft immediately after being taken out from the − 80 °C freezer and due to relatively lower trichome density compared to eudicots. This highlights another challenge that, despite optimizing the method, the plant species, as well as the anchorage, density, and orientation of trichomes, play a significant role in trichome extraction efficiency.

The difference in the trichome removal efficiency based on plant species was also evident in the eudicot species. Sunflowers (Helianthus spp.) had the highest removal efficiency compared to all other plant species. The possible explanation for this can be the difference in morphology and dimensions of trichomes belonging to different genera and families. Helianthus genus was reported to have non-glandular trichomes (NGT) and different types of glandular trichomes: capitate glandular trichomes (CGT), linear glandular trichomes (LGT), being the most common ones [2, 15, 30, 47]. This presence of diverse non- glandular trichoomes and their orientation on the leaf surface might have facilitated their removal. Tomato, S. lycopersicum have mainly glandular trichomes of type VI, which have a quadricellular head [20, 26, 48, 53, 60], Likewise, the trichomes of Cucurbita pepo were mainly glandular (types I, II, and IV) and had only one type of non-glandular trichome (type III) [28, 32]. In rice, although the trichome density was lower and the evaluated methods failed to extract trichomes compared to eudicots, the most common trichomes found were non-glandular types (macro, micro, and papillae). From these studies, it is evident that the trichomes of the tested plant species are very diverse and hence can respond differently to the extraction methods.

In the study, we used rice as the monocot study system owing to its global importance as well as the limited knowledge on extracting trichomes. However, we failed to optimize an efficient method to extract trichomes in this species. In all the tested methods, leaves were frozen as the first step. Here, the frozen rice leaves lost brittleness immediately, which made it difficult to extract trichomes. Hence, we speculate that for rice and other monocots, advanced methods might be employed to efficiently remove trichomes.

Advanced methods that are widely used for extracting trichomes are based on using organic solvents [38], or fixatives [59], liquid nitrogen, and glass beads [37]. Huebbers et al., [17] also revealed that the addition of EGTA-PBS (cation chelator ethylene glycol-bis (β-aminoethyl ether)-N, N, N′, N′-tetraacetic acid - Phosphate-buffered saline) with glass beads to a vortex or a magnetic stirrer resulted in better trichome extraction in Arabidopsis thaliana. Moreover, Qin et al., [39] showed the successful isolation of glandular trichomes from Artemisia annua using the laser capture microdissection (LCM) method. Although there are many advanced methods, we aimed to find a method that is convenient and easy to use with less prior experience and knowledge.

In conclusion, after examining the advantages and disadvantages of all the methods, we reinforce that the brushing of frozen leaves is more efficient in removing trichomes than shaving and vortexing in eudicot species. Care should also be taken while shaving and brushing that the leaf samples should not be thawed, and the trichomes should be extracted immediately after taking them out of the freezer. These results will be helpful for researchers working with trichomes to efficiently extract them with the lowest cost and no prior equipment knowledge. However, it is also evident that these methods cannot be broadly used for all plant species. Hence, further research is warranted to optimize a method to extract plant species with lower trichome density and with varied trichome orientation and firmness, with particular relevance to monocot species.
