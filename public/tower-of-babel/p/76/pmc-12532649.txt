Genotoxicity Risk Assessment in Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) from a Hatchery Exposed to Pesticides in the Haut-Sassandra Region, Côte d’Ivoire
Authors: Antoine K. Kouadio, Charles K. Boussou, Marc Canonne, Juliette Gaillard, Simon Pouil, Elodie Pepey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fish is a primary protein source in Côte d'Ivoire, accounting for 39% of animal protein intake. However, the current local production from fisheries is insufficient to meet the annual national demand. Fish farming could address this gap, but the production of high-quality fry remains a key challenge. Water quality plays a crucial role in fry production, yet many tilapia farms in the country are susceptible to agrochemical contamination from nearby agricultural activities, which can affect both fish health and reproduction. This study aims to assess genotoxicity of a hatchery water in tilapia using the alkaline comet assay. Water samples from Modern Hatchery of Gonaté in the Haut-Sassandra Region of Côte d’Ivoire were collected in November 2021 and October 2022 for pesticides analysis. Simultaneously, fish broodstock samples were collected for genotoxicity assessment. Our results show that in 2021, when pesticides were used in the surrounding area, DNA damage in the erythrocytes of tilapia broodstock (41.6 ± 6.7% tail DNA) was significantly higher (ANOVA, F(3,31) = 222.9, P < 0.001) compared to individuals sampled in 2022 (15.5 ± 4.4%) after pesticide use in the adjacent food crops ceased. Pesticide residues, including glyphosate, acetamiprid, and mancozeb, were detected in water samples from 2021 but were absent in 2022, coinciding with the cessation of pesticide use near the hatchery. These findings underscore the impact of agrochemical exposure on DNA damage in tilapia, which may have detrimental effects on fry production.

Introduction

In many southern countries, fish and other aquatic animal play a critical role in food security, providing essential nutrients and serving as a primary source of animal protein. In Côte d'Ivoire, fish accounts for 39% of total animal protein intake (CPCO 2019). However, the country’s fish production—estimated at approximately 100,000 tonnes in 2022, primarily from fisheries (FAO 2024)—falls far short of the national demand of 650,000 tonnes annually. Overfishing and human activities have severely depleted local fisheries (Failler et al. 2014), forcing the country to rely heavily on imports, particularly frozen fish, costing 573 billion FCFA in 2020 (Trend Economy 2022). This dependency exacerbates food insecurity and limits access to affordable animal protein, especially for vulnerable populations (Failler et al. 2014).

Aquaculture offers a promising solution to reduce this supply deficit. With its significant natural and human resources, Côte d’Ivoire has strong potential for aquaculture expansion (Amian et al. 2017). However, despite its potential, aquaculture contributes just 1.5% to national fish production (MIPARH 2004; FAO 2024). Developing this section could reduce import reliance, enhance food security, and alleviate rural poverty (MIPARH 2014; Amian et al. 2017).

One major challenge to aquaculture development is ensuring high-quality fry. In Southern Africa and Côte d’Ivoire, fry production has been identified as a major bottleneck in tilapia farming due to limited expertise and infrastructure (Moyo and Rapatsa 2021; FAO 2022). Although hatcheries have been established by the government and private operators, concerns persist about fry quality (Niamien 2019).

Water quality is another critical factor aquaculture success (Nasr-Allah et al. 2014; Saraswathy et al. 2015). Key parameters such as temperature, salinity, pH, hardness, dissolved gases directly impact fish growth, reproduction and survival (Chainark and Boyd 2010). Many tilapia farms in Côte d’Ivoire depend on water sources near agricultural areas, making them vulnerable to agrochemical contamination (Avit et al. 2012; Niamien 2019; Ue Bi et al. 2021). Runoff from farmlands often introduces pesticides like glyphosate, acetamiprid, and mancozeb, which have been shown to cause genotoxic effects in tilapia (El-Garawani et al. 2022; Temiz and Kargın, 2024).

Genotoxicity can be assessed using the alkaline comet assay, a sensitive biomarker for detecting DNA damage in aquatic environments (Frenzilli et al. 2009). This assay, also known as single-cell gel electrophoresis, measures DNA strand breaks at the single-cell level, making it ideal for assessing pollution-induced genotoxic effects (Collins 2004). It is particularly effective in monitoring aquatic species exposed to complex mixtures of pollutants, including heavy metals and pesticides (Jha 2008). The assay detects various types of DNA damage, such as oxidative lesions, single- and double-strand breaks, and alkali-labile sites (Olive and Banáth 2006), making it essential for understanding the impacts of pollutants on aquatic organisms (Sánchez-Galán et al. 2010). Additionally, the method is non-lethal, adaptable, and suitable for use in areas with limited laboratory facilities (Azqueta & Collins 2013).

Numerous studies have validated the comet assay as a tool for biomonitoring aquatic environments. It has been widely used to detect DNA damage in fish exposed to pesticides, industrial effluents, and heavy metals (Cavaş and Könen 2007; Pandey et al. 2011; Osman et al. 2012). Strong correlations have been observed between environmental contaminants and increased DNA damage in fish erythrocytes, highlighting its value as a biomonitoring tool (Cavalcante et al. 2008). The assay’s speed, sensitivity, and versatility further enhance its utility, even in remote settings (Pepey et al. 2023).

This study, among the first to evaluate genotoxicity in fish hatchery water, hypothesizes that agrochemical use near hatcheries may cause genotoxic effects in tilapia. The objective is to assess the genotoxicity of hatchery water using the alkaline comet assay on erythrocytes.

Materials and Methods

Sampling Location and Periods

Gonaté is situated in the Haut-Sassandra region of Côte d'Ivoire, approximately 22 km from the regional capital, Daloa. The region experiences a humid tropical climate characterized by two rainy seasons, with a long dry season lasting from November to March (Barima et al. 2016; Yao 2015). The average annual temperature is 26.5 °C, and the average annual rainfall is 931 mm (Kouadio et al. 2022; Yao 2015). The local economy primarily revolves around agriculture, focusing on perennial cash crops such as coffee, rubber, oil palm, and cocoa (Kouadio et al. 2022).

The Gonaté hatchery, operational since October 2019 (coordinates: 6°54′20"N 6°15′52"W), includes 10 large concrete tanks for housing male and female broodstock for reproduction and fry pre-growth. It also features 10 smaller tanks used for hormonal sex reversal of fry using 17-alpha-methyltestosterone. The tanks are supplied by two boreholes. The site was originally a 0.3-hectare cocoa plantation, later converted into a cashew field, although some cocoa plants remain. Until 2021, the area also functioned as a plantain nursery covering 0.2 hectare, located about 50 m from the hatchery. After the hatchery's establishment, the promoter-initiated market gardening alongside plantain production. Phytosanitary products, including fungicides (mancozeb), herbicides (glyphosate and 2,4-D amine), and insecticides (acetamiprid and bifenthrin), were regularly applied.

In 2022, the fish farmer ceased market gardening alongside plantain production due to increasing labor costs, leading to the discontinuation of fungicide and insecticide use. However, herbicide application persisted in reduced quantities, primarily for weed control around the hatchery.

To assess the genotoxicity of water used for tilapia broodstock, sampling (water and fish) was conducted in November 2021 and October 2022. These periods were selected to evaluate the potential impact of halting pesticide use around the hatchery on the genotoxic potential of its water supply.

Ethical Statement

The study was conducted in accordance with Directive 2010-63-EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The protocols were reviewed and approved by the ethical committee C2EA-36 (Comité d'éthique en expérimentation animale Languedoc-Roussillon) and authorized by the French Ministry of Higher Education and Research (APAFiS no. 2021121416256284).

Fish Blood Sampling

The broodstock used in this study originated from the same group stocked in the hatchery since 2019. Thus, those sampled in 2021 had been in the hatchery for three years, while those sampled in 2022 had been there for four years. Fish were caught from cement tanks using nets. In 2021, 12 individuals (body weight: 194 ± 44 g), comprising 5 males and 7 females, were sampled. In 2022, 23 individuals (body weight: 171 ± 64 g), consisting of 11 males and 12 females, were collected. The variation in sample size reflects the availability of broodstock during each sampling period. Fish were transported in oxygenated bags to the Hydrobiology and Ecological Engineering Laboratory at Jean Lorougnon Guédé University in Daloa, Côte d’Ivoire.

In the laboratory, fish were anesthetized using eugenol (0.5 mL L−1). Blood samples (0.2 mL) were drawn from the caudal vein using heparinized syringes and transferred into sterile microcentrifuge tubes. These were diluted 1:500 (v/v) with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and stored at 4 °C in a light-protected cooler. The comet assay was then performed immediately following the blood sampling. The size and sex of each fish were recorded prior to analysis.

Comet Assay

The alkaline comet assay on Nile tilapia erythrocytes was conducted according to the protocols of Singh et al. (1988) and Osman et al. (2012), with modifications as described by Pepey et al. (2023). Prior to the assay, frosted microscope slides were pre-coated with 1% (w/v) normal melting agarose (NMA) and allowed to air-dry at room temperature for at least two days.

To prepare the samples, 40 µL of the blood-PBS mixture was combined with 360 µL of 0.8% low-melting agarose (LMA). A 75 µL aliquot of this mixture was placed onto each pre-coated slide (two spots per slide), and immediately covered with a coverslip. The slides were then incubated at 4 °C for 15 min to allow the agarose to solidify. After solidification, the coverslips were carefully removed, and the slides were submerged in a cold lysis buffer (2.5 M NaCl, 100 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], 10 mM Tris base, pH 10, 1% Triton X‐100, 10% dimethyl sulfoxide) at 4 °C overnight. Following the lysis step, the slides were transferred to an alkaline electrophoresis solution (300 mM NaOH, 1 mM EDTA, pH ≥ 13) and incubated at 4 °C for 40 min to unwind the DNA. Electrophoresis was then performed under alkaline conditions for 40 min at 4 °C, using an electric field of 0.8 V/cm and 300 mA. After electrophoresis, the slides were neutralized by immersion in 0.4 M Tris buffer (pH 7.5) for 10 min, followed by a 5-min rinse with ultrapure water. The slides were then dehydrated by immersion in pure ethanol (≥ 99.6%) for 10 min, and subsequently dried at 45 °C for at least 1 h until the ethanol had fully evaporated.

Finally, the slides were stained with 100 μL of SYBR Gold (Thermo Fisher Scientific), diluted 10,000 × in Tris/EDTA buffer (pH 8.0), and the comets were visualized using an epifluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse NI‐E; Plan APO 0.75 NA × 20 objective). For each fish, 200 cells were analyzed, and image analysis was performed using the OpenComet plug-in of ImageJ software (Gyori et al. 2014). DNA strand damage was quantified using parameters such as tail length, tail moment, and the percentage of DNA in the comet tail (% tail DNA). Osman et al. (2008) identified % tail DNA as the most reliable indicator for assessing DNA strand breaks in fish, as it measures the proportion of DNA in the tail (Kumaravel and Jha 2006).

Water Analysis

In parallel to fish collection, physico-chemical parameters (temperature, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, and suspended solids) were measured in situ using a portable Hanna HI 98194 multiparameter instrument from a composite water sample obtained by pooling water from each of the tanks used for broodstock sampling.

For pesticide residue analysis, two composite water samples were collected from the broodstock tanks during each sampling campaign. The first, intended for glyphosate quantification, consisted of one liter of pooled water from each sampled tank and was stored in pre-cleaned polyethylene bottles. The second, also one liter, was collected using the same method for the analysis of acetamiprid and mancozeb residues and stored in amber glass bottles to prevent photodegradation. All samples were immediately cooled to 4 °C, filtered through 0.45 µm borosilicate glass fiber filters to remove suspended particles, and analyzed within 24–48 h to ensure data integrity.

The targeted molecules were analyzed using High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) by Akouaba Consulting Service, Abidjan (Côte d’Ivoire). For glyphosate, the method used here followed the recommendations of the standard ISO 16308:2014. This method employs HPLC coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) for high specificity and sensitivity. The process involves sample preparation and clean-up steps to isolate glyphosate, followed by HPLC–MS/MS analysis. The extraction and analysis of acetamiprid and mancozeb from water involved a solid-phase extraction (SPE) to isolate residues, followed by HPLC with UV for quantification. Method validation involved spiking blank water samples with predefined concentrations of each pesticide to establish recovery rates and limits of detection (LOD). The LOD achieved for glyphosate was 0.006 µg L−1, while acetamiprid and mancozeb showed LODs of 0.01 µg/L and 0.02 µg L−1, respectively. Recovery rates for all pesticides exceeded 85%.

Statistical Analysis

Percentage tail DNA was selected as the primary endpoint for quantifying DNA strand breaks, as recommended by Osman et al. (2008) and supported by Mitchelmore et al. (1998). Differences in DNA damage by sex and year were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with post hoc pairwise comparisons, after verification of normality and homoscedasticity assumptions.

For categorical DNA damage analysis, chi-squared tests (χ2) were performed to assess differences in distribution between categories. In case of significant effects, post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using the chisq.posthoc.test package in R, with Bonferroni-adjusted P-values to control for type I error due to multiple testing.

Given that chi-squared tests are sensitive to sample size, an additional effect size measure was calculated to ensure a more robust interpretation of the results. Indeed, very small P-values may indicate statistical significance without biologically meaningful differences. Therefore, Cramer’s V was calculated and interpreted according to Cohen’s (1988) thresholds:V < 0.10: negligible effect0.10 ≤ V < 0.30: small to moderate effectV ≥ 0.30: large effect

A two-way ANOVA was conducted to assess the effects of sex, sampling year, and their interaction on DNA damage levels. The main effect of the year was highly significant (F(1, 31) = 222.8, P < 0.001), while the main effect of sex alone was not significant (F(1, 31) = 3.34, P = 0.077). However, a significant interaction between year and sex was detected (F(1, 31) = 8.04, P = 0.008), indicating that the effect of sex on DNA damage depended on the year of sampling. Post hoc comparisons revealed that in 2021, females exhibited significantly higher DNA damage levels than males (P < 0.05), whereas no sex-related differences were observed in 2022 (P = 0.99). In addition, a significant reduction in DNA damage levels was observed from 2021 to 2022 for both sexes.

A detailed analysis of DNA damage profiles (Fig. 2) highlighted a marked evolution between 2021 and 2022. In 2021, tilapia predominantly exhibited moderate to high levels of DNA damage, with a particularly high proportion of severe damage in females compared to males (the combined proportion of these two categories reached 80% in females versus 70% in males). In 2022, the damage profile was reversed. Minimal and low DNA damage became predominant in both females and males (> 80% in both sexes). Simultaneously, the proportions of severe and moderate damage sharply decreased (e.g., 15% of severe damage in females).

The chi-squared test indicated that this variation in the distribution of damage categories across the four groups (Females 2021, Males 2021, Females 2022, Males 2022) was highly significant (χ2 = 3029.6; P < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons confirmed these differences. In particular, the DNA damage profiles of females in 2021 differed significantly from those of females in 2022 (Cramer’s V = 0.742), males in 2022 (V = 0.704), and males in 2021 (V = 0.280).

A significant difference was also observed between males in 2021 and males in 2022 (P < 0.001, V = 0.519). In contrast, the difference between males and females in 2022 was weak, although statistically significant (P = 0.002, V = 0.067), suggesting a homogenization of DNA damage profiles between sexes in that year.

Thus, 2021 was characterized by more severe DNA damage profiles, particularly in females, whereas in 2022, DNA damage was predominantly minimal to low for both sexes. These results confirm a global improvement in the genotoxic status in 2022, accompanied by a reduction in sex-related differences.

Discussion

Water analysis revealed the presence of three key pesticides—acetamiprid, glyphosate, and mancozeb—in water samples collected in November 2021. However, these pesticides were no longer detected in October 2022. This temporal variation coincided with significantly higher levels of DNA damage in tilapia broodstock erythrocytes in November 2021 (41.6 ± 6.7%) compared to October 2022 (15.5 ± 4.4%). The absence of pesticides in the 2022 water samples therefore coincides with a significant reduction in DNA damage levels, supporting the role of these pesticides as genotoxic agents in aquatic environments.

Numerous studies have shown that exposure to glyphosate, acetamiprid, and mancozeb, even at environmentally relevant concentrations, induces significant DNA damage in fish, including Nile tilapia (Alvarez-Moya et al. 2014; Acar et al. 2021; Aribisala et al. 2022; El-Garawani et al. 2022; Rahman et al. 2023). Aribisala et al. (2022) reported that exposure to glyphosate significantly increased the frequency of micronucleated cells in Nile tilapia, even at low concentrations (0.01 mg L⁻1). Biochemical biomarkers also showed elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) and glutathione-S-transferase (GST) activity in fish exposed to subacute concentrations, while superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was significantly reduced following glyphosate exposure.

Acetamiprid, a neonicotinoid insecticide, induces the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), impairing antioxidant defenses and disrupting the cellular redox balance in tilapia, thereby increasing vulnerability to oxidative DNA damage (El-Garawani et al. 2022). Mancozeb, a dithiocarbamate fungicide, contributes to oxidative stress by generating ROS and inhibiting key antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and catalase, thereby promoting oxidative DNA damage and impairing DNA repair mechanisms, posing a long-term risk in aquatic environments (Rahman et al. 2023).

Moreover, the high DNA damage levels observed in 2021 may also be related to the simultaneous presence of several pesticides, which likely exacerbated their genotoxic effects. Combined exposure to pesticides can lead to additive or synergistic effects, amplifying oxidative stress and DNA damage beyond the impact of individual compounds (Rizzati et al. 2016; Veedu et al. 2022; El-Gendy et al. 2024; 2025). Together, these pesticides promote oxidative stress, DNA strand breaks, and disruption of DNA repair, with serious implications for fish health and ecosystem stability.

These results confirm previous studies showing that environmental pollutants such as pesticides negatively affect aquaculture by inducing DNA damage in reproductive cells, potentially compromising fry production, which is essential for commercial aquaculture. Exposure to genotoxic agents causes DNA strand breaks, mutations, and chromosomal aberrations (Acar et al. 2021; Rahman et al. 2023), potentially reducing fry survival, reproductive success (Devaux et al. 2011), and marketability of fish (Haredi et al. 2020). This highlights the importance of understanding how pesticide mixtures affect aquatic organisms.

The DNA damage levels observed in 2022 were close to reference values considered low for freshwater pollutant exposure, as well as to DNA damage levels reported in negative controls from laboratory studies on tilapia species, generally ranging from 5 to 25% (Ahmed et al. 2011; Vijitkul et al. 2022; Pepey et al. 2023). This reduction in DNA damage suggests that environmental or biological changes influenced genotoxicity between sampling periods. In particular, modifications to agricultural practices around the hatchery may have been a key factor explaining the absence of pesticides in the 2022 water samples.

Field surveys conducted at the Gonaté hatchery support this hypothesis. Between 2021 and 2022, mechanical weeding using brush cutters replaced glyphosate-based herbicides for site maintenance. In addition, the cessation of vegetable farming activities led to the discontinuation of insecticide and fungicide applications previously used to control crop pests. Furthermore, the reduction in plantain banana nursery production, due to marketing difficulties, was accompanied by decreased use of mancozeb for shoot treatment and insecticides for controlling leaf pests on young plants. Such changes are known to improve water quality, as documented by Laurent (2015), who reported similar improvements following adjustments in agricultural practices in French watersheds. Thus, adopting sustainable agricultural practices that minimize pesticide use is essential to protect genetic integrity, enhance fry production, and ensure the long-term viability of aquaculture.

The statistical analysis of DNA damage revealed a weak and non-significant main effect of sex, suggesting that sex alone does not explain the observed variations. This finding is consistent with several previous studies, such as those of Wirzinger et al. (2007) and Santos et al. (2016), which reported no significant sex effect on DNA damage in three-spined stickleback exposed to various pollutants.

However, a significant interaction between sampling year and sex was detected in our study, revealing notable differences depending on the temporal context. In particular, females exhibited significantly higher DNA damage levels than males in 2021, which was no longer the case in 2022. This fluctuation in sex differences may reflect variable sensitivity to the specific environmental conditions of each year, or transient physiological differences (reproductive cycle, oxidative stress, etc.) influencing vulnerability to genotoxic stress.

Although sex is not a major explanatory factor in itself, these results indicate that sex-related differences should not be systematically overlooked, especially when they interact with other environmental or temporal variables. This highlights the importance of considering factor interactions in genotoxicity studies. Despite these occasional variations, Nile tilapia remains a robust bioindicator species, as DNA damage profiles remain interpretable regardless of sex in most situations. This stability, combined with its sensitivity to pollutants, reinforces its relevance for assessing the quality of aquatic environments.

Conclusion

This study represents the first investigation into genotoxicity caused by pesticide exposure in a fish hatchery in Côte d’Ivoire. The findings demonstrate a clear link between environmental changes and DNA damage in Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), emphasizing the need to monitor genotoxic stress in aquatic ecosystems. The significant reduction in DNA damage observed between November 2021 and October 2022 correlates with reduced pesticide use in areas surrounding the hatchery, highlighting the direct impact of agricultural practices on water quality and aquatic health.

The relationship between genotoxicity and fry production is pivotal for sustainable aquaculture. DNA damage in broodstock not only reduces reproductive success but also may compromises offspring quality, with long-term implications for population resilience. Sustainable agricultural practices that mitigate genotoxic stress are essential for maintaining healthy fish populations and supporting the growth of a sustainable aquaculture industry in Côte d’Ivoire.
