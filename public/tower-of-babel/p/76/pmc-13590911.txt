Effusion and synovial hyperplasia: Distinct constructs in early and late-stage knee OA
Authors: Robert Dima, Trevor Birmingham, Holly Philpott, Dianne Bryant, Aaron Fenster, C Thomas Appleton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUltrasound (US) permits the assessment of synovial effusion and synovial thickening (hyperplasia) in knee osteoarthritis (OA). We aimed to measure the associations of these features with radiographic OA severity and patient-reported knee-specific pain using a quantitative US image analysis approach.MethodsPatients with symptomatic knee OA were included. The Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) pain subscale, Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grade, and lateral suprapatellar recess B-mode images were acquired for Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) measurement and grading. US images of effusion-synovitis were manually segmented to calculate effusion and hyperplasia area. Correlation was assessed, and associations with KL grade/KOOS pain were modelled using multivariable linear and quadratic regression with and without adjustment for age, sex, and body mass index (BMI).ResultsQuantitative measures of effusion and hyperplasia area correlated strongly (ρ 0.83 to 0.86) with OMERACT grades and effusion-synovitis depth. Adjusting for age, sex, and BMI, synovial effusion and hyperplasia area are non-linearly related to radiographic severity, a pattern also observed in models of OMERACT global synovitis and effusion-synovitis depth. KOOS pain was associated with effusion-synovitis depth (β=-0.53) and borderline associated with OMERACT hyperplasia (β=-5.55), but not with OMERACT global synovitis, effusion, nor any quantitative effusion-synovitis area measures.ConclusionMinimal US effusion-synovitis among knee OA patients with late-stage disease may indicate maladaptive remodeling of the synovium instead of reduced inflammation. Discriminating between patients with and without maladaptive synovial remodeling in early and late-stage disease should be a priority, as it may improve our ability to predict joint failure.Keywords: Ultrasonography, Osteoarthritis, Knee, Synovitis, Regression analysis

Introduction

Inflammation, represented on medical imaging modalities as synovial effusion and synovial thickening (hyperplasia or synovitis), is associated with knee pain [[1], [2], [3]], and is a driver of disease progression [4,5], highlighting its importance as a potential treatment target in knee osteoarthritis (OA). These findings are especially promising given that there are currently no disease-modifying treatments available for OA, despite being a leading cause of disability worldwide [6]. However, it remains unclear whether imaging features of synovial inflammation uniformly reflect active disease processes, or whether they may also capture chronic synovial remodeling- particularly in joints with advanced structural damage. Ultrasound (US), while being highly operator-dependent, offers several advantages over non-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for the assessment of effusion-synovitis in the knee. It is inexpensive, useable at the point of care, and can discriminate between effusion and hyperplasia, the components of effusion-synovitis, without the need for contrast enhancement [7].

The Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) US scoring system characterizes effusion-synovitis severity using a semi-quantitative grading scale that assigns separate grades for global synovitis, effusion, and hyperplasia based on standardized measurement criteria [8]. Oo et al. has since validated these measures in patients with symptomatic knee OA [9], demonstrating that global synovitis (0–3), synovial hyperplasia (0–1), and effusion (0–1) are moderately linearly associated with the Knee Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) symptoms subscale. Among the OMERACT effusion-synovitis measures, only synovial hyperplasia was significantly associated with both KOOS symptoms and NRS pain [9].

Other studies produce conflicting results; Chan et al. found that effusion and synovial hyperplasia on US were only weakly (Pearson’s r = 0.22) correlated with ambulatory knee pain [10], and Bevers et al. did not find any statistically significant association between US features of effusion-synovitis and pain measured by KOOS or numerical rating scale [11]. Hall et al. showed that synovial hyperplasia detected on US is more commonly found in patients with symptomatic radiographic OA (ROA) compared to patients with asymptomatic ROA. In the same study, however, the amount of US-detected effusion and synovial thickening was more strongly correlated with radiographic severity (r = 0.6 and r = 0.7, P < 0.01) than with pain severity (r = 0.3, P < 0.01), and pain severity did not change in tandem with a change in synovial hyperplasia over time [12]. Similarly, Sarmanova et al. demonstrated in a population-based case-control study, that the association between US-detected effusion and pain diminished substantially after adjusting for radiographic severity, while the association between synovial hyperplasia and pain retained statistical significance [13]. As a result, there is still debate on whether effusion-synovitis visualized on US represents active joint inflammation or if it should instead be considered a part of the total organ pathology, and used as a marker of disease severity and joint damage [12]. The issue is further compounded by the fact that synovial effusion and synovial hyperplasia, despite often occurring together in variable amounts [14], are separate pathological processes. It remains unclear whether or not the composite measure of effusion-synovitis can or should be used as a proxy for joint-level inflammation, or whether the components are best used as separate features of disease in models of knee OA. More recently, Philpott et al. tested the association between OMERACT-defined effusion-synovitis severity and pain experiences captured with the Measure of Intermittent and Constant Osteoarthritis Pain (ICOAP) tool, finding that moderate to severe effusion-synovitis on US was associated with constant pain, but the association was only present among patients with early-stage joint damage defined by Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grade [15]. It’s possible that effusion-synovitis visualized on US may reflect both active inflammation and chronic synovial remodeling, to varying degrees between individuals. Just as the disease process of OA causes degenerative changes to the bony architecture of the joint, the synovium may also be irreversibly modified by recurrent bouts of inflammation over time, possibly manifesting on US as thickening and fibrosis of the synovial membrane with or without effusion. The results from the Philpott study led us to further hypothesize that effusion and/or synovial hyperplasia may reflect active inflammation in early-stage disease (KL grade ≤2), and chronic damage in late-stage disease (KL grade ≥3).

Our objective was to determine whether synovial hyperplasia and effusion exhibit distinct associations with radiographic OA severity and knee-specific patient-reported outcome measures. Specifically, we hypothesized that: i) effusion and hyperplasia would follow different, potentially non-linear patterns of association with radiographic severity across the spectrum of KL grades; and ii) these features may reflect active inflammation in early-stage disease (KL ≤2) but chronic, maladaptive synovial remodeling in late-stage disease (KL ≥3). Harnessing the inherent contrast resolution of high-frequency US, we employ a quantitative US image analysis approach where effusion and hyperplasia area (mm²) are analyzed separately and on the same continuous scale.

Patients and methods

The procedures followed were in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional and national) and with the Declaration of Helsinki (revised in 2000). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved on scientific and ethical grounds by the local research ethics board (Health Sciences Research Ethics Board, London ON CA #109,255).

Participants were recruited as part of the ongoing Western Ontario Registry for Early Osteoarthritis (WOREO) Knee Study, a prospective, single-centre, multi-clinic (St. Joseph’s Rheumatology clinic, Fowler Kennedy Sport Medicine Clinic, and Rorabeck Bourne Joint Replacement Clinic) cohort study. Objectives of the observational cohort study are to investigate clinical, biomechanical, and pathophysiological features of symptomatic knee OA in patients spanning early to late stage of disease. We included patients over the age of 18 with a diagnosis of knee OA based on clinical assessment by a rheumatologist or orthopaedic surgeon, and frequent knee symptoms defined as pain, aching, or stiffness on most days for the last 4 weeks within the past year. We excluded patients with a history of inflammatory arthritis, disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug (DMARD) use, oral corticosteroid use, or any knee procedure or corticosteroid injection within 6 months of enrollment. In this paper, we present analyses on baseline data from the index knee of WOREO Knee Study participants.

Fixed-flexion AP radiographs were acquired at baseline visits on all study participants. Radiographic severity was quantified using the Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grading system. Up to one week before the baseline visit, all patients completed the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), which is a knee-specific outcome measure. BMI was measured using an Omron KaradaScan BF511 bio-impedance monitor. In the standard fashion, participants were asked to remove their shoes and socks, stand upright on the lower limb electrodes, and hold the upper limb electrodes outwards from their body.

US image acquisition and grading

US imaging was performed at regularly scheduled clinic visits by operators with formal training in musculoskeletal US imaging. Inter-rater reliability for the US acquisition protocol was not formally assessed in this study. Each participant received the same US protocol, comprising representative B-mode and Doppler images from the lateral suprapatellar recess at 30⁰ knee flexion, using a triangular pillow. The lateral suprapatellar recess was selected as the imaging site as it is the most commonly assessed site in OMERACT consensus-based US protocols for knee effusion-synovitis. Participants were imaged with a GE Logiq E9 or GE Logiq S8 unit using a high-frequency (8–12 MHz) linear-array probe. The standard knee US preset available from GE was used for all participants, and basic machine parameters (frequency, depth, focal depth) were adjusted as needed to optimize image resolution and contrast. US caliper placement was performed during the scan to measure effusion depth and effusion-synovitis depth in each image from the lateral suprapatellar compartment. For caliper placement, the largest anterior-posterior (AP) distance perpendicular to the femur was measured three times per image, and the largest of the three measurements was recorded. For effusion, the caliper measurements included only anechoic signal within the synovial recess. For effusion-synovitis, the caliper measurements included both anechoic and hypoechoic components of visible inflammation and excluded any components of the adjacent pre-femoral and suprapatellar fat pads. Sonographers were not blinded to participants' clinical status, as imaging was performed at regularly scheduled clinic visits. However, US imaging was performed before or on the same day as radiographic acquisition in most cases, meaning that KL grades were typically not yet determined. OMERACT grades for global synovitis, effusion, and hyperplasia were assigned according to published criteria [8] by a single experienced rater. Effusion-synovitis was graded 0–3 based on the maximal anterior-posterior depth of anechoic and/or hypoechoic signal in the lateral suprapatellar recess. Hyperplasia and effusion were defined as the presence of significant abnormal hypoechoic intra-articular tissue and joint distension by anechoic fluid, respectively."

Quantitative image analysis

For each patient, the B-mode US image of the lateral suprapatellar recess of the index knee was used to quantify findings of effusion-synovitis. Quantitative segmentation was performed by a single trained rater for all participants. Intra-rater reliability for this method has been reported previously [16], with intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC) of >0.95 for total effusion-synovitis area, hyperplasia area, and effusion area (Fig. 1). Briefly, US images of effusion-synovitis were grayscale-normalized in ImageJ and both hyperplasia and effusion components were separately segmented using the Otsu threshold in 3DSlicer. The segment statistics module in 3DSlicer was used to export quantitative image data from these segmentations, including total effusion-synovitis area, hyperplasia area, effusion area, and the mean signal intensity of segmented hyperplastic synovium.

Statistical modelling and analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA/IC version 16.1. Demographics and descriptive statistics are reported as means with standard deviation, or proportions where applicable. Correlation was measured using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) and was estimated using the bootstrap method with 1000 repetitions. Univariable and multivariable linear regression models were used to test the association of US features of effusion and synovial thickening with radiographic severity and the KOOS pain sub-scale. Quantitative effusion-synovitis area measures were log-transformed in all models due to significant right-skewness. All models underwent diagnostic tests for linearity, heteroscedasticity, and normality of residuals. There were ten instances of missing data, five of these corresponded to missing BMI data, and five corresponded to critically missing US data. The missing BMI entries were imputed from measurements of height and weight in the patients’ medical chart, which were acquired by the clinical team at the time of the visit. The five patients with missing US caliper measures were removed from the analysis. Eq. Visual inspection of marginal prediction plots (Figs. 2a/b) suggested a non-linear relationship between KL grade and effusion-synovitis area measures, prompting the inclusion of a quadratic term for KL grade in the relevant regression models. This modelling decision was data-driven rather than specified a priori. Results

Images were analyzed from knee OA patients who participated in the ongoing WOREO Knee Study. Consecutive ultrasound scans from baseline visits were collected, and all images (n = 235) with complete data were analyzed. 139 samples were collected from consecutive patients in the standard-of-care arm (early to late-stage knee OA), and 96 samples were collected from consecutive patients in the arthroplasty arm (late-stage knee OA). Demographic characteristics (Table 1) were representative of a typical OA cohort, with an average BMI of 31.9 kg/m2, age of 66 years, and a female majority.

Quantitative analysis of synovial hyperplasia and effusion

Mean effusion-synovitis area was 157.9 mm2, mean effusion area was 88.5 mm2, and mean hyperplasia area was 69.4 mm2. Total effusion-synovitis area was strongly correlated to semi-quantitative OMERACT synovitis grades (ρ=0.86, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.90) and to unidimensional caliper-based effusion-synovitis depth (ρ=0.84, 95%CI 0.79 to 0.90). Effusion area and unidimensional caliper-based maximal effusion depth were also strongly correlated (ρ =0.83, 95%CI 0.77 to 0.88).

Associations between effusion-synovitis measures and radiographic grade

KL grade was not statistically significantly associated with hyperplasia area in univariate analysis (β=0.27, p = 0.09). When adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, the strength of the relationship increased (β=0.33) but did not reach significance (p = 0.08). On marginal prediction plots, the model-predicted values of hyperplasia area increased with KL grade at lower levels of radiographic severity but leveled off and began to decrease at higher KL grades (Fig. 2a), suggesting a quadratic rather than monotonic linear relationship. Because this pattern indicates that hyperplasia does not continue to increase progressively with worsening joint damage, further investigation of the non-linear model specification was undertaken. A test of linearity using contrasts of marginal linear predictions revealed that the model follows both a linear and quadratic form (p-value for linear contrast=0.01 and for quadratic polynomial contrast=0.02). Adding a second squared term for KL grade increased the strength of the relationship between KL grade and hyperplasia area (β=1.79, 95% CI 0.55 to 3.02, p < 0.01) and the squared term was negative, confirming that a decreasing trend is evident at higher levels of KL grade. Inclusion of a squared term for KL grade was also accompanied by an increase in the adjusted R2 (from 0.012 to 0.032). In this model, when adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, each unit increase in KL grade is associated with an approximately 5-fold (see Eq. (1) and Supplementary Table 1) increase in hyperplasia area in early to established OA, with the relationship falling off in late-stage disease.

Similarly, KL grade demonstrated a weakly positive linear relationship with effusion area on univariate analysis with β=0.34 and p = 0.04, which increased in magnitude after adjustment by age, sex, and BMI (β=0.42, p = 0.03). The same trend of plateauing effusion area in patients with high radiographic severity was visualized on the marginal predictive plot for this model (Fig. 2b), which again prompted a test of linearity using contrasts of marginal linear predictions. The effusion area model also follows both a linear and quadratic form (p-value for linear contrast = 0.001 and for quadratic polynomial contrast = 0.003). Adding a term for the square of KL grade into the model increased the strength of the relationship between KL grade and effusion area (β=2.20, CI 0.95 to 3.45, p < 0.01) and the squared term was negative. Inclusion of a squared term for KL grade was also accompanied by an increase in the adjusted R2 (from 0.0332 to 0.0639). In this model, when adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, each unit increase in KL grade is associated with an approximately 8-fold see Eq. (1) and Supplementary Table 1) increase in effusion area in early to established OA, with the relationship falling off in late-stage disease.

The non-linear relationship between effusion-synovitis and radiographic severity was confirmed in models of semi-quantitatively graded OMERACT global synovitis as well as effusion-synovitis depth in mm measured by US calipers (Supplementary Table 1). The relationship between KL grade and effusion-synovitis features was also modelled linearly and stratified by early (KL grade 0–2) and late-stage (KL grade 3–4) disease (Table 2).

Discussion

The intrinsic contrast between synovial effusion and synovial hyperplasia on US allowed us to measure these constructs separately using a novel published quantitative analysis methodology [16]. Quantitative synovial measures using this approach agree closely with data from Wang et al., who measured effusion-synovitis as fluid-equivalent signal on T2-weighted MRI in a similar way [20]. We used these measures to investigate associations with joint damage (radiographic stage) and KOOS pain. The quantitative area measures of effusion-synovitis showed excellent construct validity (ρ=0.86) with OMERACT semi-quantitative global synovitis grades, and criterion validity (ρ=0.84) with effusion-synovitis depth caliper measurements. This technique has the advantage of producing continuous and reproducible measures of synovial effusion and hyperplasia at the point of care and agrees very well with existing semi-quantitative and caliper-based measures of effusion-synovitis.

Both hyperplasia area and effusion area were non-linearly associated with radiographic severity while adjusting for age, sex, and BMI. We compared the adjusted R2 of the linear models to the adjusted R2 of the quadratic models, which included a squared term for KL grade to explain the plateau phenomenon we observed on the marginal prediction plots (Fig. 2), allowing us to directly compare the variance explained by both models. A quadratic regression model explained more of the variance in the relationships between KL grade and effusion area/hyperplasia area, reflecting that in our sample, effusion and hyperplasia area both increase in association with joint damage in early-stage disease, but plateau and taper off in patients with late-stage disease. In replicated models of OMERACT synovitis and effusion-synovitis depth, the addition of a squared term for KL grade increased the magnitude of the relationship between KL grade and effusion-synovitis, improved the adjusted R2 of the models, and passed the test of contrasts of marginal linear predictions, indicating the presence of both linear and quadratic effects. We also fit linear models of KL grade regressed against ultrasound-synovitis features stratified by disease stage (Table 2), which confirmed that individuals with early-stage disease tend to demonstrate higher levels of effusion and hyperplasia on US as radiographic severity increases, whereas patients in the late-stage group demonstrate the opposite effect (Fig. 3A/B). One may suggest that a plateauing effect of synovitis severity in late-stage disease may simply reflect ceiling effects related to the measurement of effusion-synovitis, but this is unlikely to explain the observed relationship, given that only 12 of 148 (8%) participants with late-stage knee OA demonstrated grade 3 OMERACT synovitis. While the adjusted R2 values provide statistical support for quadratic models, the variance explained remained low, indicating that KL grade explains only a small proportion (3–6%) of the inter-individual variability in effusion-synovitis area. Other factors we could not measure in this study, such as acute inflammatory flares, physical activity levels, prior anti-inflammatory treatments, and individual differences in inflammatory phenotype may be important drivers of variability in synovial pathology measured on US.

These data indicate that, while joint damage is associated with worse effusion-synovitis overall (Table 1), a sub-group of knee OA patients display low suprapatellar effusion and synovitis measures on US despite advanced joint damage. In these patients, low inflammatory measures may indicate maladaptive remodeling of the synovial membrane (synovial damage) due to chronic unresolved inflammation. Scarring and microvascular dysfunction of the synovium may arise secondary to chronic exposure to growth factors, cellular infiltration, and fibrosis, ultimately culminating in a failure of the innate immune response. This theory is supported by prior work showing increased synovial fibrosis in late-stage disease [21], heightened inflammatory infiltration and mediator expression in early-stage disease [22], and an inverse correlation between synovial fibrosis and markers of synovial inflammation including lining thickness, infiltration, and vascularization [23]. Recent work shows that individuals with histological evidence of synovial damage (lining erosion, vasculopathy, and fibrosis) have significantly lower odds of moderate/severe synovitis on US, smaller effusion-synovitis depth measures, and smaller effusion depth measures [24]. Together, these findings indicate that synovitis on imaging represents a mix of inflammatory and remodeling processes within synovium, and that chronic joint inflammation results in structural damage to the synovium in at least some patients with late-stage disease. Low inflammation among patients with advanced disease may also be attributable in part to reduced physical activity leading to lower mechanical provocation of synovitis, and prior treatment effects including corticosteroid injections, which were not captured in this study.

We also analyzed the mean grayscale signal intensity of segmented hyperplastic synovial tissue as an indirect measure of tissue composition.We hypothesized that hyperplasia mean signal intensity would increase linearly with radiographic severity, under the assumption that as the synovium undergoes fibrosis, the acoustic properties of synovium would progress toward those of a denser medium (hyperechoic). This approach to US image analysis has been used to measure grades of liver fibrosis and demonstrated good construct validity with histologic grading [25]. Instead, we saw a weakly negative trend, with synovium demonstrating lower average signal intensities among those with advanced radiographic stage, though the model did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.2). Many unmeasured factors affect the amplitude of the returning US signal from a medium, including depth of penetration, the amount of subcutaneous fat in the near-field, acoustic artefacts, angle of insonation, among others [26]. Although our method uses within-image grayscale normalization, which reduces the influence of between-unit differences in absolute grayscale output, residual variability attributable to differences in transducer characteristics and signal rendering between units cannot be excluded.

We failed to find any significant associations between quantitative area measures of effusion-synovitis and KOOS pain, despite confirming significant associations with OMERACT and caliper-based measures of synovitis in the same dataset. It’s possible that the presence of ≥4 mm of hyperplasia may capture a clinically meaningful boundary of synovial thickening related to a painful inflammatory phenotype, the effects of which are diluted when distributing values along a linear continuum, as in the quantitative area measures. Also, because both the OMERACT grading system and caliper-based measures emphasize the AP dimension of joint distension, stronger associations with KOOS pain may be more directly related to capsular stretch and associated mechanical nociception than total cross-sectional area.

OMERACT hyperplasia, but not effusion, was borderline associated with worse KOOS pain when adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and radiographic stage, which is consistent with prior work [9,13]; the presence of hyperplasia was associated with a 5.55-point lower KOOS pain score, approaching but not exceeding the established MCID of approximately 8 points [18,19]. As expected, KL grade was strongly and significantly associated with KOOS pain (β=−4.40, p < 0.01). In an unadjusted analysis of z-scores for KOOS pain plotted against effusion-synovitis area, we found a convincing pattern that the highest scores for effusion-synovitis were associated with average KOOS pain, whereas the patients with the lowest and highest levels of knee pain both demonstrate average/low levels of effusion-synovitis (Fig. 4). Low effusion-synovitis in the low pain group (predominantly early-stage) may represent participants with early inflammation, whereas low levels of effusion-synovitis in the high pain group (predominantly late-stage) may represent participants with synovial damage. Future work is needed to find ways of reliably discriminating between patients with and without synovial damage at the point of care. Identifying patients with synovial damage may allow for more accurate allocation to clinical and surgical interventions, improving clinical outcomes through a precision medicine approach.

Limitations

Studies using MRI have shown that effusion-synovitis tends to accumulate predominantly in the posterior recess, followed by the suprapatellar recess [27]. We chose to measure effusion-synovitis in the suprapatellar recess due to its accessibility with US using an anterior approach We used the lateral suprapatellar compartment as a proxy for knee inflammation, but this region may not fully reflect joint-wide inflammatory burden in all patients. We and others [28,29] have found that most suprapatellar effusion-synovitis on US occurs in the lateral recess, likely due to the distribution of muscles around the joint; namely a highly muscular vastus medialis insertion and tendinous vastus lateralis insertion on the superior patellar pole. Limb alignment data were not available, precluding evaluation of compartment-specific influences on effusion-synovitis at this site. We employed a cross-sectional study design to investigate the relationship of effusion-synovitis features with radiographic severity, the gold-standard measurement of OA progression. Because patients with knee OA progress radiographically at different rates [[30], [31], [32]], our cross-sectional design limits our ability to make interpretations of the data. A longitudinal repeated measures design would have allowed us to directly assess within-individual changes in hyperplasia and effusion along with radiographic progression over time. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied given the exploratory nature of these analyses. US Doppler images were not analyzed, as the primary focus of this manuscript was B-mode quantitative analysis methodology. Doppler data may have a role in distinguishing between active and non-active inflammation in OA and should be a priority for future studies.

Conclusion

Effusion and synovitis are major contributors to the pathology of OA but do not increase linearly with joint damage. Because synovial damage at the microscopic level is inversely related to US-synovitis, this finding may be explained by the existence of a sub-group of knee OA patients with significant remodeling of both the bony/cartilaginous and synovial joint structures. Focusing on effusion-synovitis as a single construct risks mischaracterizing this group of patients as those with “low” or “resolved” inflammation. Identifying patients with synovial damage and determining which factors influence the transition from active inflammation to tissue remodeling will be critically important to improving clinical outcomes in this group. Further studies are needed to identify patients with synovial damage at the point of care. This could be approached with the use of compositional imaging (using shear wave elastography, contrast-enhanced US, or radiomics) to identify synovium which has undergone structural changes. Longitudinal study designs should also be deployed to determine whether synovial damage occurs in all patients over time, and which factors influence the transition from active inflammation to synovial remodeling.
