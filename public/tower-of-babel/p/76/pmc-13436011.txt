Learning to engage in clinical research: a qualitative study developing a behavioural pathway model among nurses in China
Authors: Qian Wang, Qian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeveloping the capacity of health professionals to engage in research and practice improvement is an important goal of health professions education. In nursing, research literacy is considered a key capability that enables clinicians to understand research, formulate practice-based questions, and initiate research or quality improvement activities. In China, rapid expansion of nursing education has strengthened academic preparation; however, the ability of clinical nurses to translate clinical problems into research or improvement initiatives remains uneven across practice settings. Little is known about how nurses develop research literacy in clinical environments and how workplace conditions shape their engagement in research-related practice.MethodsThis qualitative study used semi-structured interviews with 20 clinical nurses from multiple departments in a tertiary teaching hospital in China. Participants were purposively sampled to capture variation in career stage, educational background, and research exposure. Interviews explored how nurses understood research, interacted around research ideas, evaluated research value, and attempted to initiate or sustain research or improvement projects. Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis, informed by sensitising concepts from research literacy theory and the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) behavioural framework.ResultsFour interrelated themes were identified. Nurses commonly accessed research evidence but often relied on conclusion-oriented interpretation rather than methodological appraisal, which limited their ability to adapt evidence to local practice. Research-related dialogue was mediated by hierarchy, professional positioning, team climate, and perceived psychological safety. Participants distinguished clinically meaningful research from “formalistic” projects perceived as procedural, symbolic, or driven mainly by institutional requirements. This distinction suggested that nurses were not rejecting research itself, but were critically judging whether research activity was clinically meaningful, morally legitimate, and feasible within everyday practice.Translating clinical concerns into research or improvement activities emerged as a fragile and non-linear pathway: ideas could be initiated, stalled, faded, or reactivated depending on workload pressures, leadership support, organisational opportunity, psychological safety, and perceived clinical meaning.ConclusionsEngaging in research in clinical nursing appears to be a socially situated professional practice rather than an individual technical skill. Research engagement develops through the interaction of research literacy, professional positioning, psychological safety, motivational commitment, and organisational opportunity within clinical workplaces. Based on these findings, the study proposes a heuristic behavioural pathway model describing how nurses move from interpreting research and recognising clinical problems to evaluating research value and attempting research or improvement action. The model highlights that this process is recursive and contingent rather than a stable linear progression, and may help identify where research engagement is likely to be interrupted or sustained in clinical nursing practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09592-y.Keywords: Research engagement, Research literacy, Clinical nurses, Health professions education, Qualitative research, COM-B model

Introduction

In contemporary healthcare systems, disciplinary development increasingly depends on the interaction between research and practice. In mature professional fields, research generates conceptual and methodological advances, while clinical work continuously raises problems that shape research agendas [1]. Frontline practitioners are therefore not only implementers of knowledge but also contributors to improvement and inquiry [2]. Preparing health professionals who can interpret evidence, engage in research-informed dialogue, and translate clinical observations into action has consequently become a central concern in global medical and nursing education [3].

Over the past two decades, nursing education in China has undergone rapid expansion and restructuring. Earlier training models were primarily designed to address workforce shortages and emphasised vocational competence rather than research capability [4]. With the expansion of the healthcare system and higher education, undergraduate and postgraduate programmes have grown substantially, and nursing has increasingly been recognised as a nationally regulated academic discipline [5]. This shift reflects a broader transition from nursing as a technical occupation towards a profession expected to contribute to knowledge development and service improvement [6].

Despite these structural advances, the clinical nursing workforce now shows clear internal stratification. Many nurses trained through vocational pathways have accumulated two to three decades of frontline experience and have become highly skilled clinical experts. However, most received little formal research education during training [7]. They therefore possess substantial experiential knowledge but often lack the conceptual tools to translate clinical problems into research questions or to develop systematic inquiry [8]. As a result, valuable clinical expertise remains insufficiently connected to the development of nursing knowledge.

At the same time, increasing numbers of university-trained nurses are entering clinical practice with stronger academic preparation and greater exposure to research methods. However, their clinical integration, practical authority, and influence on everyday clinical decision-making may still be developing, particularly during the early stages of their careers. This misalignment is therefore better understood as a transitional feature of the current Chinese nursing workforce rather than a fixed or permanent divide. It is likely to persist during this transitional period because experienced vocationally trained nurses continue to hold substantial clinical authority and practical knowledge, while university-trained nurses may have stronger research preparation but may still be developing the clinical experience and workplace credibility needed to influence frontline practice [9]. Ideally, disciplinary development relies on a reciprocal cycle in which research guides practice and clinical realities shape research priorities [10]. In Chinese nursing, however, this cycle remains fragile. Research outputs often stay confined to projects, publications, or policy requirements and are not consistently translated into routine practice. Conversely, many clinically important problems fail to enter research agendas because mechanisms linking frontline observations to structured inquiry remain weak [11]. Clinical care therefore continues to rely heavily on experience and institutional routines, while research risks drifting away from practice realities.

In many Western healthcare systems, the connection between research and practice is partly supported by differentiated professional roles. Hospitals often employ clinical research nurses, nurse scientists, or advanced practice nurses whose responsibilities explicitly include coordinating research, facilitating evidence translation, and linking clinical observations to structured inquiry. These roles provide institutional pathways through which research knowledge can circulate within clinical teams [12]. This contrast is important for the present study because, where such formal research-oriented nursing roles are limited, research engagement is less likely to be embedded in routine work and may depend more heavily on individual initiative, local leadership, and informal organisational support.

In China, however, such role differentiation remains limited. Most nurses are positioned primarily as clinical practitioners, and research engagement is typically pursued in addition to routine clinical duties rather than through formally recognised research-oriented positions [12]. As a result, the translation of research into practice depends largely on individual initiative rather than embedded organisational mechanisms. This structural configuration may further weaken the continuity between clinical experience, research production, and evidence-informed action.

Policy initiatives have largely focused on expanding education, funding research, and promoting evidence-based practice. Yet these approaches do not fully explain why research-informed action remains difficult in everyday clinical work [13]. To examine this issue, the present study uses research literacy and the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) model as complementary sensitising concepts. Research literacy directs attention to how nurses understand, discuss, and evaluate research, whereas COM-B helps examine how research-related behaviour is shaped by capability, opportunity, and motivation within clinical workplaces [14, 15]. This concise conceptual framing allows the study to explore research engagement not simply as an individual skill, but as a situated professional behaviour shaped by both literacy and workplace conditions.

Using qualitative interviews, this study explores how clinical nurses in China understand research, how they decide whether and how to apply research-informed practices, and which contextual factors enable or constrain their engagement in research and improvement activities. By analysing nurses’ accounts of their everyday experiences, the study aims to clarify the mechanisms through which research-oriented behaviour develops in practice and to provide a conceptual foundation for strengthening the links between nursing education, clinical practice, and disciplinary development.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted an interpretive qualitative design using semi-structured interviews to explore how clinical nurses understand research, engage with evidence, and translate clinical concerns into research or improvement actions in everyday hospital practice [16]. The study was informed by an interpretivist orientation, focusing on how meanings and research-related behaviours are constructed within clinical contexts rather than testing predefined hypotheses.

Two complementary frameworks were used as sensitising concepts: a research literacy perspective distinguishing functional, interactive, and critical dimensions [15], and the COM-B behavioural system, which conceptualises professional behaviour as shaped by capability, opportunity, and motivation [14]. Functional research literacy referred to nurses’ ability to access and understand research information and extract clinically relevant meaning from it. Interactive research literacy referred to their ability to discuss, negotiate, and apply research knowledge within clinical teams. Critical research literacy referred to their ability to appraise the credibility, relevance, feasibility, and value of research in relation to local clinical practice. Research literacy was used to sensitise the analysis to how nurses interpreted, discussed, and evaluated research, whereas COM-B was used to examine how such literacy was, or was not, translated into research-related action under specific organisational conditions. These concepts informed interview development and interpretation, while analytic categories were generated inductively from participants’ accounts rather than imposed as a fixed coding template. Thus, the frameworks were not used as a priori coding categories, but as interpretive resources for examining how research literacy was translated, or not translated, into research-related behaviour in clinical settings.

Setting and participants

Participants were recruited from a large tertiary teaching hospital in a major city in southern China. The hospital has a history of over one hundred years and serves as the largest regional referral centre. It provides clinical education for students from multiple educational pathways, including vocational college programmes, undergraduate nursing degrees, and postgraduate training. The institution includes a wide range of clinical departments and specialist services and functions as a training base for nurses at different stages of professional development.

Purposive maximum-variation sampling was used to include nurses from different departments, career stages, professional roles, educational pathways, and levels of involvement in research or quality improvement activities [17]. This sampling strategy was chosen to capture diverse experiences of research engagement across nurses with different clinical authority, educational preparation, and exposure to research or quality improvement.

Potential participants were first identified with the assistance of departmental nurse managers and clinical contacts who were familiar with nurses’ roles and research-related experience. Eligible nurses were then approached individually by the research team, either in person or through telephone or WeChat contact, and were provided with written and verbal information about the study. Nurses were informed that participation was voluntary, that declining to participate would not affect their professional evaluation or workplace relationships, and that they could withdraw at any time before data analysis without consequence. Those who expressed willingness to participate were contacted by the interviewer to arrange an interview at a convenient time and private location.

Before the interviews, participants completed a brief information form documenting their professional background. These data were used to support comparison across cases during analysis.

Sampling and analysis proceeded concurrently. Recruitment was guided by conceptual sufficiency rather than a predetermined sample size. After approximately the 16th interview, no substantially new patterns were identified in relation to the main analytic domains, including how nurses interpreted research, negotiated research-related dialogue, evaluated the value of projects, and attempted to translate clinical problems into research or improvement activities. Four additional interviews were conducted to test and refine the developing interpretation across different career stages, educational backgrounds, and clinical areas. Recruitment stopped after the 20th interview when the dataset was judged to provide sufficient depth, variation, and conceptual richness for the study aims [16].

Data collection

Data were collected through individual semi-structured interviews. The interview guide was informed by the research literacy framework and the COM-B behavioural system and organised around six domains: functional research literacy, interactive research literacy, critical research literacy, behavioural capability for research or improvement, organisational opportunity structures, and motivational conditions for engagement. The full interview guide is provided as Supplementary File 1 to support transparency and replication.

Interview questions focused on concrete experiences rather than abstract views. Follow-up probes were used to elicit specific episodes, decision processes, interpersonal dynamics, and emotional responses related to research engagement and practice improvement. Example questions included: “Can you describe a recent situation in which you used research or guidelines to address a clinical problem?”, “What usually happens when you raise a research-related idea in your department?”, and “How do you judge whether a research or improvement project is meaningful for clinical practice?”

Interviews were conducted face-to-face in private rooms within the hospital between 1 December 2025 and 1 February 2026. All interviews were conducted in Mandarin Chinese by the first author, a female clinical nursing researcher with clinical nursing experience and prior training in qualitative interviewing. She had experience conducting qualitative interviews and was familiar with the hospital context, nursing work processes, and local professional terminology. Each interview lasted between 40 and 70 min. With participants’ consent, interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were first produced in Chinese. Selected quotations were translated into English for publication, and the translations were checked by both authors to ensure that the meaning, tone, and contextual nuance of participants’ accounts were preserved. Field notes were written immediately after each interview to capture contextual observations, interviewer reflections, and early analytic questions. These notes were not treated as primary data equivalent to interview transcripts, but were used to support reflexive memo writing and to contextualise early analytic impressions during subsequent analysis.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis [16]. The analysis aimed to identify recurring patterns in how nurses described understanding research, engaging with evidence, and navigating organisational conditions that shaped research- and improvement-related behaviours.

Analysis proceeded iteratively. First, transcripts were read repeatedly to develop familiarity with participants’ accounts. During this stage, the researchers noted initial impressions, recurrent meanings, striking cases, and possible tensions between participants’ accounts.

Second, initial codes were generated inductively, focusing on descriptions of experiences, actions, explanations, and perceived constraints related to research engagement. Codes were kept close to participants’ accounts at the early stage and included both semantic descriptions, such as reading conclusions or lacking time, and more interpretive codes, such as uncertainty about speaking, legitimacy of research, and project fading. One author led the initial coding process, while the second author reviewed selected transcripts, coding summaries, and early code groupings.

Third, codes were compared across interviews and clustered into candidate themes capturing both shared patterns and meaningful variation across roles and career stages (for example, staff nurses versus nurse managers, or vocationally trained versus university-trained trajectories). Candidate themes were refined by examining whether they captured coherent patterns across the dataset, whether they were sufficiently distinct from one another, and whether they explained variation across participants with different educational backgrounds, professional roles, and research exposure. Negative or less typical cases were discussed to avoid treating dominant accounts as universal. Both authors discussed code groupings, candidate themes, discrepant or less typical cases, and illustrative extracts. Differences in interpretation were not treated as coding errors, but as opportunities for reflexive discussion and further engagement with the data.

The research team refined theme definitions through repeated movement between data extracts and interpretive accounts. Theme names and boundaries were revised several times as the analysis moved from descriptive categories, such as “difficulty understanding methods” and “lack of time,” toward broader interpretive themes concerning functional interpretation, interactive positioning, critical evaluation, and fragile action.

Analytic decisions, coding refinements, and theme development were documented in memos to maintain an audit trail.These memos recorded emerging interpretations, questions about the influence of hierarchy and institutional culture, reflections on the interviewer’s insider position, and decisions about how empirical themes related to the developing behavioural pathway model. Field notes written after interviews were also reviewed during analysis to contextualise participants’ accounts and to support reflexive interpretation, although interview transcripts remained the primary data source.During theme refinement, these notes helped the research team consider how interview context, participant hesitation, emotional tone, and the interviewer’s immediate reflections might inform the interpretation of sensitive issues such as hierarchy, psychological safety, and perceived formalistic research practices.

After candidate themes had been developed inductively, the research team returned to the sensitising concepts of research literacy and COM-B to examine how the empirical patterns could be interpreted conceptually. This step was not used to recode the data into predefined theoretical categories. Instead, it helped clarify how nurses’ ways of accessing, discussing, and evaluating research interacted with capability, opportunity, and motivation to shape research-related behaviour. Throughout this process, both authors participated in analytic discussions to refine theme boundaries, examine alternative interpretations, and ensure that the final themes remained grounded in participants’ accounts while offering a coherent conceptual interpretation.

Reflexivity

The interviewer was a female clinical nursing researcher working within the hospital, with clinical nursing experience and prior training in qualitative interviewing, which supported contextual understanding but required awareness of potential role-related assumptions. This insider position enabled the interviewer to understand local terminology, organisational routines, and the practical constraints of clinical nursing work. However, it also created potential power and relational dynamics that could have influenced what participants chose to disclose, particularly when discussing hierarchy, institutional research culture, or projects perceived as procedural, symbolic, or administratively driven. Some participants may have known the interviewer or recognised her institutional role, which could have encouraged context-rich accounts but may also have made them cautious about expressing negative or critical views. Participants were informed that the study explored experiences rather than performance. Before each interview, the interviewer emphasised that participation was voluntary, that there were no right or wrong answers, that declining or withdrawing would not affect professional evaluation or workplace relationships, and that findings would be reported anonymously. The interviews were conducted in private rooms to reduce the possibility that participants’ responses would be overheard by colleagues or managers. The interviewer also used open-ended, non-judgemental prompts and avoided confirming or correcting participants’ accounts during the interviews.

Reflexive notes were maintained and interpretations were discussed within the research team to ensure findings remained grounded in participants’ accounts [16]. After each interview, reflexive memos were written to document the interviewer’s impressions, possible assumptions, emotional responses, and contextual observations. During analysis, the research team revisited these memos when interpreting sensitive findings related to professional hierarchy, psychological safety, and research practices perceived as procedural or symbolic. Disagreements or alternative interpretations were discussed to reduce the risk that the interviewer’s insider knowledge or prior expectations would dominate the analysis. Transcripts were not returned to all participants for correction, and formal participant validation of the final themes was not conducted. However, when clarification was needed during or shortly after interviews, the interviewer used follow-up questions or informal clarification to ensure accurate understanding of participants’ accounts. Emerging interpretations were examined within the research team rather than treated as participant-validated findings.

Reflexivity was therefore treated as an ongoing analytic practice rather than a single procedural step.

Reporting rigour

This study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ). A completed COREQ checklist is provided as Supplementary File 2. The checklist was used to review the reporting of the research team and reflexivity, study design, participant recruitment, data collection, data analysis, findings, and ethical considerations.

Results

Participant characteristics

Twenty clinical nurses participated in the study. Participants represented a wide range of clinical settings, including obstetrics, gynaecology, internal medicine, surgical specialties, outpatient services, and specialist nursing teams. Their clinical experience ranged from 1 to 39 years, reflecting the coexistence of early-career nurses and long-serving clinicians. Educational backgrounds ranged from vocational training to postgraduate degrees, and participants differed in exposure to research training, involvement in quality improvement initiatives, and current career progression pressures. This variation allowed comparison of how research engagement was shaped differently across career stage, educational preparation, professional role, and prior research exposure. In particular, early-career and postgraduate-trained nurses tended to describe stronger familiarity with research methods but less clinical authority, whereas experienced nurses and nurse managers often described stronger practical insight and organisational responsibility but more uneven confidence in methodological appraisal or research language. A detailed summary of participant characteristics is provided in Supplementary Table 1 .

Theme 1: nurses accessed research evidence, but understanding centred on conclusions and translation remained difficult

Participants consistently described regular exposure to research, guidelines, and professional evidence. Sources included departmental teaching sessions, conferences, databases, online resources, and problem-driven searches triggered by clinical uncertainty. Nurses often reported actively seeking information when faced with patient-management questions.

For example, one nurse manager described modifying patient education after consulting guideline evidence:

“I checked relevant guidelines and studies, adjusted our dietary record form, and then used it in patient education. It worked quite well.” (P1, nurse manager, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

Another nurse described consulting guidelines when clinical disagreement arose:

“I look up the latest guidelines and discuss them with doctors and the head nurse to see whether we can adjust our plan.” (P4, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

These accounts indicate that nurses were not disconnected from research information and often recognised the need to consult evidence.

However, understanding of research content was uneven. Participants commonly reported that they focused on conclusions or clinical recommendations, while methodological and statistical elements were difficult to interpret.

“I can usually understand the background and conclusions, but the statistics and detailed data are hard.”(P1, nurse manager, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

“I mainly read the abstract and conclusion. If the statistical model is complicated, I don’t go into detail.” (P6, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

A respiratory nurse similarly explained:

“If the conclusion is directly related to practice, I’ll use it, but I don’t always fully understand the methods.” (P20, nurse manager, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

Differences were visible across educational and career categories. Experienced nurses and nurse managers were often able to identify clinically relevant problems and judge whether evidence appeared useful for practice, but they more frequently described difficulty with statistical reasoning, methodological appraisal, or translating practical insight into formal research language. By contrast, postgraduate-trained or early-career nurses tended to report greater familiarity with research methods, but their ability to apply evidence was constrained by limited clinical experience, staffing conditions, or workflow realities.

Postgraduate-trained nurses showed greater methodological comprehension but still described practical barriers to application:

“I can understand the methods, but whether we can implement them depends on staffing and workflow.” (P19, staff nurse, master’s degree, < 5 years).

Participants also described a persistent gap between reading research and changing practice.

“Some studies look good, but after reading them I still don’t know exactly what to change for my patients.” (P5, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

“Some models are excellent, but with our staffing and workflow we simply can’t carry them out.” (P11, specialist nurse, master’s degree, 5–10 years).

Overall, these accounts suggest that access to research was widespread, but methodological interpretation and contextual translation remained challenging. This challenge differed by participant category: experienced nurses often encountered difficulty in formalising clinical insight into researchable questions, whereas postgraduate-trained nurses more often described difficulty in gaining sufficient practical authority and organisational conditions to implement what they understood methodologically. Conceptually, this theme shows that functional research literacy was not simply a matter of accessing research information. It involved the capacity to move from recognising evidence to appraising its methodological credibility and adapting it to local clinical conditions. The gap between reading conclusions and changing practice therefore represented an early interruption point in the development of research-related behaviour.

Theme 2: speaking up about research depended on hierarchy, professional identity, and psychological safety

Participants described that discussing research or evidence with colleagues was not simply a matter of knowledge but was shaped by interpersonal dynamics, hierarchy, and perceived safety.

In smaller clinical discussions directly linked to patient care, nurses often felt able to introduce evidence.

“If it’s directly related to the patient, I’ll check the literature and discuss it with the doctor and head nurse.”(P4, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

“If the evidence is clear, I’ll still try to discuss it with the doctors.”(P9, staff nurse, master’s degree, 5–10 years).

However, in formal meetings or hierarchical settings, many nurses described becoming cautious.

“If leaders are present or it involves existing procedures, I don’t easily raise objections because I worry it will seem like causing trouble.” (P5, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

“When senior doctors are there, many people speak less.” (P14, nurse manager, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

The effects of hierarchy also varied across professional roles. Staff nurses were more likely to describe research-related speech as dependent on permission, encouragement, or the perceived openness of senior colleagues. Nurse managers had more formal authority to organise discussion or initiate projects, but they also had to balance research ideas against departmental routines, medical hierarchies, workload pressures, and institutional expectations. Thus, positional authority did not remove the influence of hierarchy; rather, it changed how nurses negotiated it.

Participants also described a tension between educational background and clinical authority. Postgraduate nurses worried their limited experience would reduce credibility:

“Sometimes I have evidence, but I worry others will think I don’t have enough clinical experience.” (P19, staff nurse, master’s degree, < 5 years).

Conversely, experienced nurses worried they lacked research language:

“I know where the problems are, but I’m not good at expressing them in research terms.” (P3, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

This contrast was particularly important. Postgraduate-trained nurses could possess evidence or methodological knowledge but still feel uncertain about whether their suggestions would be accepted in practice. Experienced nurses, in contrast, often had strong experiential authority and deep knowledge of clinical problems, but did not always feel able to frame these problems in research terminology. Research-related dialogue therefore depended on the alignment between educational capital, clinical credibility, and psychological safety.

These accounts suggest that research interaction in clinical teams was shaped less by individual competence than by social positioning, professional legitimacy, and organisational culture. This theme extends the notion of interactive research literacy by showing that the ability to discuss evidence was not only a communication skill. It was a socially situated capacity shaped by whether nurses were perceived as credible speakers, whether the team climate allowed questioning, and whether hierarchy made research-related speech safe or risky. Psychological safety therefore functioned as a condition through which research knowledge could become speakable and actionable in practice.

Theme 3: nurses distinguished meaningful research from “formalistic” projects based on clinical relevance

Participants consistently described evaluating research based on whether it genuinely improved patient care. Studies or projects perceived as directly addressing clinical problems were seen as meaningful and worth effort.

“If research really improves patient outcomes, we’re willing to invest time because it feels meaningful.” (P1, nurse manager, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

Contextual feasibility was central to these judgements:

“We sometimes find that a model doesn’t fit our patients or our conditions.” (P11, specialist nurse, master’s degree, 5–10 years).

At the same time, nurses frequently described encountering projects perceived as institution-driven or performed primarily for inspections, performance assessments, or promotion requirements.

“Sometimes projects are done just because inspections require them.” (P5, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

“Some projects start strongly but later no one continues them.” (P6, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

A nurse manager explained:

“Sometimes the focus is on reports and materials rather than real care changes.” (P14, nurse manager, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

The basis for judging research value also differed by role. Staff nurses tended to judge projects primarily through their immediate relevance to patient care, workload, and feasibility within daily routines. Nurse managers, while also emphasising clinical value, more explicitly described the tension between genuine care improvement and organisational requirements such as inspections, performance assessment, or documentation. Postgraduate-trained nurses often attempted to reconcile these two demands by connecting required projects with real clinical problems.

Despite recognising these tensions, participants often described navigating a pragmatic balance between institutional demands and clinical value.

“If it’s required by leadership we still complete it, but we try to connect it to real practice.” (P19, staff nurse, master’s degree, < 5 years).

In this analysis, “formalistic” is used as an interpretive label rather than a direct participant quotation. It refers to projects that participants described as being organised primarily for inspection requirements, reports, materials, performance assessment, or symbolic completion, with limited connection to patient outcomes or sustainable practice change. These accounts suggest that nurses were not rejecting research itself; rather, they were questioning whether some research or improvement activities carried clinical and moral legitimacy when they were experienced mainly as administrative compliance.

These accounts indicate that nurses actively evaluated research agendas rather than passively adopting them. Across participant categories, the distinction between meaningful and “formalistic” research functioned as a form of critical judgement: nurses assessed not only whether a project was methodologically or administratively acceptable, but whether it was clinically legitimate and worth sustained professional effort. This finding suggests that critical research literacy included moral and organisational judgement, not merely methodological appraisal. Nurses assessed whether research activity aligned with patient care, professional values, and feasible clinical change. In this sense, their scepticism toward “formalistic” projects reflected an active judgement about the legitimacy of research, rather than resistance to research itself.

Theme 4: translating clinical problems into research unfolded as a fragile and interruptible pathway

Participants described frequently noticing clinical problems or improvement opportunities.

“Every day we see things that could be improved, like documentation or education processes.” (P6, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

“Some problems are very obvious in practice, but turning them into a project is difficult.” (P9, staff nurse, master’s degree, 5–10 years).

The nature of this difficulty differed across categories. Experienced nurses and nurse managers were often well positioned to recognise recurring clinical problems because of their long-term exposure to patient care and workflow management. However, they did not always have the time, methodological confidence, or writing support needed to convert these observations into formal proposals. Early-career or postgraduate-trained nurses were more familiar with research procedures, but they often lacked the clinical authority, team resources, or organisational leverage required to move an idea forward.

Participants portrayed research initiation as a multi-step pathway: recognising a problem, expressing an idea, gaining support, developing a plan, and sustaining implementation. Projects often stalled early in this sequence.

“We sometimes discuss problems, but no one actually writes the proposal, and then it just fades.” (P5, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

“At first everyone agrees it’s important, but once it comes to writing protocols, ethics, or data collection, it slows down.” (P4, staff nurse, bachelor’s degree, 11–20 years).

Even when projects began, clinical workload and limited organisational support often disrupted continuity.

“When clinical work becomes busy, research gets pushed back.” (P11, specialist nurse, master’s degree, 5–10 years).

“If there isn’t fixed time or support, enthusiasm gradually drops.” (P10, nurse manager, master’s degree, 11–20 years).

Participants noted that projects rarely ended formally; instead they gradually disappeared.

“Some projects are not officially stopped; people just stop mentioning them, and gradually no one follows them anymore.” (P14, nurse manager, bachelor’s degree, 21–30 years).

These descriptions suggest that research engagement was not a single decision but a fragile process requiring sustained organisational and relational support. The fragility of this pathway was therefore category-specific as well as organisational: different groups encountered different interruption points, including lack of methodological language, limited authority to mobilise others, insufficient protected time, weak mentorship continuity, and difficulty sustaining collective commitment. Analytically, this theme shows that research engagement depended on the conversion of insight into collective and sustained action. Even when nurses recognised problems and possessed motivation, projects could disappear if organisational opportunity, leadership endorsement, and practical support were insufficient. The pathway from clinical problem recognition to research action was therefore recursive and vulnerable rather than progressive and secure.

Overall synthesis

Across themes, nurses’ research engagement appeared as a situated process shaped by interpretation, interaction, evaluation, and feasibility. Participants frequently accessed research and recognised clinical problems, but their understanding often centred on conclusions rather than methodological appraisal. Acting on research required navigating hierarchical relationships, assessing whether projects were clinically meaningful, and moving through a sequence of practical steps vulnerable to disruption. These dimensions were analytically connected rather than separate: how nurses interpreted research shaped whether they felt able to discuss it, how research-related dialogue was positioned within teams influenced how ideas were judged, and these judgements affected whether clinical problems could be converted into action.

Between-category analysis further showed that research engagement was shaped by different combinations of literacy, authority, and organisational position. Experienced nurses often possessed rich clinical insight but had difficulty translating experiential knowledge into research language or formal study designs. Postgraduate-trained and early-career nurses often had stronger research preparation but less clinical authority and workplace credibility to influence practice. Nurse managers occupied an intermediary position: they could mobilise teams and connect projects to organisational priorities, but they also had to manage workload, institutional expectations, and the risk that research became procedural rather than clinically meaningful.

Taken together, these patterns informed the development of a heuristic behavioural pathway model of nurses’ research engagement. The model was derived from the four empirical themes and is intended to make visible how research-related behaviour may develop, stall, regress, or be reactivated in clinical settings. Theme 1 highlighted the interpretive dimension of engagement, showing how nurses accessed research and made sense of evidence, often in conclusion-oriented rather than methodologically analytic ways. Theme 2 illuminated the relational dimension, showing that speaking about research depended on hierarchy, professional legitimacy, and psychological safety. Theme 3 captured the evaluative dimension, in which nurses distinguished clinically meaningful research from projects perceived as procedural or formalistic. Theme 4 demonstrated the action dimension, showing that translating clinical problems into research or improvement activity was fragile, interruptible, and difficult to sustain without organisational support. The model should not be read as a fixed or universal sequence. Rather, it represents an interpretive abstraction of how research engagement may emerge, stall, regress, or be reactivated in clinical settings. Nurses may move from interpreting evidence or recognising clinical problems to speaking about research ideas, evaluating their clinical meaning, and attempting research or improvement action. However, this movement is contingent on professional positioning, psychological safety, leadership support, workload, and available organisational resources.

Figure 1 presents this heuristic behavioural pathway model in visual form. The figure depicts research engagement as a recursive process involving four interrelated moments: interpreting research or recognising clinical problems, positioning oneself to speak or negotiate within the team, evaluating the clinical and organisational legitimacy of research ideas, and attempting research or improvement action. These moments are connected by feedback loops rather than a one-way sequence. Nurses may return to earlier moments when methodological uncertainty, hierarchical constraints, weak psychological safety, limited time, or insufficient support interrupt action. Projects may therefore move forward, stall, fade gradually, or become reactivated when new support, leadership attention, or clinical urgency emerges. The interruption points shown in the figure further clarify where and why the pathway may become fragile: early interruptions may arise from methodological uncertainty, conclusion-oriented reading, and difficulty adapting evidence; relational interruptions may arise from hierarchical constraints, weak psychological safety, and limited credibility to speak; and implementation interruptions may arise from workload pressure, insufficient protected time, limited mentorship, or limited organisational support.

Feedback loops and interruption points indicate that ideas may stall, fade, regress, or become reactivated under changing workplace conditions. The interruption points correspond to different moments in the pathway: early interruptions may arise from methodological uncertainty, conclusion-oriented reading, and difficulty adapting evidence; relational interruptions may arise from hierarchical constraints, weak psychological safety, and limited credibility to speak; and implementation interruptions may arise from workload pressure, insufficient protected time, limited mentorship, or limited organisational support. The model is intended as a heuristic abstraction derived from the empirical findings, rather than a deterministic or universal sequence

These findings suggest that research participation in clinical nursing is not simply an individual attribute but an emergent process shaped by how nurses understand research, how they are positioned within teams, how they evaluate the value of projects, and whether organisational conditions allow action to be sustained. The development of research-related behaviour therefore depended not only on whether nurses had research knowledge, but also on whether their career stage, role identity, and organisational position allowed that knowledge to be expressed, negotiated, and sustained in practice. Thus, the behavioural pathway model offers a process-oriented explanation of nurses’ research engagement while preserving the complexity, contingency, and non-linearity described in participants’ accounts.

Discussion

Overall interpretation

This study examined how clinical nurses in China understand research, decide whether to apply it, and navigate the conditions that enable or constrain research-related behaviour in everyday clinical work. Across participants, research engagement could not be explained simply by differences in knowledge, training, or individual motivation. Instead, it emerged as a situated and socially mediated process shaped by how nurses interpreted research, how they were positioned within professional hierarchies, how they evaluated the legitimacy and usefulness of projects, and whether organisational conditions allowed actions to be initiated and sustained. The findings indicate that research engagement was not only enabled or constrained by whether nurses possessed knowledge, but also by whether they were socially authorised to use that knowledge within clinical relationships.

These findings therefore suggest that research participation in clinical nursing is better understood not as a discrete competence to be acquired, but as a practice that develops through the interaction between interpretive literacy, professional identity, organisational expectations, and structural feasibility [18]. In this sense, engagement with research appears less as a technical skill and more as a form of workplace behaviour that is negotiated within specific institutional contexts [19]. These interpretations should be read in relation to the organisational context of Chinese tertiary hospitals, where professional hierarchy, administrative evaluation, and limited formal research roles shape how research engagement is made possible or constrained.

Theoretical interpretation: integrating research literacy and COM-B

To account for this pattern, we interpreted the themes through an integrated conceptual lens combining research literacy and the COM-B behavioural system [14, 15]. Rather than treating the findings as a list of barriers, this approach helped develop a heuristic interpretation of how research-related behaviour may emerge, stall, regress, or be sustained in clinical settings. Figure 1 presents the heuristic behavioural pathway model derived from the analysis. Supplementary Table 2 provides the detailed theme-to-theory mapping, and Supplementary Table 3 provides additional details supporting the behavioural pathway model shown in Fig. 1.Together, these materials enhance analytic transparency and clarify how empirical findings informed the theoretical interpretation.

Within this interpretation, functional literacy primarily shaped how nurses made sense of research, often supporting conclusion-oriented reading while limiting methodological appraisal. Interactive literacy influenced whether nurses felt able to speak, negotiate, or introduce evidence within hierarchical teams. Critical literacy informed how they judged the authenticity, relevance, and practical value of research initiatives. At the same time, the COM-B dimensions clarified why some ideas progressed into action while others dissipated: capability supported interpretation, opportunity enabled expression and implementation, and reflective motivation shaped commitment to particular projects [20]. Importantly, these dimensions did not operate as discrete stages. They overlapped and interacted, so that limited opportunity could weaken motivation, insufficient capability could restrict participation in dialogue, and lack of psychological safety could prevent otherwise capable nurses from acting on research ideas.

Taken together, this integrated reading suggests that research engagement develops through a recursive and contingent process in which interpretation, professional positioning, evaluative judgement, and action attempts continually influence one another. Behaviour therefore depends not only on what nurses know, but on whether their knowledge can be enacted within the social and structural conditions of practice [21]. The behavioural pathway model should therefore be understood as a heuristic abstraction rather than a definitive sequence of stages. It illustrates possible movement between interpretation, dialogue, evaluation, action, interruption, and reactivation, while recognising that nurses’ research engagement may be discontinuous, fragile, and shaped by changing workplace conditions. While COM-B helped explain how capability, opportunity, and motivation shaped research-related action, the findings also required attention to the social production of professional legitimacy and psychological safety within hierarchical clinical teams. These issues are further discussed in relation to organisational and sociological perspectives below.

Relation to existing literature

Our findings extend previous work on evidence-based practice by demonstrating that access to research information alone does not ensure implementation [22]. Similar to international studies showing that nurses often feel confident reading conclusions but less confident appraising methods [23, 24], participants in this study frequently relied on conclusion-focused interpretation. However, our data suggest that this pattern is not simply a deficit in individual skill but reflects workflow realities that encourage time-efficient reading strategies.

The study also reinforces implementation science findings that organisational climate and leadership engagement strongly influence whether evidence is adopted [25, 26]. Participants described carefully assessing whether speaking about research was socially safe and professionally legitimate, echoing research on psychological safety in healthcare teams [27]. Importantly, our findings add nuance by showing that legitimacy signals are transmitted not only through interpersonal relationships but through institutional narratives about what counts as “real work.” In this study, hierarchy did not simply suppress research dialogue; rather, it shaped who could speak, when evidence could be introduced, and whose interpretation of evidence was likely to be taken seriously. Professional legitimacy was produced through the interaction of clinical seniority, educational background, managerial position, and perceived practical credibility. This helps explain why postgraduate-trained nurses could feel uncertain despite stronger methodological preparation, while experienced nurses could recognise important clinical problems but struggle to express them in research language. Psychological safety therefore operated not only as a general team climate, but as a relational condition that determined whether research knowledge could enter clinical discussion without being perceived as inappropriate, disruptive, or lacking practical authority.

Furthermore, the distinction participants made between meaningful research and “formalistic” projects reflects wider concerns about performance requirements, promotion-driven publication expectations, and metric-based evaluation systems in healthcare and higher education [28]. Rather than rejecting research, nurses appeared to engage in pragmatic moral reasoning, attempting to reconcile institutional demands with patient-centred values — a pattern consistent with sociological analyses of professional practice [29]. This finding should also be understood in relation to the Chinese tertiary hospital context. In such settings, research engagement may be shaped by professional title promotion, departmental performance assessment, quality-improvement inspections, and administratively driven project requirements. These structures can create opportunities for nurses to participate in research, but they may also encourage research activity to be experienced as documentation, compliance, or symbolic performance when it is weakly connected to clinical change.

In this discussion, “formalistic” research is used as an analytic label for this pattern rather than as a direct participant quotation. Interpreted through the lens of audit culture and performativity, it refers to research or improvement activity that is made visible and accountable through documents, indicators, reports, or project completion, but is not necessarily experienced by nurses as producing meaningful change in care. Participants’ criticism of such projects therefore reflected a professional concern about the displacement of clinical value by performative accountability. Their responses also show that accountability mechanisms are not inherently negative: when organisational requirements were connected to genuine clinical problems, nurses tried to transform required projects into meaningful improvement work. The issue was not accountability itself, but whether accountability strengthened or weakened the relationship between research activity and professional care values.

Finally, the staged and fragile pathway from recognising a clinical issue to sustaining a project resonates with implementation models describing innovation as a process involving initiation, adoption, and maintenance phases [30]. Our findings extend this work by illustrating how such processes unfold within nursing practice and how projects may fade gradually rather than fail abruptly.

Implications for education and organisational practice

This study highlights that research participation in clinical nursing depends not only on what professionals learn, but on whether workplace environments allow that learning to be enacted [31]. The findings therefore suggest that strengthening research engagement requires alignment between educational strategies and organisational conditions.

From an educational perspective, the results indicate that teaching research methods or evidence appraisal alone is insufficient. Research engagement appears to function as a socially negotiated workplace practice rather than a purely individual competence. This observation aligns with theories of learning transfer and communities of practice, which emphasise that professional capability develops through participation in real organisational contexts rather than through knowledge acquisition alone [32].

Educational strategies may therefore need to move beyond presenting research as a technical skill and instead address three interconnected domains: interpretive literacy, dialogical participation, and contextual adaptation. Embedding research learning within authentic clinical projects, providing mentorship in navigating institutional structures, and fostering safe environments for evidence-based dialogue may be as important as methodological training itself.

At the same time, the findings indicate that organisational signals strongly influence whether research engagement is sustained. Leadership framing appeared to shape whether research was perceived as meaningful professional work or as administrative compliance. When institutions positioned research as integral to clinical practice and provided visible support, nurses described greater willingness to participate. Conversely, when research was associated primarily with audits or promotion requirements, engagement tended to become procedural.

Sustained research activity also depended on structural support, including protected time, mentorship continuity, and mechanisms linking frontline observations to decision-making processes. Without these supports, even well-motivated initiatives were vulnerable to gradual decline. These implications are particularly relevant in Chinese tertiary hospitals, where most nurses remain primarily employed in clinical roles and formally differentiated research positions, such as clinical research nurses or nurse scientists, are still limited. Under these conditions, research engagement is often added to routine clinical work rather than embedded within protected roles or institutionalised research pathways. Hospitals seeking to strengthen nurses’ research engagement may therefore need to move beyond individual training and establish organisational mechanisms such as recognised research time, mentorship structures, departmental research facilitation roles, and channels through which frontline clinical problems can be converted into feasible research or quality-improvement projects.

Taken together, these findings suggest that developing research-active clinicians cannot be achieved through education alone. Instead, it requires coordinated attention to training design, professional identity formation, and organisational infrastructure so that research-oriented behaviour can be both learned and enacted within everyday clinical work, particularly in clinical systems where nurses’ research responsibilities are not yet supported by clearly differentiated roles or protected organisational pathways.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study lies in the use of qualitative interviews to examine research engagement as it unfolds within real clinical contexts. This approach allowed exploration of how nurses interpret research, negotiate professional positioning, and respond to organisational conditions—processes that would be difficult to capture using survey methods. The purposive maximum-variation sampling further enhanced the design by enabling comparison across departments, roles, career stages, and educational pathways, thereby supporting conceptual transferability.

The study also has limitations. First, the study was conducted in a single tertiary teaching hospital in China, and all participants were recruited from the same institution. The findings should therefore not be interpreted as statistically generalisable to all nurses, hospitals, or national contexts. Instead, their relevance lies in theoretical transferability to settings with similar features, such as hierarchical clinical structures, limited formal research nursing roles, promotion-related research expectations, and strong administrative evaluation systems. Readers should assess transferability by considering the extent to which their own institutional and professional contexts resemble these conditions. Participation was voluntary, which may have resulted in a sample more reflective about research than the wider workforce. In addition, transcripts were not returned to all participants, and formal participant validation of the final themes was not conducted. This may have limited opportunities for participants to comment on the researchers’ interpretations. To mitigate this limitation, the analysis relied on repeated engagement with transcripts, reflexive memo writing, team discussion, and careful comparison between data extracts and interpretive claims. Finally, only nurses were interviewed; future studies including educators, physicians, and managers could further clarify how interprofessional and organisational dynamics shape research engagement.

Conclusion

Overall, the study positions research participation as a socially situated professional practice rather than an optional academic skill. By showing how literacy, identity, and organisational conditions interact to shape research-related behaviour, the findings provide a framework for understanding how education strategies and workplace structures can jointly support more sustained research-informed clinical practice. The proposed behavioural pathway model is intended as a heuristic tool for understanding this process, rather than a fixed sequence through which all nurses necessarily move.
