Methylation of LINE-1 and Alu repetitive sequence in keloid
Authors: Piyawan Prabsattru, Tanchanok Nakbua, Suthida Sengphairogh, Nakarin Kitkumthorn, Jiraroch Meevassana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Keloid is a trauma-induced fibroproliferative condition characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and aberrant keloid fibroblast activation, leading to physical, psychological, functional, and cosmetic impairments. This study investigates DNA methylation alterations at Long Interspersed Nuclear Element-1 (LINE-1) and Alu repetitive elements in keloid tissues compared to normal skin tissues. Methylation levels and patterns were analyzed in keloid (n = 38) and normal skin tissues (n = 32). The results revealed significantly lower LINE-1 (P < 0.0001) and Alu (P = 0.0147) methylation levels in keloids, with hypomethylation inversely correlated with chronological age. Younger individuals exhibited higher methylation levels compared to older individuals. These findings offer critical insights into the epigenetic mechanisms underlying keloid formation and progression, providing a foundation for developing epigenetic-based therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17646-2.

Introduction

Keloid is a dermal fibroproliferative condition brought on by excessive extracellular membrane buildup and aberrant fibroblast activity1. It is characterized by its raised, thick, and often shiny appearance, usually arising from a site of injury due to abnormal wound healing2,3. Abnormal growth creates undesirable symptoms such as itching and soreness, particularly in the joint area. As a result, the joint loses its usual function. Furthermore, it affects the aesthetics and appearance of the patient, as well as their mental state. Currently, treatments of keloids give unsatisfactory outcomes, whether as preventive or therapy after keloids. It also found a recurrence even with combination therapy, such as surgery with injected steroids, post-surgical radiation therapy, and pressure therapy4.

Transposable elements (TEs), such as LINE-1 and Alu, make up about 34% of the human genome and can influence gene expression. Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, regulate their activity5. Methylation typically represses genes (hypermethylation), while hypomethylation enhances expression. Other epigenetic mechanisms include non-coding RNAs and histone modification6–9. Numerous studies have shown that changes in DNA methylation, such as hyper-methylation and hypo-methylation frequently found in Alu and LINE-1 elements, contribute to genomic instability. DNA methylation alterations are linked to a variety of genetic diseases and cancers, including Apert syndrome, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, hemophilia, neurofibromatosis, cystic fibrosis, β-thalassemia, and hypercholesterolemia, as well as breast and colon cancers10–12.

These epigenetic modifications affect critical pathways involved in cell proliferation, apoptosis, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Furthermore, keloids exhibit tumor-like properties including uncontrolled growth potential, local tissue invasion, and high recurrence rates following treatment, suggesting shared molecular mechanisms with malignant processes13,14. Epigenetic alterations, including abnormal DNA methylation, histone modifications, miRNAs, and lncRNAs, have been implicated in keloid development by disrupting normal skin regulation. These changes may clarify keloid pathogenesis and support future therapeutic strategies15,16.

This study focuses on investigating the methylation status of LINE-1 and Alu repetitive elements in keloid pathogenesis. Given their substantial genomic representation and known association with genomic instability, we hypothesize that altered methylation of these elements contributes to the dysregulated wound healing response observed in keloids. By comparing methylation patterns between keloid and normal skin tissues, we aim to provide novel insights into the epigenetic mechanisms underlying keloid formation and identify potential therapeutic targets for this challenging condition. Our findings may contribute to the development of more effective treatment strategies for this clinically problematic fibroproliferative disorder.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The current study was approved by Chulalongkorn University’s Faculty of Medicine’s Ethical Committee under IRB number 1506/2023. All samples were obtained between January 2023 and January 2024 in the Plastic Surgery Department at Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Thailand. Keloid specimens (treatment-naïve patients) were procured from a cohort of 38 patients, comprising 12 males and 26 females, with a mean age of 36. Similarly, normal skin specimens were obtained from the upper eyelid regions of 32 plastic surgery patients undergoing blepharoplasty composed of 14 males and 18 females, with a mean age of 48, with no prior history of scarring or trauma at the excision sites. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study, and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

DNA extraction and bisulfite treatment

Small pieces of tissue were cut, and it was digested by incubating it in a water bath at 50 °C overnight with 500 µl of extraction solution containing 20 mg/ml proteinase K (Gproan Biotech, Beijing, China), lysis buffer (0.5 M EDTA, pH 8.0 and 5 M NaCl), and 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich). The mixture was extracted using a phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) solution, and it was centrifuged for 15 min at 4˚C at 14,000 rpm. The upper aqueous phase was carefully collected, moved to a different tube, precipitated with 100% isopropanol, and centrifuged for 15 min at 4 ˚C at 14,000 rpm. Following removing the supernatant, the DNA was centrifuged for 5 min at 14,000 rpm and twice washed at 4˚C with 70% ethanol. The DNA to was first allowed air dry before resuspending in 20 µl of an elution buffer and stored at −20 ˚C. The EZ DNA Methylation-GoldTM kit (Zymo Research, Orange, CA, USA) was then used to treat 500 ng of genomic DNA with sodium bisulfite by the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of COBRA LINE-1 and COBRA Alu utilizing the COBRA technique

DNA modified with sodium bisulfite was amplified using the LINE-1 primer; the sequence of forward is 5’-CCGTAAGGGGTTAGGGAGTTTTT-3’, and the reverse is 5’-RTAAAACCCTCCRAACCAAATATAAA-3’. Similarly, for the Alu primer, the sequence of forward is 5’-GGYGYGGTGGTTTAYGTTTGTAA-3’, and the reverse is 5’-CTAACTTTTTATATTTTTAATAAAAACRAAATTTCACCA-3’, where R represents adenine (A) and guanine (G).

The LINE-1 PCR was performed using the following conditions: 45 s of denaturation at 95 °C, followed by 35 cycles; 45 s of annealing at 53 °C, and 45 s of extension at 72 °C. TaqI-v2 in CutSmart® Buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and TasI in Buffer B (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were then used to digest the PCR products overnight at 65 °C. Likewise, Alu PCR, the following conditions: 45 s of denaturation at 95 °C, followed by 35 cycles; 45 s of annealing at 57 °C, and 45 s of extension at 72 °C, followed by digestion with TaqI-v2 in CutSmart® Buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) at 65 °C overnight. SyBr Green (Gelstar, Lonza, USA) was used to stain the 8% polyacrylamide gel after separation17 were demonstrated in Supplementary.

Band intensities were then identified and measured using an Azure c300 imaging system (Azure Biosystem Inc., Dublin, CA, USA) and ImageQuanT software (Amersham Biosciences, Little Chalfont, UK). All intensity values were normalized using: Normalized Intensity = [(Mean of Positive Control)/(Individual Gel Positive Control)] x (Raw Intensity of each sample). Methylation analysis

The methylation percentage formulas for Alu and LINE-1 elements are derived from the COBRA (Combined Bisulfite Restriction Analysis) method. This technique involves bisulfite conversion, PCR amplification, and digestion with a methylation-sensitive restriction enzyme like TaqI, which only cuts DNA at TCGA sequences if the cytosine is methylated. After digestion, DNA fragments of different lengths appear on a gel depending on the methylation status of CpG sites. For Alu, six possible fragment patterns (A–F) correspond to fully unmethylated (uCuC), partially methylated (mCuC or uCmC), and fully methylated (mCmC) states. These band patterns are used to calculate total and individual methylation percentages based on their relative intensity. The key idea behind the formulas is that only methylated CpGs preserve the enzyme recognition site after bisulfite conversion, allowing cleavage and producing shorter fragments. Therefore, methylated DNA results in more digested bands, which are placed in the numerator of the methylation calculation. For instance, in Alu methylation, the bands labeled E and B represent methylated states and are used to calculate total methylation as a fraction of all fragments (including unmethylated). This method allows semi-quantitative assessment of global DNA methylation in repetitive elements, providing a cost-effective alternative to whole-genome methylation sequencing. For LINE-1 larger undigested bands (e.g., 160 bp) represent unmethylated DNA (enzyme could not cut). Smaller fragments (e.g., 98 bp, 80 bp) represent digested products = methylated CpG sites were intact and recognizable by enzyme. Therefore, in %methylation formula, the digested fragments (indicative of methylation) go in the numerator18–22.

The following method was used to determine band intensities for the methylation of LINE-1 (Fig. S1A) using the product sizes (160, 98, 80, and 62 bp) to calculate the following formula: The total methylation level% of LINE-1 = (C + A)/(C + A + A + B + D) × 100: where A band intensity is 160 bp/160, B is 98 bp/94, C is 80 bp/79, and D is 62 bp/6218. Similar to the methylation of Alu, the product sizes (133, 90, 75, 58, and 43 bp) (Fig. S1B) were used to compute band intensities using the following formula: the total methylation level% of Alu = (E + B)/(E + B + 2 A + C + D) × 100, where A has a band intensity of 133 bp/133, B is 58 bp/58, C is 75 bp/73, D is 90 bp/90, and E is 43 bp/41. To correct for inter-assay variance, use the same positive control16,23,24.

Statistical analysis

For all statistical studies, GraphPad Prism version 10.2 was used. To compare the methylation levels of keloids and normal skin tissues, an independent sample t-test (two-tailed t-test) was employed. Shapiro-Wilk tests confirmed normal distribution for LINE-1, Alu methylation level and age. Pearson’s correlation was therefore applied. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, and the G*Power 3.1 program was used to estimate the sample size for the target population.

Results

Differences in DNA methylation levels for LINE-1 and Alu were observed between keloid tissues and normal skin tissues

The COBRA-LINE-1 and COBRA-Alu studies showed different levels of LINE-1 and Alu methylation, as shown in Fig. 1. These include patterns of tissue from keloid patients (n = 38) compared to normal skin tissues (n = 32). Figure 1a illustrates the trends of LINE-1 methylation level. The hypermethylated at both CpGs (mCmC) was slightly lower in keloid tissues (24.7%) than in normal skin (29.2%), but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.0584). the partially methylated uCmC pattern showed no significant difference between keloid and normal skin tissues (P = 0.5687). However, the partially methylated mCuC pattern was significantly lower in keloid tissues (30.2%) compared to normal skin (35.6%) (P = 0.0210). The percentage of mCuC methylation in keloid tissues is 30.2%, whereas the percentage in normal skin tissues is 35.6%. Additionally, the fully hypomethylated LINE-1 pattern (uCuC) was significantly more common in keloid tissues compared to normal skin (30.2% vs. 24.9%, P = 0.0011). As for the levels of Alu methylation and patterns in keloids from the patients (Fig. 1b), a thorough analysis of each pattern revealed that the hypermethylated at both CpGs (mCmC) exhibited no statistically significant difference when compared to normal tissue samples (P = 0.3576). The percentage of hypermethylation in keloid tissues was 9.8%, whereas the normal skin tissue was recorded at 11%. Additionally, the proportion of partial methylation associated with the uCmC pattern for Alu was found to be significantly diminished in keloid tissues relative to normal skin tissues (P = 0.0016), with uCmC methylation percentages of 30.4% and 35.7%, respectively. However, the percentages of the mCuC pattern for Alu in both keloid tissues and normal skin tissues (P = 0.8213) did not exhibit a statistically significant difference. Moreover, the incidence of hypomethylation at both CpGs (uCuC) about Alu was markedly elevated in keloid tissues in comparison to normal skin tissues (P = 0.0127). The corresponding observed hypomethylation percentages for keloid and normal skin tissues were 31.7% and 28.9%, respectively. Additionally, the proportion of partial methylation associated with the uCmC pattern for Alu was found to be significantly diminished in keloid tissues relative to normal skin tissues (P = 0.0016), with uCmC methylation percentages of 30.4% and 35.7%, respectively. However, the percentages of the mCuC pattern for Alu in both keloid tissues and normal skin tissues (P = 0.8213) did not exhibit a statistically significant difference.

Gender-based differences in methylation level of LINE-1 and Alu

The differences in methylation level of LINE-1 between male and female patients were not statistically significant in tissues from both keloid and normal skin tissues (P > 0.05; Fig. 2a). In contrast, Alu methylation levels showed a significant difference between male and female patients in normal skin tissues (P = 0.0018; Fig. 2b), while no significant difference was observed in keloid tissues (P = 0.1162; Fig. 2b).

The correlation between age and LINE-1 and Alu methylation level

DNA methylation in both LINE-1 and Alu was inversely correlated with age at the visit (Fig. 3). The decrease in LINE-1 methylation was not statistically significant in both tissue from patients with keloids (r = −0.2713, P = 0.0994) and normal skin tissues (r = −0.2158, P = 0.2354) (Fig. 3 a and b), while the decrease in Alu methylation was significant in tissue from both patients with keloids (r = −0.3705, P = 0.0220) and normal skin tissues (r = −0.4856, P = 0.0048) (Fig. 3c and d).

Discussion

Although keloids are considered benign tumors, their clinical appearance frequently leads to them being misdiagnosed as malignant ones. Usually elevated, amorphous growths, these scars can expand into surrounding tissues and go beyond the limits of the initial injury site. Keloids can develop in people who have had any kind of skin trauma, such as surgery, tattoos, piercings, bug bites, immunizations, or any condition that causes inflammation of the skin25,26. Keloids can be treated surgically, not surgically, or with combination therapies that include laser therapy, cryotherapy, radiation therapy, topical steroids, and surgical removal25. There is disagreement over the best, most uniform method for permanently reducing aberrant scarring, even though these therapies often have a high success rate and minimal recurrence. This is brought about by inconsistent treatment objectives, inadequate follow-up care, and a dearth of prospective research. Additionally, Intralesional corticosteroid injections can also cause osteoporosis, which is another negative effect of various treatments27. A study Lawera, et al.28 reported a meta-analysis focusing on keloid intralesional excision (KILE) and evaluated its efficacy in preventing recurrence among 608 keloids. The study found that while surgical excision alone often results in high recurrence rates, KILE reduces the likelihood of recurrence. However, recurrence rates for keloids are still somewhat high, even with the use of numerous adjuvant therapies in addition to surgical techniques. These results imply that although potential therapies are available, no one strategy is the most effective way to stop keloid recurrence. Furthermore, Lv, et al.29 DNA methylation, an epigenetic alteration that involves adding a methyl group to cytosine residues in DNA, is important for gene regulation and could be connected to the migration, differentiation, apoptosis, and proliferation of keloid-derived fibroblasts. Therefore, we intended to verify our hypothesis that the development of keloids may be linked to epigenetic modifications by comparing the amounts and patterns of LINE-1 and Alu methylation between keloid tissues and normal skin tissues. Using the COBRA approach, we investigated LINE-1 and Alu methylation patterns in keloid and normal skin tissue samples.

According to our research, keloid tissue samples had significantly lower methylation levels of LINE-1 and Alu than normal skin tissues. When each pattern was analyzed, the levels of LINE-1 methylation in partially methylated (mCuC) patterns decreased significantly, while the uCuC pattern increased significantly. In contrast, the mCmC and uCmC patterns showed no significant differences in tissues from keloid compared to normal skin tissues. Similarly, the methylation of Alu in partially methylated (uCmC) patterns decreased significantly, while the uCuC pattern increased significantly. However, the mCmC and mCuC patterns showed no significant differences in tissues from keloid compared to normal skin tissues.

We analyzed the methylation levels of males and females to see if sex affected the methylation levels of LINE-1 and Alu. The participants were divided into two groups: those with keloids and those with normal skin tissues. This finding aligns with previous studies that have indicated sex does not impact LINE-1 and Alu methylation30.

There is increasing confirmation that as we age, DNA methylation tends to decrease throughout the genome31–34. Our study confirms this finding, showing that in both keloid tissues and normal skin tissues, LINE-1 and Alu methylation levels are negatively correlated with age. This correlation is consistent with the associations observed between aging23,24,35 LINE-1 and Alu hypomethylation, and cancer19,36. Furthermore, Lange, et al.37 found that global hypomethylation in lung fibroblasts was inversely correlated with advanced chronological age. They also found that in a cohort of older, mostly white men from North America, LINE-1 hypomethylation was linked to a more rapid decline in lung function, while Alu hypomethylation was linked to lower lung function. Additionally, we found that Alu methylation changed more significantly than LINE-1 methylation, which is consistent with results from several previous investigations. Bollati, et al.38 also emphasize that average Alu methylation decreased noticeably with age, while the association with LINE-1 methylation was less strong and only marginally significant. Furthermore, LINE-1 methylation and age did not correlate in other investigations39,40.

The main modulators of physiological DNA methylation levels are DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs)41. Research has indicated that DNMT activity declines with age, leading to a reduction in global methylation35,42. According to the review study, Reduced DNMT activity may play a significant role in the aging-related reduction of global DNA methylation. Therefore, we would like to investigate the relationship between aging and changes in DNMT activity. This study may offer more proof of the processes behind age-related alterations by integrating the findings for LINE-1 methylation and Alu. LINE-1 and Alu hypomethylation may contribute to keloid pathogenesis by promoting genomic instability and dysregulating gene expression involved in fibroblast proliferation and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling. Normally, these repetitive elements are heavily methylated to maintain genomic stability and repress their transcriptional activity. Hypomethylation can lead to aberrant activation of nearby genes, including those involved in wound healing and fibrotic signalling pathways such as TGF-β, contributing to the persistent and excessive fibroblast activity seen in keloids. Additionally, hypomethylated LINE-1 RNA has been shown to activate innate immune responses via interferon pathways, potentially sustaining chronic inflammation—a known driver of keloid formation8. This epigenetic deregulation mirrors mechanisms observed in tumorigenesis, supporting the classification of keloids as pseudo-malignant lesions19. Preventing keloid formation may involve restoring normal DNA methylation through DNMT-activating agents, silencing active LINE-1/Alu transcripts via siRNA therapy - as demonstrated by Patchsung et al.16 and reducing inflammation to maintain epigenetic stability. In the future, LINE-1 and Alu methylation levels could serve as biomarkers for keloid risk or treatment response. This opens the door for personalized prophylactic strategies post-injury, especially in individuals with low baseline methylation. Moreover, targeted epigenetic therapies, such as topical or injectable agents modulating methylation, may offer novel, effective interventions for keloid prevention and control8,16.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the association between LINE-1 and Alu methylation in keloid tissues compared to normal skin. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our use of bulk tissue samples precludes the identification of methylation changes specific to distinct cell types such as fibroblasts or keratinocytes. Second, the control skin tissues were not age- or location-matched to the keloid samples, introducing potential confounding effects due to inter-individual variability and site-specific epigenetic patterns. Third, the cross-sectional design of this study limits the ability to assess temporal changes or causality. In future studies, we recommend using paired normal and keloid tissues from the same individual, ideally collected prospectively with matching anatomical sites and ages. Single-cell or cell-type-specific methylation analysis would also enhance the resolution of disease-specific epigenetic alterations. Furthermore, we did not evaluate keloid severity using validated clinical grading systems such as the Vancouver Scar Scale or the Patient and Observer Scar Assessment Scale (POSAS). Consequently, we could not assess potential correlations between methylation levels and clinical severity. Future investigations incorporating clinical scoring, even retrospectively via standardized photographic evaluation, would be beneficial to explore the epigenetic basis of keloid progression and severity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, alterations in methylation levels of LINE-1 and Alu methylation levels and patterns in keloids compared to normal skin tissues were significantly lower. Furthermore, LINE-1 and Alu hypomethylation were inversely correlated with chronological age in keloids, as methylation levels were significantly higher in younger individuals compared to older individuals. This study highlights the importance of understanding the mechanisms regulating LINE-1 and Alu methylation, particularly their correlation with age, which may provide insight into the epigenetic alterations involved in keloid formation.
