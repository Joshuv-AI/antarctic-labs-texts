Eating disorders symptoms and associated risk factors among medical students in France: a nationwide cross-sectional study
Authors: Marie Pierre Tavolacci, Nicolas Doudeau, François Vilain, Kahina Sadat, Joël Ladner, Yannick Morvan, Ariel Frajerman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedical students are exposed to multiple stressors that increase their vulnerability to mental health disorders, including eating disorders (ED). Despite growing concern, limited data are available in France regarding ED symptoms and associated psychosocial, behavioral, and environmental factors in this population.ObjectivesTo (1) estimate the risk of ED symptoms among French medical students according to gender and academic stage; (2) identify associated risk factors, including psychological, social, and economic determinants; and (3) explore relationships between ED symptoms and mental health indicators.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted from June to July 2024 among medical students in France, from the second year of training through residency. Participants completed a questionnaire assessing sociodemographic characteristics, mental health, experiences of humiliation and sexual violence, risky behaviors, and healthcare access. ED symptoms were screened using the French version of the SCOFF questionnaire (cutoff ≥ 2). Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with ED symptoms.ResultsAmong the 7506 respondents included in the analysis, the overall estimated risk of ED symptoms was 26.2% (95% CI [25.2–27.2]), with significantly higher rates among women (30.9%) and non-binary students (26.0%) compared to men (14.4%) (p < 0.0001). Estimated risk of ED symptoms decreased with academic progression: 36.8% in pre-clinical students, 31.2% in clinical students, and 25.2% in residents (p < 0.001 for trend). In multivariable analysis, ED symptoms were associated with female or non-binary gender, earlier academic stage, anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation, alcohol misuse, tobacco use, experiences of humiliation, sexual harassment or assault, financial hardship, and having forgone healthcare due to financial or confidentiality-related barriers.ConclusionED symptoms are highly prevalent among medical students, particularly women, gender minorities, and students in the early years of training. This burden is compounded by mental health symptoms, exposure to violence, financial insecurity, and care avoidance. These findings underscore the need for targeted, multidimensional prevention strategies and easily accessible mental health services tailored to the needs of medical students.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-025-01436-2.

Introduction

Medical students are particularly vulnerable to mental health issues due to a combination of intense academic stress, high performance pressure, heavy workloads, early exposure to patient suffering, and a competitive environment that may stigmatize psychological distress [1]. Several studies have reported a high prevalence of anxiety and depressive disorders, suicidal ideation, and care avoidance behaviors in this population [2, 3]. Moreover, the structure of medical training with its irregular hours and the emotional and physical demands of clinical placements amplifies the risk of disordered eating behaviors and the development of eating disorders (ED) symptoms. EDs encompass a range of conditions such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge-eating disorder, all of which are associated with significant physical and psychological impairment. The COVID-19 pandemic further exacerbated ED symptoms among students, driven by social isolation, increased academic stress, and uncertainty about the future [4, 5]. In the general young adult population, the estimated prevalence of ED symptoms ranges from 8 to 15% [6, 7], depending on diagnostic criteria. A meta-analysis of 35 studies including over 21,000 medical students worldwide estimated a global prevalence of ED symptoms at 17.35% [8]. This study has identified medical education itself as a major confounding factor in the development of EDs. Few studies have examined EDs from the very beginning of medical training and into internship years, even though interns and clinical-stage medical students typically face higher levels of stress and workload due to direct involvement in hospital care setting [9].

Eating disorders are often associated with other risky behaviors, such as alcohol, tobacco, or drug use [10]. Risky behaviors frequently associated with eating disorders [11] such as excessive alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and other maladaptive emotional regulation strategies are also prevalent among medical students, who often combine high performance pressure with a tendency to minimize their own vulnerability [12, 13]. The literature also highlights several determinants of ED in the general population, including sexual violence [14]. However, its specific influence on medical students remains underexplored [15]. A recent cross-sectional study from Uganda reported an estimated 16.6% of medical students screened positive on the SCOFF, with female sex and suicidal ideation among the factors associated with higher risk, illustrating the global and context-dependent nature of ED risk in medical trainees [16. Financial insecurity is also a significant risk factor for ED, due to the chronic stress it generates and its impact on food access, healthcare utilization, and self-image [17]. Medical students face financial challenges [18], lack of social support, and sometimes difficult interpersonal relationships—factors that could contribute to increased psychological distress and ED. In France, there is a lack of up-to-date, detailed data regarding the eating behaviors of medical students particularly concerning the prevalence of EDs and associated socio-environmental factors. Most available research has focused broadly on mental health, without specifically addressing eating disordered in this unique academic context. Improved understanding of these factors is essential to guide targeted prevention and intervention strategies for this at-risk population.

Then this study aimed to (1) estimate the prevalence of ED symptoms among medical students according to gender and level of training; (2) identify associated risk factors, including social, economic, and psychological factors; and (3) explore the relationship between ED symptoms and mental health indicators.

Methods

This cross-sectional epidemiological study was conducted among French medical students from the second year of training through the final year of residency. According to publicly available data, approximately 90,000 students are enrolled in medical studies in France, with an estimated 40,000 at the pre-residency level (second to sixth year of medical school) and 50,000 in residency. In France, medical education is structured as a continuous program starting after high school and lasting at least nine years. Residents (internes) are physicians in postgraduate training but are still officially considered medical students. For the purpose of this study, all stages from the second year through residency were include. All eligible students received an invitation to participate from June 10 to July 28, 2024. Survey invitations were sent by the academic offices of each medical school to students’ official institutional email addresses. These offices manage the administrative mailing lists of enrolled students, ensuring data protection and confidentiality. The research team did not have direct access to students’ email addresses. Subsequently, local student associations used their email lists and social media channels to disseminate the survey further. Students were included if enrolled in the second year of medical school, up to the last year of residency. Students on a gap year, those under 18 years old, or those who declined participation were excluded. First-year students were also excluded because, during the study period, they had not yet integrated medical training, as competitive entrance examinations were ongoing Items answering were mandatory to progress to the following questions (“I don’t know” or “refuse to answer” were considered as an answer).

Data collection

Socio-demographic characteristics

Age, gender, familial situation (single or in a relationship) and year of study classified in preclinical phase (years 2–3), a clinical phase (years 4–6), and residency, (years 7 and beyond) were collected. Perceived financial difficulties were assessed using four response categories: “Very significant,” “Quite significant,” “Slight,” and “Not at all significant.” For analytical purposes, the first two categories were grouped under “Yes”, and the last two under “No”. Participants were asked whether they had considered dropping out of their studies. Responses were categorized into two groups: “No”, which included “Never” and “Occasionally during the year,” and “Yes”, which included “Once a month,” “Several times a month,” “Once a week,” “Several times a week,” and “Every day.

Eating disorder

To screen for ED symptoms, the present study used the SCOFF questionnaire, a brief and widely validated screening tool commonly applied in epidemiological research. Students filled in the French version of the five-item “Sick, Control, One stone, Fat, Food” (SCOFF) questionnaire. A screening threshold was fixed at two positive responses with a sensitivity of 0.88 and a specificity of 0.93 [19].

Humiliation, sexual harassment and sexual assault

Humiliation was evaluated using a self-reported item asking participants whether they had repeatedly been mocked, given derogatory nicknames, belittled, or humiliated during their studies, either face-to-face or online. Sexual harassment was assessed using two self-reported items related to experiences during their studies. The first item inquired about repeated exposure to sexual comments or behaviors causing discomfort, including intrusive questions about private life, lewd remarks, mimed sexual gestures, or exposure to pornographic content, either face-to-face or online. The second item addressed repeated persistent sexual advances that made the respondent feel uncomfortable. Sexual assault was assessed with a self-reported item asking participants whether, during their studies, someone had, against their will, touched their breasts or buttocks, held them to force a kiss, or rubbed or pressed their body against them. “Sexual assault was assessed with a self-reported item asking participants whether, during their studies, they had, against their will, been forced to perform or undergo sexual touching, or whether someone had attempted or succeeded in having sexual intercourse with them (Appendix 1).

Mental health

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used to assess anxiety and depressive symptoms. Each subscale (HADS-A and HADS-D) consists of 7 items, scored from 0 to 21 (cut-off > 10) [20]. Both subscales have shown good psychometric properties in student populations, with Cronbach’s alpha values generally ranging from 0.78 to 0.90 [21]. Participants were asked whether they had experience suicidal ideation in the past 12 months.

Risky behaviors

Alcohol consumption was assessed using the AUDIT (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test) questionnaire. A score of ≥ 8 in men and ≥ 7 in women and non-binary individuals indicates hazardous alcohol use. A score of ≥ 13 in men or ≥ 12 in women and non-binary individuals may suggest harmful alcohol use or an alcohol use disorder [22]. Smoker was defined as smoking at least 1 cigarette/Participants were asked whether, in the past 12 months, they had foregone a medical consultation or psychotherapy due to financial reasons. Participants were asked whether, in the past 12 months, they had foregone healthcare for reasons other than financial constraints as lack of confidentiality, organizational difficulties and academic workload.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted to characterize the study population and to estimate the estimated risk of ED symptoms with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). In the univariate analysis, the associations between EDs symptoms and each independent variable were assessed using chi-square tests for categorical variables. Variables associated with EDs at a p-value < 0.20 in the univariate analysis were then included in a multivariable logistic regression model. The final model was systematically adjusted for age, academic phase, and gender as potential confounders. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals were estimated. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed using ExcelStat software (Addinsoft, Paris, France), which provided descriptive statistics, bivariate analyses, and multivariate modeling.

Ethics

Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and students' consent was collected at the beginning of the questionnaire. Students were asked to give explicit consent to be included in this study. The data management complies with GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) guidelines and was registered with the French National Commission for Information Technology and Civil Liberties (CNIL) under 2,232,169. This research was accepted by Paris Saclay University’s ethics committee under number CER-Paris-Saclay-2024-20.

Results

A total of 9035 medical students participated in the study, of whom 7506 completed the SCOFF test and provided information on their year of medical training (academic stage). (Fig. 1). Students who completed the SCOFF questionnaire were compared to those who did not complete it. No significant gender differences were observed between the two groups; however, academic level differed, with residents being more likely to respond (Appendix 2). The characteristics of medical students were described in the Table 1. Most participants were aged between 22 and 25 years (41.2%), followed by those aged ≥ 26 years (30.5%) and 18–21 years (28.3%). Women represented the majority of the sample (70.9%), men accounted for 28.4%, and non-binary participants for 0.7%. Regarding academic level, 38.0% were clinical students, 33.9% were residents, and 28.1% were in the preclinical phase. Overall, 51.8% of students reported anxiety symptoms (HADS-A > 10) and 16.1% depressive symptoms (HADS-D > 10). The overall estimated risk of ED symptoms was 26.2% (95%CI [25.2–27.2]), with rates of 30.9% (95%CI [29.7–32.1]) among women, 14.4% among men (95%CI [12.9–15.9]), and 26.0% among non-binary individuals (95%CI [13.8–38.1]) (p < 0.0001). The estimated risk of ED symptoms varied significantly according to gender and stage of medical training (Fig. 2). Among female students, estimated risk of ED symptoms was highest during the preclinical phase (36.8%), then gradually decreased over the course of the studies: 31.2% during the clinical phase and 25.2% among residents. A similar trend was observed among male students, with an estimated risk of ED symptoms rates of 16.9% in the preclinical phase, 15.7% in the clinical phase, and 10.9% during residency. Multivariate analyses identified several factors significantly associated with ED symptoms among medical students (Table 2). Women were nearly three times more likely to report ED symptoms than men. Non-binary students also showed an increased risk. Academic level was associated with ED symptoms: pre-clinical students and clinical students were at higher risk compared to residents. Several psychosocial factors were significantly associated with ED symptoms: students reporting humiliation, sexual violence, sexual assault, financial difficulties, depression, anxiety, or suicidal ideation. Behavioral factors were also relevant: alcohol misuse and dependence, and tobacco use were all significantly associated with ED symptoms. Finally, forgoing healthcare for financial reasons was associated with increased risk, whether for medical consultations (or psychotherapy. Discussion

This study highlights a concerning estimated risk of ED symptoms among medical students, along with a range of significant risk factors including psychosocial determinants (gender-based and sexual violence, financial insecurity), psychiatric factors (anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation), behavioral aspects (substance use), and contextual influences (stage of training, barriers to accessing care). The overall estimated risk of ED symptoms in our sample (26.2%) is consistent with previous studies among medical students, both in France and internationally, which report rates ranging from 20 to 30%, highlighting the persistent vulnerability of this population [7, 23, 24]. The SCOFF has higher sensitivity for anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa but is less sensitive for binge eating disorder and other presentations more frequent in men. Nevertheless, the SCOFF is one of the most widely used tools in large epidemiological surveys, which ensures comparability of our findings with international studies conducted among medical students and young adults. Consistent with prior studies, women exhibited a higher prevalence of ED symptoms than men, both in student and general populations, however, the notable prevalence among non-binary individuals in our data aligns with emerging international findings suggesting that gender-diverse students may be particularly at risk. This aligns with recent studies showing that gender minorities are more vulnerable to EDs due to discrimination, minority stress, and body image concerns [24–28]. However, this association has not yet been specifically documented in medical student populations, making our findings particularly relevant.

The stage of medical training emerged as a significant determinant of ED symptoms. Medical students in the earlier phases of their curriculum, particularly during pre-clinical and clinical years, showed a notably higher estimated risk of ED symptoms compared to residents, in both women and men. Specifically, among women, ED symptoms prevalence was 36.8% during pre-clinical years, decreasing to 31.2% in clinical years, and further declining to 25.2% during the medical internship. A parallel downward trend was observed among men, with rates of 16.9%, 15.7%, and 10.9%, respectively. This trend could be accounted for by these stages of training correspond to late adolescence and early adulthood, a period of increased psychological vulnerability and peak onset for eating disorders and early hospital internship is associated with intense academic stress and identity development, which can act as triggers for maladaptive coping behaviors [29]. Notably, the prevalence remained substantial even among residents, particularly female residents, suggesting that ED symptoms may persist throughout medical training or even emerge during the course of education. Financial hardship emerged as a risk factor of ED, likely reflecting increasing precarity among healthcare students exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic [30]. Chronic stress, social isolation, and foregone healthcare access may contribute to this association. Although medical students are often perceived as socioeconomically privileged, our results highlight their vulnerabilities, which can substantially increase ED symptoms risk.

The strong association between ED symptoms and experiences of sexual harassment or violence is particularly concerning. The psychological impacts such as anxiety, PTSD, low self-esteem, and suicidal ideation often lead to compensatory behaviors like disordered eating [31]. Medical students are especially vulnerable to such violence due to the nature of their training environment, early exposure to clinical settings, hierarchical hospital-university structures [32], and social rituals that may normalize sexist and harmful behaviors [33]. Although women are the primary victims, male students may also be affected, particularly in contexts involving power imbalances or homophobia [15]. These findings emphasize the urgent need for institutional prevention policies and robust support mechanisms to address academic violence across the entire medical student population [34].

Our results reveal a high burden of mental health issues among medical students consistent with previous studies [9, 35, 36]. These comorbidities may exacerbate the severity and chronicity of ED symptoms, complicating both diagnosis and treatment [37]. Furthermore, we observed an association between ED and thoughts of dropping out of medical school. While this relationship remains underexplored, it is plausible given the considerable psychological and physical burden associated with EDs. Cognitive symptoms such as preoccupation with food, physical consequences including fatigue, and emotional difficulties like anxiety and low self-esteem may impair academic performance and clinical responsibilities. Conversely, the rigorous demands of academic and clinical duties may exacerbate or trigger these symptoms, creating a detrimental feedback loop [38]. Risky behaviors such as alcohol and tobacco use also emerged as markers of broader psychological vulnerability. While these behaviors are frequently influenced by social contexts, they may also reflect maladaptive coping strategies in response to the high-stress academic environment [39, 40]. This underscores the necessity for comprehensive mental health strategies that concurrently address EDs and associated risky behaviors.

Finally, barriers to accessing care, whether financial, related to academic workload, or concerns over confidentiality, pose critical obstacles. Our results indicate that students who forgo care due to such barriers are at heightened risk of ED symptoms [41]. This paradoxical situation, where future healthcare professionals struggle to access care themselves, is compounded by the stigma and logistical challenges. The fear of judgment or being perceived as weak may deter help-seeking, particularly within a culture that tends to valorize resilience and self-sacrifice [42–44]. Consequently, these dynamics contribute to under-detection and delayed diagnosis of EDs, increasing the likelihood of chronicity and complications. This vulnerability of medical students highlights the need for confidential, independent, and easily accessible mental health services to overcome such barriers. Beyond their own health, ED are a concern for students’ future clinical practice. As personal experiences,including weight-related stigma,may shape their willingness to address body image, weight, and eating disorders with patients [45].

Limitations and strength

This study presents several limitations. First, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability and recall biases, which may affect the accuracy of the reported information. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences; observed associations with potential risk factors should therefore be interpreted as correlations rather than evidence of causation. Furthermore, the sample exhibited a gender imbalance, with women comprising 75% of participants higher than the estimated 60–65% in the general medical student population. This discrepancy likely reflects participation bias common in online surveys. Nevertheless, gender-stratified analyses and adjustment for gender in regression models mitigate, though do not entirely eliminate, this issue. The response rate was approximately 10% (7506/90,000), which may have introduced selection bias. While residents were more likely to respond, potentially underestimating prevalence, it is also possible that students with ED symptoms were more motivated to participate, potentially overestimating prevalence. Nevertheless, this response rate is comparable to other large-scale online student surveys. Despite these limitations, this study has several notable strengths. The inclusion of underexplored contextual variables such as forgone healthcare, financial hardship, and experiences of gender-based or sexual violence offers a comprehensive and multidimensional perspective on ED symptoms risk factors among medical students. The large and diverse sample, encompassing participants from pre-clinical students through residents, enhances the generalizability of the findings across different stages of medical training. Moreover, the integration of academic, psychiatric, and behavioral factors in the analysis provides a holistic understanding of the complex interplay of determinants influencing ED vulnerability. The use of validated screening tools such as SCOFF adds methodological rigor, and the gender-stratified approach strengthens the ability to identify differential risk patterns.

Conclusion

These findings underscore the urgent need for a targeted and integrated approach to the prevention of ED among medical students. Strengthening early detection and providing comprehensive psychological support, particularly during the initial years of medical training, are critical steps. Special attention must be devoted to vulnerable subgroups, including gender minorities, students exposed to gender-based or sexual violence, and those facing financial insecurity. Given their future role as healthcare providers, medical students will be uniquely positioned to identify and manage eating disorders in their patients. Therefore, supporting their mental health is not only essential for their individual well-being but also represents a vital investment in the quality of future patient care. To deepen the understanding of vulnerability trajectories and protective factors throughout medical education related to ED, longitudinal studies are essential. Additionally, qualitative studies exploring the lived experiences and coping strategies of students with ED symptom could provide valuable insights, allowing for the development of tailored prevention efforts that address the unique psychological, academic, and social challenges students face during medical training.
