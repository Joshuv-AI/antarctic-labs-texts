Post-translational modification-regulating biomaterials for regeneration
Authors: Adilijiang Abudousu, Yunkai Tang, Baikun Liu, Lei Xiang, Zhen Wang, Yihan Li, Ding Zhao, Wenguo Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-translational modification (PTM) refers to covalent chemical alterations occurring on proteins after their biosynthesis, encompassing phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination and hundreds of other documented variants. These modifications act as dynamic molecular codes that precisely regulate protein subcellular localization, functional activity and interaction networks. Through core biological processes like signal transduction and metabolic regulation, they ultimately define cellular identity while maintaining adaptive responses to environmental stimuli. Emerging evidence links PTM dysregulation to various disease pathways, where impaired tissue regeneration emerges as a strategic intervention window for targeted PTM-based therapies. As core components of regenerative medicine, biomaterials with designable physicochemical properties can achieve precise PTM regulation through physicochemical signal transduction, overcoming conventional limitations and providing innovative platforms for tissue regeneration. This review analyzes how intrinsic material properties, surface modifications and ROS-regulating capabilities influence PTM control, while exploring the potential of biomaterials in precision PTM modulation. We then focus on advancements in bone, vascular, muscle and neural regeneration, demonstrating how biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation enables tissue-specific repair. Finally, we discuss current challenges and propose bioengineering strategies for advanced PTM control.Keywords: Post-translational modification, Biomaterials, Modulation, Regeneration

Introduction

Post-translational modification (PTM) refers to chemical modifications of amino acid residues in proteins that occur after translation, through enzymatic or non-enzymatic reactions. These modifications typically target specific amino acids such as serine, threonine, lysine, and tyrosine, by adding or removing chemical groups such as phosphate, acetyl, glycosyl, or ubiquitin, or through covalent conjugation with metabolites. Such changes can profoundly affect a protein's physicochemical properties, conformation, and biological functions [1]. Tissue damage resulting from external trauma or internal pathological conditions leads to structural and functional impairment at the cellular, tissue, or organ level, ultimately affecting overall health. In response, the organism initiates regenerative mechanisms to restore the structure and function of damaged tissues. During tissue regeneration, PTM play crucial roles in regulating cell proliferation and differentiation and influence the efficiency and quality of regeneration through complex signaling networks [[2], [3], [4]]. For example, phosphorylation catalyzed by kinases that transfer a phosphate group from adenosine triphosphate (ATP) to target proteins modulates the stability and activity of key regulators such as cyclin D1. The phosphorylation status of cyclin D1 determines its ability to drive the cell cycle from G1 to S phase [5,6]. Similarly, the transcription factor MYC is finely regulated by phosphorylation during regeneration. Under mitogenic stimulation, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and cyclin-dependent kinase 2 (CDK2) phosphorylate MYC at Ser62, enhancing its stability and activation. The phosphorylation-dependent prolyl isomerase peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase NIMA-interacting 1 (PIN1) interacts with phospho-Ser62 MYC and catalyzes cis-trans isomerization at Pro63, inducing a conformational change that promotes MYC binding to the nuclear pore complex. In this state, MYC recruits histone acetyltransferase general control nonderepressible 5 (GCN5) to the nuclear pore, facilitating local histone acetylation and transcriptional activation of target genes, thereby enabling precise control of gene expression [[7], [8], [9]]. Histone acetylation modulates gene transcription by altering chromatin architecture. For instance, sirtuin 3 (SIRT3) regulates mitochondrial protein acetylation, influencing cellular metabolism and proliferation [10,11]. The functional versatility of ubiquitination largely arises from the capacity of ubiquitin molecules to assemble polyubiquitin chains via distinct linkage configurations. This diversity in linkage patterns creates substantial complexity within ubiquitination. Distinct chain architectures operate as molecular codes, which are recognized by dedicated cellular reader proteins to initiate discrete biological outcomes [12,13]. K48-linked polyubiquitin chains serve as canonical signals targeting substrates for proteasomal degradation. By contrast, K63-linked chains predominantly mediate non-degradative processes including signal transduction, DNA damage repair and endocytosis [14]. Advancements in high-resolution mass spectrometry and ubiquitin linkage-specific antibodies over recent years have facilitated in-depth characterisation of atypical polyubiquitin linkages such as K6, K11, K27, K29 and K33. Accumulating evidence highlights critical roles for these atypical chains in cell cycle control, DNA damage response, autophagy and immune signalling [15,16]. These findings have substantially broadened current understanding of ubiquitin-mediated cellular activities. SUMOylation, a process involving the covalent attachment of small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins to target substrates, exhibits dynamic crosstalk and functional synergy with ubiquitination [17]. For example, the K242 residue of promyelocytic leukemia zinc finger protein (PLZF) is SUMOylated under low reactive oxygen species (ROS) conditions to promote nuclear localization, while oxidative stress triggers its ubiquitination and degradation [18]. Additionally, SUMOylation can also serve as a precursor signal for ubiquitination, such as the SUMOylation of cyclin-dependent kinase 6 (CDK6) at K216 directly inhibiting its ubiquitination at K147, blocking proteasomal degradation to maintain protein stability [19]. In addition to these covalent modifications, proteins are often modified through post-translational cleavage and a stepwise mechanism of protein maturation or activation, thereby playing a complex regulatory role in tissue regeneration.

It is noteworthy that regenerative capacity varies significantly across different tissues. For instance, bone tissue can self-repair through osteocyte proliferation and differentiation in cases of minor fractures or defects, while muscle tissue regenerates via satellite cell activation and differentiation following mild injury. In contrast, the central nervous system exhibits limited regenerative potential due to its high degree of cellular differentiation and specialized microenvironment [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. The biological mechanisms of tissue regeneration are complex, involving stem cell self-renewal and multilineage differentiation, regulation of cell signaling pathways, and dynamic remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [24]. In recent years, biomaterials have demonstrated substantial advances in tissue regeneration. They not only serve as structural scaffolds for repair but also critically influence cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, and microenvironment modulation [25,26]. Different types of biomaterials exhibit distinct mechanisms and application potentials. For example, surface roughness and chemical modifications can directly regulate cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, while mechanical properties such as elastic modulus and compressive strength modulate stem cell fate through mechanotransduction [[27], [28], [29]]. Moreover, electrical conductivity and applied electric fields show promise in neural and bone tissue engineering [30,31]. Leveraging their unique physicochemical properties and spatiotemporal controllability, biomaterials enable precise intervention in key PTM processes within regenerative microenvironments. Importantly, biomaterial design can be tailored to the biological characteristics of specific tissues, achieving targeted PTM regulation through surface functionalization, mechanical optimization, and chemical signaling. This review examines how intrinsic physicochemical properties, surface modifications, and ROS regulatory capabilities of biomaterials influence PTM dynamics. We further explore the capability of biomaterials for precision PTM modulation. Focusing on critical stages in bone, vascular, muscle, neural, and other tissue regeneration, this review details how biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation facilitates tissue-specific repair (Scheme 1). Finally, we offer our perspectives on current challenges and future opportunities.

Mechanisms of PTM modulation by biomaterials

Biomaterials offer a unique platform for precise regulation of PTM through their tunable physicochemical properties (Table .1). The dynamic nature of PTM is central to cellular function regulation, yet traditional biological or chemical methods are limited in spatiotemporal specificity and coordinated multi-pathway control. By employing multi-dimensional design strategies, biomaterials can emulate physiological microenvironments or actively modulate cell signaling pathways to directionally regulate key PTM processes. This section systematically elucidates the multi-dimensional mechanisms of biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation, reveals the correlations between material properties and PTM signaling pathways, and establishes a foundation for developing tissue-specific regenerative strategies.

The regulation of PTM by biomaterials.

Impact of intrinsic physicochemical properties of biomaterials on PTM regulation

Mechanical properties in PTM regulation

Cells are constantly exposed to mechanical stimuli under both physiological and pathological conditions. Such stimuli originate from the ECM, fluid shear stress and tensile force. Mechanical signals propagate into the cytoplasm through dedicated mechanotransduction cascades. These signals regulate gene expression, cytoskeletal remodeling and metabolic homeostasis [60,61]. Regulation of intracellular PTM by mechanotransduction constitutes a central mechanism for cellular sensing and adaptation to external mechanical microenvironments. For instance, ECM stiffening rewires glutamine metabolism and elevates microtubule glutamylation. Modified microtubules gain structural stability and support elevated cellular invasiveness [62]. The Hippo/YAP cascade enables cellular detection of diverse mechanical inputs and drives pro-inflammatory gene transcription. Laminar flow upregulates the abundance of NAD+-dependent deacetylase sirtuin 1 (SIRT1). Enhanced SIRT1 activity triggers YAP deacetylation. This modification suppresses YAP activation and nuclear accumulation, and subsequently reduces pro-inflammatory gene transcription [63]. The mechanical stiffness of biomaterials can mimic the mechanical microenvironment of the ECM, triggering intracellular mechanotransduction pathways through cell surface integrin receptors. This process influences cytoskeletal reorganization and the regulation of key PTM processes [[64], [65], [66]]. For example, poly (ethylene glycol) diacrylate (PEGDA) hydrogels allow tunable mechanical properties through concentration adjustments, thereby simulating ECM biomechanical variations. With increasing hydrogel stiffness, phosphorylation levels of focal adhesion kinase (FAK) at Y397 significantly rise, further promoting phosphorylation of ERK at T202/Y204. This cascade ultimately enhances both adhesion capacity and proliferation rate in renal proximal tubular epithelial cells (RPTECs) (Fig. 1a–d) [67]. Similarly, in polyacrylamide (PAAm) hydrogels, the phosphorylation level of zeta-chain-associated protein kinase 70 (ZAP70) increases with elevated Young's modulus, indicating that T cells receive stronger activation signals on stiffer substrates. However, F-actin clearance and the centralization of ZAP70 phosphorylation do not exhibit stiffness dependence and are primarily driven by biochemical signals following T cell receptor (TCR) activation (Fig. 1e) [68]. Furthermore, biomaterials with different stiffness levels differentially regulate PTM by modulating cell morphology. Relatively stiff substrates promote increased cell spreading and typical spindle-like morphology by enhancing integrin-mediated focal adhesion maturation. In contrast, soft substrates often cause cells to maintain a rounded shape with limited lamellipodial extension. Such morphological changes may affect intracellular metabolic processes and signal transduction via cytoskeletal tension [[69], [70], [71]]. For example, the transport and localization of proteins requiring compartment-specific modifications could be changed by cytoskeletal alterations, thereby influencing area-specific PTM. In cancer research, cancer stem cells (CSCs) characterized by self-renewal, slow proliferation, and strong drug resistance represent critical therapeutic targets [72]. Mechanical force-induced PTM alterations facilitate CSC identification within tumor tissues. For instance, double-network hydrogel (DN gel) composed of poly (2-acrylamido-2-methylpropanesulfonic acid) (PAMPS) and poly (N, N-dimethylacrylamide) (PDMAAm), which exhibit ∼90% water content and a Young's modulus of approximately 300 kPa, can mimic the elasticity of human tissues. These hydrogels apply mechanical forces to cell membranes, activate mechanosensitive receptors, and trigger intracellular signaling cascades. They significantly induce phosphorylation of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), mesenchymal-epithelial transition factor (MET), sarcoma proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (SRC), ERK1/2, FAK, and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK), ultimately leading to cancer cell reprogramming into CSCs and improving rapid clinical detection capabilities [57].

Size and charge effects on PTM regulation

The size of biomaterials alters their binding mode and binding strength to cell surface receptors. Different sizes induce varying degrees of receptor clustering and conformational changes. These differences lead to distinct downstream signaling cascades. At the post-translational level, these cascades differentially regulate the modification status and sites of intracellular proteins. For instance, ultrasmall silica nanoparticles (USSNs) with diameters of 3.6 nm and 5 nm can more effectively approach the TCR-CD3 complex via electrostatic interactions compared to 8 nm or 15 nm USSNs. These smaller particles rapidly induce phosphorylation of key signaling proteins such as ZAP70 and linker for activation of T cells (LAT), thereby initiating early T-cell signaling [73]. However, USSNs alone are insufficient to fully activate T cells and require additional co-stimulatory signals. Nonetheless, due to their low cost, rapid hydrolysis into harmless orthosilicic acid, and direct T-cell activating ability, USSNs show promise in cancer therapy, autoimmunity, and transplant tolerance [73,74]. Biomaterial size also influences cellular internalization. Small particles enter cells more readily, whereas large particles tend to remain extracellular. This differential localization engages distinct signaling pathways at the plasma membrane and within the cytoplasm, potentially leading to differential regulation of PTM on target proteins. Silica nanoparticles (SiNPs), for example, enter cells via endocytosis and enhance ERK phosphorylation, promoting the proliferation of human adipose tissue-derived stem cells (hADSCs). In contrast, silica microparticles (SiMPs), due to their larger size, cannot be internalized but instead induce intracellular p38 phosphorylation through oxidative stress, leading to apoptosis [75]. The relationship between biomaterial size and cell fate is not simply linear and depends on specific material properties and target proteins. For example, at the same concentration, smaller titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) induce higher levels of p53 phosphorylation at Ser15, increasing the Bcl2-associated X protein/B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bax/Bcl-2) ratio and subsequently activating caspase proteins, ultimately resulting in apoptosis [76]. It is noteworthy that reduced size does not always enhance PTM modulation. Both submicrometer-sized graphene oxide (SGO) and nanometer-sized graphene oxide (NGO) significantly increase phosphorylation of c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) at T183/Y185, which promotes Bcl-2 phosphorylation at Ser70. This causes dissociation of the Beclin-1–Bcl-2 complex and induces autophagy [77]. Although SGO and NGO exhibit stronger interactions with cellular components due to their larger specific surface area compared to micrometer-sized GO (MGO), graphene quantum dots (GQDs) do not show the strongest effect. Unlike the partially oxidized surfaces of other GO materials, GQDs are highly oxidized due to "secondary oxidation,” which may hinder JNK phosphorylation in endothelial cells. Furthermore, the extremely small size of GQDs may fall below the critical threshold required to trigger the JNK-mediated autophagy pathway, resulting in quantum confinement effects.

The membrane potential is not only the basis of cellular excitability but also serves as a dynamic platform for signal integration, translating electrical signals into chemical instructions for PTM by regulating ion concentrations and second messenger systems [78,79]. Conversely, various PTMs reshape membrane potential homeostasis by modulating the localization, activity and stability of ion channels and transporters. For example, S-persulfidation is an important redox-sensitive PTM and can regulate membrane potential and vascular tone by altering the open probability of ion channels such as KATP and CaV1.2 [80]. Biomaterials offer a new perspective for modulating PTM through their effects on membrane potential or charge interactions with the membrane. The charge properties of material surfaces or externally applied electric fields may alter local ion distribution and potential status near the cell membrane. Such physical perturbations could indirectly affect the activity of modifying enzymes such as protein kinases and phosphatases on or near the membrane, or the accessibility of their substrates. Furthermore, fluctuations in membrane potential may change the position or conformation of charged amino acid residues within transmembrane regions, and such changes could influence their probability of being modified [[81], [82], [83]]. For instance, among similarly sized solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), cationic SLNs (cSLNs) exhibit stronger regulatory effects on PTM compared to neutral SLNs (nSLNs). cSLNs likely interact with lipid raft domains in the plasma membrane of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) via electrostatic attraction, compromising membrane integrity and triggering Ca2+ influx. This significantly enhances phosphorylation levels of p38 and JNK, thereby promoting superoxide anion production and elastase release in PMNs, ultimately inducing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation [84]. Furthermore, the synergistic effect of size and charge can enhance the PTM-regulatory capacity of biomaterials. For example, when comparing different mesoporous silica-coated gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) in modulating phosphorylation, bare AuNPs (unmodified by any coating) possess the smallest size and the highest positive zeta potential. Janus Au@mSiO2NPs, due to their asymmetric structure, exhibit the largest size and an almost neutral surface charge, while core–shell Au@mSiO2NPs fall between the two in both size and charge [85]. Although all three significantly promote JNK phosphorylation and suppress ERK phosphorylation, bare AuNPs exert the most pronounced effect on p38 phosphorylation. Bare AuNPs also demonstrate the highest cellular uptake, along with loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) and cytochrome c release, ultimately leading to apoptosis [85]. Biomaterials designed for membrane potential modulation provide a non-canonical strategy to intervene in PTM. Rational design of surface charge density and distribution may enable controlled perturbation of the membrane potential, and such perturbations could differentially affect the extent and type of PTM. However, the quantitative relationships between charge type, charge strength and PTM changes remain to be systematically clarified. How the duration and reversibility of membrane potential perturbations influence modification dynamics is still unknown. Furthermore, the molecular pathways that relay signals from membrane potential changes to specific modification sites await experimental validation.

Metal/metal ion-mediated PTM regulation

Metal ions play crucial regulatory roles in various PTM processes by participating in enzyme active site formation or activating signaling pathways [86,87]. For example, citrullination, a PTM catalyzed by calcium-dependent peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs), plays important roles in stem cell pluripotency and gene expression regulation [[88], [89], [90]]. During wound healing, increased extracellular calcium concentrations activate PADI2, which catalyzes histone H4 citrullination to modulate wound-induced cell proliferation and promote effective tissue regeneration [91]. Furthermore, metal ions can form coordination bonds with amino acid residues in proteins, helping maintain tertiary structure and providing a foundation for subsequent PTM [92]. Metal and metal ion-based biomaterials provide powerful tools for the precise modulation of PTM, owing to their tunable coordination chemistry, catalytic activity, biocompatibility and responsive release properties [93,94]. Transition metal-catalyzed bioorthogonal reactions enable specific modification of proteins under physiological conditions without interfering with native biological processes [95,96]. For instance, the palladium-catalyzed Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reaction has been successfully applied to the chemoselective modification of native proteins, demonstrating the potential of metal catalysis in complex biological media [97]. Metal coordination chemistry can also be used to construct reversible protein-metal interactions, allowing dynamic regulation of PTM. Multivalent chelators (MCH), for instance, mediate reversible binding to oligohistidine (His)-tagged proteins through transition metal ions, providing a platform for spatiotemporally controlled protein modification [98,99]. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are crystalline porous materials assembled from metal nodes and organic linkers via coordination bonds. Their highly tunable pore structures and surface functionalization capabilities make them ideal carriers for loading PTM enzymes or substrate proteins [100]. Metal-based nanoparticles, with their controllable ion release properties, can directly or indirectly participate in PTM regulation. For instance, zirconia nanoparticles (ZrO2 NPs) mimic native alkaline phosphatase (ALP) by catalyzing the dephosphorylation of 4-methylumbelliferyl phosphate (4-MUP). Interestingly, oxide nanoparticles such as MgO, CuO, Fe3O4, TiO2, ZnO, Al2O3, SiO2, SnO2, NiO, and Co3O4 do not exhibit phosphatase-mimetic activity, highlighting the specific role of Zr (IV) in this function [101]. Notably, ZrO2 NPs catalyze dephosphorylation of ATP and o-phospho-L-tyrosine but show no activity toward deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) strands, likely due to steric hindrance from the two ester bonds in DNA phosphates impeding nucleophilic substitution. This indicates higher selectivity than ALP. Moreover, ZrO2 NPs demonstrate greater stability and catalytic activity across a wider range of pH and temperatures compared to ALP (Fig. 2a–c) [101]. Enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) specifically catalyzes the addition of methyl groups to lysine 27 of histone H3 (H3K27me3), a modification associated with chromatin condensation and gene repression [103]. Low-dose exposure to zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) suppresses EZH2 levels, thereby reducing H3K27me3 and increasing runt-related transcription factor 3 (Runx3) expression. This effect can be reversed using the zinc chelator N,N,N′,N'-tetrakis(2-pyridylmethyl)ethylenediamine (TPEN) [104]. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are widely used in biomedicine as antimicrobial agents and drug delivery vehicles to enhance efficacy and reduce side effects [105,106]. Their capacity to modulate phosphorylation depends on cellular uptake and Ag+ release. For example, AgNPs enhance phosphorylation of ERK, JNK, p38, and histone H3 at serine 10 (p-H3S10), promoting cell transformation and tumorigenesis [107]. Inhibiting endocytosis reduces p-H3S10 levels, whereas promoting Ag + release with H2O2 increases it [108]. Additionally, ultraviolet A (UVA) exposure enhances Ag+ release and exacerbates oxidative DNA damage, triggering phosphorylation of histone H2AX (gammaH2AX), a key step in recognizing and responding to DNA double-strand breaks that recruits DNA repair proteins [[109], [110], [111]]. In histone methylation, AgNPs reduce global levels of H3K4me3 and H3K79me1 by decreasing histone methyltransferases disruptor of telomeric silencing 1-like (DOT1L) and mixed-lineage leukemia (MLL), while also inhibiting ribonucleic acid (RNA) polymerase II binding and impairing β-globin gene transcription. AgNPs may also sterically hinder histone methylation by binding to the H3 tail (Fig. 2d and e) [102]. Although free Ag+ ions alone do not alter histone methylation intensity, they may exert effects through the "Trojan horse” mechanism via intracellular release from AgNPs.

Other physicochemical properties influencing PTM

Due to specific structural or chemical features, the regulatory capacity of biomaterials on PTM may not be dominated solely by size or charge effects. For instance, although black phosphorus quantum dots (BPQDs) are small in size, black phosphorus nanosheets (BPNSs) possess an irregular layered structure that enables them, like BPQDs, to promote dephosphorylation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) at Ser1177 and increase phosphorylation at Thr495. This effectively inhibits eNOS enzymatic activity and reduces nitric oxide (NO) production, significantly impairing the angiogenic capacity of human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Fig. 3a and b) [112]. The unique irregular layered structure of BPNSs may provide more active sites and a larger surface area, facilitating more efficient interaction with phosphorylation sites of eNOS and exerting PTM regulatory effects comparable to those achieved through size manipulation. Similarly, rhein molecules self-assemble into helical nanofibers that are more readily internalized by cells. These nanofibers bind to the MD-2 subunit of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), creating steric hindrance that prevents lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from binding to TLR4. This suppresses inhibitor of κB α (IκBα) phosphorylation and degradation, thereby reducing the expression of inflammation-related genes. Furthermore, rhein forms tight interactions via hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking with key amino acid residues in TLR4, including Ser120, Phe121, Gly123, Ile124, Lys125, and Lys122, further enhancing its inhibitory effect on IκBα phosphorylation (Fig. 3c–e) [113]. Methionine, a major target of ROS, can be oxidized to methionine sulfoxide (MetO). This process is reversible through the methionine sulfoxide reductase (MSR) system, thereby restoring protein function [114]. Nickel nanoparticles (NiNPs) can inhibit the MSR system and disrupt oxidative protein modifications [115]. Notably, compared to carbon-rich C-NiNPs, NiNPs exhibit higher nickel content and greater aggregation tendency, leading to more significant inhibition of MSRA and MSRB3 activity. This elevates methionine oxidation in bovine serum albumin (BSA), increases the level of benzo(a)pyrene diolepoxide (BPDE)-protein adducts, severely disrupts protein homeostasis and function, and ultimately induces apoptosis. Additionally, NiNPs suppress microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3) expression, further inhibiting MSRA and MSRB3 activity and blocking the autophagic clearance of oxidatively damaged proteins. This aggravates intracellular oxidative stress and promotes cellular dysfunction [115].

It is also worth noting that the composition of liposomes, commonly used as drug carriers, can be tailored for specific applications, thereby influencing their PTM regulatory properties. For example, a bilayer liposome composed of cardiolipin and dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC) at a 3:1 ratio can mimic the mitochondrial inner membrane environment. However, the presence of DMPC may affect membrane fluidity and limit the accessibility of succinyl-CoA to lysine residues in mitochondrial trifunctional protein (TFP), thereby reducing TFP succinylation [116]. Reduced TFP succinylation may preserve the structural integrity of the enzyme by maintaining its membrane-bound state, thus protecting it from unfolding and degradation that can result from excessive succinylation. It may also reduce competition between succinylation and ubiquitination at the same lysine residues, thereby modulating TFP stability and mitochondrial protein homeostasis.

Surface modifications of biomaterials for PTM regulation

Surface modifications of biomaterials can dynamically influence PTM in the local microenvironment by regulating cell membrane receptor distribution and intracellular signal transduction, offering novel approaches for targeted modulation of disease-related PTM pathways. When biomaterials enter biological systems, they inevitably adsorb plasma proteins [117], and the resulting protein corona significantly affects their PTM regulatory capacity. For instance, after incubation with fetal bovine serum (FBS), starch-coated poly (methyl methacrylate-co-acrylic acid) (PMA/starch) NPs and AuNPs form substantial protein coronas that induce phosphorylation of VE-cadherin at residues Y658 and Y731. This creates noticeable intercellular gaps in human mammary microvascular endothelial cells (HMVECs) monolayers, facilitating cancer cell migration. In contrast, low-adsorption starch-coated poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA/starch) NPs, gelatin-coated poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA/gelatin) NPs, starch-coated gold (Au/starch) NPs, and gelatin-coated gold (Au/gelatin) NPs effectively prevent VE-cadherin phosphorylation due to their minimal protein corona formation, thereby suppressing the nanomaterial-induced endothelial cell leakiness (NanoEL) effect and reducing cancer metastasis risk [118]. Furthermore, specific bioactive peptide modifications can enhance the targeted PTM regulatory capacity of biomaterials, providing new therapeutic strategies. For example, while transforming growth factor-beta-induced protein (TGFBIp) mediates severe inflammatory responses in sepsis through integrin αvβ5, the peptide TAIP (APVYQKLLER), designed from TGFBIp residues 662-683, binds to acetyltransferase CREB-binding protein/E1A-binding protein p300 (CBP/p300). Conjugation with cell-penetrating peptide 9-arginine (9r) and polyamidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimers improves TAIP bioavailability and cellular delivery efficiency. The resulting construct, 9rDT, inhibits K676 acetylation of TGFBIp, reducing TGFBIp secretion and alleviating sepsis-induced vascular inflammation (Fig. 4a and b) [119]. Notably, surface modifications on biomaterials can differentially affect PTM levels of specific receptors, leading to distinct cellular behaviors. In immune responses, the Fc region of antibodies binds to Fcγ receptors (FcγRs) on macrophages, initiating antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) [123,124]. FcγR activation depends on phosphorylation of its immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM), regulated by the balance between Src family kinases (SFKs) and tyrosine phosphatases [[125], [126], [127]]. A synthetic FNIII domain-based protein (Fibcon) with variable repeat numbers can control antigen height. When Fib1L (5 nm height on supported lipid bilayer, SLB) is used, tyrosine phosphatases like CD45 are physically excluded, enhancing FcγR phosphorylation and promoting efficient macrophage phagocytosis. In contrast, with Fib7L (over 10 nm height on SLB), CD45 remains accessible, leading to FcγR dephosphorylation and near-complete loss of phagocytic activity (Fig. 4c–f) [120]. Beyond biological modifications, chemical functionalization also effectively modulates the PTM regulatory capacity of biomaterials. PAMAM dendrimers, highly branched spherical polymers, allow precise control over size, branching degree, and surface chemistry [128]. Widely used as gene and oligonucleotide delivery vehicles due to their nucleic acid binding and transport capabilities [129,130], different PAMAM types exhibit structure-, dose-, and disease-dependent effects on protein phosphorylation. In normal kidneys, Superfect (SF) promotes while Polyfect (PF) inhibits phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and p38. In diabetic nephropathy (DN), both PF and SF (1-5 mg/kg) induce hyperphosphorylation of ERK1/2 and p38. However, at 10 mg/kg, PF significantly suppresses ERK1/2 phosphorylation with reduced effect on p38, whereas SF strongly inhibits p38 phosphorylation with diminished impact on ERK1/2 [131]. Lower doses may facilitate formation of localized high-concentration hotspots within cells, more effectively promoting phosphorylation of signaling proteins.

Lipid rafts are dynamic, ordered membrane microdomains formed through hydrophobic interactions between cholesterol and sphingolipids. These structures exhibit distinct physical properties, such as increased membrane rigidity and reduced fluidity, creating relatively isolated microenvironments within the cell membrane [[132], [133], [134]]. The membrane localization of proteins is crucial for their PTM. During cellular signaling, lipid rafts serve as signaling platforms that selectively recruit and anchor relevant signaling molecules. They facilitate the assembly of modification complexes or create specialized membrane zones with distinct enzymatic activities and substrate concentrations, thereby promoting PTM of themselves or downstream target proteins. Moreover, the physical nature of lipid rafts confers selective permeability, allowing them to function like a "molecular sieve” that regulates protein entry and exit, thus fine-tuning various PTM processes. Lipid rafts also reduce protein mobility within these domains, prolonging interaction times with modifying enzymes and increasing the likelihood of PTM occurrence. Therefore, the regulation of PTM processes by biomaterials is highly dependent on lipid raft structures. For example, exposure to synthetic amorphous silica nanoparticles (SAS-NPs) first induces the formation of lipid rafts on the membrane of dendritic cells (DCs). This enhances the phosphorylation of spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk), triggering DC maturation and T-cell responses, characteristics resembling an immune "danger signal” (Fig. 4g) [121]. It is particularly important to note that the dynamic nature of lipid rafts enables them to respond to the structure and quantity of ligands. By expanding membrane domain area or adjusting compositional ratios, they trigger microdomain reorganization, enhancing receptor clustering and assembly of signaling modules. Thus, compared to monovalent ligands, multivalent ligands (MLs) more effectively promote the formation of receptor clusters on the cell membrane. By simultaneously engaging multiple binding sites of cell surface receptors, MLs elicit stronger signaling and exhibit significantly different PTM regulatory capabilities compared to monovalent ligands [[135], [136], [137]]. For instance, the EGFR is heterogeneously distributed on the cell membrane and enriched in specific membrane regions spanning tens of nanometers [138]. When conjugated to the same number of AuNPs, higher densities of epidermal growth factor (EGF) ligands result in stronger ROS generation and greater inhibition of protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs), leading to enhanced EGFR phosphorylation compared to AuNPs with lower ligand density [139]. Furthermore, precise PTM regulation strictly depends on the functional integrity of membrane rafts. For example, intact raft structures mediate interactions with multivalent EGF-AuNPs, leading to the clustering of EGFR in the binding region. This induces phosphorylation of ERK and suppression of protein kinase B (Akt) phosphorylation, followed by internalization via caveolin-mediated endocytosis, ultimately triggering apoptosis. However, when lipid rafts are disrupted using β-cyclodextrin, the pro-apoptotic activity of EGF-Au NPs is inhibited. The phosphorylation states of ERK and Akt are reversed, restoring the anti-apoptotic activity of the cells (Fig. 4h) [122].

ROS-regulating capabilities of biomaterials in PTM modulation

ROS play a dual role in physiological and pathological processes. The precise regulation of their concentration and spatiotemporal distribution is central to cellular signal transduction. Under physiological conditions, low levels of ROS act as important second messengers and participate in signal transduction through reversible oxidative PTMs (Ox-PTMs). However, when ROS production exceeds the cellular antioxidant defense capacity, oxidative stress ensues. This leads to irreversible oxidative damage to proteins and is closely associated with the pathogenesis of various diseases [140,141]. PTMs are the core effectors of ROS-mediated signal transduction. ROS trigger various Ox-PTMs either by directly oxidizing protein amino acid residues or indirectly through reactions with lipid peroxidation products [142,143]. The functional design of biomaterials offers new strategies for actively modulating ROS levels, either by scavenging excess ROS or by locally generating ROS, thereby reshaping the microenvironmental homeostasis and influencing the PTM status of key proteins. For example, the phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 alpha (eIF2α) is a critical cellular response to oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress. By inhibiting translation initiation and promoting stress granule formation, it helps conserve energy and reduce damage caused by misfolded protein accumulation [[144], [145], [146]]. Cold atmospheric pressure plasma (CAP)-induced oxidative stress, combined with trehalose, enhances eIF2α phosphorylation and initiates immunogenic cell death (ICD) (Fig. 5a) [147]. Additionally, nucleus pulposus cells (NPCs) in intervertebral discs survive in a hypoxic environment. Once disc injury occurs, the extracellular environment becomes relatively oxygen-rich, leading to increased ROS levels, activation of inflammatory pathways, and ultimately triggering apoptosis and matrix degradation [149,150]. Superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), a key antioxidant enzyme, protects cells from oxidative stress by catalyzing the dismutation of superoxide anion (O2−) into oxygen (O2) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The expression and activity of SOD1 are essential for maintaining the antioxidant capacity and health of NPCs [151,152]. Prussian blue nanoparticles (PBNPs) exhibit multiple enzyme-like activities, including catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPX), enabling them to scavenge intracellular ROS and alleviate oxidative stress [153]. Importantly, PBNPs also significantly inhibit the ubiquitination of SOD1, further enhancing cellular ROS clearance capacity (Fig. 5b) [148]. However, nanoparticles can exhibit potential toxicity by inducing ROS production and interfering with normal PTM regulation. For instance, ZnO NPs induce ROS generation both intracellularly and extracellularly, leading to significantly increased expression of histone methyltransferase genes G9a and GLP, and decreased expression of histone acetyltransferase genes GCN5, P300, and CBP. This results in increased histone H3K9 methylation and reduced H4K5 acetylation, inducing G2/M cell cycle arrest and apoptosis mediated by the p53-Bax mitochondrial pathway [154]. Furthermore, exposure to iron oxide nanoparticles can significantly increase intracellular ROS levels through the Fenton reaction or by inhibiting antioxidant enzyme activity, leading to protein carbonylation [155]. Significant variations exist across studies in terms of material design, ROS modulation thresholds and PTM targets. Moreover, some materials show marked efficacy in acute inflammation models but may lose effectiveness in chronic autoimmune conditions owing to long-term ROS fluctuations. Identifying the 'window period' and 'threshold effects' of ROS and PTM in specific disease microenvironments could better inform the design of biomaterials.

Harnessing biomaterials for precision PTM control

The site specificity of PTM underpins its functional precision. Aberrant modification sites or imbalanced modification states can disrupt protein function and contribute to various severe pathological conditions. Cells employ multiple sophisticated molecular mechanisms to ensure the positional accuracy of PTM. The specificity of phosphorylation, for instance, arises mainly from the ability of modifying enzymes such as kinases and phosphatases to recognize specific amino acid sequences or structural features on substrate proteins [156]. This consensus-sequence-based recognition mechanism not only endows kinases with high substrate selectivity but also allows a single kinase to regulate multiple functionally related substrates, thereby forming complex signaling networks. Deubiquitinases exhibit differential selectivity for ubiquitin chain linkage types, a property that stems from the recognition of ubiquitin chain topology by their catalytic domains [157]. Notably, many kinases and methyltransferases have limited intrinsic ability to recognize substrate sequences through their catalytic sites. These enzymes have evolved additional domains located away from the catalytic center, such as SH2, SH3 or WW domains, to recognize docking sites or docking motifs on substrates. This mechanism greatly increases the affinity and specificity of enzyme-substrate interactions, ensuring the accuracy of modifications [158]. A key technical approach to achieving in situ and controllable PTM lies in biomimetic chimeric strategies that integrate enzyme recognition sequences into the material matrix. This strategy centers on mimicking the specific docking mechanism between enzymes and their substrates, thereby allowing synthetic biomaterials to achieve site-specific enzymatic modification with minimal interference from endogenous PTM machinery. Notably, the biomimetic incorporation of enzyme recognition sequences is not limited to a single PTM type. By combining multiple consensus sequences, multifunctional materials that respond to various PTM enzymes can be constructed, mimicking the complex regulatory networks of PTM crosstalk within cells [159,160]. Lysine is one of the most abundant amino acids on protein surfaces, accounting for approximately 5.9% of all amino acids in human proteins. Due to its nucleophilicity, abundance, and surface accessibility, lysine serves as an ideal modification site [161]. However, existing lysine functionalization methods largely rely on differences in lysine reactivity, making it challenging to achieve complete site selectivity for less reactive lysine residues [[162], [163], [164]]. In PTM processes, biological enzymes recognize the chemical, supramolecular, and environmental context of amino acids to enable precise modifications [165]. Molecular imprinting technology (MIT), which uses template molecules to guide the formation of specific recognition sites, mimics the selective binding and catalytic functions of natural enzymes. Artificial enzymes produced by MIT exhibit stability advantages, such as resistance to acids, bases, and high temperatures, and have been successfully applied in biosensing and multi-step catalytic systems [[166], [167], [168], [169], [170]]. For example, a molecularly imprinted polymer nanoparticle (NP) can precisely identify the chemical and supramolecular environment of lysine and deliver labeling reagents specifically to the ε-amino group, enabling single-site functionalization of target lysine residues (Fig. 6a) [171]. Notably, this nanoparticle demonstrates strong binding affinity and selectivity for target peptides and parent proteins. In an equimolar mixture containing lysozyme, cytochrome C, BSA, ovalbumin, horseradish peroxidase, α-amylase, and β-galactosidase, only lysozyme is labeled. Furthermore, using the L2 label in the presence of NP(N3)-NP(N8) results in exclusive labeling at the N-terminal lysine (K1) [171]. The site selectivity of the nanoparticle primarily stems from its exceptional molecular recognition capability, allowing it to accurately distinguish single methyl or methylene group additions, removals, or displacements in the template. It is noteworthy that lysine typically exists in a protonated form under physiological conditions. The nanoparticle alters the surrounding microenvironment through hydrophobic effects and electrostatic interactions, thereby modulating the pKa of lysine and facilitating labeling reactions. This chemical approach achieves precise PTM, making it possible to obtain pure proteins with specific PTM. Moreover, IgG contains over 80 lysine residues, with approximately 20 being highly exposed [173]. A peptide-guided, proximity-driven group transfer reaction has been developed to specifically acetylate a single lysine residue (Lys 248) in the Fc region of human IgG. In this method, the glutamate residue of the Fc-III peptide is replaced with a derivative containing a phenylazidoacetyl group to generate the modified peptide F1. When F1 is mixed with IgG, the azidoacetyl group is positioned near Lys 248, inducing a nucleophilic reaction that transfers the azidoacetyl group from the peptide to Lys 248 without requiring additional catalysts [172]. This specific modification strategy enables the construction of immunoliposomes targeted to cancer cells, significantly enhancing binding efficiency to tumor cells (Fig. 6b–f) [172]. Additionally, this method allows for the specific installation of other functional groups such as azide, alkyne, fluorescent molecules, or biotin at Lys 248, providing opportunities for further multifunctionalization of IgG.

Chemical proximity-induced PTM technologies represent a major breakthrough in chemical biology in recent years. These technologies use bifunctional molecules to bring target proteins into close proximity with specific PTM regulatory enzymes, thereby inducing site-specific modification events [174]. Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) are heterobifunctional molecules that operate through a proximity-driven mechanism. They simultaneously bind a protein of interest (POI) and an E3 ubiquitin ligase, forcing the formation of a stable ternary complex, which leads to ubiquitination of the POI and subsequent proteasomal degradation [175]. By inducing complete protein degradation rather than merely inhibiting its activity, PROTACs can effectively overcome drug resistance caused by target mutations or bypass pathway activation, a limitation commonly encountered with conventional small-molecule inhibitors [176]. Moreover, PROTACs have been successfully applied to target traditionally undruggable proteins, including transcription factors and scaffold proteins, greatly expanding the scope of therapeutically targetable proteins [177]. The development of PROTACs, however, still faces several challenges. These include poor pharmacokinetic properties and insufficient cell permeability resulting from large molecular weight, as well as selective toxicity arising from differential expression of E3 ligases between tumor and normal tissues [178]. To address these issues, researchers have developed various improvement strategies. Molecular glue degraders (MGDs), for instance, are smaller molecules that remodel the surface of E3 ligases to induce interactions with substrates, exhibiting more favorable physicochemical properties [179]. Strategies such as photocaged PROTACs and prodrug PROTACs have also been used to improve spatiotemporal control and safety of degradation [180]. Next-generation PROTAC design is now integrating DNA-encoded libraries and artificial intelligence (AI) to accelerate the discovery of highly active and selective degraders [181]. These strategies collectively drive PROTACs towards greater efficacy and safety. Inspired by PROTACs, researchers have successfully extended the logic of chemical proximity induction to other PTM systems, substantially enriching the chemical proximity toolbox. Deubiquitinase-targeting chimeras (DUBTACs), for example, recruit deubiquitinases to remove ubiquitin chains from POIs, thereby stabilizing target proteins against degradation. Phosphorylation-targeting chimeras (PhosTACs) recruit kinases or phosphatases to achieve precise regulation of POI phosphorylation levels. Acetylation-targeting chimeras (AceTACs) recruit acetyltransferases such as p300/CBP to induce acetylation of POIs and modulate their functions. The first AceTAC molecule targeting the p53Y220C mutant, MS78, can effectively acetylate this mutant and restore its tumor-suppressive function, offering a new strategy for targeting undruggable tumor suppressor proteins [182,183]. These emerging technologies not only expand the application scope of chemical proximity induction but also provide multidimensional tools for the precise regulation of protein function.

The confinement of enzymes and substrates within specific microenvironments, achieved through membranous structures such as the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, plasma membrane or nucleus, is a core mechanism for achieving spatiotemporal specificity of PTM. Histone acetylation within the nuclear envelope, for example, is primarily regulated by nuclear acetyltransferases, whereas acetylation events in the cytoplasm depend on distinct enzyme systems. Such spatial segregation ensures that PTM occurs only in the correct cellular compartments, avoiding global random modification. Biomaterial design is now pursuing the construction of artificial nanoscale compartments or signaling platforms that confine specific enzymes and substrates within defined areas, enabling precise regulation of local PTM. Materials such as liposomes, polymeric vesicles or microphase-separated hydrogels can be used to mimic key cellular features, including local ATP sequestration, regulation of protein concentration and crowded microenvironments. The organization of biomacromolecules in nanoscale domains is critical for the regulation of metabolic processes, and crowding effects, nanodomain formation and local viscosity changes significantly influence protein modification processes [184]. Encapsulation of peptide asparaginyl ligases (PALs) within protein cages formed by engineered lumazine synthases (AaLS), for instance, has been used to create artificial organelles with substrate-sorting capabilities. Although the activity of the encapsulated enzymes is currently reduced, this strategy demonstrates the feasibility of controlling enzyme activity through spatial confinement [185]. The precise regulatory capabilities of biomaterials hold significant implications for PTM. Biomaterials with different properties can be used not only to achieve precise PTM in vitro but also to correct aberrantly modified proteins in vivo and restore their normal functions, thereby advancing precision medicine.

Biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation for tissue regeneration

Precise regulation of PTM provides molecular-level intervention targets for tissue regeneration and directly influences cell fate transitions and regenerative efficiency (Table .2). Through tailored physicochemical properties and surface modifications, biomaterials can modulate key PTM processes, aligning with the regenerative biological characteristics of different tissues. For instance, the responsiveness of bone tissue to mechanical environments necessitates biomaterials with appropriate mechanical strength and the ability to simulate complex biomechanical conditions. Such materials enable the regulation of dynamic modifications in osteogenesis-related proteins via mechanical signaling [[208], [209], [210]]. Additionally, the specific binding capacity of metal ions can be harnessed to target and modulate PTM of key proteins involved in bone metabolism. Surface modifications on biomaterials can also mediate osteogenic differentiation under various PTM patterns through integrin signaling. In vascular and muscular regeneration, which are highly sensitive to hemodynamics and mechanical stress [211], biomaterials can improve hypoxic microenvironments through targeted supply strategies, thereby regulating corresponding PTM processes and promoting endothelial cell proliferation and migration. Correspondingly, precise delivery of mechanical stimuli and appropriate stimulation patterns critically determine the efficiency of myocyte generation [[212], [213], [214]]. The uniqueness of neural regeneration lies in its sensitivity to both external microenvironments and internal inhibitory molecules [215,216]. Biomaterials with specific surface topographies and distinct physicochemical properties can guide the direction of neural differentiation, overcoming the limitations of conventional neurotrophic factor delivery strategies. Furthermore, the ability of biomaterials to specifically target PTM processes and inhibit the pathogenic effects of aberrant proteins constitutes a unique mechanism supporting neural regeneration. This interaction between biomaterials and PTM, based on tissue-specific requirements, advances regenerative medicine from mere physical support to a new stage of dynamic molecular regulation. By deciphering tissue-specific characteristics and key protein modifications during regeneration, biomaterial design is progressively shifting toward active intervention strategies, offering a new theoretical foundation for the regeneration and repair of complex tissues and organs. It should be noted that different PTM types do not operate in isolation. They often engage in crosstalk, forming complex regulatory networks that finely modulate the intensity and duration of key signaling pathway activation during regeneration. Addressing this complexity requires consideration of how the diverse properties of biomaterials can be harnessed to modulate PTM, so as to cope with the intricate interplay of PTM networks.

PTM regulations in various tissue regeneration.

PTM in bone regeneration

Bone regeneration is a complex physiological process involving multiple cellular and molecular mechanisms [217]. Following bone injury, local inflammatory responses are activated and release chemokines to recruit bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), which secrete bone matrix. Simultaneously, osteoclasts are activated to maintain bone remodeling homeostasis [218,219]. Additionally, vascular endothelial cells promote the formation of new blood vessels to supply nutrients and oxygen for bone regeneration [220,221]. Growth factors such as bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and angiogenesis through their respective signaling pathways [222,223]. Throughout bone regeneration, PTM dynamically modulate cellular functions and matrix remodeling to achieve precise bone repair. Ubiquitination plays a critical role in the osteogenic differentiation of human BMSCs (hBMSCs) and the balance between osteogenesis and osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 7a) [224]. For example, the deubiquitinating enzyme ubiquitin specific peptidase 53 (USP53) competitively binds to β-catenin with F-box only protein 31 (FBXO31), reducing FBXO31-mediated ubiquitination of β-catenin. This stabilizes β-catenin and enhances Wnt/β-catenin signaling, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation of hBMSCs (Fig. 7b and c) [189]. Ubiquitin specific peptidase 1 (USP1) inhibits osteoblast pyroptosis by suppressing tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) ubiquitination [227], whereas ubiquitin specific peptidase 4 (USP4) inhibits Wnt/β-catenin signaling through deubiquitination of dishevelled (Dvl) protein, attenuating osteoblast differentiation [228,229]. Cylindromatosis (CYLD) inhibits osteoclast formation by suppressing receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK) signaling via deubiquitination of TRAF6 [230]. Furthermore, the E3 ubiquitin ligase Smurf1 not only inhibits osteoblast differentiation and contributes to osteoporosis by ubiquitinating and degrading runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and BMP [[231], [232], [233]], but also impairs mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) function via the P53/miR-17/Smurf1 pathway, thereby affecting bone formation [234]. Smurf2, which is highly homologous to Smurf1, inhibits transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling by ubiquitinating TGF-β receptors and Smad proteins, thus modulating bone homeostasis [235]. In recent years, circular RNAs (circRNAs) have emerged as important regulators in various physiological and pathological processes [236]. For instance, circular RNA phosphodiesterase 4B (circPDE4B) acts as a scaffold that facilitates the interaction between resistance to inhibitors of cholinesterase-8A (Ric-8A) and the E3 ubiquitin ligase MID1. This promotes ubiquitination of Ric-8A at lysine 415, leading to its proteasomal degradation. Consequently, phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of p38 MAPK are inhibited, resulting in elevated expression of sry-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), collagen type II alpha 1 chain (COL2A1) and aggrecan, which enhances chondrocyte proliferation and ECM synthesis (Fig. 7d and e) [225].

Histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs) play critical roles in bone remodeling (Fig. 7f) [226]. For instance, p300 and CBP acetylate key transcription factors such as gata binding protein 4 (GATA4), Runx2, and osterix, enhancing their stability and transcriptional activity during osteoblast differentiation [226]. Additionally, p300 facilitates the recruitment of RNA polymerase II and increases histone acetylation at the matrix metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13) promoter in response to parathyroid hormone (PTH) signaling, thereby participating in ECM remodeling [237]. P300/CBP-associated factor (PCAF), a member of the Gcn5-related N-acetyltransferase (GNAT) family, promotes osteoblast differentiation by acetylating and activating Runx2 [238], while GCN5 enhances histone acetylation at Wnt gene promoters to stimulate osteogenic differentiation of MSCs [239]. Moreover, tat-interactive protein 60 kDa (Tip60) activates chondrogenesis-related genes by increasing the acetylation of SOX9 [240]. However, under pathological skeletal conditions, dysregulated HAT activity and altered histone acetylation patterns disrupt normal bone development, homeostasis, and cellular function, thereby contributing to skeletal disorders. For example, mutations in lysine acetyltransferase 6A (KAT6A) lead to aberrant H3K9 and H3K18 acetylation and impaired P53 signaling, resulting in a novel syndrome characterized by microcephaly and global developmental delay [241]. In osteosarcoma, p300 acetylates the JmjC domain-containing histone demethylase 1A (JHDM1A) at lysine 409, which attenuates its binding to nucleosomes and suppresses its H3K36me2 demethylase activity. This leads to transcriptional upregulation of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1a (p21) and p53 up-regulated modulator of apoptosis (PUMA), ultimately inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis [242]. Notably, microRNAs (miRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) regulate histone acetylation through diverse mechanisms, influencing skeletal and chondral development. For example, miR-132-3p directly targets the histone acetyltransferase E1A binding protein p300 (Ep300) and inhibits its activation of Runx2 [243]. In contrast, miR-29a counteracts the adverse effects of glucocorticoids on mineralization and adipogenic responses in the bone microenvironment by suppressing HDAC4 expression, thereby stabilizing the acetylation of Runx2 and β-catenin [244]. Furthermore, lncRNA AK141205 upregulates CXC chemokine ligand-13 (CXCL13) expression by promoting histone H4 acetylation at the CXCL13 gene promoter, which in turn enhances the expression of genes associated with osteogenic differentiation [245]. This multi-dimensional regulatory network provides dynamic molecular-scale control mechanisms for bone regeneration.

Biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation for bone regeneration

Bone regeneration critically depends on the mechanical microenvironment, where bone tissue cells utilize mechanotransduction networks to achieve tissue-specific regeneration. Under physiological conditions, dynamic mechanical loading maintains bone metabolic homeostasis by balancing osteoblast and osteoclast activity. This balance is strictly dependent on matrix stiffness. When the elastic modulus of a material deviates from that of natural bone tissue, it disrupts the activation threshold of the integrin-mediated FAK/p130Cas signaling axis, impairs cytoskeletal tension transmission efficiency, and consequently hinders osteogenic differentiation of MSCs [[246], [247], [248], [249]]. It is important to note that the nucleus serves as a critical mechanosensor, directly influencing epigenetic modifications and gene expression, thus representing a key regulatory target in bone regeneration [250,251]. For instance, in photocrosslinked hydrogel systems, increasing matrix stiffness from 1 kPa to 12 kPa significantly enhances cytoskeletal tension, which is transmitted to the nucleus. This nuclear tension subsequently inhibits HDACs activity, elevates histone acetylation levels, and upregulates the expression of the osteogenic transcription factor Runx2, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 8a–d) [252]. Therefore, selecting biomaterials that match the mechanical properties of native bone tissue can better facilitate cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, ultimately improving bone regeneration efficiency. For instance, citrate is a key metabolite in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle that strengthens hydrogen bonding and ionic coordination with gelatin and chitosan through the Hofmeister effect. This significantly enhances the mechanical properties and anti-swelling capacity of composite hydrogels. The transmitted mechanical signals help increase intracellular acetyl-CoA levels and provide more acetyl groups, promoting H3K9 acetylation. This enhances the transcription of Runx2 and osterix and advances bone regeneration (Fig. 8e) [253]. Collagen is the most abundant protein in mammals and exhibits excellent cell affinity. It is widely used as a natural material in bone tissue engineering. However, collagen alone suffers from poor mechanical properties and rapid degradation [256,257]. Chitosan has a lower degradation rate and higher mechanical strength. When combined with collagen, it compensates for these limitations. The composite significantly increases ERK1/2 phosphorylation and activates Runx2 transcriptional activity through ERK1/2, thereby promoting osteogenic differentiation and mineralization [258]. The BMP family represents the most commonly used growth factor for inducing osteogenesis. However, the high cost of BMP and complications such as ectopic bone formation and resorption at supraphysiological doses highlight the need to explore alternative clinical strategies for bone regeneration [259,260]. In the canonical BMP pathway, BMP dimers bind to BMP receptor complexes and activate Smad1/5/8 phosphorylation, which then complexes with Smad4 and translocates into the nucleus to initiate transcription of various genes. In contrast, non-canonical pathways activate ERK and p38 phosphorylation and target receptor Smads for proteasomal degradation, thereby inhibiting Smad-mediated transcriptional responses [261]. Notably, the mineralization capacity of bone regeneration scaffolds can influence protein phosphorylation independently of BMP, initiating distinct signaling pathways for bone regeneration. For instance, incorporating nanoparticulate calcium phosphate mineral enables collagen-glycosaminoglycan scaffolds to gain mineralization capacity. Mineralized scaffolds exhibit significantly improved mechanical properties due to calcium phosphate deposition, which better promotes Smad1/5/8 phosphorylation to activate the canonical pathway while suppressing ERK1/2 phosphorylation to attenuate the non-canonical pathway. This results in higher osteogenic gene expression in human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) [261]. Similarly, nanoparticulate mineralized collagen glycosaminoglycan (MC-GAG) scaffolds can induce bone regeneration without exogenous growth factors, differentially regulating PTM based on their mechanical properties. For example, non-crosslinked MC-GAG (NX-MC) has a lower elastic modulus, whereas chemical crosslinking with 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) significantly increases the modulus of crosslinked MC-GAG (MC). Compared to NX-MC, MC-cultured hMSCs show higher phosphorylation of FAK and Smad1/5, promoting osteogenic differentiation [262]. Although the difference in Smad1/5 phosphorylation between MC and NX-MC diminishes over time, the early phosphorylation events in MC still play a dominant role in driving bone differentiation.

Cell-adhesive peptides modified on biomaterial surfaces can mimic ECM proteins and bind to integrins, thereby promoting cell adhesion [263]. By altering the types of surface-modified peptides, it is possible to regulate binding to different integrin molecules, influence downstream protein phosphorylation, and control the direction of osteogenic differentiation. For example, under stimulation by BMP-9 and its derived peptides, fibronectin-derived adhesive peptides immobilized on polycaprolactone (PCL) films bind more tightly to the β1 integrin subunit on the cell surface, thereby accelerating JNK and p38 phosphorylation and enhancing the osteogenic differentiation [264]. In contrast, adhesive peptides derived from bone sialoprotein inhibit JNK phosphorylation and promote ERK1/2 phosphorylation by binding to integrin αvβ3, consequently suppressing osteogenic differentiation [264]. Bone defects caused by trauma, infection or tumors often cannot heal naturally and require autologous or allogeneic bone grafts for repair. However, these approaches face challenges such as high cost, limited donor availability, donor site complications, and infection risks [265]. As a novel bone repair material, magnesium phosphate cement (MPC) exhibits good biocompatibility and degradability. The addition of carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC) results in CMPC, which extends the curing time of MPC and improves its injectability. This modification significantly enhances fibronectin adsorption, providing more adhesion sites for cells. Simultaneously, released magnesium ion modulate integrin conformation and activity, strengthening their binding capacity to fibronectin. This notably promotes phosphorylation of FAK and ERK, thereby enhancing cell proliferation and osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 8f) [254]. Furthermore, metal ions exhibit unique effects in promoting cartilage repair. For instance, lithium-substituted bioglass ceramic (Li-BGC) release lithium ions, which enhance the expression of miR-455-3p in exosomes secreted by BMSCs. This suppresses HDAC2 and increases histone H3 acetylation, promoting chondrocyte proliferation, migration, and matrix synthesis, thereby accelerating cartilage regeneration (Fig. 8g and h) [255]. Notably, BMSCs treated with lithium chloride (LiCl) demonstrate similar exosome-mediated regulation and cartilage repair effects, further confirming the role of lithium ions in modulating PTM for biological outcomes [255].

PTM in vascular and muscular regeneration

Angiogenesis reestablishes microcirculation through endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and lumen formation [266]. VEGF and its receptors play critical roles in this process [267]. Membrane-localized vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-1 (mVEGFR1) is generally considered a decoy receptor that negatively regulates vascular morphogenesis, and its stability is maintained by palmitoylation [205]. Vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) primarily mediates VEGF-induced endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and survival [268]. Ras-related in brain (Rab) proteins belong to the small GTPase family and regulate vesicle transport and fusion [269]. Specifically, ras-related in brain 27a (Rab27a) influences VEGFR1 stability by controlling the localization of the palmitoyltransferase DHHC3. Knockdown of Rab27a increases DHHC3 accumulation in the Golgi apparatus, which reduces mVEGFR1 palmitoylation and promotes VEGFR1 lysosomal degradation. This process enhances vascular sprouting and angiogenesis in vivo (Fig. 9a–d) [205]. Ubiquitination also modulates angiogenesis by regulating hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) stability. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α forms a heterodimer with hypoxia-inducible factor 1-beta (HIF-1β) to promote angiogenesis-related gene expression, while von hippel-lindau tumor suppressor (VHL) targets HIF-1α for ubiquitination and degradation, thereby influencing tissue blood supply (Fig. 9e) [270]. Normal endothelial cell metabolism represents another important driver of angiogenesis [272]. Glycosylation substrate function as novel regulators of angiogenesis that modulate endothelial cell metabolism through glycosylation [273]. For example, D-mannosamine (ManN) inhibits lipid-linked oligosaccharide synthesis and alters glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) biosynthesis and hybrid glycan production, consequently impairing N-glycosylation of proteins including VEGFR2 [202]. Additionally, ManN reduces ion intensity of O-glycans such as Sialyl-Core1, di-sialylated Core1, Core2, and di-galactosylated Core2, leading to impaired O-glycosylation [202]. This glycosylation inhibition remodels endothelial cell metabolism through activation of the JNK signaling pathway and endoplasmic reticulum stress-mediated UPR, collectively promoting a pro-angiogenic phenotypic shift and accelerating blood flow recovery in skin wounds.

The regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle primarily relies on muscle stem cells (MuSCs). Upon stimulation by injury or activation signals, MuSCs become activated and enter the cell cycle, where they undergo proliferation and differentiation regulated by a series of myogenic regulatory factors [274,275]. During this process, histone lysine demethylases (KDMs) utilize metabolic cofactors such as flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and α-ketoglutaric acid to regulate MuSC activation, proliferation, and differentiation [276]. For instance, KDM2B inhibits muscle differentiation by demethylating serum response factor (SRF) and blocking its binding to muscle gene promoters [277], whereas KDM4A promotes cyclin D1 expression and drives myoblast proliferation by demethylating H3K9me3 on the myogenic factor 5 (Myf5) promoter [278]. Iron, as an essential trace element for numerous metabolic and biochemical processes [279,280], plays a critical regulatory role in PTM during skeletal muscle differentiation and regeneration. Ferritinophagy mediates ferritin degradation through nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) to replenish intracellular iron ion levels [281,282]. However, under iron-deficient conditions, NCOA4 binds to the E3 ubiquitin ligase ring finger protein 20 (RNF20) and degrades it via the autophagy-lysosome pathway. This reduces histone H2B monoubiquitination at lysine-120 (H2Bub1) levels on promoters of skeletal muscle regeneration-related genes such as MyoD and MyoG, thereby inhibiting skeletal muscle differentiation and regeneration. Consequently, restoring RNF20 expression or inhibiting ferritinophagy can reverse the suppressive effects of iron deficiency on skeletal muscle regeneration (Fig. 9f and g) [271]. Interestingly, muscle injury can induce systemic responses, where damage triggers reactions in undamaged tissues remote from the injury site. For example, leg muscle injury in mice activates stem cells in the contralateral leg, transitioning them from a quiescent state to an alert state to prepare for potential future injuries [283,284]. Notably, injury-induced ERK activity waves propagate in planarian flatworms at remarkably high speeds (approximately 1 mm/h), which is 10–100 times faster than in other multicellular tissues [285]. This rapid propagation depends on the longitudinal body wall muscles of planarians, where muscle cells form dense parallel tracks that function as a "highway” for signal transmission. This allows rapid ERK phosphorylation and activation, relaying signals to other cell types to activate wound responses in distal tissues and coordinate whole-body regeneration.

Biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation for vascular and muscular regeneration

eNOS plays a key regulatory role in vascular regeneration by catalyzing the production of NO from L-arginine [286]. NO activates soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC), increasing cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) levels to promote endothelial cell migration and inhibit apoptosis, thereby establishing a foundation for new blood vessel formation [287]. Furthermore, NO enhances the pro-angiogenic effects of VEGF by stabilizing HIF-1α, while also mitigating inflammatory damage to the vascular endothelium [288]. More importantly, NO-mediated vasodilation improves microcirculation in ischemic tissues, creating a favorable microenvironment for vascular regeneration [289]. Under physiological conditions, phosphorylation of eNOS at serine 1177 promotes NO release and regulates collateral vessel adaptation in ischemic organs [290]. However, under hypoxic conditions, factors such as reduced blood flow, increased arginase expression, and decreased arginine availability lead to diminished eNOS phosphorylation. An injectable chitosan hydrogel incorporating nanocurcumin and L-arginine (nC/R gel) leverages the antioxidant properties of nanocurcumin and the eNOS-promoting effects of L-arginine to significantly enhance eNOS phosphorylation, synergistically preventing hypoxia-induced endothelial dysfunction and promoting angiogenesis and repair [291]. Embryonic stem cells (ESCs), capable of self-renewal and differentiation, can be effectively directed toward endothelial lineage using a hydrogel system formed by a β-galactosidase (β-Gal)-caged NO donor conjugated with chitosan. Controlled NO release mediates phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) degradation, promoting Akt phosphorylation and facilitating endothelial differentiation of ESCs, thereby providing a robust cellular source for improving neovascularization and blood flow recovery in ischemic tissues [292]. Notably, diabetic ulcers (DUs) are difficult to heal due to reduced blood supply and infection [293]. In a near-infrared (NIR)-triggered NO nanogenerator (SNP@UCM), an upconversion nanoparticle (UCNP) core converts NIR to visible light, triggering degradation of the NO donor sodium nitroprusside (SNP) and subsequent NO release. NO reduces the binding between HIF-1α and pVHL, inhibiting HIF-1α ubiquitination, prolonging its stability, and promoting VEGF secretion and angiogenesis, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for DUs (Fig. 10a–d) [294]. Critical limb ischemia (CLI), primarily caused by occlusion of major lower extremity vessels, is characterized by microcirculatory impairment, muscle necrosis, and inflammation [297]. Traditional revascularization strategies such as angioplasty or surgical bypass often fail to meet limb salvage expectations, with amputation rates reaching 10%–40% within six months [295,298]. Thus, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches. Recent studies have highlighted the role of glycosylation in ischemia-related pathological events. For example, the relative abundance of high-mannose structures such as Man7 and Man9 increases significantly after ischemia, while the abundance of hybrid and bi-antennary N-glycans decreases markedly [295]. These dynamic glycosylation changes reflect cellular adaptive responses to environmental stress under ischemic conditions and may play important roles in tissue damage and repair. Elastin-like recombinamer (ELR) hydrogels, designed based on the pentapeptide repeat sequence (VPGXG, where X is any amino acid except proline) of natural elastin, can be engineered to carry matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-binding sites and arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) sequences. These hydrogels attract MMPs and enhance their degradative activity, releasing elastin-derived peptides with pro-angiogenic potential. Simultaneously, RGD mediates integrin binding, promoting cell adhesion and migration to advance angiogenesis. Importantly, ELR hydrogel treatment significantly reduces the relative abundance of total high-mannose structures and markedly increases α-(2,6)-sialylation, thereby substantially improving vascular regeneration and tissue repair in ischemic environments (Fig. 10e) [295].

Myogenesis involves the proliferation and differentiation of muscle progenitor cells, their fusion into myotubes, and eventual development into contractile muscle fibers [299]. Among various external regulatory signals, dynamic mechanical loading plays a crucial role in muscle tissue development and functional maintenance [300]. Optomechanical actuators (OMAs) are composite nanoparticles consisting of a gold nanorod (AuNR) core and a responsive hydrogel shell. The AuNR core strongly absorbs light in the NIR region at 785 nm wavelength, rapidly converting optical energy into heat. This triggers a volume phase transition in the hydrogel shell, enabling reversible mechanical force application at the nanoscale with high spatiotemporal precision. Furthermore, RGD peptides conjugated on the OMA surface specifically bind to integrin receptors on cell membranes, directly transmitting mechanical forces into cells. This significantly promotes ERK phosphorylation and yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1) nuclear localization, thereby enhancing the expression of myogenesis-related genes (Fig. 10f–i) [296]. Notably, OMA stimulation exhibits spatiotemporal specificity, where the locality and directionality of force application are critical for cellular responses. For instance, linear stimulation patterns promote cell alignment and myogenesis more effectively than circular patterns. Significant enhancement of YAP1 nuclear localization and ERK phosphorylation occurs only when stimulation is applied to specific cellular regions, particularly near the nucleus. Interestingly, although mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1/2 (MEK1/2) serves as the key upstream kinase activating ERK1/2 in the ERK signaling pathway, OMA stimulation can restore myogenesis-related indicators and promote muscle regeneration even in the presence of MEK1/2 inhibitors.

PTM in neural regeneration

Axonal regeneration is a critical process for functional recovery after nerve injury. When nerve fibers are severed, damaged axons regrow to restore neural signal transmission. The growth and regeneration of neuronal axons require complex molecular mechanisms that regulate specific transcription factors, growth cone components, and signal transduction mediators. Damaged axons in the adult central nervous system (CNS) fail to regenerate due to a marked decline in the ability to activate growth genes and form growth cones as neurons mature, combined with inhibitory molecules and scar tissue in the CNS microenvironment [301,302]. During neural regeneration, maintaining ubiquitination homeostasis helps provide a stable internal environment for repair [303]. For example, Inhibitor of DNA binding 2 (Id2) promotes axonal growth by binding to basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) factors and inhibiting their transcriptional activity [304]. However, cdc20 homolog 1 (Cdh1), a regulatory subunit of the E3 ubiquitin ligase anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of Id2. Reactivating the Akt/Id2 signaling pathway in the injured CNS phosphorylates Id2 at serine 14, reducing its binding to Cdh1 and enhancing Id2 stability. This enables Id2 enrichment in the peripheral region of the growth cone and promotes its interaction with radixin, significantly improving growth cone morphology and motility, thereby facilitating axonal growth and branching [305]. Additionally, the E3 ubiquitin ligase Neurl1 regulates the level of jagged canonical notch ligand 1 (Jagged1), a ligand of neurogenic locus notch homolog protein 1(Notch1), through ubiquitination and endocytosis, thereby influencing Notch pathway activation [306]. Neuritin, a neurotrophic factor, markedly reduces neuralized E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (Neurl1)-mediated ubiquitination of Jagged1 by inhibiting Neurl1 enzymatic activity and altering the affinity between Neurl1 and Jagged1. This increases the accumulation of Jagged1 on the cell surface, inhibits Notch signaling, promotes neurite outgrowth and synaptic maturation, and suppresses neuronal apoptosis. Neuritin also accelerates the degradation of Neurl1 via the 26S proteasome pathway, further inhibiting Jagged1 ubiquitination (Fig. 11a) [306]. It is noteworthy that SUMOylation, a modification analogous to ubiquitination, participates in neural regeneration through its unique functions [310]. For instance, under normal conditions, tyrosyl-DNA phosphodiesterase 2 (TDPT-1) inhibits the transcriptional activity of ETS variant transcription factor 4 (ETS-4) by inducing its SUMOylation, thereby suppressing the expression of the suppressor of VHP-1-2 (svh-2) gene. However, after axonal injury, the degradation of MAD-like 1 (MDL-1) releases MAX-like 1 (MXL-1), which interacts with TDPT-1 and inhibits the SUMOylation of ETS-4. This allows ETS-4 to be phosphorylated by protein kinase A (PKA) and form a complex with CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein 1 (CEBP-1), ultimately promoting the expression of svh-2 and facilitating axonal regeneration (Fig. 11b) [307].

Microtubules serve as the core structural components of the neuronal axon cytoskeleton. They are polymerized from α/β-tubulin heterodimers and maintain structural stability through dynamic assembly and disassembly. During neural regeneration, the rigid structure of microtubules provides physical support for the axonal growth cone and guides the directional growth of newly formed axons [[311], [312], [313]]. Furthermore, microtubules function as intracellular transport tracks, enabling motor proteins to deliver proteins, organelles, and other essential materials from the cell body to the distal axon, thereby supplying the necessary resources for regeneration [314,315]. Studies indicate that protein phosphorylation regulates multiple signaling pathways within neurons through reversible modifications, influencing microtubule function and neural regeneration. For example, phosphorylation of β-tubulin at Ser172 significantly shortens neurites in posterior lateral mechanosensory neuron (PLM) and anterior lateral mechanosensory neuron (ALM) neurons and confers a strong anti-neurite growth phenotype. Conversely, its dephosphorylation induces aberrant outgrowth in the posterior neurites of ALM neurons (ALM-PN). Phosphorylation of β-tubulin at Ser172 also increases the number of end-binding protein 2 (EBP-2) comets, enhancing microtubule dynamics [316]. Additionally, lncRNAs contribute to neural development and injury repair through spatiotemporal regulation of gene expression [[317], [318], [319]]. For instance, neurite growth-related lncRNA 1 (Ngrl1) is rapidly upregulated after sciatic nerve injury and enhances Akt phosphorylation levels, effectively promoting axonal regeneration via activation of the PI3K–Akt signaling pathway (Fig. 11c and d) [308].

PARylation is a PTM process catalyzed by the poly (ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs). PARylation regulates protein function through the covalent attachment of ADP-ribose to target proteins [320]. In non-neural tissues, PARP1 accounts for over 90% of cellular protein PARylation and plays critical roles in DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, gene expression regulation, and cell survival [321,322]. Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an inflammatory demyelinating disease of the CNS characterized by oligodendrocyte damage and failed remyelination. The differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) and subsequent myelination are essential processes for CNS myelination and regeneration [[323], [324], [325]]. Research demonstrates that PARP1 modifies the myelin expression factor 2 (Myef2) protein via PARylation, leading to its dissociation from myelin protein mRNA. This relieves the inhibitory effect of Myef2 on myelin protein expression and promotes OPC differentiation and myelination (Fig. 11e) [309]. Moreover, enhancing PARylation stability by inhibiting the poly (ADP-ribose) glycohydrolase (PARG) enzyme further facilitates OPC differentiation and myelination. These findings indicate that PARP1-mediated PARylation activity is vital for oligodendrocyte regeneration and myelin repair, suggesting its potential as a novel therapeutic target for demyelinating diseases. Farnesyltransferase (FT) and geranylgeranyltransferase (GGT) are key enzymes involved in protein isoprenylation. FT catalyzes farnesylation by adding a 15-carbon farnesyl lipid group to proteins, whereas GGT catalyzes geranylgeranylation through the addition of a 20-carbon geranylgeranyl lipid group [[326], [327], [328]]. Under normal conditions, elevated levels of protein isoprenylation function as an endogenous brake that restricts axonal growth. In contrast, during brain injury or neurodegenerative diseases, the use of statins and protein isoprenylation inhibitors significantly promotes axonal growth by suppressing the protein isoprenylation pathway (Fig. 11f) [206].

Biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation for neural regeneration

Neural stem cells (NSCs) are multipotent cells with self-renewal capacity in the CNS. Under specific conditions, they can differentiate into neurons, oligodendrocytes, and astrocytes [329]. Precise control of NSC differentiation is central to their application in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine [330]. Recent advances in nanotechnology have introduced new possibilities for stem cell research. For instance, graphene oxide nanoparticles (GONPs) exhibit size-dependent effects on the differentiation of mouse neural stem cells (mNSCs). GONPs with sizes of 417 nm and 663 nm better maintain mNSC self-renewal capacity in the absence of EGF and bFGF, and 417 nm GONPs significantly enhance mNSC migration. However, only 4651 nm GONPs promote mNSC differentiation into neurons and astrocytes by downregulating transient receptor potential canonical 2 (TRPC2) gene expression and triggering ERK1/2 phosphorylation (Fig. 12a–d) [331]. It is noteworthy that direct lineage reprogramming (DLR) enables the direct conversion of one somatic cell type into another through the expression of specific transcription factors, without passing through a pluripotent intermediate state [333]. Compared to induced NSCs, DLR avoids the potential accumulation of genetic abnormalities and tumorigenic risks associated with induced pluripotent cells [334]. Moreover, surface topological features of biomaterials can regulate PTM during DLR to influence neural regeneration efficiency. Cell patterning dictates the mode of cell-cell contact, the establishment of polarity and the mechanical environment experienced by cells. Each of these factors can influence PTM through the activation of specific signaling pathways. For example, nanogrooves with a width of 400 nm induce the highest degree of cell alignment compared to flat surfaces and 1.2 μm wide microgrooves. These nanogrooves significantly upregulate histone H3K4 trimethylation levels, thereby enhancing the reprogramming efficiency of fibroblasts into induced dopaminergic neurons (iDA neurons) (Fig. 12e–h) [332]. Nanogrooves induce cytoskeletal reorganization, which in turn alters nuclear morphology and nuclear matrix tension, thereby promoting epigenetic modifications such as histone H3K4 trimethylation. Cell patterning thus acts as an upstream regulatory factor, indirectly modulating the dynamic balance of PTM through changes in local signaling molecule concentrations and the mechanical environment, and consequently influencing cell function and tissue homeostasis. Electromagnetic fields (EMFs) can promote DLR efficiency by delivering specific stimuli. For instance, mercapto (methoxypolyethylene glycol) (mPEG350-SH) and the heptapeptide CYGRGDS form stable covalent bonds with gold and induce magnetic polarization through the Fermi hole effect, improving electromagnetic energy transfer efficiency. Under specific EMF conditions, magnetized AuNPs efficiently promote DLR both in vitro and in vivo, inducing the formation of dopaminergic neurons [335]. Notably, electromagnetic stimulation specifically activates the histone acetyltransferase Brd2, enhancing histone H3K27 acetylation and promoting robust activation of neuron-specific genes [335].

Traumatic neuropathies are primarily induced by physical injury or sustained mechanical compression, characterized by axonal rupture accompanied by myelin sheath disruption, leading to impaired nerve conduction [336]. For instance, spinal cord injury (SCI) triggers an inflammatory response involving glial scar formation and increased vascular permeability at the lesion site. The glial scar constitutes a major impediment to axonal regeneration after SCI [337]. Tauroursodeoxycholic acid (TUDCA), a hydrophilic bile acid with cytoprotective properties and chaperone-like activity, mitigates liver injury and endoplasmic reticulum stress [338]. TUDCA exhibits anti-neuroinflammatory effects following SCI. Its incorporation into hydrogels significantly enhances dephosphorylation of key proteins such as ERK and JNK, reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine production and cell apoptosis, thereby promoting axonal repair and functional recovery [339]. Protein misfolding and aggregation underlie neurodegenerative diseases, which pose growing public health challenges worldwide. Misfolded and abnormally deposited proteins not only directly cause mitochondrial dysfunction and synaptic loss in neurons but also activate microglia, leading to chronic neuroinflammation and forming a regeneration-hostile microenvironment [340]. Alzheimer's disease (AD), for example, is pathologically defined by extracellular deposition of insoluble β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein in neurons [341]. Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles bind specifically to Aβ1-42 seeds in a dose-dependent manner, effectively inhibiting Aβ-induced tau phosphorylation and NFT formation. Increasing the glycolic acid content in PLGA enhances nanoparticle hydrophilicity and sterically hinders tau self-aggregation, further suppressing NFT formation and supporting neural repair [342]. Additionally, Aβ oligomers disrupt histone H2B acetylation in the hippocampus, impairing synaptic plasticity and memory-related gene expression. CREB-binding protein (CBP), a key transcriptional coactivator in histone acetylation, binds to enhancer regions of neuroplasticity-related genes and facilitates gene expression via histone acetylation [343]. However, CBP function is compromised under AD pathological conditions. TTK21, a small molecule activator of histone acetyltransferases CBP/p300, when conjugated with glucose-derived carbon nanospheres (CSPs) to form CSP–TTK21, potently restores H2B acetylation levels and promotes neural repair [344]. Huntington's disease (HD) is caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the huntingtin (HTT) gene, resulting in polyglutamine (polyQ)-expanded mutant HTT protein that misfolds and aggregates, ultimately leading to neuronal dysfunction and death [345]. Therapeutic options for HD remain limited, and reducing mutant HTT accumulation represents a promising strategy. Manganese ferrite (MnFe2O4) nanoparticles facilitate ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS)-mediated degradation of mutant HTT by enhancing K48-linked ubiquitination and promoting Ubiquilin-1-dependent delivery [58].

Liver regeneration

The liver possesses a remarkable regenerative capacity, enabling it to restore essential tissue architecture following long-term injury, a process dynamically regulated by PTM networks [[346], [347], [348]]. For example, after partial hepatectomy, upregulation of Nedd4-1 enhances monoubiquitination of epidermal growth factor receptor pathway substrate 15 (Eps15), promoting its binding to EGFR and accelerating EGFR internalization, thereby activating downstream mitogenic signaling pathways (Fig. 13a) [349]. In recent years, mitochondrial dysfunction has been recognized as another key factor in ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) [352], Effective inhibition of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1)-mediated mitochondrial fission can protect the liver from stress-induced damage such as IRI. For instance, during hepatic IRI, augmenter of liver regeneration (ALR) inhibits the nuclear translocation of the transcription factor YY1, which in turn reduces the transcriptional level of ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme 2 (UBA2), a subunit of the SUMO-E1 enzyme complex. This significantly suppresses Drp1 SUMOylation, attenuates its translocation to mitochondria, maintains mitochondrial integrity and function, and reduces IRI-induced apoptosis. Additionally, ALR inhibits phosphorylation of Drp1 at Ser616, further preventing its mitochondrial translocation and thereby protecting mitochondria from IRI-mediated damage [195]. Crotonylation is a novel type of PTM catalyzed by crotonyltransferases, which use crotonyl-CoA as a substrate to covalently attach a crotonyl group to lysine residues on proteins. Crotonylation can alter protein structure, activity, localization, and function. It occurs on both histone and non-histone proteins and participates in various physiological processes such as gene transcription, cell differentiation, and aging [[353], [354], [355]]. Compared with healthy individuals, patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) exhibit lower levels of crotonylation in liver tissues. Increased expression of histone deacetylases HDAC1/2 reduces crotonylation of ALR, while overexpression of lysine acetyltransferase 8 (KAT8) enhances ALR crotonylation at lysine 78. This modification helps maintain mitochondrial membrane potential, strengthens interaction with mitofusin-2 (MFN2), restores mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs), and ameliorates intracellular lipid accumulation (Fig. 13b) [350]. Histone methylation involves the addition of methyl groups to specific lysine or arginine residues on histone tails by methyltransferases, and its function is site-specific [356]. For example, H3K4me3 generally activates gene transcription, whereas H3K27me3 and H4K20me3 promote heterochromatin formation and suppress gene expression [[357], [358], [359]]. Notably, H3K27me3 exhibits reversibility during liver regeneration. In aged livers, H3K27me3 shows bidirectional dysregulation characterized by global elevation and local loss, along with abnormal enrichment at nuclear lamina regions, collectively leading to disrupted gene expression and declined cellular function. However, liver regeneration reactivates the cell cycle and triggers active dilution of H3K27me3, reducing its global levels to alleviate chromatin over-condensation while specifically restoring local patterns to reestablish cellular identity and function (Fig. 13c and d) [351]. These diverse PTMs offer potential therapeutic targets that may be leveraged by biomaterial-based strategies to promote liver regeneration and repair after injury.

Inflammatory response modulation

Inflammation initiates tissue regeneration by clearing pathogens and necrotic cellular debris from damaged areas, creating conditions for subsequent repair [360]. Macrophages serve as key immune cells that rapidly switch activation states in response to signals from the injury microenvironment, thereby modulating inflammation and tissue repair [361]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) is an important regulator of cellular energy homeostasis. Dephosphorylation at its threonine 166 (T166) site promotes macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, enhancing their role in tissue repair [362]. Additionally, T166 dephosphorylation of PPARγ strengthens its binding to promoter regions of lipid synthesis-related genes, increasing fatty acid synthesis (FAS) activity in macrophages. Enhanced FAS leads to lipid accumulation, which helps macrophages boost anti-inflammatory cytokine and reparative growth factor production via signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling activation and promotes the secretion of repair-associated proteins, thereby advancing wound healing (Fig. 14a–f) [362]. In contrast, the makorin ring finger protein 1 (MKRN1) and fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4) promote PPARγ ubiquitination and degradation, inhibiting M2 macrophage polarization and exacerbating inflammation [[364], [365], [366]]. Nod-like receptor family card domain containing 3 (NLRC3) acts as a key negative regulator linking inflammation and cell proliferation during wound healing. It mediates p53 ubiquitination and degradation in an Hsp90-dependent manner, slowing wound repair [367]. The NF-κB signaling pathway plays a critical role in inflammation during wound healing [368]. The enzyme A20 has dual ubiquitinating and deubiquitinating activities. It suppresses NF-κB activation by deubiquitinating TRAF6 and promoting the degradation of receptor-interacting protein 1 (RIP1), attenuating chronic inflammation [[369], [370], [371]]. Glycosylation supports regeneration through multiple mechanisms, such as promoting cell adhesion, tissue remodeling, and balanced inflammatory responses at injury sites, thereby aiding the establishment and maintenance of a regenerative microenvironment [4,372]. For example, O-linked N-acetylglucosaminylation (O-GlcNAcylation) is a dynamic and reversible PTM. It involves adding O-linked β-D-N-acetylglucosamine (O-GlcNAc) monosaccharides to serine or threonine residues of proteins [373,374]. Inhibiting O-GlcNAcase (OGA) enhances O-GlcNAcylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (STAT6), increasing its stability and transcriptional activity. This activates the STAT6/peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-beta (PGC-1β) axis, promoting lipid uptake and fatty acid oxidation in M2 macrophages to alleviate inflammation and support tissue repair (Fig. 14g–i) [363]. Pro-inflammatory mediators like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) from macrophages help clear pathogens and necrotic tissue early in injury. However, excessive levels can cause fibrosis and impaired regeneration [375,376]. A-AuNPs are gold nanoflowers synthesized using root and stem bark extracts of Acanthopanax sessiliflorus. These nanoparticles have a flower-like structure and high specific surface area. They can be rapidly synthesized at room temperature and demonstrate good stability and biocompatibility. A-AuNPs reduce LPS-induced NO and PGE2 production by inhibiting phosphorylation of MAPK signaling proteins (p38, ERK, JNK) and the inhibitor of nuclear factor kappa b kinase (IKK) complex. They also suppress protein and mRNA expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), thereby decreasing inflammatory mediator production in macrophages [40].

Epithelial repair

Different types of epithelial cells rapidly restore tissue barrier function by covering wounds through proliferation, migration, and differentiation [377], a process finely regulated by PTM. For example, Nedd4 suppresses keratinocyte proliferation by promoting the ubiquitination and degradation of glycoprotein 130 (GP130) [378], while ubiquitin specific peptidase 15 (USP15) enhances epithelialization through deubiquitination and stabilization of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4A1 (EIF4A1) [379]. In corneal epithelial wound healing (CEWH), miR-30 and miR-200 directly target the 3′UTR of the E3 ubiquitin ligase Nedd4 to upregulate its expression. This increases PTEN ubiquitination, leading to its degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome and lysosomal pathways. As a result, PTEN-mediated inhibition of p-STAT3 is alleviated, accelerating corneal epithelial repair (Fig. 15a) [380]. In intestinal epithelial injury repair, cullin 4B (CUL4B) acts as a scaffold protein for the cullin-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase 4B (CRL4B). It inhibits proteasome activator subunit 28 γ (PSME3) ubiquitination, promoting PSME3 accumulation and negatively regulating p53-mediated apoptosis to maintain intestinal homeostasis [381]. However, following ionizing radiation, CUL4B dissociates from PSME3, increasing PSME3 ubiquitination and reducing its inhibitory effect on p53, which enhances p53-dependent apoptosis. Concurrently, CUL4B translocates to gammaH2AX foci in the nucleus, where it suppresses breast cancer gene 1 (BRCA1) phosphorylation and rad51 homolog (RAD51) recruitment, thereby hindering DNA repair and facilitating the clearance of damaged cells. This process creates space for the regeneration and differentiation of intestinal stem cells (Fig. 15b and c) [381]. Notably, intestinal epithelial cells exhibit a high turnover rate, and their frequent proliferation and differentiation render them more vulnerable to oxidative stress. These cells are also continuously exposed to various exogenous factors, such as dietary oxidants, pathogens, and drugs, which further intensify oxidative stress [382,383]. Therefore, maintaining intracellular redox balance is essential for normal intestinal function. Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) serves as a key transcription factor that regulates the cellular antioxidant response by binding to antioxidant response elements (AREs) and activating the expression of antioxidant genes [384]. Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 associates with Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) to form an Nrf2-Keap1-Cul3 complex, leading to Nrf2 ubiquitination and degradation. However, under oxidative stress, the interaction between Nrf2 and Keap1 is disrupted, allowing Nrf2 to accumulate and translocate into the nucleus to activate ARE-driven gene expression [385,386]. Additionally, Nrf2 activation involves multiple mechanisms, including small molecule interactions and kinase pathways. Phosphorylation of Nrf2 at Ser40 is critical for its release from Keap1 and nuclear translocation [387]. Biogenic nanoselenium (BNS) particles are produced by reducing soluble selenium salts using microorganisms, plant extracts, or other biological reductants under mild conditions. This approach is cost-effective, environmentally benign, and enables control over nanoselenium morphology and size, enhancing stability and application potential [388]. Importantly, BNS facilitates Nrf2 phosphorylation at Ser40 via p38, ERK1/2, and Akt-mediated pathways, promoting Nrf2 accumulation and nuclear translocation to strengthen the antioxidant capacity of intestinal epithelial cells (Fig. 15d and e) [49].

Conclusions and perspectives

PTM precisely regulates critical biological processes such as cell proliferation, differentiation, migration, and ECM remodeling by dynamically modulating protein functional activity, thereby serving as a central regulatory mechanism in the construction of regenerative microenvironments. Biomaterials, through tailored design, enable targeted intervention in PTM networks. For instance, mechanotransduction mechanisms mimic tissue-specific mechanical environments and regulate PTM of mechanosensitive proteins via the cytoskeletal tension transmission system. Chemical composition modulation utilizes signaling molecules such as metal ions and ROS to selectively alter PTM of distinct proteins. Moreover, surface modifications allow dynamic and precise manipulation of PTM networks through interactions with specific regions of the cell membrane. Depending on tissue-specific properties and regenerative requirements, biomaterials demonstrate significant pro-regenerative effects by modulating PTM, while mitigating the systemic toxicity risks associated with conventional strategies like growth factor delivery. However, current research lacks a comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted PTM pathways involved in complex tissue regeneration. In addition, the quantitative relationship between PTM cascades and material properties remains poorly elucidated, and the complex interplay between biomaterials and PTM requires further investigation. This gap currently hinders any in-depth discussion of clinical translation. Future efforts should focus on dissecting the direct causal links between biomaterials and specific PTM events, and on establishing standardized evaluation systems in both in vitro and in vivo settings to address the current lack of preclinical data.

Here are some forward-looking perspectives.

1)Engineering artificial receptors to improve biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation

Engineering artificial receptors to improve biomaterial-mediated PTM regulation

The cellular response to external signals depends on signal transduction networks mediated by receptor proteins [389]. When ligands such as hormones and cytokines bind to plasma membrane or intracellular receptors, conformational changes in the receptors activate downstream signaling cascades. These cascades regulate transcription factor activity through PTM including phosphorylation and ubiquitination, ultimately determining cell fate such as proliferation, differentiation, or apoptosis [390,391]. Building on a thorough understanding of these natural signaling pathways, synthetic receptor design has emerged as a major focus in cell engineering. Rational design of synthetic receptors enables cells to produce predefined responses to external stimuli. This design process involves engineering signaling domains and selecting receptor scaffolds, both of which critically impact signal transduction efficiency [[392], [393], [394]]. For instance, three artificially designed synthetic receptor scaffolds incorporate JAK-binding domains and specific tyrosine motifs as signaling modules, with Myc tags attached at their C-termini. Among these, the Myristoylated (Myr) receptor is anchored to the inner plasma membrane through an N-terminal myristoylation signal sequence. In contrast, the Cytosolic (Cyt) receptor lacks any signal sequence and is expressed in the cytoplasm. The Transmembrane (TM) receptor differs by containing an anti-fluorescein single-chain Fv fused to the D2 domain of the erythropoietin receptor (EpoR), and it shows reduced phosphorylation levels of janus kinase 2 (JAK2), tyrosine kinase 2 (Tyk2), signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5), mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase (MEK) and Akt compared to the Cyt receptor (Fig. 16a) [395]. Notably, phosphorylation of the TM receptor decreases in the presence of tyrosine motifs. This variation may result from negative regulation at the plasma membrane by signaling molecules with SH2 domains, such as SHP1, acting through ITIM sequences, while such inhibitory mechanisms are absent in the cytoplasm. Consequently, the Cyt receptor more effectively promotes cell growth, offering insights for designing synthetic receptors with high signal transduction efficiency. Additionally, AI technologies leverage deep learning to rapidly analyze receptor-ligand interaction patterns, substantially improving the design efficiency and regulatory precision of synthetic receptors. This approach facilitates optimization across the entire process from molecular recognition to signal output, delivering predictable and verifiable engineering solutions for cell reprogramming and PTM regulation [397].

AI technologies demonstrate significant advantages in the study of PTM, enabling effective integration of sequence and structural information for complex computational modeling (Fig. 16b) [396]. For example, advanced programs like RosettaFold All-Atom and AlphaFold3 utilize denoising diffusion probabilistic models to directly predict protein structures incorporating PTM modifications [396]. Pre-trained protein language models achieve context-dependent prediction of protein structure and function through natural language processing techniques [398]. Models such as LMPhosSite significantly enhance phosphorylation site prediction accuracy by integrating deep semantic embeddings from these language models. Additionally, mass spectrometry-based proteomics technologies provide large-scale PTM datasets for machine learning, while public databases like PhosphoSitePlus and UniProt offer abundant resources for model training and validation [[399], [400], [401]]. In the field of structure prediction, the AlphaFold model has achieved a milestone breakthrough via its Evoformer neural network module. This model deeply integrates evolutionary information from multiple sequence alignments with physical geometric constraints, employing iterative optimization to simultaneously predict atomic-level structures of both backbone and side chains, accurately resolving domain assemblies even for long-chain proteins [402]. This high-precision predictive capability not only accelerates protein structure determination but also provides new tools for understanding PTM-induced conformational changes and their functional impacts. Currently, AI applications in PTM research primarily focus on site prediction; future efforts should strengthen exploration of functional prediction, particularly for modification events such as phosphorylation that may induce significant conformational changes, necessitating deeper integration of structural biology research with AI modeling techniques. Furthermore, with the increasing precision of virtual cell models, it may become possible in the future to predict the dynamic changes of PTM under specific signaling perturbations through computer simulation. Combined with synthetic biology and gene-editing technologies, such predictions could guide the design of engineered enzymes or scaffold proteins with site-specific modification capabilities, enabling artificial reprogramming of PTM.

3)Reverse engineering of biomaterials for targeted PTM modulation

Reverse engineering of biomaterials for targeted PTM modulation

The reverse design strategy for biomaterials targeting specific PTM centers on establishing a precise mapping relationship among tissue regeneration requirements, PTM regulatory targets, and material property parameters. This process begins with multi-omics integration to identify key PTM regulatory nodes during target tissue regeneration. Examples include the phosphorylation dynamics of postsynaptic density proteins during synaptic formation in spinal cord injury repair [403], or changes in acetylation levels of sarcomeric proteins in myocardial regeneration [404]. Building on this foundation, machine learning algorithms are employed to decipher the activity regulation patterns of modifying enzymes within the target PTM pathway and their quantitative relationships with material physicochemical properties. This enables the construction of mathematical models capable of predicting the effects of material characteristics on specific PTM pathways. For instance, molecular dynamics simulations can forecast how material surface topography influences the conformation of the integrin β1 intracellular domain, thereby quantifying its regulatory efficiency on FAK kinase autophosphorylation. Further validation involves high-throughput microfluidic platforms to examine dose-response relationships between material parameters and PTM signaling pathways. Gradient stiffness hydrogel arrays, for example, allow parallel assessment of how varying elastic moduli influence phosphorylation thresholds of YAP protein, helping to identify critical parameter ranges for material optimization. Additionally, an evaluation system should be established, incorporating techniques such as single-cell mass spectrometry to dynamically monitor PTM network reprogramming at the material interface. This reverse design strategy offers a significant advantage by transcending the conventional trial-and-error approach, thereby enhancing the precision and predictability of interventions targeting specific PTM pathways.

4)Site-specific and multiplex protein modification technologies based on Sortase-mediated transpeptidation

Site-specific and multiplex protein modification technologies based on Sortase-mediated transpeptidation

Similar to PTM, a key objective of protein engineering is to introduce desired properties into proteins that naturally lack specific functions. For instance, attaching fluorophores to proteins enables their application in live-cell microscopy imaging, while fusing antibodies with toxins or antigens supports therapeutic or vaccine development [405,406]. Current approaches for protein modification include genetic engineering, chemical modification, enzymatic modification and chemoenzymatic methods. However, genetic engineering may compromise protein expression, folding or function; chemical modification often lacks precision and can damage proteins; and enzymatic modification may require the incorporation of large catalytic domains or impose specific structural constraints [[407], [408], [409]]. Sortase-mediated transpeptidation has emerged as a promising protein modification strategy due to its high site specificity and broad applicability. Sortases are membrane-associated transpeptidases derived from Gram-positive bacteria that recognize specific amino acid sequences and catalyze cleavage and ligation reactions, enabling the introduction of functional groups at the C-terminus or internal sites of proteins [410,411]. For example, Sortase A recognizes the LPXTG motif and cleaves between threonine and glycine to form an acyl-enzyme intermediate [411]. This is followed by nucleophilic attack from Lipid II, anchoring the protein to the cell wall. Additionally, Lipid II can be substituted with desired functional groups to achieve site-specific modifications at various protein locations. Notably, introducing the LPXTG sequence at the C-terminus or internal loops of target proteins achieves modification efficiencies exceeding 90%, and the reaction products are clearly distinguishable by SDS-PAGE. Moreover, employing Sortases with different specificities allows for multiple modifications to address more complex requirements. However, practical applications of Sortase face challenges such as limited substrate specificity and insufficient stability, necessitating ongoing optimization to expand its use in bioconjugation, protein modification, and drug development [410].

5)Thermo-responsive biomaterials for precision regulation of PTM

Thermo-responsive biomaterials for precision regulation of PTM

Protein switches regulate downstream protein functions and cellular behaviors by controlling protein conformational changes in response to external signals such as chemicals, light or temperature, enabling precise manipulation of biological processes. However, chemical induction requires molecular delivery, and optical control, despite its high spatiotemporal precision, cannot penetrate deep tissues [412,413]. Temperature, as a dynamic inducer, offers advantages such as tissue penetration and physiological relevance. Nevertheless, existing thermosensitive proteins suffer from limitations including denaturation at elevated temperatures, irreversibility, lack of modularity and potential off-target responses to non-thermal stimuli [414]. The naturally occurring photothermal-sensitive protein BcLOV4 aggregates and translocates to the cell membrane under blue light and low-temperature conditions [415]. Introduction of the Q355N mutation yields Melt, a variant that responds exclusively to temperature, enabling thermal regulation of its membrane localization and oligomerization. This allows precise control over cellular signaling, proteolytic activity and cell fate. For example, fusion of Melt with tobacco etch virus protease (MeltTEVp) permits temperature-dependent modulation of TEVp protease activity. Additionally, combining Melt with nuclear localization sequences (NLS) and nuclear export sequences (NES) enables dynamic nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of target proteins [416]. Thermally controlled biomaterials leverage the dynamic properties of temperature-sensitive proteins to precisely regulate key processes in tissue regeneration. For instance, Melt-mediated membrane localization can be used to real-time modulate the phosphorylation status of growth factor receptors, thereby activating osteogenic and angiogenic signaling pathways. When integrated with thermoresponsive hydrogel systems, localized heating triggers membrane-anchored protease cleavage, enabling on-demand release of active regenerative factors to promote stem cell differentiation. Furthermore, Melt-functionalized biomaterials can simultaneously regulate ubiquitination levels of MMPs, dynamically balancing ECM remodeling in response to thermal gradients. Finally, temperature-dependent nuclear shuttling systems driven by Melt can direct the localization of HATs to specifically activate expression of regeneration-associated genes.

6)Stimulus-responsive PROTACs and their regulatory potential

Stimulus-responsive PROTACs and their regulatory potential

External stimuli such as light, hypoxia and X-rays offer innovative solutions for remote and spatiotemporally controllable regulation of PROTAC activity. Light, a non-invasive stimulus with high spatiotemporal resolution, has been widely used to develop photo-controllable PROTACs. By introducing photocleavable caging groups, researchers have designed opto-PROTACs. These compounds remain inactive in the dark and release the active PROTAC only upon irradiation with specific wavelengths of ultraviolet light, enabling precise spatiotemporal control over target protein degradation [417]. Similarly, PHOTACs incorporate photoswitchable modules to achieve reversible activation and deactivation of protein degradation in response to different wavelengths of light. These light-based strategies significantly reduce off-target toxicity and improve therapeutic specificity [418,419]. The limited tissue penetration depth of ultraviolet light, however, restricts their application in deep-seated tumors. To address this, near-infrared (NIR)-activated PROTAC nanocages have been developed. These systems use upconversion nanoparticles to convert NIR into ultraviolet light, allowing precise control of bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) degradation in deep tissues in vivo [420]. Beyond light, the hypoxic feature of the tumor microenvironment has been exploited to design hypoxia-responsive PROTACs. Nitroreductase (NTR), which is highly expressed in hypoxic tumor tissues, has been used as a trigger for such systems. NTR-responsive PROTACs incorporate nitroimidazole or nitrophenyl groups as caging moieties. These groups are reduced by NTR only under hypoxic conditions, releasing the active PROTAC and enabling selective protein degradation in tumor tissues [421]. X-rays, with their superior tissue penetration, have also been used to activate PROTACs. For example, X-ray irradiation activated a PROTAC prodrug containing a fluorine-containing caging group, achieving in situ and efficient protein degradation at the tumor site and markedly suppressing tumor growth [422]. Smart biomaterial design offers an ideal platform for the efficient delivery and local activation of PROTACs, laying the groundwork for the development of next-generation dynamic and programmable protein degradation therapies.

7)Novel PTM modifications offer new therapeutic targets

Novel PTM modifications offer new therapeutic targets

Emerging types of protein PTM offer new possibilities for tissue regeneration. For instance, beyond its roles as an energy source and metabolic byproduct, lactate can modulate gene expression through histone lactylation (Kla) [423]. The levels of histone Kla are regulated by glycolysis and lactate production. Under conditions such as local hypoxia or bacterial infection following tissue injury, increased lactate generation significantly stimulates histone Kla [424]. During the late phase of M1 macrophage polarization, genes associated with wound healing marked by histone Kla are more prone to activation or reactivation, exhibiting features reminiscent of the M2 macrophage phenotype [423]. Moreover, in rotator cuff tendinopathy (RCT) patients, 872 Kla sites across 284 proteins have been identified in tendon tissues, showing marked differences compared to normal tissues and highlighting considerable potential as therapeutic targets [425]. Notably, vitamin C (vitC) can directly modify lysine residues on proteins via a non-enzymatic reaction, resulting in lysine vitcylation. This process is dose- and pH-dependent and preferentially targets the K298 site of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1). Vitcylation blocks the interaction between STAT1 and the T cell protein-tyrosine phosphatase (TCPTP), thereby inhibiting dephosphorylation at Y701. This stabilizes STAT1 in its activated state, amplifies type I interferon signaling, upregulates major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecule expression on tumor cells, and enhances CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [426]. In tissue regeneration, similar immune activation mechanisms may facilitate fibrotic repair or graft integration by recruiting regulatory T cells or suppressing excessive inflammatory responses. As research progresses, more novel PTM types are expected to be discovered and harnessed for modulating tissue regeneration.

In the future, the field of biomaterials and tissue regeneration will deeply integrate cutting-edge multidisciplinary technologies to enable precise regulation from the molecular to the tissue level. Through intelligent design and synthetic biology approaches, the development of novel biomaterials with self-adaptive and dynamically responsive capabilities is expected to overcome the limitations of conventional repair strategies. By integrating AI and big data analytics, researchers will gain deeper insights into the complex mechanisms of cellular microenvironments and tissue regeneration, providing a theoretical foundation for personalized regenerative therapies. Furthermore, exploring the interactions between advanced biomaterials and cells will facilitate the creation of smart biomaterials capable of precisely modulating cellular behavior and tissue regeneration processes. These advancements will bring revolutionary breakthroughs in regenerative medicine, elevating tissue repair and regeneration technologies to new heights and offering more efficient and safer solutions for clinical therapies.
