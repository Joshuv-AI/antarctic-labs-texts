A universal atomically dispersed cobalt catalyst for alkylation of ketones alcohols and lignin-derived compounds
Authors: Zhuang Ma, Binyu Zhang, Yanbin Cui, Changyue Ren, Xinmin Li, Rajenahally V Jagadeesh, Matthias Beller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCatalytic carbon-carbon bond-forming reactions provide the basis for synthetic organic chemistry. To move towards a circular carbon economy, it is imperative to efficiently convert sustainable and inexpensive starting materials, e.g. broadly available renewable feedstocks, in an economically viable and practical manner. In this work , we present a method for the atom-efficient and “waste-free” construction of C-C bonds in ketones with alcohols, including lignin-derived compounds. The broad applicability of this protocol is based on the development of a novel heterogeneous cobalt-based single atom catalyst, which is produced by subjecting a cobalt-poly(p-phenylenediamine)-silica template to pyrolysis and subsequently removing residual silica. The most effective catalyst material consists of isolated cobalt atoms with Co-N4 active sites dispersed on mesoporous carbon. The resulting material is both highly stable and reusable. The presented method facilitates the general and selective C-alkylation of aromatic, heterocyclic, and aliphatic ketones, as well as secondary alcohols. Applications include the functionalization of bioactive molecules and the preparation of pharmaceutical drugs and valuable methylated compounds. The value of the cobalt-single atom catalyst is further demonstrated in the industrially relevant C-alkylation of KA oil.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Catalyst synthesis

Introduction

The development of novel and improved sustainable catalytic methodologies for the construction of C–C, C–N, and C–O bonds is an important aspect for the advancement of modern organic synthesis. Specifically, C–C bond-forming reactions are privileged ones and are the most widely applied processes to produce all kinds of compounds, ranging from bulk chemicals to advanced and complex life science molecules1–10. For instance, transition-metal-catalyzed cross-coupling reactions have transformed the manufacturing of numerous small-molecule drugs over the past few decades. However, these processes commonly entail the utilization of halogenated compounds or organometallic reagents as coupling partners, resulting in the generation of substantial amounts of waste materials11,12. Consequently, there is an ongoing need to develop more sustainable and environmentally beneficial catalytic methodologies for C–C bond construction that utilize inexpensive and readily available starting materials and do not generate any waste. In this regard, catalytic hydrogen auto-transfer reactions, also known as borrowing hydrogen (BH) methodologies, have emerged as a promising approach for the construction of both C–C and C–N bonds1–3,5,7–10,13,14. Among the known BH methodologies, the direct catalytic C-alkylation of ketones and alcohols using primary alcohols as alkylating agents offers many advantages. The starting materials, ketones and alcohols, are easily accessible and inexpensive. Moreover, many of these substrates are either naturally occurring or can be derived from renewable resources, further enhancing the appeal of this method. This methodology employs alcohols as dual-role reagents, serving as both alkylating substrates and intrinsic hydrogen donors. This innovative approach eliminates the need for additional reducing agent, aligning with step- and atom-economy principles, and generates water as the sole by-product.

Generally, ketones and alcohols are important building blocks that can be readily transformed into a variety of products, including advanced molecules and polymeric materials15. Furthermore, keto and alcohol moieties are integral components of numerous pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and biomolecules (Fig. 1a)16–18. Given their inherent value and wide-ranging applications, there is sustained interest in developing advanced catalytic methods for the preparation and valorization of these feedstocks19. In addition to the available ketones and alcohols, which are commonly derived from petrochemical-based compounds, the utilization of biomass-derived feedstocks is of considerable importance. To avert the depletion of fossil-based resources, which are currently the primary sources of chemical production and energy, the effective utilization of renewable resources, particularly biomass-based feedstocks, has emerged as a pivotal area of chemical research20. Among the various types of biomasses, lignin, a polymeric material that is both abundant and renewable, has garnered particular attention as a potential and promising source for a wide array of aromatic compounds, such as benzene, toluene, phenols, ethers, ketones, alcohols, and aldehydes, among others21–26. Accordingly, the transformation of lignin and its derived feedstocks can contribute to a renewable synthesis of various advanced compounds. This subject has attracted considerable research attention, as it plays a significant role in addressing environmental challenges and enhancing sustainable chemical technologies. It has been demonstrated that lignin-derived compounds containing hydroxy or carbonyl groups can undergo catalytic alkylation with alcohols to yield valuable products27–31. However, to the best of our knowledge, the C-alkylation of such lignin-derived feedstocks has not yet been established.

To date, homogeneous complexes have been the primary focus for C-alkylation of ketones and alcohols8–10.13,14, Nevertheless, several heterogeneous catalysts based on Pd32, Zn33, Ni (175 °C, >20 mol% Ni)34, Cu (180 °C)35, and Fe (>30 mol% Fe)36, have been developed, too (Fig. 1b). These include also MOF-based Co (AgNTf2 as additives)37, and polymer-based Mn38. However, it should be noted that these non-noble metal-based heterogeneous systems typically require elevated reaction temperatures or high catalyst loading, as well as difficult recycling or reuse processes. Therefore, designing more active and robust heterogeneous catalysts based on earth-abundant metals for this transformation is of paramount importance for advancing sustainable synthetic protocols. Accordingly, a promising avenue for enhancing the activity of heterogeneous-based metal catalysts involves the creation of supported highly dispersed or isolated single metal atoms (SA), which emulate both heterogeneous materials and homogeneous complexes in catalysis aspects39–43. In consideration of the importance of C–C bond-forming reactions and the potential advantages of single atom catalysts (SACs), this study proposes the development and utilization of a highly dispersed cobalt catalyst for C-alkylation and -methylation of various ketones, alcohols, and lignin-derived feedstocks, as well as KA oil employing different alcohols and methanol (Fig. 1c). Notably, 3d metal single atoms have been scarcely explored in the context of C–C bond-forming reactions by BH methodologies. This is particularly evident in the realm of C-alkylation of lignin-derived ketones or alcohols, a subject that has not been previously reported.

Results and discussion

In recent years, a substantial portion of our research endeavors has been dedicated to the development of innovative heterogeneous materials for advanced organic synthesis. A particular emphasis has been placed on 3d metals in this regard. Furthermore, we have been engaged in the valorization of renewable feedstocks and energy technologies. Following these concepts, a series of Fe-, Co-, Ni- and Cu-based nanoparticles (NPs)-based44–55 and single atoms (SA)-based42,56–60 catalysts were developed and applied for synthetically relevant hydrogenation44,45,49,50, oxidation46,53,54,58,60, amination42,47,50 and coupling reactions48,55 as well as conversion of biomass-based feedstocks57,58. The development of these catalytic materials was contingent upon the utilization of specific nitrogen- or oxygen-ligated metal complexes, or analogous metal-organic frameworks, as precursors. These precursors are then immobilized on heterogeneous supports and subsequently pyrolyzed under argon, nitrogen, or hydrogen gas. This process leads to the formation of nanoparticles or highly dispersed metal atoms on various supports. It is noteworthy that this preparation method has emerged as a potential technology and has been widely applied by other research groups27,30,61–65 besides ours to produce desired nanostructured and single-atom-type catalysts.

Preparation and catalytic evolution of cobalt single atom-materials

To prepare a selection of materials with highly dispersed cobalt atoms on an appropriate support with active metal–nitrogen centers for BH processes, we started to explore suitable nitrogen precursors to create active metal centers with Co–N interactions. Compared to mono- and diamine ligands, nitrogen-based polymers such as poly(p-phenylenediamine) (PPDA) and polyaniline (PANI) derived from p-phenylenediamine (PDA) and aniline (phenylamine: PA), respectively, have been rarely employed for the construction of well-defined Co–Nx coordination sites on supports, particularly in the context of BH coupling reactions. During our preparation process, we first synthesized a poly(p-phenylenediamine)–cobalt template on silica and then pyrolyzed the materials followed by the removal of the residual SiO2 (Fig. 2). In a typical experiment, we mixed p-phenylenediamine (PDA) and LUDOX®HS-40 colloidal silica (40 wt% suspension in H2O) in HCl solution (0.5 M, 100 mL) and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. To this mixture, ammonium persulfate (APS) solution (dissolved in water) was added slowly and vigorously stirred to generate poly(p-phenylenediamine (PPDA). At this point, it should be noted that the preparation of poly(p-phenylenediamine) (PPDA) or phenylamine is typically carried out under oxidative conditions in an acidic medium using ammonium persulfate as the oxidant66. Subsequently, Co(OAc)2·4H2O was added to the suspension to form a cobalt(II)–PPDA complex. The resultant mixture was then stirred for 24 h at room temperature to ensure complete conversion. Subsequently, the removal of the solvent was conducted via freeze-drying, thereby yielding a Co-PPDA template supported on silica (Co-PPDA@SiO2). The solid material was subjected to a drying process and subsequent pyrolysis under argon at 800 °C for a duration of 2 h. The resultant pyrolyzed material was then treated with a mixture of 4 M NH4HF2 and 1 M HNO3, with the objective of removing the SiO2 template and any residual metal particles. The obtained catalytic material is denoted as Co@NC-800-PPDA, where 800 and PPDA represent the pyrolysis temperature and poly(p-phenylenediamine), respectively. In a similar manner, another material, Co@NC-800-PANI employing aniline was also prepared.

Schematic illustration of the synthetic procedure for Co–N–C catalysts via silica-template-assisted pyrolysis of cobalt-coordinated polymers.

To evaluate the catalytic performance of the prepared cobalt materials and to optimize reaction conditions, the alkylation of a lignin β-O-4 model compound, 2-phenoxy-1-phenylethanol 1, with the renewable platform alcohol 2 (furfuryl alcohol) was selected as a benchmark reaction. The following sequence of reactions is involved in this transformation: Initially, 1 and 2 undergo a dehydrogenation process, which is followed by the cleavage of the Cα–OH bond and the Cβ–O bond, resulting in the formation of acetophenone 1h and phenol 5. Subsequent to this, 1h further reacts with furfural 2b to yield 3-(furan-2-yl)−1-(4-methoxyphenyl)propan-1-one 3, which is the desired product (Fig. 3). In addition to 3 and 5, the presence of product 4, an intermediate in the formation of 3, was also observed. It is noteworthy that both the prepared materials Co@NC-800-PPDA and Co@NC-800-PANI exhibited activity and selectivity for both the hydrogenolytic cleavage of C–O bonds in 1 and the C-alkylation to provide 3, with yields reaching up to 85% (turnover frequency (TOF) = 143 h−1). However, the material prepared from p-phenylenediamine (Co@NC-800-PPDA) demonstrated slightly superior performance in comparison to the material produced using aniline (Co@NC-800-PANI, TOF = 132 h−1) (Fig. 3, columns 1 and 2). Next, Co@NC-T-PPDA was subjected to pyrolysis at temperatures of 400, 600, and 1000 °C, and the reactivity of these materials was examined in the model reaction, too. As shown in Fig. 3, columns 1, 3–5, the pyrolysis temperature plays a pivotal role in the genesis of the active catalyst. Specifically, the sample pyrolysed at 400 °C (TOF = 52 h−1) demonstrated significantly less activity, while the samples pyrolyzed at 600 (TOF = 107 h−1) and 1000 °C (TOF = 132 h−1) exhibited favorable activity and selectivity, though lower than that observed for Co@NC-800-PPDA.

Conversions and yields were determined by GC using n-hexadecane standard based on 1. For homogeneous catalysis conditions: 10 mol% of Co(OAC)2.4H2O and 30 mol% of PDA were used.

For comparison, the material prepared by the pyrolysis of cobalt acetate without any nitrogen ligand on SiO2, which contains mainly cobalt nanoparticles (Co-NPs-800), was also tested. This material demonstrated significantly reduced activity (TOF = 41 h−1, Fig. 3, column 6). The material containing unremoved silica, Co@NC-SiO2-800-PDA, exhibited poor activity, yielding 15% of 3 (TOF = 23 h−1, Fig. 3, column 7). Next, the unpyrolyzed material, Co-PPDA@SiO2, and the homogeneous catalyst (Co(OAc)2 + PDA) similarly exhibited low activity levels (Fig. 3, columns 8 and 9). Furthermore, to probe the role of the Co–N sites, we carried out a poisoning experiment using potassium thiocyanate (KSCN), which can effectively block the metal–N sites64. When 1.0 equiv. of KSCN (30 mg) was introduced into the benchmark reaction system, the catalytic activity dropped sharply (conversion of 1 <10%), clearly indicating that the active sites were effectively inhibited (Supplementary Table 4). This pronounced decline of catalytic activity strongly suggests that the Co–N coordination sites are the primary active centers responsible for the observed catalytic activity. Interestingly, the optimal catalyst Co@NC-800-PPDA is also active in the absence of a base, which is uncommon for such alkylations1–5,42,48, yielding 35% of product 3 (Supplementary Table 7). It is also noteworthy that the use of 0.6 equivalents of K3PO4 resulted in the attainment of 85% yield of 3 at 95% conversion. As expected, in the absence of a catalyst, the base did not promote the desired reaction. Next, to optimize the benchmark reaction, a comprehensive evaluation of various solvents, bases, and additional reaction parameters, including temperature, catalyst amounts, alcohol, and reaction time, was conducted (Supplementary Tables 4–6). The results of the study indicated that the most optimal conditions for the C-alkylation of 2-phenoxy-1-phenylethanol 1 with furfuryl alcohol 2 was achieved using 40 mg of Co@NC-800-PPDA (2.8 mol% Co), in the presence of 60 mol% K3PO4, under a temperature of 120 °C for a duration of 24 h in tert-BuOH as solvent.

Characterization of the as-prepared Co-based materials

To verify the detailed structural features and to understand the observed catalytic performances, the optimal catalyst Co@NC-800-PPDA, the recycled material (Co@NC-800-PPDA), and a less active catalyst (Co@NC-SiO2-800-PPDA) were characterized by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analysis, X-ray powder diffraction (XRD), scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM), Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis, Raman spectroscopy, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS).

The content of cobalt in Co@NC-800-PPDA was determined by ICP-OES, and it was found to be 1.24 wt%. The XRD patterns displayed two distinct diffraction peaks around 25° and 44° (Supplementary Fig. 1), which can be ascribed to the (002) and (101) crystal planes, respectively42. Notably, there are no diffraction peaks related to crystalline metallic cobalt or cobalt oxide that can be detected in Co@NC-800-PPDA. These XRD findings, along with the STEM analysis (Supplementary Figs. 4a, b and 6), suggest that in the optimal catalyst material, single Co atoms and/or highly dispersed Co species are present on the N-doped mesoporous carbon matrix. Indeed, Co-single atoms on carbon were also observed by HAADF-STEM analysis (Fig. 4c). These cobalt atoms are clearly identified by several separated bright dots and marked by red arrows in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 6. Besides, EDS provides the evidence of homogeneous distribution of cobalt and the presence of nitrogen species embedded in the mesoporous “carbon flakes” (Supplementary Fig. 7). In the case of the less active material, Co@NC-SiO2-800-PPDA (with remaining silica), SiO2 dominates the catalyst phase (Supplementary Fig. 4). BET measurements show that the surface area and pore volume of Co@NC-800-PPDA (SBET: 563 m2/g, pore volume: 0.114 cm−3*g−1) is much higher than that of Co@NC-SiO2-800-PPDA (SBET: 11.3 m2/g, pore volume: 0.007 cm−3*g−1). From the HAADF-STEM and BET analysis (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2), we conclude that the high surface area and mesoporosity of the optimal catalyst originate from the removal of SiO2, resulting in the generation of a mesoporous N-doped carbon framework. Afterward, the two peaks with Raman shifts around 1366 cm−1 (D bond) and 1598 cm−1 (G bond, ID/IG 1.13) observed in the Raman spectra of Co@NC-800-PPDA (Supplementary Fig. 3) also provide evidence for defects of the carbon and the graphitic carbon structure, respectively.

Next, XPS analysis was carried out to determine the surface elemental compositions and the valence state of these cobalt materials. XPS survey analysis of Co@NC-800-PPDA revealed the presence of C, N, Co, and O as well as trace amounts of Si (Supplementary Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 10). The N 1s spectra (Fig. 4d) displayed five peaks fitted with binding energies at 398.39, 399.77, 400.98, 402.90 and 405.82 eV corresponding to pyridinic, pyrrolic, graphitic, and oxidized N-atoms as well as nitrile peaks, respectively. The unexpected presence of nitrile groups is probably a result of the nitric acid etching process42,55. In the high-resolution Co 2p region, according to the deconvoluted Co 2p1/2 curves, two sub-peaks were located at approximately 780.43 and 782.63 eV are assigned to surface Co2+ and Co3+ species with a 790.80 eV satellite peak (Fig. 4e)56. Notably, XRD, STEM, and XPS (Supplementary Figs. 1, 8 and 13) analysis of the recycled catalyst Co@NC-800-PPDA-R revealed that this material displayed comparable structural features to those of the fresh catalyst (Co@NC-800-PPDA).

To further investigate the local coordination environment and chemical state of the Co atoms, Co@NC-800-PPDA was subjected to X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) analysis with Co foil, CoO, and CoPc (cobalt phthalocyanine) as reference samples. As shown in Fig. 4f, the absorption energy position of Co@NC-800-PPDA is higher than that of CoO, suggesting the oxidation state of cobalt is more than +2, which is in line with the XPS results. The local coordination environment of isolated Co atoms was probed by extended X-ray absorption fine structure spectra (EXAFS). As shown in the Fourier-transformed (FT) k2-weighted EXAFS spectra in the R space (Fig. 4g), a prominent peak at 1.41 Å (not phase-corrected) is assigned to the Co–N scattering path. In contrast to the references of Co foil and CoO, Co–Co (2.15 Å) bonds or Co–O–Co (2.58 Å) bonds were not observed in Co@NC-800-PPDA. This excludes the presence of metallic Co or CoOx nanoparticles in this sample. The atomic dispersion of Co species in Co@NC-800-PPDA was further demonstrated by wavelet transform (WT) analysis. As shown in Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 14, the WT contour plot of Co@NC-800-PPDA exhibits one intensity maximum at around 3.6 Å−1, suggesting the sole presence of isolated Co–N sites. EXAFS fitting analysis (Fig. 4h and Supplementary Fig. 15) suggests the coordination number of Co is ~4.2 with a bond distance of 1.97 Å (Supplementary Table 3), indicating the presence of Co-N4 moieties in Co@NC-800-PPDA. All these characterization data revealed that the optimal catalyst Co@NC-800-PPDA contains atomically dispersed single Co-sites on mesoporous N-doped carbon with Co–N4 centers, which apparently constitute a key structural feature of this catalyst material, which seems to be necessary to achieve high product yields and high selectivity for the C-alkylation process.

Demonstrating stability, recycling, and reusability of Co@NC-800-PPDA

To ensure potential applications, it is imperative to prioritize high stability, along with convenient recycling and reusability, for a given heterogeneous catalyst. Indeed, these aspects are crucial for achieving chemical synthesis in a more practical, sustainable, and cost-effective manner. To demonstrate the stability and recyclability of the optimal material, an examination of the heterogeneous nature of Co@NC-800-PPDA was first conducted. For this purpose, following a 10-h reaction in which 50% of the product was observed to be formed, the catalyst was removed by means of hot filtration. Thereafter, the alkylation reaction was performed once more with the filtrate. In this instance, the reaction did not proceed (Supplementary Fig. 16a; red curve), and the filtrate obtained post-reaction completion was subjected to ICP-OES analysis, which revealed the absence of cobalt species. Meanwhile, the cobalt content of recovered catalyst remained essentially identical to that of the fresh sample (Supplementary Table 11). Hence, we propose that Co@NC-800-PPDA functions as a genuine heterogeneous catalyst and that no leaching of cobalt species occurred. Afterward, the recycling and reusability of Co@NC-800-PPDA were investigated. The recycling experiments were conducted at ~50% and full conversion after 10 and 24 h, respectively. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16b, Co@NC-800-PPDA demonstrated good stability within six reaction cycles without a substantial decline in catalytic efficacy. The characterization data of the recycled catalyst also supports these results (Supplementary Figs. 1, 8, and 13).

Mechanistic investigations

To ascertain the mechanism and propose a reaction pathway for this single-atom cobalt-catalyzed C-alkylation transformation, comprehensive kinetic and control experiments have been conducted. Initially, the time course of the benchmark reaction was collected to observe the progress of the reaction and the potential formation of intermediates and byproducts. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 17, the conversion of substrate 1 was initiated immediately and was fully completed after 12 h. Simultaneously, the formation of the α,β-unsaturated product 4 increased initially and subsequently decreased over time. In addition to products 3, 4, and 5, the presence of acetophenone 1h and 2-phenoxyacetophenone 1i was detected in the gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 18). These compounds exhibited comparable reaction trends to that of product 4. To further substantiate the reaction pathway, a series of control experiments was conducted (Fig. 5a). Under standard reaction conditions, in the absence of 2, the reaction of 1 (complete conversion) gave 70 % 1h and 18 % of 1i along with the formation of 81% of 5 for 12 h (Fig. 5a (1)). Subsequently, the reaction of 1i produced 1h and 5 under the identical reaction conditions (Fig. 5a (2)). Next, the reaction of 1i and 2 provided 74% of 3 at 12 h in the presence of the catalyst (Fig. 5a (3)). These results indicate that 1h, 1i, and 4 are intermediates in the overall transformation. Further, the reaction of 1i and furfural 2b generates the α,β-unsaturated product 1j and 4 (64%), which suggests that the Co@NC-800-PPDA and base facilitate C–O cleavage (Fig. 5a (2) and (4)). Similarly, the aldol-condensation of 1h with 2b proceeds smoothly in the presence of base, even without a cobalt catalyst (Fig. 5a (5)).

a Control experiments. Reaction conditions: 0.3 mmol 2-phenoxy-1-phenylethanol, 0.6 mmol furfural alcohol, 0.18 mmol K3PO4, 2 mL tert-BuOH, 120 °C, 12 h, Ar and b possible reaction pathway.

Based on these results and consistent with the identified Co–N4 active sites, we propose the following reaction pathway for the cobalt single-atom-catalyzed C-alkylation process of lignin-derived compounds (Fig. 5b). Firstly, the well-defined electronic environment of the isolated Co–N4 moiety facilitates the catalytic dehydrogenation of the substrate 1 and primary alcohol, resulting the corresponding 1i (from 1) and aldehyde (from primary alcohol) along with the generation of active cobalt-hydride species. Next, 1i is likely converted to 1h and 5 via C–O bond cleavage, followed by condensation of 1h with the in-situ generated aldehyde to form the respective α,β-unsaturated ketone intermediate. Finally, the hydride species stabilized on the Co–N4 center is transferred back to the α,β-unsaturated ketone, leading to its reduction and the formation of the desired alkylated ketone product, thereby regenerating the catalyst and completing the borrowing-hydrogen cycle.

Co-single atoms catalyzed C-alkylation of lignin models compounds with various alcohols

After having identified a selective and active catalyst (Co@NC-800-PPDA) as well as the possible reaction pathways for the benchmark reaction, we then explored the general applicability for the construction of C–C bonds utilizing structurally diverse lignin-derived compounds, ketones, and alcohols. First, we investigated the scope of different alcohols for the alkylation of 2-phenoxy-1-phenylethanol 1. As shown in Fig. 6, various benzylic, heterocyclic, and aliphatic primary alcohols were successfully reacted with 1 and gave the corresponding alkylated ketones, indicating that C–O bond cleavage associated with C–C bond formation occurred in a one-pot process. Various benzylic alcohols containing electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups, including functionalized ones, were smoothly reacted with 1 and gave α-benzylated ketones in good to excellent yields. Interestingly, fluor-, chlor-, trifluoromethyl-, trifluoromethoxy-, and ether-substituents were well tolerated, and the corresponding products were isolated in yields up to 84% (Fig. 6; products 9–16). Such halogenated and functionalized alkylated ketones are valuable products useful in organic synthesis and drug discovery. Next, different heterocyclic alcohols containing benzodioxole-, benzodioxine-, thiophene-, pyridine-, and furan motifs were coupled with 1, and the corresponding heterocyclic ketones were obtained in good to excellent yield (Fig. 6; products 17–21). Interestingly, when 2,6-pyridinedimethanol was used as a coupling partner, the catalyst allowed for the reaction of one hydroxy group, while the other one was untouched (Fig. 6; product 21).

Reaction conditionsa: 0.3 mmol 2-phenoxy-1-phenylethanol, 0.6 mmol alcohol (2.0 equiv.), 40 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA (2.8 mol% Co), 0.18 mmol K3PO4 (0.6 equiv.), 2 mL tert-BuOH, 120 °C, 24 h, isolated yields; bGC yield; csame as “a” with 0.5 mmol K3PO4 (1.0 equiv.) at 130 °C; dsame as “a” with 0.5 mmol tert-BuOK (1.0 equiv.) at 140 °C.

In addtion, multi-substituted benzylic alcohols are amenable to this C-alkylation process and the corresponding structurally complex ketones were obtained in up to 82% yields (Fig. 6; products 14 and 16). In general, compared to benzylic or heterocyclic alcohols, aliphatic alcohols are less reactive and hence their utilization as alkylating agents is a more challenging process. Nevertheless, our Co-single atom-based catalyst is promising for the activation of aliphatic alcohols, too. As a result, phenyl-alkyl, cyclo-aliphatic, and simple aliphatic alcohols gave up to 74% of the alkylated ketones at 130–140 °C in the presence of tert-BuOK (Fig. 6; products 22–29).

Next, we explored the scope of different lignin model compounds with benzylic alcohol as a coupling partner. The results obtained showed that this cobalt-catalytic system was effective for the valorization of various β-O-4 model compounds. As shown in Fig. 6, all the β-O-4 compounds bearing methoxy groups (1b, 1c, 1d, 1f) with different numbers and positions could be converted to the desired ketones 6, 30, 31, along with the corresponding phenol derivatives 5a–5c. However, the highly substituted β-O-4 model compound with γ-OH (substrates 1e and 1g) led to a comparatively lower yield of desired products 3 and 30 due to the formation of several byproducts. Based on all the results above, we can conclude that our cobalt catalytic system has a great ability not only to dehydrogenation of Cα–OH and cleavage of the C–O bonds, but also to promote C–C bond formation, highlighting the practical advantage in the conversion of lignin β-O-4 model compounds to provide valuable ketone-based products.

Co@NC-800-PPDA catalyzed C-alkylation of ketones with alcohols: Applicability for the synthesis and alkylation of bioactive molecules

Following the successful alkylation of lignin derivatives, direct C-alkylation of various ketones with benzylic and heterocyclic alcohols was carried out as well (Fig. 7). Various aromatic and aliphatic ketones were selectively alkylated with alcohols, yielding a variety of functionalized and structurally diverse ketones, including cyclic and aliphatic ones, in good to excellent yields (Fig. 7; products 32–41). The functionalization of existing natural or synthetic bioactive compounds enables the easy preparation of novel molecules, which is an important aspect in drug development. With this in mind, we applied our catalytic strategy to the derivatization of hormones. Interestingly, estrone-3-methyl ether was effectively alkylated with benzyl alcohol at the carbon atom adjacent to the carbonyl group, yielding product 42 in a 64% yield. Furthermore, the synthetic usefulness of this cobalt-based BH method is demonstrated for the preparation of selected drug molecules such as donepezil, azaperone, and loureirin A (Fig. 7; products 43–45). In general, these and related drug molecules are prepared by nucleophilic substitution reactions of the corresponding halogenated compounds16,17.

Co@NC-800-PPDA catalyzed C-alkylation of secondary alcohols with primary alcohols

After successfully alkylating ketones, we asked ourselves whether it would be possible to selectively alkylate secondary alcohols with primary alcohols as alkylating agents. Such coupling reactions between two alcohols for the selective formation of C–C bonds are obviously challenging, which is why such transformations have been less researched (Fig. 1b (2))67. Fortunately, Co@NC-800-PPDA showed excellent activity for the coupling of secondary alcohols with primary alcohols, and by changing the base and the reaction temperature, the selective formation of alkylated ketone or alkylated secondary alcohol can be controlled. In the presence of K3PO4 at 120 °C, the reaction of secondary alcohols with benzyl alcohol yielded α-alkylated ketones, while in the presence of KOMe at 150 °C, α-alkylated secondary alcohols were obtained.

First, the synthesis of α-alkylated ketones from various secondary alcohols and benzyl alcohol was carried out. A series of substituted 1-phenylethanols was alkylated with benzyl alcohol, yielding the corresponding alkylated ketones in yields of up to 90%. Similarly, heterocyclic and aliphatic secondary alcohols also reacted well, yielding the corresponding ketone products (Fig. 8; products 46–68). Next, the synthesis of C-alkylated secondary alcohols was carried out starting from various secondary alcohols and primary alcohols, which corresponds to the classic Guerbet reaction67,68. Although such a conversion is difficult to achieve compared to the formation of alkylated ketones, the alkylated secondary alcohols were smoothly obtained from commercially available secondary and primary (aliphatic) alcohols in the presence of our cobalt catalyst and KOMe (Fig. 8; products 69–81).

Reaction conditions for the synthesis of ketonesa: 0.5 mmol secondary alcohol, 1.0 mmol primary alcohol (2.0 equiv.), 50 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA (2.11 mol% Co), 0.15 mmol K3PO4 (0.3 equiv.), 2 mL toluene, 120 °C, 24 h; bsame as “a” with 0.5 mmol K3PO4 (1.0 equiv.); cGC yield. Reaction conditions for the synthesis of secondary alcoholsd: 0.5 mmol secondary alcohol, 1.5 mmol primary alcohol (3.0 equiv.), 80 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA (3.37 mol% Co), 0.5 mmol KOMe (1.0 equiv.), 2 mL toluene, 150 °C, 24 h, isolated yields; esame as “d” with 0.75 mmol KOMe (1.5 equiv.).

Applications of the Co@NC-800-PPDA material for C-methylation reactions using methanol and for C-alkylation of KA oil

Additionally, C-methylation reactions are of paramount importance in organic synthesis as well as in medical and biological chemistry13,14. Traditionally, these reactions rely on Grignard reagents or activated methylating agents such as methyl iodide, methyl sulfate, or diazomethane. However, these reagents are not only unsafe but also produce significant amounts of (hazardous) waste, which hinders their applicability for sustainable methylations in general. In contrast, methanol (MeOH) offers an extremely attractive alternative source of methylation within the principles of green and sustainable chemistry. In this context, we focused our interest on using MeOH as a methylating agent using this Co-single-atom-based BH methodology for the synthesis of C-methylated products. To our delight, this Co-SAC material showed promising activity for the selective C-methylation of primary and secondary alcohols and lignin model compounds with methanol (Fig. 9; products 82–98). In addition, substituted α-methylketones were also produced in good yields from ketones and methanol (Fig. 9; products 99–102).

Co@NC-800-PPDA catalyzed methylation reaction using methanol.

Reaction conditionsa: 0.5 mmol alcohol, 3.0 mmol MeOH (6.0 equiv.), 100 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA (4.22 mol% Co), 1.0 mmol KOMe (2.0 equiv.), 2 mL toluene, 150 °C, 24 h, isolated yields; bGC yield; csame as “a” with 6.0 mmol MeOH; dsame as “a” with 70 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA (2.95 mol% Co).

Normally, KA oil (an industrial mixture of cyclohexanol and cyclohexanone) is an important starting material for the production of adipic acid, a precursor for Nylon 6,6 polymer. Conventionally, KA oil is usually produced by the oxidation of cyclohexane, which is obtained from petrochemical raw materials (Fig. 10)69,70. Alternatively, a renewable route to KA oil exists via the hydrogenation of phenol, which can be sourced from lignin-containing biomass. To demonstrate this synthesis, the phenol obtained from our benchmark reaction was separated and hydrogenated with the Co@NC-PPDA-800 catalyst. The hydrogenation of phenol proceeded well and yielded 80% KA oil 103 (ketone to alcohol ratio 30:70) (Fig. 10). This shows that Co@NC-PPDA-800 can also be used as a catalyst for the hydrogenation of arenes in addition to the C-alkylation processes vide supra. Next, the KA oil 103 obtained from phenol was reacted with benzyl alcohol under two different reaction conditions using Co@NC-PPDA-800. In the presence of K3PO4 at 120 °C, the KA oil was selectively converted to C-benzylated ketone (2-benzylcyclohexanone, product 33) in a yield of 90%. However, when KOMe was used at 150 °C, the corresponding C-benzylated alcohols (2-benzylcyclohexanol, product 78) were selectively obtained in a yield of 85%.

Reaction conditions for alkylation of 1 with 2: 1.5 mmol 1, 3.0 mmol 2, 120 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA, 0.9 mmol K3PO4, 10 mL tert-BuOH, 120 °C, 24 h, phenol was separated after reaction for the next hydrogenation. Reaction conditions for the hydrogenation of phenol: 1.3 mmol phenol, 200 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA, 5 mL tert-BuOH, 50 bar H2, 140 °C, 16 h. Reaction conditions for synthesis 33: 1.0 mmol KA oil, 2.0 mmol benzyl alcohol, 100 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA, 1.0 mmol K3PO4, 5 mL toluene, 120 °C, 24 h. Reaction conditions for the synthesis 78: 1.0 mmol KA oil, 3.0 mmol benzyl alcohol, 160 mg Co@NC-800-PPDA, 0.5 mmol KOMe, 5 mL toluene, 150 °C, 24 h. All GC yield.

In conclusion, we developed an atomically dispersed cobalt catalyst that enables highly selective C–C bond formation through C-alkylation of lignin model compounds, ketones, secondary alcohols, KA oil, and primary alcohols. The catalyst, comprising atomically dispersed Co–N active centers embedded in mesoporous N-doped carbon prepared via a polymerization/Co-assembly/template-sacrificial strategy, exhibits high activity, stability, and reusability. Using this system, a broad spectrum of functionalized and structurally diverse alkylated ketones and alcohols, including pharmaceutically relevant molecules, can be efficiently synthesized. Importantly, methanol has been successfully employed as a C1 source for methylation via the Guerbet reaction, further highlighting the versatility of this approach. Beyond its synthetic scope, this methodology leverages abundant, low-cost, and renewable alcohols as alkylating agents, underscoring its economic and environmental advantages. The use of an abundant metal in a robust, recyclable catalyst framework reinforces the practicality and sustainability of the process. More broadly, this work not only establishes a scalable and environmentally benign route to construct molecular complexity through selective C–C bond formation but also lays a foundation for value-added upgrading of biomass-derived alcohols. It establishes a practical platform for upgrading biomass-derived alcohols, advancing sustainable catalysis, and contributing to the carbon economy.

Methods

General procedure for the C-alkylation of lignin models, ketones, and secondary alcohols

The magnetic stirring bar, 0.3 mmol lignin model compound or 0.5 mmol ketone or 0.5 mmol secondary alcohol and 2.0 equiv. primary alcohol, 40 mg catalyst (Co@NC-800-PPDA, 2.8 mol% Co) and 0.18 mmol potassium phosphate (K3PO4) were transferred to 20 mL pressure tube. Then, 2 mL t-BuOH (in case of ketones. Toluene in case secondary alcohols) was added, and the pressure tube was flushed with argon and fitted with screw cap. The pressure tube containing the reaction mixture was placed in an aluminum block, and the reaction was allowed to progress under stirred conditions at 120–140 °C for the required time. After the completion of the reaction, the pressure tube was cooled to room temperature, and the cap was slowly removed. Then, the reaction products were removed from the pressure tube, and the solid catalyst was filtered off and washed thoroughly with ethyl acetate. The reaction products were analyzed by GC–MS. The corresponding products were purified by column chromatography (silica; pentene-ethyl acetate mixture) and characterized by NMR and GC–MS spectral analysis. The following procedure is applied for determining the conversion and yield by GC: After completion of the reaction, n-hexadecane (0.3 mmol) as a standard was added to the reaction pressure tube, and the reaction products were diluted with ethyl acetate, followed by filtration using a plug of silica. Then the filtrate containing products was analyzed by GC.
