Prostate Stem Cell Antigen-Targeted DNA Aptamer Probe with Ultrahigh Tumor Contrast for Precision Fluorescence-Guided Surgery of Prostate Cancer
Authors: Fen Hu, Yong Liu, Mao-Yin Li, Yu-Bin Cui, Yi-Qian Wan, Xin Gao, Man-Na Huang, Yu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) offers a potential strategy to improve complete tumor resection in prostate cancer (PCa); however, clinical application has been limited by an insufficient tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) of available probes. Here, we report the development of GGA-sNIR, a near-infrared (NIR) fluorescent probe engineered by conjugating a high-affinity DNA aptamer specific for a prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) with a monocarboxy indocyanine green (ICG) derivative. The conjugate adopts a stable three-dimensional structure that not only enables precise target recognition but also protects against nuclease degradation, markedly enhancing its in vivo stability. GGA-sNIR exhibits nanomolar binding affinity (K d = 54.91 nM) and is specifically internalized by PSCA-positive cells. In subcutaneous tumor models, the probe achieved a peak TBR of 26 and showed prolonged tumor retention exceeding 48 h. Crucially, in an orthotopic PCa model, GGA-sNIR allowed real-time, high-contrast visualization of tumor margins during laparoscopic surgery, facilitating precise and complete resection. Further validation using ex vivo human lymph node specimens confirmed its ability to selectively detect PSCA-positive metastases. With its ultrahigh contrast, demonstrated efficacy in intraoperative guidance, and strong translational potential, GGA-sNIR represents a promising molecular tool for advancing precision surgery in prostate cancer.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) represents the second most prevalent malignancy and fifth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men worldwide, underscoring its significant global health burden Radical prostatectomy (RP) combined with extended pelvic lymph node dissection (ePLND) remains the standard curative treatment for localized and locally advanced disease. However, contemporary surgical management faces two fundamental challenges that significantly impact oncological outcomes and patient prognosis. The primary challenge involves achieving complete tumor eradication while preserving negative surgical marginsa critical determinant of long-term survival. The anatomical complexity of the prostate, particularly the intimate relationship between malignant tissue and neurovascular bundles, presents substantial technical difficulties for surgeons attempting precise tumor resection while preserving functional outcomes. The second challenge centers on the detection and removal of micrometastatic disease, particularly in lymph nodes located adjacent to critical anatomical structures including major vessels, nerve plexuses, bladder neck, and rectum. These anatomical constraints considerably complicate surgical decision-making and increase the risk of incomplete resection.

The clinical significance of these challenges is reflected in compelling epidemiological data. Unlike developed nations where earlier detection is common, over 68% of Chinese patients present with locally advanced or metastatic disease at initial diagnosis. Comprehensive analyses reveal alarming rates of positive surgical margins following RP: 19.58% in pT2 disease and 45.95% in pT3 tumors. Furthermore, lymphovascular invasion occurs in 4.9% of pT2, 67.5% of pT3, and 75% of pT4 cases. Perhaps most concerning, Schiavina and colleagues demonstrated that 13% of lymph nodes initially classified as pathologically negative harbored occult micrometastases when subjected to enhanced immunohistochemical and RT-PCR analysiseven in patients with apparently organ-confined disease. This finding highlights the limitations of conventional histopathological assessment in detecting minimal residual disease.

The evolution toward minimally invasive robotic-assisted laparoscopic techniques, while offering significant advantages in terms of recovery and cosmetic outcomes, has paradoxically exacerbated these challenges. The reduced tactile feedback inherent to robotic surgery compromises surgeons’ ability to palpate tumor margins, assess tissue consistency, and evaluate lymph node characteristicssensory information traditionally crucial for intraoperative decision-making. Consequently, rates of positive surgical margins and residual disease have increased, directly impacting patient prognosis and necessitating additional adjuvant therapies that substantially increase healthcare costs and resource utilization.

Fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) has emerged as a promising solution to address these fundamental limitations, offering real-time visualization capabilities that enhance surgical precision and improve oncological outcomes. − The success of FGS relies critically on two components: advanced imaging instrumentation and molecularly targeted fluorescent probes, with the latter representing the key determinant of clinical efficacy.

Despite extensive research efforts targeting prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)the most intensively studied PCa biomarkerclinical translation has been limited by several technical barriers. Current imaging modalities present distinct limitations: radionuclide-based agents are impractical for real-time surgical guidance; antibody-based probes suffer from high production costs, limited stability (shelf life <1 month at 4 °C), prolonged circulation times, and potential immunogenicity; peptide-based agents are susceptible to rapid proteolytic degradation by serum peptidases; and nanoparticle-based systems raise concerns regarding metal toxicity and suboptimal biodistribution profiles. ,

Most critically, existing PSMA-targeted fluorophores demonstrate insufficient tumor accumulation, resulting in suboptimal tumor-to-background ratios (TBR) that compromise the ability to accurately delineate tumor margins and detect micrometastatic foci. This limitation necessitates the development of next-generation imaging agents that combine serum stability, cost-effectiveness, superior TBR, and rapid clearance from nontarget tissues.

Prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) represents a compelling alternative target that addresses many limitations associated with current approaches. PSCA is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored cell surface protein belonging to the Ly-6/Thy-1 family, characterized by high expression in PCa with minimal presence in normal tissuesan ideal profile for targeted imaging applications. Extensive clinical studies have established strong correlations between PSCA expression levels and adverse pathological features, including high Gleason scores and advanced clinical stages. Importantly, PSCA maintains robust expression across the disease spectrum, including localized, metastatic, and castration-resistant PCa. , Unlike other prostate-associated antigens, PSCA functions as a truly prostate-specific membrane-bound protein that undergoes ligand-induced endocytosis, facilitating efficient cellular internalization of targeted imaging agents. ,

To overcome the limitations of conventional targeting approaches, we pursued an innovative strategy utilizing nucleic acid aptamers conjugated with optimized near-infrared fluorophores. Our previous work established the Wy-5a DNA aptamer through systematic Cell-SELEX screening against PSCA-overexpressing PC-3 cells. This aptamer demonstrates exceptional specificity for PSCA-positive cells with nanomolar binding affinity while showing no cross-reactivity with PSCA-negative control lines (22Rv1, HeLa). Critically, in situ hybridization studies confirmed that Wy-5a can reliably distinguish high-risk PCa from benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) in clinical specimens, validating its diagnostic potential.

Building upon these foundations, we developed GGA-sNIR through strategic conjugation of the Wy-5a aptamer with monocarboxy-indocyanine green (ICG), creating a novel three-dimensional conformation conjugate with enhanced stability and targeting properties. Comprehensive validation studies, including pull-down assays and mass spectrometry analysis, confirmed PSCA as the specific molecular target. Epitope mapping using established methodologies identified the precise binding interface between GGA-sNIR and PSCA, providing mechanistic insights into probe-target interactions.

The comprehensive characterization presented herein demonstrates that GGA-sNIR exhibits exceptional binding specificity for PSCA-expressing cells with nanomolar affinity, generating robust near-infrared fluorescent signals upon cellular internalization. Pharmacokinetic profiling in subcutaneous xenograft models revealed sustained tumor retention for up to 48 h with a remarkable peak TBR of 26representing a substantial improvement over existing imaging agents. To rigorously assess its potential for clinical translation, we evaluated GGA-sNIR in an orthotopic prostate cancer model under realistic surgical conditions. The probe enabled clear real-time delineation of tumor margins during fluorescence-guided laparoscopic surgery, facilitating precise and complete resection. This successful demonstration in a pathologically relevant model, combined with its high contrast and favorable safety profile, establishes GGA-sNIR as a promising aptamer-based imaging platform for precision prostate cancer surgery.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization of GGA-sNIR

The PSCA-targeted fluorescent probe GGA-sNIR was synthesized by covalently conjugating the DNA aptamer Wy-5a with the near-infrared fluorophore monocarboxy-ICG via amide bond formation (Supporting Information, Figure S1). The structural integrity and purity of both the synthetic intermediate (monocarboxy-ICG) and the final conjugate (GGA-sNIR) were confirmed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and mass spectrometry (MS) analysis (Figure a and Supporting Information, Figure S2). Ultraperformance liquid chromatography (UPLC) analysis determined the final purity of GGA-sNIR to be >95% (Supporting Information). Comprehensive structural validation was further achieved through multinuclear NMR spectroscopy, including Hydrogen-1 (1H), Carbon-13 (13C), and Phosphorus-31 (31P) NMR analyses, which confirmed the covalent linkage between the aptamer and the fluorophore, as well as the integrity of the oligonucleotide backbone and the ICG-derived polyene system (Supporting Information, Figures S3.1–S3.7 and Tables S1 and S2). Three-dimensional structural modeling of the GGA-sNIR conjugate, performed using ChimeraX 1.10.1, visualized its folded conformation and predicted its molecular interface with the target protein PSCA (Supporting Information, Figure S4).

(b) Emission spectra of GGA-sNIR at various concentrations (100, 50, 25, 12.5, 10, 5, and 2.5 mg/L) with excitation at 780 nm. (c, d) Penetration depth of GGA-sNIR (maximum 8 nm) tested using 1 mm thick lamb slices. (e) Photostability of GGA-sNIR in serum, blood, and PBS under visible light over 50 h. The spectroscopic profile of GGA-sNIR was characterized by spectrofluorometric analysis. The conjugate exhibited concentration-dependent near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence upon excitation at 780 nm, with a characteristic emission peak at 820 nm (Figure b). A strong linear correlation (R 2 = 0.998) was observed between fluorescence intensity and probe concentration (2.5–100 mg/L), confirming its suitability for quantitative imaging applications. Critical for surgical guidance, tissue penetration depth was assessed using sequential 1 mm lamb tissue sections. GGA-sNIR demonstrated superior penetration, achieving a depth of 8 mm, which was significantly greater than that of control groups (ssDNA-ICG and free ICG) (Figure c,d).

The photostability of GGA-sNIR was evaluated under physiological conditions. The probe maintained over 90% of its initial fluorescence intensity for 50 h when stored in serum, whole blood, or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under ambient light, with no statistically significant decay (P > 0.05, one-way ANOVA) (Figure e). Under continuous 780 nm laser excitation, progressive photobleaching was observed, with complete signal quenching occurring after approximately 60 min (Figure f), indicating the need for controlled light exposure during procedures. Thermal stability, assessed by UV–vis and circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy, confirmed that GGA-sNIR maintains its structural integrity after thermal denaturation at 95 °C, with characteristic hypochromic effects and restored secondary structure upon cooling (Supporting Information, Figure S5). Furthermore, GGA-sNIR demonstrated stability in Sprague–Dawley (SD) rat plasma at 37 °C for 24 h, as analyzed by LC-MS/MS. This robust stability profile ensures functional reliability following standard aptamer activation protocols (Supporting Information, Figure S6 and Table S3).

Specific Cellular Binding and Receptor-Mediated Internalization

Flow cytometric and confocal microscopic analyses demonstrated highly selective binding of GGA-sNIR to PSCA-overexpressing prostate cancer cell lines. Fluorescence microscopy revealed intense red fluorescence in PC-3 and C4-2 cells, both known to highly express PSCA, while negligible signal was observed in PSCA-negative control cell lines (HeLa and 22RV1) (Figure a). Flow cytometry quantitatively confirmed this selectivity, with PC-3 cells exhibiting a > 10-fold increase in mean fluorescence intensity compared to control cell lines (Figure b).

Saturation binding analysis across a concentration range of 1–500 nM revealed typical receptor–ligand binding kinetics (Figure c). Nonlinear regression analysis yielded an equilibrium dissociation constant (K d) of 54.91 ± 3.2 nM (Figure d), indicating high-affinity binding comparable to therapeutic monoclonal antibodies. The Hill coefficient of 1.02 ± 0.08 suggests 1:1 binding stoichiometry between GGA-sNIR and PSCA.

Temperature-dependent binding studies provided mechanistic insights into the cellular uptake process. At 4 °C, GGA-sNIR remained predominantly membrane-bound, while incubation at 37 °C promoted receptor-mediated endocytosis (Figure e). Trypsin treatment prior to GGA-sNIR exposure significantly reduced binding, confirming interaction with cell surface proteins rather than intracellular targets. Time-lapse confocal microscopy revealed progressive internalization kinetics, with initial membrane binding followed by cytoplasmic accumulation and perinuclear localization over 2 h (Figure f).

Mass Spectrometric Identification and Validation of PSCA as the Molecular Target

To unambiguously identify the cellular target of GGA-sNIR, we employed affinity purification coupled with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Pull-down assays using biotinylated GGA-sNIR and streptavidin-coated magnetic beads isolated a predominant protein band at approximately 15 kDa from PC-3 cell lysates (Figure a). LC-MS/MS analysis with database searching against the human proteome (UniProt) definitively identified this protein as prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA) with high confidence (protein score >95%, sequence coverage >60%) (Figure b and Supporting Information, Figure S7).

Identification of PSCA as the target of GGA-sNIR. (a) Pull-down assay identifying a 15 kDa protein as the target of GGA-sNIR. (b) Mass spectrometry confirming PSCA as the target of GGA-sNIR. (c) Competitive binding assay with FITC-labeled anti-PSCA antibody. (d) Co-localization of GGA-sNIR (red) and PSCA-FITC (green) in PC-3 cells. (e–g) Effects of PSCA mutations (14–28 amino acids) on GGA-sNIR binding.

Competitive binding assays using FITC-conjugated anti-PSCA antibody provided orthogonal validation of PSCA as the GGA-sNIR target. Preincubation with anti-PSCA antibody dose-dependently inhibited GGA-sNIR binding, confirming target specificity (Figure c). Confocal colocalization studies demonstrated excellent spatial overlap between GGA-sNIR (red) and PSCA-FITC (green) signals, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.89 ± 0.04 (Figure d).

Epitope mapping using site-directed mutagenesis identified the critical binding region within PSCA. Systematic alanine substitution mutagenesis revealed that mutations in amino acids 14–28 dramatically reduced GGA-sNIR binding affinity by >80%, while other regional mutations had minimal impact (Figure e–g). This finding localizes the GGA-sNIR binding epitope to a specific extracellular domain of PSCA, consistent with the observed receptor-mediated internalization via the caveolin-1 pathway (Supporting Information, Figure S8).

Superior In Vivo Tumor Targeting and Pharmacokinetic Profile

The in vivo targeting efficacy and pharmacokinetics of the GGA-sNIR aptamer–fluorophore conjugate were systematically evaluated in mice bearing PC-3 xenograft tumors. Fluorescence imaging at serial time points (2, 4, and 8 h postinjection) demonstrated significantly superior tumor accumulation of GGA-sNIR compared to control agents (ssDNA-ICG, ICG, and PBS) (Figure a). A dose optimization study (0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mg per mouse) identified 1.0 mg as the optimal dose, providing the clearest tumor visualization with minimal background signal (Figure b). At this dose, GGA-sNIR achieved a peak tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) of 26.3 ± 2.8 at 8 h postinjection (Figure c), representing a >6-fold improvement over previously reported probes.

Temporal analysis of tumor signal revealed rapid accumulation and sustained retention. The tumor fluorescence intensity peaked at 8 h and remained high at 24 h (TBR = 18.2 ± 3.1), with detectable contrast still present at 48 h (TBR = 8.9 ± 1.7) (Figure d). This extended retention profile offers a practical time window for surgical scheduling. Furthermore, ex vivo biodistribution analysis at the 8 h time point confirmed the preferential accumulation of GGA-sNIR in tumor tissue (Figure e). Quantitative fluorescence measurement of major organs demonstrated that the tumor signal was significantly higher than that in all other tissues, with the liver and kidneys showing the next highest levels among nontarget organs (Figure f). Notably, minimal fluorescence was detected in critical organs such as the heart, lungs, and brain, supporting the conjugate’s favorable in vivo safety profile.

Successful Fluorescence-Guided Resection of Orthotopic Prostate Cancer

To evaluate the clinical potential of GGA-sNIR for precision surgery, we established an orthotopic PC-3 xenograft model to mimic the human disease microenvironment. Following intravenous administration of GGA-sNIR, real-time fluorescence-guided laparoscopic surgery was performed using the Optmedic imaging system.

As depicted in Figure a, GGA-sNIR enabled clear intraoperative delineation of the orthotopic tumor throughout the critical stages of resection. Under brightlight (BL) mode, the tumor margin was poorly distinguishable from surrounding tissues. In stark contrast, switching to near-infrared fluorescence (FL) mode revealed intense and specific accumulation of the probe within the tumor, providing a definitive visual guide. The fusion view seamlessly integrated anatomical and molecular information. This high contrast was maintained from the initial exposure (before resection), through the dissection process (during resection), and was completely eliminated upon complete resection, confirming the removal of all targeted tissue with no apparent residual signal.

Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed the malignant nature of the fluorescence-positive tissue, as evidenced by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Figure b). Furthermore, to validate the specificity of the observed fluorescence, consecutive sections of the tumor were analyzed. Figure c,d demonstrates that the GGA-sNIR signal colocalized with strong membrane-specific immunohistochemical staining for prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA), unequivocally verifying that the intraoperative fluorescence originated from the specific binding of GGA-sNIR to its molecular target on PC-3 cells.

GGA-sNIR Accurately Targeting Human Prostate Cancer and Metastatic Lesions in Ex Vivo Clinical Specimens

To bridge the translational gap, we evaluated GGA-sNIR performance in human tissue specimens and conducted a pilot clinical study. Ex vivo staining of prostate tissue sections demonstrated selective binding to malignant regions with minimal signal in benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) tissue (Figure a). Co-localization analysis with PSCA immunofluorescence confirmed targeting specificity, with excellent spatial correlation (Pearson’s r = 0.91 ± 0.05) in cancer regions (Figure b).

This high specificity was further confirmed in metastatic lesions. As demonstrated in Figure c,d, GGA-sNIR staining of fixed clinical tissues showed that intense fluorescence was exclusively localized to PSCA-positive areas, encompassing both primary tumors and their metastatic deposits in lymph nodes. Subsequent immunohistochemical staining for PSCA on consecutive sections from these guided regions confirmed that the intense GGA-sNIR fluorescence colocalized perfectly with areas of high PSCA protein expression (Figure e,f). This strong correlation between the probe’s signal and the target’s presence in a variety of clinical samplesincluding primary tumors and metastasesprovides compelling evidence that GGA-sNIR retains its high specificity and targeting efficacy in human prostate cancer tissues.

Comprehensive Safety and Toxicological Assessment

Comprehensive safety evaluation was conducted through both in vitro cytotoxicity assays and in vivo toxicological studies. MTT viability assays demonstrated no significant cytotoxicity across a wide concentration range (0.1–10 μM) in multiple cell lines, with cell viability consistently >95% relative to untreated controls (Supporting Information, Figure S9).

In vivo safety assessment involved systematic monitoring of hepatic, renal, cardiac, and neurological function markers in C57BL/6J mice following intravenous administration of 1.0 mg GGA-sNIR (Supporting Information, Figure S10). Serum biochemical analysis over an 8-day observation period revealed no significant alterations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT, Figure a), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, Figure b), or creatinine (Cr, Figure c) levels, indicating preserved hepatic and renal function. Similarly, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, Figure d) and neuron-specific enolase (NSE, Figure e) remained within normal physiological ranges, demonstrating absence of cardiac or neurological toxicity.

Systemic safety assessment of GGA-sNIR in mice. Serum levels of key biomakers were monitored over 8 days following a single intravenous injection of GGA-sNIR (1.0 mg per mouse) or PBS. (a) Alanine aminotransferase (ALT). (b) Aspartate aminotransferase (AST). (c) Creatinine (Cr). (d) Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). (e) Neuron-specific enolase (NSE). Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 mice per time point). Statistical analysis was performed using repeated measures one-way ANOVA. For all biomarkers, the values at all time points postinjection were not statistically different from the baseline (day 0) level (p > 0.05 for all comparisons).

Histopathological analysis further corroborated the safety profile of GGA-sNIR. H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, intestine, and brain) harvested 24 h postinjection showed normal tissue architecture with no evidence of necrosis, inflammation, hemorrhage, or other pathological alterations (Supporting Information, Figure S10).

Statistical analysis using repeated measures ANOVA confirmed no significant time-dependent changes in any monitored biochemical parameters (P > 0.05 for all comparisons), with all values remaining within 10% of baseline measurements. Additional serum biochemical analyses (Supporting Information, Figure S11) also showed no significant abnormalities. Collectively, these in vitro and in vivo data demonstrate that GGA-sNIR exhibits favorable biocompatibility and supports its potential suitability for clinical translation.

Discussion and Conclusions

We have successfully engineered GGA-sNIR, a novel aptamer-fluorophore conjugate targeting prostate stem cell antigen (PSCA), which demonstrates exceptional potential for fluorescence-guided surgery (FGS) in prostate cancer. Our strategic approach combined the rational selection of a clinically validated tumor antigen with the inherent advantages of DNA aptamer technology, culminating in a probe with superior imaging performance and a favorable safety profile in preclinical models.

The choice of PSCA as a molecular target was driven by its optimal expression profile, characterized by high-frequency overexpression in prostate adenocarcinoma (>94%), association with aggressive disease, and limited expression in normal tissues, thereby minimizing potential on-target, off-tumor effects. The recent clinical validation of PSCA as a therapeutic target, evidenced by the activity of PSCA-directed CAR T-cells, strongly supports its relevance for targeted imaging. To engage this target, we selected a DNA aptamer over conventional antibodies, capitalizing on aptamers’ low molecular weight for rapid tissue penetration, synthetic robustness, lack of immunogenicity, and facile chemical modification. A critical challenge in aptamer-based probe development has been their rapid systemic degradation in vivo. We addressed this by conjugating the PSCA-specific 57-nucleotide aptamer with monocarboxy-ICG, formulating the resulting construct as a sodium salt. This design yielded a stable conjugate (GGA-sNIR) with enhanced water solubility, optimized pharmacokinetics favoring renal clearance, and a nuclease-resistant conformation that preserves both targeting fidelity and fluorescence emission.

Our comprehensive mechanistic studies provide a solid foundation for GGA-sNIR’s function. Pull-down assays coupled with mass spectrometry definitively confirmed PSCA as the binding target, with epitope mapping localizing the interaction to a specific extracellular region (amino acids 14–28). Furthermore, we elucidated that cellular internalization proceeds via the caveolin-1 pathway. These insights are crucial for understanding the probe’s behavior in vivo and for guiding future optimization strategies.

The performance characteristics of GGA-sNIR represent a significant leap forward for surgical imaging. The achieved tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) of 26 under optimal conditions far exceeds the established clinical thresholds for effective intraoperative discrimination (TBR > 2) and excellent performance (TBR > 5). , This high contrast, combined with an 8 mm penetration depth and a 48-h retention window, directly address both the challenge of deep tumor visualization and the logistical need for operative planning. The favorable pharmacokinetic profilerapid tumor accumulation, sustained signal, and efficient dual-pathway clearancealong with minimal nonspecific organ accumulation, underpins the probe’s excellent safety and biocompatibility.

The translational promise of GGA-sNIR is strongly supported by our ex vivo validation using human clinical specimens. The correlation between fluorescence intensity and PSCA expression in primary tumors and metastatic lymph nodes confirms target-specific binding in human disease. Most notably, the probe’s ability to identify micrometastatic lymph nodes missed by conventional assessment highlights its potential to improve surgical staging accuracy and reduce the risk of residual disease, a key determinant of long-term patient outcomes.

Despite its promising performance, several limitations of the current GGA-sNIR conjugate highlight avenues for future optimization. First, while the conjugate demonstrated remarkable stability in plasma for 24 h, the long-term in vivo fate and potential degradation pathways of DNA aptamers, especially in nuclease-rich environments over extended periods, require further investigation for chronic or repeated dosing scenarios. Second, although DNA aptamers are generally considered to have low immunogenicity, a comprehensive assessment of potential immune responses in immunocompetent models is necessary prior to clinical translation. Third, while the achieved penetration depth of 8 mm is suitable for many surgical scenarios, visualization of tumors located deeper than 1 cm may remain challenging, potentially necessitating complementary imaging modalities. Finally, the probe’s efficacy is inherently dependent on PSCA expression. While PSCA is expressed in a high percentage of prostate adenocarcinomas, its heterogeneity or downregulation in some cases could limit universal application. Future work will explore combination strategies targeting multiple antigens to address tumor heterogeneity.

In conclusion, GGA-sNIR establishes a robust aptamer-based platform for precision oncologic imaging. By integrating a clinically relevant target with engineered aptamer properties, we have developed a probe capable of guiding precise tumor resection and identifying occult metastases in preclinical models. These findings, coupled with the mature clinical pathway for FGS agents, position GGA-sNIR as a promising candidate for clinical translation to address the unmet need for improved visualization in prostate cancer surgery. Future work will focus on advancing this conjugate through the requisite regulatory steps toward first-in-human trials.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of GGA-sNIR

The synthesis of GGA-sNIR involved conjugating the DNA aptamer Wy-5a with monocarboxy-ICG. Briefly, four steps were performed to synthesize monocarboxy-ICG, which was subsequently coupled with Wy-5a in a one-pot system. The detailed procedures and corresponding 1H, 13C and 31P NMR spectra are provided in the Supporting Information. The synthesized ICG and the conjugate were analyzed by LC-MS/MS (TSQ Quantum Ultra, ThermoFisher, USA).

Fluorescence Spectroscopy Analysis

The optical properties of GGA-sNIR were analyzed using a F-4500 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Hitachi, Japan). The excitation wavelength was set at 780 nm, and the emission spectra were recorded in the range of 785–900 nm. The concentration of GGA-sNIR was varied (100 mg/L, 50 mg/L, 25 mg/L, 12.5 mg/L, 10 mg/L, 5 mg/L, and 2.5 mg/L) to evaluate the concentration-dependent fluorescence intensity.

Cell Lines and Reagents

Cell lines PC-3, C4-2, 22RV-1, and HeLa were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humid atmosphere with 5% CO2 with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 units/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma). Reagents, unless otherwise noted, were purchased from Gibco. The water used was pretreated with the Milli-Q Plus System (Millipore Corporation, Bedford, USA).

DNA Sequences and Buffers

The DNA sequences used were:

Wy-5a: 5′-TGCCACTACAGCTGGTTCGGTTTGGTGACTTCGTTCTTCGTTGTGGTGCTTAGTGGC-3′

ssDNA: 5′-ACGCTCGGATGCCACTACAG-45nt-CTCATGGACGTGCTGGTGAC-3′

The Wy-5a-NH2C6 and ssDNA were synthesized by Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The washing buffer was prepared by adding 5 mM MgCl2 and 4.5 g glucose into 1 L of 0.01 M PBS (containing 8 g NaCl, 0.2 g KCl, 3.58 g Na2HPO4·12H2O, 0.272 g KH2PO4 per 1 L ddH2O, pH = 7.4). The binding buffer was prepared by adding 1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma) and 0.1 mg/mL Herring Sperm DNA (Invitrogen) to the washing buffer.

Fluorescence Stability

The fluorescent stability of GGA-sNIR (200 nM) in different solutions (PBS, blood, serum) was measured using spectrophotometer (excitation wavelength λex = 780 nm and emission wavelength λem = 820 nm) at time points of 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. Additionally, the time course of fluorescence for GGA-sNIR, ssDNA-ICG and ICG was measured by a spectrofluorometer over a period of 120 min, with data collected at 5 min intervals.

Penetration Depth

The fluorescence penetration depth of GGA-sNIR was tested using consecutive frozen Lamb slices of approximately 1 mm thickness to simulate tissue. The fluorescence signal was detected using the AniView100 multimode animal live imaging system (BLT Photon Technology, China). The same method was used to detect the fluorescence penetration depth of ssDNA-ICG, ICG, and PBS (control) at the same concentration.

Flow Cytometric Analysis

Cells were harvested, washed, and resuspended in binding buffer. For incubation, 2 × 105 cells per cell line were treated with 200 nM GGA-sNIR at 37 °C for 1 h. For dose–response analysis, PC-3 cells were incubated with GGA-sNIR (1–500 nM). The equilibrium dissociation constants (K d) were determined by fitting the data using GraphPad Prism 9.0. In competitive binding assays, cells were preincubated with a 1:1000 dilution of anti-PSCA antibody (Abcam, ab202966). Flow cytometry was performed using a FACS Calibur instrument (BD Biosciences), collecting 105 events per sample.

Confocal Image of Cells

Confocal fluorescence microscopy was used to observe GGA-sNIR binding to living cells. Cells (2 × 104) were cultured in 35 mm glass-bottom dishes for 24 h to achieve confluence. The cells were washed with cold buffer and incubated with GGA-sNIR (250 nM) at 4 or 37 °C for 30 min. After two washes, cells were imaged using spinning-disk confocal microscopy (Olympus IX71, Japan) with a 40× objective (NA 0.90, UPLSAPO, Olympus, Japan). ICG was excited at 780 nm using an argon ion laser (Melles Griot, CA). Images were analyzed using FV10-ASW software (version 3.1). For internalization studies, some cells were treated with trypsin for 2 min prior to incubation with GGA-sNIR. For denaturation, GGA-sNIR in binding buffer was heated at 95 °C for 5 min, then cooled in an ice bath for 15 min, and kept at room temperature for 30 min before use.

Pull-Down Assay and Mass Spectrometry Analysis

PC-3 cells were washed three times with cold PBS and lysed with 5 mL hypotonic buffer (50 mM) Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) containing protease inhibitors (0.1 mM PMSF and cocktail) at 4 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation, the debris was washed three times with 5 mL hypotonic buffer and dissolved in 1.5 mL lysis buffer (PBS containing 5 mM MgCl2 and 1% Triton X-100) at 4 °C for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was incubated with IgG and GGA-sNIR at 4 °C for 1 h, respectively. Subsequently, the protein-IgG-ICG or protein-GGA-sNIR complex was captured by incubating it with 2 mg (200 μL) of streptavidin beads at 4 °C for 45 min. After centrifugation, the proteins were eluted by heating in 30 μL loading buffer and analyzed by SDS-PAGE gel and silver staining. The GGA-sNIR-purified protein bands were excised for digestion and analyzed by LC–MS/MS. The search and compare database used was Homo sapiens: https://www.uniprot.org/proteomes/UP000005640. The identification protein data of the control group was deducted from the experimental group, and the remaining differential protein was identified as the target binding protein.

Construction and Transfection of PSCA Mutant Plasmids

To identify the specific binding site of PSCA for GGA-sNIR, a series of mutant plasmids were constructed using the pHEK293 Ultra Expression Vector. Specific amino acid regions within the PSCA protein sequence were targeted by substituting residues with alanine. The mutants generated included PSCA-WT (wild-type PSCA), PSCA-1–13A (alanine substitution at amino acids 1–13), PSCA-14-28A (alanine substitution at amino acids 14–28), and additional mutants targeting other regions of PSCA (Figure ). The accuracy of the mutations was confirmed by DNA sequencing. 22RV1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well and transfected with the mutant plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent analyses.

In Vitro and In Vivo Cytotoxicity Assay

The vitro cytotoxicity of GGA-sNIR on prostate cancer cells was determined by MTT assay. C57BL/6J mice were used to assess the in vivo safety of GGA-sNIR. Mice received a single intravenous injection of 1 mg GGA-sNIR or PBS control. Blood was collected on days 1, 2, 3, 4, and 7 for serum biochemical analysis (ALT, AST, Cr, LDH, NSE) using commercial ELISA kits. Animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Laian Technology (Guangzhou) Co., Ltd. (Approval No. G2024066) and were performed in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. (Detailed surgical procedures for model establishment and fluorescence-guided surgery are provided in the Supporting Information, Experimental Section).

In Vivo and Ex Vivo Fluorescence Imaging

Subcutaneous xenograft models were established by inoculating PC-3 cells into the dorsal flank of BALB/c nude mice. Tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with GGA-sNIR at specified doses (0.1–2.0 mg per mouse; approximate dose range: 4–100 mg/kg). Fluorescence images were acquired at serial time points using an IVIS Lumina II system. The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was calculated as the following formula: TBR=fluorescenceintensityinthetumorregionfluorescenceintensityinthenon−tumorregion

For biodistribution, major organs and tumors were harvested 8 h postinjection for ex vivo imaging and quantitative analysis. (Detailed surgical procedures for model establishment and fluorescence-guided surgery are provided in the Supporting Information, Experimental Section).

Orthotopic Model and Fluorescence-Guided Surgery

Orthotopic prostate cancer models were established by surgical implantation of PC-3 cells. Mice bearing orthotopic tumors were injected with 1.0 mg GGA-sNIR 8 h prior to surgery. Laparoscopic resection was performed under real-time NIR fluorescence guidance (excitation: 780 nm, emission: 820 nm). (Detailed surgical procedures for model establishment and fluorescence-guided surgery are provided in the Supporting Information, Experimental Section).

Postoperative Analysis

The resected specimens (tumor and surrounding tissues) were processed as follows: (1) ex vivo fluorescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Lumina II system; (2) tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (in PBS, pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 24 h and washed with PBS; (3) fixed tissues were analyzed by H&E staining and PSCA immunohistochemistry to confirm tumor identity and resection margins.

Ex Vivo Staining and Fluorescence Imaging of Human Clinical Specimens

Surgical specimens of human prostate cancer tissue and metastatic lymph nodes were obtained from patients undergoing radical prostatectomy at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) tissues were collected from patients undergoing transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) as controls. Immediately after resection, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h to preserve tissue architecture and antigen integrity.

The fixed tissues were then incubated with 200 nM GGA-sNIR in binding buffer at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing to remove unbound probe, the specimens were imaged using the IVIS Lumina II in vivo imaging system (Caliper Life Sciences, USA) to capture the near-infrared fluorescence signal. A fluorescence intensity matrix was constructed for each specimen to map the signal distribution across different tissue regions. This fluorescence-guided matrix was subsequently used to direct the sampling for histological processing and PSCA immunohistochemical staining, ensuring that the analyzed regions corresponded precisely to the areas of probe binding.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, Inc.) or SPSS (version 26.0, IBM Corp.). For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t test was used. For multiple comparisons, one-way or two-way ANOVA was applied, followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc tests, as appropriate. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), as indicated in the figure legends.
