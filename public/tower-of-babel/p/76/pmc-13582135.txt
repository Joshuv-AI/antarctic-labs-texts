Practice Members’ Perspectives on the Implementation of Colorado Integrated Behavioral Health Plus (CIBH +)
Authors: Jodi Summers Holtrop, Dennis Gurfinkel, Vanessa Owen, Douglas H Fernald, Lakshmi Karra, Patrick Hosokawa, Shandra Brown Levey, Lauren Tolle, Jay Shore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntegrated Behavioral Health is a key solution in addressing significant problems of access and quality of care. This study assessed practice members’ perspectives on the feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of an integrated care model called Colorado Integrated Behavioral Health Plus (CIBH +) in a large integrated health system. Seven family medicine practices implemented CIBH + . Practice members completed surveys regarding feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness (analyzed with descriptive statistics) and semi-structured interviews (analyzed using a grounded theory hermeneutic editing approach). A total of 156 practice members responded to the survey (62% response rate) and 46 participated in interviews (78% response rate). Respondents indicated high levels of agreement about the perceived feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of CIBH + . Qualitative themes included: (1) observing the benefits to patients and comprehensive team care reinforced the value of integrating care; (2) supports from teamwork, leadership, and telehealth facilitated implementation; and (3) limitations from role clarity, staffing, space, telehealth, and lack of long-term care options remained challenging for implementation. Identifying the key features of CIBH + may make practical implementation in other settings more feasible to implement and acceptable for patients and care teams to use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10880-026-10134-3.Keywords: Behavioral health, Primary care, Qualitative research

Introduction

Integrated behavioral health (IBH), the provision of behavioral health care in primary care settings, is a key solution in addressing significant problems of access and quality of care for the United States mental health system. The newly expanded quintuple aim for health care improvement (building up from the triple then quadruple aim) advocated for improving population health, enhancing the care experience, reducing cost, decreasing provider burnout, and advancing health equity (Itchhaporia, 2021; Nundy et al., 2022). Well-developed integrated care models address these challenges and have relevance in addressing disparities in underserved populations including challenges related to access, health care coverage, lack of preventive care, mental health workforce shortages, and deployment of evidence-based practices (Mongelli et al., 2020).

There is a wide range of integrated care models and approaches (Ramanuj et al., 2019; National Council for Mental Wellbeing, 2025), and even when two healthcare systems adapt the same model, the final resulting services can have a wide variability based on the local context, resources, workforce, workflow, and healthcare settings. The best integrated care services are those most specifically adapted to their environments (Gilmer et al., 2016; Padwa et al., 2016; Burns et al., 2023). Achieving sustained implementation of integrated services requires understanding core principles of integration and tailoring approaches into a specific service ecology. Integrated care is team-based care occurring across a broad range of providers as well as administrative and clinical staff. Assessing implementation across those who work in integrated care teams is essential in understanding effective implementation.

Colorado Integrated Behavioral Health Plus (CIBH +) Model

The Colorado IBH + (CIBH +) model is similar to the primary care behavioral health model and builds on evidence of collaborative care (Goodrich et al., 2013; Kathol et al., 2010; Peek & National Integration Academy Council, 2013; Reiter et al., 2018). CIBH + extends and adapts traditional collaborative care models, taking a generalist approach to primary care. There is a central leadership and implementation team with the model being tailored at each of the individual clinics to match the resources, workforce, and environmental contexts. Teams consist of psychologists who provide focused and episodic care, diagnostic clarification, psychological testing, and warm hand-offs with primary care providers (PCPs) and psychiatrists (via telehealth), who help in diagnostic evaluation, case conceptualization, and treatment recommendations including medication management. Psychiatrists not only provide consultations as in the Collaborative Care Model but also provide direct patient care, which is distinct from the Collaborative Care Model (Shore et al., 2025). Digital tools (i.e., patient portal and secure team chat functionalities) as well as planned and just-in-time team meetings facilitate important communication. All patients accessing a clinic with CIBH + are eligible to be seen; there are no exclusion criteria. Patients return for future episodes of care as needed and long-term mental health services are bridged into community mental health settings as appropriate and requested. CIBH + was initiated in 2012 as a pilot in one clinic and grew over the next eight years in all seven clinics described here with the telepsychiatry portion being added in 2015, and the full program became fully staffed by 2022 (Waugh et al., 2019). Thus, all clinics were fully CIBH + functional for numerous years before the methods of this study were conducted. Throughout this paper, the term CIBH + refers to the specific model practiced in this healthcare system. IBH is used when referencing integrated behavior health in general.

This study utilized both quantitative and qualitative methods to assess practice members’ perspectives on feasibility, acceptance, and appropriateness of implementation and functioning in a large integrated health system. These perspectives provide important lessons for the field on the perception of specific integrated care models among practice members. This information distills important lessons on factors that facilitate or impede successful implementation and adaptation of integrated care models.

Methods

The settings for this study included seven family medicine clinics within one health system in metro Denver, Colorado (Table 1). All behavioral health providers (BHPs) referred to in this paper are doctorate level psychologists integrated into the clinics with either a Doctorate of Philosophy (PhD) or a Doctorate of Psychology (PsyD). All psychiatrists are physicians with a medical doctorate (MD) or an osteopathic medical doctorate (DO). The integrated primary care teams at the clinics are comprised of primary care providers (PCPs), BHPs, psychiatry, social work, care management, medical assistants, nurses, and pharmacy.

Data Collection

The Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board reviewed this study, and it was exempted from IRB review. Data were collected from surveys and interviews. Survey data were collected between January and March 2022 and interview data were collected between December 2022 and March 2023, with analysis of these data and manuscript preparation occurring between July and December 2023. In addition, to describe patients, we pulled visit data and patient demographics from all electronic visit records from 7/1/2022 to 6/30/2023. To describe practice staffing, we obtained practice personnel numbers from clinic records in 2023–2024.

Surveys

All practice members received surveys to gain a comprehensive assessment of CIBH + perspectives. Surveys included a scaled assessment on the feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness of CIBH + (12 questions, 5-point Likert scale from completely disagree to strongly agree) as well as participant demographics (primary practice location, position, full- or part-time status, gender, and length of time in role and in the field) (Weiner et al., 2017). Surveys were distributed electronically using the REDCap online survey management system in early 2022. Participants were not individually incentivized due to project funding restrictions; however, gift baskets were provided as a thank you.

Interviews

This study utilized a purposeful sampling strategy to glean narrative expressions of CIBH + from practice members. The sample included the study team purposefully inviting specific roles for participation: all psychologists, psychiatrists, medical directors, and practice administrators. Then clinic team members were invited based on recommendations of medical directors and practice administrators to provide key perspectives based on participation with CIBH + and role they represented. This frequently included additional physicians, administrative staff such as front desk staff and managers, and broader behavioral health team members such as social workers and behavioral health medical assistants.

The interview guide was designed within the overall RE-AIM evaluation framework (Glasgow et al., 1999). We developed the semi-structured interview guide to elicit information about reach into the patient population, adoption and implementation of the CIBH + components, and maintenance of IBH. In addition to perceptions of barriers and facilitators to implementation, we sought further details on mental models and team function, both known to affect implementation of practice changes and interprofessional teamwork (Salas et al., 2005; Cronholm et al., 2013; Bentley et al., 2018; Holtrop et al., 2021; Lessard et al., 2016). Zoom interviews were conducted individually by members of the qualitative research team, including a physician trained in qualitative research (LK), a PhD qualitative researcher (JH), and a masters-trained research assistant (VO). All interviews were digitally recorded, professionally transcribed, and de-identified. We reviewed transcripts for completeness and uploaded them to ATLAS.ti (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, version 23) for analysis. Memos were immediately completed after each interview to capture key points, the interviewer’s impression, and any areas needing follow-up.

Data Analysis

Surveys

We completed descriptive statistics including total frequencies, median and range by clinic for clinic descriptions, and percentages and ranges for the description of patients at the clinics, and means and standard deviations for feasibility, acceptability, and appropriateness results, and frequencies and percentages for the employee participant demographics.

Interviews

We used template and grounded theory hermeneutic editing approaches, a systematic way to inductively identify patterns in qualitative data, to identify themes related to qualitative outcomes (Addison, 1999). An a priori template coding schema was used to initially segment data for further analysis. The deductive a priori codes were determined at the start by the analysis team to organize interview data around broad project evaluation questions (importance of and support for IBH, implementation successes, challenges, and recommendations, adaptations, workflows, patient experience, and roles and teamwork). In the subsequent editing approach to coding, analysts enter into the text data without a predetermined coding scheme, allowing new more detailed concepts and categories to emerge through direct interaction with the data (Crabtree & Miller, 1999). A coding team consisting of LK, VO, and two qualitative analysts (DG and DF, qualitative leader) initially reviewed 10% of transcripts together, discussed and refined (deductive) a priori codes, and developed an early set of inductive (emergent) codes. The remainder of transcripts were independently coded, with frequent team meetings to discuss findings and code usage. LK, DG, and DF conducted analyses and summarized quotation reports from codes independently. They then discussed and merged findings. DF read all quotation summaries to identify the overarching themes for final team review and revision.

Themes and sub-themes that emerged from the interview analysis of the CIBH + program with supporting quotations

Theme 1: Observing the benefits to patients and comprehensive team care reinforced the value of integrating care. Respondents universally agreed that patients benefited from CIBH +, observing specific benefits that reinforce the value of their efforts to integrate care. These benefits included easier, more seamless access to behavioral health and enhanced team care for more comprehensive services. PCPs discussed how BHP consults made them more comfortable addressing behavioral health needs, either by themselves or by referring patients. The extra support enabled medical providers to focus on the medical side of patient care while still being able to offer BH services when needed. With easier access, respondents expressed confidence that they were reaching more patients with CIBH + in settings that may be less stigmatizing than outpatient community mental health clinics.

Understanding the benefits to patients, respondents also articulated ways that they could improve reach. Several mentioned that they could raise utilization of BH services by raising awareness among patients about available BHs services (through patient education or in-office posters) or by adding BHP capacity and support staff support to manage demand.

Theme 2: Supports from teamwork, leadership, and telehealth facilitated implementation.

Respondents reflected on the supports for teams and workflows that facilitate successful implementation of IBH. Across role types, respondents expressed how the practice teams themselves made the implementation successful. Implementation of CIBH + is a team effort requiring communication, adaptation, and coordination across multiple roles. In several practices, there were also learners—medical and psychology trainees—to work into practice routines. Trust in these team members and acknowledgement in their supportive roles was high. Communication across team members benefited from multiple communication channels, including real-time warm handoffs, email, and secure chat messaging in the EHR. It is noted that trust in PCPs by patients was an important facilitator to accept BH services and support.

Dedicated time and space for integration by BHPs and support staff was often instrumental to true integration. Multiple and frequent standing communication opportunities (e.g., team meetings, huddles) enhanced understanding of team roles while facilitating problem solving for CIBH + implementation issues and supporting team members, while opportunities for ad hoc discussions about patients during visibility laps in the office added incentive and reminders to utilize BH services. Space for BHPs and supporting staff was dedicated in a way to make them visible and accessible to PCPs and their support teams. One medical director commented on the benefit of dedicated time for BHP support for quality improvement initiatives at the practice level for a variety of projects as well as to work with local leadership on practice transformation and allow for creativity to flow more freely.

Telehealth options were described as facilitators for increasing accessibility, scheduling internally, and allowing BHPs to provide cross-clinic coverage. Telepsychiatry also ensured all clinics had access to psychiatric services for their patients who needed medication consultation. The availability of telepsychiatry also was thought to improve care delivery and confidence from PCPs and psychologists, who received consultation advice on patient care from the psychiatrists during standing check-ins and as needed.

The perception that clinic and departmental leadership is strongly supportive of IBH, which created opportunities (through dedicated time, resources, and vocal support) for practice members to work together on implementing, innovating, and adapting for CIBH +, was apparent from feedback across role types.

Theme 3: Limitations from role clarity, staffing, space, telehealth, and lack of long-term care options remained challenging for implementation.

Lack of role clarity regarding what type of behavioral care can be offered in an integrated model may unintentionally limit services available to patients. Psychologists shared the view that they could offer a broader range of behavioral health services and evidence-based treatments to patients beyond common mental health concerns such as depression and anxiety. This view of skills limited services for which patients could benefit. Doubt was expressed regarding leadership’s understanding and appreciation for the value that doctorally trained psychologists bring as opposed to master’s level clinicians.

BHP staffing was uneven across sites, with some sites benefiting from sufficient BHP staffing, while others experienced insufficient staffing to meet patient demand, sometimes delaying care and reducing the number of warm handoffs. Several respondents added that with an increase in BHP demand, BHPs and others would benefit from dedicated time to work on practice improvements. Not having adequate space for the BHPs can limit integrated care opportunities.

Several respondents also noted that adding additional BHPs to a practice also increased the workload for supporting staff (notably MAs).

While the model encourages in-person warm handoffs, finding enough physical space for BHP’s patient care was challenging, and the space may not have been ideal (i.e., exam room instead of dedicated behavioral health space). This was especially true when adding BH trainees. Further, limits on physical space may inhibit one-on-one interactions among providers and space for formal and informal information exchange (i.e., consultations, off-the-cuff questions) that enhance team communication.

Though telehealth may help with implementation (including addressing limited available space), telehealth may not always be the best approach, even though it is what is available. Respondents reported telehealth may not be appropriate or available for every patient, so in-person options are still important and may help expand reach. Remote or virtual work is highly beneficial and may limit the ability to connect with teammates as fully as in person. Collaboration and trust among team members is important and needs continuous tending to, particularly when services are primarily virtual.

BHPs and support staff try to arrange care for patients in need of long-term therapy in community settings, but this care is often lacking or inaccessible due to long wait times or payment issues. BHPs sometimes reported having to pick up care needs, at least on a limited basis, which in turn would stress already overburdened staff.

Discussion

Due to increased demand for mental and behavioral health services, primary care practices across the United States are increasingly integrating BHPs within their clinics. CIBH + offers a glimpse at a robust integration effort in a large practice system in Colorado. CIBH + integration implementation is intentional and impacts the practice workflows of all practice members. Surveys indicated high levels of agreement about feasibility, acceptance, and appropriateness of our model among practice members. CIBH + was viewed as feasible to implement within the clinics, appropriate for patient care, and acceptable for practice member workflow changes. Interviews with key informants across clinical roles captured perceived reasons patients benefit from CIBH +, such as expanded access and improvements in care quality. Interviewees discussed elements of CIBH + implementation that helped the model operate smoothly and also identified pain points. Together, we found that CIBH + presents a workable, flexible model for BH integration into primary care and provide recommendations based on the experiences in Colorado.

Themes noted in our findings are consistent with literature in the field on integrated primary care, both in terms of facilitators for integration (team work, dedicated and visible space for BHPs, leadership support (Supper et al., 2015; Monaghan & Cos, 2021; Peer & Koren, 2022)) as well as barriers (desire for improved long-term mental health resources in the community, space constraints, role clarity with regard to breadth of behavioral medicine expertise BHPs have beyond traditional mental health roles (Supper et al., 2015; Grazier et al., 2016; Monaghan & Cos, 2021; Rawlinson et al., 2021; Nederveld et al., 2023)). Our findings further aligned with three review articles/studies that describe important common factors that can advance or hinder the success of integrated care (Looman et al., 2021; Coates et al., 2022; Jewiss et al., 2023). Common factors across articles related to this work include importance of local contextual and cultural factors, leadership and governance, workforce and team dynamics, communication, and IT infrastructure. The importance of leadership commitment across multiple organizational levels, shared values and expectations, team dynamics, practice culture, expectation/role communication, appropriate communication processes and technology to enable integration are all key factors to facilitate or hinder processes. These findings are also consistent with our work considering the perspectives of patients who were participants in this care process (Gurfinkel et al., 2024). Additionally current data from these services support the integration of this model including 26,425 encounters completed by CIBH + providers (6.7% of total clinic visits). However, this figure underrepresents the broader scope of integrated care, which includes case-specific consultations and education provided to primary care providers. Additionally, mixed-methods analysis from a subsample of patients highlights improved perceived access and quality of behavioral health care (Gurfinkel et al., 2024). Ongoing work is focused on capturing more granular clinical and system-level outcomes, including metrics such as warm handoffs and same-day appointments. Further detail on the model, in light of these findings, is available (Shore et al., 2025).

This study adds the integration of telepsychiatry, which is absent from many IBH models. This layer of assistance for patients and the team supports the complexities of care needed for patients in primary care. Also key is the importance of the “teamness” of IBH that has not been highlighted, with work favoring the more practical aspects of implementation instead of the conceptual. The BHPs, psychiatrists, and others in the practice, such as social workers, work together in an in-person and virtual way to create a team approach to caring for the patient. The theme of the IBH team “helping out” the busy PCP was evident and is critically different from co-located models of care (Looman et al., 2021; Coates et al., 2022; Jewiss et al., 2023). The CIBH + model is conceptually different from a co-located or consultant model where behavioral health is part of the team vs. providing consultation outside the team.

This study has several limitations. These data reflect the views of the practice team members from seven primary care practice practices in metro Denver, Colorado, and not at other locations, health systems, or differently implemented models. Also, these results do not represent the views of patients or their families participating in IBH; thus, data may be interpreted as such. As authors, we developed the recommendations listed above for others to consider when implementing IBH models. In developing those recommendations, we included individuals associated with the implementation and evaluation of IBH; however, the respondents did not have a chance to review or comment on the recommendations. It is also important to note that these seven clinics are all quite different from each other in terms of culture, space, and staffing support, so individual responses are not intended to be representative across practices. The multiple methods utilized enhanced input from practice members and the convergence of results across methods strengthen the likelihood of accuracy and may not represent all perspectives.

Conclusion

With any intervention involving clinical care, there are opportunities and challenges. This study found CIBH + was implemented well overall as represented by practice members surveys and interviews. Some limitations were discussed, and constraints of practicing clinical medicine in the U.S. creates opportunities for adjustments resulting in smoother and more accessible service for patients. We look to address these opportunities in our setting, and it is our hope others find these reflections beneficial for practice improvement and innovation as well.
