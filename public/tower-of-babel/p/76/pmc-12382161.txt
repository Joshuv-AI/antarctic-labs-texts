Nonparametric analysis of quality-of-life measures for randomized clustered design: the R package npclust
Authors: Yue Cui, Curtis W Noonan, Erin O Semmens, Tony J Ward, Solomon W Harrar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-Post intervention factorial design with multiple observations for each subject before and after the intervention frequently arises in Quality-of-Life (QoL) research. Usually not only a global hypothesis on intervention is of interest, but also the hypotheses of pre-post change and also the interaction between intervention and pre-post change. In most practical situations, the distribution of the observed data is unknown and there may exist a number of atypical measurements and outliers. More importantly, QoL outcomes are measured in rating scales and the multiple Quality-of-Life measurements before and after the intervention present complex within-subject correlations. Hence, use of parametric and semi-parametric procedures that impose restrictive distributional and correlation assumptions becomes questionable. This emphasizes the demand for statistical procedures that enable us to accurately and reliably analyze QoL outcomes with minimal conditions. Nonparametric methods offer such a possibility and thus become of particular practical importance in the field of QoL. In this article, we aim to expose researchers and practitioners in the biomedical and behavioral science to nonparametric methods for the analysis of data collected in clustered randomized design. We also illustrate the use of the R package npclust we developed for an easy and user-friendly access to nonparametric methods for the analysis of Quality-of-Life data. It provides procedures for pre-processing the data, performing various hypothesis tests and computing confidence intervals for the estimated effects. The procedures also contain methods for visual display of the results. We illustrate the implemented procedures with Pediatric Asthma Quality-of-Life data collected from the Asthma Randomized Trial of Indoor wood Smoke (ARTIS).Keywords: Relative effects, Nonparametric methods, Hierarchical data, Pre-post intervention design, R, npclust

Introduction

Quality of Life (QoL) is a concept which aims to capture the well-being of a population or individual, regarding both positive and negative elements within the entirety of their existence at a specific point in time [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines QoL as an individual’s perception of their reality relative to their goals as observed through the lens of their culture and value system (https://www.who.int/tools/whoqol). To assess this, WHO developed a generic, patient-completed health-related quality of life instrument WHOQOL-100 [2]. The shorter version, WHOQOL-BREF, often embedded in epidemiological studies, assesses four domains: physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environment. There are additional two items that measure overall QoL and general health. All the items are assessed in a rating (1-5) ordinal scale.

While the vast majority of evaluation of treatment efficacy and effectiveness uses randomized controlled trial (RCT) design, factorial designs permit evaluation of multiple intervention components with good statistical power and offer the opportunity to detect interactions amongst intervention components [3]. Indeed, factorial designs with pre-post repeated measures is a widely used layout in Quality of Life research [4, 5]. Data from these studies are typically analyzed by means of parametric procedures, e.g., generalized linear mixed effects model. However, the corresponding parametric assumptions are typically hard to meet in practice. Further, these models become inappropriate if outcomes are measured in a non-metric metric scale such as rating scale in the sense that group means are meaningless in describing effects.

In more complicated designs where clusters are used as sampling or experimental units in repeated measures factorial designs, the problem becomes more challenging because units within a cluster cannot be assumed independent. For example, in community psychology research data are collected at the individual level within communities or clusters [6]. In this setup, the clusters may be assumed to be independent within factor level combinations but the observations on the subunits or members of the same clusters cannot, in general, be independent.

By and large, the most common method for analyzing pre-post data in factorial design are the marginal and random effects models [7]. For continuous outcome variables, alternative methods based on ANCOVA analysis of gain scores (post minus pre measurements) are found scattered in the literature, e.g. see Laird [8] and O’Connell et al. [9] and the references therein. Semi-parametric models where parts of the parametric assumptions are relaxed have also been proposed [10]. However, these methods still have parametric assumptions for the distribution of the response variable and the random effects, the conditional means and the correlation structure. Fully nonparametric methods provide robust alternatives to overcome these limitations.

There are also discussions about whether one should choose nonparametric methods over parametric models for ordered categorical data like quality-of-life measurements. Kahler et al. [11] investigate ordered categorical data from health-related quality-of-life (HRQoL) studies, and compare results from parametric and nonparametric methods. They conclude that ordinal character of HRQoL scores can lead to problems when parametric methods are applied, thus recommend categorical based statistical methods for analyses and interpretation of HRQoL scores to avoid inappropriate conclusions.

In psychological health research, some authors suggest that the Mann-Whitney’s U test, which is the default nonparametric test for whether a variable tends to be larger in one population than in another, should be replaced by Brunner-Munzel’s test [12]. The authors claim that when the unrealistic exchangeability assumption is violated, Mann-Whitney’s U test leads to wrong statistical inference. In pre-post intervention study, the exchangeability assumption is particularly restrictive as it requires that under the null hypothesis of no effect, pre- and post-intervention measurements can be treated as interchangeable without altering the underlying statistical properties. The main argument in favor of exchangability is that it allows for robust inference even when the data does not meet the assumptions of normality or homoscedasticity required by parametric tests. However, the Brunner-Munzel test is known to perform well without the exchangeability assumption. Nevertheless, it is only applicable to comparisons of independent samples and would not be appropriate for clustered data.

The objective of this manuscript is to introduce researchers and practitioners in biomedical and behavioral sciences to nonparametric methods for analyzing data collected in clustered randomized designs. We aim to make these methods more accessible by presenting the R package npclust, which includes detailed guidance on data management, model fitting, effect size estimation, and result interpretation. To further facilitate practical application, we provide sample code for both analysis and visualization, demonstrating the complete work flow.

The outline of this manuscript is as follows. The ARTIS data section provides an overview of the ARTIS data, including the trial design and the limitations of analyzing it with existing models. The Statistical model, effects, and hypotheses section elaborates on the statistical model, introducing the nonparametric effect size and the corresponding hypothesis tests. Software section highlights the key functions of the R package npclust and presents an example analysis using the ARTIS data. Mixed effects model section showcases the linear mixed effects model for readers as reference, and provides different aspects in model fitting. Finally, the manuscript concludes with a discussion in Discussion section.

The ARTIS data

The Asthma Randomized Trial of Indoor wood Smoke (ARTIS) [13–15] is a three-arm randomized trial of interventions to improve childhood asthma in homes with wood-burning stoves. The study is a randomized placebo-controlled intervention trial with two intervention strategies for reducing in-home woodsmoke particulate matter. The households were randomly assigned to a placebo group or one of the two active intervention groups: placebo group receiving sham air-filtration devices, wood stove group receiving improved-technology wood-burning appliance, and air-filter group receiving air-filtration devices. Eligible participants included children with asthma, aged 6 to 18 and residing in a non-tobacco smoking household that used older model wood stoves as primary heating source. All children who have asthma in the participating household were included in the study. ARTIS study was conducted over 5 years and each household participated in two consecutive winter periods, where the intervention was applied in the intervening time.

In this trial, Pediatric Asthma Quality of Life Questionnaire (PAQLQ) was employed to assess the primary outcome. PAQLQ has 23 asthma-specific questions in 3 domains: symptoms (10 items), activity limitation (5 items), and emotional function (8 items). Children were asked to think about how they have been during the previous week and to respond to each question on a 7-point scale. Questions about symptoms or limitation of activity were framed around concern (7 = not bothered at all; 1 = extremely bothered) or frequency (7 = none of the time; 1 = all the time). The overall PAQLQ score is the mean of all 23 responses and the individual domain scores are the means of items in those domains.

Ninety-eight homes completed pre-intervention winter measures and were randomized (sham: ; wood stove: ; air filter: ). Of these, 37 sham, 14 wood stove, and 35 air filter homes completed measures in the winter following intervention implementation. There are a small portion of households that have multiple participating children but the numbers vary on different responses and intervention plan. For example, there are 6 households in the placebo group, 4 households each in the wood-stove and air-filter group that have two or three participating children on the symptom domain. Data were captured at up to four visits, two visits in the winter before intervention and two visits in the subsequent intervention winter. Table 1 was adapted from Noonan et al. [14], which demonstrates the location and winter periods when the interventions were conducted. The quality of life data collected in this manner consist of three hierarchies of clustering (from bottom to top): multiple visits within the pre- and post-intervention periods, pre- and post-intervention measurements for each participating child, and multiple participating children clustered in the same household. This structure of hierarchies and non-metric nature of outcomes make the ARTIS data complex. Another challenge is the presence of missing values. Though four visits were planned, not all participants had responses collected for them all, and some of them missed all visits during the intervention winter. Details on the missing patterns are provided in Sample size and cluster size allocations section.

The objective of the study is to estimate the difference in PAQLQ scores across placebo, wood-stove and air-filter intervention groups, as well as the pattern of changes in scores in the pre- and post-intervention periods. These changes can be summarized into intervention effect and time effect. If one is interested in the cross effect between the two, then the interaction effect can be further investigated. The interaction effect evaluates whether intervention assignment modified the pre-intervention to post-intervention change in PAQLQ scores.

Naturally, a linear mixed effects model is adequate to answer the aforementioned research questions under certain assumptions [14]. However, the normality and homoscedasticity assumptions of linear mixed effects model are hard to verify in practice. To overcome these limitations, we will analyze the ARTIS data with the nonparametric approach developed in Harrar and Cui [16] and implemented by the npclust package in R.

Mixed effects model

As discussed in The ARTIS data section, parametric linear mixed effects model is widely used to analyze clustered data by imposing the appropriate random effects, if its assumptions can be verified. For the purpose of comparison, we also analyze the ARTIS data with linear mixed effects model. As such, the linear mixed effects model includes fixed effects for intervention assignment and time. It also includes an interaction term to assess effect modification by intervention assignment. Random effects for both the intercept and the slope (i.e. time) are included, which in effect allow each participant to have his/her own intercept and slope describing change in PAQLQ scores from the pre-intervention to post-intervention periods. Discussion

Clustered data in pre-post intervention design are frequently encountered in Quality of Life research. More often, outcomes are assessed in rating scales (ordered categorical scales), which makes parametric methods inappropriate. The package npclust provides a nonparametric method particularly suited for this type of the data, and implements other helpful visualization tools. The package provides local test decisions in terms of p-values and simultaneous confidence intervals after multiplicity adjustment. They further allow for user-defined contrasts.

As previously mentioned, the top-level clustering has been removed from the ARTIS data. While it is possible to extend this method to multi-level clustering, the associated theories and methodologies are quite complex. In cases where the subunits within higher-level clusters are highly correlated and the clustering structure is straightforward, random sampling may be a feasible approach. However, this strategy is not recommended if there is significant variation among the subunits or if a substantial portion of the data would be excluded, as this could lead to biased or imprecise results. A more rigorous approach would involve repeating the analysis multiple times by randomly selecting one kid from each household and averaging the results—a method known as within-cluster resampling [24, 25]. The methodological details for obtaining estimates of nonparametric effects, along with the associated standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis testing procedures, still need to be fully developed.

Another important consideration is power calculation, which can guide sample size estimates. Intensive simulation studies on power, as conducted in Harrar and Cui [16], suggest that the tests exhibit reasonable power even with relatively small sample sizes and cluster sizes, provided there is sufficient difference between the underlying marginal distributions. Generally, clustering has an effect of reducing power. A future study that addresses power and sample size calculation in the context of nonparametric analysis of randomized clustered design would be of great importance.

Overall, npclust provides effect size estimates along with their confidence intervals (adjusted or unadjusted). In the field of Quality of Life, where data are generally collected by questionnaires and recorded by ordinal or ordered categorical metrics, this R package npclust could be invaluable and easy to access resource. A notable novel feature of npclust is that it can easily be extended to introduce additional methods. For example, an extra function for multiple comparison among the estimated effect sizes for significant tests could be added for pre-planned or post-hoc comparisons. We also aim to include a function where multivariate data can be analyzed with the same nonparametric method. For example, the QoL scores from all three domains can be tested jointly. So far there is no method or package available when the data are not missing completely at random, i.e. missing at random (MAR), we plan to implement this mechanism in npclust at a later time. In summary, we plan to update the package npclust on a regular basis as new nonparametric statistical procedures for clustered data become available.
