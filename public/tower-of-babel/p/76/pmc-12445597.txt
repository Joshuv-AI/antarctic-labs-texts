Estimating the number of cardio-pulmonary events potentially averted by fine particulate matter air quality index activity guidelines
Authors: Robert D Brook, Pia-Allison Roa, Phillip D Levy, Samantha J Bauer, Steven Korzeniewski, Laurence S. Sperling, Sadeer Al-Kindi, Sanjay Rajagopalan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFine particulate matter <2.5 µm (PM2.5) air pollution increases cardio-pulmonary morbidity and mortality. To inform the public regarding local air quality and reduce short-term exposures to PM2.5, daily Air Quality Indices (AQIs) with activity guidance are issued across the United States (US). We aimed to estimate the cardio-pulmonary health benefits of the current AQI strategy.MethodsWe estimated the number needed to treat (NNT) per county per day to avoid 1 atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) or pulmonary event during each PM2.5 AQI action day (defined as a day with an AQI >100 for PM2.5) for 3 groups (healthy adults, ASCVD and pulmonary disease patients) in the US during 2022. The theoretical number of adverse events potentially prevented per county was estimated each day as the relevant county population divided by the corresponding NNT. These results were summed for all AQI action days among US counties to equal the potential total number of ASCVD and pulmonary events avoided nationwide.ResultsThere were 1081 AQI action days for PM2.5 (58.0 ± 32.4 µg/m3) across 236 counties in 2022, accounting for an estimated excess of 164 ASCVD and 3963 pulmonary events. Approximately 31 ASCVD and 796 pulmonary events could be theoretically avoided if all populations followed activity guidelines during every AQI action day.ConclusionsDaily AQI activity guidelines, as currently structured and implemented, likely prevent few cardio-pulmonary events per year in the US. Additional research is warranted to determine how to improve the overall approach to more effectively protect public health.

Fine particulate matter <2.5 µm (PM2.5) air pollution is a leading risk factor for both cardiovascular and pulmonary morbidity and mortality[1,2]. Long-term exposures to higher levels of PM2.5 promote cardiometabolic disorders and significantly increase the risk for cardiopulmonary events[1,2]. National ambient air quality standards (NAAQS) issued by the United States (US) Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) have led to lower annual average levels of PM2.5 over the past few decades. This has translated into improved health outcomes and increased life expectancy in the US[1,2].

Beyond the impact of long-term exposures, day-to-day elevations in PM2.5 can additionally trigger acute cardiopulmonary events. To raise awareness about daily local air quality and to help protect the public health from any residual short-term elevations in PM2.5, the EPA issues Air Quality Indices (AQIs) with corresponding activity guidance on a daily basis[2]. AQIs are calculated using a formula to convert 6 individual air pollutant concentrations to a scale of 0–500, with higher values indicating worse air quality (https://www.airnow.gov/aqi/). The pollutant with the highest value, which is nearly always either PM2,5 or ozone in the US, serves as the overall AQI for the day. This process is done to relay air quality information to the public in an easy-to-understand format. The rational for the activity guidance is to inform the public on how to take feasible personal-level actions that may help reduce short-term exposures. The ultimate goal, however, is for these actions to translate into reductions in adverse health effects. Lowering brief day-long exposures should prevent acute cardio-pulmonary events[1]. Nonetheless, this logic does not necessarily mean that the specific recommendations and overall approach taken by the AQI activity guidance as currently designed is an evidence-based or effective strategy.

We recently demonstrated that the number of people per day required to follow the AQI recommendations to prevent a single adverse event is extremely large - many millions for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and tens of thousands for pulmonary events[3]. While these findings suggested that current AQI activity guidance is unlikely to translate into an effective strategy, the potential impact upon national public health was not assessed. As such, we herein aimed to estimate the number of cardio-pulmonary events prevented per year by following current AQI activity guidelines in the US. This information can help inform whether these guidelines have merit as a public health policy as well as a preventive medicine strategy that healthcare professionals (and organizations) should continue to endorse in order to help support the well-being of patients[1].

Methods

Institutional Review Board approval was not needed as this was a cross-sectional study that did not meet criteria for human participation research as publicly available de-identified data were analyzed. We collected daily AQI levels as well as demographic and disease-state data for US counties in 2022 as a representative year[[4], [5], [6]].

The number needed to treat (NNT) per county per day to avoid 1 ASCVD or pulmonary event in 3 population groups by following recommendations during each individual PM2.5 AQI action day (defined as days with AQI levels >100) was calculated using 4 factors as previously described using our publicly available online calculator (https://phoenix.wayne.edu/learning-lab)[3,7]. The calculator first estimates the baseline absolute ASCVD event rate per day per million people in the healthy adult population (10-year ASCVD risk of 5 %) and patients with established ASCVD (10-year ASCVD risk of 20 %) [interim result A]. For example, the calculation for a population with ASCVD is: [(0.2/10)/365] x 1000,000 = 54.8 events per day per million people. Next, the excess absolute ASCVD event rates per day per million people due to exposures are calculated by multiplying baseline event rates by the increases in risks from elevations in PM2.5 at each AQI level [interim result B]. A monotonic 1.0 % increase in risk for ASCVD (and pulmonary) events per day for every 10 µg/m3 elevation above an AQI of good (PM2.5= 9 µg/m3) was used in our modeling[1,3]. The AQI to PM2.5 conversion model employed by the EPA in 2022 (i.e., prior to the changes in 2024) were used because they correspond to the appropriate period. For example, at an AQI of 150 (PM2.5= 55 µg/m3), we subtracted 9 from 55 to yield an increase in exposure of 46 µg/m3 (above the NAAQS goal of 9 µg/m3) which corresponds to a 4.6 % increase in ASCVD (and pulmonary) events per day. For patients with a 20 % ASCVD risk at this exposure the calculation is: 54.79×0.046 = 2.52 excess events per day per million people. Third, 24-hour exposure reductions incurred by following AQI activity guidance were estimated. As there is no scientific consensus on this topic, we modeled optimistic values to provide best case scenarios as a basis to begin to explore potential public health ramifications[3,7]. At each AQI, the absolute excess event rate per day per million people was multiplied by the percentage of exposure reduction to provide the number of events prevented per day per million people [interim result C]. Using the prior example, the calculation is: 2.52×0.15 (i.e., 15 % exposure reduction by following activity guidance at an AQI of 150 for ASCVD patients) = 0.378082 events prevented per million people per day. The NNT per day was calculated (per definition)[8] as the reciprocal of the absolute number of events prevented (i.e., absolute risk reduction) per day per million people multiplied by 1 million: (1/0.378) x 1000,000 = 2644,928 people.

The NNTs for avoiding pulmonary events, including asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations of any severity, were calculated for all individuals (adults and children) with preexisting lung disease using the same basic methodology as above in the online calculator assuming an average baseline pulmonary event rate of 0.5 events per year as previously described[3,7]. The NNTs to prevent ASCVD events were calculated for healthy adults because these events occur in seemingly healthy people at a predictable rate in the population (i.e., the ASCVD risk score) and PM2.5 is well-known to increase this risk[1]. Conversely, the NNTs for pulmonary events were not calculated for the healthy adult population. Asthma and COPD events are only established to be triggered by acute PM2.5 exposures in patients with preexisting lung disease[9,10]. Additionally, pulmonary events do not predictably occur in the healthy adult population – except if underlying lung disease is undiagnosed among a percentage of individuals (see sensitivity analyses). As such, NNT results for the healthy adult population apply to ASCVD events alone and include only the relevant AQI action days when activity recommendations are issued (i.e., AQIs ≥151). The NNT results for ASCVD patients and individuals with pulmonary disease apply to all AQI action days (AQIs >100).

We next estimated the “total number of AQI action person-days for PM2.5″. This was equal to the sum of the relevant population groups within US counties added each time there is an AQI action day for that location during 20,222. County populations can be counted more than once. The healthy adult population (>18 years old) of each county was estimated as the adult population (78 % of the total population) subtracting adults with ASCVD (10 %) and with lung disease (15 %). All individuals with lung disease were estimated as 15 % of the adult population (adults with asthma [9 %] or COPD [6 %]) plus 7 % of children (asthma only). Demographic and disease-state estimates were acquired from the US Census Bureau and the US Center for Disease Control[5,6].

The “number of adverse events prevented per county” was next calculated for each AQI action day as the relevant county population for each specific group divided by the NNT for that group (relevant population per county/NNT ratio). This calculation is analogous to multiplying the number of events prevented per day per million people [interim result C] by the county population (in millions). The relevant population per county/NNT ratios were summed for all AQI action days in all US counties during 2022 to equal the “total number of adverse health events avoided in the US”. Last, the “number of AQI action person-days required to prevent 1 adverse health event” was calculated as the total number of AQI action person-days divided by the total number of adverse health events avoided in the US during 2022.

In sensitivity analyses we employed additional plausible estimates of factors involved in the calculations of our results including a range of 0.5 % to 2.0 % increase in risk for ASCVD and pulmonary events per 10 µg/m3 increase in 24-hour average PM2.5 concentration and alternative effectiveness estimates of activity changes for reducing exposures. We also evaluated the impact of using varied population size estimates for ASCVD and pulmonary disease patients.

Results

There were 1081 AQI action days for PM2.5 during 2022, occurring in 236 out of 3143 US counties (Table 1). During AQI action days the mean 24-hour PM2.5 concentration was 58.0 ± 32.4 µg/m3 (range: 36.0–246.0 µg/m3). Without following AQI activity guidance, this short-term worsening of air quality would account for an estimated excess of approximately 164 ASCVD and 3963 pulmonary events (Table 1). The NNT to prevent 1 ASCVD event exceeded the county populations for the healthy adult public (population/NNT ratio 0.036) and patients with ASCVD (population/NNT ratio 0.017) during all AQI action days (Table 1). As such, in no county could a single ASCVD event be avoided on 1 day by following AQI activity guidance during 2022. In sensitivity analyses we applied alternative population sizes (e.g., 25–75 % over- or under-diagnosis of ASCVD and COPD/asthma) and a range of plausible alternative assumptions for the 4 factors to calculate NNTs. The numbers of total adverse health events prevented were minimally altered, ranging from 400–600 to 1200–1600. Applying the more stringent AQI criteria promulgated in 2024 modestly increased many NNT calculations, but did not meaningfully change the number of cardio-pulmonary events prevented[11]. Conversely, employing logic whereby an event could only be avoided if the relevant county population at least equals the NNT on any given AQI action day resulted in no ASCVD events and only 189 pulmonary events being prevented during all of 2022.

Discussion

Reducing long-term ambient PM2.5 concentrations improves public health[1]. Additionally reducing exposures to short-term periods of higher PM2.5 levels should help further decrease acute adverse health effects. Nevertheless, we demonstrate for the first time that the current approach taken by daily AQI activity guidelines, which aim to reduce residual short-term PM2.5 exposures, may be suboptimal and prevent few cardio-pulmonary events per year in the US given present-day air quality. Most of the events are pulmonary-related, whereas only a handful of cardiovascular events might be averted.

Our findings have important clinical and preventive medicine implications. Foremost, they question the real-world effectiveness of AQI activity guidance and whether healthcare professionals should continue to endorse them as has been formally recommended by both cardiovascular and pulmonary medical societies[12,13]. At the very least our results support that more evidence-based research is needed to improve the potential effectiveness of future guidance. In this regard, we recently demonstrated that tailoring the intensity of activity recommendations to baseline ASCVD or pulmonary risk can significantly enhance their efficiency and potential clinical effectiveness by significantly lowering the NNT[7]. However, we acknowledge that any single analysis is usually insufficient to change standards of public policy and clinical preventive medicine. It is therefore our opinion that healthcare professionals should not (as of yet) stop endorsing AQI guidelines but should support the call for more evidence-based research.

Our results can be explained by several factors including the low baseline absolute risk per day for ASCVD events in healthy adults (13.7 per million) and even among ASCVD patients (54.8 per million), the small increase in this risk (3–10 %) due to day-long elevations in PM2.5, the relatively few AQI action days per year (1081) under present-day conditions in the US, and the marginal plausible effectiveness of the promulgated activity restrictions to reduce acute exposures (15–35 %)[3,7]. A greater number of pulmonary events could be prevented. This is explained by a much higher baseline rate of events (e.g., roughly 1370 per million per day) in patients with preexisting lung disease. However, asthma or COPD exacerbations (albeit clinically important) do not typically equate to the same level of medical seriousness of a myocardial infarction or stroke. It is crucial to note that for each of the 4 factors in our models used to calculate the NNT (and ultimately the number of events prevented), we used estimations that overall favored a greater effectiveness of AQI activity guidance. In fact, a lower range of exposure-risk associations (or a supra-linear dose-response) and lower effectiveness of activity guidance to reduce exposures than we modelled could be rationally defended. In this context, if anything our modeling provided a higher range of the potential effectiveness of AQI guidance.

Our findings should be considered best case scenarios. The marginal benefits we demonstrated would be further cut in half by assuming more real-world public awareness and adherence to AQI guidance as shown in surveys[14,15]. While some sub-groups are more susceptible to PM2.5 (e.g., socially vulnerable communities) [1], our results are meant to provide an estimation for the entirety of the US population. Even so, the findings did not meaningfully change if more robust risk-exposure relationships were assumed.

It could be logically conjectured that by following AQI activity guidelines throughout an entire year that repetitive activity changes could translate into reductions in annual average PM2.5 exposures. Since there is conclusive evidence that lowering chronic PM2.5 levels leads to improved public health, this speculation would have merit if true[1]. Unfortunately, our results do not support this hypothesis. AQI action days for PM2.5 are relatively uncommon events under present-day air quality: 1081 occurrences during 2022 out of a theoretical possibility of 1147,195 alerts (3143 counties x 365 days). These action days were also isolated to a minority of counties (236 out of 3143) in limited geographic areas (Fig. 1). The vast majority of these counties were impacted on only a handful of occurrences (1–5 times per year) and at the lowest AQI range (i.e., unhealthy for sensitive groups) (Table 2). It is crucial to highlight that by far the greatest proportion of adverse public health effects from PM2.5 in the US result from chronic low concentrations (9–35 µg/m3) over years of exposure[1,12,13]. These ambient levels are below the thresholds that prompt any AQI activity recommendations and are thus not directly addressable by current daily alerts[2]. Taken together, this data suggests that following daily AQI activity guidelines will have a negligible impact on the overall chronic annual PM2.5 exposure (and health effects due to long-term exposures) faced by US citizens.

We acknowledge several limitations. We focused solely on PM2.5. This was intentional because it causes by far the most air pollution-induced disease[1]. Different modeling would also be required to include ozone, which poses less clear ASCVD risks[1,16]. An even greater percentage of adverse health impacts would be due to pulmonary events, which would also be mostly confined to warmer seasons when ozone is elevated.

Second, the real-world capability of activity changes to translate into successful reductions in PM2.5 exposures is uncertain[3]. This has never been systematically studied and therefore our analyses (like any modeling) were required to provide estimations of their potential effectiveness. Moreover, there would necessarily be a wide variation day-to-day and between individuals on their effectiveness and adherence. Outdoor-to-indoor penetration of particles, air conditioning, indoor sources, and adherence to the guidelines will influence their capacity to reduce exposures[12,13]. With this inherent limitation in mind, we intentionally projected optimistic exposure reductions as well as ideal awareness and adherence[3,7]. As such, our analyses may have over-estimated (perhaps by 50 %) the true public health benefits of AQI activity guidance. In our prior paper we provided a potential range of effectiveness of the interventions and modelled the impact on the NNT[3]. There were minimal changes in the end results. We additionally performed sensitivity analyses in this analysis estimating a range of effectiveness of the activity changes in reducing exposures. As shown in the results, given all the factors combined, the final range of NNT values and subsequent cardiopulmonary events prevented were minimally changed even across a wide variation in this estimation. The results were not sensitive to variations in this estimated effectiveness of activity changes for reasons highlighted earlier. The ultimate number of events prevented will remain low because baseline daily cardiopulmonary events rates per day are rare in the population and the risks posed by 24-hour exposures are small. Therefore, the estimations of exposure reductions we used in this study are plausible and are not a fundamental limitation. Conversely, patients with COPD (∼6 % of adults)[17] were only assessed for reductions in pulmonary events despite also being at high risk for ASCVD[18]. This may have led to a modest under-calculation of the total number of ASCVD events avoided (∼5–10 events per year).

The dose-response relationship between exposure and cardiopulmonary events needs to be estimated for our models. For the main analyses we also selected a risk at the higher end of the published range from the largest meta-analysis: a 1.0 % increase in events per 10 µg/m3 in daily PM2.5 levels[19]. Most studies and the pooled risks (0.92 % and 0.73 %, for cardiovascular and respiratory mortality), in fact, show slightly smaller risks of cardiopulmonary events of 0.4 % to 0.9 %[19]. We selected a slightly higher risk because we also included nonfatal cardiopulmonary events as part of our outcomes – which tend to show higher risks (1.0 % to 2.0 %)[1]. Ultimately, our estimations provide a higher range of NNT results and, thus a potentially high estimate of the number of cardiopulmonary events prevented. In our sensitivity analyses we provided a range of results derived from a range of this dose-response estimate from 0.5 to 2.0 % per 10 µg/m3 in daily PM2.5 levels. For the same reasons previously outlined, the overall results were minimally sensitive to this change. We also used a monotonic linear risk-exposure relationship. While a supra-linear risk has been found in some acute and chronic studies, across the range of exposures in our study a linear risk association was shown in the latest meta-analysis[19]. However, if we modeled an attenuating impact of risk at higher levels of PM2.5, (i.e., supra-linear model), we would have actually calculated an even lower number of events prevents. As with the effectiveness of exposure reductions provided by the AQI activity guidance, we modeled an exposure-risk relationship that would provide generally more favorable estimations of cardiopulmonary events prevented.

Finally, our study represents a cross-sectional modelling analysis only. The reduction in actual health events due to AQI guidance cannot be validated by direct assessments. However, our findings are supported by similar conclusions reported by a prior smaller study that evaluated mortality reductions among elderly adults in 3 US cities[20].

In conclusion, current AQI activity guidance likely prevents a limited number of cardio-pulmonary events per year in the US. Our findings reinforce that national priorities should focus on NAAQS that improve long-term air quality to protect public health. Daily AQIs remain important to inform the public regarding local air quality. However, more evidence-based human and population-level research is needed to advance the relevance and effectiveness of the corresponding activity guidelines for them to be more effective public health and preventive medicine strategies moving forward.
