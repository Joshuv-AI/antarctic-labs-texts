The CCR/WC ratio as a predictor of dyslipidemia and Its mediating effect on hypertension in middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Yang Yang, Yufang Hu, Xiaodan Li, Qi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe creatinine-to-cystatin C/waist circumference ratio (CCR/WC) combines renal function and adiposity metrics, yet its predictive value for dyslipidemia remains undefined. This research examined CCR/WC’s capacity as a dyslipidemia biomarker and its mediating role in hypertension pathogenesis.MethodsUtilizing the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort, we employed multivariable-adjusted logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models to quantify CCR/WC-dyslipidemia associations. Nonlinear patterns were assessed using restricted cubic splines, and mediation analysis was conducted to evaluate the role of dyslipidemia in the relationship between CCR/WC and hypertension.ResultsAmong 7,117 participants, elevated CCR/WC demonstrated a 36% reduced dyslipidemia risk (fully adjusted OR = 0.64; 95%CI: 0.43–0.95). Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Dyslipidemia, characterized by abnormal lipid levels, is a primary modifiable risk factor for CVD [2, 3], contributing to endothelial dysfunction and plaque progression [4–6]. Furthermore, dyslipidemia promotes the development of hypertension through multiple mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, and increased arterial stiffness [7]. Understanding the interplay between metabolic markers, dyslipidemia, and hypertension is crucial for early prevention [8].

The creatinine-cystatin C-waist circumference ratio (CCR/WC) represents a novel composite biomarker that integrates indicators of renal function (serum creatinine and cystatin C) with a measure of abdominal adiposity (waist circumference) to assess metabolic health [9–11]. Metabolic syndrome (MetS), characterized by central obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, and insulin resistance, is a major driver of cardiovascular risk [12]. Waist circumference, a core component of MetS, reflects abdominal adiposity and visceral fat accumulation [13]. Serum creatinine is a byproduct of muscle metabolism [14], while cystatin C acts as a muscle-independent marker of renal function [15]. The creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio (CCR) is increasingly recognized as a surrogate index for skeletal muscle mass [16]. Therefore, the CCR/WC ratio integrates muscle mass status and central adiposity, effectively capturing the sarcopenic obesity phenotype often observed in MetS.

By integrating these biomarkers and clinical measures, the CCR/WC ratio serves as a comprehensive metabolic indicator reflecting both kidney function and abdominal fat accumulation [9, 11]. This composite metric may offer a more effective assessment of an individual’s metabolic health and associated risks than traditional single markers. Preliminary studies have demonstrated associations between CCR/WC and various conditions, including diabetes, hypertension, and chronic lung disease [9–11]. However, despite the theoretical basis for using CCR/WC as a predictor, research on its specific relationship with dyslipidemia remains limited. This gap in knowledge constrains our understanding of CCR/WC as a holistic predictor of metabolic health and its potential utility in clinical risk assessment.

Therefore, identifying effective predictive markers is crucial for the early detection and management of dyslipidemia and its associated risks. This study aims to systematically evaluate the association between the CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [17]. Based on existing literature suggesting a potential link between CCR/WC and metabolic diseases, we hypothesize that a higher CCR/WC ratio is significantly associated with a lower risk of dyslipidemia and may serve as an independent predictor of dyslipidemia risk. By investigating this relationship, we aim to provide new evidence to support the use of CCR/WC in clinical practice for assessing dyslipidemia risk.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective cohort study utilized data from CHARLS [17]. CHARLS is a nationally representative survey of Chinese residents aged 45 years and older, with biennial data collection since baseline (2011–2012). The study enrolled approximately 17,500 participants from 150 counties using multistage sampling to ensure national representativeness. Data collection encompassed demographics, health status, socioeconomic factors, and biomarkers including blood tests, anthropometric measurements, and physical performance assessments [18–20].

This study consisted of both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. The cross-sectional analysis assessed the association between the CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia using baseline data from 11,711 participants (2011–2012). Participants were excluded if they had missing data for age (n = 84), serum creatinine (n = 211), cystatin C (n = 2,757), waist circumference (n = 1,342), dyslipidemia status (n = 136), abnormal waist circumference values (n = 124), cancer history (n = 42), or abnormal CCR/WC ratios (n = 34), resulting in a final sample of 7,117 participants. Participants with a history of cancer were excluded to minimize the potential confounding effect of cancer-related cachexia on muscle mass and serum creatinine levels [21, 22]. Additionally, extreme outliers in waist circumference were excluded to reduce the impact of measurement errors or pathological fluid retention [23]. The longitudinal analysis examined the association between CCR/WC and incident dyslipidemia. Further exclusions for this analysis included participants lost to follow-up (2013–2018; n = 243), those with missing follow-up dyslipidemia data (n = 19), and those with baseline dyslipidemia (n = 661), resulting in 6,194 participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Review Committee at Peking University (IRB00001052-11014). All participants provided written informed consent.

Exposure and outcome definitions

Blood samples were collected after overnight fasting. Serum creatinine was measured using the compensated kinetic Jaffe method, and cystatin C was assessed using a particle-enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay. All laboratory measurements followed standardized protocols to ensure accuracy and consistency (https://charls.charlsdata.com).

Waist circumference was measured with participants standing upright using a flexible tape placed horizontally at the midpoint between the lowest rib and iliac crest during normal exhalation. The creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio (CCR) was calculated as serum creatinine (mg/dL) divided by cystatin C (mg/L), multiplied by 100. The CCR/WC ratio was calculated by dividing CCR by waist circumference (cm). Dyslipidemia was defined based on a physician’s diagnosis. Participants were identified as having dyslipidemia if they reported being diagnosed by a doctor (including conditions such as high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [≥ 160 mg/dL], triglycerides [≥ 200 mg/dL], total cholesterol [≥ 240 mg/dL], or low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [< 40 mg/dL]) or were taking lipid-lowering medication [24]. Hypertension was similarly defined by a physician’s diagnosis (systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg) or the current use of antihypertensive medication [25].

Covariate definitions

Covariates included demographic variables: age (years), sex, residence (urban/rural), and marital status (married/cohabitating vs. single). Education was categorized as less than lower secondary, upper secondary and vocational training, or tertiary. Lifestyle factors included smoking and alcohol consumption (both binary). Clinical measurements included fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, glycated hemoglobin, serum uric acid, serum creatinine, and cystatin C. Activities of daily living (ADL) impairment was assessed based on self-reported difficulty with essential tasks (dressing, bathing, eating, mobility, toileting, and bowel control) and coded as a binary variable. Medical history included self-reported diagnoses of hypertension, diabetes, lung disease, dyslipidemia, stroke, and kidney disease (all binary).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation, SD) and compared using t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests based on distribution. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests.

For the cross-sectional analysis, multivariable logistic regression models evaluated the associations between the CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated, adjusting for potential confounders selected a priori. Restricted cubic splines with 4 knots were used to assess potential nonlinear relationships between the CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia. To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses by (1) excluding participants with kidney disease, chronic lung disease, or asthma, and (2) replacing binary smoking and drinking variables with quantitative measures of consumption intensity. The association between CCR/WC and dyslipidemia was re-evaluated using the same logistic regression models. Detailed results are presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

For the cohort analysis, participants with baseline dyslipidemia were excluded. Kaplan-Meier curves assessed time to new-onset dyslipidemia across CCR/WC quartiles, with log-rank tests used to compare groups. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs, adjusting for confounders. Mediation analysis was used to explore whether dyslipidemia mediates the CCR/WC-hypertension relationship, using bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples for confidence intervals. Subgroup analyses were performed stratified by sex (male vs. female) to explore potential differences in the association between CCR/WC and dyslipidemia. The statistical significance of the interaction was evaluated by including a cross-product term (CCR/WC × sex) in the models.

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.3, with a 2-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 7,117 participants were included in this analysis, with a mean age of 59.96 ± 10.17 years (Table 1). The study population comprised 46.3% males and 53.7% females. At baseline, 65.3% of participants resided in rural areas, and 82.0% were married or cohabitating. Regarding lifestyle factors, 69.8% of participants were never smokers, while 30.2% were current smokers. For alcohol consumption, 61.6% reported never drinking alcohol, whereas 38.4% reported regular consumption. The mean waist circumference was 85.20 ± 10.22 cm.

The overall prevalence of dyslipidemia was 9.7%. Notably, a clear gradient was observed across CCR/WC quartiles, with the highest prevalence (12.5%) in Q1 and the lowest prevalence (8.1%) in Q4, suggesting a potential inverse association between the CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia prevalence.

Association between CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated a consistent inverse association between CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia risk across all analytical models (Table 2). In the crude model, each unit increase in CCR/WC ratio was associated with a 48% reduction in dyslipidemia risk (OR = 0.52; 95% CI, 0.37–0.74; P < 0.001). This association remained robust after adjustment for demographic factors in Model 1 (OR = 0.53; 95% CI, 0.36–0.77; P = 0.001). In the fully adjusted Model 2, which incorporated lifestyle factors, biochemical parameters, functional status, and comorbidities, the protective association persisted with some attenuation (OR = 0.64; 95% CI, 0.43–0.95; P = 0.030). The association between CCR/WC and dyslipidemia remained robust after adjusting for the quantitative intensity of smoking and alcohol consumption, indicating that the use of binary variables in the main analysis did not substantially bias the findings.

Dose-response relationship

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a significant overall inverse association between the CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia risk (P for overall association = 0.027) (Fig. 2). The relationship demonstrated a monotonic decrease in odds ratios with increasing CCR/WC values up to an inflection point, followed by a plateau at higher levels. Importantly, no significant evidence of nonlinearity was detected (P for nonlinearity = 0.104), supporting the appropriateness of linear modeling approaches.

Mediation Analysis Results

Mediation analysis elucidated the mechanistic pathway linking the CCR/WC ratio to hypertension through dyslipidemia (Table 4). The total effect of the CCR/WC ratio on hypertension risk was significant (β = −0.227; 95% CI, − 0.290 to − 0.169; P < 0.001). After accounting for dyslipidemia as a mediator, the direct effect remained substantial (β = −0.210; 95% CI, − 0.274 to − 0.154; P < 0.001), while the indirect effect through dyslipidemia was statistically significant (β = −0.017; 95% CI, − 0.033 to − 0.006; P = 0.014), indicating partial mediation.

Discussion

In this large population-based cohort study, a significant inverse association between the CCR/WC ratio and the risk of dyslipidemia was identified. Longitudinal analysis further confirmed that a higher baseline CCR/WC ratio predicts a lower risk of new-onset dyslipidemia, with a linear dose-response relationship observed. Subgroup analyses indicated that these protective associations were more pronounced in males. Furthermore, our mediation analysis revealed that dyslipidemia significantly mediates the pathway between CCR/WC and hypertension, highlighting the complex interplay between sarcopenic obesity, lipid metabolism, and blood pressure regulation. These findings indicate that the CCR/WC ratio holds significant clinical potential for the early identification and prevention of dyslipidemia.

To the best of our knowledge, this investigation represents the first comprehensive examination of the relationship between the CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia risk, potentially reflecting enhanced metabolic health through the preservation of muscle mass relative to central adiposity. While previous studies have explored CCR/WC in predicting various chronic diseases, its association with lipid metabolism disorders has remained largely unexplored. Chen et al. [9] demonstrated that elevated CCR/WC ratios predict reduced type 2 diabetes risk in Chinese populations, establishing its utility as an early marker for glucose metabolism dysfunction. Similarly, Li et al. [11] identified this ratio as a predictor of severe airflow limitation in older adults, highlighting its relevance in pulmonary function assessment. Yang et al. [10] further reported associations between CCR/WC and hypertension, suggesting broader cardiovascular risk implications. These convergent findings position CCR/WC as a versatile biomarker across multiple chronic disease domains. Our results extend this evidence base by demonstrating its predictive value for dyslipidemia, thereby contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of CCR/WC in metabolic health surveillance and disease prevention strategies.

Recent studies have highlighted the prognostic value of individual metabolic and anthropometric markers. For instance, recent research [26–28] has demonstrated the utility of novel biomarkers in predicting cardiovascular events. Similarly, other studies [29, 30] have discussed the independent roles of muscle mass and adiposity indices. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating that combining these metrics into the CCR/WC ratio provides an integrated view of metabolic risk, particularly relevant for the aging population where sarcopenia and central obesity often coexist. Furthermore, our comparative analysis confirmed that the CCR/WC ratio offers superior predictive value compared to its individual components, likely by capturing the balance between muscle quality and central obesity.

The restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a sophisticated dose-response relationship between CCR/WC and dyslipidemia risk, characterized by a significant overall inverse association, with a potential plateau at higher values. While increases in CCR/WC generally conferred protective benefits, the plateau and modest risk elevation at extreme values—though not statistically significant for nonlinearity—may indicate biological saturation points or compensatory mechanisms. This pattern suggests that optimal CCR/WC ranges may exist for maximal dyslipidemia protection, beyond which additional increases yield diminishing returns or potentially reflect underlying pathophysiological alterations. Such threshold effects are consistent with established concepts in metabolic regulation, where homeostatic mechanisms maintain optimal ranges for physiological parameters. Understanding these inflection points is crucial for developing evidence-based intervention targets and warrants mechanistic investigation into the biological pathways governing this relationship.

Our longitudinal analysis strengthens the evidence for a causal relationship between CCR/WC and dyslipidemia development, demonstrating clear dose-response patterns across quartiles with progressively lower risks at higher CCR/WC values. These findings align with emerging research emphasizing the critical role of muscle mass preservation and central adiposity reduction in metabolic health optimization [31, 32]. The observed sex-specific differences, with more pronounced protective effects in males, likely reflect fundamental differences in body composition, hormonal regulation, and adipose tissue distribution patterns that influence lipid metabolism. Men typically exhibit greater muscle mass and distinct central fat accumulation patterns compared to women [33, 34], potentially amplifying the discriminatory power of CCR/WC in this population. Although the interaction between sex and CCR/WC did not reach statistical significance, the magnitude of effect differences suggests clinically relevant heterogeneity that merits further investigation. These sex-specific patterns may inform personalized risk stratification approaches and highlight the need for population-specific validation studies to optimize CCR/WC utility across diverse demographic groups.

Our mediation analysis provides critical mechanistic insights into the pathways linking the CCR/WC ratio to hypertension risk. The significant indirect effect of dyslipidemia suggests that part of the protective effect of a higher CCR/WC ratio on hypertension is mediated through improvements in lipid metabolism. Dyslipidemia contributes to endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness through multiple pathways, including impaired nitric oxide bioavailability and increased oxidative stress, which are established precursors to hypertension development [35]. Elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and triglycerides directly compromise endothelial function, resulting in increased peripheral vascular resistance and elevated blood pressure [36]. By demonstrating that dyslipidemia partially mediates the effect of CCR/WC on hypertension risk, our study indicates that interventions targeting lipid abnormalities may help reduce hypertension risk associated with unfavorable CCR/WC ratios. Furthermore, the significant direct effect of CCR/WC on hypertension after adjusting for dyslipidemia suggests that other mechanisms may also be involved. These may include insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and oxidative stress—pathways that independently influence both metabolic health and blood pressure regulation [37–39]. The CCR/WC ratio, reflecting the balance between muscle mass and central adiposity, may modulate these mechanisms through distinct but interconnected pathways. Strategies aimed at increasing muscle mass, reducing central obesity, and improving lipid profiles may have synergistic effects in lowering hypertension risk [40].

The physiological mechanisms underlying the protective association between a higher CCR/WC ratio and dyslipidemia likely involve intricate crosstalk between skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. Skeletal muscle is not only the primary site for insulin-stimulated glucose disposal but also a key determinant of whole-body lipid oxidation. A higher CCR, indicative of preserved muscle mass, suggests enhanced metabolic flexibility and efficiency in fatty acid utilization [41, 42]. Muscle contraction releases myokines, such as irisin and interleukin-15, which have been shown to promote browning of white adipose tissue and improve lipid profiles [43]. Conversely, a lower waist circumference reflects reduced visceral adiposity. Visceral fat is metabolically active, secreting an excess of free fatty acids (FFAs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) directly into the portal circulation [44]. This influx of FFAs to the liver stimulates the synthesis of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and induces hepatic insulin resistance. Furthermore, insulin resistance decreases lipoprotein lipase (LPL) activity in skeletal muscle, impairing the clearance of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins. Therefore, a high CCR/WC ratio represents a phenotype with high muscle/low visceral fat, characterized by optimal insulin sensitivity, reduced systemic inflammation, and a favorable adipokine profile. However, these proposed mechanisms warrant further verification through experimental studies.

Our study’s principal strengths include its large-scale prospective cohort design, providing robust statistical power to detect clinically meaningful associations and establish temporal relationships between the CCR/WC ratio and cardiovascular outcomes. The comprehensive mediation analysis elucidates potential causal pathways, while extensive covariate adjustment enhances the validity of our findings. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational design precludes definitive causal inference, necessitating validation through randomized controlled trials. Second, potential residual confounding from unmeasured variables—including detailed dietary patterns [45], genetic polymorphisms [46], and medication use—may influence the observed associations. Third, the study population’s demographic characteristics may limit generalizability to diverse ethnic groups and geographic regions. Finally, reliance on baseline measurements without accounting for temporal changes in body composition and metabolic parameters may not capture the dynamic nature of cardiovascular risk evolution [47]. Future research should incorporate repeated measurements to assess the impact of changes in the CCR/WC ratio over time, investigate the role of genetic factors in modifying these associations, and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions aimed at optimizing body composition for cardiovascular health.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the CCR/WC ratio represents a composite biomarker that integrates skeletal muscle mass and central adiposity to effectively predict the risk of dyslipidemia in middle-aged and older adults. Our findings demonstrate that a higher CCR/WC ratio is significantly associated with reduced dyslipidemia risk and partially mediates the relationship with hypertension. Given its non-invasive nature and cost-effectiveness, CCR/WC holds significant clinical utility for early risk stratification and the identification of high-risk phenotypes, such as sarcopenic obesity. Routine monitoring of this ratio could facilitate timely, targeted interventions aimed at optimizing body composition to prevent dyslipidemia and its cardiovascular sequelae. Future research is warranted to validate these findings across diverse populations and further elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
