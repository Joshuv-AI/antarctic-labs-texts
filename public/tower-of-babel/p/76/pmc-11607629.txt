Cysteine‐rich 61 inhibition attenuates hepatic insulin resistance and improves lipid metabolism in high‐fat diet fed mice and HepG2 cells
Authors: Yu Jung Heo, Jieun Park, Nami Lee, Sung‐E Choi, Ja Young Jeon, Seung Jin Han, Dae Jung Kim, Kwan Woo Lee, Hae Jin Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is strongly associated with insulin resistance development. Hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation are considered the main drivers of hepatic insulin resistance in MASLD. Cysteine‐rich 61 (Cyr61 also called CCN1), a novel secretory matricellular protein, is implicated in liver inflammation, and its role in MASLD is not clearly understood. Therefore, we investigated the role of Cyr61 in hepatic insulin resistance and lipid metabolism as major factors in MASLD pathogenesis. In high‐fat diet (HFD)‐fed C57BL/6J mice, Cyr61 was downregulated or upregulated via viral transduction. Measurements of glucose homeostasis, histological assessment of liver tissues, and gene expression and signaling pathways of lipogenesis, fatty acid oxidation, and inflammation were performed using liver samples from these mice. Cyr61 levels in HepG2 cells were reduced using RNAi‐mediated gene knockdown. Inflammation and insulin resistance were evaluated using real‐time polymerase chain reaction and western blotting. HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mice exhibited enhanced glucose tolerance via the protein kinase B pathway, reduced hepatic inflammation, decreased lipogenesis, and increased fatty acid oxidation. Notably, HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mice showed elevated protein expression of sirtuin 6 and phosphorylated‐AMP‐activated protein kinase. In vitro experiments demonstrated that inhibition of Cyr61 downregulated pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‐1 beta, IL‐6, and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha via the nuclear factor kappa B/c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase pathway, and alleviated insulin resistance. Cyr61 affected hepatic inflammation, lipid metabolism, and insulin resistance. Inhibition of Cyr61 reduced inflammation, recovered insulin resistance, and altered lipid metabolism in vivo and in vitro. Therefore, Cyr61 is a potential therapeutic target in MASLD.

INTRODUCTION

Obesity is a chronic disease and represents the cause of several metabolic diseases, leading to various complications such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). 1 , 2 MASLD can progress to more severe diseases, such as metabolic‐dysfunction‐associated steatohepatitis (MASH) and cancer. 3 Several reports have demonstrated that MASLD is associated with increased secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, infiltration of immune cells, and tissue dysfunction related to glucose homeostasis. 4 , 5 , 6 Additionally, lipid metabolism dysfunction in MASLD pathogenesis can impair insulin signaling. 7 Circulating free fatty acids (FFAs) can impact insulin‐target tissues by activating inflammatory signals via pattern recognition receptors on cell surfaces. 6 A tightly regulated balance between fatty acid intake, synthesis, and oxidation is critical for maintaining normal lipid metabolism, with prolonged disruption of this balance resulting in lipid accumulation and insulin resistance. 8

Insulin target tissues, including the liver, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscles, play crucial roles in glucose uptake, production, and processing. 9 Therefore, these tissues are key targets in the progression of obesity and insulin resistance. The liver plays an important role in regulating lipid and glucose homeostasis. 10 Lipid accumulation in the liver is strongly linked to the development of hepatic insulin resistance, leading to aberrant glucose and lipid metabolism. 10 The AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) and NAD+‐dependent sirtuins (Sirt 1 and Sirt6) have emerged as key factors for sensing energy status and regulating glucose and lipid metabolism. 11 Hepatic Sirt6 deficiency induces increased glycolysis, elevated uptake of long‐chain fatty acid, reduced fatty acid β‐oxidation, and increased triglyceride (TG) synthesis and lipogenesis, eventually. 12

Cysteine‐rich 61 (Cyr61), also known as CCN1, is a cysteine‐rich protein that belongs to the CCN family. 13 Members of the CCN family include Cyr61, connective tissue growth factor, nephroblastoma overexpressed, Wnt‐inducible signaling pathway protein‐1 (WISP‐1), WISP‐2, and WISP‐3. 14 Cyr61 plays a crucial role in connecting the cell surface with the extracellular matrix and is involved in various physiological and pathological processes, including cell adhesion, proliferation, migration, differentiation, and angiogenesis, during normal development and pathophysiology. 13 Cyr61 is involved in the development of various diseases, including inflammation, fibrosis, carcinoma, and autoimmune diseases, via autocrine or paracrine mechanisms. 15 , 16 , 17 Recently, it has been reported that Cyr61 is induced by FFAs in hepatocytes, leading to macrophage infiltration and hepatic inflammation in a MASH model. 18 Another study reported that loss of Cyr61 alleviates MASH in mice induced by low‐dose carbon tetrachloride injections along with a western diet. 19 However, the effects of Cyr61 expression on hepatic insulin resistance and lipid metabolism in MASLD remain unknown.

The present study investigated the role of Cyr61 in hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin resistance and evaluated the related molecular mechanisms using HepG2 cells in vitro and high‐fat diet (HFD)‐fed mice in vivo. Our data suggest that the knockdown of Cyr61 in HFD‐fed mice improved insulin resistance and regulated the expression of lipid metabolism‐related genes in the liver. Furthermore, inhibition of Cyr61 expression in HepG2 cells prevented palmitic acid (PA)‐induced insulin resistance and reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines.

Animal studies

Male C57BL/6J mice (age, 8 weeks) were purchased from Guangdong GemPharmatech (Guangzhou, China). The mice were housed in a temperature‐controlled room at 22 ± 2°C with a 12‐h light/dark cycle and fed ad libitum. After 1 week of adaptation, the diets provided included the HFD comprising 60% fat (D12492, Research Diet, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) and a normal chow containing 10% fat (D12450B, Research Diet) for 18 weeks. To study the effect of Cyr61 knockdown, AAV‐shCyr61 was intravenously injected into male C57BL/6J mice fed with a control diet (CD) or HFD (2 × 1011 active virus particles in 200 μL saline/mouse) 6 weeks before euthanization. Mice were categorized into the following groups: (1) AAV8‐shCyr61‐injected, HFD group (HFD/AAV‐shCyr61; n = 7) and (2) AAV8‐shGFP‐injected, HFD group (HFD/AAV‐shGFP; n = 7). To study the effect of Cyr61 overexpression, hepatocyte‐specific overexpression was implemented using the AAV serotype 8 and the thyroxine‐binding globulin (TBG) promoter in C57BL/6J mice. The mice were randomly assigned into the following two groups: (1) AAV‐TBG‐GFP‐injected, CD group (CD/AAV‐GFP; n = 5) and (2) AAV‐TBG‐Cyr61‐injected, CD group (CD/AAV‐Cyr61; n = 5). Mice were administered AAVs (AAV‐GFP or AAV‐Cyr61) via intravenous injection (2 × 1011 active viral particles in 200 μL saline/mouse) 6 weeks before euthanization. All experiments and animal care were conducted according to the Ajou Institutional Animal Care guidelines and were approved by the Ajou Institutional Animal Care Committee (Approval Number: 2022–0069).

Construction of a hepatocyte‐specific Cyr61‐expressing AAV

To overexpress Cyr61 in liver hepatocytes, a hepatocyte‐specific expression vector system using a TBG promoter was used. 20 Plasmid vector pAAV[Exp]‐TBG > mCcn1 [NM_010516.2] was constructed, and CCN1‐expressing AAVs (AAV‐CCN1) were then produced using the AAV8 packaging system (VectorBuilder, Chicago, IL, USA).

Construction of a shCyr61‐expressing AAV (AAV‐shCyr61)

To knockdown Cyr61 in the liver, a plasmid vector pAAV[shRNA]‐EGFP‐U6 > mCyr61[shRNA#1] (VB230308‐1516gha) was initially constructed, and Cyr61‐knockdown AAVs (AAV‐shCyr61) were produced using the AAV8 packaging system (VectorBuilder). The target sequences of shCyr61 were 5′‐TTGTGATGAAGACAGCATTAA‐3′ and located at 388 to 435 of the mouse Cyr61 gene (VB230308‐1516gha).

Preparation of PA

PA conjugates were prepared by soaping fatty acid with sodium hydroxide followed by mixing with BSA. PA (20 mM, each in 0.01 M NaOH) was separately incubated at 70°C for 30 min and formed a complex with 5% BSA in phosphate‐buffered saline at a 1:3 volumetric ratio. Differentiated HepG2 cells were treated with BSA/PA conjugates consisting of 5% BSA and 5 mM PA at the indicated concentrations.

Cell culture

HepG2 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained in high‐glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Welgene, Daegu, Korea) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum and antibiotics (10 μg/mL streptomycin and 100 IU/mL penicillin) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2 (v/v). For subsequent experiments, the cells were seeded at a cell density of 1 × 105 cells per well in a six‐well plate. To block Cyr61 activity, Cyr61 neutralization antibodies (1:1000, #NB100‐356; Novus Biologicals, Centennial, CO, USA) were added to the culture medium.

H&E staining

Mouse livers were excised, fixed in 4% formalin and paraffin, sliced into 5‐μm‐thick sections, mounted onto slides, and sequentially stained in hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) solution (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) according to standard procedures.

Oil red O (ORO) staining

The presence of lipid droplets in liver tissues was determined by ORO staining. Liver tissues frozen in Optimum Cutting Temperature compound were cut into five‐micrometer thick sections, mounted onto slides, fixed with 10% formalin for 30 min, soaked in 60% isopropanol solution for 5 min, and then stained in ORO solution (0.5% in 60% isopropanol solution) for 10 min. After rinsing the tissue sections three times in water, the nuclei were stained by soaking the tissue slides in hematoxylin solution for 1 min. Red oil drops were observed under a microscope using Aperio ScanScope CS Slide Scanner (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

Tissue sections were incubated with a primary antibody against Cyr61 (dilution 1:25) in a moisturized chamber at 4°C overnight. The membranes were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated anti‐rabbit secondary antibody (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). The brown color was developed using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine as the HRP substrate, which was supplied by the Vector DAB Peroxidase Substrate Kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Nuclei were stained by incubating the sections in hematoxylin solution (Abcam) for 1 min. Brown macrophages with small violet nuclei were detected using an Aperio ScanScope CS Slide Scanner.

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay

Cyr61 concentrations in mouse plasma were quantified using the mouse cysteine‐rich protein 61 (Cyr61 / CCN1) enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The interleukin (IL)‐6, tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), and IL‐1β concentrations in culture supernatants were measured using human IL‐6 DuoSet ELISA (DY206‐05), human IL‐1 beta/IL‐1F2 DuoSet ELISA (DY201‐05), and human TNF‐alpha DuoSet ELISA (DY210‐05) kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Western blot analysis

Radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (pancreatic extract, pronase, thermolysin, chymotrypsin, and papain) (Roche Applied Science, Mannheim, Germany) was used to extract cellular proteins. Equivalent amounts of protein (40 μg) were separated on 4–15% (w/v) polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, the target antigens were reacted with primary antibodies, followed by the addition of secondary antibodies (HRP‐conjugated anti‐goat IgG or anti‐rabbit IgG). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech, Piscataway, NJ, USA).

Real‐time quantitative reverse transcription‐PCR (RT‐PCR)

Semi‐quantitative mRNA expression levels were determined using the Takara PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit, TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II qPCR Kit, and a TP850 real‐time PCR instrument (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). Briefly, total RNA samples were extracted using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA were synthesized using PrimeScript RTase and random nonamers and then used as templates for PCR amplification. Real‐time PCR was performed using the primers listed in Table 2. The relative quantities of the amplified DNA were analyzed using software included with the TP850 instrument (Takara). Gene expression levels were normalized to the levels of acidic ribosomal phosphoprotein P0.

Transfection of small‐interfering RNAs (siRNAs)

siRNA duplexes were designed and synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea). The sequences were as follows: GFP (GenBank: GU983383), sense‐GUUCAGCGUGUCCGGCGA, antisense‐CUCGGCGGA CACGCUG AAC; human Cyr61 (GenBank:NM_001554.4), sense‐CAGUGUACAGCAGCCUGAA, and antisense‐UUCA GGCUGCUGUACACUG. HepG2 cells were transfected with siRNA duplexes using the Lipofectamine® 2000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfected HepG2 cells were seeded onto growth plates and incubated for 3 days, followed by treatment with 200 or 400 μM PA (or no treatment) for 12 h.

Glucose and insulin tolerance test

Mice were first subjected to 6 h of fasting and injected intraperitoneally with glucose (1 g/kg) or insulin (0.75 U/kg) for the glucose tolerance test (GTT) or insulin tolerance test (ITT), respectively. Blood was collected from the tail at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after glucose infusion or at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after insulin injection. Blood glucose levels (mg/dL) were then measured using Accuchek (Korea Roche Diagnostics, Seoul, Korea).

Measurement of TG levels

Tissue or cellular TG was extracted using the Folch extraction method (J Biol Chem 226, 497 509. 1957). Total TG was determined using the EZ‐Triglyceride Quantification Assay Kit (DOGEN, Seoul, Korea) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). The TG content was calculated using a standard curve.

Plasma biochemistry

Blood obtained from mouse heart tissues was immediately centrifuged at 1500 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Plasma was collected and stored at −80°C. Biochemical parameters including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), TG, and FFA levels were analyzed at the central laboratory (SCL Healthcare, Seoul, Korea).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using PRISM9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. Differences between two groups were compared using Student's t test. Differences between multiple groups were compared using the one‐way or two‐way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test for comparison between two groups. A p‐value of <.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Cyr61 expression was increased in the liver of HFD‐fed mice

Cyr61 is known to be involved in inflammation and fibrosis through autocrine, endocrine, and paracrine mechanisms and is particularly secreted by hepatocytes in liver diseases. 19 To determine whether Cyr61 is potentially involved in the pathogenesis of MASLD, we used a mouse model fed with a high‐fat diet for 18 weeks. IHC of the livers of mice that were fed HFD showed increased Cyr61 expression (Figure 1A). Moreover, we detected the Cyr61 expression levels in the livers of HFD‐fed mice compared with those in CD‐fed mice using RT‐PCR (Figure 1B) and western blotting (Figure 1C and Figure S1). The mRNA and protein levels of Cyr61 were significantly higher in the HFD‐fed mice than in the CD‐fed mice. Furthermore, Cyr61 expression levels in the plasma were higher in HFD‐fed mice than in CD‐fed mice (Figure 1D).

Cyr61 overexpression (AAV‐Cyr61) induced hepatic steatosis and inflammation

Hepatocyte‐specific Cyr61‐expressing adeno‐associated serotype 8 viruses (AAV8‐TBG‐Cyr61; AAV‐Cyr61) were intravenously injected into CD‐fed mice (Figure 2A). Cyr61 overexpression was confirmed by RT‐PCR (Figure S3C) and IHC for Cyr61 (Figure 2B). AAV‐Cyr61 mice showed increased lipid accumulation (Figure 2B) and elevated TG levels in the liver (Figure 2C). Cyr61 overexpression increased the expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL‐6 and monocyte chemoattractant protein‐1 (MCP‐1; Figure 2D). AAV‐Cyr61 mice showed worse liver dysfunction with higher ALT and AST levels and higher TG, FFA, and Cyr61 levels in the plasma than those in AAV‐GFP mice (Figure 2E and Figure S2A). Cyr61 overexpression had no effect on body weight and liver weight (Figure S3A, B), but negatively affected glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity based on GTT and ITT results (Figure 3A,B). Additionally, the expression of genes involved in lipid synthesis such as fatty acid synthase (FASN), stearyl CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1), and peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐gamma (PPARγ) was higher in the liver of AAV‐Cyr61 mice than that in AAV‐GFP mice (Figure 3C). The levels of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor‐gamma coactivator‐1 alpha (PGC‐1α), peroxisome proliferator activated receptor‐alpha (PPARα), and fatty acid oxidation‐related genes were reduced in the liver of AAV‐Cyr61 mice compared to those in AAV‐GFP mice (Figure 3D). Notably, the gene expression levels of Sirt1, Sirt5, Sirt6, and Sirt7 were reduced in the liver of AAV‐Cyr61 mice (Figure 3E).

Cyr61 knockdown reduced hepatic steatosis and inflammation

Studies have reported that sustained HFD intake induces prominent lipid accumulation in the liver. 19 To determine whether silencing of Cyr61 improves liver injury, shCyr61‐expressing AAVs (AAV‐shCyr61) were injected into HFD‐fed C57BL/6J mice (Figure 4A). Cyr61 knockdown was confirmed using real‐time PCR (Figure 4B). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed the presence of lipid droplets, and ORO staining clearly demonstrated large red‐colored lipid droplets in liver tissues of HFD/GFP mice. HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mice showed reduced hepatic lipid accumulation in histological analysis (Figure 4C), and they contained lower liver TG levels than those in HFD/GFP mice (Figure 4D). HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mice showed recovery of liver function with lower plasma ALT, AST, TG, and Cyr61 levels than those in HFD/GFP mice (Figure 4E and Figure S2B). Moreover, Cyr61 knockdown decreased IL‐6 expression in the liver (Figure 4F).

Cyr61 knockdown alleviated HFD‐induced insulin resistance and altered lipid metabolism in the liver

Insulin resistance is closely associated with lipid metabolism. 8 HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 treatment had no effect on body weight and liver weight (Figure S4A,B) but alleviated glucose intolerance and insulin resistance based on GTT and ITT results (Figure 5A,B). In conjunction with lipid accumulation in the liver of HFD‐fed mice, the expression of genes involved in lipid synthesis, such as sterol regulatory element‐binding transcription factor (SREBF), FASN, diacylglycerol O‐acyltransferase 1 (DGAT1), SCD1, PPARγ, and acetyl‐CoA carboxylase (ACC) was increased in the liver of the HFD/GFP group. HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 treatment significantly prevented the upregulation of lipid synthesis genes in HFD‐fed mice (Figure 5C). The mRNA levels of key regulators of fatty acid oxidation, such as PPAR α, enoyl‐CoA hydratase and 3‐hydroxyacyl CoA dehydrogenase (EHHADH), acyl‐coenzyme A oxidase 1 (ACOX1), and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) were significantly increased in HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mice compared to those in HFD/GFP mice (Figure 5D). Notably, HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mice recovered hepatic Sirt6 expression (Figure S5) and showed increased protein levels of Sirt6 and phosphorylated (p)‐AMPK compared to those in HFD/GFP mice (Figure 5E). Insulin signaling was enhanced in HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mouse livers, as reflected by the increased IRS1 and AKT phosphorylation levels compared with those in HFD/GFP mice (Figure 5F). These results indicated that Cyr61 regulates hepatic lipid metabolism and insulin resistance.

Inhibition of Cyr61 improved PA‐induced insulin resistance and inflammation in HepG2 cells

We examined the effects of PA on the expression of Cyr61 in HepG2 cells. Cells were treated with PA concentrations beginning at 200 μM, and the cells showed upregulation of Cyr61 with increasing PA concentrations compared to that in control BSA‐treated cells (Figure 6A). Furthermore, Cyr61 production was increased by PA treatment (Figure 6B). The expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α were increased by PA treatment (Figure 6C). PA treatment increased the expression of stress and inflammatory markers, such as p‐ JNK and p‐NF‐κB, in HepG2 cells (Figure 6D). Furthermore, the levels of various insulin signaling molecules, including p‐AKT and p‐GSK3 α/β were reduced by PA treatment (Figure 6E). Taken together, these results suggest that PA treatment increase insulin resistance and inflammation. Next, we evaluated whether Cyr61 was also involved in insulin resistance and inflammation in PA‐stimulated HepG2 cells using Cyr61 siRNAs. Inhibition of Cyr61 expression was confirmed by real‐time PCR (Figure 7A). The PA‐induced increases in the expression levels of IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α were significantly reduced upon transfection of Cyr61 siRNA (Figure 7B). Inhibition of Cyr61 significantly rescued the expression of lipid synthesis‐related genes such as PPARγ, SREBF, and FASN (Figure 7C). PA treatment increased the expression levels of fatty acid oxidation‐related genes, PPARδ, PPARα, acyl‐CoA dehydrogenase very long chain (ACADVL), and CPT1, which were unaffected by siCyr61 transfection (Figure 7C). We also investigated changes in expression of inflammation and insulin signaling proteins via western blotting. The p‐JNK and p‐NF‐κB levels decreased after siCyr61 treatment (Figure 7D). After insulin stimulation, the levels of p‐AKT and p‐GSK3α/β were significantly increased in siCyr61‐transfected cells compared to those in siGFP‐transfected cells (Figure 7E). Next, we confirmed changes in inflammation and insulin signaling proteins in PA‐stimulated HepG2 cells using a neutralizing antibody of Cyr61. The PA‐induced increases in the levels of IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α were reduced by treatment with a neutralizing antibody of Cyr61 (Figure S6A). Neutralization of Cyr61 decreased NF‐κB and JNK phosphorylation, which was increased by PA treatment (Figure S6B). Neutralization of Cyr61 restored the diminished expression of p‐AKT and p‐GSK‐3α/β (Figure S6C). DISCUSSION

Obesity and excessive systemic lipid levels are strongly associated with the development of insulin resistance. 2 The liver, a target tissue of insulin, plays a crucial role in the development of insulin resistance. 6 Impaired fatty acid oxidation and increased lipid content in the liver lead to accumulation of intracellular fat and chronic inflammation. 21 , 22 In the present study, we investigated the effects of Cyr61 on insulin resistance and lipid metabolism in HFD‐induced MASLD mouse models and HepG2 cells. The data showed that the overexpression of Cyr61 in vivo had negative effects on glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity. Additionally, treatment with AAV‐shCyr61 increased Sirt6 protein expression, downregulated lipogenic genes, and upregulated fatty acid oxidation genes in HFD‐fed mice. Moreover, HFD/AAV‐shCyr61 mice showed reduced inflammation and restored insulin resistance. Similarly, in vitro, inhibition of Cyr61 in PA‐treated HepG2 cells prevented insulin resistance, reduced inflammatory signal production, and affected lipid metabolism. These findings suggest that Cyr61 may enhance insulin resistance and inflammation induced by lipotoxicity in the liver.

Cyr61 has been implicated in key biological processes such as bone development, wound healing, fibrosis, and cancer. 13 Further, Cyr61 functions as an inflammatory mediator in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, lungs, colon, and liver. 15 , 16 , 23 , 24 Cyr61 induces IL‐6 production by fibroblast‐like synoviocytes, thereby promoting Th17 differentiation in rheumatoid arthritis. 15 In the colonic mucosa of patients with Crohn's disease, Cyr61 expression increases in inflamed mucosa, and this is associated with increased expression of inflammatory genes, including IL‐6 and Toll‐like receptor 4. 25 Cyr61 induces severe hepatic inflammation and apoptosis in HFD‐fed mice. 26 Further, Cyr61 overexpression in the liver of methionine choline‐deficient mice, a model for MASH, significantly upregulates fatty acid metabolism‐associated genes, as demonstrated by qPCR‐based array analysis. 18 However, another study reported that high concentrations of Cyr61 can be beneficial in effectively alleviating or resolving liver fibrosis by inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress and promoting apoptosis in hepatic stellate cells. 27 Here, we report for the first time the role and signaling pathway of Cyr61 in insulin resistance and lipid metabolism in MASLD mice and PA‐treated HepG2 cells.

In obesity‐related liver disease, pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α can activate intracellular signaling molecules, including JNK and AKT. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 These signaling molecules play pivotal roles in inflammatory signaling and can detrimentally impact insulin action. 9 Our results showed that Cyr61 specifically increased the expression of IL‐6. Chronic exposure to IL‐6 inhibits insulin signal transduction in both HepG2 cells and primary mouse hepatocytes. 32 The effect of IL‐6 is characterized by the inhibition of insulin‐dependent IRS‐1 tyrosine phosphorylation, the association with the p85 subunit of phosphoinositide‐3‐kinase, and the suppression of insulin‐induced AKT activation in HepG2 cells. In primary hepatocytes, IL‐6 also inhibits AKT activation in response to insulin and hinders a crucial physiological response to insulin and glycogen syntheses. 33 Wallenius et al 34 observed that IL‐6−/− mice develop maturity‐onset obesity and decreased glucose tolerance when compared to lean wild‐type controls. Our results showed that inhibition of Cyr61 in mice suppressed the expression of Il6 and improved insulin resistance via IRS tyrosine phosphorylation and AKT signaling. Additionally, inhibition of Cyr61 in cells alleviated PA‐induced hepatic inflammation and restore insulin resistance. These results suggest that Cyr61 plays a role in hepatic inflammation and insulin resistance.

Liver‐specific Cyr61 knockdown significantly decreases lipid metabolism and increases fatty acid oxidation in vivo. Recently, it was reported that Sirt6 is an important regulatory protein that inhibits Hippo downstream effectors including YAP, TAZ, and TEAD. 35 Sirt6, an NAD + ‐dependent deacetylase/deacylase, plays a crucial role in the maintenance of hepatic function and health by regulating glucose and lipid metabolism and protecting against the development of steatosis and inflammation. 36 Cyr61 is a known target gene in the Hippo pathway, 37 and its expression is increased in Sirt6‐deficient hepatic stellate cells. 35 Previously, we demonstrated that Cyr61 expression increases via the Hippo pathway in choline‐deficient and amino acid‐defined high‐fat diet‐induced liver fibrosis in vivo and hepatic stellate cells in vitro. 38 Although the mechanisms of obesity‐related lipid metabolism and insulin resistance may vary depending on tissue type and condition, our data show that Cyr61 knockdown increases Sirt6 and AMPK protein expression, which was reduced in HFD‐fed mice.

In conclusion, our study showed that Cyr61 knockdown in HFD‐fed mice ameliorated insulin resistance and improved lipid metabolism. Similarly, inhibition of Cyr61 in PA‐stimulated HepG2 cells prevented insulin resistance and attenuated inflammatory signaling and cytokine production. Our study showed that Cyr61 is a promising therapeutic target for MASLD.
