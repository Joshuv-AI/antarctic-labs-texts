Clinical and radiographic changes following transcrestal sinus augmentation: A scoping review of the last 25 years
Authors: Ann M Decker, Sandra Stuhr, Tiziano Testori, Hom‐Lay Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMaxillary sinus floor elevation is a surgical procedure intended to increase the volume of the bone vertically to accommodate dental implant placement. This intervention is frequently required for implant installation in the posterior maxilla, where the bone may be insufficient for securing implants of necessary length and stability. Sinus floor elevation can be completed either through a direct approach with a “window” through the lateral wall of the alveolar ridge or an indirect “transcrestal/transalveolar” sinus floor elevation (TSFE), which accesses the sinus floor through the crest of the edentulous ridge. Our study aims to provide a comprehensive scoping review of research conducted over the past 25 years on TSFE, specifically.MethodsA literature search aimed at identifying pertinent literature for the purpose of this PRISMA‐ScR‐compliant scoping review was conducted. Only randomized controlled trials, non‐randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, and case series that met the eligibility criteria were selected. Relevant data from these studies were extracted. Primary outcome measures included radiographic bone levels and implant failure >5 years. Secondary outcome measures included implant stability at time of placement and complications. Interventions reported in the selected studies were grouped based on treatment modality, which were then compared with the control therapy (traditional osteotome technique) after a minimum of 12 months healing period.ResultsOur search yielded 633 records, and after deduplication, 574 of these were screened. Application of the eligibility criteria led to the inclusion of 37 articles in the final selection. Case selection for included studies enrolling subjects: Four different transcrestal sinus elevation treatment modalities were identified: (a) osteotome, (b) piezoelectric surgery, (c) osseodensification, and (d) hydraulic techniques. Due to the heterogeneity of the studies, no superior approach for TSFE could be identified. Overall, all techniques demonstrated high implant survival rates.ConclusionComprehensive understanding of the patient's medical history, available armamentarium, and post‐operative complications/management strategies are all essential to the completion of a successful TSFE approach for implant placement in the posterior maxilla, regardless of the treatment modality used.Keywords: alveolar bone loss, bone regeneration, dental implants, scoping review, sinus floor augmentation

INTRODUCTION

Tooth loss is a palpable public health issue on the global stage. In fact, a recent National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey reported that seniors (aged 65 years and older) have an average of 20.7 remaining teeth or an average loss of over 12 teeth. 1 Tooth loss can impact a patient's quality of life in various ways, encompassing functional and psychological aspects such as physical and mental health and social interactions. As such, restoring lost dentition is a priority for individuals of all ages, as it is crucial for maintaining overall well‐being and functionality.

The biological mechanisms that are involved in wound healing following tooth loss involves alveolar resorption and sinus pneumatization, which can significantly impact restorative options. 2 However, with the advent of sinus elevation procedures, oral rehabilitation via implant placement has become a reliable answer for edentulous individuals. Elevation of the sinus floor was first reported by Boyne in the 1960s. 3 In 1980, Boyne and James described maxillary sinus elevation using autogenous particulate iliac bone via a lateral window approach. 3 Summers expanded elevation techniques to a minimally invasive transcrestal sinus floor elevation (TSFE) approach in 1994. 4

Since the introduction of Summers' osteotome approach to TSFE, there has been a robust expansion of armamentarium and approaches including osteotomes, piezoelectrics, osseodensification, and hydraulic approaches. Further diversity within these approaches includes with or without space maintenance options, graft material selection, and implant type. The current research body of evidence demonstrates that with the proper clinical preparation, TSFE is safe, with high survival implant rates with residual bone height of >4 mm. 5 In tribute to the 25th anniversary of CIDRR, this article provides a scoping review of the past 25 years in TSFE.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This scoping review fully adhered to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for systematic reviews and meta‐analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR, Supplemental File 1). 6

Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcomes (PICO) question

In adult patients undergoing TSFE procedures, what is the impact of various surgical techniques on clinical and radiographic outcomes compared to standard methods?

P: Adult human subjects undergoing TSFE and/or augmentation procedures.

I: TSFE is considered a minimally invasive technique aimed at elevating the sinus floor by creating a controlled fracture. This is accomplished using mechanical methods, like osteotomes or drills, to gain access to the sinus cavity and facilitate the placement of implants. Various TSFE techniques have been proposed and may include the incorporation of osteotomes, burs, piezoelectric, hydraulics, or a combination of approaches. This may or may not also include augmentation with bone grafting material, collagen matrices, and/or biologics.

C: The comparison was done to compare TSFE to a standard technique sinus floor elevation technique.

O: The outcomes measure the effectiveness of each technique and involve assessing the residual bone height, the immediate increase in bone height post‐procedure, and the final bone height achieved after the healing period.

Clinical Outcomes: Primary clinical outcomes include the success rate of dental implants and the incidence of sinus membrane perforation during the procedure. Secondary outcomes included implant stability.

Article eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria included peer‐reviewed randomized controlled trials (RCT), non‐RCT, cohort studies, and case series that enrolled adult patients receiving TSFE for implant placement. For a series of studies investigating the same cohort in different follow‐up periods, only the study with the longest follow‐up time point was included. Included studies must have included at least a 1‐year follow‐up. Furthermore, included studies must have reported at least one of the outcomes of interest required by this study (i.e. clinical and radiographic). Only studies published in English were included.

In vitro studies, animal studies, commentaries, narrative reviews, unpublished data, expert opinions, and case reports were excluded.

Information sources and search protocol

Three electronic databases were searched for articles relevant in the context of this scoping review: National Library of Medicine (MEDLINE‐PubMed), Cochrane Library, and EMBASE.

Studies published between January 1, 1999 and January 1, 2024 were included. Comprehensive search strategies were designed for each database, based on the use of MeSH terms, keywords, and free terms structured as depicted in Table S2. The last electronic search was conducted on February 27, 2024. Details on search terms are included in Supplemental File 1.

Article selection

Two reviewers (A.D. and S.S.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of the entries yielded from the initial electronic database search using software that randomized and blinded reviewers (Covidence Systematic Review Software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia). Following the initial screening, a full‐text review was completed by the same reviewers to make the final selection of articles to be included in the review. This final selection was based on the pre‐established inclusion and exclusion criteria described previously. Any disagreement in the final selection was resolved by open discussion between both reviewers. In the case that no agreement could be reached, another co‐author (H.‐L.W.) acted as an arbiter.

Data extraction

Data from the studies included in the final selection were extracted by two authors (A.D. and S.S.) and verified with a subsequent review of each article.

Apart from the previously mentioned clinical and radiograph outcome measures, additional extracted data included year of publication and first author, study design, final number of implants included in the statistical analyses, follow‐up period, number of males and females included, technique utilized for TSFE, smoking and/or diabetes inclusion, and whether or not bone grafting material and/or other biologics were used. If studies reported two measurements per site (e.g. mesial and distal radiographic bone measurements), the average of both measurements were calculated and reported in the table.

DISCUSSION

Regardless of the approach, TSFE is a safe and predictable procedure when the necessary anatomical and biological considerations are appropriately assessed. 44 , 45 , 46 Generally, TSFE uses an armamentarium to access the sinus through a crestal aspect/portal window. The tools used in each technique differ by (1) portal/window fabrication and (2) sinus lifting. Fabrication of the portal window from the crestal aspect can be completed with specialty instruments that are cutting (i.e. rotary diamond/carbide, trephine, piezoelectric) or non‐cutting (i.e. osseodensification/osteotome). Then, sinus elevation portion of the protocol can also be completed using diverse armamentarium including hand instruments, piezoelectric tips, or balloon inflation.

Osteotome group

One of the most commonly used TSFE method is the Summers' osteotomy technique, whereby a series of conical osteotomes are used to (1) increase the density of the maxillary soft bone (type III and IV) through lateral condensation resulting in bone compression stiffness for implant security and (2) to elevate the sinus to the proper height avoiding drills altogether (Figure 2A). 4 The osteotome is a specially designed surgical instrument that can vary in design at the tip of the instrument (i.e. flat or concave). 4 In this approach, the sequential use of osteotomes with increasing diameters is conducted to incrementally lift the sinus membrane. This technique relies significantly on the clinician's ability to use tactile sensation transmitted through the osteotomes to avoid perforation of the membrane. 4

(A) Osteotome technique using the Summer's approach. (a) Round burs can be used to initiate the osteotomy followed by a drill series until 1–2 mm of bone remains adjacent to the sinus. (b) Osteotome is used to create separation of the final 1–2 mm of bone. (c) That remaining 1–2 mm of bone can be elevated without perforation of the Schneiderian membrane to the height of the final implant placement. (d) Implant can be secured without sinus perforation. (B) Over the past few decades, the Summer's osteotome technique has been modified. Technique modifications include the use of an expanded armamentarium: (a) Initial drill, (b) Non‐end cutting Reamer, (c) Trephine, (d) DASK bur, (e) Osteotome, and (f) Bone spreading instrument.

Modifications of the Summers' technique include addition of various drills to a precise bone height 1–2 mm below the sinus followed by use of the osteotomes to achieve direct access to the sinus and/or proceed to elevate the sinus (Figure 2B). 47 , 48 Furthermore, the space can be maintained without bone graft, with bone graft agents, or other materials (i.e. PRF, B‐TCP, etc.). 49 This approach is more minimally invasive compared to the direct sinus elevation (i.e. lateral window) approach, resulting in less surgical trauma and post‐operative discomfort, and shorter healing time. 50 It also avoids the need for extensive flap elevation, and is completed through the implant osteotomy, often allowing the clinician to place the implant in same visit as the TSFE. 50

Despite the high success rate of this procedure, some associated complications are reported. 51 Complications include but are not limited to: benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, due to extensive malleting, 31 bleeding (although low incidence but can happen), infection, implant migration 52 , 53 lack of primary implant stability (lack of initial bone height, poor bone quality, over‐preparation and premature loading). Other complications can occur include: postoperative hemorrhage, nasal bleeding, blocked nose, hematoma, loosening of cover screws, benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, 54 and migration of dental implants into the sinus. 55 Furthermore, the lack of direct visual control to prevent sinus membrane perforation. The mechanical “tapping” force applied can lead to patient discomfort, and the amount of sinus membrane elevation is generally less than what is possible with a lateral approach. 49 Therefore, cases utilizing TSFE are limited based on the residual alveolar bone height. 49 The majority of the studies included in this scoping review used the osteotome in some form, with or without bone grafting. The average residual bone height for the osteotome group in our study was 5.7 mm, ranging from 2.2 to 9.8 mm.

Double osteotome approach

Although not included in scoping review due a follow‐up time period outside of the constraints of this study, the double osteotome approach, an adaptation of the traditional osteotome method, has also been described in the literature. A case series report demonstrated a dual TSFE method as an alternative to the conventional lateral approach for augmenting a minimal residual alveolar ridge height of 4–6 mm. 56 The study findings indicated that an average elevation height (EH) of 5.81 ± 0.7 mm was achieved following the initial surgery. Prior to the second surgery, the EH was recorded at 5.15 ± 0.91 mm. After administering 0.2 mL of the filling material (for a cumulative volume of 0.5 mL), the EH increased to 6.69 ± 0.89 mm. Subsequently, with a 0.4 mL injection (bringing the total to 0.7 mL), the EH reached 8.11 ± 1.24 mm. This case series suggests that using two successive TSFE procedures can attain bone height augmentation comparable to that obtained via the lateral window approach.

Piezoelectric group

The use of a piezoelectric drill system, as demonstrated in Figure 3, allows gradual lifting of the maxillary sinus using high‐frequency ultrasonic micro‐vibrations (25–29 kHz) that are caused by piezoelectric effects to cut only mineralized tissue leaving soft tissue (i.e. nerves, vessels, mucosa, Schneiderian membrane) uninjured. 57 The piezoelectric effect is created by electrical stimulation on the crystals/ceramics that expand and contract repetitively. 58 These vibration kinetics cause micro‐level vertical and horizontal displacement of the cutting surface and lead to the observed incision in the hard tissue interface. While piezosurgery offers safety as a touted feature it does increase the surgical time for bone preparation. 59 Our study found four articles that used piezoelectric armamentarium for the osteotomy and sinus elevation. Half of the studies in this group evaluated the technique in combination with PRF and the other half evaluated piezoelectric techniques in combination with a bone graft. The piezoelectric technique when combined with bone graft had an average of 5% perforation rate. Notably the average residual bone height for the piezoelectric group was 5.8 mm, ranging from 3 to 8 mm. The final bone height increase for the piezoelectric technique was 6.5 mm ranging from 3.5 to 10 mm. Further the implant stability average was 68.925 for the group with an average failure rate of two implants. Additional comparisons between PRF and bone graft could not be made due to the heterogeneity of the data and for comparisons of this nature to be made a specific RCT may be necessary in the future.

Osseodensification group

The use of osseodensification technique utilizes burs that are specifically designed to preserve the bone (Figure 4). Instead of rotating clockwise, they rotate counter‐clockwise which compacts and autografts the bone within the socket reducing the amount of the bone lost. It was previously reported that the benefits of osseodensification include increased bone‐implant contact, bone density, and primary stability. 60 In the present study, four groups were included based on selection criteria and process. 9 , 13 , 32 Following use of the osseodensification surgical protocol, grafting material was placed to aid in sinus elevation and this material was different among the three different studies including β‐TCP, viscoelastic putty, and allograft cancellous grafting material. Initial bone height ranged from 4 to 7 mm among the groups with an average of 4.15 mm. This average bone height was reported to increase to 10.2 mm at the final measurement time point at >1 year follow‐up measurements. None of the studies in the present group reported implant stability average values; however, all of the included osseodensification studies did report zero implant failures.

An osseodensification technique utilizes a non‐cutting bur sequence to prepare space for implant placement without eliminating bone. To achieve this end, (A) the site is surgically prepared for alveolar access. (B–E) A sequence of burs (at increasing diameters) is utilized to create the osteotomy push bone to the margins of the socket while simultaneously raising the sinus. (F) Once the osteotomy reaches the pre‐planned size, an implant may be placed.

Hydraulic group

Use of a nasal balloon to separate the sinus membrane was another technique added to the sinus elevation armamentarium. 61 , 62 This treatment modality is illustrated in Figure 5. Moreover, the antral membrane balloon elevation technique utilizes a series of osteotomy drills and osteotome to gain access to the sinus floor. In the present study, all reviewed articles in this category examined the integrity of the sinus membrane by Valsalva maneuver and direct visualization. Then a dedicated balloon was inflated to advance the membrane using a contrast medium (for radiographic opacity) or saline. After optimal advancement of the membrane either grafting material, or PRF, or nothing was added to the site, followed by implant placement.

The hydraulic technique utilizes physical principles of distributed pressure to achieve sinus elevation. (A, B) The osteotomy is created utilizing burs and reamers to gain complete access to the sinus membrane. (C) A balloon is subsequently inserted into the osteotomy and water pressure is used to expand the balloon at the sinus‐bone interface allowing release and elevation. (D) Following sinus release from the alveolar bone, grafting material and/or implants may be placed.

Two articles elected not to use additional materials before implant placement. 19 , 23 In these articles, the mean implant failure rate was 0%. Of the groups that added bone graft the failure rate averaged to 1.84%. There was a single paper that utilized PRF as a retentive material with no reported implant failures. 19 Among the different grafting modalities, utilizing a bone graft yielded the highest bone gain values compared to PRF or no grafting modality at all. The effects of grafting modality on perforation rate cannot be reported because the articles that discussed use of PRF or no graft did not report perforation rate.

Contributions of medical history to TSFE outcomes

Schneiderian membrane perforation during sinus floor augmentation can be influenced by the thickness and inflammatory status of the membrane. 63 Smoking is associated with pathological thickening and atrophy of the sinus membrane and is a proposed risk factor for perforation during transcrestal surgery. 64 The study that reported the highest perforation rate of 45% included patients with a history of smoking using the hydraulic approach. 16 However, further analysis comparing the effects of smoking on the success of the hydraulic balloon technique or others specifically was not feasible due to the heterogeneity between studies. As such, a future line of investigation might look into this relationship further to determine a correlation as well as mechanism of action to improve success rates.

One group completed a study to evaluate implant success in diabetic patients, stratifying patients by well controlled (HbA1c <7%), fairly well‐controlled (HbA1c 7%–9%), and poorly controlled (HbA1c >9%) groups. 65 Notably patients with a mean HbA1c of 7.2% in the perioperative period had the same overall survival rate as controls in conventional and advanced implant therapy. Among factors of age, gender, HbA1c, diabetes diagnostic duration, and smoking, HbA1c is the most important factor affecting implant complication rate. 65 In the present study, all articles utilized patients that fell into the controlled category with an HbA1c <7%, which supports the present findings that no additional complications were observed with the diagnosis of diabetes.

Technique comparison

The techniques reviewed included the osteotome, piezoelectric, hydraulic, and osseodensification, and comparisons are reviewed in Table 4. Notably, the osteotome technique had diverse armamentarium options including complete use of osteotomes or rotary combinations. The osteotome protocol has limitations including pressure points at the sinus, patient discomfort during tapping, and higher risks of vertigo sequelae following the procedure in comparison to other techniques. The hydraulic technique generally touts minimized risks of sinus perforations but carries other complications and possible lost small components of the apparatus in the sinus if failure occurs. Osseodensification preserves the elastic spring‐back nature of the cortical bone to enhance the primary stability of implant placement, research evaluating the limits of sinus elevation and crestal bone dynamics with this technique is still ongoing. Lastly, piezoelectric tools used during sinus elevation minimize the risk of membrane perforation due to contact with a drill surface; however, may in some instances extend operation time.

Strengths and limitations

The limitations of the present study are inherent to the scoping review study design and sample characteristics. Often studies in the literature reported metrics in a heterogeneous way or not at all making statistical analysis difficult in many categories. Due to the heterogeneity of reporting in the literature, the present study was unable to perform statistics to complete a meta‐analysis comparing different treatment modalities. Generally, manuscripts lacked standardization in reporting patient demographics, pre‐operative assessment, surgical method description, intra‐operative outcomes, post‐operative clinical outcomes, radiographic outcomes, patient‐reported outcomes, long‐term follow‐up details, and statistical analysis methodology. Furthermore, reported data are generated from a minority of the included studies and due to the nature of statistical measures using smaller data sets ranges and averages are largely influenced by outliers. In addition, there are still confounding factors that may not have been controlled or discussed in many articles including but not limited to smoking/marijuana/vaping, autoimmune diseases, etc. Moreover, Schneiderian membrane perforation during TSFE was reported with great variability, making it difficult to compare articles within and between groups. As such, we call upon the field to report homogenous variables using the suggested checklist in Supplemental File 2.

Despite these limitations, a scoping review of this nature does benefit the readership to (1) map out the existing literature on the topic of modern approaches to TSFE; (2) clarify the importance of reporting (i.e. bone height throughout the course of experiments, following cases of TSFE for more than a year, and looking for perforation rates, and contributing factors to bone homeostasis/development; and (3) identify research gaps. Despite the limitations, this review remains a crucial step in the continuum of evidence synthesis particularly in the evolving field of TSFE.

CONCLUSION

Numerous instruments and techniques have been developed to optimize results with TSFE technique; nevertheless, the combined use of osteotomes and bone grafting materials remains the most prevalent and established method. Factors to be considered during the TSFE procedure include but are not limited to bone quantity (both horizontal and vertical dimensions), anatomy of the site, bone quality, Schneiderian membrane characteristics, and systemic/environmental characteristics. The transalveolar method is becoming a standard surgical procedure because it remains less invasive. A thorough comprehension of the patient's health history, available armamentarium, and strategies for managing post‐operative complications is imperative for the successful execution of TSFE for dental implantation in the posterior maxilla.
