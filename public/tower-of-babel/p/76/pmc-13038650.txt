Assessing health risks of extremely hot weather and the need for enhanced heat alerts in a City with a subtropical climate
Authors: Hiu Fai Law, Yi Hin Chan, Sze Ning Chong, Janice Ying-en Ho, Pui Hing Chau, Hon Yin Yeung, Pak Wai Chan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Hong Kong Observatory has operated the Very Hot Weather Warning since 2000 to warn the local population of very hot weather, defined as when the daily maximum temperature (Tmax) reaches 33 °C. Recent years have seen an increasing number of days with Tmax reaching 35 °C, hence necessitating a review and update of the warning system. In this study, the health impacts of heat were assessed to inform and support the update of the Very Hot Weather Warning. A retrospective study of heat-health related mortality and emergency calls of the Hong Kong population in 2012 to 2022 was conducted. Generalized linear models and distributed lag non-linear model were performed to estimate relative risk (RR) regarding different levels of Tmax. Sensitivity analyses were performed with other indicators of heat exposure. There was a dip of RR at Tmax starting from around 34 °C for cardiovascular disease and heat stroke emergency calls for those aged 0 to 64, although not reaching statistical significance. Among those aged 0 to 64, the RR for heat-health related mortality and emergency calls increased to 1.148 (95% CI: 0.832 to 1.584) and 1.074 (95% CI: 0.984, 1.171) at Tmax of 35.5 °C, respectively. In terms of lag effect, it was found that the lag effect of high temperatures on emergency call was within 1 day. Among alternative heat exposure indicators, daily minimum temperature (Tmin) consistently demonstrated elevated risk in extreme hot temperature for all the outcomes. This observational study of association between adverse health outcomes and extreme high temperature supports the addition of an advisory message when Tmax reaches 35 °C for Hong Kong. Further enhancement may involve other heat stress indicator.Keywords: Extreme weather, Extremely hot weather, Heat-health impact, Subtropical urban city, Hong Kong

Introduction

Against the backdrop of global warming and local urbanisation, the temperatures in Hong Kong, a city with subtropical climate, have exhibited a significant long-term increasing trend. Analysis of the annual mean temperature data showed that there was an average rise of 0.14 °C per decade from 1885 to 2024. The increasing rate more than doubled to 0.37 °C per decade during 1995–2024 (HKO 2025). The occurrence of very hot weather, defined as a day with a daily maximum temperature (Tmax) of at least 33 °C recorded at the Hong Kong Observatory Headquarters (HKO) located at the urban area of Tsim Sha Tsui of Hong Kong, has increased in frequency. The climatological normal of very hot weather has increased from an annual average of 9.83 days in 1971–2000 to 17.47 days in 1991–2020 (HKO 2023a).

On top of a general increasing trend of the local temperatures, according to Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change Sixth Assessment Report, the frequency, regional changes in mean climate and extremes have become more widespread and pronounced (IPCC 2023). An extremely hot day is defined as a day when Tmax at the HKO is at least 35 °C, which is ranked among the Tmax recorded at the station as the 99.8th -percentile based on the 1991–2020 climate period (data available at [DPO 2025]). While the annual average number of extremely hot days during this period is only 0.77 days, the maximum number reached 15 in 2022 (HKO 2023b), which is much higher than the average.

The enhancement of heat-related warning systems has become increasingly important as the impacts of climate change intensify. Many countries have implemented heat warning systems with more than one threshold to address the health risks posed by extreme temperatures. For example, the China Meteorological Administration adopts a three-tier warning system (yellow, orange, and red) to issue alerts based on temperature at different values and specific durations (CMA 2012).

In Hong Kong, since 2000, HKO has operated the Very Hot Weather Warning (VHWW), defined as Tmax of at least 33 °C, which is disseminated via multiple channels and issued whenever the territory is expected to be generally threatened by very hot weather to alert members of the public to the risk of heat stress and potential heatstroke. While reference was made to the temperature at the HKO Headquarters in early years, with more automatic weather stations (AWS) available, nine reference stations (Cheung Chau, Lau Fau Shan, Sai Kung, Sha Tin, Shek Kong, Ta Kwu Ling, Tseung Kwan O, Tuen Mun, Wong Chuk Hang) spread across the territory were selected and among which the sixth highest temperature had been considered in the current warning system. From multiple linear regression models between VHWW and the daily mortality rates from ischaemic heart disease and stroke, it was found in a previous study that the absence of VHWW was associated with an increase of the mortality rates (Chau et al. 2009). In 2014, the service was enhanced to alert the public, especially those at-risk, with a “Special Weather Tips” notification of heat stress on days when the threshold of VHWW may not be reached. In 2021 (HKO 2021), the service was enhanced further to warn the public of prolonged heat. In view of the elevated temperatures and the increase of extremely hot days, an extra “Special Weather Tips” notification on top of VHWW would be delivered to alert the public of extremely hot weather from 26 May 2023.

There is no universal threshold for heat warnings; effective criteria should account for local climatology and societal impacts. As there are well-documented adverse effects on human health, reflected through emergency ambulance, hospitalisation or mortality, associated with high temperatures (Wong and Lai 2012; WHO 2018, Cheng et al. 2019; Ho et al. 2023, 2024; Guo et al. 2024a, b), heat-health warning systems have been developed in many places, sometimes after major heat events (Martinez 2019). Under a warming climate, in order to maintain the effectiveness of the warning systems, these measures have to be reviewed and updated regularly (for example, Arbuthnott and Hajat 2017, Benmarhnia et al. 2019). However, evaluation of these systems encounters different challenges (Dwyer et al. 2022), including the difficulty to include all factors affecting heat health, as well as to establish the hypothetical scenario for comparison when the system is not in place.

Due to the increasing frequency of extremely hot days seen in Hong Kong in the recent years, this article aims to assess the health risks under extremely hot weather faced by the population of Hong Kong. Specifically, this study aims to explore whether a second level of advisory message for extremely hot weather could be justified by examining the relationship between high temperatures and health impacts, reflected by mortality and frequency of emergency calls of the population.

Materials and methods

Meteorological data

Situated at 22 N along the south China coast, Hong Kong experiences a subtropical climate with hot months from May to September, occasionally extending at the ends by a month. As ambient temperature is not the sole indicator of heat stress (Lee et al. 2016), we used various meteorological data, including wind speed, rainfall and relative humidity to reflect exposure to heat. For temperatures, several measurements were used, including the daily maximum and minimum temperatures measured at the HKO Headquarters (Tmax and Tmin, respectively), which serve as an indicator of the situation of the urban areas, and Rank 6 Tmax, defined as the sixth highest temperature of nine reference AWS, which serves as an indicator of the general hotness of the territory. These stations are well spread across the territory with a range of distance from the sea. Prevailing wind speed, which facilitate evaporative cooling, was taken from the exposed island of Waglan Island, about 20 km to the southeast of the HKO Headquarters to approximate the winds for the territory. Rainfall was measured as the daily rainfall and humidity was assessed using daily mean relative humidity, both recorded at the HKO Headquarters. Moreover, it is useful to consider meteorological variables in a dynamic way for its combined impact, since how much heat stored in the human body depends on how quickly heat is eliminated from the body, which depends on humidity, wind and solar radiation among other factors. For this purpose, we will use the Hong Kong Heat Index (HKHI) (Lee et al. 2016), which is defined as 0.8*the natural wet bulb temperature + 0.05*the globe temperature + 0.15*the dry bulb temperature, and developed through studying local health impacts in Hong Kong and the measurement located at King’s Park (about two kilometres to the north of HKO Headquarters). From the formula, it can be seen that HKHI was motivated by WBGT, but was formulated considering the health impact of heat stress, a step that is critical in designing public weather service..

Having a subtropical monsoon climate, Hong Kong experiences tropical cyclone and heavy rain in summer. Excluded from the current analysis were dates on which a Black Rainstorm Warning Signal (issued when there is widespread and persistent heavy rain reaching 70 mm/hr) or a Tropical Cyclone Signal No. 8 (issued when gale or storm force wind is blowing or expected generally near the sea level) or above was issued by the HKO, where most socioeconomic activities stop and public transportation is suspended (except for some core services). These extreme weather events and their warnings may have dissuaded people from seeking medical help. Further exclusions were the years of 2019 and 2020, where the social unrest and COVID-19 pandemic may have affected help-seeking behaviour and might possibly confound the effect of temperatures, as in particular the calls/death pattern in 2020 did not follow the seasonal trend.

Air quality data

The Environmental Protection Department (EPD) of Hong Kong measures air quality data, including the respiratory suspended particulates (RSP) at various stations. We take the average of the readings at the stations as a measure of the general air quality of the day. The data is available at (EPD 2025).

Mortality

Daily mortality data between 2012 and 2022 were obtained from the Census and Statistics Department (C&SD). The data were stratified by age and the cause of death. We analysed deaths related to cardiovascular disease (CVD) and other causes related to hot weather (ICD-10: I20-25, I50, I60-69, T67, X30; Appendix I), which were well-established to associate with high temperatures (Ebi et al. 2021; EPA 2016).

Emergency calls for assistance

Two datasets of emergency calls were used. Firstly, call records of Fire Services Department (FSD) have been suggested to represent demand of prehospital care under adverse weather (Wong and Lai 2010), and thus emergency calls to the FSD due to the heat-related causes of calls, namely CVD and heat stroke (HS), were obtained for the years 2012–2022. These emergency calls due to problems with CVD or heat stroke were combined in the analysis due to the low number of calls.

The second dataset contained calls to the Senior Citizen Home Safety Association (SCHSA) via the Personal Emergency Link (PE-link), which has been previously used in local studies (Mok and Leung 2009, Chan et al. 2011). SCHSA is a self-financed non-profit organisation that operates a round-the-clock vigilance service and provides emergency aid. A user may call the hotline or press a button on a personalised device to request aid. The operator may call the emergency contact of the user or send for the FSD for emergency rescues in case the user does not respond within two minutes after triggering the device for aid. Despite its name, SCHSA is open to users of all ages, though the number of users aged below 65 is relatively small. As not every call via the PE-link requires subsequent hospitalisation, we used those calls in 2012–2022 that were heat-health related, for example due to fainting, shortness of breath, and confusion, as suggested by the medial advisor of SCHSA.

Population data

As our study period was from May to October, we used age-specific mid-year population obtained from the C&SD as an offset in the model.

Statistical analysis

The analyses were stratified by age groups. A preliminary investigation suggested that the number of daily calls related to those younger than 18 were often zero. To avoid zero-inflation, the mortality and emergency call data were grouped into 0–64, 65–74, 75 or above, and all ages.

First, the health impact of temperature exposures was modelled using the generalized linear model (GLM) (McCullagh and Nelder 1989), based on the glm function in R using a quasi-Poisson family of functions and logarithm as the link function. The outcome variable was the daily number of calls or daily mortality in the corresponding analyses. The main exposure variable was Tmax at the HKO, used as a Boolean variable, according to multiple temperature thresholds from 28 °C to 36 °C at an interval of 0.5 °C. Control variables included rainfall, wind speed, relative humidity, day of the week, and public holiday. The logarithm of mid-year population in the study period was used as an offset in GLM for deaths and FSD emergency calls to account for the yearly change of population. For SCHSA, the raw number of calls was used as the number of users across the years remained the same according to the SCHSA.

For each age group, the formula for the quasi-Poisson model was as follows:

log(daily count of outcome) = factor(Temp thresholds) + factor(DOW) + factor(PH) + lin(Wind speed) + lin(Sqrt. of daily rainfall) + lin(RH) + lin(RSP) + ns(Year, df = 3) + offset(log(mid-year population)),

where factor() indicates categorical independent variables, lin() for linear terms, ns() for natural spline, and offset() for an offset variable. Temp thresholds stand for the binary indicator to indicate whether the temperature exceeds the threshold of Tmax. DOW stands for day of week (1–7), and PH represents whether the day is a public holiday or not. RH refers to the relative humidity. RSP is respirable suspended particulates. Sensitivity analyses were performed by using Tmin, Rank 6 Tmax, and HKHI to define the heat exposure.

Next, to investigate the delay of health risks posed by high temperatures, we used the distributed lag nonlinear model (Gasparrini 2011), up to a period of four days to capture the effect using the R package “dlnm”. In (Bao et al. 2016), it was reported that there was increased relative risk (RR) of mortality of some Chinese cities for extreme heat up to a lag of three days using a distributed lag nonlinear model. In another study examining calls to SCHSA, a four-day lag was used (Wong et al. 2015). As this study also assessed the population of Hong Kong, a lag of maximum four days was adopted. A quadratic polynomial was used, with the exposure variables used as continuous variables with cubic spline.

For each age group, the formula for the distributed lag nonlinear model was as follows:

log(daily count of outcome) = cb(Tmax) + factor(DOW) + factor(PH) + lin(Wind speed) + lin(Sqrt. of daily rainfall) + lin(RH) + lin(RSP) + ns(Year, df = 3) + offset(log(mid-year population)),

where cb() is the crossbasis function of Tmax exposure, factor() indicates categorical independent variables, lin() linear terms, ns() natural spline, and offset() an offset variable. A level of significance of p < 0.05 was used in all analyses.

Main findings using the GLM model

Figure 1 shows that association between exposure to extreme hot weather and the three adverse health events that are related to heat. The RR of heat-health related mortality demonstrated an increase for ages 0–64 as Tmax increased above 35 °C. The RR at 35.5 °C was 1.148 (95% Confidence Interval [CI]:0.832 to 1.584). For the FSD CVD/Heat stroke calls, there was a slight drop of RR for age 0–64 as Tmax increased above 33.5 °C whereby the risk increased again at 35.0 °C. For the group 0–64, the increase at 35.5 °C was close to statistical significance with RR 1.074 (95% CI: 0.984 to 1.171). SCHSA heat-related calls demonstrated a small drop in RR as Tmax rose above 34 °C for 65–74, but a slight rise at 35.5 °C for users aged 75 or above.

Relative risk of three adverse health events associated with exposure defined by Tmax at HKO. The asterisk marks the drop of RR at around 34 °C

For sensitivity analyses, the results using other indicators than Tmax at HKO can be found in the Appendix II. For heat-health related mortality, elevated risk (thought insignificant) for those aged 0–64 was observed when Tmin reaches 26.5–28.5 °C, but no obvious elevated risk when using Rank 6 Tmax or HKHI as heat exposure. On the other hand, the FSD CVS/HS calls showed a significant and increasing trend in elevated risk when Tmin increases above 27.5 °C, Rank 6 Tmax above 34 °C, or HKHI above 30.5 °C. For heat-related calls to SCHSA, there was an elevated risk for those aged 0–64 when Tmin reaches 27.5 °C, and a significant increase for the same group at Rank 6 Tmax of 36 °C, but no obvious pattern was noted for HKHI.

Main findings incorporating the lag effect

In general, there was an immediate acute effect for all the assessed adverse health outcomes at high temperatures among those aged 0–64. The increased RR for FSD CVS/Heatstroke calls demonstrated a lag of within 1 day when the temperature reaches the very hot level. For all-cause mortality, the lag effect was slightly longer and ranged from 1 to 2 days. In both cases, there appeared a slight trend that the lag decreases as temperature increases at the extremely hot level. Discussion

The increasing occurrence of extreme heat events calls for enhancement of heat-health warning systems and heat plans for cities, such as the densely populated subtropical city of Hong Kong. The Very Hot Weather Warning was introduced in 2000 and Tmax of 33 °C at HKO was ranked about 97-th percentile in the climate 1971 to 2000. However, based on 1991 to 2020, the ranking has dropped to 95-th percentile (see [DPO 2025] for raw data). Meanwhile, Tmax of 35 °C has become more often. This study demonstrated the elevated health risk from extreme heat events using different outcomes, including mortality, FSD calls, and SCHSA calls. Although the risk of heat-health related mortality, FSD CVD/HS calls, and SCHSA heat-related calls increased at or after Tmax of 35 °C, statistical significance was not reached due to the small number of days with extreme heat and the relatively small number of events observed. While a revisit of the impacts and risks is suggested in the future when more days with the extreme level of high temperature are recorded, we could not afford to wait for more adverse events to occur before introducing new initiatives to protect the population.

Associations between extreme hot weather and health outcomes

Regarding mortality, we found elevated heat-health related mortality risk for those aged 0–64 after Tmax of 35.5 °C, although such pattern was not observed for the older age groups. Heat-health related mortality were directly induced by the extreme hot weather, which is more prone to those with direct exposure to it. It is likely that the bulk of this group include the working adult population and incorporates those who engage in more outdoor activities. This is also consistent with a recent local study that demonstrated that heat stress had the greatest effect size on emergency department visits among the male population aged 19 to 64 as compared to other population subgroups (Chau et al. 2022). All-causes mortality also showed elevated risk for those aged 0–64 but to a smaller extend than the heat-health specific one. The unexpected risk reduction among those aged 65–74 warrants further investigation in future.

Regarding the emergency calls, there was a slight drop of the risk of FSD CVD/HS in the age group 0–64 as the Tmax increased to 33.5 °C. This may suggest that the currently operating VHWW might have played a role in reducing the health risk, echoing the result in (Chau et al. 2009). Yet, when the temperatures increased further to the extreme level (35 °C), there are indications that the relative risk may rise again. The higher risk among those aged 0–64 also echoed our findings for heat-health related mortality. Although there was not a clear trend of increased risk across different levels of hot weather for the SCHSA heat-related calls, a previous study examined the SCHSA all-cause calls requiring hospitalisation between 2004 and 2008 and found that the probability of a larger number (that is more than one standard deviation from the hot season average number of calls) of calls increases from 15% for Tmax ≥ 28 °C to 25% for Tmax ≥ 34 °C (Mok and Leung 2009). In our study, the different trends of RR among those aged 65–74 and aged 75 or above in the high temperatures suggested the help-seeking behaviour might be different in the age groups. Similarly, the difference between FSD CVD/HS calls and SCHSA calls among those aged 65–74 might not be simply explained by the health impacts alone. There could be a variety of reasons contributing to an absence of help-seeking behaviour by older adults, including cultural expectations or personal preference (Woods et al. 2005). Literature reported that those who were more likely to call SCHSA were more health conscious and willing to overcome the psychological barrier to seek help (Wong et al. 2015). Another possibility could be related to the low level of awareness of older adults towards the adverse effect of hot weather. Older adults may have the perception that it is normal to feel discomfort during hot weather and reduce the urge to seek help (Lai et al. 2023). There seems to be a need to increase the awareness of health impacts in the hot weather so as to capture the narrow window for emergency assistance.

We also compare the Tmax at HKO with other indicators of heat exposure. Among Tmin, Rank 6 Tmax and HKHI, Tmin demonstrated more clearly the elevated risk in high minimum temperatures for both calls for assistance. High predictive performance of Tmin has been reported in the local studies. Studies reported that mortality among older adults were more vulnerable to hot nights, defined as Tmin above 28 °C, compared with very hot days, defined as Tmax above 33 °C (Wang et al. 2019; Shi et al. 2019). Another recent study demonstrated that nighttime heat is significantly associated with increased hospitalisations (Guo et al. 2024a, b). Similarly, Tmin was also found to correlate well in terms of health impacts of mortality and hospitalisation (Ho et al. 2025). Tmin usually occurred at night, we would adopt Tmin instead of the night time temperature for simplicity. Patterns of our findings from Tmax and Tmin were largely consistent. As heat stress depends on a variety of meteorological factors (and physiological ones), so that composite heat stress indicators are helpful in public weather service, e.g. (Lee et al. 2016; Guo et al. 2024a, b). Moreover, heat stress indicators composing from raw weather elements could have better predictive power on health impacts (Guo et al. 2025). On the other hand, the predictive power could be affected by local characteristics and raw meteorological data sometimes have better skill. In this study, we focus on the Tmax as the primary indicator due to its familiarity to the public.

Lag effects of extreme hot weather

In terms of the analyses incorporating lag, our findings identified an acute effect of high temperature with a limited delay of the health events, with emergency calls within 1 day and mortality within 2 days. This is consistent with other local study on all-causes mortality that reported a lag of within 1 or 2 days (Ho et al. 2017). The RR for heat-health related mortality and that for calls to SCHSA for aged 0–64 both showed a high risk at day 0 while a dip around day 2. This could be due to a harvesting effect of the initial high risk. On the other hand, there is a rebound at day 4, which could be due to overlap with the next episode of high temperature, as each case was considered individually. Similar drop in mortality at lag 2 to 3 after heat stress was also reported locally (Ho et al. 2017). The drop in emergency department visits following heat stress at lag 1 was also reported locally (Ho et al. 2024).

Practical implications

Currently, HKO alerts the public via the VHWW and an additional “Special Weather Tips” notification to extended periods of very hot days or hot nights when VHWW is in force. VHWW will be issued when Tmax reaches 33 °C. With the increasing occurrence of extremely hot day, HKO strengthened the warning system by issuing “Special Weather Tips”, an additional advisory message, of extremely hot weather (HKO 2023c). There is also an ongoing study of extended periods of extremely hot weather and the impacts. Given the good performance of Tmin in correlating with heat-health related adverse outcomes, Tmin could be considered to enhance the warning system. While HKHI is a comprehensive index incorporating humidity, wind and solar radiation, its associations with the heat-health related events are less obvious. Moreover, the public is more familiar with the simple concept of Tmax and Tmin. Therefore, our findings support the use of Tmax at 35.5 °C as a threshold of increased relative risk to the public, but for operation and ease of use, Tmax of 35 °C is more appropriate, as it is easier to communicate integer value and a slightly lower threshold may help to advance the alert to health impacts. Furthermore, on top of issuing the “Special Weather Tips” notification when there is an extended period of hot nights, ways to alert the public to high Tmin may be explored.

The enhanced Special Weather Tips notification on extremely hot weather is aimed to raise awareness of heat-health impact of vulnerable groups, which include older persons, pregnant women, infants and children, those with chronic illnesses, such as heart disease or high blood pressure, as well as the obese (Ebi et al. 2021). Their health conditions should be monitored and adequate rest ensured to maintain a normal body temperature. Evidence-based measures in response to warnings should be advocated in order to mitigate the impact of heat on health (Johar et al. 2025). Collaboration with partners, including the Labour Department, Occupational Safety & Health Council, Department of Health and Home Affairs Department, to ensure information on high temperature events is delivered, will remain a key to the successful operation of heat plans. Public education on precautionary actions concerning heat health is needed. For outdoor workers, provision of heat shelters and sufficient fluid intakes are important. For those engaged in outdoor activities, they should reconsider their schedule and to avoid having unnecessary outdoor activities during extremely hot weather, and to consider rerouting to reduce heat exposure. For those staying indoor, awareness of the possibility of indoor heat stress should be noted and precautionary measures should not be neglected. In long run, better town planning to minimize heat island effects should be considered. Moreover, as behaviour changes vulnerability, the measures of heat plans should tailor for different groups to alert them of the appropriate actions under the heat stress. Other societal factors could include increasing the efficiency of air-conditioning as well as improving ventilation in the building design level are critical to mitigate health impacts.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study were the use of multiple health outcomes and various exposure indicators. However, owing to the observational nature of the study, causal relationship could not be assured. Furthermore, individual-level confounders such as area of residence, heat island effect, type of building, and vulnerability level of older people, as well as some environmental-level confounders such as influenza epidemics were not controlled in the analysis. Moreover, the small occurrence number of the events and the small number of days reaching very extreme hot weather limited the statistical power in detecting significant associations. While only selected causes for FSD and SCHSA calls were available for analyses, future study may revisit all-causes data as well.

Conclusion

This observational study of association between adverse health outcomes and extreme high temperature supports the addition of an advisory message when Tmax reaches 35 °C for Hong Kong. Further enhancement, such as using other statistics from the temperatures or other heat stress indicators, may be considered in the future.
