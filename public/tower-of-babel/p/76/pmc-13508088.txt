The FAD-Dependent Monooxygenase Albo65 Mediates Self-Resistance in Albofungin Biosynthesis
Authors: Zehui Liu, Chunshuai Huang, Hao Xu, Lingyang Zhu, Huimin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The production of specialized small molecules in microorganisms often involves self-resistance mechanisms to prevent autotoxicity. Albofungins belong to the xanthone-containing aromatic polyketides with potent antimicrobial activities. However, its self-resistance mechanism remains unknown. Here we report the discovery of a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent monooxygenase, Albo65, that mediates self-resistance in albofungin biosynthesis in Streptomyces monomycini NRRL B-24309. Deletion of albo65 resulted in almost no albofungin production and increased the sensitivity of the mutant strain to albofungin. Heterologous expression of albo65 in a nonproducing host increased the resistance of the host to albofungin. Biochemical studies showed that Albo65 catalyzes the oxidative inactivation of albofungin in vitro, and the resulting reaction mixture exhibited reduced antibacterial activity. Based on this discovery, the albofungin–albo65 pair was successfully developed as a highly sensitive selection system for yeasts. This work provides insights into the self-resistance mechanisms in albofungin biosynthesis and explores potential applications of Albo65 in basic and applied biological research.

Introduction

Microorganisms are prolific producers of secondary metabolites with scaffold diversity and structural complexity. These specialized small molecules have various applications in agriculture and medicine, and are of paramount importance in the antibiotic discovery and development pipeline. Antibiotics represent one of the most transformative pharmaceutical agents in human health care, significantly extending the average life expectancy by at least one to two decades over the past century. , However, rampant use of these life-saving medications has also given rise to a new challenge, the emergence of antibiotic resistance. Increasing antibiotic resistance greatly weakens the therapeutic effectiveness of existing drug candidates, leading to unavailability of clinical prescriptions and failure of therapy. Consequently, it necessitates a thorough understanding of the mechanisms of resistance to design promising treatment strategies before the resistance evolution and dissemination get out of clinical control. ,

Self-resistance is essential for the survival of antibiotic-producing microorganisms, allowing microorganisms to produce and accumulate secondary metabolites to kill or inhibit the growth of competing species without harming themselves. Molecular mechanisms of antibiotic resistance typically include: (1) efflux pump that transports antibiotics out of cells into the extracellular space, (2) cell membrane changes to reduce permeability, (3) molecular target alteration to decrease binding affinity, and (4) enzyme-mediated modification and degradation of antibiotics (Figure S1). , These mechanisms can function either as general resistance strategies or, in some cases, as dedicated self-resistance mechanisms when encoded in producer organisms. The most prominent example of enzymatic antibiotic inactivation is that β-lactamases inactivate β-lactam antibiotics via hydrolyzing the strained 4-membered lactam rings. Another example is that aminoglycoside-inactivating enzymes catalyze the phosphorylation, acylation, nucleotidylylation, or other modifications of the amine or hydroxyl groups of aminoglycoside scaffolds to deactivate efficacy. Moreover, one of the occurring resistance mechanisms to tetracyclines has been characterized as the oxidative destruction of the naphthalene core by the family of flavoenzymes, resulting in the loss of affinity to the inhibitor-binding pocket. Related enzymatic transformations have also been implicated in self-resistance in certain natural product biosynthetic systems. Albofungin, which was discovered in the 1970s, belongs to the xanthone-containing aromatic polyketides with potent biological and pharmacological activities, such as antibacterial, antifungal, and antitumor activity. − Noteworthily, despite the increasing prevalence of antibiotic resistance, albofungin resistance has not thus far been documented and the self-resistance mechanism of albofungin remains unknown.

In this study, we uncover the self-resistance mechanism within the biosynthesis of albofungin and identify the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD)-dependent monooxygenase as a key factor mediating albofungin self-resistance. Specifically, the biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) for synthesis of albofungins (albo) was identified from Streptomyces monomycini NRRL B-24309 and heterologously expressed in the model host Streptomyces albus J1074. The role of Albo65 in self-resistance was investigated through overexpression, gene knockout, and complementation, as well as in vitro biochemical characterization, which supports an oxidative self-protection mechanism. Finally, we develop a highly sensitive selection system based on the albofungin-albo65 pair for basic and applied biological studies of yeasts.

Results

Discovery, Heterologous Expression, and Bioactivity Evaluation of Albofungins

Albofungin is a rare polycyclic aromatic polyketide featuring a heavily oxygenated angular hexacyclic framework, which exhibits strong antimicrobial, antitumor, and anti-HIV activities. , Recently, the albofungin BGCs from Streptomyces chrestomyceticus BCC 24770 (alb), Streptomyces tumemacerans JCM5050 (abf), and Streptomyces monomycini CGMCC 4.3581 (afn) have been reported. These gene clusters encompass open reading frames encoding type II polyketide synthases (PKSs), transporters, regulators, and corresponding tailoring enzymes. By genome mining and comparative genomic analysis, three homologous gene clusters putatively encoding albofungin biosynthesis were obtained (Figure a). The albofungin BGC (albo) from S. monomycini NRRL B-24309 shares high sequence similarity to alb and abf. This gene cluster was cloned by the CAPTURE method and heterologously expressed in S. albus J1074, leading to the production of albofungin, chrestoxanthones A and C (1–3) (Figure b,c, Figures S2–S4, and Tables S2–S4). These compounds were evaluated for their antimicrobial activities against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, as well as fungal species. Albofungin exhibited excellent antibacterial and antifungal activities against Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633, Bacillus halodurans C-125, Micrococcus luteus ATCC 4698, Streptococcus mutans ATCC 25175, Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4741, and Saccharomyces cerevisiae YSG50 with minimum inhibition concentration (MIC) values less than 1 ng mL–1 (Figure d). Albofungin can also moderately inhibit the growth of S. albus J1074, Staphylococcus epidermidis 15 × 154, Enterobacter cloacae, Pseudomonas fluorescens Pf-5, Candida and Aspergillus species, with MIC values range from 0.032 μg mL–1 to 4 μg mL–1.

Probing the Albofungin-Resistant Gene in the albo BGC

As mentioned above, albofungin showed certain inhibition activity toward S. albus J1074 (Figure d). Unexpectedly, the heterologous host S. albus J1074 harboring the albo BGC was able to generate albofungin and maintain normal cell growth, potentially attributable to a candidate gene within this cluster that appears to confer resistance to albofungin. To pinpoint the gene responsible for albofungin resistance, we first generated a segment-based expression library spanning the albo BGC by cloning contiguous multigene fragments into pIJ10257 (Figure a and Figure S7). These constructs collectively covered the biosynthetic and candidate resistance region (albo8–65) in the albo cluster and were individually introduced into E. coli and subsequently transferred into S. albus J1074 via conjugal transfer. The potential resistance gene was identified by conducting antimicrobial assay with albofungin against these recombinant strains. In this initial screen, only the strain carrying segment albo60–65 showed a resistant phenotype comparable to that of S. albus J1074 harboring the intact albo BGC, whereas the other tested segments did not show detectable resistance under the assay conditions. (Figures b and Figure S7). Notably, unlike the MIC-based study of Wang et al., in which the transporter abfL (homologous to albo8), and halogenase abfA (homologous to albo19) produced a modest increase in albofungin tolerance, neither albo8–14 nor albo15–19 showed a detectable resistant phenotype in our disk diffusion assay. We then performed a second-round narrowing analysis within the positive albo60–65 region by subcloning smaller fragments and individual genes, including albo60–61, albo62, albo63–65, albo63, albo64, and albo65 (Figure S7). Among these follow-up constructs, only the recombinant strain S. albus J1074::albo65 exhibited a minimal inhibition zone, identifying albo65 as the minimal gene sufficient for the observed resistance phenotype. The FAD-dependent monooxygenase-encoding gene albo65 was therefore identified as a key self-resistance-associated determinant that conferred a robust albofungin resistance phenotype.

In parallel, to further clarify the functional role of albo65 as a resistance determinant, we implemented gene knockout and complementation strategies to assess the impact of this gene on the metabolic profile of albofungin production. The resulting mutant strain Δalbo65 was fermented and analyzed using HPLC. Not surprisingly, the production of albofungin (1) was drastically abolished (Figure c and Figure S8). Subsequent complementation of the Δalbo65 mutant strain with albo65 partially restored the production of albofungin, indicating that albo65 is functionally coupled to productive pathway output in vivo rather than behaving as a late-stage, optional tailoring enzyme. A corresponding peak at 16.2 min was observed in both the albo strain and the complementation strain, with broadly similar UV–vis profiles (Figure S9), consistent with the same or a closely related metabolite. Although the identities of the altered metabolites remain unresolved, the targeted HPLC/LC-MS data show substantial pathway perturbation upon albo65 deletion and partial restoration following complementation. Taken together, these results indicate that albo65, which encodes a predicted FAD-dependent monooxygenase, is required for the albofungin-producing phenotype and mediates the albofungin resistance.

In Vitro Characterization of Albo65 as an FAD-Dependent Albofungin-Modifying Monooxygenase

FAD-dependent monooxygenases are a diverse family of enzymes that catalyze the addition of one oxygen atom to a variety of substrates. A better understanding of the function and regulation of Albo65 is not only pivotal for unraveling the intricate biosynthetic pathway of albofungin, but also, for evaluating its role in producer self-protection. To examine whether Albo65 directly modifies albofungin in vitro, N-(His)6-tagged Albo65 was heterologously produced in E. coli BL21­(DE3), and purified as a brightly yellow protein, suggesting the existence of a prosthetic group (Figure S15). Analysis of the heat-denatured Albo65 supernatant by HPLC, UV–vis spectroscopy, and LC-MS revealed a cofactor profile consistent with FAD binding (Figure S11). Purified Albo65 was incubated with albofungin (1) and the reaction mixtures were analyzed by HPLC (Figure a). We discovered Albo65-dependent formation of an oxygenated albofungin-derived product 4, which was detected only in reactions containing active Albo65 and NADPH, and was not observed in the absence of NADPH or enzyme, or when Albo65 was heat-inactivated, consistent with an NADPH-dependent, enzyme-catalyzed transformation. Because FAD-dependent monooxygenases require molecular oxygen as the terminal oxidant, we further tested this Albo65 reaction under anaerobic conditions and observed complete loss of product formation, confirming oxygen dependence of the Albo65-catalyzed reaction (Figure S10).

To evaluate the impact of Albo65 treatment on albofungin bioactivity, the in vitro reaction mixtures were directly evaluated in a disk diffusion assay against Bacillus subtilis ATCC 6633 (Figure b and Figure S12). Compared with the control reaction lacking Albo65, the complete Albo65 reaction mixture produced a smaller inhibition zone, indicating reduced antibacterial activity after enzymatic treatment. This result provides functional support that Albo65-mediated modification attenuates albofungin bioactivity, consistent with its proposed self-protection role. HR-MS analysis of 4 indicated a mass shift corresponding to the incorporation of one additional oxygen atom relative to 1 (Figure c). Compound 4 was unstable and available only in limited quantity, precluding full NMR characterization (Figure S5G) and direct MIC determination; nevertheless, comparative spectra of 1 and 4 are consistent with a site-specific oxidative modification (Table S7). Specifically, the 1H and 13C chemical shifts of protonated groups remain largely similar, including preserved COSY/HSQC correlations, indicating that the transformation does not broadly perturb rings bearing most protonated positions. In contrast, the disappearance of 13C resonance for ketone C8 near 182.1 ppm is consistent with alteration of the original carbonyl environment, and the OH signal on C6 shifts from ∼ 13 ppm in 1 to 5.53 ppm in 4, consistent with a substantially changed deshielding and/or hydrogen-bonding environment. Given the precedents for Baeyer–Villiger oxygen insertion in aromatic polyketide tailoring, including MtmOIV, XanO4 and FlsO1; − and the sequence homology of Albo65 to them (Figure S13), we propose a Baeyer–Villiger oxygen insertion at the C8 carbonyl as a plausible route to the proposed lactone 4 (Figure d). Taken together, these results support Albo65 as an FAD-dependent monooxygenase that catalyzes oxidative modification of albofungin in vitro, consistent with a self-protection role during albofungin biosynthesis. The understanding of the potency of albofungin together with its cognate resistance determinant Albo65 motivates antibiotic research addressing emerging drug resistance and may be leveraged in further applications.

Development of an Albofungin-albo65 Based Selection System

New selectable marker systems in yeasts remain valuable for both basic and applied studies, because many model and especially nonmodel yeasts still have a limited repertoire of robust antibiotic selection markers. Given the high antifungal potency of albofungin and the resistance phenotype conferred by albo65, we evaluated the efficacy of the antibiotic and antibiotic resistance gene pair (albofungin-albo65) in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and nonmodel yeasts Issatchenkia orientalis and Rhodotorula toruloides. All three species were highly sensitive to albofungin in our growth assays, with complete growth inhibition at concentrations as low as 10–7 M (Figure a), consistent with our previous finding that albofungin is a broad-spectrum antifungal compound. Heterologous expression of albo65 (episomal expression in S. cerevisiae and I. orientalis; chromosomal expression in R. toruloides) restored growth in the presence of albofungin, whereas the negative control expressing a green fluorescent protein (GFP) remained inhibited (Figure b). These results confirm that albo65 can function effectively as a selection marker. We further evaluated the albofungin-albo65 based selection system for transformation by performing plate-based selection. Transformants were selected exclusively using albofungin, and colonies were screened by colony PCR for the presence of the albo65 gene. All tested colonies displayed the expected band (Figure S16). Notably, selection in S. cerevisiae required only 2.5 × 10–6 M albofungin, < 1% of the typical working concentration for antibiotic G418. Taken together, these results demonstrate the albofungin-albo65 pair is a highly sensitive selection system for genetic manipulation in both model and nonmodel yeasts.

Discussion

Self-resistance is a central constraint in antibiotic biosynthesis, and resistance determinants embedded within biosynthetic gene clusters often illuminate both pathway logic and the chemical liability of the product. Here, our genetic and biochemical analyses support that an FAD-dependent monooxygenase, Albo65, mediates self-resistance in Streptomyces species through oxidative inactivation of albofungin. More broadly, resistance-guided genome mining can accelerate natural product discovery by focusing on biosynthetic gene clusters that encode putative self-resistance genes, which often flag bioactive scaffolds and inform pathway logic. ,

At the same time, the Δalbo65 phenotype, which nearly abolished albofungin production (Figure c), suggests that Albo65 is not simply acting as a fully separable late-stage inactivation enzyme, but instead plays a pathway-coupled self-protection role in which resistance-associated chemistry is functionally linked to productive metabolite output. Similar coupling between self-protection and pathway progression has been noted in other antibiotic systems. In apramycin biosynthesis, disruption of a cryptic self-protection-associated gene abolished accumulation of the mature products and altered the metabolite profile, whereas in the LaPhzX/myxin system, loss of a resistance-associated monooxygenase changed the time-course conversion of the bioactive metabolite and its derivatives. In our case, the partial restoration observed in the Δalbo65::albo65 complementation strain may reflect non-native expression of plasmid-borne albo65, and the altered abundance of the 16.2 min peak in the albo and complementation strains may arise from perturbed pathway flux, partial restoration, or altered regulation. This pathway-coupled self-protection model also reconciles how the producer maintains sufficient levels of bioactive albofungin without indiscriminate degradation, with resistance-associated transformation likely coupled to pathway flux rather than acting as a constitutive degradative sink. While a more comprehensive untargeted metabolomic analysis would help clarify the altered metabolite profile and refine the precise pathway role of Albo65, the observed resistance and metabolic phenotypes support Albo65 as a key factor in self-resistance during albofungin biosynthesis.

Enzymatic antibiotic inactivation by flavin-dependent monooxygenases is an established resistance strategy, exemplified by tetracycline destructases. , FAD-dependent monooxygenases span multiple subclasses with distinct cofactor-handling strategies and reaction outcomes. Baeyer–Villiger oxidation has been observed among FAD-dependent monooxygenases in secondary metabolism and in enzymatic detoxification. For example, the mycotoxin zearalenone is converted via a Baeyer–Villiger-type lactone intermediate en route to the detoxified product. Accordingly, oxygen insertion is a reasonable working hypothesis for Albo65-mediated albofungin inactivation. Although the full chemical route remains to be defined, we propose that Albo65 generates the anionic peroxyflavin (flavin–O-O–), which attacks the C8 carbonyl carbon of 1, thus initiating a Baeyer–Villiger–type reaction that generates the proposed ring-expanded lactone 4, and offers a plausible structural basis for the downstream decarboxylation and 1,4-dioxin formation (Figure S14). Given that related flavin-dependent tailoring oxygenases can deviate from canonical Baeyer–Villiger paradigms, mechanistic assignment will benefit from targeted validation, including time-resolved profiling, oxygen-source isotope labeling, and strategies to stabilize or trap reactive intermediates. ,

More broadly, elucidating resistance chemistry helps connect biosynthetic self-protection to general principles that can inform antimicrobial strategies. In this context, Albo65 extends the theme of flavin-dependent enzymatic antibiotic inactivation and highlights how oxidative tailoring can couple pathway-associated self-protection with scaffold remodeling. Defining substrate tolerance and reaction outcomes may enable repurposing Albo65-like monooxygenases for controlled oxidative transformations of aromatic small molecules.

We also demonstrated the practical use of the albofungin–albo65 pair as a selection system in yeasts where albofungin achieves effective selection at much lower concentrations than commonly used antibiotics such as G418. Beyond its dosage advantage, this system expands the repertoire of available selection markers, which is important for combinatorial and multiplex strain engineering, especially in nonmodel hosts where marker recycling systems remain underdeveloped. Although the commercial cost of albofungin is relatively high, its low dosage requirement partially offsets this limitation. In addition, albofungin can be produced via fermentation at titers exceeding 100 mg/L, highlighting its potential for scalable and cost-effective production. With further optimization of biosynthetic processes and increased demand, we anticipate that production costs will decrease, thereby improving its practicality for broader research and industrial applications.

In conclusion, we identified the FAD-dependent monooxygenase Albo65 as a key factor in the self-resistance in albofungin biosynthesis and defined its functional role through gene deletion, complementation, and in vitro biochemical characterization. In addition, we developed a highly sensitive selection system based on the albofungin-albo65 pair for basic and applied biological studies of yeasts. This study has laid the foundation for further mechanistic studies and potential biotechnological applications of this unique FAD-dependent monooxygenase.

Methods

Direct Cloning of the albo BGC

The albo BGC was cloned via the CAPTURE (Cas12a-assisted precise targeted cloning using in vivo Cre-lox recombination) method. Briefly, the S. monomycini NRRL B-24309 strain was cultured at 30 °C in 100 mL of modified MYG medium (10 g L–1 malt extract broth, 4 g L–1 yeast extract, 4 g L–1 glucose) using 250 mL baffled flasks containing glass beads. The culture was harvested by centrifugation at 3220 × g for 15 min. The direct cloning experiments, including the genomic DNA isolation, in vitro guide RNA transcription, DNA receiver preparation, genomic DNA digestion and Cre-lox in vivo recombination, were performed as described elsewhere. The cloned BGC (pBE48-albo) was then transformed into E. coli WM6026 (supplemented with 40 μg mL–1 2,6- diaminopimelic acid for growth) and conjugated into S. albus J1074 for heterologous expression using the protocol described elsewhere.

Fermentation, Extraction, and Isolation of Albofungin in Wild Type and Mutant Strains

The fermentation of S. albus J1074 harboring the wild type albo BGC or its mutants was performed in MYM medium (malt extract 10 g L–1, yeast extract 4 g L–1, maltose 4 g L–1, pH 7.2–7.4) at 30 °C for 3–5 days. Fermentation cultures (5.0 L) were harvested and separated into supernatants and mycelia by centrifugation. The supernatants were extracted with an equal volume of ethyl acetate. After the removal of the ethyl acetate by evaporation under vacuum, the crude extract was further separated and purified by semipreparative HPLC.

Antimicrobial Assays

The antimicrobial activities of compound 1 were measured against 9 bacteria and 10 fungi indicator strains (Figure d) using the broth microdilution method. B. halodurans, B. subtilis and M. luteus were grown in 5 mL of Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) at 30 °C. E. cloacae and P. fluorescens were grown in Nutrient Broth (NB) at 28–30 °C. Other bacterial strains were grown in Brian Heart Infusion (BHI) at 37 °C. Fungi were inoculated from glycerol stocks and grown in 5 mL of YPAD on a rotary shaker (250 rpm) at 30 °C. Overnight cultures were diluted into fresh sterilized medium and regrown to achieve an optical absorbance of 0.04 – 0.06 at 600 nm. The cells were diluted 10-fold in fresh medium to give the final concentration of ≈106 cfu mL–1 and were distributed into 96-well microtiter plates, which were supplemented with compounds ranging from 64 to 0.02 μg mL–1. The Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MIC) value was the concentration that resulted in no visible growth after 16–18 h cultivation. All experiments were tested in triplicates. Ampicillin (Amp) and amphotericin B were used as positive controls for antibacterial and antifungal assays, respectively.

Construction of albo Gene Expression Strains

Biosynthetic gene segments from albo were amplified from the pBE48-albo by PCR and cloned into pIJ10257 plasmid under the ermE*p promoter using the HiFi DNA assembly kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). Colonies were grown in LB supplemented with 100 μg mL–1 hygromycin at 37 °C overnight. Correct clones were verified by colony PCR, and the plasmid DNA was purified using QIAGEN Plasmid Midi kit (Qiagen, Germany) and confirmed by whole plasmid sequencing. Correct plasmids were transformed into E. coli WM6026 and conjugated into S. albus J1074.

Overexpression and Purification of Albo65

The albo65 gene was PCR-amplified from the plasmid pBE48-albo and cloned into the vector pET28a. After sequencing confirmation, the recombinant plasmid was introduced into E. coli BL21­(DE3) to produce the N-(His)6-tagged Albo65 protein. Single colony was used to inoculate 5 mL LB medium containing 50 μg mL–1 kanamycin and grown overnight at 37 °C at 250 rpm. Each 100 mL of fresh LB medium with the same antibiotic selection was inoculated with 1 mL of the overnight culture and incubated at 37 °C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.4–0.6. Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by further incubation for 18–24 h at 16 °C. E. coli cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, washed with deionized water, and suspended in 20 mM Tris-Cl, 1 M NaCl, followed by sonication on ice. Lysed cells were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 50 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant, as crude enzyme, was purified with the following gradient buffer system: buffer A (20 mM Tris-Cl, 1 M NaCl) and buffer B (20 mM Tris-Cl, 1 M NaCl, 500 mM imidazole) from 0% B to 100% B. Fractions containing the recombinant protein were analyzed on sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and concentrated with Amicon centrifugal filter units (at suitable molecular weight cut-offs) to around 2 mL. Concentrated proteins were loaded to Cytiva PD-10 columns and eluted by storage buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 5% (v/v) glycerol, pH 7.5). Proteins were flash frozen by liquid nitrogen and stored at – 80 °C until further use.

In Vitro Enzymatic Assays for Albo65

A typical in vitro enzymatic reaction of Albo65 was conducted in 100 μL Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, pH 7.5) comprising 200 μM albofungin, 2 mM NADPH, 10 μM Albo65. The reactions were incubated for 30 min at 30 °C and then were quenched by mixing with 100 μL of ice-cold MeOH. For oxygen-dependence assays, an identical reaction was assembled under oxygen-depleted conditions using degassed buffer and anaerobic handling procedures, and incubated in parallel with the aerobic control. Reactions were quenched in the same manner and analyzed by LC-MS. When needed, the reaction mixtures were further purified by semipreparative HPLC. For disk diffusion evaluation of Albo65-treated albofungin, reaction mixtures were used directly as described in Figure S12.
