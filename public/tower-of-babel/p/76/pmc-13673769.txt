Recurrent purulent cardiac tamponade caused by rupture of an anterior mediastinal teratoma: a case report
Authors: Jun Gao, Wenyu Zhang, Pan Gao, Hairong Chang, Liye Shao, Jianjun Zhang, Jinrong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mediastinal teratomas are rare germ cell tumors that may present with acute complications, such as rupture, resulting in purulent pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade. Prompt recognition and intervention are essential to prevent life-threatening deterioration. A 21-year-old previously healthy man presented with acute severe chest pain, dyspnea, and dry cough. Initial chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a 9 × 7 cm anterior mediastinal mass suggestive of teratoma, with minimal pericardial effusion confirmed by echocardiography. Vital signs indicated tachycardia and tachypnea, accompanied by leukocytosis but normal cardiac markers. Within 16 h, the patient developed fever, hypotension, hypoxemia, and oliguria, progressing to septic shock. Bedside ultrasound revealed a large pericardial effusion with signs of tamponade, leading to urgent ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis that drained purulent fluid. Tumor aspiration confirmed rupture into the pericardial sac, yielding similar purulent material containing hair and fatty globules. Hemodynamic stabilization followed, but the effusion reaccumulated by day 7, requiring repeat pericardiocentesis via a left lateral approach. On hospital day 30, surgical resection revealed a fistula connecting the mass to the pericardium, along with intraoperative findings of pus, hair, sebum, and adhesions. Pathology confirmed a mixed germ cell tumor (predominantly mature teratoma with 5% seminoma). No adjuvant therapy was required. This case highlights the rare yet fulminant complication of mediastinal teratoma rupture leading to purulent pericarditis and tamponade. Bedside ultrasound-guided interventions were pivotal in management, emphasizing the importance of serial monitoring and multidisciplinary care. Introduction

Mediastinal teratomas are rare extragonadal germ cell tumors that predominantly originate from primordial germ cells in the anterior mediastinum [1]. These cells are pluripotent and can differentiate into components of all three germ layers. Consequently, the resulting tumors typically contain various heterologous tissues, such as fat, hair, bone, teeth, or skin [2]. Most mediastinal teratomas are histologically mature and benign, with low potential for malignant transformation [3]. Although rare overall, mediastinal teratomas account for approximately 15% of anterior mediastinal tumors in adults and 25% in children [4]. This makes them one of the more common anterior mediastinal tumors and a major subtype of mediastinal germ cell tumors. Most mediastinal teratomas are asymptomatic and often incidentally discovered during imaging examinations. Symptomatic cases typically result from tumor compression of adjacent structures, leading to chest pain, cough, or dyspnea, rather than invasive growth [5].

Acute cardiac tamponade due to spontaneous tumor rupture into the pericardial cavity is a life-threatening complication of mediastinal teratoma. These events are rare but documented in pediatric and adult patients [6–8]. Tumor rupture typically causes massive pericardial effusion and rapidly progressive cardiac tamponade. This complication carries high mortality if not promptly diagnosed and managed. Emergency pericardial drainage relieves cardiac compression, followed by surgical tumor resection. These interventions stabilize the patient and prevent effusion recurrence. We report a case of a 21-year-old man who developed severe purulent pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade from spontaneous rupture of an anterior mediastinal mixed germ cell tumor into the pericardium. Unlike prior cases, this patient had progressive recurrence of pericardial effusion while awaiting surgery. We alleviated cardiac tamponade via two ultrasound-guided pericardial punctures with catheter drainage using different approaches, maintaining stability until surgery. This case underscores that mediastinal tumors, even those mainly comprising mature teratoma, can cause fatal complications. It also highlights the value of early bedside ultrasound for timely detection of cardiac tamponade and staged, individualized management—including repeated pericardial drainage and radical surgery—for mediastinal tumor rupture emergencies.

Case presentation

A 21-year-old male college student presented to the emergency department with acute severe chest pain of 6 h’ duration, accompanied by dyspnea and dry cough, but no fever. He was previously healthy, with no significant past medical or family history.

Physical examination showed acute distress, with blood pressure 136/72 mmHg, heart rate 118 bpm, respiratory rate 29 breaths/min, and temperature 37.0 °C. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were normal except for faint bilateral basal rales.

Initial emergency room evaluation included chest computed tomography (CT) (Figure. S1), revealing a well-circumscribed anterior mediastinal mass (9 × 7 cm) adjacent to the inferior aspect of the heart, with minimal pericardial effusion. Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) confirmed pericardial effusion depth < 10 mm. Based on these findings, he was diagnosed with a suspected teratoma in the anterior mediastinum and admitted to thoracic surgery for management.

Laboratory studies showed leukocytosis (white blood cell count 18.44 × 109/L, 86.9% neutrophils), platelet count 304 × 109/L, and hemoglobin 128 g/L. Myocardial injury markers (D-dimer, Creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme (CK-MB), Cardiac troponin I (cTnI), N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP)) were normal. Arterial blood gas (no supplemental oxygen) showed pH 7.27, partial pressure of arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2) 37 mmHg, partial pressure of arterial oxygen (PaO2) 108 mmHg, base excess (BE) −9.4 mmol/L, potassium (K+) 4.1 mmol/L, sodium (Na+) 137 mmol/L, and lactate (Lac) 1.4 mmol/L.

While awaiting surgery, his condition deteriorated rapidly within 16 h. He developed fever (38 °C), tachypnea (40 breaths/min), tachycardia (145 bpm), hypotension (92/60 mmHg), and oliguria for 6 h. Pulse oximetry showed 90% saturation despite mask oxygen (8 L/min). Repeat arterial blood gas showed pH 7.26, PaCO2 18.1 mmHg, PaO2 77.5 mmHg, K+ 6.9 mmol/L, BE −17.3 mmol/L, and Lac 5.8 mmol/L. Despite aggressive fluid resuscitation, no improvement occurred, leading to intensive care unit (ICU) transfer.

On ICU admission, bedside ultrasound revealed large pericardial effusion with tamponade signs (Fig. 1a and d, Video S1a-1d). Signs included diastolic effusion > 20 mm, swinging heart, dilated inferior vena cava (IVC) without respiratory variation, right atrial collapse (late diastolic/early systolic), and right ventricular collapse (early diastolic). Urgent ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis was performed via parasternal long-axis view (Figure. Discussion

This report describes a rare case of acute cardiac tamponade in a young male patient, resulting from the rupture of an anterior mediastinal mature teratoma into the pericardium. Anterior mediastinal teratomas are usually benign and asymptomatic. Spontaneous rupture into adjacent structures is extremely rare, and invasion into the pericardium leading to pericardial effusion is even less common. Less than 1% of benign mediastinal teratomas involve rupture of cyst contents into the pericardium [9]. Previous reports indicate that teratoma rupture into the bronchial tree may manifest as hemoptysis or trichoptysis, the latter serving as a diagnostic hallmark for airway involvement [10]. In contrast, this case involved tumor rupture into the pericardial cavity, resulting in massive pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade, without respiratory symptoms. This manifestation of teratoma rupture as cardiac tamponade is exceedingly rare and represents a severe complication.

The mechanism of spontaneous teratoma rupture remains incompletely understood. Some researchers propose that teratomas secrete digestive enzymes (e.g., amylase) internally. These enzymes continuously erode adjacent tissues, forming fistulas and allowing tumor contents to enter the pericardium or pleural cavity [11]. Other reports indicate that secondary infection within the teratoma may trigger rupture [12]. Teratoma contents (e.g., sebum and hair) are inherently inflammatory and irritative [13]. Rupture into the pericardium can induce purulent pericarditis and massive effusion. In this case, the pericardial effusion was purulent. Hair and fatty substances were found in the tumor puncture irrigation fluid, confirming entry of cyst contents into the pericardium. Similarly, recent literature describes a case of infected mediastinal teratoma rupture in a 12-year-old child. Pericardial puncture drained a large volume of purulent fluid [14]. Surgery confirmed a pus-filled mature teratoma. Therefore, infection may play a crucial role in teratoma rupture. Tumor rupture into the pericardial cavity often rapidly causes pericardial empyema and cardiac tamponade, leading to life-threatening complications.

Cardiac tamponade is a life-threatening emergency. It arises from rapid fluid accumulation in the pericardial cavity, which elevates intrapericardial pressure. This restricts diastolic heart filling and reduces cardiac output [15]. Typical clinical manifestations include hypotension, tachycardia, jugular venous distension, muffled heart sounds, and pulsus paradoxus. However, in this case, the patient initially presented with chest pain and dyspnea. This was followed by hypoperfusion signs, including hypotension and oliguria. This suggests rapid progression of pericardial effusion to tamponade. Bedside ultrasound is crucial for diagnosing cardiac tamponade. Echocardiography may show massive pericardial effusion with cardiac chamber compression signs. These include late diastolic right atrial collapse and early diastolic right ventricular collapse. Additional findings are IVC dilation with minimal inspiratory variation and a swinging heart. These features facilitate timely tamponade recognition and guide interventions [16]. In this case, bedside ultrasound promptly detected the occult rapid increase in pericardial effusion and right heart compression signs. This allowed time for emergency management.

Pericardial puncture and drainage is an emergency intervention for cardiac tamponade. It rapidly reduces intrapericardial pressure and restores hemodynamic stability. In this case, the patient underwent successful ultrasound-guided pericardial puncture under ICU monitoring. A single drainage removed approximately 900 mL of purulent pericardial fluid. This resulted in marked improvement in hemodynamic status. Similar literature reports describe symptom relief in adult patients after draining approximately 800 mL of brownish pericardial effusion. This facilitated subsequent surgery [8]. However, drainage alone does not address the underlying cause. If the tumor continues leaking contents into the pericardium, effusion may recur. In this case, ultrasound on day 7 post-initial drainage showed effusion recurrence. This was accompanied by hemodynamic changes, including elevated NT-proBNP levels and increased left ventricular outflow tract VTI. This indicated ongoing cardiac dysfunction due to residual pericardial fluid. Consequently, a second pericardial puncture and drainage was performed to relieve tamponade symptoms. Therefore, before tumor resection, close monitoring of pericardial effusion is essential. Repeated drainage may be required to maintain stability.

In this case, the access route was selected from the window with the largest accessible fluid pocket and the shortest safe needle path while avoiding the myocardium, pleura, liver, and tumor. The first procedure used a parasternal long-axis route because the enlarged left hepatic lobe made the subcostal route unsafe. At recurrence, the effusion was predominantly posterior; the left parasternal, subcostal, apical, and para-apical windows were inadequate, and the right parasternal window was close to the tumor. The left lateral fourth-intercostal route provided the safest access. These changes show why the puncture window should be reassessed before each drainage procedure.

Studies highlight that anterior mediastinal tumors invading the pericardium pose a life-threatening risk necessitating urgent intervention [17]. Complete surgical resection is the sole curative strategy. It simultaneously alleviates compression, eradicates the infection source, and prevents recurrence. Complete tumor excision confirms histological diagnosis and markedly enhances patient prognosis [18]. In this case, following adequate drainage for infection control, the patient underwent tumor resection and pericardial defect repair on admission day 30. Intraoperatively, fistulous communication between the tumor cyst and pericardial cavities was confirmed. Extensive pericardial adhesions were also noted, aligning with preoperative ultrasound findings. Post-surgery, the patient recovered uneventfully. Postoperative pathology confirmed a mixed germ cell tumor, predominantly mature teratoma with a 5% seminoma component. Mediastinal germ cell tumors are histologically divided into seminomas and nonseminomatous subtypes, including embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumor, choriocarcinoma, teratoma, and mixed forms. Mature teratomas are benign and usually cured by surgical resection alone, showing minimal response to chemotherapy [1]. Seminomas respond well to radiotherapy and chemotherapy. They are often treated with chemotherapy-based multimodal approaches [19]. For this predominantly mature teratoma-based mixed tumor, the minimal seminoma component was fully resected. Thus, no postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy was needed, only close follow-up.

For this mixed tumor, predominantly mature teratoma, international guidelines suggest omitting postoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy if complete resection is achieved without evident malignancy. Close follow-up is sufficient [1, 20]. In this case, tumor markers remained normal throughout. The seminoma component was minimal and fully resected. Thus, strict postoperative follow-up was employed without adjuvant therapy. Literature indicates that mature teratomas have an excellent prognosis after complete resection, with 5-year survival rates nearing 100% [21]. In this case, the patient was followed for 14 postoperative months. No recurrence, metastasis, or new lesions were detected on imaging. This underscores the vital role of timely surgical resection in managing these rare complications. In summary, for young patients with anterior mediastinal tumors and unexplained pericardial effusion, teratoma rupture should be considered. Timely imaging and interventions are crucial for enhancing prognosis and saving lives.

Conclusion

This case highlights the rare yet rapidly progressive complication of mediastinal teratoma rupture, which manifests as purulent pericarditis and cardiac tamponade. Bedside ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis was crucial for initial hemodynamic stabilization. Serial monitoring enabled timely detection of effusion reaccumulation. Multidisciplinary intervention, culminating in surgical resection, confirmed a pericardial fistula and mixed germ cell tumor histology. This obviated the need for adjuvant therapy. At the 14-month follow-up, the patient showed no evidence of recurrence or metastasis. This outcome underscores the efficacy of prompt recognition and staged management in optimizing results for this life-threatening condition.
