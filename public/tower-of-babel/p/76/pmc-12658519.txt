Functional Outcomes, Lung health and Livelihood Outcomes among people With Tuberculosis (FOLLOW-TB): study protocol for a multicentre, prospective cohort study in Canada
Authors: Jonathon R. Campbell, Allita Rodrigues, Eri Flores, Kamila Romanowski, Tenzin Kunor, Zolelwa Sifumba, Dick Menzies, Kevin Schwartzman, Zofia Zysman-Colman, Andrea Benedetti, James C. Johnston
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTuberculosis (TB) is a major global cause of morbidity and mortality. Emerging evidence in high-burden settings suggests significant long-term sequelae among people surviving TB; however, evidence from high-income, low-TB burden settings like Canada is lacking. In a person with TB infection, provision of TB preventive treatment (TPT) can prevent TB disease and its sequelae, but remains underused. We propose the Functional Outcomes, Lung health and Livelihood Outcomes among people With Tuberculosis study, a multicentre, prospective cohort study in Canada to help improve our understanding of the impacts of TPT and TB disease on individuals.Methods and analysisThis is a prospective cohort study taking place in Montreal and Vancouver, Canada. We aim to recruit and retain at least 120 people with microbiologically confirmed TB disease, 340 people treated for TB infection and 120 without TB disease or infection who will be considered our unexposed group. All participants must be ≥6 years of age. Participants with TB disease or infection will be recruited within 2 weeks of treatment initiation. We will follow-up unexposed participants and participants with TB disease for 24 months, and participants with TB infection for 12 months. Throughout follow-up, participants will complete assessments measuring lung health and function, quality of life, disability, dyspnoea, psychological distress, as well as changes in employment and direct and indirect costs incurred because of treatment. Among participants with TB disease, our primary outcome is the difference in quality-adjusted life years between participants with TB disease and those unexposed at 24 months. For participants with TB infection, our primary outcome is the identification of non-patient characteristics (eg, patient cost, quality of life) associated with participant decision to discontinue treatment. Patient partners have contributed to the design of the study and will be involved with the study through to its dissemination.Ethics and disseminationThis study has been approved by institutional ethics review boards at The Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (2025–10344) and The University of British Columbia (H24-02071). All participants will provide informed consent (and assent, if required) prior to participating in the study. We will disseminate study results to participants, national and international organisations, and through open-access peer-reviewed academic journals and conferences.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global and public health problem. An estimated 1.7 billion people have evidence of (latent, asymptomatic) TB infection1 and more than 10 million of these people progress to TB disease every year, 1.25 million losing their lives as a result in 2023.2 There were an estimated 155 million TB survivors in 2020 (ie, people considered cured of TB disease) equivalent to 1 in 50 people globally.3 Recent literature has highlighted the increased mortality risk of TB survivors,4 as well as the potential for disability and its impact on functional ability and health-related quality of life (HRQoL),5 6 and the significant financial impacts of TB disease on patients and families/caregivers.7 Yet, studies have traditionally framed TB disease as an acute, but ‘curable’ illness, with limited costs and no long-term impacts for those ‘cured’. The broader health and economic impacts of TB disease remain neglected, impeding appropriate clinical care and development of supportive systems and structures necessary for persons affected by TB disease.

Most TB literature analyses TB disease from diagnosis to treatment completion, with a focus on end-of-treatment outcomes. Unfortunately, the impacts of TB disease extend beyond these outcomes and do not fall neatly within the boundaries of these phases. There are often significant delays between symptom onset and diagnosis of TB disease, and healthcare use sharply increases in the 6-month period prior to diagnosis.8 Meanwhile, TB survivors have mortality rates 3.8 times higher than matched controls in the 5 years after TB treatment completion4 and are more likely to experience morbidities such as depression6 and pulmonary disease.5 TB disease also impacts people’s HRQoL and ability to function socially and professionally.9 Finally, although the health system costs of TB disease are known to be considerable in Canada ($C15 772 per person10; 23 times more expensive than TB preventive treatment (TPT)), these figures do not consider costs borne by patients and families/caregivers, such as lost wages, unemployment, childcare, travel and out-of-pocket medical costs—which may be substantial.7 Data quantifying these costs are completely absent in Canada, obscuring the larger impact of TB disease on affected populations.

The pervasive framing of TB disease as an acute, curable illness with minimal long-term impacts acts as a significant barrier to scaling up TB prevention. One of the most effective ways of preventing TB disease—and its consequences—is providing TPT to people with TB infection, making it a key component of WHO strategies for TB elimination.11 But despite clear evidence of the effectiveness of TPT in preventing disease (reducing risk by ∼90%), a minority of people with TB infection receive TPT—only ∼7300 people per year among the more than 2 million people estimated to have TB infection in Canada.12 This is influenced by the perceived limited and uncertain benefits of TB prevention compared with the known risks of adverse events with TPT, as the number needed to treat to prevent one person with TB disease varies by population from fewer than 10 to several hundred.13

Indeed, the adverse events, costs and other potential consequences of TPT may disrupt patients’ lives and act as important barriers to completion. TPT regimens are 3–9 months in duration and even with the shorter of these regimens, 20–30% of people discontinue treatment prior to completion.14 Patient characteristics are inconsistently associated with discontinuation,15 suggesting other factors influence this key determinant of TPT benefit. These may include costs to patients (eg, attending clinical follow-ups, missed work/school) and medication safety as TPT may cause serious adverse events (occurring in 2–5% of people depending on regimen) and medication intolerance (occurring in 50–75%).16–19 There may also be psychological and social consequences as well. A diagnosis of TB infection may cause mental distress due to uncertainty of the diagnosis and fears of future progression to TB disease,20 while reviews have highlighted health-related stigma among people with TB infection, largely due to its conflation in social circles with TB disease.21 These issues highlight a need to better characterise the consequences of TPT, which may act as barriers to completion.

We propose a multicentre, prospective cohort study in Canada to better understand the costs and consequences of treating both TB disease and infection, with the goal for these data to inform patient support and clinical care, as well as motivate the scale-up of TB prevention programmes.

Methods and analysis

The Functional Outcomes, Lung health and Livelihood Outcomes among people With Tuberculosis (FOLLOW-TB) study is a multicentre prospective, longitudinal cohort study to characterise the consequences of TB infection and disease in Canada. Canada is a low TB incidence country with a TB incidence of 5.5 per 100 000 in 2023.22 Approximately 80% of TB in Canada occurs among people born outside of Canada, and 95% occurs in people at least 15 years of age. The two participating sites are the Montreal Chest Institute in Montreal, Quebec, and the British Columbia Centre for Disease Control (BCCDC) in Vancouver, British Columbia. The Montreal Chest Institute is primarily a centre for TB screening and surveillance for newly arrived adult migrants to the Montreal area in Quebec, Canada, while the BCCDC runs a centralised provincial TB programme with two TB clinics in the Vancouver area. Together, the two sites provide treatment for ∼310 people with TB disease and ∼900 people with TB infection annually, the vast majority of people born outside of Canada and over 18 years of age.

Study oversight and partners

Overall project coordination will be at The Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre in Montreal, Canada. Study principal investigators (JRC for Montreal, JJ for Vancouver) are responsible for the day-to-day operations of the study at their respective sites, with the support of dedicated research coordinators and staff. Study monitoring will be performed by the coordinating institution on a regular basis, through routine review of the data collected and monitoring visits to participating sites; a quarterly review of study progress will occur between the coordinating institution and participating sites.

The scientific direction of the study is guided by a Scientific Advisory Committee (SAC) consisting of five arms-length experts in the field of TB. The SAC meets with the study principal investigators annually to review study progress and results and provide advice. Two people with lived experience (TK, ZS) are partnered with the study team. They meet with the study team at least annually; they have been involved in the development of the study design and procedures and will be engaged throughout the study as well as providing input into results interpretation and dissemination.

Design

This prospective, longitudinal cohort study will recruit participants being treated for TB infection or TB disease. As our primary outcome among participants with TB disease is comparative (see below), we will also recruit a population without TB and unexposed to treatment. Participants with TB infection will be followed for 12 months from enrolment, while unexposed participants and participants with TB disease will be followed for 24 months from enrolment. Time points for evaluation are as follows: date of recruitment, and after month 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24 (the latter two only for unexposed participants and participants with TB disease), which we have selected to coincide as closely as possible with routine follow-up visits during treatment. We will conduct all evaluations within 2 weeks of the specified time point in the first 6 months, and within 4 weeks of the specified time point thereafter. Most evaluations performed after the time of recruitment can be done remotely and we will offer this option to participants. Participants will be compensated $C20 cash per visit. Additional compensation of the same value will be provided for any event-based additional evaluations. Recruitment began in May 2025; we anticipate recruitment concluding in April 2027 (for TB disease and unexposed participants) or April 2028 (for TB infection) or once our target sample size is reached. We plan to complete all study follow-ups by April 2029.

Participant inclusion criteria

We will recruit consecutive, eligible, consenting participants initiating treatment for TB infection or TB disease over a 24-month period at the two participating sites. Inclusion criteria will be persons (1) ≥6 years of age; (2) with microbiologically confirmed TB disease within 2 weeks of treatment initiation (TB disease) or initiating treatment for TB infection in the past 2 weeks and with a positive tuberculin skin test or interferon-gamma release assay within the last 6 months (TB infection); (3) ability to understand and communicate in English, French, Simplified Chinese, Creole or Punjabi (these latter three are the most common languages spoken by people treated for TB at the participating sites); (4) do not belong to a group routinely tested for TB as part of their occupation (eg, some healthcare workers); (5) do not have plans to relocate outside of the province in the next 12 months. In the case of children aged <10 years initiating treatment for TB disease, they may also be included if they are not microbiologically confirmed but clinically diagnosed and meet the criteria for intrathoracic TB, which requires meeting at least two of the following criteria: (1) symptoms/signs suggestive of TB, (2) chest radiograph consistent with TB and (3) close TB exposure or immunological evidence of TB infection.23 For exclusively extrapulmonary TB disease, we will accept detection of acid-fast bacilli in histopathological staining of affected tissue or presence of necrotising granulomas with clinical evidence as confirmation.

Our unexposed population will be recruited from the two participating sites and composed of people similar to people treated for TB disease at our sites, but who do not have TB disease (presently or in the past) and are not initiating any form of TB treatment and meet inclusion criteria (1), (3) and (5) described above. Unexposed participants will be non-household contacts of people with TB disease, but who do not have TB infection or disease themselves or those who present to either site as an immigration medical surveillance referral who do not have TB, or significant respiratory conditions, or radiographic abnormalities, or those who are referred to one of our sites after being evaluated for TB as part of new, permanent employment.

We will review characteristics of participants with TB disease and unexposed participants routinely (after the first 30 participants and then every 20 participants thereafter) to ensure balance and adjust how we target recruitment accordingly. We will exclude persons with an altered mental state preventing informed consent.

Participant recruitment and sample size

Participants will be identified in two ways: by treating clinicians who refer them to the study team or by prescreening potential participants through medical record review and inviting those potentially eligible to meet the study team. The study will be advertised at each site and presented to clinicians to promote referral and recruitment.

We hope to recruit 25% of people initiating TB disease or TB infection treatment. We expect dropout rates to be variable between groups given the differential follow-up. We will conservatively estimate dropout to be 15% in the first year and an additional 8% in the second year. These were based on a 5% dropout rate seen in a recent randomised trial with a 28-month follow-up at both participating sites,14 which we inflated to account for the increased and consistent participant demand in our study. Retention will be promoted through: (1) providing $C20 cash per visit, to help offset time and travel costs; and (2) providing flexible data collection, with many evaluations possible to complete remotely. We will perform monitoring to identify potential issues with retention or participant fatigue with the questionnaires: we will monitor the number of reminders sent to participants before follow-up questionnaires are completed; we will monitor the number of missed follow-ups; we will monitor the consistency of responses across questionnaires (ie, do questionnaires contradict each other); and we will informally speak to participants at follow-ups about their feelings on study time requirements.

Given the BCCDC treats more people for TB disease and the Montreal Chest Institute treats more people for TB infection, we have structured recruitment accordingly. In Montreal, we aim to recruit 30 people with TB disease, 232 with TB infection and 78 unexposed participants during the study, with 23, 197 and 60 completing study follow-ups, respectively. In Vancouver, we aim to recruit 126 people with TB disease, 170 with TB infection and 78 unexposed participants during the study, with 97, 143 and 60 completing study follow-ups. Therefore, the total number of participants we aim to recruit at the Montreal Chest Institute is 340 (280 retained) and at the BCCDC is 374 (300 retained).

The primary outcome in people with TB disease is the difference in total quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) over the 24-month study period compared with unexposed participants. To detect a difference of 0.113 QALYs over the study period9 with 80% power, type I error rate of 5% and accounting for clustering, we need 92 people each with TB disease and unexposed. The primary outcome among participants with TB infection is non-patient characteristics (eg, costs, disability, psychological distress) associated with TPT discontinuation. With 340 participants, we expect 102 discontinuations, which is sufficient to evaluate 10 covariates. Quantifying health system and patient costs is also a key outcome. Sample sizes of 120 for TB disease and 340 for TB infection permit estimation of these costs with a precision of ±$C4200 and ±$C78, respectively.10

Study procedures and outcomes

All potential participants referred to the study team (either through referral or identification in medical record review) will be screened for eligibility by a member of the research team. Those deemed eligible will be told about the study and consent will be collected from participants ≥18 years. For people aged 6–17 years, consent will be collected from guardians and the participant will also need to provide assent. Lung health and functional outcomes

We will evaluate lung health and functional outcomes using several assessments. We will perform pulmonary function testing, including spirometry, plethysmography and diffusing capacity of carbon monoxide, perform a 6 min walking test, administer the St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire25 or Liverpool Respiratory Symptom Questionnaire26 (age-dependent), and evaluate dyspnoea using the modified Medical Research Council questionnaire,27 and cough and other respiratory symptoms using a study-developed questionnaire (see online supplemental appendix). Pulmonary function testing will be done once as early as possible after recruitment for unexposed participants, and 6, 12 and 24 months after recruitment for participants with TB disease.

Financial and other health outcomes

To assess financial and other health outcomes, other assessments will be performed. We will interview participants to estimate direct medical and non-medical expenditures and indirect costs (eg, lost income) associated with prediagnosis, treatment and post-treatment phases, as well as changes in employment. To complement these estimates, at the end of the study, we will conduct medical chart reviews to estimate healthcare utilisation and health system costs associated with TB for each participant. We will assess quality of life using EuroQol 5-dimension 5-level (EQ-5D-5L) in adults and EQ-5D-Y-5L in participants <16 years of age.28 Psychological distress will be measured using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10)29 or Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale for Children30 (age-dependent), and disruptions to school and work will be measured using the Sheehan Disability Scale.31

Event-based measurements

Given the paucity of data on the impacts of adverse events during TB infection and disease treatment on participants’ HRQoL, financial and other outcomes, we will schedule a virtual follow-up visit with any participant discontinuing treatment for TB infection or disease due to an adverse event as soon as possible after they occur. We will administer an EQ-5D asking specifically about the participant’s experience during the adverse event and will collect information, if relevant, on when symptoms started, the duration of impact, when the symptoms resolved and when quality of life returned to ‘normal’. We will collect specific information from participants on care received during the adverse event and impacts on their indirect and direct costs (see online supplemental appendix). In addition, we will speak to the treating physician to gather their judgement on whether the event was treatment-related (not related, unlikely, possibly, probably) and the type and severity of the event.

Reasons for premature discontinuation of TPT and TB disease treatment are also not well understood. If a participant prematurely discontinues TPT or TB disease treatment due to reasons other than an adverse event, the study team will attempt to schedule a virtual follow-up as soon as possible to collect additional information. We will administer a specific questionnaire aimed to understand the influence of several potential factors that could play a role in a participant’s decision to discontinue treatment: duration, tolerability, burden of monitoring, out-of-pocket costs, opinion on value or necessity of treatment has changed or a free-text option participants can complete. We will ask participants to rank the options in terms of importance (see online supplemental appendix).

Long-term outcomes

Observing the long-term consequences of TB requires extended follow-up, as some consequences will not necessarily be observed in 2 years. These consequences include cardiovascular disease, chronic lung infections, some airway diseases, lung cancers and mental health disorders. While many of these can be detected during our currently planned follow-up period, they can also be detected in health administrative data from healthcare utilisation.5 32 Though we plan to seek additional funding to extend follow-up of participants, we will ask participants to consent to allow the study team to access their data held by the entities responsible for holding health administrative data at participating sites (eg, Population Data BC in British Columbia and Santé Québec in Quebec). The time period for the study team to access participant data will be 10 years, to allow for extended follow-up of participants, their long-term health outcomes and their healthcare utilisation. Consent to this long-term follow-up is not necessary to participate in the rest of the study.

Other outcomes

We plan to expand the number of sites and scope of FOLLOW-TB, as well as implement substudies over the course of the study. For example, one such substudy includes biobanking plasma and peripheral blood mononuclear cells from all participants for analyses in the future. Another substudy includes leveraging qualitative methods to understand TB survivor needs and co-develop post-TB care recommendations. Substudies will be developed in collaboration with our patient partners and other collaborators and reviewed by our SAC before implementing. Additional consent and/or assent forms will be developed and administered as appropriate.

Data management and monitoring

All participant data will be collected securely in a REDCap33 database developed specifically for this study. We will collect information necessary to contact participants, as well as allow Santé Québec and Population Data BC to identify them for the purposes of long-term follow-up. All completed questionnaires and evaluations will be entered into developed electronic case report forms in REDCap. The REDCap database will be secured, and access only permitted to the principal investigators and study coordinators. Data will be monitored and reviewed periodically by coordinators and the principal investigator at the Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (JRC); phone and written communication between sites will be used as required. At least twice during the study, the lead study coordinator and principal investigator at the Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (AR and JRC) will visit the BCCDC for monitoring visits.

Analysis

We have preplanned analyses around the primary outcomes in this study for each population: participants with TB disease and TB infection. Our primary outcome in TB disease is the overall difference in QALYs between unexposed participants and participants with TB disease, while in TB infection the primary outcome is non-patient characteristics associated with TPT discontinuation.

For each unexposed participant and participant with TB disease, we will use algorithms derived from a Canadian reference population to convert EQ-5D-5L responses to health utilities in order to calculate QALYs.34 This analysis will be limited to adult participants unless an algorithm for those <16 years is developed before analysis begins.35 QALY estimates between visits will be linearly interpolated and an overall QALY estimate will be generated by calculating the area under the curve. We will use linear mixed models, treating study site as a random intercept, to compare overall QALYs between participants with TB disease and unexposed participants, adjusting for demographic and clinical factors. Given we expect a large fraction of our participants to be born outside of Canada and recent immigrants, we will also explore different value sets for the EQ-5D-5L based on specific countries of origin to see how this impacts findings.

For participants with TB infection, we will exclude all participants discontinuing treatment because of a treatment-related adverse event (ie, participant stops treatment due to an adverse event the treating clinician deems probably or possibly related to treatment). We expect this to be ∼2% of all participants (seven total).17 This decision has been made as we are interested in factors associated with participant decision to discontinue treatment, rather than regimen-related factors. Given participants can discontinue treatment at any point and participants will have multiple follow-ups, we will define our outcome as premature discontinuation before the next study visit. We will use generalised linear mixed models, treating participant and study site as random intercepts, to estimate the odds of treatment discontinuation before the next follow-up visit. Models will be adjusted for non-patient characteristics (psychological distress, disability and HRQoL at the previous follow-up visit, and cumulative patient costs in relation to income), as well as demographic and clinical factors. The selection of factors to include in the model will be made prior to analysis, in consultation with our patient partners and SAC and based on the number of events that occur.

Other statistical analyses, such as those around lung function, breathlessness and psychological distress, will use descriptive and inferential methods and be developed throughout the study. We will plan these analyses prior to locking the database after the last participant has completed follow-up. Any additional analyses will be clearly specified as post hoc.

Methodological challenges and limitations

As with any prospective study, recruitment and retention of participants may become issues. After input from patient partners, we have already reduced the intensity of follow-ups and increased participant compensation to pre-emptively support recruitment and retention. We will monitor these metrics and work with site coordinators, our patient partners and others if follow-up appears too burdensome. Children and adolescents are under-represented in TB research; however, they also represent only 5% of the TB burden in Canada. We have included child and adolescent-appropriate questionnaires and are working with paediatric infectious disease clinicians at our sites to encourage their recruitment and gather data on an understudied group.

Identification of an unexposed group also represents a unique challenge. Given the expected demographic characteristics of people treated for TB disease at each site, we have carefully selected inclusion criteria for unexposed participants to align as closely as possible to those with TB disease. However, there may still be differences between groups, which we will describe and attempt to mitigate through adjusted statistical analyses. Missing data may also be an issue during the study, as participants may miss follow-up visits or data may not be recorded. We will periodically review data to attempt to complete missing information and will implement appropriate statistical methods in analysis, such as multiple imputation, to address missing data. Finally, over a 24-month follow-up period, people’s health may change, and new onset comorbid conditions or interventions (eg, physical therapy or pulmonary rehabilitation) may influence outcomes. At each visit, we ask participants about any new illnesses and healthcare utilisation and will review their medical records, supporting their consideration in analyses.

Patient and public involvement

We engaged three TB survivors (ie, people with lived experience) in the development of the study design and protocol, of which two (TK and ZS) have agreed to remain engaged throughout the conduct of the study. They will work closely with us on issues of recruitment, acceptability and new areas of focus, while also contributing to interpretation and dissemination of results. We will consult with our patient partners several times over the first 2 years of study recruitment as issues arise and need to be addressed, and then at least annually thereafter.

Ethics and dissemination

This cohort study was approved by institutional ethics review boards at The Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (2025–10344) and University of British Columbia (H24-02071). As described previously, all participants will be required to understand and sign informed consent forms, and if necessary, assent will also be required.

All study participants will be provided with a unique participant identification number; if more than one participant is from the same household, we will assign them a unique household code. These are intended to maintain confidentiality; unique participant identification numbers will be included on all questionnaires and no other identifiable information will be present. These identification numbers will be used to link different questionnaires with the same individual.

There are no risks to participants with respect to questionnaires, although some may find some of the questions upsetting or distressing, such as those around income and experiences with TB. We will inform participants that they can take the time they need to complete questionnaires and do not need to answer questions they find upsetting or distressing. There are minimal risks associated with some of the evaluations performed in this study. In all cases, medical care will be provided if necessary. In some cases, assessments may reveal abnormal results. We will share all abnormal findings with the participant’s treating physician at the study site so that appropriate care can be provided.

Outcomes of this study will be shared with participants who consented to this activity once our primary analysis is completed. We will develop an infographic summarising in lay terms the findings and their interpretation. This will cover all broad categories of data collected, summarising trends in health, quality of life and financial indicators over time. If the sample size permits (minimum 20 participants), we will also share findings from key stratified analyses. Materials will be developed to ensure confidentiality of participants is maintained and individual-level data cannot be identified. Materials will be developed in the languages of participants included in this study and in consultation with knowledge translation experts and our patient partners. We plan to publish our findings in peer-reviewed journals, present at academic conferences and provide seminars to interested stakeholders. We will disseminate our findings to national and international organisations, such as the Public Health Agency of Canada, TB People Canada, STOP TB and WHO. As before, all materials will be developed in consultation with knowledge translation experts and our patient partners.
