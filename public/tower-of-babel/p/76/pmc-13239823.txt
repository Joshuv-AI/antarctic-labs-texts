Trametinib Therapy for Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy and Pulmonary Hypertension in a Child With RAF1‐Related Noonan Syndrome (p.Ser257Leu): A Case Report
Authors: C. Noah Nilsson, Othman A. Aljohani, Michael A. Smith, Rachelle Durand, Inger Norlyk Sheyanth, Hythem Nawaytou, Elliot Stieglitz, Russel Valle, Sanjeev A. Datar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case describes a female infant with RAF1‐related Noonan syndrome who developed severe hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, pulmonary hypertension, and cardiorespiratory failure that responded to trametinib treatment but ultimately progressed to death following dose tapering and discontinuation of therapy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case with detailed respiratory information in a trametinib‐treated patient with RASopathy‐related pulmonary disease.

Introduction

Hyperactive RAS‐MAPK signaling, predominantly due to gain‐of‐function germline variants in genes encoding proteins within the RAS‐MAPK signaling pathway, has been described in a group of genetic syndromes collectively referred to as RASopathies. Noonan syndrome (NS) is the most common RASopathy and represents a clinically and genetically heterogenous multisystem disorder, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 1000–2500 [1]. Depending on the underlying pathogenic variant, approximately 20% of children with NS develop hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) [2, 3], which is associated with a poor prognosis and significantly increased mortality when diagnosed within the first year of life [4], particularly in infants presenting with HCM‐related heart failure [5, 6]. Genotype–phenotype correlations between pathogenic RAF1 variants and HCM have been reported, with HCM observed in up to 75% of affected individuals [3, 7]. Furthermore, allele specificity within the RAF1 gene has been documented, with the p.Ser257Leu pathogenic variant associated with a particularly severe, early onset, and progressive HCM phenotype, reported in up to 92% of affected individuals and frequent co‐occurrence of pulmonary hypertension (PH) [8]. Historically, heart transplantation was considered the only therapeutic option for severe HCM refractory to first‐line pharmacologic therapies (including beta‐blockers and calcium channel blockers) or surgical interventions such as myectomy or alcohol septal ablation [9]. However, preclinical studies [10, 11], multiple case reports, and more recently a retrospective study including 30 patients with RASopathy‐associated HCM treated with MEK inhibitors (MEKi) have demonstrated clinical benefits, including reduced need for cardiac surgery for outflow tract obstruction, heart transplantation, and death [12]. There are limited reports describing MEKi effects in RASopathy‐associated pulmonary disease, including PH and respiratory failure [12].

We present detailed respiratory data from a girl with NS due to a de novo RAF1:c770C>T (p.Ser257Leu) pathogenic variant, suffering from HCM and PH, whose clinical condition significantly improved following initiation of MEKi therapy. Following discontinuation of MEKi treatment, she clinically decompensated and ultimately died from cardiorespiratory failure.

Case Presentation

The patient was born at 36 weeks and 2 days of gestation following a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios, ascites, and non‐immune fetal hydrops. Fetal echocardiogram revealed no structural abnormalities. She was delivered via spontaneous vaginal delivery, with a birth weight of 3.0 kg. She remained hospitalized during the first month of life because of intermittent oxygen desaturation related to feeding and abnormal swallowing. A nasogastric tube was initially placed, followed by gastrostomy tube placement for ongoing nutritional support.

The patient exhibited dysmorphic features, prompting genetic testing.

Differential Diagnosis and Investigations

Chromosomal microarray analysis was normal, while whole exome sequencing identified a well characterized, heterozygous pathogenic variant in the RAF1 gene (c.770C>T [p.Ser257Leu]), associated with NS. Early postnatal echocardiography demonstrated a non‐restrictive bidirectional patent foramen ovale versus secundum atrial septal defect, mildly thickened atrioventricular and semilunar valves, and mild mitral regurgitation, without evidence of additional obstruction or significant valvular disease.

Following discharge, the patient demonstrated appropriate weight gain on a combination of breast milk and formula administered via gastrostomy tube. Neurological evaluation revealed hypotonia with gross motor delay. She was followed closely by pediatric cardiology given the known association between NS and congenital heart disease/cardiomyopathy. Although initially asymptomatic from a cardiac standpoint, serial echocardiograms demonstrated progressive HCM that eventually progressed to hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM), for which propranolol therapy was initiated.

At 8 months of age the patient was admitted to the pediatric cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) with respiratory distress and her course was complicated by methicillin‐sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) pneumonia leading to septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and acute cardiopulmonary failure. She was treated with broad‐spectrum antibiotics, vasoactive support, and mechanical ventilation. As vasoactive medications were weaned, propranolol was reintroduced for heart rate control and optimization of diastolic function. Cardiac catheterization demonstrated severe PH and after a multidisciplinary discussion, triple pulmonary vasodilator therapy with sildenafil, bosentan, and treprostinil was introduced with caution. Heart transplantation was contraindicated due to severe pulmonary vascular disease. Despite maximal medical therapy, her clinical status continued to deteriorate, with worsening pulmonary edema on chest radiographs (Figure 1A; Supplementary Figure 1) and escalating ventilatory support (Figure 2).

Treatment

With parental consent, off‐label treatment with trametinib was initiated at a dose of 0.125 mg daily and increased to 0.25 mg daily (0.03 mg/kg/day) after a week. Over the ensuing months, she experienced multiple episodes of acute clinical decompensation, including one requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation, as well as recurrent bacterial infections and an episode of candidemia. Following several unsuccessful extubation attempts, a tracheostomy was performed. After 4 months of trametinib therapy, in conjunction with ongoing propranolol and triple therapy for PH, her hemodynamics stabilized. Repeat cardiac catheterization demonstrated a marked reduction in pulmonary vascular resistance from 13.7 to 2.1 indexed Wood units. Serial chest radiographs showed improvement in pulmonary edema, and echocardiography demonstrated improvement in HCM, with a reduction in left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) gradient. Following sustained improvement in respiratory status and successful discontinuation of mechanical ventilation, the tracheostomy was decannulated (Figures 1 and 2).

Outcome

Four days later the patient developed hematochezia and pneumatosis intestinalis concerning for necrotizing enterocolitis, prompting discontinuation of trametinib. The necrotizing enterocolitis resolved with antibiotic therapy; however, trametinib was not resumed because of concern for any potential immunosuppressive effects. Over the subsequent weeks, she developed progressive cardiopulmonary failure refractory to medical management, manifested by elevated B‐type natriuretic peptide levels, tachycardia, tachypnea, oxygen desaturation, and worsening pulmonary edema on chest radiographs (Figure 2; Supplementary Figure 4B–D). Her clinical deterioration was further complicated by acute kidney injury and feeding intolerance.

This period of decompensation was accompanied by fever, leukocytosis, and elevated procalcitonin levels, raising concern for recurrent infection and precluding re‐initiation of trametinib. She required multiple courses of antibiotics, intermittent vasoactive support, escalating diuretic therapy (for pulmonary indications), and increasing respiratory support, ultimately necessitating reintubation. She required another tracheostomy. After control of infectious complications, trametinib was reintroduced at a reduced dose of 0.125 mg daily and subsequently decreased to 0.0625 mg daily because of concern for hyperpyrexia.

Several weeks later, the patient was discharged home with a tracheostomy and home ventilatory support under palliative care but was shortly thereafter readmitted with infection and respiratory failure (Supplementary Figure 7). Her condition rapidly progressed to acute cardiac, respiratory, and renal failure. After discussion with the family, life‐prolonging therapies were withdrawn, and the patient died shortly thereafter at 1 year and 9 months of age.

The family declined autopsy.

Discussion

We present detailed clinical data from an infant with NS and associated HOCM, severe PH, and respiratory failure, whose clinical course included distinct periods on and off trametinib therapy. The patient achieved her most favorable status during trametinib therapy and clinically deteriorated during periods of discontinued and reduced trametinib therapy. Trametinib was held due to NEC, recurrent infections, and hyperpyrexia, highlighting the challenge of balancing efficacy and safety in off‐label treatment. We remained cautious in our use of trametinib due to the patient's multi morbidity and unstable condition, along with the incompletely described adverse effects and potential drug interactions in RASopathy patients. However, a recent retrospective study of 30 patients treated with MEKi for RASopathy‐associated cardiomyopathy observed no adverse events related to immunosuppression. Furthermore, no life‐threatening adverse events related to MEKi were observed, although temporary cessation and dose reduction were required in 7 patients [12].

Prior reports have demonstrated beneficial effects of trametinib on HCM in RASopathy patients [12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. In this case, improvement may have been partly mediated by amelioration of HOCM‐related cardiogenic pulmonary edema, supported by reductions in B‐type natriuretic peptide levels during both full‐ and reduced‐dose trametinib therapy, as well as a decrease in left ventricular outflow tract (LVOTO) gradient. However, the correlation between LVOTO and clinical improvement was less consistent at lower doses. Dynamic echocardiographic findings did not fully match the clinical response, and unlike prior reports, no sustained reduction in global left ventricular mass was observed [12, 13, 15, 17, 18]. Notably, a recent adult case of RAF1‐associated HCM similarly demonstrated improvement in LVOTO without a corresponding decrease in left ventricular mass, with clinical improvement attributed to reduced systolic anterior motion rather than myocardial regression [19].

Radiographic trends paralleled trametinib exposure, with pulmonary edema improving during treatment despite lower ventilatory support and worsening during interruptions (Figure 1; Supplementary Figures 1–7). The patient's pulmonary vascular resistance decreased markedly from 13.7 to 2.1 indexed Wood units, suggesting the clinical improvement might also have been partly mediated by amelioration of her pulmonary vascular disease. While treatment included pulmonary vasodilators, the improvement occurred even after treprostinil discontinuation, and her clinical response appeared to be temporally associated with trametinib treatment. While trametinib has no established role in primary pulmonary hypertension, one could speculate whether off‐label use may have addressed complex NS‐associated pulmonary and lymphatic pathology. Mussa et al. reported progressive pulmonary vascular disease in a patient with RAF1‐related NS receiving trametinib, despite sharing the same pathogenic variant [14]. Importantly, histopathologic findings in that case suggested pulmonary capillary hemangiomatosis, indicating a likely distinct pathophysiologic mechanism.

Lymphatic abnormalities are well‐recognized in NS [20, 21] with reported responses to MEKi [15, 17, 18, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27], and may have contributed to respiratory failure in this patient. However, despite antenatal hydrops raising further suspicion for an underlying lymphatic disorder, dynamic contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance lymphangiography (DCMRL) performed 3 weeks after trametinib initiation demonstrated no pulmonary lymphatic abnormalities.

Wolf et al. have suggested that myocardial effects of RASopathies may be age dependent, with younger patients potentially deriving greater benefit from MEKi, implying the existence of an optimal therapeutic window [12]. Similar age‐dependent responses have been observed in NS patients with lymphatic disorders, possibly reflecting heightened plasticity of the developing lymphatic system [28].

Whether a longer uninterrupted course of therapy could have yielded sustained cardiorespiratory stabilization in this patient remains speculative. Overall, this case underscores the need for prospective, controlled studies of MEKi in RASopathy‐associated disease, which are challenging due to the rarity, genetic heterogeneity, and phenotypic diversity of affected patients.
