Robot-assisted laparoscopic radical nephrectomy for renal cell carcinoma with direct colon invasion and concomitant inferior vena cava tumor thrombus: A case report
Authors: Kosuke Hamada, Takeshi Yamasaki, Nawa Masaki, Shoma Yamamoto, Taisuke Matsue, Nao Yukimatsu, Taiyo Otoshi, Minoru Kato, Katsuyuki Kuratsukuri, Junji Uchida
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Resectable renal cell carcinoma (RCC) with colon invasion is extremely rare and is usually managed by open surgery. To our knowledge, this is the first case report documenting the management of a patient with robot-assisted laparoscopic radical nephrectomy (RARN) with colon resection and inferior vena cava thrombotectomy. The procedure was completed without conversion to open surgery or major peri- and post-operative complications, confirming the technical feasibility of RARN in this challenging setting. However, given the complexity and high morbidity risk, this approach should be reserved for experienced surgeons in high-volume centers, and further evidence is required to define its role.

Introduction

Approximately 5 %–15 % of renal cell carcinomas (RCCs) exhibit direct invasion into adjacent organs, with synchronous metastases frequently present,1,2 often restricting the possibility of surgical treatment. However, direct invasion of the colon is rare,3,4 presenting with symptoms, such as gastrointestinal bleeding or abdominal pain. A systematic review has addressed the surgical management of RCC with direct colonic invasion; however, only five case reports were included, all of which were managed using open surgical approaches.5

In 2005, Klingler et al. first described robot-assisted laparoscopic radical nephrectomy (RARN),6 which has become an increasingly feasible option for complex cases owing to its advantages.1,7,8 Literature is scarce on the use of minimally invasive techniques for locally advanced RCCs with adjacent organ invasion7. To our knowledge, no case reports have documented the feasibility and surgical technique of RARN combined with colon resection. We present a case of RCC with direct colonic invasion and concomitant inferior vena cava (IVC) tumor thrombus that was successfully managed with robot-assisted surgery.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old man presented with bilateral leg edema. Preoperative evaluation revealed an American Society of Anesthesiologists score of 3 and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 1.

The patient underwent RARN with IVC thrombectomy and descending colon resection following intraoperative detection of colon invasion (Fig. 1B). The operative time was 701 min (console time, 441 min), with an estimated blood loss of 2220 mL. No intraoperative complications occurred.

Histopathological examination confirmed clear cell RCC (ccRCC), grade 4 (World Health Organization/International Society of Urological Pathology9,10), pT4 (colon invasion) with positive margin at the IVC resection site, whereas both the renal tumor and colon resection margins were negative.

Postoperatively, the patient developed paralytic ileus (Clavien–Dindo grade II), which was resolved conservatively. He was discharged on postoperative day 25. Adjuvant pembrolizumab was discontinued after the second cycle owing to immune-related interstitial pneumonitis. At the time of tumor recurrence, 8 months after surgery, the patient exhibited a decline in activities of daily living and was unable to attend outpatient visits; therefore, best supportive care was selected. The patient died of the disease 3 month later.

Surgical approach

The patient was positioned in the right lateral decubitus position, with the flank extended in a jackknife configuration. The four-arm robotic surgical platform was used, and two assistant ports were placed. Mobilization of the descending colon was challenging because of tumor invasion (Fig. 1B). Colon resection was performed at the splenic flexure and at the lower pole level of the kidney using a linear stapling device. To ensure that the remaining colon is adequately perfused, the main trunk of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) was preserved, and mesenteric division was minimized (Fig. 2A–D). This maneuver enabled successful mobilization of the pancreas and spleen, thereby allowing adequate access to the renal hilum. The tumor was placed in a retrieval bag, and the camera port incision was enlarged for removal from the body. A gastrointestinal surgeon completed the procedure. After confirming colon perfusion using indocyanine green, colon anastomosis was performed through the specimen retrieval site during the open surgery.

Discussion

This case report demonstrates the feasibility and surgical technique of RARN with concomitant colon resection for locally invasive RCC with direct colonic invasion and IVC tumor thrombus. Previous reports have shown that compared with radical nephrectomy (RN), multiorgan resection is associated with a 2.5-fold increased risk of major complications.7 There are a few reports on RN for RCC with multiorgan invasion, and careful consideration is required when determining the surgical indication and approach. Nonmetastatic resectable RCC with local invasion into the adjacent organs is rare (only 1 % of RCC cases)11 and has traditionally been managed by open surgery.5,7,11 Robotic surgery could offer advantages over open surgery in terms of reduced blood loss and complications.12 Furthermore, the technical advantages of robotic surgery, such as enhanced dexterity, three-dimensional visualization, and improved precision, have contributed to its reported feasibility in highly complex cases, including those involving multiorgan invasion or tumor thrombus.13 Currently, no established evidence supports the optimal surgical approach for RARN in cases of RCC with direct colonic invasion. In our institution, a key modification in such cases involved performing colon and mesentery resection before nephrectomy, allowing for adequate mobilization of the surrounding organs, facilitating access to the renal hilum, and enabling completion of RN using a standard technique thereafter. However, the tumor is often large in RCCs of locally advanced disease.11 We consider open conversion under the following conditions where robotic surgery may not be feasible: (1) inadequate visualization and working space for robotic manipulation; (2) extensive bowel resection and difficulty in mobilizing the colon for reconstruction; and (3) mesenteric resection extending to the root of the IMA, posing a risk of colonic ischemia. Due to the technical demands and potentially high risks of complications, this procedure is not recommended for routine practice and should be reserved for high-volume centers with surgeons experienced in advanced intraperitoneal organ handling.

Surgical resection remains the only curative option and may help control local symptoms caused by the tumor, such as pain and gastrointestinal bleeding, thereby improving patient quality of life.

However, previous studies have shown limited benefit of surgery alone for nonmetastatic, locally invasive RCC requiring multivisceral resection. Margulis et al. demonstrated that among patients who underwent RN with adjacent organ resection, the 3-year cancer-specific survival (CSS) rate was 66 % and 22 % for those with <pT4 and pT4, respectively.11 Karellas et al. reported that of 38 patients with nonmetastatic, locally invasive RCC who underwent RN with adjacent organ resection, 34 (90 %) died of the disease at a median of 11.7 (range: 5.4–29.2) months following surgery.1 Adjuvant pembrolizumab, a widely accepted therapy, has been demonstrated to prolong overall survival in patients at high risk of recurrence after nephrectomy14. In contrast, there is no clear evidence that neoadjuvant therapy improves prognosis in localized RCC15, and we found no reports specifically evaluating the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy for locally invasive RCC with multivisceral involvement. However, in RCCs with IVC tumor thrombus, Hara et al. reported that neoadjuvant therapy reduced surgical invasiveness and recurrence risk16. However, in metastatic RCC, Pignot et al. highlighted that primary tumor downsizing after immune-based therapy was associated with increased intraoperative complexity (adhesions and inflammatory reactions).17 On the other hand, the extent of local invasion is often overestimated on preoperative imaging18, and approximately 60 % of patients clinically diagnosed with cT4 disease are reported to be overstaged11. Consequently, some potentially resectable tumors may be managed with systemic therapy. Therefore, although neoadjuvant systemic therapy for locally invasive RCC may achieve tumor downsizing, it should be considered with caution due to the potential risks of increased peritumoral adhesions leading to greater surgical complexity and therapy-related adverse events (AE).

In the present case, consistent with previous reports, the patient experienced recurrence and died within 1 year of surgery, highlighting the poor prognosis. Possible contributing factors include the inability to complete adequate adjuvant pembrolizumab therapy due to immune-related AE and the inability to initiate systemic therapy at recurrence because of poor performance status. These observations suggest that systemic therapy may be essential to achieve long-term overall survival in patients with locally invasive RCC. In addition, a positive surgical margin, which has been reported to contribute to postoperative recurrence and inferior CSS19, was also present in this case and likely influenced the poor prognosis. As systemic therapy could not ultimately be delivered and an IVC tumor thrombus was present in this case, the option of administering neoadjuvant therapy to achieve downstaging before surgery might have been a reasonable alternative. In the current era, where adjuvant immunotherapy is approved and immune-based therapy are available as standard systemic therapy at recurrence, surgical indications for locally invasive RCC may be further expanded. Nonetheless, such procedures remain associated with high operative burden and complications, and careful patient selection with thorough counseling regarding prognosis is imperative.

Conclusion

In this case report, we demonstrated that RARN with colon resection is feasible if performed by a highly experienced surgeon. However, the oncologic prognosis in this case was poor. For such locally invasive RCC, integrating systemic therapy with surgery is paramount to optimize patient outcomes. Moreover, considering the high operative burden and risk of complications, careful patient selection, meticulous surgical planning, and thorough informed consent outlining the potential risks and benefits are essential.

Nevertheless, the current evidence base remains limited, and larger multicenter studies are needed to further clarify the role of robotic surgery in these challenging RCC cases.
