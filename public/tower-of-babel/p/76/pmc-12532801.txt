An artificial neural network approach for predicting infant mortality status in Ethiopia
Authors: Mekbib Berihun Dessie, Molalign Aligaz Adisu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfant mortality is a major public health issue that is rooted in the larger problem of socio-economic and healthcare disparities. Deep learning techniques were employed in this study to predict infant mortality using data gathered via 2019 Ethiopia Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS). An Artificial Neural Network (ANN) was applied in four layers (input, two hidden, and output), where ReLU and sigmoid activation functions were applied, and the optimization was an Adam algorithm. This was tested for 96.2% accuracy, while a precision of the “immortal” class was 99%, with an AUC of 0.99, i.e., the model could differentiate between mortal and immortal cases with high confidence. Some of the key predictors are infant age, birth order, mother’s age, and socio-economic predictors such as household size and wealth index. These findings affirm the capability of the deep learning paradigm in capturing complex and non-linear relationships that occur within health data to enable useful implications to policymakers and health workers alike. One limitation arises from the interpretability problem for algorithms and local generalizability; however, the study throws the spotlight on the potential of AI to reverse the trend towards reducing infant mortality in resource-scarce settings. Their inclusion in real-time surveillance systems with maximum emphasis on model interpretability for long-term uses is something future studies need to prioritize.Keywords: Infant mortality, Deep learning, Ethiopia

Introduction

Infant mortality, or infant death, within the first year of life is a critical marker of a population’s overall health and economic status. Inequalities remain widespread even though remarkable global successes have been realized over the past decades in declining infant mortality rates (IMR), especially among poor nations like Ethiopia. As of 2020, the infant mortality rate in Ethiopia was the highest globally, with a rate of over 33 deaths per 1,000 live births, according to the World Health Organization [1]. This is significantly higher than the world average, and it highlights the importance of targeted interventions and predictive factors to address this public health challenge.

The key determinants of infant mortality in Ethiopia are a complex interplay of poor access to health care services, maternal ill health, malnutrition, infection, and socio-economic disparities [2].These determinants have been analyzed and infant mortality rates predicted using conventional statistical techniques. Such techniques, nevertheless, frequently are unable to reflect the complex, non-linear interactions among the many factors that contribute to infant mortality. Deep learning, a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), provides an encouraging option in this regard. Deep learning algorithms, which have the ability to model complicated patterns and associations in big data, can transform the way we foresee and comprehend infant mortality [3].

The aim of this finding is to explore whether deep learning techniques can be applied to predict infant mortality rates in Ethiopia. This research seeks to develop a strong predictive model that can inform policy-making and intervention strategies using large datasets that encompass a wide range of variables, such as socioeconomic factors, access to healthcare, and environmental conditions. The ultimate goal is to help lower Ethiopia’s infant mortality rate by making pragmatic recommendations that can be used to guide resource planning and the design of focused health programs.

Advances in the emerging fields of machine learning and deep learning have purportedly offered concrete avenues to predict adverse child health outcomes in LMICs. Several studies trained conventional machine learning methods (logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting) and deep models to make predictions of neonatal mortality and under-five mortality using Standard Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and other routine sources. These demonstrate that the non-linear methods can effectively pick up interactions among socio-demographic variables and health variables that linear models may overlook. Operational deployment of these models, however, is impeded by apprehensions over interpretability, class imbalance, and generalizability across different regions and survey waves. This study contributes to the literature by [1] applying an ANN to the infant cohort in the 2019 EDHS [2], addressing class imbalance explicitly [3], benchmarking against simpler classifiers, and [4] using SHAP values for improving the interpretability of the model from the perspective of health practitioners.

Significance of the study

Infant mortality is a marker of the more general socioeconomic and environmental conditions within which a population lives and is not a solely health matter. The high infant mortality rate in Ethiopia is caused by the fact that the nation still suffers from poverty, poor healthcare, and lack of education access. The government of Ethiopia has, therefore, taken a big step towards alleviating these challenges in partnership with a number of international agencies. But it has been unbalanced and the nation still maintains strong impediments to the realization of the child health-related Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [4].

The relevance of this study is that it can give a more precise and sophisticated insight into the determinants of infant mortality in Ethiopia. In the multifaceted reality of infant mortality, traditional prediction models like logistic regression and decision trees tend to rely on linearity and independence assumptions between variables, which may not always hold true. However, deep learning is especially well-suited for such studies since it can identify non-linear relationships and interaction among a vast number of variables [5–7].

In addition, enhancing the accuracy of prediction is not the only goal in using deep learning techniques here. Uncovering hidden patterns and snippets of information that could inform better interventions is another goal. Such a model through deep learning can, for instance, pinpoint specific combinations of environmental and socioeconomic conditions that are strongly predictive of infant mortality and hence identify which among these conditions must be targeted by interventions. Better outcomes for babies and families as well as the more effective use of resources may be the result. In general, predictive machine-learning models like the one proposed here help to identify high-risk groups based on key predictors, such as infant age, mother’s age, and socio-economic conditions. By knowing the features most associated with infant mortality, policymakers and health workers may intervene in a focused manner in areas such as maternal health, educational programs for families, or resource application to selected regions or populations.

Objectives of the study

The overall objective of this research is to develop and validate a deep learning model for the prediction of infant mortality rate in Ethiopia. Specifically, the study will identify the key predictors, develop a predictive model, compare model performances, and aims to provide an efficient tool for policymakers and healthcare providers, ultimately leading to the prevention of infant mortality and the improvement of child health outcomes among one of the world’s most at-risk populations.

Methodology

Data source

This study uses data from the 2019 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS). The 2019 EDHS are currently the latest and part of the world demographic and health survey series that are conducted every 5 years. It is a nationally representative household survey that collects data on a broad range of population and health issues to enhance maternal and child health in Ethiopia. The EDHS used a multi-stage stratified sampling procedure to select respondents from households in a total of 624 clusters [8].

When designing a model for predicting infant mortality rates in children, the sources of data play a crucial role in ensuring the model’s accuracy and relevance. From those DHS are large-scale household surveys conducted in many countries, providing comprehensive data on demographics, health, and nutrition. These surveys often include demographic information, dietary information, and health-related behaviors. In this study, the raw data has been taken from nationally representative secondary data of EDHS to predict mortality [9, 10]. It has been designed to cover all the regions of Ethiopia in both urban and rural areas. Among urban and rural areas, the EDHS dataset consisted of different types of demographics like sex, child’s age, mother’s age, maternal educational level, parental educational level, and wealth index, place of residence, region, and place of delivery, birth order, breastfeeding status, water source, sanitation facilities, household size, and number of children, media exposure and the dependent variable mortality rate. In this case, the record consists of 15,888 instances together with 1072, variables (factors) which are 16 of them used in this study. To study mortality, it was very important to know the main predictors of every infant child individually from those families. In this study, the target variable infant mortality was labeled as a binary classification depending on the child survival status variable from the 2019 EDHS dataset. Infants who died before their first birthday are assigned a label of ‘1’ (mortal), whereas infants who survived beyond one year are assigned a label of ‘0’ (immortal).

The dataset was stratified to maintain the proportion of the infant mortality class across subsets into training (70%), validation (20%), and testing (10%). In the course of model selection, a stratified k-fold cross-validation procedure (k = 5) was performed on the training set to guarantee a robust hyperparameter tuning. Class weighting and resampling methods, were tested to counteract class imbalance (see Class Imbalance subsection). Hyperparameter tuning was carried out using randomized search against learning rates, neuron counts, dropout rates, and batch sizes, with a criterion to maximize validation AUC.

Given the typically low prevalence of infant death, we evaluated three strategies: [1] class weighting in the loss function [2], random under sampling of the majority class, and [3] SMOTE oversampling of the minority class. Each approach was evaluated using cross-validation, and final model selection considered both recall on the mortal class and overall AUC.

We used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to explain model predictions at both global and local levels. SHAP summary plots provide a ranked list of features by their average absolute contribution to the model output; SHAP force plots illustrate individual predictions and support clinical review of high-risk cases. Permutation importance was also computed for robustness.

Feature extraction

It was very challenging to define mortality for the infant child’s using the secondary sources of data. Some of the features do not have any impact on mortality, in general. Therefore, the labeling of data and features extraction was very important. In this research it has been found 16 most significant features by literature review. Those features were extracted with respect to Primary Sampling Unit (PSU). PSU is used to select individual child information from EDHS data source. Among 1072 features in EDHS 2014, 16 most significant features were extracted.

Data preprocessing

Preprocessing the EDHS data involves several steps to clean and prepare the datasets. In this stage, we would have handled the missing values, encoding the categorical values by using numerical features, normalizing the numerical feature, and splitting the data into training, validation, and test sets.

Handling missing values: in this stage, after selecting the related features the next stage is to check the missed values and understand the variables. When we check the variables there is a missed value in place of delivery, fathers’ educational level, children’s breast-feeding status, and sanitation facilities. After identifying those missed values there is a missing value handling mechanism that is deletion: Remove rows or columns with a high proportion of missing values if they are not critical for analysis, Imputation: Mean/Median Imputation for numerical values and Mode Imputation for categorical values as well as Predictive Imputation: Use machine learning models (e.g., K-Nearest Neighbors, regression) to predict missing values based on other features. So, for our predictive algorithm, we have used all of these missing values handling mechanisms and deleted the father’s educational level because from 15,888 observations 11,342 of them are missed so when we check the importance and effect of this variable less significant and also for place of delivery, breast-feeding status, and sanitation facilities use mode imputation. When we did those activities, we used Panda’s library.

Feature selection is a critical step in building a predictive model, as it helps in identifying and selecting the most relevant features that influence the mortality rates. After conducted a literature and calculating the correlation between each feature and the target variable (mortality rates) to identify potentially relevant features.

While many standard preprocessing methods were implemented, the following limitations exist in our approach. First, variables with a large percentage of missing values, such as father’s education, were dropped, rather than utilizing more advanced imputation methods such as multiple imputation or model-based estimation. Second, while mean and mode were used for imputing missing values, respectively, in numerical and categorical feature variables, we did not use more advanced methods that would preserve variance and relationships better. Third, baseline comparisons with simpler methods, such as logistic regression or decision trees, were not done; such assignments would have presented useful benchmarks against which to evaluate the performance of the ANN method.

Model selection

For this study, we choose a deep learning approach. Deep learning algorithms are a class of machine learning algorithms that are inspired by the structure and function of the human brain, particularly the neural networks in the brain [11]. In this research, ANN is used to develop a predictive model for the mortality status of children under the age of five years. However, more focus is on ANN because the deep learning algorithm is important due to the effective performance of the sequential datasets, the non-linear relationship between the datasets, and the ability to learn meaningful representations of the data through multiple layers of hidden units. Each layer learns to extract higher-level features from the previous layer, enabling the model to automatically discover relevant patterns and representations in the data. This can be advantageous when dealing with complex datasets where manual feature engineering may be challenging or time-consuming. Additionally, ANN is beneficial for addressing flaws in other DNN models. According to Schmidhuber (2015), deep neural networks, especially ANNs, have become capable of reaching state-of-the-art performance in various domains because of their depth, flexibility, and ability to approximate any complicated function.

Model training

The next stage is to train and assess the deep learning model for predicting the status of mortality among Ethiopian children who are infants after the dataset is preprocessed and prepped. To make sure the models are reliable and applicable to a wide range of situations, the dataset is divided into training, validation, and test sets. The validation set is used for model selection and hyper-parameter tuning, and the training set for training the models. However, in this study, we have used a random search algorithm to select the appropriate hyper-parameters. The parameters to be optimized are neurons or connections in each layer, learning rate, dropouts, activation function for input, and hidden and output layers are the parameters that would be analyzed to get the best result [12]. Lastly, the performance of the chosen model is assessed using the test set. The EDHS dataset’s sequential data is used to teach the deep learning models, including ANN, during the training phase. Based on the training data, the models iteratively modify their parameters to reduce prediction errors and boost accuracy. Following training, several metrics, such as accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, is used to assess the models’ performance [13].

The prediction problem would appear to classify the cases or individuals into two classes: immortal or mortal. Mortal (Positive Class): Those cases in which the subject or individual is predicted as “mortal.” These are the true positives when properly identified. Immortal (Negative Class): Those cases in which the subject or individual is predicted as “immortal.” These are the true negatives when properly identified. The ROC curve is used to determine how well the model can distinguish between these two classes. With an AUC of 0.99, it is extremely good at accurately predicting mortality (true positives) without producing too many false alarms (flagging immortal cases as mortal). This type of classification would be crucially important in health or risk assessment applications where accurate prediction incurs a high cost (Fig. 3).

The confusion matrix evaluates the performance of the classification model in distinguishing between immortal (0) and mortal [1] cases. The model correctly predicted 203 mortal cases (true negatives) and 1991 immortal cases (true positives). It also misclassified 17 mortal cases as immortal (false positives) and 90 immortal cases as mortal (false negatives). This provides an overall accuracy of approximately 95.5%, indicating good performance. The model is 99.15% precise, meaning the vast majority of its immortal predictions were correct, and 95.67% complete, meaning its ability to identify immortal cases correctly. It has some difficulty with the mortal class, as the 17 false positives can attest, but in general, the model is quite good at distinguishing between the two classes (Fig. 4).

Overall, the accuracy of the model is 96.2% and 96% of all cases are correctly classified. Macro average, which equally weighs both classes, has precision at 88%, recall at 95%, and F1-score at 91%. Greater distinction of mortal cases can strengthen and further balance the model’s performance on both classes.

The full dataset has 15,888 records and has been split into training, validation, and test sets to properly train and test the model. The test set has 10% of the overall data, i.e., 1588 records, and is used to validate the performance of the model on new data. These divisions ensure that the model is given enough data to learn from (training set), is adequately tested during development (validation set), and is thoroughly tested for generalization on new data (test set).

Threshold sensitivity analysis for classification performance

In any binary task, such as the prediction of infant mortality cases, deciding on an appropriate cutoff threshold for labeling observations becomes quite paramount. We performed the threshold sensitivity analysis by varying the decision threshold between 0.3 and 0.7.

As shown in Fig. 6 and lower thresholds are associated with higher recall and lower precision-where missing a true mortal case is far more damaging than over-flagging. For example, at a threshold of 0.3, the recall was almost 98% with a precision of 65%. At a threshold of 0.7, the recall dropped to 76%, and precision increased to 87%.

Threshold sensitivity analysis showing the trade-offs between precision, recall, and F1-score for the “mortal” class at different classification thresholds

On the other hand, a threshold value of 0.5 might be considered a balanced operating point, as observed from the testing results: the precision is at 76%, and recall is at 94%, which yielded an F1-score of 0.84. These scenarios might be referring to a public health scenario whereby false positives or false negatives do bear significant consequences-where planning interventions and risk surveillance are implemented.

Feature importance

As seen in the analysis pertaining to feature importance (Fig. 7), we see age (in months) is the prime feature responsible for predicting infant mortality pertaining to frailty during one’s neonate stage and the few months that follow. Outcomes are also significantly affected by maternal age and birth order, where younger and older mothers, as well as later-order births, experience higher risk. Features such as household size and region depict socio economic and geographical imbalances, determining healthcare access and resources which are available. We also observe moderately important features like increase in number of children, wealth index and access to clean water which mark as strong indicators of one’s socio economic standing along with their living conditions. The role of maternal education stresses the lower importance of knowledge when it comes to improving childcare practices. Whereas, other important factors such as sanitation facilities, breastfeeding status and urban versus rural residence hold lesser relevance yet aid in defining the context for the envisioned interventions. Recommended actions aimed at alleviating infant mortality include focusing on under-maternal age groups, enhancing maternal health programs, poverty mitigation, infrastructural development, and improved community awareness. Next in line is the more concentrated study of gathering information on nutrition, health, and healthcare services relative to their infrastructure by examining the interplay of multiple components in detail.

SHAP summary plot for showing global feature importance

To improve the interpretability of the ANN model, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) was used to attribute and visualize the contribution of each predictor to the model’s output (Fig. 8). The SHAP summary plot ranks features by their average absolute SHAP values and illustrates their overall impact on model predictions. Each point represents an individual observation; the color of a point indicates feature value (red = high, blue = low); its horizontal position indicates the SHAP value, i.e., the impact magnitude or direction of the feature on the predicted probability of infant survival. Infant age (months) was found to be the most influential predictor in the model. Infants with a lower age parameter (blue dots in the negative SHAP region) were considered at higher risk of death, while older infant ages (red dots in the positive SHAP region) weighed predictions toward survival. Mother’s age, on the other hand, was the performer number two, with younger and older maternal ages showing patterns that increase mortality risk compared to mothers in their mid-twenties. Birth order was found to be important too, higher birth orders being inclined toward a greater predicted risk of mortality. Socioeconomic and environmental factors contributed less largely. Home delivery has a positive SHAP value toward mortality risk compared to institutional births, underlining the key role of skilled birth attendance. Household size, wealth index (poor), and lack of improved sanitation facilities have smaller yet consistent associations with increased mortality risk. Regarding categorical features, a moderate association was seen with residence (urban/rural) and sex of the child.

This highlights the need for public health interventions that focus on early-life care, institutional deliveries, and overcoming socio-economic barriers to maternal and child health services.

Discussion

Deep learning techniques may help greatly in the prediction of infant mortality in Ethiopia, the study concludes. The proposed artificial neural network (ANN) model has showed good performance in terms of accuracy, precision, and recalls-the very reasons why it is best in identifying important predictors of infant mortality. The model achieved an accuracy of 96% and an AUC of 0.99, which affirms its applicability to policymakers and healthcare providers.

The one finding that was most relevant in this study is that age (in months) was the highest predictor of infant mortality, a conclusion that has also been noted earlier in literature pointing out that the infant has an increased vulnerability during the neonatal period as well as later in life. In addition to which mother’s age and birth order were influenced by the model, the model also mentions that interventions targeted at younger mothers and older mothers as well as babies birthing in higher orders might have a higher effect on mortality. Another interesting aspect is wealth index, household size and regions which play a role in trying to illustrate just how many socio-economic variables may be involved in this analysis reflecting wider socio-economic disparities contributing to infant health outcomes. Another reason likely for the widespread adoption of deep learning over conventional statistical methods is probably the modeling of those non-linear interactions caused by interaction variables ANN model was chosen as one which can effectively represent the very complex nature of the interaction (leading to much deeper understanding of the nature of the complex phenomenon concerning infant mortality) [14–16].

This study is, however, not without limitations despite these considerable improvements in most of the results it has presented. First, it is based on secondary data, the EDHS, which is likely to have biases on collection methods or missing values. Despite attempts at rigorous pre-processing, there are limitations in the underlying dataset that cannot be completely removed. Second, the extremely good performance of the model may not be uniformly generalizable to every region of Ethiopia due to variation in local health infrastructure and socio-economic conditions. Future studies should therefore incorporate localized data to give the model more relevance.

The research has opened up several directions for potential research. First, updating the dataset with newer, more exhaustive measures of health such as maternal nutrition, immunization rates, and disease burden would increase the predictive capability of the model. Second, the use of different other deep-learning architectures such as CNN could be explored for complementary concepts, specifically of the temporal or spatial data. The model would be implemented within a real-time surveillance system to facilitate healthcare professionals to recognize vulnerable infants and act pre-emptively. The establishment of partnerships with regional health authorities would be welcomed to enable operationalization of such systems to translate model predictions into health policy.

Conclusion

Overall, the study has established the effectiveness of deep learning approaches in the prediction of infant mortality in Ethiopia at high accuracy and identification of robust predictors. The results show the capability of AI to solve public health problems in a resource-poor setting. The results have the potential to empower policymakers and health care practitioners to apply intervention for infant mortality reduction and improvement of child health. Future work will need to concentrate more on the quality of the data and making the model more interpretable, with a view to implementing these predictive tools within health systems for optimal benefit.
