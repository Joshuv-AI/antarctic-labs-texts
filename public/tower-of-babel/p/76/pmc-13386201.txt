Succinate-assisted synthesis of ZnO–CuO heterostructure electrocatalysts for tunable hydrogen evolution reaction
Authors: Hilda Karen Perinne Nzeket, Rosie Lauriane Therese Ngono, Paulin Kammi Yontchoum, Ekane Peter Etape, Kenneth Mbene, Roussin Lontio Fomekong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing noble-metal-free electrocatalysts for the alkaline hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) requires strategies that overcome the intrinsically poor electronic conductivity and suboptimal hydrogen adsorption properties of metal oxide semiconductors. Herein, ZnO–CuO n–p heterojunction composites were synthesized via a succinate-assisted co-precipitation route to systematically investigate the influence of Cu incorporation (6, 12 and 24 at%) on HER performance in 1 M KOH. FTIR analysis confirms that succinate ligands coordinate both Zn2+ and Cu2+ precursor species, while X-ray diffraction reveals biphasic wurtzite ZnO/monoclinic CuO composites with progressively increasing heterojunction density as the Cu content increases. Among all compositions, the 24 at% Cu sample (ZCu-C) exhibits the best HER activity, requiring an overpotential of only 301 mV to achieve a current density of 10 mA cm−2, representing an improvement of 238 mV compared with pristine ZnO. Furthermore, its electrochemically active surface area (ECSA)-normalized specific activity is more than three times higher than that of ZnO. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was analysed using six candidate equivalent-circuit models. Comparison based on the reduced chi-squared criterion identified the two-time-constant circuit, Rs + (Rct‖CPE) + (Rct‖CPE), as the most appropriate representation of all impedance spectra, consistently yielding reduced chi-squared values one to two orders of magnitude lower than conventional single-time-constant Randles-type circuits. The two distributed relaxation processes are consistent with charge-transfer phenomena occurring at ZnO grain boundaries and at the ZnO–CuO heterojunction interfaces, respectively. These findings demonstrate that engineering oxide–oxide heterojunctions through a coordination-chemistry-assisted synthesis strategy provides an effective, scalable and noble-metal-free approach for enhancing alkaline HER electrocatalysis.

Introduction

The growing demand for energy, combined with climate mitigation imperatives, is pushing the world toward carbon-neutral and sustainable energy solutions.1 In this context, hydrogen (H2) has emerged as a promising clean energy carrier2 owing to its high energy density,3 wide availability from water,4 and zero-carbon combustion.5 Among the various hydrogen production pathways, electrochemical water splitting6 powered by renewable electricity represents one of the most environmentally benign and scalable approaches, in which hydrogen is generated via the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) at the cathode.7

Despite its apparent simplicity, the HER is intrinsically limited by sluggish reaction kinetics, requiring large overpotentials to achieve industrially relevant current densities.8 To date, platinum-group metals (PGMs) which are critical raw materials (CRM), particularly Pt, remain the benchmark HER electrocatalysts due to their optimal hydrogen adsorption energy and near-zero overpotential.9 However, the scarcity, high cost, and poor long-term sustainability of noble metals severely hinder their large-scale deployment,10,11 stimulating intense research efforts toward the development of efficient, earth-abundant, and low-cost alternatives. In recent years, considerable progress has also been achieved using carbon-based electrocatalysts and carbon-supported hybrid materials. Nitrogen-doped graphene, carbon nanotubes, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) and related architectures have demonstrated that coupling active transition-metal species with highly conductive carbon frameworks can significantly enhance HER activity by improving electron transport, increasing the density of accessible active sites and strengthening interfacial electronic interactions.12–14 For example, Younas et al. recently reported CuO/ZnO nanowires supported on reduced graphene oxide with an overpotential of approximately 358 mV at 10 mA cm−2 in 1 M KOH, highlighting the beneficial role of conductive carbon supports in oxide electrocatalysts.15 Nevertheless, oxide-based catalysts remain attractive because of their low cost, chemical robustness and stability in alkaline media. Improving their intrinsically poor electrical conductivity through interface engineering therefore remains a major challenge.

Zinc oxide (ZnO), an n-type semiconductor, has attracted considerable attention as a base material for electrocatalytic applications owing to its chemical stability, non-toxicity, low cost, and facile synthesis.16,17 Moreover, zinc is not considered as critical raw material. However, its wide bandgap (∼3.37 eV) and poor electrical conductivity severely limit HER activity.18,19 Strategies such as transition-metal doping19–22 and p–n heterojunction engineering23,24 have therefore been explored to improve charge transport and interfacial electron-transfer kinetics. ZnO has also been investigated for photoelectrocatalytic hydrogen production, where bandgap engineering enhances visible-light absorption and promotes photogenerated charge carriers that contribute to HER.18 Nevertheless, these approaches generally remain insufficient to achieve catalytic performances comparable to those of noble metals.25

Among the various heterojunction systems, coupling n-type ZnO with p-type copper(ii) oxide (CuO) has emerged as a particularly attractive strategy. CuO possesses a narrow bandgap (1.2–1.7 eV), relatively higher electrical conductivity and favourable electronic interaction with ZnO.25,26 The resulting ZnO/CuO heterostructures can promote interfacial charge redistribution and facilitate electron transfer across the semiconductor interface, making them promising candidates for alkaline HER electrocatalysis.26

ZnO/CuO heterostructures have been widely investigated for photocatalysis,27 gas sensing,28 photodetection,24 optoelectronics29 and electrocatalysis. Prabhu et al. demonstrated enhanced photocatalytic hydrogen production over CuO@ZnO heterojunctions,30 while Kumaresan et al. reported improved HER/OER activity for CuO–ZnO nanocomposites in alkaline media.25 Although these studies demonstrate the potential of ZnO/CuO heterostructures, the influence of the synthesis route on the formation of electronically coupled interfaces and its relationship with HER activity remains insufficiently understood. Moreover, few studies have systematically combined structural, spectroscopic and electrochemical analyses with rigorous equivalent-circuit validation of impedance data.

In this respect, the synthesis route plays a decisive role in controlling composition, morphology and interface quality. Recent studies have confirmed that rational synthesis design can substantially improve the performance of oxide electrocatalysts.15,31 The co-precipitation method offers several advantages including simplicity, scalability, and precise compositional control.32 The use of chelating ligands such as succinate ions further enables the formation of homogeneous metal–organic precursors, directing particle growth and morphology during thermal decomposition.33 Previous studies on Fe-doped and Zn-based oxides have demonstrated that succinate ligands can stabilize mixed-metal complexes and promote controlled nanostructure formation through their flexible coordination modes.20,21,33

In this work, ZnO/CuO heterostructured composites containing 6, 12 and 24 at% Cu were synthesized through a succinate-assisted co-precipitation route. The influence of Cu loading on the structural, morphological, optical and electrochemical properties was systematically investigated using XRD, FTIR, SEM/EDX, UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy, XPS and electrochemical measurements. Emphasis was placed on establishing the relationship between composition, interfacial electronic interaction and HER behaviour through a comprehensive impedance analysis in which six equivalent electrical circuits were statistically compared to identify the most appropriate description of the electrode response. A scalable ligand-assisted synthesis strategy with complementary physicochemical characterization and rigorous electrochemical modelling, this work provides new insight into how controlled Cu incorporation modifies the interfacial electrochemical behaviour of ZnO/CuO composites and offers practical guidelines for the design of low-cost, PGM-free oxide electrocatalysts for sustainable alkaline hydrogen production.

Experimental section

Materials

Simple and mixed metal succinates were synthesized using high-purity reagents: succinic acid (C4H6O4, 98%, Sigma-Aldrich), copper(ii) acetate hydrate ((CH3COO)2Cu·H2O, 99%, Sigma-Aldrich), zinc acetate dihydrate ((CH3COO)2Zn·2H2O, 99.5%, Merck), and sodium carbonate (Na2CO3, 99.9%, F.S.I.C).

Synthesis of ZnO–CuO composites

Preparation of metal-succinate precursors

Simple (Zn, Cu) and mixed zinc–copper succinate precursors were prepared via a carbonate-to-succinate transformation (Fig. 1). Stoichiometric amounts of zinc acetate dihydrate and copper acetate dihydrate were dissolved in distilled water. Sodium carbonate was used as a precipitating agent to form a mixed Zn–Cu carbonate intermediate, which was then reacted with an ethanolic solution of succinic acid (20 mL, 95% ethanol) at room temperature for 1.5 hours. This acid–base reaction effects a ligand exchange, replacing carbonate groups with bifunctional succinate ions (C4H4O42−) forming a stabilized metal–organic complex and producing only CO2 and water as byproducts, ensuring freedom from contaminating inorganic salts. The pH of the reaction mixture was measured continuously with a calibrated pH meter and remained stable at 5.01 ± 0.05 throughout the reaction (measured at 27 °C). This value sits between the two pKa values of succinic acid (4.21 and 5.64), a range in which both carboxylate arms of the succinate dianion are partially deprotonated and available for coordination to Zn2+ and Cu2+ simultaneously. No base or acid was added to adjust the pH; the value arises spontaneously from the dissolution of the metal carbonate precursor in the succinic acid solution. The carbonate intermediate was added in small portions (approximately 0.1 g per minute) under continuous magnetic stirring at 100 rpm.

Synthesis route of all the developed composite-based materials.

Three mixed zinc–copper succinate precursors were synthesized with nominal copper atomic percentages of 6%, 12%, and 24% relative to the total metal content (Cu/(Cu + Zn)), denoted as ZnCuSuc-A, ZnCuSuc-B, and ZnCuSuc-C, respectively. The Cu loadings of 6, 12 and 24 at% were selected on the basis of prior work on succinate-derived doped ZnO electrocatalysts from our group,20,21,33 in which this composition range was found to span three distinct microstructural regimes: highly dispersed CuO at low loading, maximal ZnO–CuO interfacial contact at intermediate loading, and incipient CuO agglomeration at high loading. Below 5 at% the dopant effect on structure is minimal; above 25 at% CuO domain formation dominates over heterojunction effects. The doubling sequence was chosen to keep the number of samples tractable while still resolving composition-dependent trends. As a representative example, for preparing 0.544 g (3 mmol) of ZnCuSuc-A (6 at% Cu): 0.354 g (3 mmol) of succinic acid was dissolved in 20 mL ethanol, and 0.376 g (3 mmol, 2.82 mmol ZnCO3 + 0.18 mmol CuCO3) of the mixed zinc–copper carbonate was added in portions under continuous stirring. After reaction, precipitates were filtered, washed with ethanol, and dried at 85 °C for 2 hours.

Thermal decomposition to metal oxides

The final phase transformation into metal oxides was achieved by thermal decomposition (calcination) of the dried succinate precursors. Approximately 1 g of each precursor powder was placed in a ceramic crucible and calcined in a muffle furnace under ambient air at 550 °C for 60 minutes, using a heating rate of 10 °C min−1. This pyrolysis yielded simple oxides (ZnO and CuO) from their respective pure precursors, and mixed ZnO–CuO composites (ZCu-A, ZCu-B, and ZCu-C) from the ZnCuSuc-A, -B, and -C precursors, respectively. The chosen temperature and duration ensure complete removal of the organic matrix while preventing excessive grain growth to maintain a high density of active sites.

Physicochemical characterization

The structural, morphological, compositional, and optical properties of the precursors and oxide materials were investigated using complementary techniques.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet 6700, 4000–373 cm−1, 4 cm−1 resolution) was used to identify succinate coordination in the precursors and to verify the removal of organic species after calcination. Phase composition and crystallinity were determined by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Bruker D8 Advance, Cu Kα, λ = 1.5406 Å, 2θ = 5–100°, step size 0.05°).

Morphology and microstructure were examined by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Zeiss Ultra 55, 8 kV, Pt-sputtered samples). Elemental composition and spatial distribution of Zn, Cu, and O were analyzed by Energy dispersive X-ray (EDX) coupled with SEM.

Optical properties were assessed by UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy using a Jasco V750 spectrophotometer equipped with an integrating sphere over the 200–800 nm range at a scan rate of 400 nm min−1, allowing estimation of the optical bandgap.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed using a SSX 100/206 photoelectron spectrometer (Surface Science Instruments, USA) equipped with a monochromatized micro-focused Al Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV), operated at 20 mA and 10 kV. Powder samples were pressed into 4 mm diameter stainless-steel sample holders and mounted on a conductive aluminium carousel. The base pressure during analysis was approximately 1 × 10−6 Pa. Spectra were acquired with a pass energy of 150 eV, corresponding to a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of approximately 1.6 eV for the Au 4f7/2 peak of a clean gold reference. Surface charging was compensated using an 8 eV electron flood gun in combination with a Ni mesh positioned approximately 3 mm above the sample surface. Survey spectra were first recorded, followed by high-resolution spectra of C 1s, O 1s, Zn 2p, Cu 2p, and a second C 1s spectrum to verify charge-compensation stability during acquisition. Binding energies were calibrated by setting the adventitious carbon C–C/C–H component to 284.8 eV. Spectral analysis was carried out using CasaXPS (Casa Software Ltd, UK). After subtraction of a non-linear Shirley-type background, selected spectra were fitted using a Gaussian/Lorentzian (85/15) product function. Surface atomic concentrations were determined from peak areas corrected using the instrument sensitivity factors and acquisition parameters supplied by the manufacturer.

Electrochemical measurements

Electrocatalytic measurements were performed at room temperature using a CorrTest CS310M potentiostat/galvanostat in 1.0 M KOH electrolyte. Catalyst inks (1.5 mg oxide in 0.5 mL 95% ethanol, ultrasonicated 10 min) were drop-casted (40 µL, ∼0.12 mg catalyst) onto polished glassy carbon electrodes (3 mm diameter) and dried with Nafion binder at 55 °C for 15 min.

A standard three-electrode configuration was used, consisting of the catalyst-coated glassy carbon working electrode, a Hg/HgO (1 M NaOH) reference electrode, and a graphite rod counter electrode. All potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using:ERHE = E(Hg/HgO) + 0.098 + 0.059 × pH

For 1.0 M KOH (pH = 14), this gives:ERHE = E(Hg/HgO) + 0.924 V

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1. The overpotential (η) was calculated as:η = ERHE − 0 V

The overpotential required to reach a current density of 10 mA cm−2 was extracted from 85% iR-corrected polarization curves. A compensation lower than 100% was intentionally adopted to avoid overcompensation and potentiostat instability while significantly reducing the effect of the uncompensated solution resistance. The same correction procedure was applied to all samples to ensure a consistent comparison of HER performance.

Tafel slopes were obtained by fitting the linear region of the Tafel equation:η = b log(j) + aHere (b) is the Tafel slope (mV dec−1) and (j) is the current density (mA cm−2).

The electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was estimated from the double-layer capacitance (Cdl). Cyclic voltammetry was performed in a non-faradaic region (0–0.1 V vs. RHE) at scan rates of 10–100 mV s−1. The capacitive current density difference was calculated as:Δj = (ja + |jc|)/2where ja and jc are the anodic and cathodic current densities at the midpoint potential. The slope of the plot of Δj versus scan rate yields Cdl:Cdl = Δj/ν

The ECSA was calculated using:ECSA = Cdl/Cswhere Cs is the specific capacitance of a smooth surface (typically 40 µF cm−2 in alkaline media).34 Since the actual specific capacitance depends on the surface composition, morphology, and electrochemical environment, the reported ECSA values should be considered approximate rather than absolute. Nevertheless, because the same assumption was applied to all samples prepared and measured under identical conditions, the calculated values provide a meaningful basis for relative comparison within the present ZnO/CuO series.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was conducted over the frequency range 0.1 Hz–100 kHz with an AC amplitude of 10 mV at −0.301 V vs. RHE (corresponding to 10 mA cm−2). Long-term stability was assessed by chronopotentiometry at a constant current density of 10 mA cm−2 for 15 h.

Results and discussion

Structural and compositional evolution from precursors to oxide nanocomposites

Molecular-level characterization of metal-succinate coordination precursors

The FTIR spectroscopy provides unambiguous molecular-level evidence for successful succinate coordination to divalent metal centers and reveals systematic evolution of coordination geometry as a function of copper incorporation (Fig. 2a). The characteristic asymmetric and symmetric carboxylate stretching vibrations appear at 1522–1600 cm−1 (νa(COO−)) and 1386–1400 cm−1 (νs(COO−)), diagnostic of metal–carboxylate coordination.21 The frequency separation Δν = νa − νs serves as a sensitive probe of carboxylate binding mode.35

(a) FTIR, (b) XRD and (c) UV-vis characterization of Zn–Cu succinate precursors.

In zinc succinate (ZnSuc), Δν = 136 cm−1 indicates predominantly bidentate chelating or bridging coordination, consistent with the preference of d10 Zn2+ for regular octahedral coordination geometry.36 Copper succinate (CuSuc) exhibits substantially larger separation (Δν = 200 cm−1), signaling monodentate or highly asymmetric bidentate binding. This distinctive coordination behavior originates from the Jahn–Teller instability inherent to d9 Cu2+ centers, which induces spontaneous distortion of the octahedral coordination sphere typically elongation along one axis resulting in markedly inequivalent Cu–O bond distances and consequent asymmetry in carboxylate coordination.37,38 The heterometallic precursors ZnCuSuc-A, -B, and -C display intermediate Δν values with systematic progression toward Cu-like coordination, providing compelling evidence for genuine mixed-metal complex formation rather than physically admixed phases. The absence of acetate or residual carbonate peaks confirms >99% phase purity.

Complementary vibrational features corroborate the coordination polymer structure. Bands at 1124–1105 cm−1 (C–O stretching of the succinate backbone) and 894, 780 cm−1 (C–C skeletal modes)21 confirm ligand integrity, while low-frequency absorptions at 509 and 375 cm−1 assignable to metal–oxygen stretching and bending vibrations intensify progressively from ZnCuSuc-A through ZnCuSuc-C. This systematic enhancement in M–O vibrational intensity directly reflects increasing density of metal–ligand coordination bonds and formation of more extensive three-dimensional coordination networks with rising Cu content.38

X-ray diffraction patterns of the precursors (Fig. 2b) reveal significant structural differences as a function of Cu content. ZnSuc displays sharp, well-defined peaks characteristic of a highly crystalline Zn-succinate framework.21,36 In contrast, CuSuc shows broader peaks with reduced intensity, indicative of lower crystallinity and possible amorphous contributions.39 This loss of long-range periodicity is a direct structural manifestation of the Jahn–Teller effect: the d9 electronic configuration of Cu2+ renders the regular octahedral geometry electronically unstable, driving spontaneous distortion that disrupts three-dimensional periodicity.40 ZnCuSuc-A retains ZnSuc-like reflections with additional peaks marked by asterisks, suggesting partial Cu incorporation into the lattice or formation of mixed-metal phases. ZnCuSuc-C exhibits pronounced peak broadening and diminished intensity, consistent with increased structural disorder and microstrain arising from higher Cu content. These observations imply that Cu disrupts the Zn-succinate framework, introducing heterogeneity and reducing long-range order. Although the close ionic radii of Zn2+ and Cu2+ favor substitution at low Cu levels, their incompatible coordination geometries and coordination mode hinder the formation of homogeneous solid solutions, promoting nanoscale structural heterogeneity, a behavior consistent with previously reported Zn–Cu coordination polymers and mixed-metal oxalates, where heterometallic distribution leads to lattice distortion and reduced coherence length.41

Optical absorption measurements further support the phase coexistence and the evolution of electronic environments as a function of Cu content (Fig. 2c). ZnSuc exhibits absorption predominantly in the UV region, corresponding to ligand-centered transitions typical of d0 zinc systems (white color observed, Fig. S1). Upon introduction of Cu2+, a distinct and broad absorption band appears in the 600–800 nm region (visible range), which intensifies with Cu content. This is the characteristic d–d transition band of Cu(ii) in an octahedral or distorted octahedral field, specifically the 2Eg → 2T2g transition42 (blue color observed, Fig. S1). The broadness of the band is consistent with the Jahn–Teller effect43 and vibronic coupling.42 The absence of sharp features confirms the coordination polymer environment, as opposed to isolated ions. The increasing intensity of this band across the series (A < B < C) provides direct optical evidence for the rising concentration of Cu(ii) centers in the structure. Together with FTIR and XRD, the UV-visible data confirm that the mixed precursors consist of crystalline succinate-bound Zn and Cu species whose relative proportions are controlled by the Cu atomic fraction, supporting the interpretation that ZnCuSuc materials are intimate mixtures of Zn-succinate and Cu-succinate building blocks that will act as composite precursors upon thermal transformation.

Characterization of decomposition products: formation of ZnO–CuO mixed oxides

FTIR (Fig. 3a) confirms the transformation of Zn–Cu succinate precursors into mixed metal oxides upon calcination, as organic ligand vibrations disappear, replaced by low-frequency M–O bands around 400–600 cm−1. Zn–O stretching modes appear near 380 cm−1, while Cu–O modes emerge around 405–636 cm−1, indicating complete organic decomposition to oxide phases. The gradual increase in the intensity of these low-frequency bands with increasing Cu content further supports the progressive incorporation of CuO within the ZnO matrix. This aligns with thermal decomposition pathways of metal carboxylates/succinates, yielding metal oxides via multi-step processes.44

(a) FTIR spectra; (b) XRD patterns of pristine ZnO, CuO and all the composite materials (ZCu-A, ZCu-B and ZCu-C).

Powder XRD unambiguously establishes the phase composition and crystallographic structure of oxide products (Fig. 3b). Pure zinc oxide exhibits the characteristic diffraction signature of hexagonal wurtzite structure (space group P63mc), with prominent Bragg reflections at 2θ ≈ 31.8°, 34.4°, and 36.3° indexed to the (100), (002), and (101) crystallographic planes, respectively (JCPDS 36-1451).45 The sharp, intense nature of these peaks indicates formation of highly crystalline ZnO with well-developed long-range order and large coherent scattering domains characteristic of oxide materials derived from well-ordered coordination polymer precursors following controlled thermal decomposition.20,21

Upon copper incorporation, two additional diffraction features emerge at 2θ ≈ 35.5° and 38.7°, which are definitively assigned to the (002) and (200) reflections of monoclinic copper(ii) oxide (tenorite structure, space group C2/c, JCPDS 01-072-0629).46,47 The integrated intensity of these CuO reflections increases systematically and monotonically across the compositional series (ZCu-A → ZCu-B → ZCu-C), providing quantitative crystallographic confirmation of progressive CuO phase accumulation. Concomitantly, the relative intensity of the principal ZnO (101) reflection at 36.3° undergoes systematic diminution in composite materials relative to phase-pure ZnO. This intensity attenuation reflects two synergistic effects: (i) simple dilution of the ZnO phase by increasing CuO volume fraction, and (ii) more subtle disruption of ZnO crystalline perfection induced by nanoscale phase boundaries and interfacial strain fields at ZnO–CuO heterojunctions.48,49

Significantly, no diffraction signatures corresponding to ternary Zn–Cu oxide phases (e.g., ZnCuO2) or zinc-substituted copper oxide solid solutions are detected across the entire compositional range. This phase separation behavior wherein ZnO and CuO coexist as discrete yet intimately interfaced crystallographic domains is thermodynamically rationalized by the fundamentally incompatible crystal chemistry of the constituent oxides. Although Zn2+ and Cu2+ possess nearly identical ionic radii in octahedral coordination (0.74 Å versus 0.73 Å), they exhibit diametrically opposed coordination preferences: Zn2+ strongly favors tetrahedral sites within the wurtzite structure, whereas Cu2+ preferentially occupies square-planar or distorted octahedral environments in the tenorite lattice due to Jahn–Teller stabilization.50 These mutually exclusive coordination geometries preclude formation of extensive solid solutions, instead stabilizing biphasic nanocomposites with discrete ZnO and CuO crystalline domains separated by atomically sharp heterojunction interfaces.51

Critically, this nanoscale biphasic architecture wherein ZnO (n-type, wide bandgap) and CuO (p-type, narrow bandgap) are intimately intermixed (due to the used synthesis method) with extensive interfacial contact represents the ideal structural motif for p–n heterojunction formation. The high density of ZnO–CuO interfaces maximizes the spatial extent of band-bending regions and built-in electric fields, thereby promoting efficient charge carrier separation and interfacial electron transfer key prerequisites for enhanced electrocatalytic activity.25,26,52 Previous investigations have consistently demonstrated that ZnO–CuO composites derived from molecularly-mixed organic precursors exhibit precisely this biphasic nanocomposite character, with interfacial interactions dominating functional properties.53,54 Finally, the coexistence of Zn-succinate and Cu-succinate phases in the precursors, as revealed by their XRD patterns prior to calcination, explains the direct formation of ZnO–CuO composites. The simultaneous decomposition of both succinates ensures intimate mixing of the oxide phases from the earliest stages of thermal treatment, yielding structurally integrated composites without the need for post-synthetic processing.

Morphology of ZnO and ZnO–CuO composite oxides

Fig. 4 shows the SEM micrographs of ZnO, ZCu-A and ZCu-C obtained after calcination of the corresponding succinate precursors. The ZnO sample (Fig. 4a) is composed of quasi-spherical to slightly elongated nanoparticles with relatively narrow size distribution and moderate agglomeration. The particles are homogeneously distributed, forming a porous network typical of ZnO derived from organic coordination precursors, where gas evolution during thermal decomposition promotes nanostructuration.55

SEM images and histogram of particle size distribution with their average particle size of (a) ZnO, (b) ZCu-A and (c) ZCu-C.

In ZCu-A (Fig. 4b), the overall morphology resembles that of ZnO but with increased agglomeration and finer granular subdomains, suggesting that at low Cu content CuO is highly dispersed or below the detection limit, consistent with reports that minimal Cu incorporation often preserves the base oxide morphology.55 The homogeneous appearance implies well-distributed Cu species within the ZnO matrix, likely as highly dispersed CuO clusters or nanoscale substitutional defects resulting from intimate precursor mixing. In contrast, ZCu-C (Fig. 4c) exhibits a markedly different morphology characterized by irregular aggregates composed of smaller nanoparticles, leading to a rougher and more heterogeneous surface texture. This morphological evolution correlates well with the FTIR and XRD data, which evidenced the clear coexistence of crystalline ZnO and CuO phases at high Cu content. The presence of distinct CuO domains likely disrupts the ZnO growth mechanism during calcination, favoring nucleation of secondary particles and stronger aggregation. Similar morphological transitions from uniform ZnO nanoparticles to heterogeneous composite aggregates have been reported in ZnO–CuO systems prepared via sol–gel.54,56

Elemental composition analysis by EDX (Fig. S2 and Table 1) provides quantitative validation of the copper incorporation achieved through the succinate coprecipitation route. Optical properties

The UV-visible diffuse reflectance spectra (Fig. 5) reveal key features of ZnO and ZnO–CuO composites. Pure ZnO (Fig. S3a) appears white, consistent with its sharp absorption edge below 400 nm and wide bandgap of ∼3.2 eV (Fig. 5b and S4a).58 With Cu incorporation, the powders progressively darken, showing black coloration (Fig. S3b–e). This visual change reflects a red-shift of the absorption edge and enhanced absorption across 400–700 nm. The effect arises from CuO's narrow bandgap (∼1.7 eV), which strongly absorbs visible light and interfacial charge-transfer transitions at the ZnO–CuO junction,59 where electrons can be excited from the CuO valence band into the ZnO conduction band.60

XPS analysis

To complement the structural characterization, XPS was employed to investigate the surface chemical states of Zn and Cu and to assess possible electronic interactions between both oxide phases (Table 2 and Fig. 6). The survey spectra (Fig. 6a) confirm the presence of Zn, Cu and O as the only constituent elements of the composites, apart from the adventitious carbon commonly used for charge referencing, with no evidence of significant surface impurities.

The Zn 2p3/2 binding energy increases slightly from 1021.27 eV for pristine ZnO to 1021.52 eV for ZCu-C (Fig. 6b). In contrast, the Cu 2p3/2 main peak shifts from 932.24 eV for ZCu-A to 932.54 eV for ZCu-C, while remaining 0.40–0.70 eV lower than that of the CuO reference (932.94 eV). The opposite evolution of the Zn and Cu binding energies is consistent with electronic interaction between the ZnO and CuO phases and is compatible with the formation of Cu–O–Zn interfacial linkages. Such counter-directional shifts have been reported for electronically coupled ZnO/CuO heterostructures and are generally regarded as evidence of interfacial charge redistribution.26

The Cu 2p region of pristine ZnO is featureless, confirming the absence of detectable Cu contamination (Fig. 6c). For ZCu-A, only weak Cu 2p components are detected and no shake-up satellites are resolved, consistent with the low Cu concentration close to the XPS detection limit and possible partial photoreduction during analysis. In contrast, ZCu-B, ZCu-C and the CuO reference exhibit the characteristic Cu2+ spectral envelope with shake-up satellites between 940 and 943 eV, confirming that copper is predominantly present in the Cu2+ oxidation state at higher Cu loadings.61 The progressive increase in Cu 2p intensity from ZCu-A to ZCu-C further corroborates the increasing Cu surface concentration and independently supports the XRD and SEM results, which reveal the gradual development of a distinct CuO phase. The O 1s binding energy remains nearly constant for all ZnO-based samples (530.08–530.22 eV), whereas the CuO reference exhibits a lower binding energy (529.45 eV), reflecting the different oxygen coordination environment in tenorite. No systematic O 1s shift is observed across the composite series, indicating that the principal electronic changes detected by XPS are associated with the cationic environments rather than the oxygen lattice. These observations are consistent with the biphasic ZnO/CuO architecture evidenced by XRD and SEM and agree with previous studies in which intimate ZnO/CuO interfaces were confirmed by HRTEM.26,62 Overall, the XPS results confirm the progressive incorporation of Cu into the ZnO-based composites and provide spectroscopic evidence of electronic interaction between the ZnO and CuO phases, supporting the formation of ZnO/CuO heterointerfaces proposed from the complementary structural analyses.

Electrocatalysis performance evaluation

The electrocatalytic HER performance of ZnO and ZnO–CuO composites was evaluated through polarization curves, overpotential analysis, Tafel slopes, EIS, ECSA determination, and stability tests. A comprehensive cross-analysis of these results reveals that HER enhancement in ZnO–CuO composites is primarily governed by interfacial charge-transfer efficiency rather than surface area effects.

Overpotential analysis

Fig. 7a presents the polarization curves for bare glassy carbon (GC), pristine ZnO, and the ZnO–CuO composites (ZCu-A, ZCu-B, and ZCu-C). The overpotential required to achieve a current density of 10 mA cm−2, a standardized benchmark for comparing HER electrocatalysts,63 was extracted from the 85% iR-corrected LSV curves and is summarized in Fig. 7b.

Pristine ZnO demonstrates moderate electrocatalytic performance with an overpotential of 539 mV, which, while substantially better than GC, remains far from the activity of state-of-the-art electrocatalysts. This limited performance stems from ZnO's intrinsic drawbacks: wide bandgap (3.2 eV), poor electrical conductivity, and unfavorable hydrogen adsorption energetics on its surface.18,19 For comparison, a CuO reference electrode was also evaluated under identical experimental conditions. However, CuO did not reach a cathodic current density of 10 mA cm−2 within the investigated potential window and was therefore not included in the comparative overpotential analysis. Upon incorporation of copper, a progressive improvement in performance is observed. ZCu-A exhibits an overpotential of 499 mV, indicating a modest enhancement relative to pristine ZnO. SEM analysis (Fig. 4b) shows that ZCu-A retains a morphology similar to ZnO, suggesting that copper species at this loading level (6 at% Cu, Table 1) are relatively well dispersed, with limited formation of extended interfacial regions.

A further increase in Cu content reveals that the improvement in HER activity is non-linear. ZCu-B exhibits a substantially lower overpotential (η10 = 322 mV), representing a decrease of 217 mV compared with pristine ZnO and 177 mV relative to ZCu-A. Increasing the Cu content further to 24 at% (ZCu-C) results in a smaller additional decrease in overpotential (η10 = 301 mV), indicating that the catalytic enhancement approaches a plateau within the investigated composition range rather than increasing proportionally with Cu loading. Moreover, limiting the use of CuO is always a good deal for large scale application.

This compositional dependence is consistent with the structural and spectroscopic characterization. At low Cu loading (ZCu-A), CuO is only weakly detected by XRD and the Cu signal in XPS remains close to the detection limit, indicating that only a limited amount of CuO is present at the surface. With increasing Cu content, the CuO phase becomes progressively more evident in XRD and SEM observations, while XPS reveals binding-energy shifts consistent with electronic interaction between ZnO and CuO through Cu–O–Zn interfacial bonding. These results indicate a progressive evolution of the biphasic ZnO/CuO structure as the Cu content increases.

Interestingly, the electrochemical trends cannot be explained simply by changes in surface area. As discussed in Section 3.4.4, the electrochemically active surface area decreases from ZnO to ZCu-C, whereas the specific activity continuously increases, demonstrating that the improvement in HER performance arises primarily from enhanced intrinsic catalytic activity rather than from an increase in the number of accessible active sites. Moreover, the EIS analysis presented in Section 3.4.3 shows that the impedance response is better described by two coupled interfacial relaxation processes than by a conventional single charge-transfer process. Consequently, the superior HER performance of ZCu-C is more reasonably attributed to changes in the interfacial electrochemical behaviour induced by Cu incorporation25,26,64 than to variations in either surface area or a single charge-transfer resistance. Within the investigated composition range, ZCu-C therefore provides the best overall electrocatalytic performance. Nevertheless, the progressively smaller improvement observed between 12 and 24 at% Cu suggests that exploring intermediate compositions would be valuable for identifying the optimum balance between Cu incorporation and catalytic activity. These findings align with recent reports demonstrating that ZnO–CuO heterostructures exhibit composition-dependent electrocatalytic activity, with optimal performance achieved at intermediate Cu : Zn ratios where heterojunction density is maximized while avoiding excessive phase separation.65–67

Kinetic analysis from Tafel plots

To elucidate HER kinetics and identify the rate-determining step, Tafel analysis was performed on LSV-derived plots (Fig. 8, detailed fitting in Fig. S6). Pristine ZnO exhibited a relatively low Tafel slope of 76 mV dec−1, which falls within the range commonly associated with mixed or intermediate HER kinetics, including possible contributions from Heyrovsky-type pathways.68 While lower than expected for typical oxide systems, such values have been reported for ZnO-based electrocatalysts and may reflect complex surface processes, including hydroxylation, partial reduction, or the coexistence of multiple reaction pathways.19

Tafel plots for GC, ZnO, ZCu-A, ZCu-B and ZCu-C.

In contrast, copper incorporation led to a significant increase in Tafel slopes: ZCu-A (275 mV dec−1), ZCu-B (218 mV dec−1), and ZCu-C (334 mV dec−1). These values exceed the theoretical Volmer-limited slope (120 mV dec−1),7,9 suggesting that the reaction kinetics cannot be described by a simple elementary step model. A literal reading of the Tafel slope as a mechanistic indicator would imply that ZCu-B and ZCu-C are less efficient than ZnO and ZCu-A, which contradicts both the lower overpotentials and the higher specific activities of these samples. This apparent inconsistency is resolved by recognising that the classical Tafel framework applies only when a single elementary step is rate-limiting at a kinetically uniform surface. The EIS data for ZCu-B and ZCu-C show no resolved charge-transfer arc and a progressively diffusion-like CPE exponent (n decreasing to 0.57 and 0.48 for the fast arc), indicating that the current–potential relationship at these electrodes is shaped by several coupled processes simultaneously.4 The slope extracted from such a composite response is an operational apparent slope and carries no simple mechanistic interpretation; values exceeding 120 mV dec−1 have been explicitly noted to be non-diagnostic in heterogeneous oxide systems. The monotonic increase in specific activity across the series is a more direct and less assumption-laden indicator of the improvement in intrinsic site activity with Cu loading.

Consequently, the extracted Tafel slopes for these samples are more appropriately interpreted as apparent parameters reflecting the overall electrode response rather than the symmetry factor of a single rate-determining step.69,70 Overall, these observations highlight the limitations of conventional Tafel analysis for multiphase oxide systems. Further operando or in situ investigations would be required to disentangle the respective contributions of surface chemistry, electronic structure, and interfacial effects.71,72

EIS analysis

To gain quantitative insight into the interfacial processes governing the HER, EIS measurements were performed at an overpotential of −301 mV vs. RHE, corresponding to the potential required for ZCu-C to reach a current density of 10 mA cm−2. The Nyquist plots (Fig. 9) reveal a progressive evolution of the impedance response as the Cu content increases. Pristine ZnO exhibits a large depressed semicircle followed by a low-frequency tail, whereas Cu incorporation progressively modifies the impedance profile, leading to a less distinct separation between the high- and low-frequency contributions. This evolution indicates that the electrode response cannot be adequately represented by a single charge-transfer process.

EIS Nyquist plots for ZnO, ZCu-A, ZCu-B and ZCu-C.

To identify the most appropriate electrical representation, six equivalent circuits (C1–C6) were fitted to all four spectra using the reduced chi-squared statistic (χred2) as the goodness-of-fit criterion (Table S1). The classical Randles-CPE circuit incorporating a semi-infinite Warburg element (C1) failed to converge for ZnO and ZCu-A, with the charge-transfer resistance reaching the upper fitting limit and the CPE exponent converging towards n = 1. Likewise, the simplified one-time-constant models (C2, C3 and C6) yielded substantially larger χred2 values, indicating that a single interfacial process is insufficient to describe the impedance response. Although the bounded Warburg model (C5) provided acceptable fits for ZCu-B and ZCu-C, it failed to reproduce satisfactorily the complete dataset.

Among the evaluated models, the two-time-constant circuit, Rs + (Rct1‖CPE1) + (Rct2‖CPE2), where Rs represents the solution resistance, Rct1 the charge-transfer resistance, Rct2 the slower interfacial process (the gas-adsorption resistance73,74), and CPE a constant phase element accounting for non-ideal capacitive behavior,75–77 consistently produced the lowest χred2 values (0.34–1.41 × 10−3)78 The fit quality improved by one to two orders of magnitude compared with the conventional Randles circuit, indicating that at least two kinetically distinct relaxation processes contribute to the impedance response. Such behaviour is physically consistent with the biphasic ZnO/CuO architecture73 revealed by XRD, SEM and XPS.

The fitted parameters are summarized in Table 3. The solution resistance (Rs) remains nearly constant for all electrodes (8.4–10 Ω cm2), confirming that Cu incorporation has negligible influence on the uncompensated electrolyte resistance. In contrast, the first charge-transfer resistance (Rct1) varies significantly with composition. ZCu-A exhibits the lowest Rct1 (19.0 Ω cm2), whereas ZCu-B displays the highest value (76.1 Ω cm2). Interestingly, this evolution does not directly follow the HER activity trend, since ZCu-C exhibits the lowest overpotential despite a moderate Rct1 (42.2 Ω cm2). This observation demonstrates that the HER performance cannot be interpreted solely in terms of a single charge-transfer resistance, but instead results from the combined contribution of several interfacial processes.

The first constant phase element exhibits n1 values between 0.48 and 0.65, significantly lower than unity, confirming pronounced non-ideal capacitive behaviour associated with a distribution of relaxation times and interfacial heterogeneity.76 After a slight increase for ZCu-A, n1 decreases progressively to 0.48 for ZCu-C, approaching the value expected for transport-influenced impedance behaviour. Rather than reflecting intrinsic catalytic activity, the CPE exponent mainly describes the increasing structural and electrochemical heterogeneity of the electrode surface. This trend agrees well with the progressive evolution from predominantly ZnO particles toward a more heterogeneous ZnO/CuO biphasic structure observed by SEM, XRD and XPS, while the accompanying low-frequency contribution in the Nyquist plots indicates an increasing influence of coupled transport and interfacial polarization phenomena.

The second relaxation process could only be resolved for ZnO and ZCu-C. For ZCu-A and ZCu-B, the fitting procedure did not converge to a unique finite Rct2 because of strong parameter correlation, and these values are therefore reported as not resolved. This behaviour is consistent with the limited development of ZnO/CuO interfacial domains at low and intermediate Cu contents, where XPS still detects weak Cu signals and XRD reveals only the initial formation of the CuO phase. The appearance of a resolvable second relaxation process for ZCu-C therefore supports the existence of an additional slow interfacial contribution that becomes significant only once the biphasic architecture is sufficiently developed.

From a physical perspective, the high-frequency process is tentatively attributed to charge transfer at the oxide/electrolyte interface, including contributions from ZnO grains and exposed CuO domains, whereas the low-frequency process may originate from slower interfacial polarization across ZnO/CuO heterointerfaces73 and/or adsorption of HER intermediates.26,52,74,79 Because equivalent-circuit analysis is inherently non-unique,80 these assignments should be regarded as plausible interpretations rather than definitive identifications.

The impedance analysis is fully consistent with the structural and spectroscopic characterization. XRD demonstrates the progressive development of the CuO phase with increasing Cu loading, while XPS reveals opposite shifts of the Zn 2p and Cu 2p binding energies, consistent with electronic interaction across ZnO/CuO interfaces. Together, these observations indicate that Cu incorporation primarily modifies the interfacial electrochemical response rather than the ohmic resistance of the electrodes. Although the impedance becomes increasingly influenced by transport-related phenomena at high Cu loadings, the simultaneous decrease in HER overpotential demonstrates that the beneficial electronic interaction created by the ZnO/CuO interfaces outweighs the additional transport limitations. Consequently, ZCu-C achieves the best overall HER performance despite exhibiting the most non-ideal impedance response, illustrating that electrocatalytic activity is governed by the interplay between charge transfer, transport phenomena and interfacial electronic coupling rather than by a single kinetic parameter. However, neither EIS nor XPS alone can identify the individual catalytic active sites or directly demonstrate the presence of built-in electric fields, which would require complementary techniques such as Mott–Schottky analysis or ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy.

ECSA analysis

The ECSA, commonly used as a proxy for the number of accessible catalytic sites, was estimated from the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) obtained via cyclic voltammetry in a non-faradaic potential region (0–0.1 V vs. RHE) at various scan rates (10–100 mV s−1). Representative CV curves for ZCu-C at different scan rates are shown in Fig. S5, while the scan-rate dependence of current density for all samples is presented in Fig. 10. Fig. S7 presents the corresponding linear regression analysis with associated standard errors.

Scan rate dependence of the current densities at 0.05 V for ZnO, ZCu-A, ZCu-B and ZCu-C.

The extracted Cdl values show a non-monotonic trend across the series: ZnO (78.7 µF cm−2), ZCu-A (69.1 µF cm−2), ZCu-B (77.7 µF cm−2), and ZCu-C (55.7 µF cm−2). Using a specific capacitance value of 40 µF cm−2 for oxide surfaces in alkaline media, the corresponding ECSA values were estimated as ZnO (1.97), ZCu-A (1.73), ZCu-B (1.94), and ZCu-C (1.39).

Notably, the best-performing sample (ZCu-C) exhibits the lowest estimated ECSA, indicating the absence of a direct correlation between electrochemically accessible surface area and HER activity in this system. This suggests that factors beyond surface accessibility, including electronic and interfacial properties, contribute significantly to the observed electrocatalytic behavior, consistent with the trends identified from EIS.

The reduced ECSA of ZCu-C is consistent with its morphology (Fig. 4c), which shows heterogeneous aggregates that may partially limit surface accessibility. Nevertheless, the remaining active sites appear to exhibit higher apparent activity. This is illustrated by normalizing the current density at −0.3 V vs. RHE by ECSA, yielding ZnO (0.13 mA cm−2-ECSA), ZCu-A (0.18 mA cm−2-ECSA), ZCu-B (0.31 mA cm−2-ECSA), and ZCu-C (0.42 mA cm−2-ECSA). The specific activity increases by over 300% from ZnO to ZCu-C, confirming that each accessible site on ZCu-C is far more efficient.

Stability and benchmark comparison

The durability of the best-performing ZCu-C composite was evaluated by chronopotentiometry at a constant current density of 10 mA cm−2 for 15 h in 1.0 M KOH. As shown in Fig. 11a, the overpotential remains relatively stable throughout the test, indicating moderate electrochemical durability under the investigated conditions. A gradual increase of approximately 75 mV is observed after 15 h, corresponding to a partial loss of catalytic activity. Although this level of stability is encouraging for an oxide-based heterostructure, it remains below that typically reported for more established HER electrocatalysts such as transition-metal sulfides or phosphides, indicating that further optimization is required for long-term operation.

(a) Chronopotentiometry of ZCu-C, and (b) its LSV polarization curves before and after chronopotentiometry.

Fig. 11b compares the polarization curves recorded before and after the chronopotentiometric test. The catalyst retains a broadly similar electrochemical response, with an increase in overpotential at 10 mA cm−2 of approximately 75 mV. The overall shape of the polarization curve is largely preserved, indicating that the dominant electrochemical behaviour remains essentially unchanged after prolonged operation. Nevertheless, these electrochemical measurements alone do not allow possible surface, interfacial or structural modifications occurring during HER operation to be identified.

These observations are consistent with the impedance analysis presented in Section 3.4.3, which indicates that the electrochemical response of ZCu-C arises from multiple coupled interfacial processes rather than from a single charge-transfer step. Although the retained polarization profile indicates that the catalyst remains electrochemically active after 15 h, it cannot be used as evidence that the ZnO/CuO heterointerfaces or the phase composition remain unchanged during operation. Consequently, the electrochemical stability observed here should not be interpreted as direct proof of structural stability.

Overall, the ZCu-C composite demonstrates reasonable short-term durability, retaining its main electrochemical characteristics over 15 h of alkaline HER operation. However, this duration is insufficient for assessing the long-term stability required for practical water electrolysis. Moreover, no post-operando structural or surface characterization was performed in the present study. Therefore, possible phase, morphological or compositional changes induced by HER operation cannot be evaluated directly. Future work will combine extended durability testing with post-operando XRD, SEM and XPS analyses to identify degradation mechanisms and establish the long-term robustness of ZnO/CuO heterostructured electrodes. The benchmark comparison places ZCu-C among the most competitive non-noble-metal ZnO-based HER electrocatalysts reported to date, surpassing the succinate-derived 16% Co–ZnO (385 mV) by 76 mV without the use of any dopant transition metals other than earth-abundant copper. Compared with the Fe-doped ZnO prepared by the related malonate route,21 ZCu-C achieves a substantially lower overpotential, demonstrating that p–n heterojunction construction is a more effective strategy than single-element doping for overcoming ZnO's intrinsic conductivity limitations.

Conclusions

ZnO–CuO heterostructured composites with different Cu contents were successfully synthesized via a succinate-assisted co-precipitation route followed by thermal decomposition. This simple and potentially scalable approach enabled the formation of biphasic ZnO/CuO materials without the use of noble metals or complex synthesis procedures. XRD confirmed the coexistence of ZnO and CuO phases, while SEM-EDX revealed a homogeneous distribution of Cu within the ZnO matrix. XPS core-level analysis showed binding-energy shifts of the Cu 2p and Zn 2p peaks that are consistent with electronic interaction through Cu–O–Zn interfacial bonding.

Among the investigated compositions, the 24 at% Cu sample (ZCu-C) exhibited the best electrocatalytic performance toward the hydrogen evolution reaction in 1 M KOH, requiring an overpotential of 301 mV to achieve 10 mA cm−2 and maintaining stable operation over 15 h of chronopotentiometric testing. Although the estimated electrochemically active surface area decreased with increasing Cu content, the specific activity increased by more than threefold compared with pristine ZnO, indicating that the enhanced HER performance originates primarily from improved intrinsic catalytic activity rather than from an increase in accessible surface area.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy demonstrated that the impedance response of all electrodes is more accurately described by a two-time-constant equivalent circuit than by the conventional Randles model. The fitted parameters indicate that Cu incorporation modifies the interfacial electrochemical response and increases the heterogeneity of the electrode surface, consistent with the structural characteristics observed by SEM and the coexistence of ZnO and CuO phases. These results highlight the importance of interfacial engineering in tuning the electrochemical behaviour of oxide-based HER electrocatalysts.

Overall, this work demonstrates that controlled Cu incorporation is an effective strategy for improving the HER performance of ZnO through the formation of ZnO/CuO heterostructured composites. Future studies should focus on optimizing the Cu loading, directly probing the ZnO/CuO heterointerfaces using high-resolution techniques such as HRTEM or STEM, investigating the electronic properties of the junction by complementary methods (e.g., Mott–Schottky analysis or UPS), and evaluating long-term durability through extended electrochemical testing coupled with post-operando characterization. These efforts will contribute to the rational development of efficient, low-cost and sustainable oxide-based electrocatalysts for alkaline water electrolysis.
