Relationship between anti-diabetic medication use and glycaemic control: a retrospective diabetes registry-based cohort study in Singapore
Authors: Sze Ling Chan, Charyl Jia Qi Yap, Yingqi Xu, Sing Yi Chia, Nur Nasyitah Binte Mohamed Salim, Darryl Mingjun Lim, Edward Choke, David Carmody, Gilbert Choon Seng Tan, Su-Yen Goh, Yong Mong Bee, Tze Tec Chong

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the association between diabetes mellitus (DM) medication use and glycaemic control.DesignThis was a retrospective diabetes registry-based cohort study.SettingSingapore.ParticipantsPatients aged 18 and above with incident DM in the SingHealth Diabetes Registry from 2013 to 2020 were included. The entire study period included a 1 year baseline period, a 1 year observation period and a 3 month outcome period.Outcome measuresDrug use was measured using the proportion of days covered (PDC), and the changes in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) between the outcome and baseline periods were assessed. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is an increasingly common chronic disease. By 2030, in Singapore, its prevalence is projected to reach 13.7% in the adult population.1 DM is associated with a range of microvascular and macrovascular complications such as cardiovascular disease, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, retinopathy, neuropathy and nephropathy.2

Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), reflecting average glycaemia over the past 3 months, has been used as a measurable biomarker of glycaemic levels internationally since the late 1970s.3 As a surrogate marker, the concentration of HbA1c predicts the progression of microvascular and macrovascular complications.4 5 The well-known UK Prospective Diabetes Study confirmed that a 1% reduction in HbA1c in patients with type 2 DM was associated with a 25–37% reduction in macrovascular and microvascular complications.5 Therefore, HbA1c <7.0% is recommended as an important indicator of optimal glycaemic control.6

In addition to healthy lifestyle behaviours, the use of glucose-lowering medications, including oral hypoglycaemic agents (OHAs) and insulin, provides adequate efficacy to achieve and maintain treatment goals.7 However, it is estimated that about a third of patients with DM have poor glycaemic control,8 signalling a target area for reducing the risk of DM complications and the morbidity and mortality they carry, as well as non-adherence to treatment. Previous studies on the use of DM medication and glycaemic control have been conducted in Singapore.9 10 The first study was a retrospective cohort study involving 2463 patients with newly diagnosed DM from 2005 to 2010, which reported the prevalence of medication adherence as 65.0% and pointed out that poor adherence was associated with increased HbA1c level, hospitalisation and emergency department visit.10 Another 5 year longitudinal study of 1256 patients conducted between 2005 and 2012 also showed that receiving insulin, biguanides or alpha-glucosidase inhibitors independently increased the risk of deteriorating glycaemic control in the first 2 years of follow-up.9

However, as the data collection of these two local studies was completed a decade ago, the findings were limited by the variety of drug classes; for example, glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors were not approved in Singapore at the time of these trials. Second, only patients from primary care clinics were included, with brief baseline characteristics provided, which limited the sample size and the representativeness of the diabetic patient population. Therefore, by employing a cluster-wide diabetes registry that contains recent data in a larger population, this study aimed to determine the association between the use of DM medications and glycaemic control. Specifically, we tested two hypotheses. First, baseline glycaemic control is associated with adherence to DM medication, and second, the adherence to DM medication is associated with subsequent glycaemic control.

Methods

Setting and patient population

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients aged 18 and above with incident DM in the SingHealth Diabetes Registry (SDR) from 2013 to 2020. The SDR has been previously described, but briefly, it contains data of patients with DM across primary and tertiary care in SingHealth from 2013 onwards, an academic medical centre and the largest of three public healthcare clusters in Singapore providing primary care, tertiary care, long-term care facilities and specialty centres.11 Data for patients who satisfy the DM case ascertainment criteria are drawn into the SDR annually.11 Patients with a length of DM of 0 years at the earliest entry year were deemed to be incident cases and included in this study. Patients also need to have data across at least two calendar years and be alive over the study period (see below). In addition, only patients with complete dispensing duration data (existing or imputed) and a weighted sample of those with no dispensings of DM medications in the first 2 years were included for the first hypothesis (see below). For the second hypothesis, the subset of patients with available HbA1c values both in the baseline and outcome periods was included (figures1 2). This study was reviewed and approved by the Centralised Institutional Review Board (reference no. 2020/2587). Patients and the public were not involved in the design or conduct of this study. Informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study, and the analysis used anonymous clinical data. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Statement12 (online supplemental file 1).

Study periods

The earliest calendar year with available data in SDR for each patient was designated the baseline year, the observation period was defined as the following year and the outcome period was defined as the first 3 months of the third year. The entire period of 2 years and 3 months was designated as the study period (figure 1).

Exposures

Baseline demographics and comorbidities were summarised based on values at the baseline year. For body mass index where multiple values may exist, the latest value in the baseline year was used. If more than one result was available on the latest date, the higher value was taken. To obtain a true baseline HbA1c, the latest HbA1c reading in the baseline year before the initiation of any DM medication was taken. For patients with no DM medications dispensed in the baseline year or throughout the whole study period, the latest value in the baseline year was used. For the first hypothesis, baseline glycaemic control, categorised into good (baseline HbA1c≤7.0%) and poor (baseline HbA1c>7.0%) glycaemic control, was the exposure.

For the second hypothesis, the adherence to DM medication in the observation year was the exposure. Classification of DM medications was guided by the Anatomic Therapeutic Chemical System. Medication adherence was measured using the proportion of days covered (PDC) using dispensing data over the whole observation period, stratified by drug classes (online supplemental table S1). We also calculated PDC for ‘any DM medication’ (A10), ‘oral DM medications’ (A10B) and insulins (A10A). Missing data for dispensed duration were imputed based on the quantity of medication dispensed, by taking the median duration by patient, drug and year, followed by patient and drug (if no complete data for the same drug was available in the same year), and finally by drug (if no complete data for same drug were available for the same patient). This way, the imputed duration would match as closely to the patient’s recent dosage as possible. In calculating the PDC, carryovers (leftover medication from a previous prescription) of medications from the baseline year (if any) and overlapping dispensings (refilling a medication before the previous supply is finished) were allowed to mimic real-world usage.

Outcomes

For the first hypothesis, PDC in the observation period was the outcome. PDC was categorised into 0%, >0 to <20%, 20 to <40%, 40 to <60%, 60 to <80% and ≥80%. For the second hypothesis, the change between the latest HbA1c in the outcome period and baseline HbA1c was the outcome.

Statistical analysis

For the first hypothesis, we examined the association between HbA1c and other baseline characteristics with PDC using theX2 test or Kruskal–Wallis test, as appropriate. We then performed logistic regression of HbA1c on PDC ≥0.80, adjusting for other significant baseline variables. The association between PDC and change in HbA1c was analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. All analyses were carried out in R Version 4.2.1.13 The AdhereR package version 0.8.1 was used to calculate adherence measures.14

DM drug use

The median PDC for any DM drug, oral DM drugs and insulin during the observation period were 20.3%, 16.8% and 0%, respectively. The most commonly prescribed OHAs were metformin and sulfonylureas, with 17 901 (49.3%) and 7969 (21.9%) patients taking them in the observation period. When divided into quantiles, a U-shaped pattern can be observed (figure 3). A total of 15 737 (43.3%) patients were not on any DM drug. The proportions of patients with PDC ≥0.80 for any DM drug, oral DM drugs and insulin were 35.1%, 34.4% and 0.1%, respectively.

Association between baseline HbA1c and adherence to DM drug

At baseline, the number of patients with good glycaemic control (HbA1c ≤7.0%), poor glycaemic control (HbA1c >7.0%) and missing HbA1c were 13 841 (38.1%), 12 975 (35.7%) and 9498 (26.2%), respectively.

Those who had good glycaemic control (HbA1c) at baseline were less likely to receive DM drugs, and those with poor baseline glycaemic control or missing HbA1c were more likely to be highly adherent (PDC >80%) (p<2.2 × 10-16) (figure 4). The same relationship also holds for oral DM drugs and different classes of DM drugs (online supplemental figure S1). Insulin users (n=1,511 (4.2%)) tended to be those with poor baseline glycaemic control or have no baseline HbA1c (figure 4).

Association between adherence to DM drug and change in HbA1c

The median HbA1c change between baseline and outcome periods was −0.3% (range: −11.3 to –7.9%). Higher PDC for any DM drug and oral DM drugs during the observation period was significantly associated with a negative HbA1c change in a dose-dependent fashion (p<2.2 x 10-16) (figure 5). Commonly used classes of oral DM drugs followed the same trend (online supplemental figure S2). There was also a significant difference in HbA1c change by PDC for insulin (p<2.2 × 10-16). There was a U-shaped relationship between PDC and achievement of good glycaemic control in the outcome period but an increasing trend for poor glycaemic control and an inverted U-shape for missing outcome HbA1c readings (online supplemental figure S3).

Discussion

The relationship between adherence to DM medication and glycaemic control is a complex and non-monotonic one. We found a U-shaped pattern in PDC during the observation period. More than four in 10 patients with DM did not receive any DM drug in the year after diagnosis. Those with good baseline glycaemic control were less likely to receive DM drugs, which is unsurprising as diet and lifestyle modifications are the first line of treatment for patients with newly diagnosed and mild DM, while considering pharmacotherapy.15 Conversely, patients with poor baseline glycaemic control or missing baseline HbA1c are more likely to receive DM medications, which is expected as pharmacologic agents are more likely to be needed to achieve glycaemic control. Importantly, we demonstrated that better adherence was associated with higher HbA1c reduction, with the most adherent achieving a median of 1.5% reduction. This underscores the importance of encouraging medication adherence in patients with DM.

Among those receiving DM drugs in the observation period, the most commonly used were metformin and sulfonylureas. Furthermore, very few patients used insulin in these first 1–2 years of treatment. This is in line with the recommendations from the WHO Package of Essential Noncommunicable (WHO-PEN), which recommends metformin, sulfonylureas and insulin as the first-, second- and third-line treatment, respectively.16 However, a cohort study of 1 32 737 patients with type 2 DM who initiated second-line antidiabetic medications found that sulfonylureas were associated with comparatively higher cardiovascular risk (HR: 1.36; 95% CI, 1.23 to 1.49), while the short-term cardiovascular outcomes of new classes of OHAs, including GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT-2 inhibitors and DPP-4 inhibitors, were similar.17 Similar results were also shown in another real-world study.18

DPP-4 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT-2 inhibitors have been available since 2007, 2010 and 2014, respectively. Despite this and the fact that these agents are recommended as second-line therapies, their use was very low in our study.7 This is likely because of the high cost of these new OHAs, even though a recent systematic review discovered that newer antidiabetic agents, especially SGLT2i, are more cost-effective.19 Dapagliflozin was only added to the Medication Assistance Fund scheme, which provides subsidies for high-cost medications according to household income, only in mid-2017 and subsequently empagliflozin in 2018.20 21 Sitagliptin and empagliflozin were only added to the standard drug list in 2022 and 2023, respectively.20 22 23 Therefore, cost is still a significant barrier for most patients during the study period.

There are several limitations to this study. First, the SDR only contains data from SingHealth institutions, so data on medications or HbA1c could be missing if these were obtained from institutions outside SingHealth (eg, private general practitioners). As a result, certain patient populations, such as those who prefer private healthcare, may be underrepresented and affect the generalisability of our findings to the broader national population. However, as SingHealth is the largest public healthcare cluster in Singapore, the registry includes data from a substantial proportion of the population and reflects real-world clinical practice within the local healthcare system. Second, as the data are drawn into SDR on an annual basis, the exact date of diagnosis could not be readily determined, and patients could already be prevalent cases on cohort entry. However, the duration of DM would be less than a year in such cases. As DM medications are expected to be used on a long-term basis, this is unlikely to affect the validity of the findings. Third, as with all drug adherence studies using dispensing data, drug utilisation is contingent on the drugs being prescribed. Therefore, the assumption is that doctors would prescribe DM medications based on a patient-centred approach in line with established DM guidelines, so the observed results can largely be attributed to patients’ adherence. Lastly, a small proportion of prescriptions (<0.5%) have missing durations that needed to be imputed. Due to the small percentage of prescriptions affected, it is unlikely the results would be significantly affected.

In conclusion, patients with DM who had good glycaemic control at baseline were less likely to receive DM drugs and those with poor baseline glycaemic control were more likely to be highly adherent. Higher adherence to any DM drug and oral DM drugs was significantly associated with a clinically relevant reduction in HbA1c.
