Engineering smart bacteria for the next generation
Authors: Longliang Qiao, Zhihao Wang, Shasha Tang, Jinzhong Tian, Haifeng Ye, Fengfeng Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe rational design of smart bacteria represents a transformative advance in synthetic biology, enabling unprecedented spatiotemporal precision in microbial sensing and signal processing. By integrating sophisticated genetic circuits, these engineered systems can detect a wide range of environmental and disease-associated cues, positioning them as powerful tools for both diagnosis and therapy. Recent developments have broadened their clinical utility, including in situ therapeutic delivery and remote-controlled activation in response to chemical, physical, or pathological signals. This review summarizes recent progress in genetic circuit engineering, explores clinical applications across multiple disease contexts, and discusses key challenges and future directions for translating smart bacteria into next-generation precision medicine.Keywords: smart bacteria, synthetic biology, genetic circuits, precision medicine

Introduction

The human body harbors diverse microbial communities, with intricate host-microbe interactions exerting pivotal roles in human health and disease.1 Given their relatively simple, robust metabolism and amenability to genetic manipulation, microorganisms have been widely engineered as diagnostic tools and living factories for therapeutic production.2

Diagnostics are central to clinical practice, enabling disease detection, disease progression monitoring, and the design of efficacious treatments. However, many current state-of-the-art detection and diagnostic tools have limited sensitivity for early disease detection or require several days for a conclusive diagnosis.3 Moreover, most conventional methods are often complex and require costly equipment.4 Leveraging significant advances in synthetic biology, bacterial biosensors are constructed to function as living machines with seamlessly integrated sensing, processing, and communication units to respond to the presence of analyses and produce detectable output signals.

Engineered bacteria have emerged as a promising platform for the treatment of various diseases, with the simple introduction of a constitutively expressed therapeutic gene circuit showing success in conditions such as colitis,5 diabetes,6 and obesity.7 Compared with traditional chemical drugs and protein-based biologics, engineered bacteria offer several distinct advantages. First, they enable on-site production of therapeutics, eliminating the need for expensive purification steps associated with chemical synthesis or bioreactor-based manufacturing.8 Second, by acting as in vivo drug factories, engineered bacteria provide sustained therapeutic production, addressing the short half-life challenge faced by many protein-based drugs.9 These unique capabilities position engineered bacteria as a transformative approach for next-generation living medicines.

However, the excessive production of therapeutic proteins by engineered bacteria can lead to toxicity in healthy tissues.10 To address this, synthetic biology-driven remote control systems have been developed, which integrate synthetic genetic circuits to regulate transgene expression in response to chemical inducers,11 physical signals (e.g., heat12 and light13), or disease-specific biomarkers.14 These systems offer several key advantages: (1) precise targeting of diseased tissues with minimized off-target effects; (2) sustained, dynamic drug production to maintain efficacy over extended periods; and (3) adaptability to physiological changes, enabling real-time responses to disease environmental fluctuations and providing a level of personalization challenging to conventional therapies. Bacteria equipped with genetic circuits—capable of sensing environmental or pathological cues, processing inputs through regulatory logic, and executing defined outputs—are referred to in this review as smart bacteria.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of smart bacteria, focusing on their engineering strategies, therapeutic applications, and potential for clinical translation. By examining their development, capabilities, and future prospects, we highlight the transformative impact of smart bacteria on modern medicine and biotechnology.

Genetic circuits toolkit

The universal architecture of engineered bacterial genetic circuits typically comprises several modular components15,16: a sensor module, which detects external stimuli such as disease-associated biomarkers, chemical molecules, or physical cues; a processing module, which integrates signals via reprogrammed endogenous or orthogonal signaling pathways—synthetic pathways designed to function without interfering with the host’s native systems; and an output module, which generates a functional response. The sensor module activates a range of chimeric receptors, engineered by fusing functional domains from different proteins to confer new sensing or signaling capabilities, or orthogonal protein components specifically designed to detect and respond to selected stimuli. These inputs are then transmitted to processing modules, which control the output module.

The output can be tailored to specific applications. For example, expression of a reporter gene enables the engineered bacteria to function as diagnostic tools, while production of a therapeutic payload enables disease intervention (Figure 1 and Table S1). In the following sections, we describe representative gene circuits that have been applied in biomedical contexts.

Development of engineered bacteria with genetic circuits

Engineered bacteria are equipped with modular genetic circuits that enable precise signal sensing and response. The sensor module detects a wide range of inducers, including chemical molecules, physical stimuli (e.g., heat and light), and disease-associated biomarkers. These inputs activate chimeric or native receptors designed to respond to specific signals. Signal transduction occurs via endogenous pathways (e.g., kinase phosphorylation) or orthogonal synthetic circuits, triggering downstream responses. Representative systems include the temperature-sensitive TlpA/Tcl module and the IPTG-inducible LacI repressor. The output module can be programmed to express reporter genes for diagnostic readouts or therapeutic proteins for disease intervention.

Chemical molecule stimulation system

Chemical induction systems, including one-component systems (OCSs) and two-component systems (TCSs), are widely used to control gene expression.17 Representative OCSs include LacI/isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), TetR/anhydrotetracycline (aTc), XylS/acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), and NanR/sialic acid (SA)18,19 (Figure 2A), which offer high specificity and well-characterized dynamic ranges. L-rhamnose (Rham)- and arabinose (Ara)-inducible systems provide tight transcriptional control20,21 (Figures 2B and 2C), although induction efficiency can depend on transporter availability and metabolic burden. Quorum-sensing systems (e.g., LuxR-AHL) enable population-density-dependent regulation (Figure 2D), making them particularly suitable for coordinated bacterial therapies.22 Despite these advantages, chemical induction often requires exogenous inducers, which can hinder clinical translation due to pharmacokinetic constraints or host toxicity.

(A–D) Chemical sensors: chemical inducers—IPTG, aTc, ASA (A); L-rhamnose (B); Ara (C); and AHL (D)—interact with effector proteins (LacI, TetR, XylS, NanR, RhaR, AraC, and LuxR), triggering their binding to or release from operator sequences to regulate downstream gene expression.

(E–J) Physical sensors: upon exposure to specific temperatures (E) or blue light (F), thermosensitive proteins (cI857, TcI42, and TlpA39) or the photosensitive protein EL222 undergo conformational changes that promote binding to or dissociation from specific operator sequences, thereby regulating downstream gene expression. Upon light stimulation, photosensitive proteins (G, YtvA and DrF1; H, CcaS; I, Cph8; J, PadC) phosphorylate downstream response regulators (FixJ, CcaR, and OmpR) or produce cyclic di-GMP (c-di-GMP), thereby initiating target gene expression.

Physical signal stimulation system

Physical signals, including temperature and light, enable non-invasive, remote-controllable, and reversible regulation.23 Temperature-sensitive repressors (cI857,24 TcI42,23 TlpA3925) permit gene induction at defined thermal thresholds (39°C–45°C) (Figure 2E), but their application must remain within tissue safety limits. Light-responsive systems—such as pDawn/pREDawn (blue/red light),26,27 EL222 (blue light),28 CcaSR (green/red light),29 Cph8 (red light),30 and the near-infrared (NIR) light-mediated PadC-based photoswitch (NETMAP)31 (Figures 2F–2J) —provide high spatiotemporal precision, with deeper tissue penetration achievable using red or NIR wavelengths. Limitations include chromophore availability, tissue absorption, and light scattering in vivo.

Disease-associated signal systems

Bacteria can sense endogenous disease-associated biomarkers to precisely regulate gene expression in specific tissues or pathological contexts.32 These signals can be broadly classified into physicochemical cues and metabolites/biomarkers, reflecting their molecular properties and physiological relevance and enabling tailored therapeutic or diagnostic applications.

Physicochemical cues include oxygen tension, pH, and metabolite concentrations such as lactate, which differ markedly between healthy and diseased tissues. Oxygen levels in normal tissues typically exceed 44.39 mmHg,32 whereas hypoxic regions in tumors or the gut fall below 11 mmHg.32 Tissue pH decreases from 7.0 to 7.6 in healthy regions to 4.4–7.0 in tumors, or inflamed gut areas,33 and lactate levels rise from <1.95 μmol/g to >7.3 μmol/g.32 Sensors exploiting these gradients—such as FNR (hypoxia),32 CadC (low pH),32 and LldR (lactate)32—activate gene expression in a tissue-specific, endogenous manner (Figures 3A–3C), obviating the need for exogenous inducers. Cellular events such as host vesicle formation, mediated by the SPI2 system, can also function as signals to activate bacterial gene expression upon host invasion34 (Figure 3D).

(D) Following bacterial invasion of host cells, SPI2 promotes vesicle formation and activates gene expression via its associated promoters PSseJ and PSifA.

(E–K) Bile salts induce TcpP- (E) or VtrA-mediated (F) ligand-dependent dimerization, enabling transcriptional activation. In two-component systems, S4O62−, S2O32−, nitrate, and glucose (G) activate the sensor kinases ThsS, TtrS, NarX, and CpxA, respectively, resulting in phosphorylation of their cognate response regulators (ThsR, TtrR, NarL, and CpxR) and subsequent gene induction. NO (H), calprotectin (I), and heme and uric acid (J) are directly sensed by the transcriptional regulators NorR, Zur, HrtR, and PucR, which undergo ligand-induced changes in DNA-binding activity to bind to or dissociate from specific operator sequences. In host-derived signal sensing, extracellular ATP (eATP) activates the P2Y2 receptor (K), triggering a downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascade that ultimately drives target gene expression.

Metabolites and disease biomarkers include bile salts (Engineered Modularized Receptors Activated via Ligand-induced Dimerization [EMeRALD]/TcpP or VtrA),35 thiosulphate (S4O62−) and tetrathionate (S2O32−) (ThsSR or TtrSR),14 nitrate (NarX/NarL),36 glucose (CpxA/CpxR),37 nitric oxide (NorR),38 calprotectin (Zur),39 heme (ChuA/HrtR),40 uric acid (PucR),41 and extracellular ATP/UTP (P2Y2)42 (Figures 3E–3K). These molecules are closely associated with inflammation, infection, or tissue damage. Their corresponding sensors enable disease-specific gene activation, allowing bacteria to respond selectively at pathological sites. For instance, NorR detects NO produced during inflammation,38 while EMeRALD bile salt sensors drive gut-specific gene expression.35

Precision medicine enabled by smart bacteria

In the context of smart bacteria, precision medicine refers to the capacity of engineered bacterial systems to perform context-dependent, individualized diagnosis or therapy by sensing disease-associated biomarkers and activating responses exclusively under predefined pathological conditions.15 This strategy enables precise spatial and temporal control that is difficult to achieve with conventional systemic interventions.

Precision is achieved primarily through responsiveness to patient-specific biomarkers—such as nitric oxide, sulfur metabolites, bile acids, and inflammatory proteins—whose concentrations vary across individuals and disease stages.16 By coupling these inputs to tightly regulated genetic circuits, smart bacteria can selectively report or intervene in diseased tissues while remaining quiescent in healthy environments. Logic-gated and threshold-based circuit architectures further enhance specificity by integrating multiple signals and minimizing off-target activation.

Representative systems illustrate these principles. The ingestible micro-bio-electronic device (IMBED) enables real-time, in situ detection of gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding by coupling heme-responsive engineered bacteria with electronic readout, exemplifying precision diagnosis with high sensitivity and specificity.40 In therapeutic applications, the NETMAP system confines bacterial activation to regions exposed to NIR light, thereby achieving localized therapy while minimizing systemic toxicity.31 Together, these platforms demonstrate how smart bacteria can translate individualized biochemical information into precise diagnostic or therapeutic outputs.

Smart bacteria for diagnosis

Smart bacteria equipped with genetic circuits can detect and report specific analytes with high sensitivity and specificity. These systems typically utilize output modules based on fluorescent proteins, bioluminescent enzymes, or colorimetric reporters that respond to analyte-induced signals.15 Conventional biosensors operate by transcriptionally and translationally regulating the expression of functional proteins (e.g., green fluorescent protein [GFP]) or enzymes (e.g., β-galactosidase, LacZ) upon detecting disease-related stimuli. However, these systems are limited by their reliance on persistent stimuli; once the stimulus fades, signal output also diminishes, making it challenging to track transient biological events such as episodes of intestinal inflammation.43

To address this limitation, memory circuits—comprising sensor switches and recombinases—have been developed to record transient signals for long-term tracking38 (Figure 4A). Such smart bacteria have been designed to diagnose GI diseases, bleeding events, pathogenic infections, liver injury, glucose levels, fever, and cancer. In some advanced systems, engineered bacteria are integrated into ingestible electronic capsules, which convert biological signals into electrical outputs readable by mobile devices such as smartphones and computers.

Upon detecting disease-associated biomarkers, the bacteria activate memory elements, such as Cro/CI switches, recombinases, or base editors, which, in turn, initiate the expression of diagnostic reporters.

(B) Workflow for disease detection using engineered bacteria. Upon sensing specific biomarkers, the bacteria trigger a diagnostic output that is converted into electrical signals, which are wirelessly transmitted to external devices such as smartphones or computers for real-time monitoring.

Detecting gastrointestinal diseases

The GI tract presents unique diagnostic challenges due to its inaccessibility, complexity, and the spatial and temporal specificity of many gut diseases. Standard diagnostic techniques, such as endoscopy and colonoscopy, provide only static spatial information and are both invasive and expensive.44,45 Fecal sampling is a non-invasive alternative but lacks real-time or spatial resolution. Moreover, many gut inflammatory markers are short-lived or degraded before excretion, leading to false-negatives.

Sulfur metabolism is tightly linked to gut inflammation, with thiosulfate (S2O32−) and tetrathionate (S4O62−) serving as key biomarkers.46 Engineered E. coli systems sensing these species have progressed substantially over the past decade. Early designs employed GFP reporters in ThsSR and TtrSR circuits to detect inflammation in dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced colitis mice but required flow cytometry and were limited by delayed signal maturation.14 To overcome these constraints, memory-based circuits incorporating λ phage CI/Cro or base editors (BE2)—which comprise rat cytidine deaminase APOBEC1 fused to catalytically dead Cas9 (dCas9) to enable direct C-to-G conversion in DNA—were developed to durably record transient sulfur exposure, enabling long-term monitoring.43,47 However, these systems still relied on ex vivo analysis (Figure 4B).

A major breakthrough emerged with miniaturized ingestible devices integrating synthetic biology and electronics. Inda-Webb et al. combined ThsSR/TtrSR sensors with the recombinase Bxb1 in a <1.4-cm3 capsule implanted in pigs, generating bioluminescent signals that were wirelessly transmitted for real-time, in vivo monitoring48 (Figure 4C). Similarly, the IMBED houses heme-responsive smart probiotics coupled to an electronic readout, enabling rapid detection of upper GI bleeding in pig models with high sensitivity and specificity.40 Collectively, these technologies mark a transition from static, invasive diagnostics to dynamic, point-of-care (POC) biosensing.

Additional inflammation-associated biomarkers, including nitric oxide (NO) and nitrate, have been exploited to further enhance diagnostic robustness. AND logic-gated circuits integrating NO and tetrathionate sensors reduce false-positives, while memory elements enable durable signal recording.36 Although clinical biomarkers such as fecal calprotectin remain gold standards,49 engineered circuits using promoters such as ykgMO can generate measurable GFP signals within 12 h, demonstrating substantially faster response times.39

Detecting liver injury

Serum bile salts are established biomarkers for liver disease and the diagnostic gold standard for intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy.50 They also indicate early liver cirrhosis, hepatitis, and drug-induced liver injury, and specific bile salt profiles may correlate with particular liver pathologies.51 However, current enzymatic bile salt assays are laboratory-based, require specialized equipment, and cannot differentiate among bile salt species.52

To overcome these limitations, Chang et al. developed the EMeRALD platform, which incorporates the TcpP/TcpH and VtrA/VtrC bile salt-sensing modules.35 Sensitivity was improved via directed evolution. The output reporter was LacZ, coupled to the chlorophenol red-β-D-galactopyranoside substrate for colorimetric detection. The system is compatible with smartphone-based readouts, supporting POC or at-home diagnostics. In a study of 21 serum samples from liver transplant patients, EMeRALD generated robust, visible color changes in all samples with bile acid concentrations above 37 μM. This system offers a reliable, low-cost solution for post-transplant monitoring and broader clinical use.35

Detecting fever

Piraner et al. engineered a smart bacterial sensor capable of detecting elevated body temperatures in a murine model.53 The design was based on the thermosensitive repressor protein TlpA, specifically a mutant form (TlpA36) that is inactive at normal physiological temperatures but functional at febrile levels (∼41°C). Upon subcutaneous implantation into nude mice with induced fever, engineered bacteria expressing a fluorescent reporter exhibited detectable signals within 4 h, as confirmed by in vivo imaging. This system demonstrates the feasibility of temperature-responsive biosensors for detecting systemic inflammatory responses.

Detection of pathogenic bacteria

Cholera, a severe GI infection associated with contaminated water and inadequate sanitation, requires rapid diagnosis due to its potential for rapid dehydration and mortality.54 Mao et al. designed a hybrid receptor (HR) combining the Vibrio cholerae cholera autoinducer 1 (CAI-1)-sensing domain of CqsS with the histidine kinase signaling domain of NisK from Lactococcus lactis.55 This HR controlled the expression of the transcriptional repressor TetR via the nisA promoter. In the absence of CAI-1, TetR inhibited downstream LacZ expression. Upon CAI-1 binding, HR suppressed TetR production, activating LacZ and producing a detectable colorimetric signal.

Engineered L. lactis harboring this construct were incubated overnight with nitrocephalosporin and fecal samples from cholera-infected mice.55 The system successfully detected cholera-derived CAI-1, illustrating the potential of engineered probiotics for field-deployable, pathogen-specific diagnostics.

Detection of glucose levels

Glucose is a critical clinical biomarker, with elevated blood glucose levels serving as a diagnostic and management tool for diabetes. Glycosuria—the presence of glucose in urine—indicates hyperglycemia and poor glycemic control.56 However, as glucose is a primary carbon source for bacteria, it is difficult to use as a biosensing target without metabolic interference.57

To address this, Courbet et al. engineered a memory circuit combining the CpxA/CpxR two-component system with the site-specific recombinase Bxb1.37 Upon glucose detection, Bxb1 was expressed, irreversibly activating red fluorescent protein expression. The readout was quantified using a microplate reader. In clinical validation, the system reliably detected glycosuria in urine samples from diabetic patients, achieving 88.9% sensitivity and 96.3% specificity. This platform demonstrated an order-of-magnitude improvement in detection sensitivity compared to commercial test strips, offering a more sensitive and potentially more informative diagnostic option for diabetes management.

Detection of cancer

Early detection of cancer significantly improves patient outcomes, but current non-invasive methods, such as liquid biopsy, often suffer from low sensitivity and high cost.58 Synthetic biology has enabled the development of novel, programmable biosensors capable of detecting cancer-associated signals with high specificity.59

In 2023, Cooper et al. designed a bacterial biosensor capable of detecting tumor-derived DNA sequences in vivo.60 The system leveraged the natural competence of Acinetobacter baylyi to take up extracellular DNA, combined with an endogenous CRISPR module.61 Upon uptake of tumor DNA—specifically those carrying mutations, such as those in the KRAS gene—the CRISPR system triggered the expression of a kanamycin resistance gene (KanR), serving as a selectable marker for tumor detection.

In a mouse model of colorectal cancer, the engineered bacteria successfully acquired and responded to tumor DNA via horizontal gene transfer, leading to measurable resistance signals.60 This approach presents a novel, potentially non-invasive platform for cancer screening based on tumor-specific DNA signatures, offering promise for future clinical translation.

Smart bacteria for therapy

Smart bacteria represent a transformative paradigm in biomedical engineering, enabling the conversion of living cells into programmable platforms for therapeutic delivery. While engineered bacteria have historically been explored as drug factories through constitutive or simple inducible expression of therapeutic genes, this review specifically defines smart bacteria as systems that integrate sensing, decision-making, and actuation within engineered genetic circuits. Leveraging advances in synthetic biology, these systems can detect disease-associated cues, process inputs through logic-gated or closed-loop regulatory architectures, and initiate therapeutic responses exclusively under predefined pathological conditions. Functioning as self-regulated, in situ bioreactors, smart bacteria enable on-demand, localized drug production, thereby minimizing off-target toxicity, enhancing therapeutic efficacy, and providing a coherent conceptual framework for clinical translation.

Regulation of bacterial immunogenicity

A key challenge in live bacterial therapy is managing immunogenicity and host toxicity. One strategy involves the genetic deletion of surface antigens such as lipopolysaccharide to reduce host immune activation. However, this can result in permanent attenuation and compromised colonization, as observed in clinical trials of bacterial cancer therapy.62

Surface modification with biocompatible materials—alginate,63 chitosan,64 or nanoparticles65—has been used to camouflage bacteria, much like synthetic coatings in drug delivery vehicles.66 However, these static modifications cannot dynamically regulate bacterial interactions, potentially leading to uncontrolled proliferation, off-target toxicity, or reduced efficacy.

To address this, Harimoto et al. developed a programmable capsular polysaccharide (CAP) expression system in E. coli Nissle 1917.67 This inducible genetic circuit allows external control of CAP expression, modulating bacterial interactions with antimicrobials, acidic environments, and immune responses. This system enhances both in vivo survivability and safety, allowing higher dosing and more precise targeting for improved therapeutic outcomes.

Treatment of intestinal diseases

Conventional treatments for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)—including aminosalicylates, glucocorticoids, and immunosuppressants—are often costly, have significant side effects, and carry a high risk of relapse.68 Although wild-type probiotics have been explored for gut health, they lack therapeutic precision and efficacy.69,70 In contrast, smart bacteria offer controlled, responsive therapeutic delivery tailored to the intestinal environment.

Early approaches employed chemical inducers such as IPTG,71 arabinose,72 xylan,73,74 and xylose75 to trigger therapeutic gene expression. Bacteria were engineered to secrete enzymes that scavenge reactive oxygen species (e.g., catalase and superoxide dismutase),76 growth factors (e.g., KGF2, transforming growth factor [TGF]-β1), cytokines (e.g., interleukin [IL]-10), trefoil factors, or the immunomodulatory protein AvCystatin from Acanthocheilonema viteae.77 While effective in reducing IBD symptoms, reliance on chemical inducers raises concerns about systemic toxicity.78

Light-responsive gene circuits offer an attractive alternative due to their spatiotemporal precision, tunability, and reversibility.79 Using the blue light-responsive pDawn system, Wang’s group regulated the expression of IL-10, AG43, and TGF-β1 in E. coli Nissle 1917 to treat DSS-induced colitis.80,81,82

Moving beyond exogenous induction, researchers have also designed closed-loop circuits that respond to endogenous biomarkers of inflammation. In 2013, Benbouziane et al. used the groESL stress-responsive promoter in L. lactis to express IL-10 in response to heat shock, low pH, UV, and salt stress.83 This strategy significantly reduced colitis pathology in mice.

Zou et al. later engineered bacteria with an S2O32− sensor driving AvCystatin expression in a dose-dependent manner.43 Oral delivery in a DSS-induced colitis model improved weight loss and colon length while avoiding excessive drug secretion—a key limitation of constitutive or chemically inducible systems.84

A parallel strategy was demonstrated in Saccharomyces cerevisiae engineered to detect extracellular ATP (eATP), a pro-inflammatory signal in IBD.85 Scott et al. used a sensitized version of the human P2Y2 receptor and coupled it to potato apyrase RROP1 as the therapeutic output. This yeast strain showed efficacy in DSS- and 2,4,6-trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS)-induced colitis models, reducing inflammation and fibrosis and restoring microbiota balance.42

Treatment of neurological diseases

The blood-brain barrier remains a major obstacle in delivering central nervous system (CNS) therapeutics.86 However, the gut-brain axis offers an indirect route for modulating neurological function via microbial activity.87 Engineered microbes thus present an innovative platform for neurological therapy by producing and delivering neuroactive compounds from within the gut.88,89

In 2022, Pan et al. engineered Lactobacillus to secrete γ-aminobutyric acid and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor under the control of the pDawn system, targeting anxiety and Parkinson’s disease (PD), respectively.88 Blue light exposure (via NIR-converted upconversion materials [UCMs]) triggered therapeutic release, with measurable effects on CNS function. However, the use of UCMs raises toxicity concerns.90

To improve safety and tissue penetration, the same group subsequently developed a red-light-responsive E. coli Nissle 1917 strain (ROEN).30 Using the Cph8 photoreceptor system, they drove the expression of a neuroprotective fusion protein (Exendin-4 fused to an anti-neonatal Fc receptor affibody91). Red light exposure also triggered bacterial lysis via the phage lysis protein (X174E), enabling targeted Ex-4 release. In a PD mouse model, this approach significantly improved neuroprotection and behavioral performance, demonstrating the therapeutic potential of optogenetic gut-based interventions.30

Treatment of bacterial infections

Bacterial infections remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality. Although antibiotics are the mainstay of treatment, their overuse contributes to the rise of multidrug-resistant pathogens.92 Phage therapy presents an alternative, but often lacks target specificity and can disrupt beneficial microbiota.93 Advances in genome engineering now enable the development of smart bacterial therapies capable of sensing and eliminating specific pathogens.

In 2011, Saeidi et al. engineered E. coli TOP10 with a quorum-sensing circuit responsive to P. aeruginosa-derived AHL molecules.94 Upon AHL detection, the bacteria expressed pyocin S5, a bacteriocin toxic to P. aeruginosa, and E7 lysis protein, leading to self-lysis and release of the payload. This system inhibited >99% of P. aeruginosa in vitro. In later work, pyocin S5 was replaced with dispersin B (DspB), an anti-biofilm enzyme.95 The modified circuit was transferred to E. coli Nissle 1917 and demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing P. aeruginosa burden in a mouse infection model.96

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI), a common cause of hospital-acquired diarrhea,97 is typically treated with antibiotics, which paradoxically promote recurrence by disrupting gut microbiota.98 Since SA levels rise during antibiotic-induced dysbiosis,99 Kho et al. developed an SA-responsive circuit to express Clostridium perfringens bile salt hydrolase (Cbh).100 Cbh converts taurocholic acid (a C. difficile germination signal) into non-germinative cholic acid. In a mouse CDI model, engineered bacteria conferred 100% survival, reduced pathogen load, and significantly mitigated tissue damage. These findings highlight the promise of smart bacteria as precision antimicrobial agents that minimize collateral damage to the microbiome.

Treatment of metabolic diseases

Type 2 diabetes (T2D), characterized by insulin resistance or pancreatic β-cell dysfunction, presents a global public health challenge.101 Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) promotes glucose-dependent insulin secretion and inhibits β-cell apoptosis, making it a promising therapeutic target.102 However, its short half-life due to rapid degradation by dipeptidyl peptidase-IV limits clinical efficacy.103

To address this, Zhang et al. engineered L. lactis to express a stabilized GLP-1 analog under blue light control via the pDawn system.104 GLP-1 was C-terminally fused to IgG-Fc to enhance in vivo stability (shGLP-1). In a T2D rat model, light-triggered expression reduced blood glucose by 6 mM, although levels remained above normal. Despite this limitation, the strain offers a low-cost and controllable platform for chronic metabolic disease therapy.

Tumor therapy

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with nearly 10 million deaths annually and projections estimating 28.4 million new cases globally by 2040.105 Innovative strategies have emerged that exploit the natural tumor-homing properties of anaerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria—such as E. coli, Salmonella spp., and Listeria monocytogenes—which preferentially colonize hypoxic tumor cores.70 These microbes form the foundation of oncolytic bacterial therapy, where synthetic biology tools are used to reprogram bacteria into “biofactories” capable of producing anticancer agents locally. Although potent tumor suppression has been demonstrated in preclinical models,106 achieving precise spatiotemporal control of therapeutic expression remains a central challenge for clinical application. Early strategies relied on small-molecule inducers (e.g., arabinose, doxycycline, and acetylsalicylic acid) to regulate tumoricidal payloads—including pore-forming toxins, cytokines, immune modulators, and prodrug-converting enzymes.11,18,19,107,108,109,110,111,112,113 However, prolonged inducer administration can disrupt host physiology and the microbiota,114 motivating the development of more precise and externally controllable regulatory systems.

To improve localization and timing, quorum-sensing-based lysis circuits were introduced to couple therapeutic release to bacterial population density.115 This strategy enabled intratumoral accumulation of diverse payloads, including toxins, cytokines, chemokines, and immune checkpoint nanobodies.9,116,117,118,119,120,121 Nonetheless, quorum-based systems rely on preprogrammed genetic logic and lack adaptability to real-time tumor dynamics, with premature lysis still reported in off-target tissues.9,118

Consequently, remote control strategies have emerged as a major technical direction for improving safety and precision. Physical stimuli such as ionizing radiation, focused ultrasound, alternating magnetic fields, and photothermal heating have been used to trigger gene expression via inducible promoters or thermal switches.12,23,25,122,123,124,125,126 Although these methods provide spatial control, their reliance on heat or ionizing radiation raises concerns about tissue damage, biosafety, and system complexity.

To overcome the limitations of heat- and material-dependent approaches, increasing attention has turned to optogenetic platforms. Optogenetic control systems have evolved from simple ON/OFF switches to enabling highly precise, spatiotemporal, and dynamic regulation of bacterial activity. The latest generation, particularly systems responsive to NIR light with deeper tissue penetration, allows external, non-invasive programming of therapeutic output. By modulating light intensity, wavelength, or pulsed regimens, linear and tunable control of protein expression in vivo is now achievable.33 Early visible-light-responsive systems, including green- or blue-light circuits,13,82,121,126,127 demonstrated proof-of-concept regulation of immunomodulatory payloads but were constrained by limited penetration depth or reliance on upconversion nanomaterials, which introduce additional safety concerns.90

In this context, the NETMAP system represents a substantial advance.31 NETMAP employs an attenuated Salmonella strain with genomic integration of PadC, a photoreceptor directly responsive to 710-nm NIR light, enabling precise and reversible control of gene expression without photothermal agents or chemical modification. Notably, the system supports modular payload selection tailored to tumor immunogenicity: immune checkpoint nanobodies (CTLA-4nb and PD-L1nb) were deployed in immunogenic lymphoma models, while cytotoxic proteins (cytolysin A, ClyA, and azurin) were applied in poorly immunogenic colorectal cancer. NETMAP demonstrated robust tumor suppression, prolonged survival, and apoptosis induction in both mouse and patient-derived xenograft models, underscoring its strong translational potential.

Beyond remote control, autonomous regulation strategies have also been explored by exploiting tumor-associated cues such as hypoxia, aberrant glycosylation, and intracellular pathogen-specific signaling.34,128,129 Although these approaches enable stimulus-free activation, their reliance on endogenous tumor features may limit robustness across heterogeneous patient populations.

Challenges

Stability and safety of genetic circuits in smart bacteria

The stability and safety of genetic circuits are fundamental to the clinical deployment of smart bacteria in precision medicine.130 However, most circuit design, optimization, and validation are performed in vitro using media supplemented with antibiotics. This introduces major limitations for in vivo applications. First, the inclusion of antibiotic resistance genes poses a risk of horizontal gene transfer, potentially contributing to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant strains and endangering both host and environmental microbiomes.131 Second, in the absence of antibiotic selection pressure in vivo, synthetic circuits are prone to mutational drift or loss of function over time.132

Several strategies have been proposed to overcome these challenges. A common approach involves genome integration of genetic circuits via gene editing to ensure stable inheritance and prevent plasmid loss.128 Alternatively, engineered auxotrophic bacterial chassis can be employed, replacing antibiotic resistance markers with essential metabolic genes.34 For example, the asd gene, which encodes aspartic semialdehyde dehydrogenase, is necessary for the synthesis of DL-α,ε-diaminopimelic acid (DAP), a key component of bacterial cell walls. asd mutants cannot survive without exogenous DAP unless complemented with a plasmid encoding the missing gene along with the therapeutic transgene, thereby maintaining plasmid stability in vivo.34

Toxin-antitoxin systems provide an additional means of plasmid stabilization.31,59 One well-characterized example is the hok/sok system from E. coli plasmid R1. Here, hok mRNA encodes a toxic protein, while sok RNA is an antisense transcript that inhibits hok expression. In plasmid-containing cells, sok RNA prevents toxin synthesis. However, when the plasmid is lost, sok degrades rapidly while hok persists, leading to toxin accumulation and cell death.133 This post-segregational killing mechanism selectively eliminates plasmid-free cells, thereby improving the long-term stability of synthetic circuits.

Beyond toxin-antitoxin systems, CRISPR-Cas-based biocontainment strategies provide a more robust safeguard against environmental release and unintended horizontal gene transfer.134 These systems are engineered as “kill switches” in which guide RNAs target essential bacterial genes. The CRISPR-Cas machinery remains inactive in the presence of a designed external signal. However, if engineered bacteria escape into environments lacking this signal, Cas nuclease activity is triggered, resulting in lethal cleavage of the bacterial genome.

In vivo delivery and colonization efficiency

Oral delivery of engineered bacteria offers a non-invasive and patient-friendly alternative to injections, improving treatment adherence.135,136 These microbes also exhibit favorable biosafety profiles, robust adherence to mucosal surfaces, and the capacity to modulate the gut microbiota.137 However, the harsh GI environment, including fluctuating pH, bile salts, and peristalsis, poses significant barriers to effective colonization and therapeutic delivery.138

To improve colonization efficiency, both physicochemical and biological surface modifications have been employed. For example, Pan et al. applied polydopamine co-deposition to enhance mucoadhesion via covalent bonding and hydrophobic/electrostatic interactions, which increased bacterial retention in the mouse gut by over 30-fold compared to unmodified strains.139 Nevertheless, such coatings may compromise bacterial viability or interfere with gene expression.

Biological modifications using ligand-receptor interactions—including adhesins, natural cell membranes, and lipid coatings—can improve retention for 3–6 days and offer greater biocompatibility.140,141 However, they remain limited by rapid epithelial turnover, which shortens colonization duration.

Capsule systems offer a promising platform to extend bacterial residence time and can be applied across a variety of bacterial strains. Furthermore, integrating bacteria with electronic sensing devices opens the door to externally regulated, intelligent closed-loop systems that span real-time sensing, data processing, and precision therapy.40,48

Other in vivo delivery routes are being explored to overcome the limitations of oral administration. Intravenous injection enables systemic circulation and tumor targeting but requires careful management of immune clearance.25 Intratumoral injection permits localized, high-density bacterial delivery within solid tumors, enhancing efficacy while limiting systemic exposure.31 Rectal or intestinal administration allows targeting of distal gut regions and inflamed tissues, which is particularly relevant for IBD models.142

In addition, intranasal delivery using engineered commensal bacteria has recently emerged as a promising strategy to bypass physiological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier.7 For example, an engineered Lactobacillus plantarum strain with natural affinity for the olfactory epithelium localizes to the nasal cavity, secretes therapeutic payloads, and transports molecules into the brain via the olfactory pathway in mouse models, demonstrating potential for targeted nose-to-brain drug delivery in neurological applications.

Each delivery route presents distinct challenges, including immune clearance, tissue adaptation, and safety constraints, which must be rigorously addressed in preclinical studies. Recent work has demonstrated promising mitigation strategies, such as hydrogel-based encapsulation for oral administration, which improves bacterial survival, colonization, and therapeutic efficacy.116,143

Clinical translation

To date, most microbial therapeutics in phase 1 or 2 clinical trials involve unmodified probiotic or attenuated strains administered alongside recombinant protein therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors or cytokines (NCT03435952 and NCT04167137). Notably, no smart bacteria have yet entered clinical trials, despite encouraging preclinical performance. Translation in this field remains constrained by several key challenges.

First, limited human data and uncertain immunogenicity remain major barriers. Although animal studies demonstrate efficacy, the behavior of engineered sensing and regulatory modules in humans is largely untested. Host immune responses may neutralize engineered strains or induce unintended inflammation, compromising both safety and efficacy.137

Second, regulatory complexity substantially impedes progress. Agencies such as the US Food and Drug Administration and European Medicines Agency require rigorous evidence of genetic stability, biocontainment, reproducibility, and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant manufacturing for live bacterial therapies.144 These requirements exceed those for conventional biologics due to the involvement of genetically modified organisms (GMOs), complicating approval and scale-up.

Third, biological and environmental heterogeneity limits robustness and predictability. Tumor, gut, and inflammatory microenvironments vary widely across patients in oxygen tension, pH, metabolite composition, and immune activity. Such variability can undermine circuit reliability and necessitate extensive optimization prior to clinical application.145

Fourth, challenges in trial design, dosing, and manufacturing further slow translation. Early trials with unmodified probiotics highlight difficulties in defining standardized endpoints, achieving reproducible colonization, and scaling production while maintaining safety and functional consistency.146 These insights underscore the need for careful evaluation of circuit architecture, strain selection, and delivery strategies before advancing smart bacteria into clinical testing.

Finally, ethical and societal concerns surrounding the deployment of GMOs demand strict oversight and transparent patient communication, further complicating translation. Addressing these challenges will require close collaboration among synthetic biologists, clinicians, bioethicists, and regulatory authorities to establish standardized trial frameworks, scalable and safe manufacturing pipelines, and clear safety guidelines. Integrating lessons from prior microbial therapy trials with advances in synthetic biology may accelerate the clinical translation of smart bacteria and unlock their full potential in precision medicine.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The evolution of smart bacteria—from synthetic genetic circuits to precision therapeutic agents—holds immense promise for the development of next-generation medicines. By integrating synthetic biology with clinical need, smart bacteria offer a unique platform for targeted drug delivery, real-time disease sensing, and adaptive therapeutic responses. These capabilities open new avenues for precision medicine. However, significant challenges remain, particularly in achieving robust control of bacterial behavior in vivo, maintaining long-term safety, and navigating the regulatory landscape for live microbial therapeutics.

In the diagnostic realm, current approaches primarily rely on flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy to quantify reporter outputs. While effective, these techniques are technically complex and not widely accessible outside of laboratory settings. Future efforts should focus on integrating smart bacteria with portable electronic platforms that can convert pathological signals into electrical outputs, enabling real-time, user-friendly monitoring via mobile devices.

For therapeutic applications, small-molecule and physically inducible systems have shown considerable potential. Yet, challenges remain related to inducer toxicity, dosage control, and operational complexity. A critical goal is the development of closed-loop circuits that respond autonomously to disease-specific biomarkers, enabling self-regulated, feedback-controlled treatment cycles.

Further priorities include enhancing genetic circuit stability, minimizing bacterial immunogenicity, and improving colonization and retention within host tissues. Overcoming these barriers will be essential for safe and effective clinical translation.

The rational design of next-generation smart bacteria will be substantially accelerated by integrating artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML). By training models on large-scale datasets linking DNA sequences to input-output functions, researchers can virtually screen and predict the performance of sensor modules, logic gates, and amplifiers prior to physical assembly. ML algorithms enable optimization of circuit parameters for key metrics such as diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, and dynamic range, while minimizing metabolic burden and host immunogenicity.147 This data-driven paradigm shortens development cycles and facilitates the construction of high-order, complex circuits with capabilities that are difficult to achieve using traditional approaches, thereby enabling more adaptive and precise therapeutic responses.

Ultimately, the successful implementation of smart bacteria in precision healthcare will require sustained interdisciplinary collaboration—bringing together synthetic biologists, clinicians, engineers, and regulators. In parallel, the integration of AI and high-throughput screening technologies may greatly accelerate the rational design of smart bacterial systems. Together, these advances will pave the way for a new era of programmable living medicines, with far-reaching applications in diagnostics, therapeutics, and personalized medicine.
