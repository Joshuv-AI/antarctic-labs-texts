Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma Growing in the Pacemaker Pocket
Authors: Francesco Adamo, Carlo Colaiaco, Karim Mahfouz, Ilaria Finamora, Nicola Danisi, Augusto Pappalardo, Federica Vergoni, Fabrizio Ammirati, Luca Santini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
An 88-year-old patient underwent pacemaker generator replacement. Shortly after the replacement, the pacemaker pocket became swollen and, assuming a hematoma, underwent percutaneous drainage. However, the swelling was due to an aggressive lymphoma located in the pocket. This is the first described case of T-cell lymphoma in a pacemaker pocket.

History of presentation

In 2021, an 88-year-old male patient underwent elective replacement of his dual chamber pacemaker generator due to battery depletion (Boston Scientific Altrua pacemaker, passive fixation Medtronic 4574 and 4074 leads). Past medical history

The patient had hypertension and chronic coronary artery disease (a previous myocardial infarction in 1973), and had a dual chamber pacemaker implanted on the left side in 2013 because of unexplained syncope and bifascicular block associated with first degree atrioventricular block.

Differential diagnosis

A pocket hematoma was diagnosed. The patient was asymptomatic apart from local symptoms. Blood tests showed a slight increase in inflammation markers (C-reactive protein, white blood cell count). Aspirin was interrupted, and a prophylactic 1-week antibiotic course with amoxicillin clavulanic acid was prescribed. The patient had no benefit, with a progressive enlargement of the pacemaker pocket.

Investigations

Due to the increased tension of the swelling with consequent worsening of the pain, a puncture of the pocket was carried out with the aim of detaching the pocket and at the same time taking material for culture analysis. However, on culture analysis, the liquid taken was sterile.

Management

Because of the progressive pocket enlargement, the patient underwent to surgical revision of the pocket with the finding of thrombotic material and apparent signs of colliquative necrosis. This material was submitted to perform a culture analysis, and the patient was discharged.

While the results of the culture test were awaited, due to the onset of widespread itching, the patient went to the emergency room and was admitted to the medical ward. During the hospitalization, a total body computed tomography scan showed the presence of a neoplastic mass exactly in the area of the pacemaker pocket (Figure 3). Left axillary and subclavicular lymph nodes with a maximum diameter of 8 mm were evident in the absence of mediastinal lymphadenopathy. Percutaneous biopsy through the pocket diagnosed anaplastic large cell lymphoma (Figure 4), ALK-negative (CD45+, CD20−, CD3−, CD30+, ALK1−, EMA+/−, CD2+, CD4− CD8−, CD7−, CD138−, Gramzyme B−, CD34−, S100−, CKAE1/AE3−, EBER EBV−, proliferation index KI67 80%).Figure 3Computed Tomography Image of LymphomaThe arrow indicates the lymphoma.Figure 4Biopsy of the Lesion With 40× Hematoxylin-Eosin Staining

Discussion

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma is a relatively rare case of non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL), accounting for 2% of total cases.1 The literature shows that NHL originating in the area close to cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) are very rare, and the pathophysiologic mechanism is unknown. Thirteen cases have been described in the literature.2, 3, 4 The case we describe is the only one in which NHL was diagnosed shortly after generator replacement.

All previous cases, as described in 2020 by Keyser et al,2 involved CIEDs that had been implanted for a long time (mean device residence time 94 months). Patients undergoing repeat CIED surgery had a mean device residence time of 58 months.

The cases published later also referred to tumors that were diagnosed long after the implantation or replacement of the generator.3,4

In our case, the diagnosis of anaplastic lymphoma was made approximately 3 months after device replacement.

Moreover, it is the first case of anaplastic large cell lymphoma described in the pacemaker pocket; all the cases described above were cases of B-cell lymphoma, whereas this is a case of T-cell lymphoma.

Although there are a few clinical cases of lymphomas originating from the pacemaker pocket in the literature, it is not known whether there is a causal link or whether the association is a mere coincidence, also due to the widespread use of CIEDs. Several causal hypotheses have been formulated, which need to be verified.

The presence of infection, through activation of a chronic inflammatory response and inhibition of the immune response, has been suggested as a possible cause of tumors in the pacemaker pocket. However, in our case and in the majority of previously described cases, there were no signs of infection.

Some authors have suggested a role for titanium,5 which is present in the vast majority of CIEDs, and inflammatory oncotaxis due to chronic irritation caused by the pacemaker itself with cytokine release and propagation to the surrounding tissue.5, 6, 7

Titanium has become increasingly more popular over polyurethane as a pacemaker coat material, because it has a lower risk of infection of the device.

Several theories have been speculated on the possible role of titanium in carcinogenesis, also supported by the finding of an increased prevalence of tumors, far from the pocket, such as multiple myeloma or bladder cancer, among pacemaker patients in Denmark.8

A study performed with a mouse model showed that titanium dioxide increases tumor growth by damaging the development and proliferation of B- and T- lymphocytes, reducing macrophage activity, and decreasing natural killer cell population levels.9

Another theory is that the electrostimulation itself generated by the pacemaker may be carcinogenic.5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10

All these possible causes, given the small number of cases and the absence of conclusive evidence, remain only hypotheses, and to date, it is not known whether there is a causal relationship between the presence of CIEDs and the occurrence of lymphomas in the device pocket.

In our case, because the patient underwent generator replacement shortly before diagnosis, we can suppose that the surgical trauma may have contributed to the activation of the already present lymphoma and caused a rapid growth of the tumor and enlargement of the pocket.

This case also draws attention to the importance of carefully checking the pocket at each visit, bearing in mind that an enlargement of the pocket could also be due to a neoplasm and not just a hematoma or infection, avoiding unnecessary procedures and addressing the diagnostic-therapeutic process correctly and in time.

Follow up

After discussion with the patient and his family, considering age and comorbidities, a palliative therapy was chosen, and the patient was followed by oncologists and pain therapy physicians.

After 5 months, the patient died of cancer.

Conclusions

Although rare, there is the possibility of the occurrence of neoplasms, particularly lymphomas, in the pacemaker pocket, even a short time after implantation or replacement, as in this case.

Notably, this is the first case of T-cell lymphoma in the pacemaker pocket described in the literature, as the cases already described involve B-cell lymphomas.

It is of paramount importance to carefully assess the device pocket at each check-up and to consider that its enlargement may also be due to a neoplasm, as well as a hematoma or infection.
