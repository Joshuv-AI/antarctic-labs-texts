Differences between different antiretroviral therapy regimens switching patterns among people living with HIV
Authors: Hanxi Zhang, Ye Su, Yanping Yu, Zhennan Li, Yuxiu Xi, Aixia Wang, Jing Han, Hongxin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have extended the life expectancy of people living with HIV (PLHIV). The long-term goals of ART for PLHIV were better medication convenience and fewer side effects. The development of the Internet and increased education allow PLHIV to understand evolving and optimized regimens and participated in decision-making. Understanding ART switching patterns can help identify key populations for medical institutions or social organizations’ publicity and education.MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited PLHIV (≥ 18 years) receiving ART at the Hospital’s outpatient clinic between February 13 and October 26, 2023. Social characteristics, ART switching patterns, and health education-related information were collected. The patient-driven switching pattern was defined as PLHIV volunteering to switch treatment regimens, while the physician-driven pattern was described as doctors suggesting regimen switches based on routine test results. Multinomial regression was used to explore factors associated with ART switching patterns.ResultsOut of 1877 PLHIV analyzed, 93.98% were male with a median age of 39 (33, 48) years. 34.04% (639/1877) had switched ART regimens, and 11.88% made the switch themselves. Economic status, age, and attending education lectures were associated with ART switching patterns. Participants with an average income of less than 5000 yuan tended to switch regimens driven by physicians, while those with steady jobs and under 30 years old were more likely to switch on their own. Those who never attended education lectures or had an average income of less than 10,000 yuan tended not to change ART regimens at all.ConclusionPromoting active switching and optimizing ART may be achieved through health education in medical institutions for ART decision-making in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26699-5.

Introduction

At the end of 2022, approximately 39 million people were living with HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) globally, and 77% of adults (≥ 15 years old) living with HIV had access to antiretroviral therapy (ART) [1]. Currently, ART requires daily administration for the whole life of people living with HIV (PLHIV). International and national guidelines emphasize the importance of durable and straightforward ART regimens as the primary focus of lifelong chronic HIV treatment [2–4]. Effective ART has successfully controlled viral levels in PLHIV and significantly reduced their mortality rates [5]. Optimizing ART, including enhancing medication convenience and minimizing side effects, has become a new goal among both PLHIV and medical practitioners.

In China, standardized ART was unavailable to most PLHIV until 2003, when the national AIDS control policy “Four Frees and One Care” was announced and the National Free Antiretroviral Treatment Program (NFATP) was established. Initially, the available antiretroviral therapies in China were non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor (NNRTI) -based regimens using zidovudine (AZT) or stavudine (d4T), as well as PI-based regimens such as lopinavir/ritonavir (LPV/r). In 2011, NFATP incorporated the tenofovir (TDF)- based NNRTI regimen recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) for resource-limited settings, providing all PLHIV with TDF+3TC (lamivudine) + EFV (efavirenz) combination therapy at no cost. This regimen has remained in use since then. From 2015 onward, raltegravir (RAL) and dolutegravir (DTG) have been used in PLHIV in China. From 2018, single-pill INSTI regimens containing emtricitabine (FTC)/tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (TAF) became widely used [6–10].

According to China’s national ART guidelines, adjustment and optimization of ART regimens can be considered or encouraged for PLHIV with effective viral suppression after starting ART. This process is also known as a smooth transition of treatment in the clinic. The following situations may warrant consideration: (1) Simplifying the treatment regimen by reducing the number of tablets and frequency of administration; (2) Improving tolerance and reducing short-term or long-term toxicity; (3) Preventing or mitigating drug interactions, among others [2, 3]. A smooth conversion may facilitate the optimization of treatment plans, thereby improve medication compliance and treatment effectiveness, and contributing to achieving the goal of “U = U” - undetectable equals untransmittable - ultimately leading to an end to the AIDS epidemic.

Systematic reviews have shown that people living with HIV will nearly all have changes in their ART at some point during their follow-up [11, 12]. Between 2.4% and 50% of PLHIV who initiate ART require a regimen switch within the first year of treatment [13]. Another research showed that treatment switching was linked to the availability of routine viral load monitoring, and places without routine testing experience lower and delayed switching to second-line regimens, which indicated that expanding routine viral load testing enabled earlier detection of treatment failure and more timely switching, ultimately improving long-term treatment outcomes [14]. There are many different reasons for switching ART regimens in the HIV population, due to drug changes required for toxicity, treatment failure, or to update or simplify their regimen [15]. Cao W. et al. reviewed the optimization of treatment for adults with HIV in China. The study reported that research on ART regimen switching has focused on optimizing first-line therapy by adjusting drug combinations to reduce toxicity and improve adherence in China. And reported that ART switching can maintain virological suppression while minimizing adverse effects, supporting more effective and tolerable long-term treatment for people living with HIV [16]. ART changes in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are limited by available treatment options [17].

In this study, we included the PLHIV with effective virus inhibition and divided the PLHIV into three groups. Among them, the PLHIV who switched ART regimens had two patterns, including patient-driven and physician-driven, representing the two perspectives for ART regimen switching. The patient-driven was defined as PLHIV volunteering to switch treatment regimens, and the physician-driven pattern was defined as doctors suggesting PLHIV switch regimens based on routine test results. The objective of the present study was to assess the switch proportions and compare the possible factors related to different switching patterns in PLHIV, using a cross-sectional investigation in the HIV outpatient clinic. To know the factors related to ART switching, and to provide the policy-making basis for health education, then accomplish personalized diagnosis and treatment optimization in the future.

Methods

Study population and study site

The study population consisted of virologic suppression people living with HIV (≥ 18 years) receiving ART who were voluntarily recruited between February 13 and October 26, 2023, in the ART clinic of Beijing Ditan Hospital, which is the National Medical Center for Infectious Diseases and the first designated medical institution to carry out the ART in China. The hospital is serving an HIV-positive population of over 11,000 at the end of 2023. The antiretroviral therapy clinic operates daily clinical consultations, laboratory tests, and patient support groups; case manager counseling or testing was also available.

Study design and data collection

The study focused on the different patterns of switching ART regimens and related factors. We used a cross-sectional study design, and participants were recruited using convenience sampling from the pool of potential participants during routine ART follow-ups. The questionnaires were conducted by a frequently used electronic platform, which had advantages for privacy protection and data management. The questionnaire’s two-dimensional code was stuck to the wall of the outpatient consultation area and sent to the social media (WeChat) group to avoid selection bias. Study staff described study objectives, and clinical care providers referred interested PLHIV to the study investigators. The outline contents of the questionnaire and the respondents who were allowed to fill in were explained before the main text of the questionnaire: Dear participants, we are The Home of Red Ribbon. This questionnaire is designed to assess your attitude and status of switching to antiviral therapy. You can fill in this questionnaire voluntarily by scanning the code. At the beginning of the questionnaire, the intention of this survey was explained, that this was a voluntary survey and did not involve any personal privacy information.

The questionnaire was constructed based on discussions involving a team of case management healthcare providers in HIV and clinicians. It mainly comprised sociodemographic items, such as age, sex, job, marriage (the marital status mentioned in this article referred to being married to a partner of a different sex), and average income. Each respondent provided information about whether to switch the ART regimens, the reason for switching or not, and the current status of ART-related characteristics (ART period, follow-up times per year, and current class of ART regimens). Advocacy education or peer education acceptance was also collected.

In the study, viral suppression was defined as an HIV viral load of < 200 copies/mL. The questionnaire asked the participants about their most recent viral load test result first. Participants checked their most recent viral load from their clinical blood test results. If the result did not meet the criterion, the survey automatically skipped to the end and concluded.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was in Supplement-S0. Median and inter-quartile range (IQR) were used to describe numeric variables. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and percentages. At the bivariate level, Pearson’s Chi-square test was used for the comparison of frequencies. Multinomial logistic regression was used to explore the factors associated with ART switching patterns, and adjusted odds ratios (OR) and 95% CIs were computed using the Enter approach. Sensitive analysis was applied to estimate risk factors associated with ART switching patterns. The level of significance in this study was set at a p-value < 0.05. Data were analyzed using R studio 4.2.1 (2022-06-23) for macOS.

Results

Participant characteristics

The study received 2162 questionnaires, and a total of 1877 valid questionnaires were included ultimately (See the flow chart in Supplement-S1). The effective rate was 86.8%.

The version of the questionnaire was attached in Supplement-S2. Among the participants, 93.98% were male and the median age was 39 (33, 48) years old, 55.05% in the age group of 30–45 years old. 64.25% of the participants had a steady job, and 49.92% of them had a range of 0-5000 incomes (CNY). Meanwhile, most of them (58.76%) were single. The advocacy education or peer education acceptance of study participants in different ART switching groups were also investigated. Switching ART regimens

Of the total participants, about 34.04% (639/1877) have changed the ART regimens, and 11.88% (223/1877) were patient-driven to change the ART medication. The bivariate level Chi-square test results showed that there were distribution differences in ART regimens switched or not among participants with different characteristics (p < 0.05) (See details in Tables 1 and 2).

We set physician-driven switching as the reference group to conduct the multinomial logistic regression. The results showed that the driving reason for switching ART regimens was associated with sex, age, having a steady job or not, average income, and the frequency of attending education lectures. Participants with an average income of less than 5000 yuan tended to switch regimens driven by physicians (aOR = 0.507, 95%CI: 0.266∼0.965, p = 0.039). Participants who had a steady job and were younger than 30 years old were more likely to switch regimens driven by themselves (aOR = 1.717, 95%CI: 1.088∼2.710, p = 0.020; aOR = 2.044, 95%CI: 1.071∼3.901, p = 0.030) (Fig. 1-A). For not changing of ART regimens, participants who never attended the education lecture tended not to change the medication (aOR = 1.536, 95%CI: 1.028∼2.296, p = 0.036). Similarly, participants who had an average income of less than 10,000 yuan also tended not to change ART regimens (aOR = 2.096, 95%CI: 1.255∼3.502, p = 0.005; aOR = 2.219, 95%CI: 1.337∼3.682, p = 0.002). The male and younger than 45 years old participants also tended not to change the regimens. (Fig. 1-B). See the detailed data of multinomial logistic regression in Supplement materials-S3.

We also investigated the advocacy education or peer education acceptance of study participants in different ART switching groups (Table 2). The results showed that most PLHIV attended the education lectures with a frequency of “Sometimes”, and more than 80% of them tended to follow the latest medical progress. There was a significant difference in the way to get HIV-related information between different ART switching groups, which reported that PLHIV who get information from the Internet had a higher proportion of switching ART regimens driven by themselves. We also made a subgroup multinomial analysis of factors related to ART switching among different ways to get HIV-related information (Supplement materials-S4).

ART-related characteristics

The participants who were switching driven by physicians have a larger proportion of experienced ART of more than 10 years. There was a larger proportion of experienced ART between 3 and 5 years among participants who did not change the ART regimens (Fig. 2). At the same time, participants who did not change the ART regimens had a smaller proportion of follow-up > 4 times per year compared with the other two groups (Fig. 2). It was worth noting that, for the current class of ART, there was a large proportion of taking National free ART regimens among participants who did not change the ART regimens and switching regimens driven by patients who had a large proportion of taking medical insurance ART currently (Fig. 2), which implied that most of PLHIV switched ART regimens from National free therapy to medical insurance funded or self-funded therapy. Characteristics and reasons related to ART switching

Among those who switched ART medication, we investigated the number of times they switched the medication, the satisfaction after switching, and the reasons for switching. The results showed that no matter what kind of intention-driven switch, most of them mainly switched ART regimens once (77.13% or 71.64%), and most of them were satisfied with the regimens after switching (90.14% or 88.94%). Results also showed that the differences in reasons for switching were statistically significant. In the physician-driven switching group, the main reason was medication side effects, while in the patient-driven switching group, the main reason was inconvenient to take current medicines, or there was a better medicine added to the medical insurance (p < 0.001). For participants who did not change the ART regimens, more than half were willing to switch medication in the future if doctors suggested (55.66%). The dominant reason for not switching was that participants thought the current treatment status was stable and the viral load was undetectable (51.38%) (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to assess the different ART switch patterns from the patients’ or physicians’ angle and the reasons of ART regimens in China. At present, the adjustment of the treatment plan is mainly based on virological results. Most studies focused on the switch to second-line antiretroviral therapy after viral failure [18–20]. However, with the spread of simplified ART, the needs of PLHIV and the optimization of treatment also need to be considered. Therefore, this study conducted a cross-sectional investigation comparing the different ART switching patterns, the reasons for switching, and the health education situation of PLHIV to provide evidence for personalized and optimized treatment in the future.

Our analysis results showed that 34.04% (639/1877) had changed the ART regimens, and 11.88% were patient-driven to change the ART medication. A previous study found that at a large ART clinic in Cape Town, South Africa, approximately 30% of young people underwent a switch in their prescribed ART over the course of their care [9]. Participants with an average income of less than 5,000 yuan tended to switch regimens at the physician’s direction. Participants who had a steady job and were younger than 30 years old were more likely to switch ART regimens driven by themselves. In addition, participants who never attended the education lecture and with an average income of less than 10,000 yuan tended not to change ART regimens. The steady job and higher income were factors affecting whether the ART regimens were switched or not. China introduced the “Four Frees and One Care” policy in 2003, providing antiretroviral treatment for HIV patients, free counseling and testing, and support for affected families [21, 22]. The categories of free ART regimens were followed by the National Antiviral Therapy Manual, which illustrated that the free ART regimens were mainly two NRTIs plus one NNRTI or one PIs [23], while the INSTIs-based regimens belonged to the medical insurance category or self-funded category, which needed to be afforded a part or all costs by PLHIV themselves. This may lead PLHIV who did not have a stable job or had low income to choose not to change the medication to reduce the cost of medical treatment under the condition of virological suppression. Or switched the regimens according to the doctor’s advice when there was a situation requiring ART switching. Vikram et al. conducted a prospective cohort study established in three community medical centers and two university medical centers about early ART initiation rates and associated factors in 348 PLHIV, and the results showed that having paid employment, adjusted odds ratio (aOR: 2.43; 95% CI: 1.19, 4.95) was independently associated with early ART initiation [24].

While the participants younger than 30 years old were more likely to switch ART regimens driven by themselves. This may be related to the population of this study. In this study, 81.8% of the participants with an income of more than CNY 5,000 were under 45 years old. At the same time, Ditan Hospital, where this study was conducted, even the whole Beijing area were dominated by young male homosexual transmission [25]. Sexual transmission by men who have sex with men (MSM) disproportionately accounted for 77.97% of the newly diagnosed cases in Beijing [26]. The relatively good economic strength and relatively high educational level among MSM may promote their attention to treatment [27, 28]. At the same time, younger and employed participants being more likely to switch regimens on their own may also reflect the impact of digital health literacy and online medical information access. Studies showed that effective use of digital media can improve HIV knowledge among young people, although its impact varied by platform and type of engagement. Higher digital health literacy also led to greater stigma reduction and more inclusive HIV education [29, 30]. With the increasing availability of HIV treatment updates via social media or online platforms, younger PLHIV may more easily rely on digital information sources to guide treatment decisions.

The study showed the main reason for PLHIV who switched regimens, driven by physicians, was medication side effects (31.8%), while the main reason for PLHIV who switched regimens, driven by patients themselves, was to take medicine more conveniently (35.43%). Patient-driven switching was more common among individuals who had medical insurance or used self-funded therapy, who may have greater flexibility and motivation to optimize convenience and reduce pill burden. These individuals also tended to have more frequent follow-ups and longer ART duration, suggesting that greater engagement with care and accumulated treatment experience may heighten awareness of alternative regimen options and empower patients to initiate switching. Physician-driven switching was more pronounced among those on nationally free regimens and among individuals with moderate follow-up frequency. These individuals were primarily driven by factors that might affect daily life, such as drug side effects, without yet considering the level of treatment experience. These individuals may also pay less attention to or have limited access to newer options and lack awareness of self-initiated medication switching. Therefore, their switching decisions in this group may be more passive and depend more heavily on the clinical judgment of physicians in response to adverse effects rather than their preference.

We also found that participants who never attended the education lecture tended not to switch ART regimens. The health education in this study was hosted by The Home of Red Ribbon, which was a non-governmental organization affiliated with Beijing Ditan Hospital. The lectures were mainly delivered by physicians or case managers on antiviral treatment, drug side effects, and health management. Therefore, not attending lectures might lead to less knowledge related to antiviral therapy and the inability to timely know the progress of antiviral drugs and the latest treatment strategies, which might lead to PLHIV choosing not to switch the regimens, especially when there are no drug side effects. Given the rapid development of antiretroviral drugs, the timely dissemination of information on newly approved medications and ongoing scientific research may also enhance patients’ capacity to make informed switching decisions. Strengthening these educational efforts, such as through digital platforms, may support regimen optimization. The multinomial regression showed that the differences in the way to get HIV-related information between different ART-switching groups were not statistically significant. Nonetheless, the results showed that participants who switched ART regimens driven by physicians had a higher proportion of getting HIV information from medical institutions (Chi-square test p < 0.05). PLHIV who obtain information from medical institutions may be older or have poorer financial levels. They might seek medical treatment after discovering the side effects of drugs, and then switch medications under the guidance of doctors. PLHIV who chose to obtain HIV-related information through the Internet were those who actively pay attention to their health status or with good economic status, to make them participate in their ART, not just follow the physicians’ suggestions, which might lead them to switch ART regimens actively. Prior work among adults with low CD4 counts showed they were more likely to switch medication [31]. Another study reported that the majority of ART switches before 2010 were due to side effects (35%); however, after 2010, with the advent of better-tolerated treatment regimens, side effects only accounted for 6.6% of switches [15]. Switching may occur because patients or clinicians think a new medication may produce better results. Drug toxicity or side effects, and drug-related adverse events (AEs) tended to be the switch reasons from physicians’ angles, and unsatisfactory levels of viral suppression or just simplification of a regimen from patients’ perspectives may prompt a switch [32–34]. At the same time, health education was an important factor affecting regimen switching. So, improving the participation of PLHIV in HIV education in outpatient clinics might prompt the smooth conversion of antiviral treatment regimens and then achieve ART optimization. Developing more structured HIV related education programs, increasing the frequency and accessibility of ART-related information updates, and designing more flexible ART service models may enhance PLHIV autonomy and support timely regimen optimization.

Our study has several limitations that need to be considered while interpreting the results. First, our study population was predominantly comprised of MSM, and data were collected by convenience sampling, which limits the generalizability of study findings. However, the MSM group represents the majority of PLHIV in Beijing despite the presence of small variations. Second, recruitment through WeChat platforms may introduce self-selection bias, as individuals who are more digitally active, socially connected, or health-conscious may have been more likely to participate. Third, due to the cross-sectional design, conducted by a self-report questionnaire, and the setting of some questionnaire questions did not limit the answer time point, so we are unable to infer causal relationships between factors and switching behaviors, and were impacted by personal expectations.

Conclusion

Our study mainly focused on the difference between ART switching patterns and related factors. The results implied that the higher average income, younger age group, and ever-attended education lectures were patient-driven ART switching-driven patterns-related factors. Although most participants had not switched regimens, nearly all expressed willingness to do so under appropriate medical or economic conditions. For people living with HIV who are virologically suppressed, enhancing education on the benefits, indications, and processes of patient-driven switching may facilitate timely transitions and contribute to further optimization of ART. In addition, increasing the accessibility of education on newly available antiretroviral options, including emerging drugs with improved efficacy or fewer side effects, may help ensure that individuals can obtain updated information promptly and make informed decisions about regimen switching when appropriate.
