Evaluating the clinical decision-making performance of large language models in clinically oriented thoracic anatomy scenarios: a comparative evaluation study
Authors: Zeynep Nisa Karakoyun, Mustafa Deniz Yörük, Mehmed Emre Özdemir, Mehmet İlkay Koşar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models are increasingly used as supportive tools in medical education; however, their reliability in anatomically based clinical reasoning remains insufficiently defined. This study aimed to compare the accuracy, depth, and clinical applicability of model-generated responses to thoracic anatomy–based clinical scenarios and to assess inter-rater reliability among expert anatomists.MethodsThis exploratory comparative study evaluated four widely used models—ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V2, Gemini, and Grok—using 20 open-ended thoracic anatomy questions derived from seven clinical scenarios adapted from Moore’s Clinically Oriented Anatomy. Background

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has generated growing interest in its applications in healthcare and medical education. In particular, the emergence of large language models (LLMs) has introduced new opportunities for knowledge retrieval, educational support, and automated content generation [1–6].

In parallel with the digital transformation of healthcare, AI and natural language processing technologies are increasingly integrated into medical education and clinical decision-support systems. Large language models (LLMs), including ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V2, Gemini, and Grok, are increasingly used as supplementary learning tools due to their accessibility and perceived ability to support clinical reasoning. These AI systems may enhance medical education, assist diagnostic processes, and strengthen clinically oriented anatomical reasoning. However, their outputs can vary depending on model architecture and prompt design, and confidently presented inaccuracies may mislead users in clinical contexts. Consequently, LLMs increasingly function as virtual tutors and reference assistants by providing rapid and contextually relevant responses to complex queries [7–14]. In this context, LLMs can be considered health information technologies whose reliability must be carefully evaluated before their use in educational or decision-support settings.

Despite these promising applications, several limitations remain regarding the integration of artificial intelligence in medical education. Outputs generated by LLMs may vary depending on model architecture, prompt design, and contextual interpretation. In addition, these systems may produce “hallucinations,” presenting incorrect or fabricated information with high confidence. Such issues raise concerns about the reliability and safety of LLM-generated content in educational and clinical contexts. Therefore, careful empirical evaluation is required before these technologies can be responsibly integrated into medical education [15–17].

Beyond examination performance, LLMs are increasingly being explored as supportive tools in medical education. AI-based learning systems can provide adaptive environments that respond to students’ knowledge gaps, generate practice questions, offer explanations for answers, and simulate examination-like scenarios for self-assessment. In addition, LLMs are capable of synthesizing information from multiple medical sources, which may help students better understand complex topics across basic medical sciences [8, 18].

Traditional anatomy education has largely relied on cadaveric dissection and textbook-based learning approaches. However, these methods present several limitations, including restricted accessibility, time constraints, and limited ability to simulate dynamic pathological conditions. As access to traditional educational resources such as cadaveric dissection becomes increasingly limited, innovative teaching approaches incorporating digital technologies have emerged. In this context, the use of LLMs in anatomy education may support preparatory learning before laboratory or dissection sessions, reinforce anatomical–clinical integration through scenario-based revision, and provide structured opportunities for self-assessment under expert supervision [19–23]. Cadaveric dissection and instructor-guided laboratory teaching remain essential for developing three-dimensional spatial understanding and professional competence in anatomy [24–26].

Another important challenge concerns the variability of LLM performance across different disciplines and types of reasoning tasks. While many studies indicate that LLMs perform well on questions requiring factual knowledge, they often struggle with tasks that demand deeper clinical reasoning, contextual interpretation, or spatial understanding. For example, research in anatomy education suggests that although LLMs may function as engaging pedagogical tools, their ability to provide detailed anatomical explanations or generate accurate visual representations remains limited. Similarly, in several clinical domains, the performance of AI systems has been reported to approximate that of junior medical trainees, yet still falls short of the complex reasoning abilities demonstrated by experienced clinicians [27–29]. Artificial intelligence applications have been investigated across multiple medical disciplines, including anatomy, radiology, ophthalmology, cardiology, and dentistry [22, 30–37].

In recent years, several comparative studies have evaluated the performance of large language models across various medical and dental contexts, including anatomy education, clinical decision support, and examination-based knowledge assessment. These studies indicate that model performance varies depending on the domain, prompt design, and complexity of reasoning tasks. For example, previous research has compared the accuracy, completeness, and readability of responses generated by AI chatbots such as ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek, and Gemini in dental trauma management and endodontic decision-making scenarios [38]. Other studies have examined the ability of LLMs to generate educational content and multiple-choice questions aligned with Bloom’s taxonomy, revealing differences in cognitive depth among models including ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek, Grok, and Copilot [39] Comparative analyses in anatomy education also show that several contemporary models can successfully answer examination-style anatomy questions, although performance varies across models and question types [40].

The LLMs included in this study were selected based on their accessibility, growing use in biomedical research, and representation of different AI development ecosystems. ChatGPT and Gemini are widely investigated models in medical education, whereas emerging systems such as DeepSeek and Grok have recently gained attention in biomedical AI research, enabling a broader comparison of current LLM capabilities in clinically oriented anatomy reasoning tasks [41, 42].

AI-assisted learning materials can reinforce classroom teaching; however, such content should be carefully evaluated and contextualized by educators. Recent research also highlights the importance of enabling students to critically evaluate the accuracy and reliability of AI-generated outputs [37, 43].

Within the basic medical sciences, anatomy represents a particularly important domain for evaluating the capabilities of LLM-based educational tools. Anatomical knowledge forms the foundation of clinical reasoning by enabling the understanding of structural relationships that underlie diagnostic interpretation and therapeutic decision-making. In particular, thoracic anatomy holds a central role in both medical education and clinical practice due to the complex structural organization of vital components within the thoracic cavity, including the lungs, heart, mediastinum, and major vascular structures. Accurate interpretation of thoracic anatomy is therefore essential in several clinical contexts, such as imaging interpretation, emergency management, and surgical planning. Consequently, integrating anatomical knowledge with clinical reasoning remains an important educational objective for medical students and clinicians, yet it continues to represent a significant challenge, particularly within scenario-based learning environments [44–50].

Although studies examining the performance of LLMs in medical education and examinations have increased rapidly [8–10, 51], evidence regarding the reliability and clinical applicability of AI-generated anatomical explanations remains limited, particularly in thoracic anatomy. Most studies focus on multiple-choice examinations assessing factual knowledge rather than scenario-based reasoning. In addition, heterogeneity in scenario design, evaluation methods, and scoring frameworks limits the comparability of findings. Consequently, systematic comparative evaluations focusing on clinically oriented thoracic anatomy scenarios remain scarce.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the performance of four large language models in responding to thoracic anatomy questions embedded within clinical scenarios. The study also aimed to determine inter-rater reliability among expert anatomists evaluating model responses and to provide insight into the strengths and limitations of LLMs in anatomically grounded clinical reasoning. In addition, the scenario set included intentionally unrelated or context-mismatched questions to examine whether the models could recognize inconsistencies within the clinical context. By applying a standardized scoring approach based on expert anatomist ratings and inter-rater reliability analysis, this study proposes a reproducible framework for identifying model-specific strengths and limitations in clinically oriented anatomical reasoning.

Such benchmarking is important not only for evaluating the educational potential of AI systems but also for identifying clinically relevant failure modes, including incomplete reasoning and anatomically implausible explanations. The findings of this study may contribute to the responsible integration of AI-based tools into biomedical education and related clinical decision-support contexts.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a comparative exploratory analysis evaluating the performance of artificial intelligence–based LLMs in answering open-ended, clinically oriented thoracic anatomy questions. The primary objective was to assess the anatomical accuracy, clinical relevance, and consistency of AI-generated responses within the context of anatomy education. The overall design of scenario development, response collection, blinded scoring, and statistical analysis is summarized in Fig. 1.

Study design illustrating the evaluation workflow for clinical anatomy reasoning in large language models (LLMs). Seven thoracic clinical scenarios generated 20 open-ended questions, which were submitted to four LLMs (ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V2, Gemini, and Grok). A total of 80 responses were anonymized and randomized before double-blind evaluation by two senior anatomists using a predefined 10-point scoring rubric assessing accuracy, completeness, and reasoning. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and model performance was compared using non-parametric statistical analyses

The study did not involve human participants, patient data, or identifiable personal information. Two expert anatomists participated as independent raters to evaluate model outputs. As the study analyzed responses generated by publicly available AI systems without involving patient data or human subjects, formal ethics committee approval was not required. The study adhered to ethical principles relevant to responsible artificial intelligence research and was conducted in accordance with the general ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Development of clinical scenarios and questions

Seven thoracic clinical scenarios were developed based on Moore’s Clinically Oriented Anatomy [52], a widely used reference in anatomy education. Anatomical concepts and reference answers were cross-checked with the most recent 9th edition (2023) to ensure consistency with contemporary anatomical knowledge. Scenario stems and questions were paraphrased and adapted for research use, and no copyrighted text was reproduced verbatim.

The scenarios were selected to represent major thoracic anatomical domains commonly addressed in undergraduate medical curricula, including cardiovascular structures, pulmonary anatomy, pleural innervation, mediastinal relationships, and lymphatic pathways. This selection aimed to ensure that the evaluated questions reflected clinically meaningful anatomical contexts frequently encountered in anatomy education and clinical training.

A total of 20 open-ended questions were constructed to evaluate anatomical knowledge and clinically oriented reasoning. The questions addressed anatomical relationships, innervation and vascular supply, clinical correlations, mechanisms of injury, and surgical relevance. The questions were developed through expert consensus to represent clinically relevant thoracic anatomy concepts commonly addressed in undergraduate medical curricula, with the aim of covering a range of anatomical relationships and clinical correlations.

Clinical scenarios and question distribution

The questions were designed to evaluate the ability of AI systems to integrate anatomical knowledge with clinical reasoning, including referred pain pathways, lymphatic and vascular spread, pleural innervation, bronchial anatomy, cardiac structural defects, and mechanisms of atelectasis and thromboembolism.

To assess contextual reasoning, three questions originally belonging to Case 5 (Questions 11–13) were intentionally embedded within Case 7 as its final items (Questions 17–19). These questions were anatomically and clinically unrelated to the pulmonary thromboembolism scenario and were included to evaluate whether the models would recognize contextual inconsistency, or provide anatomically accurate yet contextually generic responses. These items were scored using the same predefined rubric as all other questions to ensure methodological consistency. Recognition of contextual mismatch did not receive additional scoring credit, and evaluation focused solely on anatomical accuracy, completeness, and clinical relevance.

AI model selection and response collection

Four widely used and publicly accessible LLMs with different technological architectures were selected for comparison: ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI, USA), DeepSeek-V2 (DeepSeek AI, China), Gemini (Google, USA), and Grok (xAI, USA). These models were chosen based on their global accessibility, established natural language processing capabilities, and increasing use in medical education contexts.

All clinical scenarios and associated questions were entered into each AI platform on the same day under standardized conditions. All models were accessed through their publicly available web interfaces using the versions available on March 10, 2025. Each model was accessed through its official web interface, and separate sessions were initiated for each platform to avoid contextual carryover between models.

For each case, the complete clinical scenario was first presented, followed by its corresponding questions entered sequentially within the same interaction window. This structure was used to preserve case continuity and simulate scenario-based learning contexts in which multiple questions are interpreted within a shared clinical framework.

No prompt engineering techniques (e.g., role assignment, chain-of-thought prompting, or iterative refinement) were applied. All questions were entered verbatim and identically across models to evaluate baseline response behavior under minimally guided conditions. Queries were submitted by an academic without formal medical training in order to minimize potential disciplinary framing effects. AI-generated responses were archived verbatim immediately after generation to preserve data integrity before expert evaluation.

A single response was generated for each question for each model in order to simulate typical real-world use by non-expert users and to avoid variability associated with repeated prompt optimization.

Scoring procedure and expert evaluation

Reference answers were constructed prior to AI model testing and included key anatomical and clinically relevant elements expected in an ideal response. These reference answers were developed through structured consensus among three senior anatomists with experience in clinically integrated anatomy teaching.

Two of the three anatomists subsequently participated as independent evaluators in the scoring process. AI-generated responses were anonymized and presented in randomized order, and raters were blinded to model identity. Each response was independently evaluated using a standardized 10-point scoring rubric emphasizing anatomical accuracy, completeness, and clinical relevance relative to the reference framework. A third anatomist served as a supervisory reviewer and was consulted only in cases of scoring discrepancy or interpretative ambiguity to ensure alignment with the predefined reference framework and maintain methodological consistency.

In addition to numerical scoring, evaluators provided qualitative reflections regarding the strengths, limitations, and potential educational applicability of each model’s responses. Although a practicing clinician did not participate directly in the reference framework development, the scenarios were selected to reflect clinically meaningful anatomical contexts in which anatomical knowledge informs clinical interpretation.

Recognition of contextual mismatch was not included as a separate scoring criterion in order to preserve uniform evaluation of anatomical accuracy and clinical relevance across all questions. However, instances in which models explicitly identified contextual inconsistencies were documented and reported descriptively in the results section.

Statistical analysis

The number of questions was determined based on the set of clinically distinct thoracic scenarios identified from the reference textbook and the feasibility of detailed expert evaluation using rubric-based scoring.

All expert ratings were initially compiled using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses were subsequently performed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc Statistical Software version 14.8.1 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium).

Inter-rater reliability between the two independent evaluators was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Since the same two raters independently evaluated all responses, ICC estimates were calculated in MedCalc using a two-way mixed-effects model with a consistency definition based on average measures (ICC[3,2]). Results were reported together with 95% confidence intervals.

The distribution of scores was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As the assumption of normality was not satisfied, non-parametric statistical methods were used. Differences between AI models were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. When statistically significant differences were identified, pairwise comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U tests with Bonferroni correction.

As the study was designed as an exploratory benchmarking analysis, no a priori power calculation was performed. Descriptive statistics are presented as median (minimum–maximum). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To further evaluate the robustness of the group comparisons, a post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on the observed group means and within-group standard deviations, the estimated effect size was f = 0.54, corresponding to a large effect size according to Cohen’s criteria. With a significance level of α = 0.05, the achieved statistical power was 94.8% (1 − β = 0.948), indicating that the study had adequate power to detect differences among the groups.

Further post-hoc analysis (implied by the superscript letters) revealed a significant difference between Grok and the other three models. Grok’s median score of 7.75 (range 5–10) was found to be statistically distinct and significantly higher than the scores of the other models. To evaluate the potential influence of the embedded questions (Q17–19), a sensitivity comparison was performed. Median scores remained stable after excluding these items (ChatGPT-4o: 4.0 vs. 4.0; DeepSeek: 5.0 vs. 4.5; Gemini: 3.0 vs. 3.0; Grok: 8.0 vs. 8.25), indicating that these exploratory questions did not substantially alter the comparative performance results.

Detection of contextual mismatch in embedded clinical questions

Three questions originally belonging to Case 5 (bronchial obstruction due to foreign body aspiration) were intentionally embedded within Case 7 (pulmonary thromboembolism) to examine contextual consistency.

ChatGPT-4o and Grok explicitly identified these questions as belonging to a different clinical scenario and indicated that they corresponded to bronchial obstruction mechanisms previously described in Case 5. In contrast, DeepSeek and Gemini did not comment on the contextual inconsistency and responded to the questions directly, providing explanations related to bronchial obstruction mechanisms similar to their earlier responses for Case 5.

The final question in this sequence (Q20) was directly related to the pulmonary thromboembolism scenario. All models addressed this question within the correct clinical context; however, expert evaluation scores varied (ChatGPT-4o: 3, DeepSeek: 4, Gemini: 3, Grok: 6).

Discussion

This study provides a comparative evaluation of the clinically oriented anatomical reasoning capacity of four widely used large language models (LLMs). The findings demonstrate clear differences in the accuracy, completeness, and explanatory depth of model responses. Among the evaluated systems, Grok achieved the highest overall performance, whereas ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-V2, and Gemini demonstrated lower and relatively comparable scores. These results suggest that although LLMs show promising potential as educational tools, their ability to support clinically contextualized anatomical reasoning remains variable across models.

As LLM-generated outputs are increasingly used by students as supplementary learning resources, systematic benchmarking of anatomy-related reasoning abilities is becoming increasingly important for both educational integration and responsible use in health-related contexts. In addition, this study incorporated a subset of contextually mismatched questions in order to explore the robustness of model reasoning when faced with inconsistencies between the prompt and the clinical scenario. Sensitivity analyses showed that the exclusion of these exploratory items did not materially alter the relative ranking of the evaluated models, supporting the overall stability of the findings. Nevertheless, the relatively small number of evaluated questions may have limited the granularity of model comparisons and the precision of reliability estimates.

Anatomy education is currently undergoing significant transformation through the integration of digital technologies, including artificial intelligence–based learning tools [22, 23]. In recent years, several studies have examined the performance of large language models across different areas of medical education. For example, Bolgova et al. evaluated multiple LLMs using embryology questions and reported relatively high accuracy rates for advanced models such as GPT-4o and Claude, although the authors emphasized that expert supervision remained essential for reliable educational use [21]. Similarly, Singal and Goyal observed inconsistent responses from ChatGPT-3.5 and GPT-4 when answering regional anatomy questions, highlighting the importance of careful expert evaluation of AI-generated explanations in anatomy education [19].

A number of previous studies in health professions education have reported strong LLM performance when models are evaluated using structured knowledge assessments. Recent studies in dental education similarly suggest that LLM performance may vary substantially according to task type and domain. For example, ChatGPT-4 demonstrated high overall accuracy but only moderate completeness when answering both frequently asked questions and curricular pediatric dentistry questions, with performance differing across topic areas such as fissure sealants and pulpal therapy [53]. Likewise, in a comparative study of pulpal and periapical diseases, ChatGPT-4o performed closer to senior dental students, whereas Gemini and Microsoft Copilot showed lower accuracy, further underscoring cross-model variability in dentistry-oriented educational tasks [54]. In addition, multimodal LLMs tested on complex dental inquiries involving text, images, and analytical data demonstrated significant differences in performance, with common weaknesses including misinterpretation of clinical scenarios and visual information [55]. Similarly, studies in dental anatomy and virology have reported relatively high accuracy when LLMs are evaluated using multiple-choice or examination-style questions [20, 32, 40]. However, such formats primarily assess recognition of correct information rather than deeper explanatory reasoning.

In contrast, the present study employed open-ended, scenario-based thoracic anatomy questions. These tasks required models not only to recall anatomical knowledge but also to integrate structural relationships with clinically relevant mechanisms and produce explanatory responses. From this perspective, scenario-based questions may provide a more demanding and ecologically valid benchmark for evaluating LLM reasoning abilities than multiple-choice examinations. Thoracic anatomy in particular involves complex spatial and functional relationships among cardiovascular, respiratory, and mediastinal structures, which may introduce additional cognitive demands for language models. This interpretation is further supported by recent anatomy-focused work showing that model performance also varies in cadaver-based educational settings. In a structured evaluation of LLM-generated step-by-step instructions for cadaveric brachial plexus dissection, ChatGPT-4o and Grok outperformed Gemini and DeepSeek in scientific accuracy and guidance quality, whereas DeepSeek showed higher readability but lower content depth [56]. These findings align with the present results in suggesting that stronger model performance in anatomy-related tasks may depend not only on factual recall but also on the ability to provide structured, context-sensitive, and educationally useful explanations.

Therefore, differences between the present findings and those reported in earlier studies may reflect variation not only in model architecture but also in assessment format, domain complexity, and evaluation methodology.

The performance differences observed across models likely reflect variation in how large language models process biomedical knowledge and generate explanations in clinically contextualized tasks. In the present analysis, Grok achieved the highest median score, whereas the remaining models produced lower and relatively similar results. One possible explanation for this difference relates to variation in training data composition and model alignment strategies. Models trained on larger quantities of biomedical literature or medically structured educational materials may be better able to connect anatomical relationships with clinically meaningful mechanisms when interpreting case-based questions [1, 57, 58]. One plausible explanation may relate to differences in exposure to biomedical or medically structured training material, although this could not be examined directly in the present study.

Another important factor influencing model performance appears to be explanatory depth. Recent studies in both dental and anatomy education indicate that high answer accuracy does not necessarily correspond to equally strong explanatory completeness. For instance, in pediatric dentistry, responses generated by ChatGPT-4o were frequently judged to be highly accurate but showed variability in completeness across topics, suggesting that apparently correct answers may still contain uneven explanatory coverage [53]. Similarly, in cadaveric brachial plexus dissection tasks, higher-performing models were distinguished not merely by correctness but by their ability to provide clearer step-by-step instructional structure, whereas models producing more readable responses sometimes lacked sufficient technical depth [56]. A comparable pattern was observed in the present study. In several responses generated by lower-scoring models, the relevant anatomical structure was correctly identified but the mechanistic explanation linking that structure to the clinical scenario remained incomplete or insufficiently elaborated. By contrast, the highest-performing model more consistently produced responses that explicitly integrated anatomical relationships with clinical interpretation.

Similar observations have been reported in previous studies evaluating LLM performance in anatomy and medical education, where models were able to identify correct answers but occasionally failed to provide detailed mechanistic reasoning or fully developed explanatory narratives, particularly in tasks requiring multistep interpretation of anatomical relationships and clinical context [8, 21, 26, 40].

The format of the evaluation may also have contributed to the observed variability. Open-ended scenario-based questions require contextual interpretation, integration of anatomical knowledge, and multistep reasoning, making them inherently more demanding than structured knowledge questions [20, 32]. In addition, the adversarial prompt design used in this study offered insights into contextual coherence. While ChatGPT-4o and Grok recognized that some questions were inconsistent with the presented clinical scenario, DeepSeek and Gemini generated answers without acknowledging this mismatch. This observation suggests that differences between models may reflect not only factual accuracy but also the ability to maintain contextual consistency during case-based reasoning. Previous research indicates that evaluating LLMs solely on final answer accuracy may overlook important aspects of reasoning quality, including contextual interpretation and multistep clinical reasoning processes in complex medical scenarios [59, 60].

The findings of this study have several implications for the integration of LLMs into anatomy education. Despite the variability in model performance, large language models may still offer educational value when used as supplementary tools within structured learning environments. Previous research suggests that AI-based systems can support medical education by facilitating knowledge retrieval, generating explanatory content, and enabling self-directed learning activities [22, 23].

One potential application of large language models in anatomy education involves pre-laboratory preparation. Emerging evidence suggests that LLMs may function as supportive tools in both preclinical and procedural learning environments. For example, research by Temizsoy Korkmaz and colleagues [56] examining LLM-generated step-by-step guidance for cadaveric brachial plexus dissection demonstrated that such systems can provide scalable instructional support, although they cannot replace direct supervision and mentorship from experienced instructors. Similarly, Sezer and Okutan [53] reported that LLM-generated responses in pediatric dentistry can support both patient-facing explanations and curricular learning, while also emphasizing the need for cautious interpretation in technically sensitive topics.

Within the context of anatomy education, these capabilities may be particularly useful prior to cadaveric dissection or anatomy laboratory sessions. Students may interact with LLM-based systems to review relevant anatomical structures, explore clinical correlations, and clarify conceptual relationships before entering the laboratory environment. Such preparatory engagement may help learners develop an initial conceptual framework, which could facilitate deeper understanding and spatial integration of anatomical relationships during subsequent hands-on learning experiences.

LLMs may also function as interactive revision tools. Students can use these systems to generate practice questions, request explanations of anatomical mechanisms, or explore clinical correlations related to specific anatomical regions. This form of interactive dialogue may promote active learning and help learners identify gaps in their understanding more efficiently than passive study strategies [37].

Another potential use involves AI-assisted tutoring. Unlike static educational materials, LLM-based systems allow learners to ask follow-up questions and iteratively explore complex concepts. This capability may support deeper understanding of how anatomical structures relate to physiological function and clinical presentation [19, 21]. However, the educational use of LLMs must be approached cautiously. Previous studies have shown that AI-generated explanations may contain inaccuracies, incomplete reasoning, or confidently presented misinformation, particularly in complex clinical contexts [5, 6, 61]. For this reason, LLMs should be viewed as complementary learning resources rather than substitutes for instructor-guided teaching or cadaver-based anatomy education. In educational practice, higher-performing models may provide more structured explanations, whereas lower-quality outputs may risk reinforcing incomplete anatomical reasoning if used without appropriate supervision.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the analysis was limited to seven thoracic clinical scenarios and 20 open-ended questions, which may not fully represent the diversity of anatomical reasoning tasks encountered in medical education [19, 37]. Second, the evaluation relied on expert assessment using a structured scoring rubric. Although high inter-rater agreement was achieved, rubric-based scoring inevitably involves a degree of subjective interpretation.

Third, LLM responses are generated probabilistically, meaning that outputs may vary across interactions even when identical prompts are used. In this study, a single response was generated for each question to approximate typical real-world usage conditions. However, this approach may not capture the full range of responses that the models could produce. Finally, because the study focused specifically on thoracic anatomy scenarios, the generalizability of the findings to other anatomical regions or clinical disciplines may be limited [22, 37].

Future research should expand the evaluation of LLMs in anatomy education by incorporating larger datasets, broader anatomical domains, and more diverse clinical scenarios. Comparative studies involving areas such as neuroanatomy, musculoskeletal anatomy, and head-and-neck anatomy may help clarify model performance across different educational contexts [19, 37].

In addition, the influence of prompting strategies warrants further investigation. Techniques such as role-based prompting or chain-of-thought prompting have been shown to improve the explanatory quality of LLM responses in medical contexts and may influence performance in anatomy-related reasoning tasks [5, 62–64].

Another promising direction involves integrating LLMs with multimodal digital learning technologies, including three-dimensional anatomical models, virtual dissection platforms, and radiological imaging datasets [22, 23]. This direction appears particularly relevant given recent findings that multimodal models show promise in answering complex dental inquiries involving text, images, and analytical data, while still demonstrating difficulty in visual interpretation and scenario analysis [55]. Similarly, benchmarking studies in medically compromised dental cases suggest that future evaluations should move beyond simple factual recall and focus more explicitly on error patterns, reasoning depth, and safety in complex case-based contexts [65]. Such integrated environments may strengthen both conceptual and spatial understanding of anatomical relationships.

Finally, the rapid development of LLMs raises broader ethical and professional considerations for medical education. While AI-assisted tools may facilitate access to knowledge and support independent learning, concerns remain regarding reliability, accountability, and the potential for confidently presented inaccuracies. Responsible integration of AI into medical education therefore requires transparent benchmarking, expert oversight, and pedagogical strategies that encourage critical evaluation of AI-generated information rather than uncritical acceptance [16, 66, 67]. Beyond educational use, broader healthcare literature also emphasizes that AI integration in clinical environments requires attention to privacy, accountability, data accuracy, and the risk of inappropriate reliance on model outputs in high-stakes settings [68].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation was based on a relatively limited dataset consisting of seven thoracic anatomy scenarios and 20 open-ended questions derived from a widely used anatomy textbook. Although thoracic anatomy offers clinically meaningful examples involving the bronchi, pleura, mediastinum, and cardiovascular relationships, this question set may not fully capture the breadth of anatomical knowledge or the diversity of clinically oriented reasoning tasks encountered in medical education. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory, and future studies including larger datasets, additional anatomical regions, and more varied clinical scenarios may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of LLM performance in anatomy education.

Second, the scoring process relied on expert evaluation of open-ended responses using a structured rubric. Although inter-rater reliability was high and all responses were anonymized before scoring, rubric-based assessment inevitably involves some degree of subjective interpretation. Scoring outcomes may vary according to evaluator background, expectations, or the structure of the scoring framework. In addition, because the same group of anatomists contributed to both question development and response evaluation, the possibility of subjective bias cannot be fully excluded.

Third, LLM outputs are probabilistic and may vary across repeated interactions even when identical prompts are used. Although all queries were submitted on the same day to reduce the potential influence of model updates, stochastic variation may still have affected individual responses. Moreover, because these systems are continuously updated, the findings reflect model behavior at the time of analysis and may not fully represent current or future performance.

Finally, the study evaluated baseline responses to scenario-based questions without applying prompt engineering strategies such as role-based prompting or chain-of-thought prompting. This approach was intentionally chosen to better reflect realistic non-expert use; however, it may underestimate the maximum performance achievable under optimized prompting conditions. In addition, the study did not examine downstream educational outcomes, learner performance, or clinical decision-making. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence supporting the autonomous use of LLMs in clinically oriented anatomical reasoning.

Conclusion

In summary, LLMs show promising potential as supplementary tools for clinical anatomy education, particularly in supporting scenario-based reasoning. In this study, the evaluated models demonstrated varying levels of accuracy and clinical contextualization, with Grok outperforming the others. However, consistent with prior research, their limitations in spatial understanding, complex structural relationships, and higher-order reasoning underscore the need for cautious and supervised use. LLMs should therefore be integrated as complementary resources—augmenting rather than replacing traditional instructional methods—while ensuring rigorous validation, expert oversight, and responsible implementation. Continued standardized benchmarking in clinically oriented settings will be important to support safe, transparent integration into biomedical education and related decision-support contexts.
