Pharmacy-Based Atrial Fibrillation Screening Model Coordinated by a Regional Stroke and Cardiovascular Disease Support Center
Authors: Toshiaki Sakamoto, Yohei Tateishi, Masaya Kurobe, Hiroki Yahata, Mana Miyakoda, Yukinobu Kodama, Akira Tsujino, Koji Maemura, Kaname Ohyama, Satoshi Ikeda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a major cause of cardioembolic stroke and often remains undiagnosed because of its asymptomatic or paroxysmal nature. Although opportunistic AF screening has been advocated, sustainable community-based screening models that link the results to medical evaluations are limited. We implemented a pharmacy-based AF screening initiative (Catch AF project) led by a regional Stroke and Cardiovascular Disease Support Center with community pharmacists and physicians to assess its feasibility and follow-up outcomes.Methods and ResultsThe Catch AF project was conducted at 34 community pharmacies in Nagasaki, Japan. A single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) device with automated analysis was installed to offer opportunistic screening for pharmacy visitors. Of the 5,371 ECG recordings, 123 (2.29%) indicated possible AF. Pharmacists recommended medical consultation for 60 individuals (49% of the detected cases). Questionnaire surveys demonstrated that stakeholders generally perceived the initiative as acceptable and feasible. In contrast, only a small number of physicians reported encounters with referred patients, indicating a marked gap between AF detection through pharmacy-based screening and subsequent medical evaluation.ConclusionsA center-led pharmacy-based AF screening initiative is feasible and well accepted in a real-world setting. However, a substantial gap between screening detection and clinical follow up highlights the need for improved referral pathways and coordination between pharmacies and medical institutions to enhance the effectiveness of community-based screening programs.

Central Figure

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a major cause of cardioembolic stroke and is associated with severe neurological deficits, long-term disability, and increased mortality.1,2 AF is frequently observed in older adults and constitutes a significant concern in Japan, where the demographic trend indicates a continued increase in the aging population.3–5 However, awareness of AF among the general public remains limited.6,7 As a result, AF is associated with higher care utilization and cost, leading to issues from a socioeconomic perspective.8 Because AF is frequently asymptomatic or paroxysmal, many patients remain undiagnosed until they present with stroke or heart failure.9 Conventional diagnostic modalities, including 12-lead electrocardiography and Holter monitoring, have limitations in detecting paroxysmal AF, particularly in asymptomatic individuals. Recently, opportunistic and systematic AF screening strategies have attracted increasing attention.10–14

Community pharmacies represent a promising setting for opportunistic screening for AF. Pharmacies are easily accessible and frequently visited by older adults and patients with cardiovascular comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure, which are closely associated with AF and stroke risk. Several pharmacy-based AF screening initiatives using simplified electrocardiographic devices have been reported.15–17 However, many of these initiatives have been limited by short implementation periods, an operational burden on pharmacists, and insufficient linkages between screening results and subsequent medical evaluations. Importantly, pharmacists lack diagnostic authority, and pharmacy-based screening programs operated in isolation may fail to translate detected abnormalities into appropriate clinical management without structured collaboration with physicians.

To address these challenges, the Stroke and Cardiovascular Disease Support Center affiliated with Nagasaki University Hospital launched the Catch AF project, a center-led pharmacy-based AF awareness and screening initiative, implemented through regional collaboration between community pharmacists and physicians. The center coordinated participating pharmacies, provided standardized training and educational materials, and established referral pathways to facilitate the timely medical evaluation of individuals flagged with possible AF. This structured, region-wide approach was designed to complement existing AF screening strategies by embedding opportunistic detection within routine healthcare interactions while maintaining close integration with the regional cardiovascular care network.

This study aimed to evaluate the awareness of AF, as well as the feasibility, acceptability, challenges, and outcomes of the Catch AF project, through the analysis of screening data collected over 18 months and questionnaire-based assessments of pharmacists, patients, and physicians. Within the framework of regional cardiovascular disease prevention, this study explored how a center-led, pharmacy-based model can contribute to the targeted detection and referral of AF.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This real-world observational implementation study was conducted in a community setting. The Catch AF project was led by the Stroke and Cardiovascular Disease Support Center of Nagasaki University Hospital and implemented in collaboration with community pharmacists and regional medical associations in Nagasaki City, Japan. The study period was from May 2024 to October 2025.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nagasaki University Hospital (approval no. 25032710-2). Participation in the questionnaire surveys was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained at the beginning of each survey. All data were collected and analyzed anonymously.

Catch AF Project: Pharmacy-Based AF Awareness and Screening

The Catch AF project was implemented as a pharmacy-based AF awareness and opportunistic screening program. Community pharmacies were recruited through the local pharmacists’ association, and 34 pharmacies located in Nagasaki City participated in this study. The participating pharmacies represented approximately 11% of all community pharmacies in Nagasaki City. Among the participants, 92% corresponded to a standard reimbursement category for community pharmacies, and the most frequently reported range for prescription receipts was between 1,000 and 2,000 per month, suggesting that the participating pharmacies largely reflected typical community pharmacy settings. The main prescribing medical institutions for these pharmacies were 5 cardiology clinics/hospitals, 2 general hospitals, and 19 non-cardiology clinics.

A schematic overview of the Catch AF project is presented in Figure 1. A finger-contact single-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) device integrated into an automated blood pressure monitor (HCR-7800T; Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd, Kyoto, Japan) was installed in each participating pharmacy. The device was connected to a smartphone (iPhone; Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA) via Bluetooth. ECG recordings were automatically analyzed using an artificial intelligence-based algorithm and classified as normal sinus rhythm, tachycardia, bradycardia, possible AF, or unclassifiable rhythm. The high accuracy of the HCR-7800 T® series for detecting AF has been reported, with a sensitivity of 96–98% and a specificity of 86–96%.18–20 Pharmacy visitors were offered opportunistic ECG screening during their waiting time without prior appointments. Pharmacists explained the purpose of AF screening and the measurement procedure using standardized educational materials developed by the center. When the device indicated possible AF, pharmacists recommended that patients consult with their primary care physicians. Regional medical institutions were informed of the project through local medical associations, and consultation with the center was recommended when specialist evaluation was considered necessary. Data Sources and Screening Outcomes

The total number of ECG measurements and recordings classified as indicating possible AF were extracted from the application logs stored on smartphones. No participant-level demographic or clinical information, including age, sex, comorbidities, or medication use, was collected during the ECG measurements.

Questionnaire Surveys

Separate anonymous questionnaires were developed for pharmacists, patients, and physicians based on previous literature and expert consensus.21 The pharmacist questionnaire assessed the project structure, device usability, workload, patient guidance, perceived added value, and overall satisfaction. The patient questionnaire evaluated awareness of pharmacy-based ECG screening, device usability, and knowledge of AF. The physician questionnaire assessed awareness of the Catch AF project, perceived usefulness, and feasibility of responding to pharmacy-based referrals.

All questionnaire surveys were conducted over a 1-month period from June 1 to June 30, 2025. Follow-up information regarding subsequent medical consultations after possible AF notifications was obtained through questionnaire responses from patients and physicians rather than through systematic clinical follow up.

Statistical Analysis

Customer satisfaction (CS) analysis was conducted to evaluate pharmacists’ perceptions of the Catch AF project and to identify its strengths and the areas requiring improvement. This analysis was based on an importance–performance analysis framework, which has been widely used in healthcare service evaluation to assess the perceived importance and performance of individual service components and to prioritize targets for improvement.22 By mapping satisfaction scores and importance scores in a 2-dimensional space, this method enables the identification of items requiring improvement, particularly those with high importance but low satisfaction.

In this framework, satisfaction was defined as the mean score for each questionnaire item on a 6-point Likert scale, representing perceived performance. Importance was defined as the Pearson correlation coefficient between each item score and the overall satisfaction score, representing the perceived contribution of each item to the overall evaluation. Negative correlation coefficients were set to zero for prioritization.

Both the satisfaction and importance scores were standardized to deviation scores (mean 50; standard deviation 10) for visualization, and the items were plotted on a 2-dimensional matrix to classify them into 4 quadrants according to their relative importance and performance. This analysis was conducted for descriptive and exploratory purposes to inform future program improvements and was not intended for hypothesis testing.

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Descriptive analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Results

Screening Outcomes: Number of ECG Measurements and Possible AF Detection

During the 18-month study period, 5,371 ECG measurements were performed at the participating community pharmacies. Among the top 10 pharmacies with the highest number of ECG measurements (i.e., >200 measurements), 8 reported cardiology or neurosurgery clinics as their primary prescribing institutions.

Of these measurements, 123 (2.29%) recordings were classified by the device algorithm as indicating possible AF. Notably, patients with known AF were not excluded at the time of measurement. According to reports from pharmacists, 30 (0.56%) cases involved patients with known AF.

Of the individuals with recordings classified as having possible AF, pharmacists recommended medical consultation for 60 patients, which corresponded to 49% of the detected cases.

The monthly number of ECG measurements and recordings indicating possible AF throughout the study period is shown in Figure 2. The measurements were sustained throughout the study period, although higher measurement numbers were observed during the initial phase following device installation. Questionnaires were returned by 25 of the 34 participating pharmacies, yielding a response rate of 73.5%.

Pharmacists reported a high level of overall satisfaction with the Catch AF project. The mean overall satisfaction score was 4.64±0.89 on a 6-point Likert scale.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the feasibility and acceptability of the Catch AF project, a pharmacy-based AF awareness and screening initiative led by a Stroke and Cardiovascular Disease Support Center and implemented through regional collaboration among pharmacists and physicians. Over an 18-month period, a large number of ECG measurements were performed in community pharmacies, and 2.29% of recordings were classified as indicating possible AF. Questionnaire-based evaluations generally demonstrated favorable perceptions by pharmacists, patients, and physicians. In contrast, the CS analysis allowed the identification of operational challenges perceived by pharmacists, including the workload associated with patient explanation and reporting procedures. Importantly, this study should be interpreted primarily as an implementation study rather than a diagnostic accuracy study, providing insights into the real-world processes and challenges involved in integrating pharmacy-based AF screening into regional healthcare systems.

AF is often asymptomatic and may remain undetected until serious cerebrovascular complications occur.10 Furthermore, it has been reported that early rhythm control in patients with asymptomatic AF also improves outcomes such as cardiovascular death, stroke, heart failure, or hospitalization for worsening acute coronary syndrome.23,24 Therefore, opportunistic and systematic AF screening strategies have been advocated to facilitate early detection and timely initiation of preventive therapies.25,26 Several screening approaches have been explored, including health check-ups and wearable or mobile ECG devices. The prevalence of irregular rhythm or suspected/possible AF detected through large-scale screening using commercially available wearable devices was reported to be 0.07–1.1%.27–30 The prevalence observed in this study was higher than that previously reported. These strategies often target relatively health-conscious individuals, potentially limiting screening efficiency in populations at higher cardiovascular risk.31 A recent large-scale electrocardiographic screening study conducted in health check-up settings demonstrated that screening-detected AF is associated with an increased relative risk of stroke and heart failure, even among working-age adults with a low baseline thromboembolic risk. The study has also shown that the absolute incidence of AF and subsequent cardiovascular events in such populations remains low, raising concerns regarding the efficiency and resource allocation of universal screening approaches in low-risk groups.32 In contrast, this study was conducted at a community pharmacy visited by many patients who are elderly and have cardiovascular comorbidities associated with AF and stroke risk.33,34 Our findings indicate that pharmacy-based screening can reach individuals who may not routinely undergo cardiovascular evaluations, thereby complementing existing screening modalities. Moreover, pharmacists often have longitudinal relationships with patients through routine medication management, allowing them to provide tailored explanations regarding AF, stroke risk, and the need for medical consultation. The questionnaire-based follow up suggested that medical consultation occurred in some patients after a ‘possible AF’ notification, supporting the role of pharmacists as facilitators of access to medical care.

A notable finding of the present study was the gap observed between the detection of possible AF and subsequent medical evaluation. This detection–referral gap represents an important implementation barrier that should be addressed to improve the clinical effectiveness of pharmacy-based screening initiatives.

Pharmacists recommended medical consultation for approximately half (49%) of the detected cases. The referral rate by pharmacists in this study is comparable with or higher than that in community pharmacy-led AF screening in the United Kingdom (approximately 33%)35 and screening studies in other fields (20–58%).36,37 Given the conditions of real-world clinical practice, pharmacy-led initiatives, and large-scale implementation, this rate may be considerable.

Several factors have been identified as barriers to these referrals. These include healthcare professionals being preoccupied with their responsibilities, which limits their ability to allocate adequate time for intervention or guidance, the subjective judgment of medical staff regarding ‘who should be referred’, and the patients’ own perception that they do not require a referral.38

Indeed, the CS analysis in this study showed that the operational burden reported by pharmacists was identified as a priority area for improvement. Although overall satisfaction with the Catch AF project was high, with particularly strong ratings in areas such as project structure, communication, and contributions to the community and society, pharmacists’ workload was identified as an issue that requires improvement. The sustained implementation of such initiatives may require structural and organizational support. From a health system perspective, pharmacy-based screening programs may benefit from policy-level considerations, including reimbursement mechanisms or institutional support, to ensure long-term sustainability without imposing an excessive workload on pharmacy staff.39

Physician surveys indicated that only a limited number of physicians reported encountering patients referred after pharmacy-based screening. Interestingly, a substantial proportion of participants had prior experience with ECG examinations, and many were at least aware of AF, indicating that the study population was not entirely naïve to cardiovascular screening. Despite this background, the rate of subsequent medical consultations following pharmacist recommendations remained limited. From the physician perspective, although the majority of respondents were from clinics without cardiologists, most physicians reported that they would be able to manage patients referred through pharmacy-based screening and perceived the initiative as clinically meaningful. However, several barriers were identified, including limitations in specialty expertise, diagnostic resources, and time constraints. We did not investigate the background factors of patients who did not seek medical consultation; therefore, the reasons for this remain unclear. However, several factors may contribute to this gap. First, many individuals who undergo opportunistic screening in pharmacies are asymptomatic, which may reduce their perceived urgency to seek medical consultation. Second, patients typically visit community pharmacies as part of the prescription-dispensing process following physician consultations. For clinically stable patients, the interval between scheduled physician visits may extend to several months. Consequently, even when possible AF is detected during pharmacy-based screening, there may be a delay before the patient undergoes further evaluation at a medical institution. Third, ECG recordings obtained in pharmacies were stored within the device application and were not directly accessible to physicians, which may have limited the ability of physicians to review the initial screening findings. These considerations indicate that the gap between detection and clinical care cannot be explained solely by patient-related factors or physician capacity but instead reflects a structural barrier in the implementation of community-based AF screening.

The temporal trend in ECG measurements suggests that screening activity remained relatively sustained throughout the 18-month implementation period, although the number of measurements was higher during the initial phase of the project. This pattern may reflect an initial novelty effect associated with device installation and project launch, followed by stabilization as the program became integrated into routine pharmacy practice. Despite this stabilization, continued participation of pharmacies indicates that pharmacy-based AF screening can be maintained over time within existing community healthcare networks.

Education and capacity building are critical for the sustainable pharmacy-based screening of AF. Structured educational programs focusing on AF and ECG interpretation have been implemented, including the program developed by the University of New Mexico, which emphasizes pharmacist-led AF screening in community settings.15,16 Our institution has collaborated with the University of New Mexico to implement this educational program for several years, and pharmacists who have completed this training have also participated in the Catch AF project. Such educational initiatives may enhance pharmacists’ competence and confidence, facilitating meaningful engagement in AF screening and patient counseling. The Catch AF project provided an opportunity to integrate education with real-world implementation, reinforcing the role of pharmacists as active contributors to cardiovascular disease prevention.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. Participant-level demographic and clinical information, such as age, sex, and comorbidities, were not collected because the program was implemented as an awareness-based community screening initiative. Therefore, detailed analyses of participant characteristics and their association with AF detection were not possible. ECG measurements were recorded as the total number of measurements rather than the number of specific individuals; therefore, repeated measurements by the same individual may have been included. However, increasing the opportunities for measurement also increases the chances of detecting AF. This observational implementation study was conducted without a control group, precluding the assessment of the clinical effectiveness of pharmacy-based AF screening in reducing stroke and cardiovascular outcomes. AF detection relied on a single-lead ECG device with automated analysis, and recordings classified as ‘possible AF’ did not constitute a definitive diagnosis. In addition, follow up for diagnostic confirmation and treatment initiation was not systematically performed. We are currently planning additional initiatives aimed at strengthening follow-up processes and addressing the limitations of single-lead ECG-based screening. Questionnaire-based self-reported information from patients and physicians may be subject to response and recall bias.40 The moderate physician response rate and single-region setting may also limit the generalizability of the results.

Conclusions

The Catch AF project demonstrated that a center-led, pharmacy-based AF screening program can be successfully implemented through regional coordination and multidisciplinary collaboration. This model has the potential to enhance AF awareness and facilitate targeted early detection by leveraging the accessibility of community pharmacies and the expertise of pharmacists. However, our findings highlight important implementation challenges, particularly the gap between screening and subsequent medical evaluations. With further refinement of referral pathways, follow-up systems, and educational support, such initiatives may serve as a practical model for the sustainable social implementation of community-based cardiovascular disease prevention programs.
