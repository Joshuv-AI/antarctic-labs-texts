Exercise trajectories and current exercise and risk of breast cancer: a cohort study of 325,953 women who participated in BreastScreen Norway
Authors: Trude E Robsahm, Nathalie C Støer, Kari Bø, Solveig Hofvind
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of the study was to elucidate life-course and current exercise levels among women aged 50–69 who participated in BreastScreen Norway during the period from 2006 to 2015, and to estimate the risk of breast cancer based on exercise trajectory classes and current exercise level.MethodsSelf-reported data from 325,953 women were utilized for descriptive and exploratory analyses. Retrospective information about exercise levels at ages 15–19, 20–29, 30–39, and 40–49 was used to establish exercise trajectory classes, while reported exercise at ages 50–69 was considered as current activity. Hazard Ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models adjusting for age, educational level and body mass index (BMI).ResultsAdjusted HR of breast cancer was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.87–0.98) for women in the stable high class and 0.94 (95% CI: 0.89–0.99) for those in the increasing trajectory class compared to the stable low class. The HRs was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.82–0.97) for 4.5 h current exercise per week and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.62–0.84) for ≥ 6 h, compared to those reporting no current exercise. Statistically significant results were observed for asymptomatic (screen-detected) and symptomatic cancers. Stratification by BMI and smoking status did not reveal substantial differences in the risk estimates.ConclusionEngaging in high-level exercise throughout one's lifetime significantly reduces the risk of breast cancer. This study highlights the importance of maintaining or increasing physical activity levels throughout life to mitigate breast cancer risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10552-026-02184-7.Keywords: Exercise, Breast neoplasm, Mass screening, Postmenopause, Life-course physical activity, Physical activity trajectories

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common cancer and the second most common cause of cancer death in women globally [1]. The World Health Organization has estimated that about 40% of all cancer cases can be related to modifiable lifestyle factors and thus prevented [2]. Scientific evidence links reproductive and hormonal factors, including reproductive history and use of menopausal hormone therapy, as well as modifiable lifestyle factors such as obesity, alcohol consumption, and tobacco smoking, to an increased risk of breast cancer [2–4]. Reproductive history is considered a key reproductive and hormonal determinant of breast cancer risk, whereas lifestyle-related factors represent potentially modifiable exposures. Furthermore, being physically active is shown to reduce the risk of the disease, although the beneficial effects may vary by age, type, intensity, frequence, and duration of the activity [2]. All physical activity, including vigorous, occupational, and recreational light activity has been shown to decrease the risk of breast cancer for postmenopausal women while vigorous activity seems to be required to reduce the risk in premenopausal women [2, 5, 6].

Physical activity of different type, intensity, frequency and duration can modify physiological mechanisms involved in breast cancer development through preventing weight gain and thereby counteract adverse alteration in hormone and growth factor profiles, and through prevention of chronic low-grade inflammations [7, 8]. Furthermore, physical activity has immunomodulatory effects resulting in strengthened immune responses that can enhance breast cancer immunosurveillance [9]. Studies have shown a dose–response relationship between physical activity and risk of breast cancer [10]. However, most of these studies are based on physical activity measured at one timepoint or data collapsed in categories not reflecting changes in activity during lifetime [10]. For most people, the type, intensity, frequence, and duration of physical activity changes throughout life. Life-course data on physical activity enables identification of patterns and estimation of risk outcomes related to changes over time. In recent years, several studies of health behaviors and outcomes have applied a life-course trajectory approach [11–14]. The study by Saint-Maurice et al. showed that maintaining life-long physical activity and increasing the activity level were associated with reduced all-cause mortality compared to those consistently inactive [12]. The findings emphasize the importance of capturing changes over time in studies of lifestyle and health outcomes.

Although several studies have examined the relationship between lifetime physical activity and risk of postmenopausal breast cancer [13, 14], to our knowledge no studies have explored this relation applying life-course trajectories. We took advantage of life-course information on physical activity among women who participated in BreastScreen Norway 2006‒2015 to (i) describe life-course and current physical activity level and (ii) estimate the associations between trajectory classes and current activity level and the risk of breast cancer among women aged 50 and older, according to BMI, smoking status, histopathological tumor characteristics and detection mode.

Methods

The study has legal basis in accordance with Articles 6 (1) (e) and 9 (2) (j) of the GDPR and was approved by the Regional Committee for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REC #510838). The data were disclosed on a legal basis in the Health Register Act, Sects. 19 e and 19 [15] and "Regulation on national solutions for accessibility of health data" [16].

BreastScreen Norway and the participants in the study

BreastScreen Norway is a nation-wide population-based screening program for breast cancer, inviting women aged 50–69 years to mammographic screening biennially. The program started in 1996 and targeted about 680,000 women in 2025. All individuals residing in Norway are assigned a unique personal identification number, which enables precise identification of women eligible for the screening program and supports the principles of equity, diversity, and inclusion by ensuring comprehensive and unbiased population coverage. The attendance rate for each screening round is about 76% while 86% attend at least once during a ten-year period [17].

Study population

The cohort of women included in this study is described in detail elsewhere [18, 19]. In short, a total of 759,168 women aged 50–69 were invited to BreastScreen Norway during the period from August 2006 to December 2015, whereas 398,634 (53%) attended and handed in the questionnaires (Supplementary Fig. 1). After exclusions, information given by 325,953 women free from breast cancer at responding to the questionnaire constituted the study sample used in exercise trajectories while information from 315,699 women constituted the study sample for current exercise (Supplementary Fig. 1).

The questionnaires

Two forms were used to collect information; Form I captured retrospectively, covering ages 15 to 49, whereas Form II collected comparable information contemporaneously at the time of completion (Supplement A). Each woman received Form I only once, while Form II was administered at every screening invitation during the data collection period. If the women submitted more than one Form II, we used the most recent one.

Vigorous physical activity, hereafter referred to as exercise, was retrospectively reported for the ages 15‒19, 20‒29, 30‒39 and 40‒49 years, and used for trajectory classification. Exercise was defined as structured intentional activities of at least 30 min duration with high intensity, such as aerobic, running and biking [20]. Frequency and duration of exercise were reported according to hours per week (no activity, 0‒1, 2‒3, 4‒5, and ≥ 6). Reporting of current exercise, for the age span 50–69 years (middle age), was based on the same definition.

Body weight and height were used to calculate BMI (kg/m2) categorized as underweight (< 18.5), normal (≥ 18.5 and < 25), overweight (≥ 25 and < 30), obesity (≥ 30) and unknown [4]. Educational level was divided into ≤ primary school or high school, university or college for 1‒4 years, college > 4 years or university, and unknown. Menopausal status was divided into premenopausal, postmenopausal and unknown, based on the women’s response on the questionnaire. Alcohol consumption (in units (glasses) per week) was categorized as no/low (< 5), moderate (5‒10), high (> 10) and unknown, while tobacco smoking was categorized as never, current, former and unknown. Other variables describing the study cohort were year at recruitment (2006‒2010 and 2011‒2015), age at recruitment, birth country (Norway, Europe, other and unknown), family history of breast cancer (yes, no, and unknown), prior benign breast biopsy (yes, no), age at menarche, age at first child birth (13‒19, 20‒29, 30‒39, and 40‒49 years, and null-parous or unknown), parity (0,1‒2, 3‒4, ≥ 5 children, and unknown), breastfeeding (never, ever, and unknown), years of contraceptive use (0, 1‒4, 5‒9, ≥ 10 and unknown), and ever use of hormonal replacement therapy (yes, no, and unknown). Data from the questionnaire were extracted from the database, March 2024.

Breast cancer was defined as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) or invasive breast cancer. Information about the date of breast cancer diagnosis, detection mode and immunohistochemical subtypes (Luminal A, Luminal B Her2-, Luminal B Her2 +, Her2 + and Triple Negative) [21] was obtained from the Cancer Registry of Norway. Vital status (alive, dead, emigrated) with dates were obtained from the Norwegian Population Registry using the personal identification number given to all inhabitants in Norway by birth or immigration. All data were available from the Cancer Registry of Norway. All diagnoses were histologically verified. Detection mode was divided into screen-detected and symptomatic detected. Screen-detected was defined as breast cancer diagnosed within six months after a positive screening examination. Symptomatic breast cancer included interval cancer (breast cancer diagnosed after a negative screening examination or 6 to 24 months after a false positive screening exam) and breast cancer detected outside the screening program among women invited but not attended and breast cancer diagnosed more than two years after the last screening examination.

Statistical analysis

The study population was followed from their first screening after August 2006 until breast cancer diagnosis, emigration, death, or administrative censoring (April 2024), whichever occurred first. Loss to follow-up was negligible, as the Cancer Registry and the Population Registry of Norway are considered nearly complete for solid cancer incidence and vital status [22]. Descriptive results were reported as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and as means with standard deviations (SD), or medians with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables.

To identify classes of individual life-course exercise trajectories, we calculated an average exercise level in each age group by converting the recorded durations into 0 (no), 0.5 (0‒1 h), 2.5 (2‒3 h), 4.5 (4‒5 h) or ≥ 6 (6 + hours). Classes of individual life-course exercise trajectories were then identified using latent class mixed models (LCMMs) [23, 24]. The model assumed that the population consisted of k latent classes (underlying unobserved groups of subjects) that follow class-specific trajectories of exercise. In each class, we modelled the average trajectory using a class-specific mixed model, using median age in each age group as timescale. The model included median age in each age group as a second order polynomial. Observations were allowed to be correlated in time by including a random intercept and the model also included class-specific effects of time (second order polynomial). LCMMs with 2‒5 classes were tested and evaluated using recommended fit-statistics [25, 26], in particular the Bayesian information criterion. Covariates such as education were not included in the latent class mixed model, as doing so would have partly defined the exercise trajectory classes by factors that later function as confounders in the association analyses, complicating the interpretation of the trajectories as representations of exercise patterns over time.

Start of follow-up for the trajectory analyses (baseline) was age at baseline response to the questionnaire. Exercise trajectories were estimated up until baseline and the women contributed with person-years of follow-up from baseline and until her first breast cancer diagnosis, emigration, death, or end of follow-up, whichever occurred first.

The association between trajectory classes and breast cancer risk was estimated with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% Confidence Intervals (CI, robust variances) using Cox proportional hazards models, with age as timescale. We accounted for classification uncertainty by allowing each woman to contribute to each trajectory class, with posterior probabilities of belonging to each class used as weights [27, 28].

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) was used to identify potential confounders in the association between exercise and breast cancer (Supplementary Fig. 2). Age and educational level were considered confounders in the analyses. We adjusted for BMI to estimate the component of exercise effect that is not mediated through BMI. This corresponds to a controlled direct effect and reflects our aim to isolate BMI‑independent pathways. We assumed that age at first birth, breastfeeding history, prior benign biopsy, parity, and family history of breast cancer are not associated with exercise level, as reflected in the DAG. While an additional causal arrow between education and some of these variables could be considered, this would not affect our adjustment strategy because this pathway is already blocked by conditioning on education. The association between current exercise and breast cancer risk was estimated with HRs and 95% CIs using Cox proportional hazards models, with age as timescale. In this analysis each woman was followed from age at recording current exercise, they contributed only once, and the Cox regression was unweighted.

The associations between trajectory classes and current exercise, and breast cancer risk were stratified by BMI (below or above 25), detection mode (screen-detected, symptomatically), smoking status (current, past, never) and molecular (immunohistochemical) subtypes.

All analyses were performed with R, version 4.2.3 and the lcmm package, version 2.1.0 [24] and the survival package, version 3.5.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Patient involvement statement

Women in the target group were involved in developing the questionnaires, but not in any part of planning or performance of this study.

Cohort characteristics of the women by exercise trajectory classes

Reported level of exercise

More than 50% of the women reported no or low level of exercise during their life span, and about 25‒30% of the women reported 1‒4 h/week of exercise at all age spans (Fig. 1). The women tended to have the highest exercise level (> 4 h/week) at the youngest ages (about 10%), with a decreasing pattern by increasing age. The exercise level did not vary substantially between women who developed and not developed breast cancer.

Average weekly level of exercise for all the women, and by those later diagnosed and not diagnosed with breast cancer, at age 15‒19 (green), 20‒29 (yellow), 30‒39 (grey), 40‒49 (red), and today (blue) reported by women at screening, aged 50‒69

Latent class mix models identified 4 lifetime exercise classes: stable lowest, decreasing, increasing and stable high (Fig. 2). The exercise level during the lifetime varied largely within each of the trajectory classes (Supplementary Fig. 3). Most women were in the stable lowest class (76.8%), with an average of about one hour exercise per week. Least women were in the increasing and stable high classes (6% in each).

Lifetime exercise trajectory classes (stable low (orange), decreasing from high to low (violet), increasing from low to high (green) and stable high (pink) among women aged 50‒69 at retrospectively reporting their exercise level at age 15–49

Women in the highest current exercise category (≥ 6 h per week) tended to have higher educational level, lower BMI, and being premenopausal, compared to women reporting no exercise per week (Supplementary Table 1). The cohorts of women without (n = 63,085) and with (n = 325,953) information about exercise and by trajectory classes is described in Supplementary Table 2. Women who reported on exercise were younger (mean age 56.2 (SD 5.6) versus 59.6 (SD 6.0)), and a higher proportion had university/college level education, were premenopausal, and more years of contraceptive use, compared to women not reporting on exercise.

Exercise and the risk of breast cancer

The HR of breast cancer, from the fully adjusted model (model III), were significantly lower for increasing (0.94, 0.89–0.99) and stable high (0.92, 0.87–0.98) trajectory classes, compared to the stable low class (Table 2A). A lower HRs were seen in women reporting currently 4.5 (0.89, 0.82–0.97) and 7 (0.72, 0.62–0.84) exercise hours per week, compared to those reporting no exercise (Table 2B). Minor differences were observed between statistical models I-III.

For symptomatically detected breast cancer, no significant risk differences were seen between the trajectory classes (Table 4A). For screen-detected breast cancer, significantly lower HRs were seen for the increasing (0.92, 0.86–0.98) and stable high (0.90, 0.84–0.97) trajectory classes compared to the stable low class. Discussion

Based on retrospective self-reported data from more than 300,000 women aged 50–69 who took part in BreastScreen Norway 2006‒2015, four life-course exercise trajectory classes were identified. More than 50% of the women reported no or low exercise levels during their life span. The exercise level decreased by increasing age. We found the stable high and increasing exercise classes to be associated with reduced risk of breast cancer. Current high-intensity exercise, at ages 50‒69, of 4.5 or ≥ 6 h per week, was also associated with reduced risk. The association was observed both for women with screen-detected and symptomatic cancer. Analyses stratified by BMI did not reveal substantial changes of the risk estimates, supporting exercise as an independent factor for prevention of breast cancer.

Several studies have investigated the relationship between lifetime physical activity and risk of breast cancer, and an overall estimate for the most active women was 21% lower risk compared to the least active [5, 6, 29]. However, these studies vary in size of study population, design, and activity measurements. As far as we are aware, our study is the first to collapse exercise data from age 15 to 49 years in exercise trajectories classes for analyzing the association between exercise trajectory and breast cancer risk among women 50 years and older. The individual exercise level varies during the life and by this approach we aimed to capture such variations.

We found decreased HRs for the increasing and stable high exercise trajectory classes, compared to the stable low class. The magnitude of the risk reduction was moderate and similar for both classes. No risk differences were observed between the decreasing and stable low class. Our results are in line with previous studies on lifetime physical activity and menopausal breast cancer [5, 6, 29], although the magnitude of the decrease in our study was lower. Although four classes were identified, the exercise pattern varied largely within the trajectory classes. The small differences in risk factors among women in the different trajectory classes strengthen our idea that contrasts between the trajectory classes might be diluted.

Analyses of current exercise level in middle-aged women showed that high intensity exercise, of 4.5 or ≥ 6 h per week, was consistently associated with reduced risk of breast cancer, in line several previous studies where exercise in recent time and later in life were associated with the greatest reduction in breast cancer risk [6, 29–34], and thus indicate that to start exercising after the age of 50 can have a protective effect, i.e., it is never too late to start exercising. Additionally, our results support that a certain intensity and frequency of exercise is needed to decrease the risk.

BMI may influence exercise level and breast cancer risk but might also affect mammographic image quality and thereby the sensitivity of mammography, particularly in screening where the radiologist has no indication of where a tumor might be located [35–37]. Because BMI mediates both disease risk and screening sensitivity, disentangling its effects is challenging, and observed associations between exercise and breast cancer risk may reflect BMI‑related differences in screening performance, not necessarily effects of exercise.

Studies using accelerometer‑based measures have generally reported associations in the same direction as self-reported measurements [38], although estimates are often attenuated, likely reflecting differences in exposure assessment and the short‑term nature of objective measurements compared with habitual activity captured by self‑report. Breast cancer subtype‑specific analyses suggests that inverse associations are most consistently observed for hormone receptor–positive breast cancer [5, 39], a pattern that is supported by our results.

It is well known that exercise influences the endogenous milieu for cell processes and growth, including insulin-glucose metabolism, sex hormone levels, immune function and gene stability [40, 41]. The insulin-glucose metabolism and hormonal levels are also related to BMI and the preventing mechanism of exercise may act through preventing weight gain [41]. The association found for both exercise trajectories and current exercise was independent of BMI, supporting exercise to prevent breast cancer among women aged 50 and older, beyond the effect of preventing increasing BMI. Our data cannot point out the acting mechanisms but can motivate further basic and clinical studies.

Our study has several limitations. Exercise was self‑reported, which may have led to some misclassification, a limitation that is likely more pronounced for activity reported retrospectively 40–50 years earlier compared with current activity level. In addition, exercise was assessed using an adapted measure, not fully aligned with widely used standardized questionnaires, such as the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) or the WHO Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) which limit direct comparability with other studies. Further, although up to four repeated self‑reported measurements of exercise from adolescence to current age were theoretically available, limited availability of complete information for earlier periods, together with our requirement of reported exercise levels across all predefined categories, reduced the number of observations eligible for trajectory analysis and may have influenced the composition of the analytic sample. Lack of detailed intensity stratification and the absence of information on habitual activities, such as occupational physical activity, may also have limited our ability to fully capture variation in overall activity levels. Potential misclassification due to these weaknesses may have attenuated the observed differences suggesting underestimation of the associations with exercise. Residual confounding may therefore still be present despite adjustment for known breast cancer risk factors. In addition, women who responded to the questionnaire may represent a selected subgroup, introducing possible selection bias. Strengths of the study include the population‑based design, responses from more than 300,000 women, complete registry follow‑up for cancer and vital status and the stratification into symptomatic and asymptomatic breast cancer.

In conclusion, this large population-based study of more than 300,000 women aged 50–69, higher and sustained levels of exercise across the life course were associated with a moderately reduced risk of breast cancer. Current high intensity exercise in midlife showed the strongest associations, suggesting that exercise later in life remains beneficial. The findings were consistent across detection mode (screen detected and symptomatic cancers), and associations persisted after stratification by BMI, supporting a BMI independent protective effect of exercise. Although the study is based on self-reported and non-standardized activity measures with potential for misclassification and selection bias, the study’s size and complete registry follow up strengthen the validity of the results. Our findings reinforce the role of regular exercise as a meaningful preventive strategy against breast cancer.
