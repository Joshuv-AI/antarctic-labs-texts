Natural History of Advanced Primary Hyperoxaluria Type 1: A Retrospective Study
Authors: John C Lieske, Jaap W Groothoff, Yaacov Frishberg, Sander F Garrelfs, Dawn S Milliner, Daniella Magen, Anne-Laure Sellier-Leclerc, Hadas Shasha-Lavsky, Sevcan Bakkaloǧlu, Sandrine Lemoine, Daniel G Fuster, Arnaud Devresse, Ruth Cytter-Kuint, Farshad Kajbaf, Weiming Du, John M Gansner

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveCharacterize the natural history of advanced primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1) in a multinational patient cohort.Study DesignA retrospective chart review.Setting & ParticipantsPatients, from participating medical centers in North America, Europe, and the Middle East, had ≥ 4 health care visits related to PH1 spanning ≥ 6 months (except deceased patients) on/after January 1, 2000, and ≥ 2 estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) values ≤ 45 mL/min/1.73m2 (if age < 12 months, 2 serum creatinine values elevated for age) were included.ExposureNone (retrospective observational study).OutcomesKidney function, liver and/or kidney transplantation, death, plasma oxalate, systemic oxalosis, emergent clinical events, and abnormal clinical laboratory values.Analytical ApproachPatients were categorized as not on dialysis (Cohort A) and receiving hemodialysis (Cohort B). The median age at entry was 11.8 years (Cohort A) and 12.2 years (Cohort B). The eGFR slope was −2.8 mL/min/1.73m2/year (Cohort A). Patients underwent hemodialysis a median of 6 days/week (range, 3-7; Cohort B). Forty-two patients underwent liver and/or kidney transplantation (median age at first transplant, 15.3 years). Primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1) is a rare autosomal recessive disease of hepatic oxalate overproduction, with symptom onset in infancy through adulthood.1,2 In PH1, pathogenic variants in the AGXT gene result in deficient activity of alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase, an enzyme that converts glyoxylate to glycine, resulting in glyoxylate accumulation and increased oxalate production.2 Excess oxalate is excreted by the kidneys, leading to kidney damage that may progress to kidney failure, elevated plasma oxalate (POx) concentration, and systemic oxalosis.2 Advanced PH1 (ie, PH1 that has progressed to chronic kidney disease [CKD] stage 3b-5) is associated with high morbidity and mortality rates, particularly among patients with symptom onset in infancy.3, 4, 5

Systemic oxalosis can involve multiple organs, most commonly in the musculoskeletal, cardiac, vascular, dermatologic, ocular (retina), or nervous system.2,6,7 Skeletal manifestations of PH1 include bone pain and pathological fractures.4,8 Cardiac manifestations of PH1 are related to oxalate deposition in the arteries, conduction system, and myocardium. Symptoms including increased left ventricular mass index, left atrium enlargement, pulmonary hypertension, diastolic dysfunction, and poor ejection fraction are common, and fatal arrhythmias and heart failure may occur.9,10 In patients with PH1, the risk for systemic oxalosis increases as kidney function declines due to the increased POx concentration.11,12

Dialysis, kidney transplantation, and combined liver-kidney transplantation have been used to manage severely compromised kidney function in patients with PH1.2,13 In the absence of RNA interference (RNAi) therapy, even intensive dialysis is unable to remove sufficient oxalate to avoid systemic oxalate deposition.10 If the hepatic oxalate overproduction is not improved, kidney-alone allografts are often lost to oxalate injury.14 Liver transplantation corrects the liver-limited metabolic defect in PH115 and may mitigate or reverse signs and symptoms of skeletal and cardiac oxalosis,4,9 but can carry significant risk.15,16 Lumasiran, an RNAi therapeutic targeting hepatic glycolate oxidase that converts glycolate into the oxalate precursor glyoxylate, is approved in multiple regions, including the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and several countries in the Middle East to treat PH1 in patients of all ages, including those with kidney impairment and kidney failure.17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 Nedosiran, an RNAi therapeutic targeting hepatic lactate dehydrogenase isoform A, which normally converts glyoxylate to oxalate, was approved in the United States in 2023 to lower urinary oxalate levels in patients age 9 years or older with PH1 and relatively preserved kidney function (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] ≥ 30 mL/min/1.73m2).1,25 Access to these treatment options varies greatly across the world.13,16,26

Limited data are available describing PH1 natural history in a multinational cohort of patients with reduced kidney function, either eGFR ≤ 45 mL/min/1.73m2 (CKD stage 3b to 4) or kidney failure (CKD stage 5).10,27 To address this gap, the aims of this study were to (1) characterize the natural history of advanced PH1 in the absence of RNAi therapeutics by retrospective chart review and (2) characterize the natural history of disease progression in patients with PH1 with kidney impairment and elevated POx or systemic oxalosis, or who require hemodialysis.

Methods

Study Design and Procedures

This was a retrospective multinational chart review study that included patients from Belgium, France, Israel, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Türkiye, and the United States. No patient received RNAi therapy or other study drug during the observation period. Data were abstracted from medical records of eligible patients and transcribed into electronic case report forms for analysis at study sites. In addition, available deidentified images and reports were analyzed centrally by an independent radiologist to assess kidney stone events. The follow-up period began on January 1, 2000; diagnosis details, laboratory values, clinical events, and imaging data were collected through August 17, 2022.

The planned enrollment was approximately 80 patients. Sample size was based on feasibility considerations, not statistical power calculations.

Patients

Eligible patients had documented or confirmed PH1 based on pathogenic AGXT changes or reduced hepatic alanine-glyoxylate aminotransferase enzyme activity. Patients ≥ 12 months of age were required to have at least 2 eGFR values ≤ 45 mL/min/1.73m2, which did not need to be consecutive, a minimum of 3 months apart obtained on or after January 1, 2000. Patients < 12 months of age were required to have at least 2 serum creatinine values elevated for age (greater than the locally established upper limit of normal), which did not need to be consecutive, a minimum of 3 months apart obtained on or after January 1, 2000. All patients needed to have at least 4 health care visits related to PH1 (determined by the investigator) on or after January 1, 2000. Visits spanned a minimum of 6 months except in cases in which an eligible patient died before that period passed. In patients who underwent liver or liver-kidney transplantation after January 1, 2000, the 4 health care visits must have occurred before the first transplantation. Patients who underwent liver or liver-kidney transplantation before January 1, 2000, were excluded.

Ethical Conduct

This study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice as defined by the International Council on Harmonisation, the principles defined in the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul, and all applicable national and international laws. Patients in France, the Netherlands, and Switzerland provided written informed consent or, if under the age of legal consent, assent was provided per local and national requirements and legal guardian(s) provided informed consent. Consent was waived in Belgium, Israel, the United States, and Türkiye. Informed consent for deceased patients was obtained per country-specific guidelines as required. A list of institutional ethics committees and institutional review boards is in the Supplementary Material (Item S1).

Outcomes

Outcome variables included kidney function assessed by eGFR over time (in patients not on dialysis); time to first organ transplantation (liver and/or kidney); time to death; POx values (with corresponding percent and absolute changes from baseline to month 6 and beyond); skeletal oxalosis assessed using bone x-ray images that were centrally evaluated and graded using a novel bone oxalosis grading scale28,29 (Fig S1; Item S2); cardiac oxalosis assessed by central and local review; and frequency of clinical events, including all-cause and PH1-related hospitalizations, health care or clinic visits, procedures in addition to hemodialysis, and kidney stone events assessed by symptoms/diagnoses. The number of hemodialysis sessions per week, duration per session, and hemodialysis modifications were captured for patients on renal replacement therapy.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted in the full analysis set, including all patients who met the study’s eligibility criteria, except for POx analyses. Patients were categorized into one or both of 2 cohorts on the earliest date they met applicable serum creatinine/eGFR and/or dialysis criteria: Cohort A (patients with reduced eGFR but not on dialysis or who received hemodialysis for less than 4 weeks consecutively) and Cohort B (patients receiving hemodialysis for at least 4 weeks without peritoneal dialysis). Patients could be in more than one cohort, but not during the same time period. Analyses of POx data were conducted in the POx analysis set, including all patients in the full analysis set who had at least 1 POx measurement on or after the date that the cohort criteria were met and a second POx measurement at least 82 days later, without a censoring event.

Censoring events (ie, events for which all data collected on and after the event date are considered unevaluable within a respective population and cohort unless otherwise specified) included participation in a therapeutic clinical trial, initiation of lumasiran, liver and/or kidney transplantation, initiation of peritoneal dialysis, initiation of hemodialysis for more than 4 consecutive weeks (Cohort A), and cessation of all ongoing hemodialysis for more than 7 days (Cohort B). All data were censored after a censoring event except for deaths and organ transplants, which were censored only when a patient participated in a therapeutic clinical trial or initiated lumasiran.

Continuous data were summarized using descriptive statistics; categorical and ordinal data were summarized using patient count and percentages in each category. Day 1 was the day after the patient met criteria for a respective cohort (A or B) and population (full analysis set or POx analysis set) unless otherwise specified; patients in multiple cohorts and populations may have had multiple day 1 dates for each respective cohort or population. Slopes were calculated with a random-effects-within-between model. Time to first organ transplantation (kidney and/or liver) or death was summarized separately by cohort using Kaplan-Meier estimates; patients without an event were censored at the last known date they were alive.

The eGFR was calculated from serum creatinine based on the Schwartz Bedside Formula30 for patients ≥ 12 months to < 18 years of age and the modification of diet in renal disease formula31 for patients ≥ 18 years of age. A formula for calculating eGFR has not been validated for children < 12 months of age with CKD; therefore, eGFR is not presented for this age range.

Emergent clinical events were defined as clinical events that occurred or worsened on or after day 1. For clinical events with partial start or stop dates, the first day and month were imputed for the start date, and the last day and month were imputed for the stop date. Clinical events for which the year is missing were excluded from the relevant analyses, and clinical events with a completely missing stop date were assumed as ongoing.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Seventy-six patients were entered in the study. Of these, 6 patients did not have a cohort assignment due to persistent peritoneal dialysis (n = 1) or receiving a transplant before cohort eligibility (n = 5). Thus, 70 patients were analyzed (full analysis set) with up to 21 years of follow-up (median [range], 5 [1-21] years). Fifty-four patients met criteria for Cohort A, 53 met criteria for Cohort B, and 37 met criteria for both (not during the same time period). Transplants and Deaths

Forty-two patients underwent liver and/or kidney transplantation at least once (Cohort A [32/54]; Cohort B [36/53]; both cohorts [42/70]). The median (range) age at first transplant was 15.3 (1.8-57.6) years. The majority of patients had a dual or sequential liver-kidney transplant; 2 patients had a kidney transplant; and 3 patients had a liver transplant. Of the 2 patients with a kidney transplant, 1 was in Cohort B just before transplant and 1 had been censored for follow-up by the time of transplant because the patient had initiated lumasiran treatment months earlier. Of the 3 patients with a liver transplant, 1 was in Cohort A just before transplant and 2 were in Cohort B before transplant. The median (range) time from day 1 to first organ transplant was 4.03 (0.0-12.8) years in Cohort A and 1.89 (0.1-10.3 [censored value]) years in Cohort B (Fig 2).

POx and Systemic Oxalosis

POx data were limited for both cohorts (11 patients qualified for the POx analysis set in Cohort A, and 11 in Cohort B; 1 patient qualified for both cohorts). POx values were generally elevated at baseline (mean ± SD, Cohort A: 16.4 ± 11.9 μmol/L; Cohort B: 89.5 ± 51.7 μmol/L) and almost always higher in Cohort B than Cohort A; neither cohort showed evidence of a reduction in average POx over time (slope [95% CI]: −1.52 [−7.62 to 4.58] in Cohort A; −13.22 [−30.19 to 3.75] in Cohort B) (Fig S2). POx values did tend to increase in individual patients in the last measures before censure, as shown in Fig S3, likely reflecting a decline in eGFR.

There was a trend toward worsening in skeletal oxalosis grade before first the organ transplant (liver and/or kidney) and improvement after first organ transplant. Skeletal oxalosis grade improvement after transplant generally took more than 1 year, as exemplified by ratings for left humerus (n = 25) and right humerus (n = 24), regions for which the most data were available among patients with available data (Fig S4). Ratings for an individual patient over time relative to dual liver-kidney transplantation are presented in Fig 4.28 In the year before transplantation, skeletal oxalosis ratings of 3 (on a scale of 0 to 4; higher values represent more advanced oxalosis28) were recorded for both left and right humeri. During the first year following transplantation, skeletal oxalosis worsened, as indicated by ratings of 4 for left and right humeri. Thereafter, ratings became variable over approximately 2 years but declined to 1 at some assessments. Ultimately, ratings of 0 were recorded for left and right humeri and remained stable through the final assessment approximately 11 years posttransplant.

Limited echocardiogram data were available (Cohort A, n = 7; Cohort B, n = 9). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was normal in all patients (both cohorts) except for 1 patient in Cohort A with persistently low LVEF (<50%; range, 35%-45%) and 1 patient in Cohort A with low LVEF at one time point (range, 46%-60%).

The most common emergent clinical events were nephrolithiasis (Cohort A) and fracture (Cohort B) (Table 3). Four events of nephrolithiasis were reported for 3 (6%) patients in Cohort A, and 14 events of fracture were reported for 6 (11%) patients in Cohort B. Exposure-adjusted incidence rates of clinical events requiring hospitalization were higher in Cohort B than Cohort A (71.6 vs 5.9 per 100 patient-years).

Discussion

This multinational study was conceived and initiated to improve understanding of the natural history of PH1 in patients with an eGFR of ≤ 45 mL/min/1.73m2, including patients receiving hemodialysis. The data suggest that such patients do poorly, with declining kidney function, radiological evidence of skeletal oxalosis and frequent bone fractures, and a large number of premature deaths, in some cases despite organ transplantation. Others have reported a similarly stark clinical picture for patients with advanced PH1.4,10,33,34 Of note, patients in this study were not treated with RNAi therapeutics for PH1 during the follow-up period.

The patients with PH1 in Cohort A with reduced eGFR who were not yet on dialysis were at risk for kidney failure within several years. In our study, the requirement of at least 4 health care follow-up visits related to PH1 spanning at least 6 months may have been a source of bias in that it minimized this risk because it may have tended to exclude patients with more severe disease and more rapid progression who hence did not have 4 documented follow-up visits. Ideally, to establish the long-term natural history of PH1, one would follow a cohort of patients initially with good kidney function for many years to determine rates of GFR loss over time.

A similar percentage of patients in each cohort had liver or combined liver-kidney transplants. Patients without transplants were more likely to die than patients with transplants, but systemic oxalosis (which was present in all patients who died) may be the most important factor influencing prognosis, and the disease course may be fatal irrespective of kidney-replacement therapy.

Increases in POx over time were expected but not observed. Neither cohort showed evidence of annual POx reduction (ie, no change was suggested by the 95% CI). However, when Cohort A is viewed by individual patient rather than group average (Fig S3), the last available POx value before censure often trended higher, likely reflecting a decrease in eGFR. The slight decline in average POx in Cohort B, with values generally remaining above 50 μmol/L, is similar to that seen in another natural history study of patients with PH1 on long-term dialysis.10 This persistent elevation of POx levels despite ongoing hemodialysis underscores the need for effective PH1 treatment to reduce continuing oxalate overproduction and excretion. With POx well above the upper limit of calcium oxalate supersaturation, oxalate was likely depositing throughout the body.

There is no easy clinical test for quantifying systemic oxalate deposition. In this study, severity of skeletal oxalosis was evaluated in the subset of patients with available x-rays using a novel, validated grading scale developed for use in PH1 (Item S2).28 The trend toward worsening in skeletal oxalosis grade before the first organ transplant and improvement in grade after the first organ transplant is important because it suggests that deposition of excess oxalate and associated damage may be mitigated by treatments that reduce oxalate overproduction and improve kidney function in PH1. Bone oxalosis improvement after liver or combined liver-kidney transplantation generally took more than 1 year, suggesting that alleviation of systemic damage may occur slowly relative to other treatment effects, such as improvement of kidney function and reduction of urinary or plasma oxalate. It is important to note, however, that definitive conclusions about the progression of systemic skeletal oxalosis cannot be made due to the retrospective data collection, small sample size (a subset of patients with available x-rays), and varied clinical indications for the x-rays.

There was little evidence of cardiac oxalosis on echocardiogram, but relatively few patients had available data, likely because 2 separate measurements were required. Two patients demonstrated low LVEF, but abnormal LVEF is typically not evident until myocardial dysfunction is severe. Subclinical myocardial disease has been previously reported in patients with PH and preserved LVEF using 2-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography.35 The majority of patients in that study had CKD stage 1 (13.3%) or 2 (73.3%), and none were on dialysis (ie, they were generally at an earlier disease stage compared with patients in the present study).35

The rate of nephrolithiasis events was low, but underreporting is possible with chart review. Fracture was a common event, consistent with observations of skeletal oxalosis. The pattern of commonly reported abnormal clinical laboratory values was in line with the renal disease characteristics of the study population; in particular, low bicarbonate levels were not surprising in these patients with advanced CKD and occurred in nearly all patients not on dialysis and most patients receiving hemodialysis.

This study has multiple strengths. The patient population is large, and the duration of follow-up was long. The intentional inclusion of multiple countries and regions allowed for representation of a diverse patient population.36 A variety of outcome measures were used to assess PH1 progression and impact on different body systems. The comparison of patient cohorts with differing severity of disease (receiving hemodialysis and not on dialysis) is informative, and the use of a validated scale for assessment of skeletal oxalosis is novel.28 The study also has limitations. Like all chart review studies, it is retrospective and based on potentially incomplete information. For example, information was not collected to confirm whether patients who died had kidney failure at the time of death, although this is suggested by universal presence of systemic oxalosis. Eligible patients were required to have a minimum of at least 4 health care visits related to PH1 spanning at least 6 months, which may have been a source of bias toward a better clinical outcome. As noted earlier, decline in eGFR may have been particularly susceptible to this bias. The short follow-up period, coupled with incomplete data in some patient records (eg, pyridoxine treatment and associated response), impacted the ability to fully characterize the patient population. Finally, the sample size for some endpoints (eGFR, POx, and cardiac oxalosis) was small.

In conclusion, advanced PH1 is associated with high morbidity and mortality in the absence of currently available RNAi therapeutics. Liver and/or kidney transplantation has frequently been pursued but carries significant risk. The novel bone oxalosis grading scale28 results suggest worsening of oxalosis in the pretransplant period and slow improvement posttransplant, consistent with mitigation of systemic damage associated with deposition of excess oxalate. Moving forward, the need for reactive treatment (eg, transplant) may be decreased by treatments that reduce excess oxalate production and lower urinary and/or plasma oxalate levels, ideally early in the disease process.
