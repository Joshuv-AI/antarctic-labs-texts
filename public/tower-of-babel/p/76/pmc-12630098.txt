Introduction

Biliary colic, characterized by intermittent right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain, is a common clinical complaint in both adults and children. When ultrasonography fails to demonstrate gallstones or structural abnormalities, hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) scans with cholecystokinin (CCK) stimulation are commonly used to evaluate gallbladder function. Based on gallbladder ejection fraction (GBEF), patients are classified as having either normal motility or biliary dyskinesia (BD), defined as impaired gallbladder contraction with an ejection fraction below 35 % [1].

BD is increasingly recognized as a cause of biliary-type pain in the absence of gallstones. The Rome IV criteria define functional gallbladder disorder as biliary pain without gallstones or structural pathology, supported by findings such as reduced GBEF (<35 %) on HIDA-CCK scintigraphy, with normal liver enzymes, bilirubin, and pancreatic enzymes. Typical pain is gradual in onset, steady, lasts ≥30 min, recurs intermittently, and is severe enough to interfere with daily activities. It is unrelated to bowel movements, posture, or acid suppression, and may be accompanied by nausea, vomiting, with radiation to the back or right infrascapular region [2]. Although GBEF is most often assessed by CCK-scintigraphy, fatty meal ultrasonography has been validated as a practical alternative [3].

An additional functional gallbladder disorder, biliary hyperkinesia, was first described in 1999 and is characterized by abnormally high GBEF (>80 %) with symptoms similar to BD. Proposed mechanisms for both dyskinesia and hyperkinesia include cystic duct dysfunction, abnormal gallbladder motility, or chronic inflammation caused by repeated mucosal stress and increased intraluminal pressure during CCK stimulation. Histopathological findings of chronic cholecystitis are frequently observed in both entities [[4], [5], [6]]. Contributing factors include chronic inflammation, gallbladder muscular hypertrophy, adhesions, and systemic diseases such as diabetes [7].

Approximately 10 % of children with functional abdominal pain localize discomfort to the RUQ, warranting evaluation for biliary causes [8]. The incidence of functional gallbladder disorders (FGBD) as an indication for cholecystectomy has risen significantly with the advent of laparoscopic surgery. In some pediatric centers, BD now accounts for up to 50 % of cholecystectomies [[9], [10], [11]]. Historically, cholecystectomy was limited to gallstone disease, but recognition of FGBD and availability of functional testing broadened surgical indications. Importantly, reproduction of pain during CCK infusion is a stronger predictor of postoperative symptom resolution than GBEF alone [12,13]. Studies further suggest that patients with normokinetic or hyperkinetic gallbladders may also benefit from surgery, with outcomes comparable to those with hypokinesia [14,15].

Cholecystectomy remains the definitive treatment for BD, as medical therapies such as prokinetic agents, cholinergic agonists, and dietary modification have limited efficacy [16,17]. Despite variability in diagnostic testing, careful clinical evaluation guided by Rome IV criteria and symptom reproduction is the most reliable predictor of favorable surgical outcomes. Overall, patients undergoing cholecystectomy for FGBD report significant and durable symptom relief, with quality-of-life improvements comparable to those achieved in cholelithiasis [18].

While gallbladder dyskinesia is well-documented in adults, it is rarely reported in children, especially those under five years of age. In resource-limited settings where HIDA-CCK scintigraphy is unavailable, fatty meal ultrasonography combined with exclusion of other pathologies provides a practical diagnostic alternative. This report presents a case of a 4-year-old boy with symptomatic gallbladder dyskinesia successfully treated with laparoscopic cholecystectomy, highlighting diagnostic considerations and surgical management in very young children. This case has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria [19].

Case presentation

A 4-year-old male from a rural area was referred to our surgical clinic by his pediatrician following failed conservative management of persistent abdominal pain. He had a six-month history of progressively worsening right hypochondrial pain, which, as reported by his mother, was not continuous but occurred in recurrent phases of exacerbation, becoming more frequent and severe in the preceding weeks. The pain was associated with right hypochondrial swelling, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, and intermittent episodes of fever. There was no history of previous abdominal surgery, trauma, or known gallbladder disease.

On physical examination, the patient appeared uncomfortable, with tenderness and a palpable mass in the right hypochondrium, accompanied by mild guarding. Vital signs were within normal limits except for low-grade fever. Laboratory investigations revealed leukocytosis (WBC: 15.7 × 109/L), mild anemia (Hb: 11.2 g/dL), and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP: 68 mg/L). Other hematological and biochemical parameters were within normal limits. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated an enlarged, distended gallbladder consistent with hydrops, without evidence of gallstones and with a normal biliary tract, raising suspicion for gallbladder dyskinesia. In this limited-resource setting, cholescintigraphy was not available; therefore, serial ultrasonography after fatty meals was performed to assess gallbladder contractility. On the first day, ultrasound was conducted twice, each at 60 min following the ingestion of a fatty meal, demonstrating persistent hydrops with failed gallbladder contraction. On the second day, the same maneuver was repeated, again showing absent contraction and persistent hydrops, correlating with right hypochondrial pain. Contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen confirmed gallbladder hydrops measuring 10.8 × 5.6 cm, with no gallstones and no alternative intra-abdominal pathology, further supporting the diagnosis of a functional gallbladder disorder (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Sagittal contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen demonstrating gallbladder hydrops without evidence of additional complications.

After detailed discussion with the parents regarding the diagnosis, natural course, and management options, laparoscopic cholecystectomy was indicated to relieve symptoms and prevent potential complications. The procedure was performed using a standard four-port technique. Intraoperatively, the gallbladder was markedly distended with surrounding adhesions consistent with acute cholecystitis. Careful dissection of adhesions was performed, followed by identification of Calot's triangle and demonstration of the critical view of safety (Fig. 4). The cystic duct and artery were clipped and divided, and the gallbladder was dissected from its hepatic bed and retrieved through the umbilical port (Fig. 5). A subhepatic drain was placed for postoperative monitoring, and the specimen was sent for histopathological examination (Fig. 6).

Intraoperative view showing a distended gallbladder with adhesions; Calot's triangle identified and the critical view of safety demonstrated.

Intraoperative view of the gallbladder bed after laparoscopic cholecystectomy with cystic duct and artery divided.

Resected gallbladder showing acute acalculous inflammation with wall thickening.

Postoperatively, the patient received intravenous ceftriaxone. Oral intake was resumed on postoperative day one. Drain output remained negligible and was removed on day two, after which the patient was discharged in good condition. Histopathological examination revealed flattened mucosal epithelium, a thickened fibrotic wall with myxoid change, and sparse inflammatory infiltrates, without evidence of malignancy, findings consistent with gallbladder dyskinesia (Fig. 7). At six-month follow-up, the child remained asymptomatic with complete resolution of abdominal pain, improved appetite, and high parental satisfaction with the minimally invasive outcome.

Discussion

Functional gallbladder disorders, including biliary dyskinesia (BD) and hyperkinesia, encompass a spectrum of conditions characterized by typical biliary pain in the absence of gallstones or structural pathology. Diagnosis remains challenging in children due to variability in pain definitions, inconsistent gallbladder ejection fraction (GBEF) thresholds, and limited reproducibility of scintigraphy. Up to 53 % of pediatric patients may demonstrate normal GBEF on repeat testing despite an initially abnormal scan [3,20].

Although BD is more commonly reported in adults, pediatric cases are increasingly recognized, likely due to heightened awareness, improved diagnostic modalities, and rising prevalence of obesity and sedentary lifestyles [21]. Children typically present with postprandial right upper quadrant pain, nausea, vomiting, or intermittent discomfort that interferes with daily activities [22]. BD is generally considered a diagnosis of exclusion, necessitating thorough evaluation to rule out other gastrointestinal and hepatobiliary conditions such as gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, inflammatory bowel disease, and cholelithiasis [23,24].

Surgical outcomes following cholecystectomy are generally excellent, with reported symptom resolution rates of 94–100 % in both adult and pediatric populations [[25], [26], [27]]. Randomized trials demonstrate superior efficacy of surgery over conservative or medical management [17,28]. Pediatric studies corroborate these findings, showing high rates of symptom relief with low morbidity and mortality. Careful patient selection, based on detailed symptom characterization, postprandial triggers, and reproduction of pain during CCK stimulation, is critical for identifying surgical candidates [[9], [10], [11]]. While some studies suggest that very low GBEF (<14–35 %) may correlate with improved postoperative outcomes [[29], [30], [31]], others report no direct relationship between GBEF values and symptom resolution [32,33]. Reproduction of characteristic biliary pain during CCK stimulation has emerged as a more reliable predictor of surgical benefit [12,29].

HIDA-CCK scanning is widely used to assess gallbladder function, but limitations include poor reproducibility, operator-dependent protocols, and inconsistent correlation between GBEF and surgical outcomes [20,29,32,33]. Some patients demonstrate normal results on repeat testing, while others with normal or elevated ejection fractions still achieve symptom resolution after surgery [[4], [5], [6],14,15]. Emerging evidence supports postprandial ultrasonography after a fatty meal as a physiologic, cost-effective alternative. This technique mimics natural CCK release, provides dynamic evaluation of gallbladder contractility, and may help identify patients with persistent hydrops likely to benefit from surgery [3,34]. In our case, serial postprandial ultrasounds confirmed non-contractility, providing sufficient confidence to proceed with laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Histopathology in BD often reveals chronic inflammation, gallbladder wall fibrosis, or mucosal flattening, even when gallstones are absent [[4], [5], [6]]. In our patient, these features were also present, supporting the functional nature of the disorder. Although the link between microscopic changes and symptoms is not always direct, these structural alterations may indicate chronic motility dysfunction and justify surgical intervention.

Overall, this case illustrates several important clinical lessons including that BD should be suspected in children with unexplained biliary pain even in very young age, and laparoscopic cholecystectomy offers reliable symptom resolution, and in the absence of HIDA-CCK scintigraphy in low resources settings, serial ultrasound scans after cholecystokinin stimulation with fatty-meals represents a practical diagnostic alternative. Future prospective studies are needed to standardize diagnostic criteria, clarify predictive factors for surgical success, and validate non-nuclear imaging modalities in pediatric populations.

Conclusion

Gallbladder dyskinesia, though rare in young children, should be considered in cases of persistent right upper quadrant pain without gallstones. Accurate diagnosis requires careful clinical assessment, supportive imaging, and exclusion of other causes of abdominal pain. In settings where HIDA-CCK scintigraphy is unavailable, serial postprandial ultrasonography after a fatty meal can serve as a practical and effective alternative. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is a safe and definitive treatment, providing rapid symptom relief and significant improvement in quality of life. Early recognition and timely surgical management are essential to prevent prolonged morbidity in pediatric patients.
