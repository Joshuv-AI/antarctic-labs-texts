Gut-derived extracellular vesicle enriched miR-125a-5p mediates cadmium-induced intestinal injury through NF-κB pathway activation
Authors: Mengzhen Song, Lina Ping, Yulin Wang, Wenjun Zhou, Jinping Fan, Shiyu Tao, Yaoqin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cadmium (Cd), as a prevalent environmental heavy metal pollutant, has an incompletely elucidated mechanism of intestinal toxicity following oral ingestion. This study aims to elucidate novel mechanisms underlying cadmium-induced intestinal injury. By establishing a murine model of cadmium exposure, it was found that cadmium not only directly disrupts the intestinal barrier structure but also induces intestinal microbiota dysbiosis. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments confirmed that the dysbiotic microbiota alone is sufficient to provoke intestinal injury, indicating that microbial dysregulation serves as a critical amplifier of cadmium toxicity. Further investigation revealed that cadmium exposure reshapes the intestinal microenvironment and alters the miRNA profile of gut-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), with miR-125a-5p being significantly enriched in EVs from the cadmium-exposed group. Mechanistically, miR-125a-5p directly targets and suppresses TNFAIP3, a negative regulator of the NF-κB pathway, thereby relieving the inhibition of this signaling axis and driving sustained inflammation and barrier dysfunction, while inhibition of miR-125a-5p effectively blocks this toxic effect. This study is the first to delineate the axis of "cadmium exposure – intestinal microbiota dysbiosis – gut EV – miR-125a-5p – TNFAIP3 – NF-κB pathway activation – intestinal injury", providing new insights for the development of biomarkers and targeted interventions for cadmium-related intestinal disorders.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04809-0.

Introduction

Cadmium (Cd) is an environmental heavy metal pollutant that poses a serious threat to human health. It primarily enters the human body through contaminated food, water, and other sources, with a biological half-life of up to 10–30 years [1]. Consequently, it tends to accumulate in the body and induces multiple adverse effects on the kidneys, bones, nervous system, and digestive tract. [2–4]. The intestine serves as the primary biological barrier following the oral ingestion of cadmium. Extensive in vivo and in vitro studies have confirmed that cadmium exposure directly compromises the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier, a mechanism closely associated with the induction of oxidative stress and the inhibition of the expression and function of tight junction proteins [5–7]. Furthermore, the gut microbiota, another critical pillar of intestinal homeostasis, is highly sensitive to cadmium exposure. Studies indicate that cadmium intake significantly alters the structure of the microbiota, reduces its diversity, and leads to imbalances in the proportions of specific bacterial phyla. This suggests that dysregulation of microbiota-host interactions may exacerbate the toxic effects of cadmium [8]. While the intestinal toxicity of cadmium described above has been widely documented, the complete signaling pathway linking "environmental exposure" to "host pathological phenotypes" still contains significant gaps. Specifically, the molecular mechanisms through which the intestinal microenvironment perceives, responds to, and transmits cadmium’s toxic signals remain largely unclear.

The gut microbiota is not only a target of cadmium exposure but also a potential key mediator of its toxic effects. Studies indicate that exposure to heavy metals can alter the composition of the gut microbiota and interact with numerous commensal microorganisms. Such dysbiotic microbial structure may exacerbate host injury through multiple mechanisms [8–10]. However, the molecular intermediaries through which the microbiota precisely transmits the "signal" of cadmium exposure to host cells remain unclear. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have recently been identified as a crucial medium for intercellular communication. EVs are nanoscale lipid bilayer vesicles actively secreted by cells, which carry bioactive molecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. They are capable of transmitting information between cells and modulating the functions of recipient cells [11, 12]. Notably, under pathological conditions such as infection or inflammatory bowel disease, intestine-derived EVs, particularly those carrying specific pathological molecules, have been demonstrated to function as effector vehicles that can directly injure epithelial cells or amplify inflammatory responses [13, 14]. Extracellular vesicles derived from dysbiotic microbiota under pathological conditions (e.g., diarrhea‑associated microbiota) may deliver molecules such as miR‑125b, thereby driving pro‑inflammatory M1 polarization and disrupting intestinal homeostasis [15]. However, under the stress of environmental heavy metals such as cadmium, the biological properties and molecular cargo of gut‑derived EVs undergo significant alterations. Nevertheless, whether these alterations play an active role as “signal transducers” in cadmium‑induced intestinal toxicity remains insufficiently explored.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of endogenous non‑coding RNA molecules approximately 22 nucleotides in length. They achieve precise post‑transcriptional regulation of gene expression by mediating either the degradation of target mRNAs or the inhibition of their translation [16]. In the field of intestinal inflammation, extensive research has identified several miRNAs—such as miR‑433‑3p and miR‑22—that function as “molecular switches” for key inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF‑κB and MAPK. By targeting critical nodes within these pathways, they precisely regulate the production of downstream pro‑inflammatory cytokines [17–19]. Notably, recent research has begun to focus on the global impact of environmental toxins, particularly heavy metal exposure, on the organism‑wide miRNA expression profile [20, 21]. Previous studies have demonstrated that cadmium exposure can alter miRNA expression profiles in intestinal epithelial cells. Yang et al. found that cadmium treatment significantly changed the miRNA expression pattern in IEC-6 cells, with seven miRNAs including miR-124-3p and miR-370-3p being upregulated in a dose-dependent manner and shown to regulate cadmium-induced apoptosis by targeting Bcl-2 [22]. Given that extracellular vesicles serve as important delivery vehicles for miRNAs, this strongly suggests that cadmium exposure may alter the miRNA cargo profile within gut‑derived EVs, thereby packaging and delivering specific pro‑inflammatory or barrier‑disrupting signals to target cells, which in turn initiates or amplifies the intestinal damage program. However, how cadmium exposure remodels the miRNA profile of gut-derived EVs and the functional roles of the differentially expressed miRNAs remain to be clarified.

Although cadmium toxicity has been extensively studied, the precise signaling pathway from exposure to cellular injury is still unclear. This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms through which cadmium induces intestinal inflammation and barrier damage from the perspective of EVs and miRNA-mediated intercellular communication. The findings are expected to offer new insights into the intestinal toxicology of heavy metals and provide potential targets for developing intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Animals and samples collection

Male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen‑free conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 12-h light/dark cycle). Mice in each experimental group were randomly assigned and provided with ad libitum access to water and food. All animal experiments and sample collection procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Huazhong Agricultural University, Hubei, China. In this study, all experimental methods were conducted in accordance with the university’s Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Ethics Approval No.: HZAUMO-2026–0024). Following collection, tissue and blood samples were either immediately fixed or rapidly frozen and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Mouse model of cadmium exposure

Following the method established by Xie et al. [1], a cadmium exposure model was constructed in this experiment. Mice were randomly allocated into a control group and a cadmium‑treated group (n = 8 per group). The control group received daily oral gavage of 0.2 mL PBS, while the cadmium‑treated group was treated with 12.5 mg/kg body weight CdCl₂ solution (dissolved in PBS) daily via oral gavage for a continuous period of 14 days. Body weight changes were recorded daily throughout the experimental period.

Fecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) experiment

To investigate the pathogenic role of the gut microbiota following cadmium exposure, an FMT experiment was established. Donor mice were selected from the aforementioned cadmium-treated and control groups. Intestinal contents were collected for the preparation of bacterial suspensions. Recipient mice underwent a 14-day pretreatment with antibiotic-supplemented drinking water and were then randomly allocated into two groups (n = 8 per group). Each group received daily oral gavage of 0.2 mL donor bacterial suspension for 14 consecutive days. Body weight changes were monitored daily throughout the experimental period. The procedure for preparing bacterial suspensions from intestinal microbiota was performed in accordance with previously described methods [23].

Gut-derived EVs intervention experiment

In this experiment, 24 mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 8 per group). They received daily oral gavage as follows: the control group was treated with PBS, the C‑EVs group received gut‑derived EVs isolated from donor mice fed a normal diet and gavaged daily with PBS, and the Cd‑EVs group received gut‑derived EVs isolated from donor mice fed a normal diet but gavaged daily with CdCl₂ solution. The intervention lasted for five consecutive days. After the intervention, samples were collected for histological analysis, detection of inflammatory cytokines, and assessment of barrier proteins.

In vivo intervention experiment with agomiR

To validate the in vivo function of miR‑125a‑5p, this study designed an agomiR intervention experiment, with the dosage based on the study by Lu et al. [24]. A total of 24 mice were randomly divided into three groups, with eight mice per group. The CON group received an intraperitoneal injection of 200 μL PBS; the agomiR‑NC group was treated with 200 μL of agomiR‑NC (2 nmol/10 g body weight) via intraperitoneal injection; and the agomiR‑125a‑5p group received 200 μL of agomiR‑125a‑5p (2 nmol/10 g body weight) by the same route. Injections were performed every other day for a total duration of five days. Upon completion of the intervention, samples were collected for histological examination, measurement of inflammatory cytokines, and evaluation of barrier protein expression.

Cell experimental procedures

The mouse jejunal epithelial cell line MODE-K was employed in this study as an in vitro model for investigating intestinal barrier function. The cells were routinely cultured in complete DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‑streptomycin (Gibco), and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Cells from passages 3–8 were used for subsequent experiments. For all assays, MODE-K cells were seeded into 6‑well plates 24 h in advance.

EVs intervention experiment

To investigate the effects of gut-derived EVs on intestinal epithelial cell function, MODE-K cells were seeded into 6-well plates. When cell confluence reached approximately 70%, the cultures were divided into the following experimental groups: the control group, which received an equal volume of PBS; the C-EVs group, treated with 10 µg/mL EVs derived from the intestinal contents of control mice; and the Cd-EVs group, treated with 10 µg/mL EVs derived from the intestinal contents of cadmium-treated mice. Each group was performed in triplicate (n = 3). After 12 h of intervention, samples were collected for analysis.

miRNA mimics transfection and functional validation

To investigate the function of miR‑125a‑5p in intestinal epithelial cells, MODE‑K cells were divided into the following three groups, each with three replicates (n = 3): the control group received only the transfection reagent; the mimics‑NC group was transfected with negative control miRNA mimics (50 nM); and the miR‑125a‑5p mimics group was transfected with miR‑125a‑5p mimics (50 nM). Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 4–6 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium, and the cells were further incubated for 48 h before being harvested for subsequent analyses. The above dosages are based on the study by Lu et al. [24].

Co‑treatment experiment with inhibitor

To verify whether miR‑125a‑5p mediates the pathogenic effects of Cd‑EVs, MODE‑K cells were divided into the following three groups, each with three replicates (n = 3): the control group was maintained in complete medium; the Cd‑EVs group was treated with 10 μg/mL Cd‑EVs; and the Cd‑EVs + inhibitor group received combined treatment with Cd‑EVs and an miR‑125a‑5p inhibitor (100 nM). Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of co‑treatment, samples were collected for subsequent analysis. The above dosages are based on the study by Lu et al. [24].

Metagenomic sequencing of intestinal contents

Upon completion of the experiment, mice were dissected and intestinal content samples were collected for metagenomic sequencing. Total DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit following an optimized protocol designed for complex microbial samples. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®. After passing quality assessment on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, the constructed libraries were subjected to paired‑end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads were first processed with Fastp for quality control, which included the removal of low‑quality sequences, adapter contaminants, and excessively short reads, yielding high‑quality clean reads. The clean reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using Bowtie2 to eliminate host‑derived sequences, resulting in non‑host metagenomic data. α‑diversity indices and taxonomic composition bar plots were computed using the MicrobiotaProcess package (version 1.4.4). Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed via the cmdscale function in the vegan package (version 2.5‑7). Differential microbial analyses were conducted using the diff_analysis function within the MicrobiotaProcess package.

Jejunal tissue transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Jejunal tissue samples from mice were collected, immediately ground in liquid nitrogen, and total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method. RNA purity was assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (OD260/280 ratio: 1.8–2.2), and integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer (RIN ≥ 7.0). For RNA samples meeting quality criteria, sequencing libraries were constructed using the Illumina® Stranded mRNA Prep, Ligation Kit. Briefly, mRNA was enriched with Oligo (dT) magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation, reverse transcription, and cDNA synthesis. The resulting cDNA underwent end repair, adenylation, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. The final constructed libraries were subjected to paired‑end sequencing (PE150) on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform.

Raw sequencing data were quality‑controlled using Fastp, which removed adapter sequences, low‑quality reads, and excessively short fragments, yielding high‑quality Clean Data. Clean reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using HISAT2. The alignment results were processed with StringTie for transcriptome assembly. Gene expression levels were quantified by RSEM and reported as Transcripts Per Million (TPM). Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, applying significance thresholds of |log₂FC|≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 to identify differentially expressed genes. GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the differentially expressed genes using Goatools and the Python scipy package, respectively, with an FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Extraction and characterization of EVs

Intestinal content samples were collected from cadmium‑exposed and control mice. Gut‑derived EVs were isolated from the intestinal contents via ultracentrifugation as described in reference [25]. For each gram of fresh content, 25 mL of ice‑cold phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS) was added and homogenized. The mixture was centrifuged twice at 4 °C (3,000 × g for 10 min each). The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter (Millipore) to remove microorganisms, followed by centrifugation at 40,000 × g for 1.5 h at 4 °C. Subsequently, the supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 150,000 × g for 2.5 h at 4 °C using an Optima L‑100XP ultracentrifuge (Beckman, USA). The resulting pellet was washed with 20 mL of pre‑cooled PBS and centrifuged again at 150,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Finally, the precipitate was resuspended in PBS for further analysis.

The particle size distribution and concentration of EVs were measured using nanoparticle tracking analysis (Particle Metrix ZetaView PMX‑120). Samples were diluted with sterile PBS to an appropriate concentration prior to detection. For morphological observation, EVs were negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate and loaded onto standard carbon‑supported copper grids. Imaging was performed under a transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 12) operating at an acceleration voltage of 100 kV.

EVs uptake by MODE-K cells

As described in previous research [26], in brief, EVs were labeled by co‑incubation with DiI (Sigma, #42,364) for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C and 120,000 × g for 1 h. The pellet was washed once under the same conditions and resuspended in cold PBS to remove excess dye. Subsequently, after cell attachment on coverslips, DiI‑labeled or unlabeled EVs were added, and the cells were further incubated for 12 h. Fluorescence microscopy was used to detect DiI‑positive cells.

miRNA extraction and sequencing

Small RNAs contained within EVs were profiled using Illumina HiSeq sequencing (Majorbio, Shanghai, China). Briefly, total RNA was extracted from purified EVs with an exosomal RNA isolation kit (Norgen) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and RNA purity was assessed by NanoDrop 2000 (A260/280 ratio). Subsequently, a small RNA library compatible with the Illumina platform was constructed by ligating specific adapters to both ends of the RNA molecules, followed by reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA and PCR amplification. Amplified products were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and target fragments were excised and recovered to construct the sequencing library. Library quality was rigorously evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with a High Sensitivity DNA chip. Cluster generation was performed on the Illumina cBot, and sequencing was carried out on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Target gene prediction was conducted using three software tools: miRanda, TargetScan, and RNAhybrid. Furthermore, GO functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed on the predicted target genes to systematically elucidate the potential biological functions of the differentially expressed miRNAs.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated from mouse intestinal tissues using the mirVana™ miRNA Isolation Kit, and reverse transcription was performed with a miR-125a-5p-specific stem-loop primer. The expression level of mature miR-125a-5p (Forward sequence: CCTGTAGAATCGGCTGCTTCCTAT, Reverse sequence: CCACTGTCACCTCACTCCATCC) was quantified by qRT-PCR, normalized to U6 (Forward sequence: GAGATTACTGCTCTGGCTCCTA, Reverse sequence: GGACTCATCGTACTCCTGCTTG) as the endogenous control, and calculated using the comparative 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

Determination of cadmium content in feces

Quantitative analysis of cadmium in fecal specimens was conducted using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS; Agilent 7800). Sample preparation involved the microwave-assisted acid digestion of 0.5 g of lyophilized feces with 8 mL of 69% nitric acid. The certified reference material ERM-DB001 was employed for quality assurance. Under these conditions, the analytical precision, expressed as relative standard deviation, fell within 0.03–17.9%, while the recovery rates ranged from 94.0% to 111%.

Histological analyses

Jejunal tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using a standardized protocol designed to preserve mucosal architecture. Morphometric analysis was conducted with the calibrated measurement tool in CaseViewer software (version 220.2.2). Villus height was defined as the distance from the villus tip to the crypt‑villus junction, while crypt depth was measured from the junction to the basal lamina.

Western blot analyses

Jejunal tissue samples were collected and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease inhibitors. After centrifugation at 4 °C and 15,000 × g for 20 min, the supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using a micro‑BCA assay with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Absorbance was measured on a Multiskan Sky microplate reader to quantify protein levels. Denatured proteins were separated by SDS‑PAGE and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane via electro‑blotting. After transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h. It was then incubated with respective primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the membrane was washed three times with TBST (10 min each) and incubated with corresponding horseradish peroxidase‑conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. Protein expression was detected and quantified using an imaging system (Bio‑Rad, USA) and Quantity One software (Bio‑Rad, USA). Details of the antibodies used are listed in Table S2.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Species‑specific ELISA kits (Shanghai Enzyme‑Linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) were used to measure the levels of inflammatory mediators—interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β), interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α), and interleukin‑10 (IL‑10)—in serum, jejunal tissue homogenates, and cell culture supernatants. Homogenates were prepared in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors and centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 15 min, after which the supernatant was collected for analysis. All procedures were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was assayed in triplicate, and freshly thawed aliquots were used to avoid experimental variability caused by repeated freeze‑thaw cycles.

Data analysis and graphical presentation

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the independent‑samples t‑test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post‑hoc test for pairwise comparisons. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05. The numbers of biological and technical replicates are specified in the figure legends to ensure reproducibility of the results. The Graphical Abstract and AOP diagram were created using BioGDP.com (https://biogdp.com) [27].

Results

Cadmium ingestion induced intestinal inflammation and disrupted gut microbiota homeostasis in mice

To investigate the systemic effects of cadmium exposure, a mouse model was established following the method reported by Xie et al. [1]. C57BL/6 mice were treated daily with either PBS or 12.5 mg/kg CdCl₂ via oral gavage (Fig. 1A). Compared with the control group, the body weight of cadmium-treated mice showed a significant decrease by the eighth day (Fig. 1B). Histological analysis revealed a marked reduction in jejunal villus length in the cadmium-treated group (Fig. 1C, D). Regarding inflammatory responses, cadmium intake elevated the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in both serum and intestinal tissues, while simultaneously reducing the level of the anti‑inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 1E, F). Cadmium ingestion induced extensive transcriptional reprogramming in the intestinal tissue of mice

To systematically elucidate the impact of cadmium exposure on intestinal gene expression, we performed RNA sequencing analysis of jejunal tissues from control (PBS) and cadmium‑treated (Cd) mice. The Venn diagram clearly illustrates the unique and shared differentially expressed gene sets between the two groups, revealing 12,370 common genes, 604 genes specific to the control group, and 289 genes specific to the Cd group (Fig. 2A). Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct separation between the control and cadmium‑treated samples, indicating that cadmium exposure induced a global remodeling of the intestinal transcriptome (Fig. 2B). Through differential expression analysis (|log₂FC|> 1), a total of 308 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, among which 176 were significantly up‑regulated and 132 were significantly down‑regulated (Fig. 2C). Functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using GO and KEGG databases. GO analysis, covering biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, revealed that enriched terms were predominantly associated with immune defense responses against pathogen invasion (Fig. 2D, E). KEGG analysis spanned metabolism, environmental information processing, cellular processes, organismal systems, and human diseases (Fig. 2F, G). We specifically focused on inflammation‑related pathways among all the enriched pathways for further validation. The enriched pathways included the NOD‑like receptor signaling pathway, IL‑17 signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, and NF‑κB pathway, among others (Fig. 2H). The gut microbiota from cadmium-exposed mice induced intestinal damage and inflammatory responses in recipient mice

To clarify the causal relationship between gut microbiota dysbiosis and intestinal inflammation after cadmium exposure, an FMT experiment was conducted (Fig. 3A). The results showed that recipient mice receiving microbiota from cadmium‑exposed donors exhibited a significant decrease in body weight (Fig. 3B) and developed marked intestinal structural damage, manifested as shortened jejunal villi (Fig. 3C, D). Inflammatory cytokine assays revealed significantly elevated levels of IL‑1β, IL‑6, and TNF‑α, along with a reduction in IL‑10 levels, in both serum and jejunal tissues of the recipient mice (Fig. 3E, F). Western blot analysis further demonstrated significant downregulation of intestinal tight‑junction proteins ZO‑1, Occludin, and Claudin‑1 (Fig. 3G, H). Quantification of cadmium levels in donor feces demonstrated that the residual cadmium administered to recipient mice via oral gavage was substantially lower than the established effective dose for intestinal toxicity (Fig. S3A), confirming that the phenotypes observed were mediated by the transferred microbiota rather than cadmium carryover. Metagenomic analysis was performed on the gut microbiota of recipient mice following fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), with species composition shown in Fig. S3B, C, D. Regarding α-diversity, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in most indices across the phylum, genus, and species levels, with the exception of the Simpson index at the phylum level (Fig. S3E-G). However, β-diversity at all three taxonomic levels exhibited significant alterations (Fig. S4A-C), indicating that cadmium exposure primarily reshaped the community structure and inter-species relative abundance relationships of the gut microbiota, rather than merely affecting species richness. We further identified the top 10 differentially abundant taxa at each taxonomic level (Fig. S4E-F) and performed correlation analyses with various pathological parameters of FMT recipients (Fig. S4G). The results revealed that at the phylum level, multiple taxa including p_Verrucomicrobiota, p_Lentisphaerota, and p_Candidatus_Cloacimonadota were significantly positively correlated with pro-inflammatory cytokines and negatively correlated with anti-inflammatory cytokines. At the genus level, g_Akkermansia, g_Bacteroides, g_Mediterraneibacter, g_Lactobacillus, and g_Sangeribacter exhibited strong correlations with multiple pathological features. At the species level, s_Akkermansia_muciniphila, s_Bacteroides_acidifaciens, s_Mediterraneibacter_agrestimuris, and s_Lactobacillus_johnsonii also showed strong correlations with various pathological parameters. Collectively, these findings suggest that these microbial taxa may play important regulatory roles in cadmium-induced intestinal homeostasis disruption.

Gut‑derived EVs from cadmium‑exposed mice induce intestinal injury and inflammatory responses in recipient mice

To investigate the role of EVs in cadmium‑induced intestinal injury, EVs were isolated from the intestinal contents of PBS‑treated and cadmium‑exposed mice and subjected to comprehensive characterization. TEM and NTA confirmed the typical cup‑shaped morphology and size distribution of the isolated vesicles (Fig. 4A, B). Western blot analysis further revealed that the isolated EVs positively expressed the epithelial cell marker EpCAM (Fig. 4C), confirming their intestinal epithelial origin. An EVs intervention experiment was subsequently performed (Fig. 4D). Discussion

Environmental cadmium pollution has become a global public health issue, with oral ingestion serving as a major route of exposure [29]. Traditional research has focused primarily on its direct damaging effects on the intestinal epithelium [30, 31]. This study, however, centers on the intestine as a complex micro‑ecosystem and aims to elucidate how cadmium exposure indirectly triggers intestinal injury by disrupting the gut microenvironment. By integrating animal models, multi‑omics analyses, and functional validation, we demonstrated that cadmium exposure disturbs the gut microbiota and enriches miR‑125a‑5p in gut‑derived EVs. This miRNA directly binds to TNFAIP3, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to pathway overactivation, which in turn triggers inflammatory responses and impairs intestinal barrier function. Collectively, this work systematically reveals a novel mechanism through which cadmium mediates intestinal injury via the "gut microbiota – EVs – miRNA" axis.

Traditional cadmium toxicology research has primarily focused on its direct cytotoxicity, such as through the induction of oxidative stress, disruption of calcium signaling, impairment of mitochondrial function, and initiation of apoptosis. These mechanisms have been particularly emphasized in studies of major cadmium‑accumulating organs, such as the liver and kidneys [32–36]. While the study does not exclude the potential direct damaging effects of cadmium ions on intestinal epithelial cells, our findings substantially expand this conceptual framework. For cadmium, which primarily enters the body via oral ingestion, the intestine serves as both the first portal of entry and an early site of action. Numerous studies have confirmed that cadmium can accumulate directly within the intestinal tract, disrupt epithelial tight junctions, induce oxidative damage, and cause villus atrophy, establishing the gut as a critical initial target of cadmium toxicity [37–39]. The barrier damage and inflammation observed in our study under direct cadmium exposure align with this conventional understanding; however, our research aims to move beyond that level. The intestinal transcriptional response to cadmium exposure reveals a unique defensive strategy. In functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes, we specifically focused on pathways related to immune and inflammatory regulation, and observed the involvement of signaling cascades such as the NOD‑like receptor pathway, IL‑17 pathway, and NF‑κB pathway. This immune‑centric transcriptional profile contrasts with the predominant disruption of metabolic pathways observed in organs like the liver following cadmium exposure [40–42]. It suggests that the intestine, as the primary site of exposure, adopts a strategy centered on maintaining immune homeostasis in response to cadmium stress. These findings provide important clues for further elucidating how cadmium may modulate intestinal inflammation through intercellular communication networks.

Increasing evidence in recent years indicates that the intestine is far from being merely a passive “target”; rather, it functions as a dynamic ecosystem and an amplifier of toxic effects. Cadmium exposure can induce severe gut microbiota dysbiosis, and the dysregulated microbiota itself has been shown to exacerbate damage to distant organs through pathways such as the gut‑liver axis and the gut‑brain axis, thereby significantly amplifying the systemic toxicity of cadmium [43, 44]. Within this theoretical framework, our FMT experiment provides the most direct causal evidence: the gut microbiota derived from cadmium‑exposed mice is sufficient to independently "replicate" the complete intestinal inflammatory and barrier‑damaged phenotypes in recipient mice without cadmium exposure. Cadmium content measurement in donor feces revealed that although the cadmium levels in the Cd-treated donor group were significantly higher than those in the control group, the potential daily intake of residual cadmium by FMT recipient mice via gavage, after calculation, was only 1/56,000 of the effective toxic dose administered to the directly cadmium-exposed mice, representing a reduction of more than five orders of magnitude. Based on this evidence, we conclude that the intestinal inflammation and barrier damage phenotypes observed in recipient mice can only be attributed to structural alterations of the transplanted microbiota, rather than the chemical toxicity of trace residual cadmium in the fecal material. This strongly supports the emerging perspective that the gut microbiota serves as a key effector amplifier of cadmium toxicity. Furthermore, metagenomic analysis of the gut microbiota in FMT recipient mice revealed that multiple genera, including g_Akkermansia and g_Enterorhabdus, exhibited significant positive correlations with pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Notably, in this study, the abundance of s_Akkermansia_muciniphila in the intestinal microbiota of cadmium-exposed mice showed a positive correlation with pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. This result differs from most previous reports describing cadmium-induced reduction in s_Akkermansia_muciniphila abundance [45]. We propose that this seemingly paradoxical phenomenon precisely reflects the drastic perturbation of the intestinal microenvironment caused by cadmium exposure. Under cadmium-induced toxic stress, the overall gut microbial community structure becomes dysregulated, and the dramatic fluctuation in s_Akkermansia_muciniphila abundance represents a component of this dysbiosis. The synchronous variation of its abundance with pro-inflammatory cytokines may reflect a compensatory stress response rather than a direct causal relationship. This finding also suggests that under specific toxic stress conditions, the association between even well-recognized beneficial bacteria and inflammation may exhibit complex patterns. Future studies are warranted to further explore the dynamic changes and functional significance of s_Akkermansia_muciniphila at different stages of cadmium exposure.

The core breakthrough of this study lies in establishing EVs isolated from mouse intestinal contents as a key mediator for transmitting pro‑inflammatory signals following cadmium exposure. EVs are membrane‑bound vesicles actively released by cells and serve as essential tools for both local and systemic intercellular communication [46]. Under various physiological and pathological conditions, EVs from specific origins can precisely regulate the function of recipient cells by delivering bioactive cargo such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. For instance, EVs derived from the probiotic Lactobacillus johnsonii deliver proteins including GAPDH, which promote M2 macrophage polarization, thereby enhancing barrier integrity and alleviating colitis [47–50]. In cirrhosis‑related sarcopenia, hepatocyte‑derived EVs transmit mitochondrial DNA to skeletal muscle, activating the cGAS‑STING pathway and driving muscle inflammation and atrophy [51]. In inflammatory bowel disease, mast cell‑derived exosomes deliver miR‑223 to intestinal epithelial cells, specifically suppressing the expression of tight‑junction proteins and thereby directly compromising intestinal barrier function [52]. These studies collectively illustrate that diseased organs, by secreting EVs carrying specific molecular cargoes, can achieve precise signal delivery across cells and even organs, thereby transforming localized damage into systemic pathological changes. In line with this paradigm, the findings of this study establish a key role for the intestine within this signaling network under cadmium exposure. Our results demonstrate that EVs isolated from the intestines of cadmium‑exposed mice (Cd‑EVs) similarly encapsulate stable pathogenic signals. Characterization analysis confirmed that these Cd-EVs were predominantly derived from intestinal epithelial cells. Both in vivo reinfusion and in vitro co‑culture with intestinal epithelial cells precisely recapitulated the typical phenotypes of cadmium exposure, including elevated inflammatory cytokines and disrupted tight junctions. Particularly significant is that this outcome reveals the independence and specificity of the pathogenic signals carried by EVs. It confirms that EVs in the local intestinal environment encapsulate stable “molecular signals” capable of driving inflammation—signals that can be recognized and responded to by target cells independently of cadmium ions and can function as purified preparations independent of living cells. This offers a novel mechanistic perspective for explaining how environmental exposures can trigger persistent local inflammation.

As core functional cargoes of EVs, miRNAs regulate the expression of specific genes at transcriptional and post‑transcriptional levels, playing a critical role as "molecular switches" in mediating intercellular communication and maintaining intestinal homeostasis [53, 54]. Particularly in the context of intestinal inflammation and barrier dysfunction, multiple studies have revealed the specific regulatory roles of various miRNAs. For instance, Hsu et al. found that environmental pollutants such as nanoplastics induce elevated levels of miRNAs, including miR‑98‑3p, in EVs derived from intestinal goblet cells. These miRNAs subsequently suppress the expression of the tight‑junction protein ZO‑1, thereby compromising intestinal barrier integrity [55]. In colitis, EV‑carried miR‑200b‑3p may enhance barrier function through interactions with the gut microbiota, while miR‑181b‑5p alleviates inflammation by promoting M2 polarization of macrophages [25]. These studies collectively establish a feasible mechanistic paradigm in which specific miRNAs are delivered via EVs and targeted to regulate host gene expression, thereby determining intestinal pathological phenotypes. Within this research framework, the present study, through miRNA profiling of Cd-EVs, combined with transcriptomic data and target gene prediction, we ultimately identified miR-125a-5p as a key differentially expressed molecule in gut-derived EVs following cadmium exposure. It is noteworthy that miR‑125a‑5p itself is not a newly discovered miRNA, and its role in immune modulation and inflammatory responses has been previously documented [28]. However, its mechanistic role in environmentally induced heavy metal toxicity, particularly within the intestine, remains unelucidated.

The NF‑κB pathway, a central hub in regulating inflammatory responses, has also been reported to be associated with cadmium toxicity in other tissues such as the kidney and liver, often in connection with oxidative stress and apoptosis [56, 57]. Studies indicate that cadmium exposure promotes the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), activates the AMPK/PPAR‑γ/NF‑κB pathway, and ultimately induces cellular autophagy and apoptosis, leading to liver damage [58]. In the intestine—a specialized immune organ—our data suggest that cadmium exposure may influence the NF‑κB pathway through a more complex network involving microbiota and gut‑derived EVs, rather than solely through direct cellular stress or canonical inflammasome activation. Through target gene prediction combined with functional validation, we confirmed that miR-125a-5p directly binds to the 3’UTR of TNFAIP3 and suppresses its expression, a finding that is highly consistent with previous reports [59, 60]. Given that TNFAIP3 serves as a key negative regulator of the NF-κB signaling pathway [61], its suppression relieves the inhibition of this pathway, leading to aberrant NF-κB activation. Consequently, overexpression of miR-125a-5p, through downregulating TNFAIP3, activates the NF-κB signaling cascade in intestinal epithelial cells, ultimately resulting in significant upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and marked downregulation of tight junction proteins, thereby exacerbating intestinal inflammatory responses. This mechanism not only links environmental exposure to intrinsic host inflammatory pathways but also highlights the crucial role of EV‑carried miRNAs as an interface for environment‑gene interactions.

This study systematically investigated the multi‑level impact of cadmium exposure on the intestinal microenvironment by establishing a cadmium‑exposed mouse model and integrating multiple techniques, including metagenomic sequencing, fecal microbiota transplantation, intestinal transcriptomic analysis, and extracellular vesicle isolation and characterization. The results demonstrated that cadmium not only directly damages the intestinal epithelial barrier but also induces structural dysbiosis of the gut microbiota; this dysbiotic microbiota alone is sufficient to independently trigger intestinal inflammation. Further mechanistic investigation revealed that cadmium exposure alters the miRNA composition of gut‑derived EVs, with miR‑125a‑5p being significantly enriched. Functional experiments have demonstrated that these vesicles and the miR-125a-5p they carry can inhibit the NF-κB pathway in intestinal epithelial cells by targeting TNFAIP3; this leads to increased release of inflammatory cytokines and downregulation of tight junction protein expression, thereby causing intestinal barrier dysfunction. This study systematically delineates a complete pathogenic cascade from cadmium exposure and gut microbiota dysbiosis to EV‑mediated delivery of miR‑125a‑5p, targeted inhibition of TNFAIP3, NF‑κB pathway activation, and ultimately intestinal injury. It expands beyond the conventional research framework focused primarily on the direct cytotoxicity of cadmium.

Conclusion

Based on the above findings, we constructed a schematic model summarizing the complete pathogenic pathway from cadmium exposure to intestinal injury (Fig. 9). In this pathway, cadmium exposure serves as the molecular initiating event (MIE). This is followed by a series of key events (KEs), including gut microbiota dysbiosis, EV-mediated enrichment of miR-125a-5p, suppression of TNFAIP3, activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, and subsequent intestinal inflammation and barrier dysfunction.
