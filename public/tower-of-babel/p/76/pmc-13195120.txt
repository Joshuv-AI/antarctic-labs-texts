Sildenafil pretreatment attenuates apoptosis and intestinal injury in a hypoxia–ischemia–induced NEC-like rat model
Authors: Şenay Kurtuluş, Özlem Öztopuz, İhsan Karaboğa, Hakan Aylanç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) is a devastating gastrointestinal disease of premature infants, in which hypoxia-induced apoptosis plays a critical role in intestinal epithelial injury. The balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins, particularly Bax and Bcl-2, as well as the hypoxia-responsive transcription factor HIF-1α, are key determinant of epithelial survival. Sildenafil citrate, a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor, has been shown to improve microcirculation and exert anti-apoptotic effects through nitric oxide–cGMP signalling, but its role in NEC-related intestinal injury remains poorly understood. This study aimed to evaluate the protective effects of sildenafil on apoptosis in a hypoxia–ischemia–induced NEC-like intestinal injury model. A total of 75 Wistar rat pups were randomly assigned to six groups: control, NEC, vehicle (saline+DMSO), and sildenafil-treated groups (1, 5, and 10 mg/kg/day). Sildenafil was administered intraperitoneally for 4 days before NEC induction. NEC was induced by hypoxia (100% nitrogen for 60 s, twice daily for 48 h) and followed by cold stress (10 min at 4 °C). Intestinal tissues were collected for histopathological scoring, caspase-3 immunohistochemistry, and quantitative RT-PCR analysis of HIF-1α, Bax, and Bcl-2 expression. Data were analysed using ANOVA and Kruskal–Wallis tests, with p ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. Pups subjected to hypoxia-induced intestinal ischemia exhibited severe villous damage, elevated histological injury scores, and marked upregulation of HIF-1α and Bax, along with downregulation of Bcl-2. Caspase-3 immunoreactivity strongly correlated with histological injury (Spearman’s ρ = 0.795, p < 0.001). Sildenafil pretreatment reduced histological damage and apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner. The Sil_10 group showed the lowest injury score (1.06 ± 0.4, p < 0.0001 vs. NEC), significantly reduced Caspase-3 labelling, suppressed Bax and HIF-1α expression, and increased Bcl-2 levels, resulting in a markedly reduced Bax/Bcl-2 ratio compared with NEC (p < 0.0001). Sildenafil attenuates hypoxia-induced, ischemia-dominant NEC-like intestinal injury by modulating apoptotic and hypoxia-related pathways, lowering HIF-1α expression, reducing Bax/Bcl-2 ratio, and preserving epithelial integrity. These findings provide novel histological and molecular evidence for the anti-apoptotic effects of sildenafil in experimental NEC, highlighting HIF-1α modulation as a potential therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) is a serious gastrointestinal disease that primarily affects premature newborns. The overall incidence of NEC has been rising due to improved survival rates among premature infants, affecting approximately 7% of very low birth weight babies1–3. Multiple risk factors, including formula feedings, microvascular events that cause hypoxia, impaired intestinal barrier and immunity, and abnormal bacterial colonisation, have been shown to play a role in NEC development4–6. Hypoxia is a significant risk factor for NEC and is thought to be a critical initial injury that triggers multiple inflammatory reactions in the gut. Ischaemia-induced hypoxia is believed to be the first injury that triggers a variety of inflammatory reactions7. Baregamian et al. found significant tissue hypoxia in the intestinal mucosa in the NEC model8. Apoptosis is critical in NEC pathology, contributing to intestinal epithelial damage and tissue necrosis9,10. Hypoxia can trigger apoptosis by activating specific pathways, and the balance between pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins determines whether a cell undergoes apoptosis or survives11,12.

Intestinal microcirculation is primarily regulated by nitric oxide (NO) produced through endothelial NO synthase (eNOS) activity13,14. Hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) is a transcription factor that regulates the cellular response to hypoxia and is essential for maintaining oxygen homeostasis.HIF-1 controls NO production for cellular adaptation to hypoxia15. HIF-1 is a heterodimer consisting of α and β subunits. Under normal oxygen levels, HIF-1α is rapidly degraded. However, when exposed to hypoxia, the HIF-1α is stabilized and forms a heterodimer with HIF-1β16–18. Additionally, upregulated HIF-1α expression suggested that hypoxia-induced intestinal cell damage and HIF-1α activation regulates critical cellular responses during NEC8. Zhang et al. observed that mice lacking HIF-1α in the intestinal epithelium exhibited an upregulation of the TLR4 signalling pathway in NEC19. These mice showed increased susceptibility to NEC-induced intestinal injury, characterized by elevated oxidative stress, increased apoptosis, and reduced cellular proliferation. These findings highlight the critical role of intestinal epithelial HIF-1α in modulating NEC pathology. Disturbances in redox chemistry have been reported to play a critical role in the pathogenesis of NEC, with hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) contributing to oxidative stress in this process20.

Apoptosis involves the activation of cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific proteases known as caspases, with caspase-3 acting as a key effector in the execution phase of programmed cell death. Once caspase-3 is activated, the apoptotic process becomes irreversible21. Hypoxia can trigger apoptosis by activating specific pathways, and the balance between proapoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins determines whether a cell undergoes apoptosis or survives11,12. Apoptosis plays a central role in the pathogenesis of NEC, contributing to intestinal epithelial damage and tissue necrosis9,10. In a neonatal rat model, it has been shown that the increase in the rate of epithelial apoptosis not only precedes subsequent gross intestinal wall necrosis but is also an underlying cause10. Apoptosis is associated with the upregulation of proapoptotic proteins and the downregulation of antiapoptotic proteins. The balance of Bax and Bcl-2 plays an important role in the process of apoptosis; furthermore, the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio can increase the Caspase-3 expression22.

Sildenafil citrate, a phosphodiesterase-5 (PDE-5) inhibitor, prevents the breakdown of 3′, 5′-cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP), a key regulator of smooth muscle relaxation23. The accumulation of cGMP enhances nitric oxide (NO) production via the NO/cGMP pathway, thereby improving intestinal perfusion. Experimental studies have shown that sildenafil improves intestinal microcirculatory function and attenuates apoptosis by modulating NO synthesis and the NO–cGMP signaling pathway24. Moreover, investigations in various tissues and animal models indicate that sildenafil may confer direct protection against necrosis and apoptosis following ischemia–reperfusion injury through NO-dependent mechanisms24–27. In addition, sildenafil has been reported to exert protective effects in other experimental models of gastrointestinal injury; for instance, in a rat model of ethanol-induced gastric injury, it conferred protection through activation of the NO/cGMP/KATP pathway, an NO-dependent process abolished by pretreatment with the nonselective NOS inhibitor L-NAME28. Beyond NO/cGMP-mediated vasodilation, sildenafil has also demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in pulmonary arterial hypertension—lowering serum 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and improving phospholipid fatty-acid profiles29.

Sildenafil remains an alternative treatment option for persistent pulmonary hypertension, particularly in settings of iNO non-response or limited access to the newborn. Given its mechanism of action, which lowers pulmonary arterial pressure, it concerns that sildenafil may cause severe systemic hypotension and severe hemodynamic instability have not been proven. While there is debate about whether sildenafil affects the normal development of visual function in premature infants through its effect on PDE6 receptors in the eye, there is no proven evidence of a direct link between sildenafil treatment and ocular complications30–32.

In this context, the present study aimed to investigate the protective effects of sildenafil pretreatment on intestinal apoptosis in a rat model of hypoxia-induced intestinal ischemic injury. Specifically, we sought to evaluate its anti-apoptotic potential in preserving intestinal epithelial integrity, a key pathogenic component of necrotizing enterocolitis.

Methods

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2022/01–05) and conformed to the standards of the Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare regarding the housing and medical care of Wistar rats used in research. This study was conducted in compliance with relevant guidelines and regulations and is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Fourteen-day-old Wistar rat pups were obtained from the Çanakkale Onsekiz Mart University Experimental Research Application and Research Centre (ÇOMUDAM). The experimental pups were separated from their mothers and placed in cages under optimal laboratory conditions (temperature: 20 ± 2 °C; humidity: 50%; light/dark cycle: 12/12 hours). All pups were individually numbered and randomly assigned to 6 groups without regard to gender.

Drug administration

Sildenafil citrate salt (Sigma-Aldrich, CAS No. 171599-83-0) was used as an active compound. The powder was weighed on a precision balance under sterile conditions and dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a concentration of 2 mg/mL to ensure complete dissolution, as sildenafil is poorly soluble in aqueous isotonic solutions (e.g., phosphate-buffered saline). Intraperitoneal (IP) administration was selected to ensure reliable and reproducible systemic exposure33.

The pups were randomly assigned to six groups: control (n = 7, no intervention), Intestinal ischemia (n = 8, hypoxia–hypothermia–induced NEC-like intestinal injury without sildenafil), Vehicle control (saline+DMSO) (n = 15, 10 µL DMSO in saline IP), Sil_1mg (n = 15, 1 mg/kg/day sildenafil in 10 µL DMSO IP), Sil_5mg (n = 15, 5 mg/kg/day sildenafil in 10 µL DMSO IP), and Sil_10mg (n = 15, 10 mg/kg/day sildenafil in 10 µL DMSO IP). All administrations were performed once daily for 4 days.

Experimental design-Intestinal Ischemia Induction and tissue collection

An experimental intestinal ischemia model, predominantly driven by hypoxia-induced ischemic injury, was employed. The induction protocol was adapted from previously published models34,35. At 18 days of age, rat pups in the experimental groups were subjected to twice-daily hypoxia (100% nitrogen exposure for 60 s), immediately followed by cold stress at 4 °C for 10 min. This regime continued for 48 h. Unlike classical neonatal NEC models that incorporate enteral feeding and bacterial exposure, the present protocol focused on hypoxia- and hypothermia-induced intestinal ischemic injury to allow controlled evaluation of epithelial damage and apoptosis. This model was specifically selected to investigate ischemia-driven epithelial injury and apoptotic pathways, which represent a central pathogenic mechanism in NEC. After the experimental period, pups were anesthetized with an intramuscular injection of ketamine (70 mg/kg) and xylazine (7 mg/kg). A midline laparotomy was then performed, the abdomen was explored, and the ileum was carefully dissected and removed. At the end of the protocol, all pups were euthanized by exsanguination via transdiaphragmatic cardiac puncture under deep ketamine/xylazine anesthesia, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines.

The intestinal lumen was flushed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). Each ileal sample was divided into two portions: one portion was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent gene expression analysis; the other portion was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation.

Histopathological examination

Intestinal tissue samples were fixed with 4% formalin, embedded in a paraffin dispenser (Leica, HistocoreArcadia), and 4–5 μm thick sections were obtained using a semi-motorised microtome (Leica, RM2245) were stained with Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) (MerckMilipore) and Masson trichrome (MT) (TK, EpiFarma, Biooptica). The prepared sections were used for histochemical and immunohistochemical labelling of Caspase-3. Two blind observers scored morphological changes in the intestinal epithelium according to the modified scoring system used by Caplan et al.36. The definition of each histological grade was as follows: 0-normal intestine, 1-mild epithelial shedding in the intestinal mucosa, 2-moderate villus damage, 3-severe villus damage, and 4-transmural necrosis.

Caspase-3 immunolabeling

Immunohistochemical detection of apoptosis-related protein, Caspase-3, was carried out on 4–5-mm-thick deparaffinized sections. Sections were hydrated in graded ethanol and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For antigen retrieval, sections were placed in citrate buffer (0.01 M, pH 6.0; Thermo Scientific) and boiled in a microwave oven at 700 W for 5 min. After cooling to room temperature, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 5 min. A hydrophobic barrier pen was then used to create a barrier around the tissue. Non-specific binding was blocked with normal serum, and sections were incubated with primary antibody (Elabscience, E-AB-63510) in a humid chamber at 37 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, sections were incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody, followed by streptavidin, for 30 min at room temperature. 3-Amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) was used as the chromogen, and Mayer’s hematoxylin was applied as the counterstain.

The obtained preparations were evaluated using an Olympus BX40 microscope (Olympus, Japan) equipped with a camera attachment and an image analysis program. Caspase-3 immunoreactivity was evaluated as previously described37. Staining distribution was scored as 0.1 (≤ 25%), 0.4 (26–50%), 0.6 (51–75%), and 0.9 (76–100%). Staining intensity was scored as 0 (no staining), 0.5 (very weak), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (very strong). The final immunohistochemical score was calculated as distribution × intensity.

Determination of HIF1-α, Bcl-2, Bax gene expressions, and Bax/Bcl-2 ratio

RNA isolation and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA isolation from the samples was performed using a combination of TRIzol™ Reagent and Purelink RNA mini kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). The purity of the collected RNAs was determined by 260/280 nm (1.8–2.0.8.0 absorption ratio) using an Optizen NanoQ microvolume spectrophotometer (Mecasys, South Korea). Complementary DNA synthesis was performed to enable the RNAs obtained after the synchronisation process to be amplified by PCR. A High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Life Technologies, USA) was used at this stage. The cDNAs obtained were stored at – 20 °C for ongoing studies.

RT-PCR to detect mRNA expression

cDNAs were obtained from RNAs isolated and amplified in qRT-PCR by the Power Sybeer Green qPCR MasterMix (Thermo, USA) protocol and were amplified using the Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR device. Ct values of the peaks obtained during amplification were used to determine gene expression. Several reports indicate that GAPDH and beta-actin (ACTB) expression varies across tissues, during cell proliferation, throughout development, and during hypoxia/serum/growth factor stimulation. Therefore, we considered the simultaneous use of the two housekeeping genes to be appropriate. With endogenous control, expression levels of HIF1-α, Bcl-2, and Bax were computed using automatic output by RT-PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primers used to investigate the changes in the expression of these genes were given in order 5′−3′ (Table 1). For the qRT-PCR reaction to take place, step 1: Enzyme activation: 95 °C–10 min; step 2: Denaturation: 95 °C–15 s; Primer Annealing-Chain extension: 60 °C–1 min; step 3: Melting curve: 95 °C–15 s, 60 °C–1 min, and 95 °C–15 s. The Ct values of the peaks obtained during the amplification process were used to determine gene expression, and gene expression was calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Parametric tests were applied for quantitative gene expression data obtained by the 2−∆∆Ct method in RT-PCR assays. Differences between group means were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s post hoc tests, as appropriate. For non-normally distributed data, including histopathological injury scores, the Kruskal–Wallis’s test was used, followed by pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Caspase-3 immunohistochemical scores were similarly analyzed using non-parametric tests. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power version 3.1. For one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and repeated ANOVA comparison (number of repetitions = 3), the effect size (“f”) was determined to be 0.25, the Type I error limit was 0.05, and the Type II error limit was 0.853738. A total sample size of 35 subjects was required to achieve a statistical power of 0.90. Thus, the number of surviving experimental animals at the end of the experiment was 33, and the statistical power was 0.90, close to the confidence interval, and within acceptable limits.

Correlation between histological injury scores and caspase-3 immunoreactivity was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation, selected as the primary method due to non-normal data distribution. For completeness, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were also calculated. All analyses were two-tailed with α = 0.05 and performed using SPSS v20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Sildenafil attenuates intestinal injury

Histopathological changes in intestinal tissue were determined by hematoxylin-eosin and Masson trichrome staining. Administration of a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor reduced the incidence and severity of hypoxia-induced intestinal ischemic injury. Histopathological examination showed significant villous damage, sometimes severe in the intestinal ischemia group, whereas moderate villous damage and epithelial shedding were observed in the Sil_1 group. The incidence of intestinal injury (defined as an injury score ≥ 2) was significantly higher in the intestinal ischemia group compared to the control group (2.89 ± 0.5 and 0.18 ± 0.4, respectively) (p < 0.0001). The Sil_1 group demonstrated partial protection, with a mean score of 2.46 ± 0.6, while the Sil_5 and Sil_10 groups showed further reduction in injury (1.68 ± 0.6 and 1.06 ± 0.4, respectively). The Sil_10 group had the lowest injury score among the experimental groups, which was significantly lower than that of the intestinal ischemia group (1.06 ± 0.4) (p < 0.0001). Masson’s trichrome staining further confirmed that the connective tissue structure of the mucosa and submucosa layers was preserved to a greater extent in the Sil_10 group compared to the intestinal ischemia group (Fig. 2).

Sildenafil reduces Caspase-3 immunoreactivity

Immunohistochemical labelling of Caspase-3, a specific marker of apoptosis, was performed to evaluate apoptotic changes in the ileum of rat pups in all experimental groups (Fig. 3). Caspase-3 immunoreactivity was predominantly localized in villus enterocytes and, to a lesser extent, in the superficial epithelium of the intestinal crypts. The NEC group exhibited significantly higher Caspase-3 positivity compared to the control group (1.38 ± 0.40, 0.001 ± 0.003, respectively) (p < 0.0001). Sildenafil treatment reduced apoptotic activity in a dose-dependent manner: mean immunoreactivity scores were 0.75 ± 0.382 in the Sil_1 group, 0.504 ± 0.329 in the Sil_5 group, and 0.216 ± 0.145 in the Sil_10 group. Among sildenafil treated groups, Sil_10 exhibited the lowest Caspase-3 activity, with values approaching those of the control group (p < 0.0001 vs. NEC) (Fig. 3).

Histological injury scores showed a strong positive correlation with caspase-3 immunoreactivity (Spearman’s ρ = 0.795, p < 0.001; Pearson’s r = 0.756, 95% CI: 0.65–0.83; N = 87), indicating that increasing mucosal injury was closely associated with enhanced apoptotic labelling. These findings indicate that increased histological injury was associated with higher caspase-3 expression, whereas sildenafil administration attenuated this apoptotic activity and preserved epithelial integrity.

Hypoxia-induced intestinal ischemia upregulates HIF-1α expression

It was found that HIF1-α gene expression was regulated at the highest level in the intestinal ischemia group, while it was regulated at the lowest level in the Sil_10 group, similar to the control group. This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.0001). The HIF1-α gene expression level in the experimental group was approximately 7 times higher than in the Sil_1 group and 13 times higher than in the Sil_5 group. (p < 0.05 and p ≤ 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 4). Marked upregulation of HIF-1α during intestinal ischemia supports a central role for hypoxia-driven HIF-1α activation in modulating cellular pathways involved in epithelial injury and apoptosis.

Effect of Sildenafil on the expression of pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic gene mRNA

To further assess apoptosis in the ileum subjected to hypoxia-induced intestinal ischemic injury, real-time PCR analysis was performed to quantify the expression levels of the pro-apoptotic gene Bax and the anti-apoptotic gene Bcl-2 (Fig. 5). Bax mRNA expression was highest in the intestinal ischemia group and was significantly downregulated in the sildenafil-treated groups, particularly in the Sil_5 and Sil_10 groups (p < 0.0001). Conversely, Bcl-2 mRNA expression was significantly increased in the Sil_10 group compared with the intestinal ischemia group (p < 0.0001). Analysis of the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio revealed a marked increase in the intestinal ischemia group, whereas this ratio was significantly reduced in all sildenafil-treated groups, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the Sil_10 group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5).

When we evaluated the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio to assess shifts in the balance of apoptotic genes that promote cell survival, we found a significant increase in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio within the intestinal ischemia group. In contrast, this ratio was significantly lower in all sildenafil-treated groups, particularly in the Sil_10 group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5). Additionally, the Sil_10 group exhibited a substantially lower Bax/Bcl-2 ratio than the Sil_1 and Sil_5 groups (p < 0.0005 and p < 0.044, respectively). These results suggest that sildenafil citrate, a PDE-5 inhibitor, shifts the balance of apoptotic proteins toward enhanced cell survival, indicating its potential anti-apoptotic effects. This increase in Bcl-2 was demonstrated in our study as a result of Sildenafil’s anti-apoptotic effect.

Discussion

NEC remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in premature infants; however, its precise etiology remains incompletely understood, and both prevention and management continue to present challenges. Histopathologically, NEC is characterized by a spectrum of intestinal injury ranging from mucosal damage to full-thickness necrosis and perforation39. Although NEC is a multifactorial disease involving feeding patterns, bacterial colonization, and hypoxic stress, it is widely accepted that intestinal ischemia represents a central initiating mechanism.

Accordingly, the present study employed a hypoxia–hypothermia–based experimental model to specifically investigate ischemia-driven epithelial injury and apoptosis and is therefore best described as an NEC-like intestinal injury model. Consistent with previous experimental studies, our histopathological evaluation demonstrated marked villous damage and epithelial disruption in the NEC group, reflecting characteristic features of NEC-associated intestinal injury.

Although the role of sildenafil in NEC has not been extensively explored, its protective effects against ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury and its capacity to preserve intestinal epithelial integrity have been well documented in experimental settings40–42. While Moreira et al. reported that sildenafil could not prevent intestinal injuries in a classical experimental NEC model43, our findings demonstrate that sildenafil pretreatment markedly attenuated ischemia-induced villous damage and epithelial shedding. These results suggest that sildenafil may preserve intestinal structural integrity by protecting epithelial and connective tissue components under ischemic conditions. Hunter et al. reported that sildenafil alleviated intestinal damage in experimental NEC through eNOS-independent mechanisms, and İpek et al. demonstrated significant anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects of sildenafil in rat-induced NEC44,45.

Mitochondria are important sources of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). When mitophagy, which maintains cellular homeostasis by selectively removing dysfunctional mitochondria, is disrupted, excessive inflammation and pathological damage can be triggered by the accumulation of mitochondrial DAMPs. Increasing evidence links defective mitophagy to the pathogenesis of NEC, and the role of mitophagy-related genes in NEC is being actively investigated46,47.

HIF-1α is a central regulator of cellular adaptation to hypoxic stress, playing a crucial role in hypoxia-induced intestinal epithelial injury11,48,49. Increased HIF-1α expression under hypoxic conditions has been shown to contribute to the maintenance of intestinal epithelial homeostasis by initiating mitophagy through activation of BCL2/adenovirus E1B 19-kDa protein-interacting protein 3-like (BNIP3L) signaling50. In addition, HIF-1α has been identified as a differentially expressed gene associated with mitophagy, and its expression has been significantly increased in hypoxia-induced epithelial damage models50.

Zhang et al. demonstrated that deficiency of epithelial HIF-1α increases susceptibility to apoptosis through enhanced TLR4 signaling, underscoring a protective role for epithelial HIF-1α during intestinal ischemic stress19. However, the biological effects of HIF-1α are highly context-dependent and may vary according to cellular conditions and post-translational modifications. Notably, dephosphorylated HIF-1α has been shown to promote apoptosis by binding to and stabilizing p53, indicating a dual and context-specific role in epithelial injury and survival51. In line with this complexity, Baregamian et al. reported that suppression of intestinal HIF-1α expression can confer protection against ischemic intestinal injury8.

In the present study, HIF-1α expression was markedly upregulated in the intestinal ischemia group compared with controls, supporting the role of hypoxia-induced ischemic stress in activating HIF-1α–mediated cellular responses. We found that sildenafil administered at 5 and 10 mg/kg/day significantly inhibited HIF-1α expression (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that sildenafil pretreatment may play a role in attenuating HIF-1α upregulation, with HIF-1α potentially contributing to its anti-apoptotic effects and the regulation of hypoxia-induced intestinal injury.

Our study demonstrated a significant increase in Caspase-3 immunoreactivity in enterocytes from the intestinal ischemia group, indicating heightened apoptotic activity within the intestinal epithelium. This finding is consistent with the established pathophysiology of ischemia-associated intestinal injury, which involves extensive epithelial cell death, contributing to mucosal injury and barrier dysfunction8,52. Caspase-3, a central executioner of apoptosis, plays a pivotal role in the terminal phase of programmed cell death; once apoptotic signaling reaches the level of caspase-3 activation, cellular recovery is no longer possible21. In the present study, sildenafil administration significantly reduced Caspase-3 immunoreactivity in enterocytes, These findings suggest that sildenafil exerts a protective effect by attenuating apoptosis, in line with previous reports showing that phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors suppress apoptotic signaling through enhancement of nitric oxide-mediated pathways24,25.

The balance between pro-apoptotic Bax and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 proteins is a critical determinant of apoptosis. Overexpression of Bcl-2 suppresses cell death, whereas an increased Bax/Bcl-2 ratio enhances susceptibility to apoptosis and promotes caspase-3 activation21,22. Evidence from human neonates suggests that an imbalance between pro-apoptotic Bax expression and anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 expression may contribute to ischemia-associated intestinal epithelial injury53. In the present study, rat pups subjected to intestinal ischemia exhibited markedly increased Bax mRNA expression. In contrast, sildenafil administration suppressed Bax expression while significantly upregulating Bcl-2 expression, particularly in the Sil_10 group, resulting in a substantially reduced Bax/Bcl-2 ratio. These findings indicate that sildenafil shifts the apoptotic balance toward enhanced cell survival, consistent with its observed anti-apoptotic effects in ischemic intestinal tissue.

Experimental studies in different ischemic injury models have demonstrated that sildenafil pretreatment exerts significant anti-apoptotic effects. In a rat model of renal ischemia–reperfusion injury, sildenafil pretreatment reduced apoptosis by increasing inducible and endothelial nitric oxide synthase (iNOS and eNOS) expression and decreasing the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio54. Similarly, Zhang et al. reported that sildenafil protected against post-resuscitation myocardial dysfunction by attenuating apoptosis, as evidenced by increased iNOS and eNOS expression together with reduced caspase-3 activity and a lower Bax/Bcl-2 ratio24. The marked reduction in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio induced by sildenafil is therefore considered a key mechanism underlying its anti-apoptotic effects.

Das et al. further provided compelling evidence for the role of NO signalling in sildenafil’s protective effects, showing that the absence of Bcl-2 upregulation in iNOS knockout mouse myocytes strongly supports a direct anti-necrotic and anti-apoptotic action of sildenafil via the NO pathway55. This mechanism, well-documented in cardiomyocytes, may similarly contribute to the protective effects of sildenafil observed in the intestinal epithelium in the intestine under ischemic conditions. Consistent with these findings, our results demonstrate that sildenafil significantly attenuates apoptosis in the intestinal tissue of rat pups subjected to hypoxia-induced intestinal ischemia. In contrast to the pronounced pro-apoptotic effects of ischemic stress, sildenafil pretreatment rendered the intestinal epithelium more resistant to apoptosis, further supporting its protective role in ischemia-driven intestinal injury.

At enrollment, rat pups weighed 24.54 ± 3.50 g (range, 16.4–33.4 g). Of the 75 randomized pups, 33 survived to the experimental endpoint (overall mortality 56%): control (n = 3), intestinal ischemia (n = 3), vehicle control (n = 6), Sil_1 (n = 8), Sil_5 (n = 6), and Sil_10 (n = 7). This mortality exceeds rates reported in some studies employing comparable hypoxia-based intestinal injury protocols42. Mortality occurred predominantly during the hypoxia challenge. Exposure to 100% N₂ triggered bradycardia within 20 s in many pups; although transient resuscitation was often successful, a substantial proportion succumbed at this stage. Consequently, pups could not be consistently exposed to 100% nitrogen hypoxia for the full 60–75 s specified in previously described protocols18,19. Although very small pups (1–3 g) are frequently used in neonatal experimental models, our experience indicates that consistent gavage feeding at these weights is technically challenging and may compromise both procedural consistency and animal welfare43.

A limitation of the present study is that the experimental model does not fully recapitulate the classical neonatal NEC model, which involves enteral feeding, bacterial exposure, and hypoxia. Instead, our model primarily reflects hypoxia- and hypothermia-induced intestinal ischemic injury, representing an ischemia-dominant, NEC-like condition. While this approach allows controlled investigation of apoptosis and epithelial injury, future studies incorporating enteral feeding and bacterial exposure will be necessary to enhance translational relevance.

A further limitation of our study is that Masson’s trichrome staining could only be evaluated qualitatively, as our current image analysis software did not allow quantitative collagen density measurements. Nevertheless, the representative images clearly demonstrated preserved connective tissue architecture in the sildenafil groups. In addition, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was not performed, as the experimental design was not primarily intended to assess survival outcomes. Future studies incorporating survival analysis may provide further insight into the potential protective effects of sildenafil under ischemic intestinal injury.

Although this simplified model enabled reproducible induction of hypoxia-driven intestinal ischemic injury, it does not fully capture the multifactorial pathogenesis of necrotizing enterocolitis observed in human neonates. Despite this limitation, the model reliably produced intestinal injury, allowing controlled evaluation of apoptosis-related mechanisms and the protective effects of sildenafil.

Another limitation is the absence of Western blot or other quantitative protein assays to validate apoptotic marker expression at the protein level. Instead, apoptosis was assessed using immunohistochemical caspase-3 labeling in conjunction with gene expression analyses of Bax, Bcl-2, and HIF-1α, which consistently demonstrated the anti-apoptotic effects of sildenafil. Future studies with dedicated funding support will be required to confirm these findings using quantitative protein-based approaches.

High mortality in this model may have limited statistical power and introduced survivor bias, thereby constraining generalizability. Although hypoxia exposure was curtailed when physiological instability occurred, mortality remained substantial, and Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was not prespecified in the experimental design. Future studies should prospectively incorporate survival endpoints and graded-severity injury protocols to improve reproducibility and enhance translational relevance.

Although sildenafil has not yet been clinically applied for the prevention or treatment of NEC, our findings provide experimental evidence supporting its potential role in modulating key pathogenic mechanisms. These results underscore the need for further preclinical validation and carefully designed clinical studies to evaluate the safety and efficacy of sildenafil in this context.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that sildenafil attenuates hypoxia-induced, ischemia-dominant intestinal injury by reducing apoptotic signaling, as evidenced by a decreased Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and suppression of HIF-1α expression. These molecular effects were accompanied by preservation of intestinal morphology and reduced epithelial apoptosis. Collectively, our findings provide novel histological and molecular evidence supporting the potential protective role of sildenafil in ischemia-driven, NEC-like intestinal injury and suggest that modulation of hypoxia- and apoptosis-related pathways may represent a promising therapeutic strategy warranting further investigation.
