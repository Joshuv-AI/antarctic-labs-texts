Antiproteinuric effect of SGLT2 inhibitors in non-diabetic glomerulopathies is dependent on body mass index
Authors: Maria J Vargas-Brochero, Ilario Russo, Tommaso Mazzierli, Anila Cara, Gian Marco Berti, Joaquim Milheiro, Charat Thongprayoon, Marco Allinovi, Chiara Somma, Valentina Raglianti, Emanuele D’Arpino, Ladan Zand, Sara Nuñez-Delgado, Maria Jose Soler, Sanjeev Sethi, Eduardo Gutiérrez, Manuel Praga, Fernando Caravaca-Fontan, Paola Romagnani, Fernando C Fervenza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSodium–glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) are emerging as an essential part of the standard of care for proteinuria in patients with chronic kidney disease. To date, no study has specifically evaluated the effects of body mass index (BMI) on the antiproteinuric efficacy of SGLT2is. Here we report the impact of BMI on the antiproteinuric efficacy of SGLT2is in non-diabetic patients with glomerular diseases.MethodsThis is a retrospective, multicentre, international observational cohort study that included non-diabetic patients with biopsy-proven glomerular disease and proteinuria >0.5 g/24 h who received SGLT2is between 2016 and 2023. Laboratory values, including proteinuria and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), were obtained at baseline and after 3–6 months. Changes in laboratory values over time were analysed using the paired signed-rank test.ResultsA total of 300 patients met the inclusion criteria. The median age was 51.82 years [interquartile range (IQR) 41–62.65], 64.7% were male and 92.7% were white. The most common glomerular disease was immunoglobulin A nephropathy (40.3%). The median eGFR was 52.26 ml/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 36.41–74.01), the median proteinuria was 1.60 g/24 h (IQR 1.15–2.91) and the median serum albumin was 4.08 g/dl (IQR 3.80–4.30). Proteinuria reduction after SGLT2i initiation was significant only in overweight and obese patients (P < .001 versus 0.18). Patients with normal BMI did not experience the expected early decrease in eGFR (P = .16).ConclusionsSGLT2is are ineffective in proteinuria reduction in patients with a BMI <25 kg/m2, which contrasts with the significant proteinuria reduction in overweight and obese patients with glomerulopathies.Keywords: body mass index, glomerular disease, proteinuria, sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors

INTRODUCTION

Sodium–glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2is) are emerging as an essential part of the new standard of care to decrease proteinuria, alongside renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors and lifestyle changes in patients with proteinuric chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1]. Large multicentre randomized controlled trials have shown that SGLT2i effectively reduces proteinuria and slows the progression of CKD regardless of diabetes status [2, 3]. Subgroup analysis of its effects in patients with immunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) enrolled into the DAPA-CKD trial and the EMPA-KIDNEY trial [2, 3] showed these agents are capable of reducing proteinuria compared with placebo by 26% in the DAPA-CKD trial and 15% in EMPA-KIDNEY trial [4]. However, the majority of the patients in these trials were diabetic, obese or overweight, with a median body mass index (BMI) of 29.4 and 29.7, respectively, and had significant CKD [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 43.2 ml/min/1.73 m2 in DAPA-CKD trial and 37.4 ml/min/1.73 m2 in the EMPA-KIDNEY trial], all situations associated with sodium retention, volume expansion and glomerular capillary hypertension, a key driver for kidney disease progression [5–7]; in addition, a significant number of patients enrolled in these trials showed no effect on proteinuria [8]. Furthermore, the DIAMOND trial (NCT03190694), a small randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial testing the effect of dapagliflozin on proteinuria in non-diabetic patients with CKD, did not find a significant reduction in proteinuria in a cohort with a mean BMI of 28 and an eGFR of 58.2 ml/min/1.73 m2, after a 6-week treatment period [9]. SGLT2i prevents proximal tubules from reabsorbing glucose, resulting in natriuresis and osmotic diuresis. Consequently, this reduces glomerular capillary hypertension, which is commonly present in volume-expanded patients like obese and diabetic patients [10]. Glomerular capillary hypertension is linked to proteinuria and progressive kidney damage [11]. Indeed, the antiproteinuric efficacy of SGLT2i has been positively correlated with higher BMI in patients with glomerular diseases [12]. Whether SGLT2is are effective in patients who are non-diabetic, have normal BMI, are not volume expanded and in whom kidney disease progression is not linked to glomerular capillary hypertension, is unknown, since conditions like Alport syndrome, genetic FSGS, lean individuals with IgAN and other autoimmune glomerular diseases were underrepresented in previous studies. The aim of the study was to evaluate the impact of BMI on the antiproteinuric efficacy of SGLT2is in non-diabetic patients with glomerular diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

This is a retrospective, multicentre, international observational cohort study. All consecutive patients with a biopsy-proven glomerular disease who were prescribed SGLT2is between 1 January 2016 and 31 December 2023 in Spain, Italy and the USA were evaluated. Data were retrospectively abstracted from electronic medical records. Baseline was defined as the time of the initiation of SGLT2i. Laboratory values, including proteinuria, serum creatinine and eGFR, were obtained at baseline and after 3–6 months.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the participating centres. The Mayo Clinic database (Mayo cohort) was obtained from all three sites in the USA (Minnesota, Florida and Arizona). Patients in Spain (Spanish cohort) were originally collected from 32 nephrology departments belonging to the Spanish Group for the Study of Glomerular Diseases (GLOSEN), although for this study the large majority came from Hospital Universitario 12 de Octubre (Madrid) and Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron (Barcelona). Patients in Italy (Italian cohort) were collected from three sites in Florence.

Eligibility criteria

Patients who met the following conditions were included in the study: patients ≥18 years of age with a biopsy-proven glomerular disease, absence of diabetes mellitus, proteinuria >0.5 g/24 h at the time of initiation of treatment despite optimized blood pressure (BP) control with RAAS inhibition, no concomitant use of induction immunosuppression therapy (use of prednisone >20 mg/day, rituximab or cyclophosphamide in the previous 6 months; stable maintenance immunosuppression was allowed) and stable proteinuria for at least 3 months prior to SGLT2i initiation. Patients with active neoplasia, monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance, IgA vasculitis, no follow-up available after initiation of therapy or kidney transplant were excluded.

Definitions and outcomes

The GFR was estimated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation (2021). Proteinuria was evaluated by 24-h urine collection or with a protein:creatinine ratio (mg/mg). Both eGFR and proteinuria were collected at baseline and at 3–6 months. Additionally, serum albumin and BMI were evaluated after 3–6 months of therapy with SGLT2i. Proteinuria 3–6 months before initiation of therapy was assessed to corroborate stable or increasing values before treatment with SGLT2i. We also evaluated changes in proteinuria, BMI and albumin after treatment in all patients.

Systolic and diastolic BP were evaluated prior to SGLT2i initiation and no changes in antihypertensive therapy were made at the time of SGLT2i initiation. Weight status was classified based on BMI in three categories: regular (BMI <25 kg/m2), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m2) and obese (≥30 kg/m2).

Kidney biopsy was evaluated for the presence of glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA), categorized as <10%, 10–25%, 26–50% or >50%. Additionally, we assessed the presence or absence of arteriosclerosis, as extracted from the original biopsy report when available.

The doses of SGLT2i were dapagliflozin 10 mg/day, empagliflozin 10 mg/day and canagliflozin 100 mg/day. Missing data were addressed by reporting the number of available data points for each variable when applicable.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were summarized using mean [standard deviation (SD)] for normally distributed data and median [interquartile range (IQR)] for non-normally distributed data; for categorical variables, data were summarized using number (%). Pearson’s chi-squared test was used to compare categorical variables between groups; for comparison of continuous variables between groups, analysis of variance or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used depending on the number of compared groups and distribution of variables. Changes in laboratory values over time were analysed using the paired t-test and the signed-rank test, depending on the distribution. Correlations between changes in eGFR and changes in proteinuria were evaluated between the continuous variables using Pearson or Spearman methods, depending on the normality of the data.

To explore the relationship between BMI and proteinuria response, we conducted a bivariate analysis stratified by BMI categories (<25, 25–29.9 and ≥30 kg/m2). Unadjusted risk ratios (RRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to estimate the relative likelihood of achieving a proteinuria response, using the BMI <25 group as the reference category. Additionally, we evaluated the association between BMI and proteinuria response stratified by baseline eGFR, categorized as <60 and ≥60 ml/min/1.73 m2. Within each eGFR stratum, we also compared the proportion of patients achieving a ≥30% reduction in proteinuria across BMI categories using chi-squared tests.

The multivariate analyses were performed using multiple linear regression models. In the first model, the change in eGFR was used as the dependent variable to explore associations with clinical and histological parameters. In the second and primary model, the change in proteinuria (dependent variable) was analysed as the main outcome of interest to evaluate the relationship between the change in proteinuria (dependent variable) and various biopsy parameters (independent variables). Due to non-normality, the dependent variables were log transformed. The covariates included in the model were BMI category, race, change in eGFR (for the proteinuria model), change in BMI after 6 months and biopsy parameters.

We also performed subgroup analyses based on baseline kidney function. Patients were divided into two groups according to eGFR: <60 and ≥60 ml/min/1.73 m2.

RESULTS

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 1295 patients were started on a SGLT2i during the study period. Patients with biopsy-proven glomerular disease were screened, of which 300 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Baseline clinical characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The mean age at the time of SGLT2i initiation was 51.41 years (SD 14.94), 194 (64.7%) patients were male and 278 (92.7%) patients were white, with the second most common ethnicity being Asian, with 9 (3%) patients. The median BMI for the entire cohort was 28.07 kg/m2 (IQR 24.95–31.99). Among the cohorts, 121 (40.3%) patients had IgAN and 107 (35.7%) had an FSGS lesion [presumed primary FSGS (pFSGS) in 7 (2.3%), secondary FSGS (sFSGS) in 69 (23.0%) and genetic FSGS (gFSGS) in 31 (10.3%), with collagen IV mutations as the most common cause]. The mean systolic BP was 128.01 mmHg (SD 13.60) and the median diastolic BP was 77 mmHg (IQR 70–83). Hypertension was present in 67% of patients. With the exception of four patients who were intolerant because of hypotension, all patients were on renin–angiotensin system inhibition at baseline. The median serum creatinine was 1.48 mg/dl (IQR 1.15–2.02) and the median eGFR was 52.26 ml/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 36.41–74.01). The median proteinuria was 1.60 g/24 h (IQR 1.15–2.91) and the median serum albumin was 4.08 g/dl (IQR 3.80–4.30).

Biopsy characteristics

We evaluated the percentage of glomerulosclerosis, IFTA and arteriolosclerosis, with no differences observed between the groups (Supplementary Table S1). The majority of patients had a glomerulosclerosis of <10% and IFTA of 10–25%, with arteriolosclerosis at 47%, with no statistically significant difference between the BMI strata.

Proteinuria response

Overall, 170 patients (56.7%) achieved proteinuria response. When stratified by BMI, 24 of 76 (31.6%) patients with a BMI <25, 78 of 121 (64.5%) with a BMI of 25–29.9 and 68 of 103 (66%) with a BMI ≥30 achieved this response. The difference among BMI strata was statistically significant (P < .001).

Compared with patients with BMI <25, the relative risk (RR) of achieving a ≥30% reduction in proteinuria was significantly higher in those with a BMI of 25–29.9 [RR 2.04 (95% CI 1.39–3.00)] and a BMI ≥30 [RR 2.09 (95% CI 1.42–3.08)] (Table 3).

Laboratory changes

The proteinuria trends and eGFR before the initiation of SGLT2i by BMI strata are reported in Fig. 2. The data from 3–6 months before SGLT2i initiation show stable or increasing proteinuria, with no prior decline in proteinuria while patients were on the maximum tolerated dose of RAAS inhibition before the start of SGLT2i treatment. Kidney function and proteinuria over time are presented in Table 4. Significant decreases in eGFR [−2.35 ml/min/1.73 2 (95% CI −4.26 to −2.00)] and proteinuria [−0.57 g/24 h (95% CI −0.73 to −0.50)] were observed at 6−months in the entire cohort compared with baseline. However, in the subgroup analysis by BMI strata (i.e. <25, 25–29.9, ≥30), the reduction in proteinuria was statistically significant only in overweight and obese patients (Fig. 3a).

Kidney function data show that the expected early decrease in eGFR was not observed in patients with a normal BMI (Fig. 3b). However, the decrease in eGFR progressively increased and was statistically significant in the obese group. We also evaluated the change in eGFR and proteinuria individually among the three cohorts, with the same findings. Patients with BMI ≥30 kg/m2 experienced the greatest proteinuria reductions [Mayo: −0.82 g/24 h (95% CI −1.23 to −0.43), Spanish: −0.80 g/24 h (95% CI −1.58 to −0.65), Italian: −0.62 g/24 h (95% CI −0.95–0.47); all P < .001] alongside significant eGFR decreases [Mayo: −4.45 ml/min/1.73 m2 (95% CI −7.23 to −1.60), Spanish: −6.01 ml/min/1.73 m2 (95% CI −10.07 to −1.35), Italian: −6 ml/min/1.73 m2 (95% CI −10 to −0.5]). In contrast, those with a BMI <25 kg/m2 showed minimal or no significant changes in either parameter (Supplementary Tables S2–S4).

We evaluated whether changes in BP, body weight (BW) or BMI were associated with changes in eGFR over 6 months. The Spearman correlation between the change in eGFR and the change in BMI was r = −0.003, with a P-value of .96, indicating an extremely weak and non-significant association. Similarly, the correlation between the change in eGFR and the change in BW was r = 0.02 (P = .65). For BP, the correlation between the change in eGFR and the change in systolic BP was r = 0.07 (P = .25) and for diastolic BP it was r = −0.0006 (P = .99). These findings suggest that changes in BP, BW or BMI were not significantly associated with changes in eGFR at 6 months.

Changes in proteinuria and eGFR were weakly correlated (Spearman’s correlation coefficient = 0.12) with a P-value of .05. We evaluated the correlation for BMI <25, 25–29.9 and ≥30 with no significant correlation among the groups (P = .14, 0.71 and 0.30, respectively).

We performed a multivariate analysis using multiple linear regression with the change in proteinuria after 6 months as the dependent variable. We found that patients with a BMI of 25–29.9 or ≥30 had a significant reduction in proteinuria, even after adjusting for other covariates such as race, gender and biopsy findings. Additionally, among patients with available follow-up data, we evaluated changes in systolic and diastolic BP, BW and BMI at 6 months, analysing differences across BMI strata. In the overall cohort, there was a significant reduction in systolic BP [−2.0 mmHg (95% CI −4.0 to −1.0), P < .001], diastolic BP [−1.0 mmHg (95% CI −2.5 to −0.00)], P = .038], BMI [−0.27 kg/m2 (95% CI −0.48 to −0.13), P < .001] and BW [−0.50 kg (95% CI −1.2 to −0.20), P = .006] following 6 months of therapy. When stratified by BMI, patients with a BMI <25 experienced a slight increase in BMI [0 kg/m2 (95% CI 0.01–0.60), P = .04], with no significant change in BW [0 kg (95% CI −0.35–1.20), P = .30] and a borderline reduction in diastolic BP [−1.5 mmHg (95% CI −4.9–0.0), P = .058]. Individuals in the overweight category (BMI 25–29.9) showed significant reductions in systolic BP [−3.0 mmHg (95% CI −5.0 to −0.5), P = .02] and BMI [−0.30 kg/m2 (95% CI −0.50 to −0.01), P = .042], while changes in BW [−0.45 kg (95% CI −1.05–0.44), P = .36] and diastolic BP [0 mmHg (95% CI −2.5–1.5), P = .54] were not statistically significant. Among those with BMI ≥30, there was a pronounced reduction in BMI [−0.79 kg/m2 (95% CI −1.20 to −0.52), P < .001], BW [−2.0 kg (95% CI −2.9 to −1.25), P < .001] and systolic BP [−2.0 mmHg (95% CI −5.5 to −0.9), P = .01], while diastolic BP showed a modest but non-significant change [−2.0 mmHg (95% CI −4.0–1.0), P = .25] (Supplementary Table S5).

In the subgroup analysis per eGFR strata, we assessed changes in both eGFR and proteinuria from baseline to 6 months, stratified by eGFR categories (<60 versus ≥60 ml/min/1.73 m2). Among participants with an eGFR ≥60 ml/min/1.73 m2, the median reduction in eGFR was −0.5 ml/min/1.73 m2 (95% CI −0.75 to −0.39) and in proteinuria was −0.51 g/24 h (95% CI −0.75 to −0.39). In the eGFR <60 ml/min/1.73 m2 group, the median reduction in eGFR was −0.62 ml/min/1.73 m2 (95% CI −0.80 to −0.51) and in proteinuria was −0.61 g/24 h (95% CI −0.80 to −0.51). The changes were statistically significant between the two strata (Supplementary Table S6).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, the present study is the first to assess the impact of BMI on the antiproteinuric efficacy of SGLT2is in a cohort of non-diabetic patients with glomerular disease. The principal finding of the present study is the lack of a significant proteinuria reduction in patients with a BMI <25 kg/m2, which contrasts with the significant reductions observed in overweight and obese patients.

Randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the impact of SGLT2is in delaying progression of CKD and improving cardiovascular outcomes in patients with or without diabetes [13]. However, the majority of these trials included patients with diabetes mellitus who were overweight or obese, as well as patients with advanced CKD (eGFR <60 ml/min/1.73 m2), all conditions associated with intravascular volume expansion [5].

In these trials, treatment with SGLT2is was associated with a reduction in proteinuria in patients with glomerular disease [−13.6% (95% CI −24.9 to −6) in DAPA-CKD and −15% (95% CI −24 to –6) in EMPA-KIDNEY], but the wide CIs indicate that a percentage of patients showed no effect in proteinuria reduction. Furthermore, patients with no biopsy-proven glomerular disease were included [4]. The ability of SGLT2is to reduce proteinuria appears to be related to the reduction of intraglomerular pressure, which may prevent development of glomerulosclerosis. This is similar to the protective mechanism observed with the use of RAAS blockers, with the renal protection related to a reduction in proteinuria [14]. It should be noted that in both the DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY trials the majority of the patients were on RAAS blockers that had been started simultaneously or shortly before initiation of SGLT2is [2, 3]. Furthermore, patients with no albuminuria receiving RAAS inhibition do not show renoprotective effects, and subgroup analysis of the EMPA-KIDNEY trial in patients with no RAAS blockers suggests that the ability of SGLT2is to reduce proteinuria was not clearly evident [14]. Thus, whether the beneficial effect of SGLT2is in patients who are not albuminuric can be observed independent of simultaneous RAAS blockade requires further analysis [15, 16].

Previous studies have documented the association of obesity with hypervolaemia [10, 17] and the increase in renal sodium reabsorption and plasma volume gain due to overactivation of the systemic/renal sympathetic nervous system and the RAAS with secondary glomerular capillary hypertension [7, 18]. These observations have been corroborated by a recent epidemiological study in >6000 patients with type 2 diabetes [19]. In this study, the kidney protective effect was dependent on BMI, with the benefit augmented in individuals with a higher BMI and negligible in those with a BMI <25 [19].

Optimized BP control significantly impacts the degree of proteinuria reduction. Our cohort included patients with adequate BP control at the time of SGLT2i initiation, with 98% of the patients already on stable, maximum tolerated RAAS inhibition. This contrast with previous trials where the time between the initiation of the RAAS blocker and introduction of the SGLT2i is not precise (at least 4 weeks before screening in the DAPA-CKD trial and 8–12 weeks in the EMPA-KIDNEY trial), making it difficult to tease out the independent antiproteinuric effect of the SGLT2i from the RAAS blocker [3, 20].

Patients who are overweight or obese commonly have insulin resistance, glomerular capillary hypertension and many other alterations in their metabolic profile [21, 22]. SGLT2is improve glycaemic control and reduce glomerular capillary hypertension, which is associated with the development of proteinuria [23]. By blocking glucose and sodium reabsorption in the proximal tubule, SGLT2is induce natriuresis and fluid loss and reduce intraglomerular pressure, with previous studies demonstrating the impact of these drugs on volume status [24].

Thus it is plausible that the effect of SGLT2is is related to reducing glomerular capillary hypertension in patients who are volume expanded. Indeed, a previous study in patients with glomerular diseases reported that BMI correlated with a reduction in proteinuria [12]. Our study confirms that SGLT2is have no antiproteinuric effect in patients with a normal BMI, likely because these patients are not volume expanded. This also explains why we did not observe an early decrease in eGFR (an indicator of a reduction in intraglomerular pressure) and, as a consequence, no reduction in proteinuria in patients with a normal BMI [25, 26]. These observations are in line with those seen in a cohort of diabetes patients with a normal BMI were no changes in BMI were observed, and patients who were not on concomitant RAAS blockers showed no statistically significant changes in eGFR slope compared with the control group [27].

The biopsy findings across the strata of BMI showed no differences, and among the cohort, most patients presented a mild or moderate chronicity score, which is an important factor to consider due to the impact of chronic damage in kidney biopsy and proteinuria reduction.

Reductions in systolic BP and BMI were more pronounced in the overweight and obese groups, which may carry greater clinical relevance in these populations. Previous studies have documented the impact of SGLT2is on lowering systolic and diastolic BP as well as BW [28, 29]. Our findings are consistent with these observations, with reductions in these parameters in our cohort. Notably, we add the novel observation that such reductions were not evident among individuals with a normal BMI. It is also important to highlight that the majority of prior studies evaluating changes in systolic BP with SGLT2is have focused on patients with higher BMI levels.

Despite a growing body of literature on the cardiovascular effects of SGLT2is in individuals with diabetes, heart failure and/or CKD, clinical data in individuals without these comorbidities is limited to generally healthy individuals with high BMIs. However, recent data from a large Japanese cohort study of 2165 patients with type 2 diabetes on a SGLT2i reported that the reduction in cardiovascular events associated with SGLT2is was not statistically significant in patients with BMI <20 kg/m2 [HR 1.08 (95% CI 0.80–1.46)], in contrast to the consistent benefits observed in overweight and obese individuals [19]. In this study, effects of proteinuria were not evaluated. Previous studies on RAAS blockers in patients with or without diabetes have clearly demonstrated that both renal and cardiovascular protection is directly related to a reduction of proteinuria [30–34]. Whether adding an SGLT2i to patients with no diabetes and a normal BMI who show no reduction in proteinuria will translate into long-term cardiorenal protection is unknown. In our study, cardiovascular outcomes were not assessed, and we do not consider our dataset appropriate for evaluating such an endpoint.

Our study has several limitations. Its retrospective design and the absence of a control group not receiving SGLT2i limit the strength of causal inferences. Also, the inability of BMI to distinguish changes in fat and lean body mass must be considered. An increase in BMI into the ‘overweight’ category can occur in subjects without any true increase in adiposity, and this index cannot fully define the fat status nor distribution [22, 35], but this deficiency is also applicable to all studies, including the DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY trials. We acknowledge the absence of additional anthropometric measures (e.g. waist circumference, waist:hip ratio or waist:height ratio) as a limitation of our study. Furthermore, our study population was composed primarily of white individuals, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other racial and ethnic groups. Notably, this limitation is shared by most randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses of SGLT2is, which have predominantly included White and Asian participants [36]. We also lack data beyond the 6-month observation period and do not have information about glycaemic control due to the non-diabetic population and irregular checks. However, since the effects of SGLT2is are haemodynamic mediated, we do not expect that changes in proteinuria and eGFR that are not observed by 6 months will be observed by longer follow-up. Furthermore, if these findings are applicable to patients with a normal eGFR cannot be definitely answered by our study. Nevertheless, the study represents a well-characterized cohort of non-diabetic patients with biopsy-proven glomerular disease on established RAAS inhibition and with controlled BP. The study shows that SGLT2is are ineffective in reducing proteinuria in patients with a BMI <25 and nephrologists taking care of these patients should be aware of these findings. This is crucial in order to advocate for a personalized approach to treatment, challenging the ‘one-size-fits-all’ strategy.
