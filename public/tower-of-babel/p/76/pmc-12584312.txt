Effect of implant angulation on the trueness and precision of intraoral scanning and photogrammetry
Authors: Tezcan Muslu, Sevcan Kurtulmus-Yilmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of implant angulation on the trueness and precision of intraoral scanning (IOS) using different prefabricated auxiliary device (PAD) designs and to compare the results with two photogrammetry (PG) systems.Materials and methodsThree master models with 5 implants were fabricated to simulate edentulous maxillary arches, featuring implant configurations including all implants parallel, one implant angulated 25° mesially, and one implant angulated 25° distally. Five digital impression techniques were evaluated: IOS (Cerec Primescan) without PADs (IOS), with crown-shaped PADs (IOS-C), with laterally extended PADs (IOS-LE), and two PG systems (ICam4D G3: PG-I, OxoCore: PG-O). Reference data were acquired using a laboratory scanner, and implant center coordinates in both reference and test datasets were determined in Geomagic Control X for deviation calculations. Linear deviations (LD), angular deviations (AD), and root mean squared error (RMS) values were calculated to assess trueness and precision, and were statistically analyzed.ResultsSignificant accuracy differences were observed between the PG systems, with PG-I exhibiting the lowest RMS values (18 ± 3.27 μm) across all models and maintaining deviations within clinically acceptable thresholds (P<0.01). IOS-LE showed significantly lower RMS and AD values compared to IOS and IOS-C. Implant angulation negatively affected the trueness of IOS, IOS-C, and PG-O, particularly with distal angulation (P<0.01), while PG-I and IOS-LE remained unaffected (P > 0.05). Implant angulation did not significantly affect precision (P = 0.328).ConclusionPG-I demonstrated the highest accuracy. Implant angulation compromised IOS trueness, but laterally extended PADs improved accuracy, making IOS comparable to PG systems.Clinical significanceThese findings indicate that, even in angulated full‑arch implant cases, photogrammetry (PG‑I) and IOS with laterally extended PADs provide clinically acceptable accuracy and may serve as reliable digital impression techniques in practice.

Introduction

Implant-supported restorations are reliable treatment options for complete edentulism, with protocols typically involving 4 to 6 implants for full arch rehabilitation [1]. For long-term clinical success, passive fit should be achieved in screw-retained restorations [2]. Passive fit is defined as the adaptation of the implant-supported restoration without tension, compression or bending forces in the absence of occlusal load [3]. Accurate impressions are the first step to ensure passively fit restorations [4]. Accuracy is the combination of two components: trueness and precision. Trueness refers to the closeness of a measurement to the reference value, whereas precision describes the repeatability or consistency of measurements under identical conditions [5]. Both components are crucial for ensuring the reliability of impression techniques, especially in challenging clinical cases with multiple implants [6].

Recent advancements in digital dentistry have led to the increasing adoption of fully digital workflows in implantology [7]. Digital implant impressions play a vital role in these workflows and can be obtained via intraoral scanners (IOSs) or photogrammetry (PG) devices [8]. The use of IOSs in completely edentulous arches is still challenging [9] and digital scans with the use of IOSs have been reported to show reduced accuracy [10, 11]. In completely edentulous arches, the lack of sufficient and stable anatomical landmarks on the mucosa between implants may complicate the image-stitching process [12] in which multiple images or videos taken by IOS are merged via algorithm [13]. Therefore, a larger scanning area and longer inter-implant distance leads to accumulated stitching errors which result in increased inaccuracies in the digital scan [14, 15]. Several methods have been suggested to overcome the limitations of intraoral scanning in complete-arch implant-supported restorations and to obtain a continuous and accurate scanning path. These include the creation of artificial landmarks on the mucosa [16, 17], splinting scan bodies [18], utilizing scan bodies with extensions [11], and employing prefabricated geometric devices (PADs) [19–23]. Splinting the scan bodies [18, 24] and the use of PADs [19, 21, 23] have been reported as effective in improving the trueness of digital scans. However, although these strategies demonstrate potential benefits, recent systematic evaluations highlight persistent limitations. According to Paratelli et al. [25], most reported techniques involve multiple intraoral scans, additional fabrication steps, or the need for preliminary impressions, thereby increasing procedural and software complexity without providing standardized improvements in accuracy. Furthermore, heterogeneity in the design and implementation of these methods limits their clinical applicability. These observations support the need for alternative PAD designs that reduce complexity while maintaining or enhancing scan accuracy.

Implant angulation has also been suggested as a factor that may influence the accuracy of digital impressions in the literature. Some recent studies [22, 26–29] have reported that implant angulation decreases the accuracy of digital scans compared to conventional impressions or negatively affects the scanning accuracy of IOSs. However, other studies have reported contradictory results, concluding that implant angulation has no significant effect on intraoral scanning accuracy [30], and that angulation differences among implants might even improve the accuracy [31]. This lack of consensus in the literature highlights the need for further investigation into the effects of implant angulation on the accuracy of IOSs.

PG is an alternative to IOSs that utilizes multiple cameras to capture three-dimensional spatial relationships by using reference points within photographs without contacting the measured object [32]. Studies reported that PG provides greater accuracy in transferring implant positions compared to both conventional techniques and IOSs [32, 33]. PG has shown promise in overcoming the limitations of IOS; however, the effect of implant angulation on the trueness of PG systems has not been studied in detail. As PG is increasingly used in dental implant impressions, accuracy of the technique has become a key study area [34].

The aim of this in vitro study was to investigate the effect of implant angulation on the trueness and precision of intraoral scanning utilizing two different PADs and to compare the performances of IOS and PG techniques. The null hypotheses were as follows: (1) Implant angulation would not significantly affect the trueness of digital impression techniques, (2) PAD use and design would not significantly influence the linear and angular deviations in intraoral scanning, (3) No significant difference would be observed in the deviations between intraoral scanning and PG techniques, (4) The precision of digital impressions would not be significantly influenced by the scanning technique or implant angulation.

Materials and methods

In this in vitro study, 3 master models were prepared to simulate different implant placement configurations in maxillary complete edentulism. Models were created by pouring auto-polymerized acrylic resin (Meliodent Rapid Repair; Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) into an edentulous silicone mould (B-3 NMG Silicone Index; Frasaco GmbH, Tetnang Germany). A silicone material (Prestige VDG Mask Rigid, Vannini Dental Industry, Grassina, Florence, Italy) with a thickness of 2 mm was applied to the models to mimic the gingiva. Multi-unit analogs (Nobel Active Multi-unit Analog Ø4.8 mm, Nobel Biocare AB, Gothenburg, Sweden) were placed in 5 predetermined areas: the canine regions (#13 and #23), the first molar on the right side (#16), and the first premolar and second molar on the left side (#25 and #27). Sockets for the analogs were prepared using a rotary instrument. A dental surveyor (Marathon 103; Saeyang Microtech, Korea) was used to assist with implant analog placement. The intended 25° mesial and distal inclinations were verified during analog positioning using a protractor. Three different master models were prepared according to the angulation of implant #16. In Model 1, all implants were parallel. In Model 2, implant #16 was angulated 25° mesially, while the others (#13, #23, #25, and #27) remained parallel. In Model 3, implant #16 was angulated 25° distally, with the rest remaining parallel. Each master model was scanned using a laboratory scanner (inEOS X5 V22.3.0; Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany), which has an accuracy of 2.1 μm/2.8 μm according to DIN EN ISO 12,836:2015). Scanning was performed with the aid of polyetheretherketone (PEEK) scan bodies (Elos Accurate Scan Body IO 2 C-A Elos Medtech, Timmersdala, Sweden), and these reference data were saved in standard tessellation language (STL) file format. The minimum required sample size was determined using a software program (G*Power, V3.1.9.7; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf). Based on an effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25), 85% power, and a significance level of 0.05, the required sample size was calculated to be 15. All digital scans were performed by a single operator (T.M.) with five years of experience in digital impression techniques. For the IOS groups (IOS, IOS-C, and IOS-LE), digital scans were captured using an IOS (CEREC Primescan, Dentsply Sirona, V5.2.7, Bensheim, Germany). Prior to scanning, PEEK scan bodies were hand-tightened to the multi-unit analogs through screws. The PADs used in the IOS-C and IOS-LE groups were designed using a 3D modeling software program (SolidWorks; Dassault Systèmes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France). The crown-shaped PAD (Fig. 4) used in the IOS-C group was a ring-like base with 8 symmetrical protrusions, resembling the shape of a crown.

In the IOS-LE group, the PAD (Fig. 5) was a modular system consisting of a central ring with adjustable perforated extensions and triangular rods of varying lengths (6.5 mm, 9 mm, and 11.5 mm).Fig. 5Design and technical drawings of the IOS-LE PAD with lateral extensions. Design and technical drawings of the IOS-LE PAD with lateral extensions. The PAD features a perforated central ring structure (a) and modular extensions with 4-prong (b), and 6-prong (c)

The perforations allowed flexibility in adapting the PAD to the arch’s specific anatomy, while the interchangeable extensions enabled compatibility with different inter-implant distances, ensuring optimal scanning for various clinical scenarios. PADs were fabricated by using a fused deposition modelling device (Prusa i3 MK3S; Prusa Research AS, Prague, Czech Republic) with a filament type of polylactic acid plus. In all IOS groups, manufacturer’s recommendations were followed for the scanning procedure. Scanning commenced from the occlusal aspect of the #27 site and progressed across the arch to the #16 implant site, and subsequently included the buccal and palatal surfaces of all scan bodies. This scanning strategy corresponds to the Occlusal–Buccal–Lingual (OBL) technique as previously defined in the literature [35]. The scan data were exported as STL files. For the PG-I group, the optical markers (iCamBodies) that had a rectangular shape and featured a black surface with white polka dot patterns were hand-tightened onto the multi-unit analogs. The device was calibrated prior to every scan. The PG device was positioned approximately 20 cm away from the scan bodies. The scanner was moved systematically from the left to the right side to register the 3D spatial positions of the implants. After implant position registration, the scan bodies were replaced with reference markers (iCamRefs; Imetric4D Imaging Sàrl, Courgenay, Switzerland) to record the soft-tissue contours using an intraoral scanner (3-shape Trios3, V20.1.3, Copenhagen, Denmark). The implant positions and soft-tissue data were merged into a single STL file for each scan using the best-fit alignment algorithm provided by the PG software (iScan 3D Dental V9; Imetric4D Imaging Sàrl, Courgenay, Switzerland) [36]. For the PG-O group, the optical markers (Oxo Markers), which had a flag-like design with a white surface featuring grey geometric patterns, were attached to the multi-unit analogs. After calibrating the device, the optical markers were scanned. Following the scanning process, the markers were unscrewed and healing caps (OXO Fit Healing Caps, Oxo Technology, Madrid, Spain) were tightened onto the multi-unit analogs for intraoral scanning using an IOS (AlliedStar AS200E, AlliedStar Medical, V1.0.34.2, Shanghai, China) to record the soft tissue contours. The implant positions and soft tissue data were subsequently merged into a single STL file using the system’s inbuilt software (OxoFit, V1.7, Oxo Technology, Madrid, Spain).

In this study, 3D surface deviations determined by root mean squared error (RMS) values were used to evaluate both trueness and precision. Angular deviations (AD) and linear deviations (LD) were also calculated to provide a more detailed assessment of trueness. The reference and test data files were imported to a metrology software program (Geomagic Control X, V2014.3.0, 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA) for 3D analysis. All analyses were conducted by a single operator (T.M.). For the analysis of AD and LD, the virtual scan bodies in the reference scan were converted into hollow cylinders using the “Create Feature” tool in the software. First, the flat surface on top of the scan body was manually selected, and a horizontal plane was created using the software. Then, to enable the generation of a cylinder, an additional plane was created at a distance corresponding to the height of the scan body (8 mm) below the initial horizontal plane. The same cylindrical features were automatically generated in the test scans to ensure standardization. Cartesian (x, y, z) coordinates representing the centerlines of these cylinders were recorded for all scan bodies (Fig. 6).

Conversion of scan bodies into hollow cylinders and determination of their central coordinates (x, y, z) in Geomagic Control X software

The line passing through the center of the scan body located at the implant #13 was considered the reference. The Euclidean linear and angular distances (Fig. 1) between the centerlines of the reference implant (#13) and the other implants (#23, #25, #27, #16) was calculated using the standard Euclidean distance formula. ΔLD and ΔAD values were determined by calculating the differences between the corresponding measurements in the reference and test data [28]. RMS values were used to quantify deviations among scan body centrelines. Rather than evaluating the entire surface, RMS values were calculated based on deviations among the centerlines of the scan bodies, specifically in the inter-implant region. This approach allowed the analysis to focus on clinically relevant implant-based surfaces. The Euclidean distances previously computed for ΔLD were used. These distances were derived from the Cartesian coordinates of the hollow cylinders representing the centerlines of the scan bodies, as determined during the AD and LD analysis. Deviations for each test scan were calculated using the RMS formula [37]. For precision evaluation, RMS deviation values were used [9]. The scan with the lowest RMS deviation in each test group was selected as the internal reference. Subsequently, RMS values were recalculated as previously described for all other scans in the group relative to this internal reference. The final RMS values were reported as the precision metric for each group. Lower RMS values indicated higher precision, reflecting the ability of the scanning technique to produce consistent results.

All data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of data was confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk test (P > 0.05), and homogeneity of variances by Levene’s test (P > 0.05). The main and interaction effects of the independent variables (digital impression technique and implant angulation) on ΔAD, ΔLD and RMS values were examined by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For post-hoc analysis where significant differences were observed (P<0.05), Tukey’s honestly significant difference test was used. A significance level of P < 0.05 was adopted for all tests.

Results

The trueness and precision data of the present study were evaluated based on predefined accuracy thresholds. The angular deviation threshold was set at 0.4° [11, 38], while the linear deviation threshold was set at 100 μm [11, 39, 40] in accordance with previous studies. Measurements exceeding these thresholds were considered clinically unacceptable. At all implant sites, the IOS group exhibited the highest ΔLD values ​​and significantly higher than those of all other experimental groups (all P values < 0.01). At site #13–16, IOS recorded a mean deviation of 30.24 ± 6.44 μm, while PG-I showed only 5.65 ± 2.08 μm, indicating a 24.59 μm difference in mean deviation. In contrast, PG-I exhibited the lowest ΔLD values ​​in all models (range: 3.25–5.65 μm) and showed statistically significant differences from the other groups (P < 0.01). Except for implant sites 13–23 and 13–16, no significant differences were observed among the IOS-C, IOS-LE, and PG-O groups (all P values > 0.05). Furthermore, no significant differences were detected between Model 1 and Model 2 across all implant regions (P = 1.000), except for the 13–23 (P<0.01) implant region. Although Model 3 exhibited the highest ΔLD values across all implant regions, the differences between Model 1 and Model 3 in the 13–23 implant region (P = 1.000) were not statistically significant.

Considering the interaction terms, IOS-C, IOS-LE, and PG-I showed relatively consistent and low deviation values ​​in Model 1. In Model 2, IOS showed lower deviations compared to Model 1 (22.59 μm vs. 24.00 μm for data 13–16), while others such as IOS-LE exhibited greater deviation in response to angulation as seen in data 13–25 and 13–27. Discussion

This in vitro study investigated the accuracy of intraoral scanning using different PAD designs and compared these with PG techniques in case of multiple implants considering variations in angulation. Based on the findings of the present study, the null hypotheses were partially rejected. The first null hypothesis, which stated that implant angulation would not significantly affect the 3D deviations, was partially rejected. No significant RMS differences were found in the PG-I and IOS-LE groups, whereas significant differences were observed among the IOS, IOS-C, and PG-O groups. The second null hypothesis, which suggested that PAD use and design would not significantly influence ΔLD and ΔAD values of intraoral scanning, was rejected. The results revealed that both the use and design of PADs significantly affected scanning trueness as lower ΔLD and ΔAD values were observed in the IOS-LE group compared to the IOS and IOS-C groups. The third null hypothesis stating that no significant differences would be found between digital impression techniques was partially rejected. Although the PG-I technique demonstrated significantly lower deviations values in comparison to IOS techniques, the PG-O technique did not demonstrate superiority over intraoral scanning techniques that utilized PADs. Finally, the forth null hypothesis was also partially rejected, since precision was significantly influenced by the scanning technique, as the highest precision was observed in PG-I, but not by implant angulation.

Implant angulation is a common clinical condition, particularly in cases involving multiple implants in the completely edentulous maxilla, where anatomical limitations such as the maxillary sinus often necessitate inclined placement of the implants [41]. In the posterior maxilla, distal angulation is often used to avoid anatomical structures such as the maxillary sinus and to allow for the placement of longer implants without additional grafting procedures [42]. Alternatively, when implants are placed at the maxillary tuberosity, a mesial inclination may be required to bypass the sinus and optimise bone engagement while avoiding grafting [43]. Therefore, the inclusion of both mesial and distal angulations in this study was intended to reflect plausible clinical scenarios encountered in maxillary full-arch rehabilitations. Accordingly, five implants were placed at different locations along a maxillary reference model with controlled interimplant distances and 25-degree angulations directed either mesially or distally. This study not only evaluated the overall 3D deviations of the scanning techniques, but also analysed ΔLD and ΔAD separately for each implant site [9, 28]. Although RMS values ​​are widely used to assess the presence of 3D deviations, it has been suggested that presenting deviations only with RMS values ​​may not fully reflect the magnitude and direction of deviations [44]. Therefore, in this study, it was therefore preferred to analyse ΔLD and ΔAD separately for each implant site to identify specific areas where deviations occur and to provide a more detailed understanding of the potential errors associated with each scanning technique. A 3D deviation threshold of 100 μm [11, 39, 40] and an angular deviation threshold of 0.4° were adopted to evaluate the accuracy of the tested techniques [11, 38].

Although the 3D deviation values calculated in the present study remained below the clinically acceptable threshold [11, 39, 40], the IOS group exhibited the significantly highest RMS values (63 ± 13.57 μm) across all models, regardless of implant angulation. Additionally, the IOS technique demonstrated the highest ΔAD values among all models, with deviations exceeding the 0.4° threshold at every implant site. Angular deviation is suggested to play an even more critical role than linear misalignment in implant-supported restorations, as angular discrepancies can amplify more stress on implant components and the surrounding bone, potentially leading to mechanical complications or biological failure [45]. The significantly highest deviations in the IOS group can be attributed to larger scanning areas in edentulous arches, which, as reported in the literature [14, 15], often result in cumulative stitching errors during intraoral scanning. Scanning was initiated from the #27 implant and progressed across the arch finishing at the #13 implant, with higher ΔLD and ΔAD differences were detected between the #13 and #16 implants. As reported in recent literature, the higher deviations in this site may be attributed to accumulated stitching errors [46].

In addition to the extended scanning distance, the absence of distinct morphological features on the mucosal surface presents an additional challenge to scanning accuracy in fully edentulous arches [11]. PADs act as artificial landmarks, enabling a continuous scan between implants and thereby improving the trueness of the stitching process [21]. The PADs evaluated in this study were designed as prefabricated components that can be easily applied to scan bodies without requiring custom fabrication, making them practical and clinically feasible for various scenarios. Unlike many previously proposed scan-aid strategies that involve splinting, customized geometries, or additional CAD/CAM steps, the present design aimed to provide a simplified, reproducible, and accessible solution aligned with routine clinical workflows. Scan-aid use significantly improved the trueness of intraoral scanning with lower deviation values were determined in IOS-LE and IOS-C groups in comparison to IOS group (Table 5). The PAD with lateral extension (IOS-LE) demonstrated superior results compared to the standard crown-shaped design (IOS-C). This finding can be attributed to the extension reaching into edentulous areas, effectively increasing the number of reference points and stabilizing the stitching process [21, 23]. Moreover, the laterally extended design of the PADs offers additional flexibility; for cases with varying inter-implant distances, the extensions can be sized accordingly, while the perforated ring structure allows for adaptable positioning, enabling the extensions to conform to the shape of the arch in different regions (Fig. 5). Fused‑deposition fabrication with PLA or PLA‑plus filaments is widely adopted in healthcare because the printers are inexpensive, safe, and easy to operate [47]. The prefabricated PADs evaluated in the study leverage this low‑cost technology: they can be printed on demand without patient‑specific customisation, making them economical and practical for routine use. PLA’s heat sensitivity, however, limits re‑use; high‑temperature steam or formaldehyde sterilisation can significantly alter the dimensional and mechanical properties of FDM‑printed components [48]. demonstrated that an optimised autoclave protocol (134 °C with high infill) achieves complete sterilisation of PLA models with minimal deformation (~ 0.14 mm). Until heat‑resistant materials or low‑temperature sterilisation methods are validated, these PADs should therefore be regarded as single‑use devices or be printed with high infill densities to tolerate autoclave cycles.

In particular, the ring-shaped component of the LE design fits securely over the scan body and includes pre-defined holes that guide the placement angle and direction of the extensions in accordance with the arch curvature. The availability of different extension lengths further facilitates adaptation across a range of interimplant distances. The overall aim of this approach was to develop a prefabricated system in which either a crown-shaped or perforated-ring component could serve as a universal platform adaptable to diverse clinical scenarios. Instead of manufacturing customized scan-aids for each patient, this design promotes the development of standardized auxiliary components that are applicable across cases while retaining the flexibility to accommodate anatomical variation. Although these devices are not yet commercially available, the concept was intended to demonstrate a clinically viable and reproducible solution that could inspire future efforts toward standardization in digital implant workflows.

The effect of implant angulation on the trueness of IOSs has been investigated in the literature [22, 26, 27, 31] but with variable results [49]. Therefore, the current study evaluated the effects of angulated implants, considering the direction of angulation. Significant RMS differences were observed between Models 2 and 3 in the IOS and IOS-C groups, with mesially angulated implants showing significantly lower RMS values than distally angulated ones. Additionally, in the IOS group, Model 2 revealed significantly higher trueness compared to parallel implants. These findings suggest that directional angulation—particularly distal tilt—can negatively affect the trueness of digital impressions in IOS-based working protocols. Clinically, this suggests that when distal angulation is anticipated, careful pre-surgical planning and the use of more accurate impression techniques such as photogrammetry or PAD-assisted IOS may help minimize potential inaccuracies. On the other hand, mesial angulation, as in Model 2 of the IOS group, had a significantly higher trueness compared to parallel implants. This may be attributed to variations in inter-implant distances caused by angulation, where mesial angulation shortens and distal angulation lengthens these distances. While few studies [15, 27, 47, 50] have evaluated the effect of inter-implant distance on the scanning accuracy of intraoral digital implant scans, findings have generally been consistent. A recent review [51] showed that scanning errors tended to increase with increasing scan length or greater distances between implants. In the current study, mesially inclined implants produced shorter distances between scan bodies, which may have contributed to the improvement in impression accuracy. These findings are consistent with the findings of a previous study [49], which reported that mesially inclined implants showed greater accuracy than both parallel and distally inclined implants. The trueness of the IOS-LE group, which was supported by sufficient reference points between implants, was not significantly affected by either 25-degree mesial or distal angulation, as no significant RMS differences were detected among the models (P >0.05). This finding may suggest that angulation differences may lead to decreased scanning trueness due to stitching errors. Therefore, the use of appropriately designed PADs can enable improvement in the trueness of impressions even in the presence of angulated implants.

PG systems are particularly recommended for cases with multiple implants due to their superior accuracy in registering implant positions independent of stitching algorithms [10, 52]. The literature investigating the effect of implant angulation on PG system accuracy is limited [1, 10, 28, 32, 53] with most being tested up to 17° and reporting no detrimental effect on trueness [1, 10, 32, 53]. One recent article, however, reported that variations in angulation to 30° did affect scanning accuracy [28]. The present study investigated the effect of 25-degree angulation differences on the accuracy of PG systems and included two different PG systems for comparison. The PG-I group exhibited the lowest RMS deviation values across all models, demonstrating higher trueness with deviations of 18 ± 3.27 μm, irrespective of the angulation direction. Additionally, the ΔAD values for the PG-I group remained below the 0.4° threshold across all implant sites and models, showing that implant angulation did not play a critical role in the technique’s accuracy. The PG-O group exhibited significantly greater RMS and ΔAD values in Model 3 compared to Models 1 and 2, with deviations considerably greater than those in the PG-I group. In contrast to IOS methods, PG-O exhibited greater trueness compared to the IOS and IOS-C groups against the IOS and IOS-C groups and had much smaller RMS deviations for all models. No statistically significant differences were observed between IOS-LE and PG-O, indicating that the laterally extended PAD design employed in IOS-LE can enhance scanning trueness to a level comparable to that of PG methods.

The RMS deviation values for the PG-I group in the present study (18 ± 3.27 μm) were lower than the range (24–104 μm) reported in previous studies [10, 38, 44, 53–55], where RMS values were determined using a best-fit algorithm for model alignment. Revilla-León et al. [56] noted that best-fit algorithms can cause inconsistencies in the evaluation of certain geometric regions, depending on how they are applied, especially in areas with repetitive or ambiguous features. Similarly, previous studies have noted that the choice of the offset calculation method and alignment technique for overlaps [37] can greatly affect the resulting measurements [54]. Therefore, the lower RMS values detected in this study may be attributed to the methodology employed, as RMS values were calculated after aligning the scan body centerlines using cylinders rather than relying on a best-fit algorithm. The manual alignment method and calculation of RMS values using the coordinates of the cylinders provided in this study might offer more precise data and lower deviation values by focusing on clinically relevant regions rather than the entire dataset.

The difference in trueness between the PG-I and PG-O systems is most likely attributable to differences in image acquisition methods. The PG-I system captures each scan body individually, allowing for more accurate localization of individual implant positions. In contrast, the PG-O system captures all implant positions simultaneously, which may increase the risk of stitching errors, especially in cases of extreme angularity, as reflected in the significant deviations observed in distally angled implants. This simultaneous acquisition method also presents challenges such as higher sensitivity to marker positioning and greater dependence on preset scanning parameters. Accordingly, it may lead to longer scanning durations and reduced practicality in complex clinical settings compared to the more controlled and operator-driven workflow of the PG-I system. A study by Revilla-León et al. [57, 58] has illustrated that the trueness and precision of PG systems could vary depending on acquisition technique and marker configuration. Additional work is required to comprehensively evaluate the performance of PG systems in various clinical environments.

In the present study, precision was not influenced by the implant angulation. The PG-I technique revealed the highest precision. Although significant trueness differences were found among the IOS groups, precision was not affected by PAD usage (Table 6). In the literature, the precision of the ICam4D PG system, as used in this study, has been reported to range between 2 and 203 μm [10, 38, 50, 55, 56]. The precision values obtained in this study for PG-I (8.13 ± 1.46 μm) and PG-O (10.93 ± 2.89) align with previously reported data. Moreover, a recent study [54] comparing the precision of the ICam4D PG system and the IOS device (Primescan) concluded that the PG system demonstrated superior precision.

This in vitro study was conducted under standardized laboratory conditions and therefore did not examine the effects of ambient lighting, temperature or humidity on scanning accuracy. Evidence from recent studies indicates that these environmental factors—such as light colour [59] and surface humidity [60] —can significantly influence trueness and scanning time in complete‑arch implant scans. As these variables were held constant, the applicability of our results to dynamic clinical settings remains limited and warrants further investigation.

In addition to ambient factors, this study also included only a single angulation degree (25°), and did not assess variations in implant placement depth, number of implants, or inter-implant distances. These limitations suggest that further studies are needed to comprehensively investigate the tested methods under more diverse conditions. A laboratory desktop scanner was used as the reference scanner, as it better simulates clinical workflows and practical use of digital impressions. However, industrial scanners provide the highest level of trueness, and their use in future studies could further validate the findings. Additionally, the assessment was performed using metrology software; yet evaluating framework fit in in vivo studies could provide results that are more clinically relevant. Lastly, the findings are specific to the devices and protocols tested, and the results may not be directly generalizable to other systems or clinical workflows.

Conclusions

Based on the findings of this in vitro study, the following conclusions were drawn:The PG-I system demonstrated the highest accuracy among all techniques, with both trueness and precision values within clinically acceptable thresholds.Implant angulation significantly affected the trueness of IOS, IOS-C, and PG-O groups, with higher deviations were detected under distal inclination.The trueness of the IOS-LE group was comparable to PG-based systems and was not affected by angulation.Implant angulation did not significantly influence the precision values of any technique.Photogrammetry (PG-I) and IOS combined with prefabricated PADs (IOS-LE) may offer clinically reliable options in full-arch cases involving angulated implants.
