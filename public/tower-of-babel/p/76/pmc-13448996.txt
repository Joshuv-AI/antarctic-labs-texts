Photochromism of Dicyanorhodanine–Pyrrole Conjugates: Substituent and Solvent Effects
Authors: Parag Das, Sydney M. Charles, Antonella Bocchieri, Łukasz Dobrzycki, Ronald K. Castellano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dicyanorhodanine (RCN)-pyrrole conjugates are a relatively new class of photochromic compounds capable of highly efficient reversible photoisomerization upon visible-light excitation in both solution and solid-state, and understanding the factors governing their photophysical behavior is essential for designing reliable photoswitches. In this study, a library of RCN-pyrrole chromophores is investigated to elucidate the influence of substituents with varying electronic and steric properties on their π–π* absorption characteristics in solution. Solvent polarity affects the frontier molecular orbital energies, resulting in a negative solvatochromic effect. Even so, (near)­quantitative bidirectional Z/E photoisomerization has been achieved for all the RCN-pyrroles. Intramolecular hydrogen-bonding (H-bonding) in the E isomers and intermolecular H-bonding in the Z isomers play key roles in their exceptional photoswitching efficiency and high thermal stability. Thermal isomerization could be tuned by solvents with different H-bonding capabilities, highlighting the importance of specific solute–solvent interactions. Replacing the pyrrole with an imidazole moiety reveals contrasting photoinduced side reactions inconsistent with Z/E isomerization. Complementary ground-(DFT) and excited-state (TD-DFT) calculations correlate well with the experimental absorption profiles and the photochemical outcomes. These structure–property photoisomerization analyses provide critical insights for the application of RCN-pyrrole-based photochromic systems forsmart materials design.

Introduction

Photochromic molecules are defined as chemical species that undergo a reversible transformation between two or more distinct states induced by noninvasive electromagnetic irradiation, i.e., light. , Molecular photoswitches are a special class of chromophores that undergo reversible photoisomerization between a “thermodynamically stable” state and a “metastable” state upon light excitation. , Due to their unique ability to reversibly control chemical and biological processes at the molecular level, classical photoswitches like azobenzene, hemiindigoids, hydrazone, , diarylethenes, , and spiropyrans have been extensively utilized in optoelectronic devices, molecular machines, supramolecular chemistry, − photopharmacology, and liquid crystals. Characteristics of a molecular photoswitch that are crucial to optimize for its effective use in soft matter applications include efficiency of photoisomerization, thermal stability of the metastable isomer, addressability, and reliability. To optimize performance properties, rigorous structure–property studies have been used to tune photochromism in diverse states. , Distinct substitution patterns impart changes in the ground- and excited-state potential energy surface and associated π–π* electronic transition that in turn influence the photoisomerization parameters. , Aprahamian et al. reported a series of hydrazone chromophores modulating the “rotor” and “stator” structures via incorporation of different electron-donating and/or electron-withdrawing substituents, which significantly affect the activation wavelength, photostationary states (PSSs), and photoisomerization quantum yields. The characteristics of E/Z isomerization of water-soluble arylazopyrazole (AAP) derivatives are influenced by steric and electronic effects. Several design principles have been established to improve the thermal half-lives of the metastable isomer for precise spatiotemporal control, particularly for molecular storage or logic-device applications. Careful structural engineering has enabled fine-tuning of the thermal isomerization behavior of phenylazoindole, iminothioindoxyl, acylhydrazone, and hemiindigoid-type photoswitches. ,−

Solvent–solute interactions can also critically influence the photophysical and photochemical properties of small-molecule photoswitches. , Solvent polarity affects the π–π* transition energy, resulting in a bathochromic or hypsochromic shift upon increasing the solvent polarity. , Feringa et al. reported solvent-dependent photoisomerization and photoluminescence behavior where the solvent polarity could synergistically modulate the magnitude of these two distinct functions. The polarity and hydrogen-bonding (H-bonding) ability of solvents have been shown to influence the photochemical pathways of hemithioindigo (HTI) and azo-based photoswitches. Hereby, understanding the solvation effects of a chromophore on its ground- and excited-state electronics is essential for the rational design of efficient molecular photoswitches. −

2-(1,1-Dicyanomethylene)­rhodanine, colloquially known as dicyanorhodanine (RCN), is an electron-deficient heterocycle derived from the rhodanine core structure. RCN units have gained popularity as a terminal electron acceptor unit in the design of acceptor–donor–acceptor (A–D–A) type organic photovoltaic (OPV) materials. − In recent years, the RCN unit has emerged as a promising photoswitch scaffold when appended to π-conjugated (hetero)­aromatic cycles. We have conducted a detailed photochemical investigation of “donor–acceptor” systems, including RCN-functionalized thiophene, − pyrrole, and [2.2]­paracyclophane (pCp)-based small-molecules. Comparing these RCN–based π-conjugated chromophores (Figure a–c), profound differences are observed in the photophysical and photoisomerization characteristics depending on the electronics of the complementary electron-rich scaffold. For example, the RCN-oligothiophenes are susceptible to reversible Z/E photoisomerization in solution; however, only incomplete photoconversion is achieved, along with evidence of a photoinduced tandem pericyclic reaction pathway, which lowers the efficiency of isomerization. , The solution absorption maximum (λmax) of the Z isomer of the RCN–thiophene derivative lies at 412 nm (Figure b), similar to the “para-methoxybenzene” derivative­(λmax = 411 nm, Figure c). As the UV–vis profiles of the E and Z isomers significantly overlap for both chromophores, only 44% and 47% of Z → E photoconversion was obtained, respectively (Figure b,c). , Interestingly, the RCN–pyrrole conjugates have shown much more proficient visible-light-driven photoswitching performance in both solution and the solid-state (Figure a). The intramolecular and intermolecular H-bonding interactions present in the E and Z isomers of the RCN-pyrroles lead to red-shifted absorption profiles for both isomers (λmax (Z) = 436 nm and λmax (E) = 466 nm). The larger separation (Δλmax = 30 nm) of the absorption maxima facilitates enhanced bidirectional photoisomerization for RCN-pyrroles, e.g., 83% Z → E (λirr = 404 nm) and 100% E → Z (λirr = 523 nm) conversion.

The parent rhodanine core has also been reported as an excellent photoswitch when appended to aromatic heterocycles. ,, In a comprehensive study, Dube et al. demonstrated the photoisomerization of imidazole-based rhodanine chromophores to the tune of 70% Z → E (λirr = 365 nm) and 94% E → Z (λirr = 415 nm) (Figure d). Hereby, the electronic properties of both “donor” and “acceptor” units strongly influence the frontier molecular orbital (FMO) energies and associated absorption spectra, which consequently govern the photoswitching efficiency of these (dicyano)­rhodanine systems, similar to previously reported HTI chromophores. , Although the RCN–pyrroles have shown the most desirable photostationary state distributions (PSDs) among related photoswitch motifs, the substituent scope at the pyrrole unit has remained narrowly explored. Elucidating substituent effects is therefore critical to improve the versatility of RCN-based photoswitches. Furthermore, the presence of H-bonding interactions in both Z and E isomers, might imply a strong sensitivity to solute–solvent interactions, governing the ground- and excited-state PES, activation wavelengths, and overall photochromic behavior. As our prior solution studies of the RCN–pyrroles are mostly confined to chloroform, investigating solvent-dependent photochromism is of particular importance. This prompted us to take a deeper dive into the substituent and solvent effects on the photochromic properties of the RCN-pyrrole conjugates.

Herein, we present a comprehensive structure–property investigation of the reversible Z/E photoisomerization behavior of RCN–pyrrole chromophores. We have judiciously installed functional groups to induce significant changes in molecular orbital energies and enable tunable photophysical properties in solution for both the isomers, which is highly advantageous for potential applications. All the studied compounds exhibit highly efficient bidirectional Z/E photoconversion upon selective visible-light irradiation. Solvent polarity is found to considerably influence the UV–vis absorption profiles, with distinct negative solvatochromism observed for selected model compounds. Although the solvent environment exerts disparate influence on the Z and E isomers, and consequently variable spectral separation in different solvents, high PSDs have been consistently maintained along with facile photoswitching performance.

The extent of thermal isomerization is reported to be profoundly affected by solvent H-bonding ability, highlighting the role of specific solute–solvent interactions. The alkyl substituents at the π-extended donor unit leads to noticeable changes in the absorption profiles and thermal stability of the isomers, underscoring the importance of even minor changes in molecular structure. Additionally, to establish a rational design principle for the (dicyano)­rhodanine-based photoswitches, we have synthesized a rhodanine–pyrrole and an RCN–imidazole chromophore, both of which show distinct photophysical and photochemical properties compared to the RCN–pyrrole systems. Complementary ground- and excited-state density functional theory (DFT) calculations show excellent agreement with the experimental observations, and help rationalize the tunability of the photochromic behavior of RCN derivatives based on the structural changes. Overall, we report an effective strategy to tune the stereoelectronics of the RCN–pyrrole chromophores, while preserving their excellent optical properties and well-separated Z/E absorption profiles, inspiring additional organic molecular photoswitch development.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

We have designed and synthesized a library of π-conjugated RCN–pyrrole molecules, varying in electronics and alkyl chain length (Figure ). Compound 1 serves as the simplest RCN–pyrrole analog, differing from compound 7 only in the length of the alkyl chain appended to the RCN unit. Compounds 2–6 are π-extended systems decorated with different functional groups and displaying different regiochemistries, to evaluate these structural changes on optical and photochemical properties. Compound 2 contains an electron-donating (EDG) para-methoxybenzene group at the donor unit, while the methoxy group is replaced by an electron-withdrawing (EWG) cyano group in compound 3, and a long octyloxy chain in compound 5. Compound 4 is a regioisomer of compound 2, with the EDG attached to the 4-position of the pyrrole unit. The tert-butyl groups are introduced at the meta-position of the EDG in compound 6 to modify the steric and electronic properties. Additionally, a rhodanine–pyrrole (compound 8) and an RCN–imidazole derivative (compound 9) are synthesized to investigate how different heterocyclic substituents at the acceptor and donor units influence the photochemical behavior, in comparison to the RCN–pyrrole chromophores.

For the preparation of the π-extended chromophores (compounds 2–3 and 5–6), a multistep synthetic route has been employed, starting with a Suzuki–Miyaura cross-coupling between N-Boc-2-pyrroleboronic acid and appropriate aryl bromides. Following Boc deprotection, the pyrrole-aryl conjugates are subjected to Vilsmeier–Haack formylation to install the aldehyde group onto the pyrrole (intermediates 12, 14, 19, and 21). The intermediate 16 is synthesized via bromination of pyrrole-2-carbaldehyde, followed by Suzuki coupling reaction with para-(methoxyphenyl)­boronic acid (Scheme S4). These formylated intermediates are subsequently condensed with the RCN unit (22), affording the target compounds 2–6 (Scheme b and S4). The syntheses of compounds 8 and 9 also involve a condensation reaction between the RCN (or rhodanine) and commercially available formylated imidazole (or pyrrole) precursors (Schemes S8,S9).

Compounds 1–8 are obtained as a mixture of Z and E isomers from the synthesis, which could be successfully separated and purified using silica column chromatography due to their distinctly different polarity. The formation of both isomers could be associated with the intrinsic stabilization through intra-(in the E state) and intermolecular (in the Z state) H-bonding plausible for these chromophores. In contrast, compound 9 has been detected exclusively as the Z isomer upon synthesis, which could be attributed to the difference in the inherent stability of the pyrrole- versus imidazole-based chromophores. Full synthetic schemes and characterization details for the target molecules 1–9 and related intermediates are provided in the Supporting Information (Schemes S1–S9, Figures S1–S52).

Single crystal X-ray analysis of 7- E (Figures S120,S121, Table S12) reveals a planar geometry (Scheme c). An intramolecular H-bonding interaction is clear between the pyrrole–NH hydrogen and the carbonyl oxygen atom, with a distance of 1.98 Å between the two participating atoms. The presence of the intramolecular H-bonding could also be confirmed by 1H NMR (in chloroform-d), as the chemical shift (δ) of the–NH proton in the E isomer is more deshielded than that of the Z isomer for compounds 1–8, consistent with the previously reported RCN-pyrroles. For example, the–NH proton of 2- E resonates at 12.87 ppm, over 3 ppm downfield of 2- Z (δ = 9.67 ppm). Moreover, the isomerizable olefin proton is more deshielded for 2- Z (δ = 7.83 ppm) than 2- E (δ = 6.91 ppm) due to its closer proximity to the RCN carbonyl group. The as-synthesized isomer of compound 9 possesses a very similar chemical shift pattern to that of the 1- Z-8- Z isomers, which confirms its Z configuration. The–NH proton of 9- Z is observed at 9.58 ppm, whereas the olefin proton is at 7.82 ppm (Figure S119).

We have performed ground-state geometry optimization of 1–9 using gas-phase density functional theory (B3LYP/6–31 + G­(d)) to gain a deeper understanding of their structural features (Figures S98–S104). For the pyrrole-functionalized RCN (and rhodanine) chromophores (1–8), the E isomer is found to be the thermodynamically more stable isomer, owing to the intramolecular H-bonding interaction. For example, the 2- E monomer is lower in energy than 2- Z by 4.26 kcal/mol (Figure ), given intramolecular H-bonding in the E isomer (O···H–N distance = 1.81 Å). Although the H-bonded 9- E conformer is found to be 4.20 kcal/mol lower in energy than its Z counterpart (Figure S104), only the latter is obtained from the synthesis. This might be associated with the intermolecular dimeric interaction possible between two Z monomers, which is thermodynamically favored over the monomeric E state.

Solution UV–Vis Profiles

Structural engineering of the RCN–pyrroles 1–7 leads to highly tunable UV–vis absorption profiles in solution (chloroform, 20 μM). Gas-phase excited-state calculations have been performed at the CAM-B3LYP/cc-pVDZ level of theory to elucidate the absorption profiles of the isomers and associated frontier molecular orbital transitions (Figures S105,S106), complementing the solution results (Figures S73–S76, S79). Both the Z and E isomers absorb strongly with a characteristic π → π* transition in the visible region, which is denoted as the primary absorption band, along with higher energy secondary π → π* transitions in the UV region. Careful inspection of the molecular orbital distributions indicates that the lowest energy absorption band can be attributed to a HOMO → LUMO transition for both the Z and E isomers (Figure ). The absorption peak maximum of the E isomer obtained both in solution and using TD-DFT is more red-shifted than its Z counterpart, which can be attributed to the alteration in their ground- and excited-state molecular orbital energy levels, leading to a lower HOMO–LUMO gap in case of the E isomers. We have explored how the electronic nature of the substituents at the pyrrole moiety of the RCN-based chromophores alter their photophysical characteristics, along with the influence of specific solute–solvent interactions through comprehensive solution and theoretical studies.

Substituent Effects

The UV–vis absorption spectra of compounds 1–7 were first recorded in the same solvent (chloroform) to scrutinize the effect of structure on their photophysical properties (Figure , Table ). The primary absorption peaks of 1- Z (436 nm) and 1- E (466 nm) give a Δλmax of 30 nm. A very similar absorption profile was obtained for 7, which suggests that the alkyl chain length of the RCN unit does not exert any influence on the optical properties of these chromophores (Figure S77c). A considerable bathochromic shift of the primary absorption bands is observed for the π-extended chromophores 2–6, as the greater extent of electron delocalization influences the difference between the FMO energy levels, resulting in a lower-energy π → π* electronic transition. The difference in the Z and E isomer absorption profiles is also enhanced for these compounds, typically preferable for more efficient photoisomerization. Compounds 2- Z and 2- E, bearing an electron-donating para-methoxybenzene unit, absorb at 492 and 534 nm, respectively, with a Δλmax = 42 nm (Figure ). Replacing the EDG methoxy (–OMe) with an EWG cyano (–CN) group in compounds 3- Z and 3- E results in a hypsochromic shift, with λmax values of 470 and 513 nm (Δλmax = 43 nm), respectively–well understood from the larger HOMO–LUMO energy gap for compound 3 (Tables S6,S7). Interestingly, when the regiochemistry of the EDG at the pyrrole unit is altered, a further blue shift is observed for both 4- Z and 4- E, with λmax at 462 and 493 nm (Δλmax = 31 nm). This highlights the lesser extent of π-electron delocalization and weaker effective conjugation in 4, compared to compounds 2 and 3, which can result in notable alteration of absorption profiles (Figure ).

Compound 5 with an octyloxy side chain at the donor unit shows a 2–3 nm red-shifted absorption spectra (Figure ) for both the Z (λmax = 495 nm) and E isomer (λmax = 536 nm) compared to 2. This might be justified by the stronger inductive effect of the longer octyl chain at the electron-rich center, although it has a minor influence on the optical properties. The TD-DFT predicted UV–vis profiles of the individual Z and E isomers, along with the trend observed in their maximum absorbance difference, match quite well with the experimental results (Figure S81).

Solvent Effects

Given the tunability of the absorption profiles due to substituent effects, we next explored the influence of the solvent environment on optical properties (Table ). We selected three chromophores (compounds 1–3) with distinct structural features and studied them in three solvents with different polarities (Figure a)–toluene, chloroform, and acetonitrile (MeCN). According to the Reichardt solvent polarity parameter, E T(30) (in kcal/mol), the polarity order is MeCN (46.0) > chloroform (39.1) > toluene (33.9). , For all three chromophores we observed that in less polar solvents (toluene and chloroform) the absorption profiles of the Z and E isomers are comparatively similar, while a considerable blue-shift is observed in the more polar MeCN (Figures S73–S75). Taking compound 2- Z as an example (Figure b–d), a 9 nm hypsochromic shift is observed upon changing the solvent from chloroform (λmax = 492 nm) to acetonitrile (λmax = 483 nm), while the shift for isomer 2- E is 15 nm (λmax = 534 nm in chloroform, and λmax = 519 nm in MeCN). This negative solvatochromism can be explained by the larger ground state stabilization of these chromophores in a more polar solvent, with relatively less stabilization in the excited state–which results in a higher energy electronic transition. ,

More interestingly, the E isomer is found to be more sensitive to the solvent medium than the Z isomer, which can be attributed to differential solvent-induced stabilization of their ground states. The hypsochromic spectral shift in MeCN is primarily associated with the lowest energy absorption band (the HOMO → LUMO transition). In a polar medium, the extent of solvent-induced stabilization of the HOMO is expected to be greater for the Z isomer, given its larger ground state dipole moment (9.52 D for 1- Z) than that of the E isomer (8.88 D for 1- E). The observations are supported experimentally by the E isomer being much less polar than the Z during purification. Overall, increasing solvent polarity leads to a decrease in Δλmax (Z/E) (e.g., for compound 2, the Δλmax observed in MeCN (34 nm) is lower than in chloroform (42 nm) and toluene (44 nm)), with this trend persisting for compounds 1 and 3 (Figure S80).

Considering the role of intra- and intermolecular H-bonding in the E and Z isomer stability of the RCN–pyrroles, we next evaluated polar aprotic dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and polar protic methanol (Figure a). According to Kamlet–Taft’s analyses, the H-bond acidity and H-bond basicity of the solvents can be expressed as α and β parameters, respectively. , While the DMSO (E T(30) = 45.0 kcal/mol) can act as a H-bond acceptor with a β value of 0.75, methanol (E T(30) = 55.5 kcal/mol) can serve as both a H-bond donor (α = 0.99) and acceptor (β = 0.62). , For the Z isomer of compounds 1–3, we have observed ∼ 10 nm red shift in the primary absorption band in DMSO, compared to methanol. The λmax for 2- Z in DMSO lies at 500 nm, which is at 490 nm in methanol (Figure e). We speculate that the LUMO of the Z isomer can be more stabilized in the stronger H-bond accepting DMSO, through the interaction with the–NH proton of the pyrrole, which can serve as an H-bond donor (Figure a). This leads to a lower HOMO–LUMO energy gap, favoring a lower energy π → π* transition in DMSO compared to methanol (Figure e).

In DMSO, fast E → Z thermal isomerization is observed for these RCN–pyrrole chromophores in DMSO which could be associated with a higher degree of stabilization of the polar transition state, restricting the absorption studies of the pure E isomer in this solvent. However, the λmax for 2- E in polar methanol (522 nm) is blue-shifted from less polar solvents (∼534 nm) and more comparable to MeCN (519 nm). Overall, these π-conjugated RCN–pyrroles are significantly influenced by the solvent medium due to distinct solvation effects through solute–solvent interactions. While negative solvatochromism is observed by changing solvent polarity, their sensitivity to the distinct H-bonding solvents is an attractive feature and critical to understand for potential downstream applications.

Z/E Photoisomerization Studies of Compounds 1–7 in Solution

1H NMR Spectroscopy.To investigate the Z/E photoisomerization behavior of the RCN–pyrrole chromophores (Table ), we have used 1H NMR spectroscopy and determined the photostationary state distributions (PSD) upon selective visible-light excitation (λirr = 404, 454, 523, and 595 nm). The specifications of the irradiation sources are detailed in the Supporting Information (Figure S53). For 1–7, a solution (chloroform-d, 15 mM) of pure Z or E isomer was prepared, which was then exposed to selective photoirradiation of the appropriate wavelength to promote Z → E or E → Z isomerization. The extent of isomerization is dependent on the separation of the Z/E absorption profiles and the excitation wavelength. Compound 1 undergoes quantitative (>99%) E → Z isomerization upon 523 nm irradiation (Figure S54), whereas the PSD obtained for Z → E isomerization (λirr = 404 nm) is 17/83 (1- Z/1- E). Compound 7, which differs only in the alkyl chain length from compound 1, shows a very similar PSS composition (Figure S66). This can be attributed to their near-identical absorption profiles. To our delight, the π-extended compound 2 (Figure S57), which possesses an increased absorption Δλmax between the Z/E isomers, shows bidirectionally quantitative photoisomerization using alternating irradiation at 404 nm (Z → E) and 595 nm (E → Z).

Compound 3 (Figure ) bearing a p-C6H4–CN unit at the pyrrole-end undergoes quantitative Z → E isomerization (λirr = 404 nm), and 523 nm excitation of 3- E results in 85% E → Z conversion (PSD: 85/15 3- Z/3- E). Compound 4 (Figure S63), with a different regiochemistry of the p-C6H4–OMe unit than compound 2 and a lesser Δλmax (31 nm), also shows quantitative E → Z photoisomerization (λirr = 523 nm). The highest PSD obtained for 4- Z → 4- E conversion (λirr = 454 nm) is 24/76 (4- Z/4- E). For compound 5 (Figure S64), near quantitative (98%) Z → E isomerization (λirr = 404 nm) was obtained, whereas a PSS composition of 83/17 (5- Z/5- E) was achieved for E → Z isomerization (λirr = 595 nm). Compound 6 (Figure ) bearing two bulky tert-butyl groups at the pyrrole-end also shows near quantitative Z/E isomerization. PSDs (6- Z/6- E) of 5/95 and 89/11 were observed corresponding to Z → E (λirr = 404 nm) and E → Z isomerization (λirr = 595 nm). The different PSDs obtained for the chromophores are associated with their structural modification and thereby distinct UV–vis profiles in the Z and E states. While the substitution patterns can regulate the isomerization efficiency, we have generally observed (near)­quantitative conversion. However, a more suitable choice of photoirradiation wavelength could improve the PSDs in a few cases.

We have also monitored photoisomerization behavior using NMR spectroscopy in different solvents (toluene and acetonitrile) using compounds 1–3 to quantitatively analyze their efficiency in different solvents (Table ). Compound 1 undergoes quantitative E → Z isomerization (λirr = 523 nm) in both toluene and MeCN. The PSS (1- Z/1- E) composition obtained for Z → E conversion (λirr = 404 nm) is 15/85 in toluene (Figure S55) and 26/74 in MeCN (Figure S56). For 2- E in toluene (Figure a), 97% E → Z isomerization (λirr = 595 nm) is observed, which results in quantitative Z → E conversion upon subsequent excitation using a 404 nm LED. In acetonitrile (Figure b), 2- Z undergoes 93% Z → E isomerization (λirr = 404 nm), which was followed by 595 nm irradiation (E → Z conversion) to obtain a PSD of 85/15 (2- Z/2- E). Compound 3 undergoes quantitative Z → E conversion (λirr = 404 nm) in both toluene and MeCN. The PSDs (3- Z/3- E) obtained for 3- E → 3- Z isomerization (λirr = 523 nm) are 94/6 in toluene (Figure S61) and 86/14 in MeCN (Figure S62). The role of solvent polarity on the PSDs, and isomer addressability, correlates with the solvent-modulated absorption profiles discussed earlier. Overall, NMR studies showcase well-behaved reversible photoisomerization with excellent PSDs and without any evidence of photoinduced side-reactions. The outstanding efficiency using all visible light excitation observed in both halogenated and nonhalogenated solvents with different polarities is highly desirable.

UV–Vis Spectroscopy

The photochromic behavior of the RCN–pyrrole conjugates 1–7 (chloroform, 20 μM) was also analyzed using UV–vis spectroscopy to investigate the spectral changes upon Z/E photoisomerization. For 1- Z in chloroform (Figure a), upon irradiation using 404 and 454 nm LEDs, a red shift of the absorption profile is observed, associated with the Z → E isomerization, with the highest spectral change obtained with 404 nm. No isomerization is observed upon 523 nm irradiation as the Z isomer does not absorb at this wavelength (Figure S82). For E → Z photoisomerization, the largest extent of spectral difference is observed at 523 nm excitation compared to 404 and 454 nm (Figure a). Similarly, for compounds 3, 4, and 7, the highest extent of Z → E isomerization is found using λirr = 404 nm, whereas irradiation at 523 nm resulted in the most E → Z conversion (Figures S88, S91, S94). As compounds 1, 3, 4, and 7 do not absorb around the 595 nm region, this excitation source could not be employed for monitoring their Z/E isomerization. As compounds 2, 5, and 6 possess more red-shifted absorption profiles than the other molecules, a 595 nm LED was used to monitor E → Z photoisomerization, resulting in the highest conversion (Figures S85, S92,S93). A similar extent of Z → E photoisomerization is observed upon 404 and 454 nm irradiation for these compounds (Figures S85, S92,S93). Overall, UV–vis photoisomerization studies demonstrate a large spectral shift of the π–π* transition band upon structural tuning, along with excellent Z/E photoconversion, consistent with the NMR outcomes.

Pure 1- Z or 1- E (black trace), and 1- Z/1- E mixtures obtained after 404 nm irradiation (pink trace), 454 nm irradiation (blue trace), and 523 nm irradiation (green trace) are depicted.

Next, we have evaluated the isomerization efficiency of compounds 1–3 in different polarity solvents, toluene and acetonitrile, where well-controlled Z/E photoisomerization is observed with the PSS obtained upon 30–300 s of selective excitation. For compound 1 in both solvents, the highest extent of Z → E isomerization has been achieved upon 404 nm irradiation, whereas 523 nm yielded the highest E → Z conversion (Figure b,c). For compounds 2 and 3, the highest E → Z isomerization is obtained using 595 and 523 nm LEDs, respectively (Figures S86,S87, S89,S90). For both 2 and 3, 404 nm excitation results in the highest extent of Z → E conversion (Figures S86,S87, S89,S90). Although these photochromic molecules show negative solvatochromism, the well-separated Z/E absorption profiles in all solvents lead to significant spectral changes upon photoisomerization. While the extent of photoisomerization is primarily irradiation wavelength dependent, we have observed that the lowest energy absorption bands of a Z/E mixture (λirr = 404 or 454 nm) are considerably more structured in the least polar toluene (Figure b) than in most polar acetonitrile (Figure c). This fine structuring of the absorption profiles in nonpolar solvents is also well-known in closely related hemiindigoid photoswitches. ,

Additionally, we have evaluated the reliability of reversible Z/E photoisomerization reactions by performing photoswitching studies with compound 1 in different polarity solvents using alternating 404 and 523 nm irradiation. Compound 1- E has been irradiated in solution (20 μM, toluene) with a shorter wavelength light source (λirr = 523 nm) to promote E → Z isomerization to obtain a PSS, while exposing the PSS mixture to a longer wavelength of irradiation (λirr = 404 nm) promoted Z → E isomerization until a new PSS is reached. Excitingly, even after ten photoswitching cycles, essentially no photodegradation has been observed (Figure ). This excellent fatigue-resistant and facile photoswitching performance in both toluene (Figure ) and acetonitrile (Figure S118) using visible light excitation is extremely encouraging.

Photochemical Behavior of Compounds 8 and 9

Following the investigation of the RCN–pyrrole molecules, we were encouraged to explore closely related rhodanine- and imidazole-based systems, when appended with pyrrole and RCN scaffolds, respectively. The UV–vis profile (chloroform) of the rhodanine–pyrrole chromophore 8 shows absorption peak maxima at 428 nm for the Z isomer and 457 nm for the E (Figure a). The blue shift (8–9 nm) observed for the 8- Z and 8- E isomers compared to compound 1 (Z and E) can be attributed to the presence of a stronger electron-withdrawing dicyanomethylene unit of the RCN acceptor (in 1 compared to the thiocarbonyl group in rhodanine) leading to smaller HOMO–LUMO energy gaps (Tables S5 and S10). This difference in the absorption profiles (of compounds 1 and 8) can also be related to the free –NH present in the rhodanine core, compared to an alkylated nitrogen center in the RCN. , The ΔE HOMO–LUMO are obtained using DFT calculations as 3.271 eV for 1- Z (and 3.094 eV for 1- E), and 3.301 eV for 8- Z (and 3.133 eV for 8- E). The optical properties of compound 9 have been assessed only in acetonitrile, due to its poor solubility in chloroform, with the absorption maximum of 9- Z at 394 nm (Figure b). The 35 nm blue shift (in MeCN) in the absorption maximum of 9- Z (λmax = 394 nm) compared to 1- Z (λmax = 429 nm) highlights the impact of the donor heterocycles on the FMOs, which results in a higher-energy π–π* transition for 9- Z. Upon investigation of photoisomerization behavior, a small change in the photostationary state (PSS) Z/E absorption profiles were obtained upon 371 and 404 nm excitation compared to pure 9- Z, which describes a lesser extent of Z → E isomerization (Figure b). However, significant spectral changes are observed for compound 8 in chloroform, upon 523 nm irradiation for E → Z and using 404 nm for Z → E conversion, confirming a large extent of photoisomerization (Figure S95). These results demonstrate the importance of suitable substituent choices at both donor and acceptor units to control photophysical and photoisomerization properties.

We have observed a striking difference in the photochemical outcomes for compounds 8 and 9 by 1H NMR spectroscopy. Although reversible Z/E photoisomerization (in chloroform-d) is observed for compound 8 with excellent PSDs (Figure S67), a thermal E → Z relaxation (chloroform-d, 25 °C) could be identified with a stationary state distribution of 30/70 (8- Z/8- E). To precisely determine the PSS composition (8- Z/8- E) and mitigate the effect of the competing thermal isomerization process in chloroform (Figure S116), we have tested the Z/E photoisomerization in DMSO (15 mM), considering the better thermal stability of 8- Z and 8- E possibly owing to intermolecular H–bonding between the–NH of the rhodanine core and the solvent in both isomers (Figure a, vide supra). The PSD (8- Z/8- E) in DMSO (Figure ) is obtained as 93/7 for E → Z (λirr = 523 nm) and 46/54 for Z → E isomerization (λirr = 404 nm). These findings reveal that replacing the RCN moiety (in 1) with rhodanine (in 8) affects both photo- and thermal-isomerization properties and clearly emphasizes the role of substituent effects on designing a photoswitch scaffold with desirable efficiency and thermal stability.

Solution (15 mM, DMSO-d 6) photoisomerization studies (monitored by 1H NMR spectroscopy) of compound 8: pure E isomer (bottom, black spectrum); PSS spectra obtained upon 523 nm irradiation (E → Z isomerization, green spectrum), and subsequent 404 nm irradiation (Z → E isomerization, pink spectrum).

Interestingly, with the RCN–imidazole conjugate 9- Z, no well-defined appearance of the E isomer signal is obtained upon 404 nm excitation (acetonitrile-d 3, 10 mM) within 1 h (Figure S69). However, upon prolonged irradiation (24 h) using 404 nm (Figure S70) and 371 nm LEDs (Figure S71), a gradual disappearance of the Z isomer peaks is observed, along with generation of a few NMR signals in both the aromatic and aliphatic regions which do not correspond to either the Z or E isomer. A sharp singlet at 5.07 ppm, accompanied by other newly formed signals, might correspond to a pericyclic product formed through tandem electrocyclization followed by 1,5-sigmatropic rearrangement under these irradiation conditions (Figure S72). We propose that one of the aromatic protons (marked in red) in the imidazole unit (compound 9) participates in a 1,5-hydride shift to form the cyclized photoproduct which causes its upfield chemical shift to ∼5 ppm (Figure S72). This shielding effect is characteristic as we have observed similar photoinduced side reactions competing with well-controlled Z/E photoisomerization for related RCN and fluorinated INCN–thiophene molecules. This in-depth NMR and UV–vis study comprehensively highlights the contrasting photochemistry of the RCN–pyrroles and closely related heterocyclic structures.

Thermal Isomerization Studies

We have observed tunable thermal relaxation behavior of the RCN–pyrrole chromophores, originating from substituent and solvent effects. While the excellent thermal stability of 1- Z and 1- E was previously reported by our group in solvents like tetrachloroethane and acetonitrile, even at elevated temperatures, in this work we have examined how different substituents and solvent environments affect the inherent stability of the isomers. While for compounds 2, 5, and 6, we have not observed any significant E → Z thermal isomerization in chloroform-d (monitored at 50 °C) within 24 h (Figures S109, S111, S113), an interesting trend in Z → E thermal conversion is observed. For compound 5 bearing a long octyloxy side chain at the donor unit, we have obtained ∼82% thermal Z → E conversion (Figure S112), whereas compound 2- Z with a methoxy substitution results in only ∼12% Z → E conversion (Figure S110). We postulate that the intramolecular H-bonding interaction present in the E isomers might not be affected by the alkyl chain length. However, the more flexible, longer octyloxy chain (in 5- Z) might considerably influence the stability of the Z isomer through restricting the dimeric association by intermolecular H-bonding, leading to faster Z → E isomerization (Figure S115). We have also tested compound 6, which shows only ∼17% Z → E thermal isomerization under similar conditions; possibly the bulky and rigid tert-butyl side chains do not affect the H-bond interactions between the monomers (Figure S114).

The dimer formation in the Z isomer has been verified using variable temperature 1H NMR (tetrachloroethane-d 2, 15 mM) using compound 6- Z (Figure S117). A gradual shielding (Δδ = 0.24 ppm) of the–NH proton peak is observed upon increasing the temperature (25 °C → 90 °C), confirming the disruption of the dimerization at high temperatures. Moreover, a gradual upfield shift of the–NH proton of 5- Z is observed from the Z → E thermal relaxation experiment at 50 °C (Figure S113). This further confirms that as the Z isomer concentration in the isomeric mixture is decreasing over time (from 0 to 24 h), the possibility of intermolecular H-bonding between the monomers is also diminishing, resulting in the shielding of the proton resonance. However, the–NH chemical shift in the E isomer peak remains unchanged (from 5 to 24 h), consistent with strong intramolecular H-bonding (Figure S113). Gas-phase DFT studies (at the B3LYP/6–31 + G­(d) level of theory) also predict a favorable binding enthalpy of the Z dimer for compound 2 (Figure ), which is calculated as – 13.4 kcal/mol (−6.71 kcal/mol per 2- Z monomer)–complementing well the experimental results.

Putative dimer formation of 2- Z through intermolecular H-bonding interactions. The distance between the carbonyl oxygen (O2) and the–NH proton is shown in parentheses.

The thermal E → Z isomerization can be significantly influenced by the solvent–solute interactions. Isomerization experiments have been performed in H-bonding capable solvents like DMSO and methanol at 50 °C, using compound 1. While in DMSO (Figure S107), quantitative 1- E → 1- Z conversion is observed within 3 h, it took >24 h to obtain ∼95% isomerization in methanol (Figure S108). The faster isomerization in DMSO again highlights the greater thermodynamic stability of 1- Z in the strong H-bond accepting solvent DMSO versus methanol, which can act as both an H-bond donor and acceptor (Figure a, vide supra). These studies show that the thermal stability of the photoswitching molecules can be modulated through suitable functional groups and appropriate solvent choices.

Conclusions

We have reported a library of conveniently accessible dicyanorhodanine-pyrrole chromophores featuring systematic variation of substituents on the π-extended pyrrole unit. These structural modifications effectively modulate the electronic properties and associated molecular orbital energies, resulting in distinct UV–vis absorption characteristics for both the Z and E isomers. Despite these differences, all compounds exhibit highly efficient and reversible Z/E photoisomerization, achieving excellent photostationary state distribution (PSDs) obtained upon selective visible-light excitation. A comprehensive investigation of solvent effects reveals negative solvatochromism for both isomers across solvents of varying polarity. Importantly, the substantial spectral separation between the Z and E isomers in all solvents enables (near)­quantitative photoisomerization in most cases. Studies in H-bonding solvents (methanol and DMSO) further elucidates the influence of solvent–solute interactions on photophysical, photochemical, and thermal isomerization behavior. The Z isomer, capable of intermolecular association, displays distinct absorption features in these media. DMSO is found to stabilize the Z isomer more effectively than methanol, leading to a faster E → Z thermal conversion. Moreover, the thermal stability of the Z isomer is strongly dependent on the nature of the alkyl substituents at the pyrrole moiety. Overall, the excellent tunability of their photophysical behavior upon structural and solvent environment modulation, preserving facile photoswitching performance, portrays the RCN-pyrroles as a suitable candidate for smart-materials applications.

Additionally, two closely related photoswitch scaffolds, rhodanine-pyrrole and RCN-imidazole derivatives, have been synthesized. While the rhodanine-pyrrole chromophore exhibits well-defined photoisomerization behavior, the RCN-imidazole appears to undergo a series of pericyclic reactions upon selective photoirradiation, as monitored using 1H NMR spectroscopy. We have also highlighted that along with the π-extended pyrrole unit, the electronic nature of the (dicyano)­rhodanine core also could tune the absorption profiles of these chromophores. Collectively, these findings underscore the critical roles of molecular substitution and solvent environment in controlling the photophysical properties, photoswitching efficiency, and thermal isomerization behavior of the π-conjugated photochromic RCN molecules, providing valuable guidelines for designing efficient and tunable photoresponsive systems.

Experimental Methods

General Methods

Reagents and solvents were purchased from commercial sources and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. THF and CH2Cl2 were degassed in a 20 L drum and passed through two sequential activated alumina purification columns under a positive argon atmosphere. Thin layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on SiO2-60 F254 aluminum plates with visualization by UV light. Column chromatography was performed using silica gel technical grade, pore size 60 Å, 230–400 mesh particle size, 40–63 μm particle size from Sigma-Aldrich. Due to the photosensitivity of the RCN-functionalized products, the reaction vessels were covered with aluminum foil during the synthesis of compounds 1–9. In an effort to minimize unintentional photoisomerization, aluminum foil was used to cover all glassware during purification processes, such that any source of excessive light exposure was eliminated while samples were dissolved in solution.

Computational Methods

Ground-state geometries were optimized in the gas phase at the B3LYP/6–31 + G­(d) level of theory as implemented in Gaussian 09. All final geometries were verified to be a true minima using standard frequency calculations to ensure the absence of imaginary frequencies. All octyl chains were truncated to methyl groups to reduce the computational cost. Thus, due to structural resemblance of compounds 1 and 7, and 2 and 5, calculations are only reported for compounds 1 and 2. Excited-state calculations were performed at the CAMB3LYP/cc-pVDZ level of theory in the gas phase starting with the structural coordinates obtained from previously optimized geometries. The geometries and the frontier molecular orbitals have been rendered using Avogadro and CYLview software.

UV–vis Photoisomerization Experiments

Solution absorption spectra were measured on a PerkinElmer Lambda 25 dual beam absorption spectrometer and a Cary 100 Bio spectrophotometer in chloroform using 20 μM solutions in 1 cm quartz cells for all RCN-functionalized target molecules 1–9. Spectrophotometric grade solvents were used for all absorption studies. Solutions were prepared and transferred into a quartz cuvette for immediate UV–vis absorption measurements. The cuvettes were removed from the spectrophotometer and covered with a glass container while irradiated at an appropriate wavelength of excitation (λirr = 371, 404, 454, 523, and 595 nm) and absorption spectra were recorded to monitor the changes in the UV–vis spectra attributed to the isomerization process. The samples were irradiated for 60–300 s until no changes in the UV–vis spectra were observed, indicating that the respective photostationary states (PSS) were reached.

NMR Photoisomerization Experiments

1H NMR spectra were recorded on an INOVA-500 (1H at 500 MHz) and a Bruker 600 MHz spectrometer (1H at 600 MHz). To determine the photostationary state isomeric composition of each RCN-functionalized target molecule (1–9), a 15 mM stock solution in chloroform-d was prepared. Approximately 0.5 mL of each stock solution was transferred to a conventional glass NMR tube. The samples were exposed to an appropriate irradiation wavelength (λirr = 371, 404, 454, 523, and 595 nm) and 1H NMR spectra were recorded to monitor the reaction progress. The integration ratios of signals corresponding to Z isomers and E isomers were compared to calculate a relative percentage of each species in solution. The samples were irradiated for 1–4 h (unless specified otherwise) and 1H NMR spectra were recorded until no changes in Z/E integration ratios were observed.
