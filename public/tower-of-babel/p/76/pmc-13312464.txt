Mechanical venous thrombectomy may provide a cost-effective treatment option for patients with iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis
Authors: Muhammad Saad Hafeez, Sahar Alimohamadi, Rabih A. Chaer, Kenneth J. Smith, Mohammad H. Eslami, Natalie D. Sridharan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMechanical venous thrombectomy (MVT) has gained popularity as an alternative to catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDL) and medical management (MM) of iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (DVT), potentially combining the benefits of a minimally invasive approach with a lower risk of bleeding and shorter hospital stay. Emerging evidence shows MVT to be an effective tool in the DVT treatment. However, the cost-effectiveness of MVT is not well understood.MethodsIncorporating data from Acute Venous Thrombosis: Thrombus Removal with Adjunctive Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis Trial, retrospectively published data, and local cost data, we compared the cost-effectiveness of these three treatment modalities (MVT, CDL, and MM) for iliofemoral DVT (IFDVT) using a Markov state-transition model to quantify lifetime costs in 2022 US dollars and effectiveness in quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). We accounted for perioperative bleeding, long-term risks of post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), and reintervention. Based on the Acute Venous Thrombosis: Thrombus Removal with Adjunctive Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis trial, we assumed a cohort average age of 51 years. A cost-effectiveness acceptability threshold of $100,000/QALY gained was used. Sensitivity analyses were performed. A treatment was dominated when it was less effective and more costly than other options.ResultsIn the base-case scenario, MVT cost $8092/QALY gained compared with MM, well below the frequently cited $100,000/QALY gained threshold. CDL was dominated, largely due to a greater periprocedural bleeding risk. In one-way sensitivity analyses, if the moderate to severe risk of PTS after MVT was >16.0% (base-case risk, 6.0%), then it would no longer be economically favorable compared with MM at its current procedural cost. In a probabilistic sensitivity analysis, varying all parameters simultaneously over distributions, MVT was favored in 93.6% of model iterations at $100,000/QALY and MM in 4.0% of model iterations.ConclusionsAt current cost and outcomes, MVT appears to be a cost-effective intervention for IFDVT compared with anticoagulation alone. Despite marginal decreases in long-term PTS rates, CDL was economically unfavorable. Patients with IFDVT should be considered for MVT from a cost-effectiveness perspective.

Iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (IFDVT) accounts for significant annual health care spending in the United States, not only at the index event, but also due to costs associated with follow-up care and the management of post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS).1 The development of PTS after the index proximal DVT can lead to significantly reduced patient quality of life and translates to substantial costs for the management of symptoms and associated venous wounds. In the past two decades, interventional procedures have become available that provide other options for IFDVT treatment beyond medical management (MM), which primarily consists of anticoagulation.

Catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDL) allows localized administration of thrombolytic agents, thereby allowing the delivery of a drug that may dissolve the clot burden, while preventing its systemic effects. Data from CaVenT (Catheter-Directed Venous Thrombolysis in Acute Iliofemoral Vein Thrombosis) suggest that CDL may reduce PTS rates, whereas ATTRACT (Acute Venous Thrombosis: Thrombus Removal with Adjunctive Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis) data suggest that intervention leads to a reduced severity of PTS rather than a reduced incidence.2,3 Despite these reported positive results, CDL treatment typically requires multiple sessions and intensive care unit monitoring and is associated with a greater risk of bleeding events compared with anticoagulation alone.3,4 The advent of mechanical venous thrombectomy (MVT) expands treatment options for patients with IFDVT, and particularly for those at a high risk of bleeding.

Over the past few years, a variety of mechanical thrombectomy devices have entered the market that can directly address clot burden. These devices may be based on aspiration, rheolytic approach, maceration, or mechanical capture of the acute thrombi. These devices combine the benefits of rapid reduction in clot burden as well as the reduced risk of bleeding events due to thrombolytic agents.5 Although the CLOUT (ClotTriever Outcomes) registry provides robust data about clinical and patient-reported outcomes for patients undergoing DVT treatment, it is a single-arm, single-device registry and does not provide a direct comparison with other treatment options.5 In the absence of a true randomized three-arm analysis, it is difficult to determine which option is statistically superior. Moreover, the clinical complexity of DVT and patient-driven factors may determine the most appropriate treatment option for a particular patient. In cases of such clinical equipoise, knowledge of the cost-effectiveness of available treatment options can further inform decision-making. To date, there is no study comparing the cost-effectiveness of all three treatment options in the US payer system. Our study aimed to bridge this gap using a robust Markov model analysis.

Methods

We performed a literature review of existing publications to inform a decision model estimating the cost-effectiveness of index therapeutic options for IFDVT: MM, MVT, and CDL. Costs were obtained from ATTRACT and local cost data, after adjusting for inflation to 2023 US dollars. Effectiveness was defined as quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). This study was exempt from institutional board review oversight and patient consent was not required.

Decision analytic model

We developed a Markov state-transition model to predict long-term outcomes for patients being treated for IFDVT with the three therapeutic options. During the initial event, patients could experiences treatment failure, in which case they would require an alternate procedure (Fig 1). In addition to this technical outcome, patients could experience long-term outcomes such as minor bleeding events, major bleeding events, death, mild PTS, and moderate to severe PTS. Patients could exist in a state of mild PTS, moderate to severe PTS, alive after a minor bleeding event, alive after major bleeding event, death after a major bleeding event, or death. Utilities

Utility weights or the quality-adjusted life index for various health starts were obtained from the literature, including ATTRACT and the CLOUT registry. Health-related quality of life data in ATTRACT and the CLOUT registry were gathered prospectively using the Medical Outcome Study Short-Form 36. The specific utility weights for each health state and the disutility associated with health states during follow-up were assumed to follow a beta-distribution. Base-case utility values are summarized in Table I.

Costs

For MM and CDL, procedural costs were obtained from ATTRACT data. For the MVT arm of the study, we obtained data for the procedural costs from the University of Pittsburg Medical Center finance department, by evaluating the mean charges for patients treated with various devices, after adjusting for cost-to-charge ratios. Costs of the care of complications and those associated with PTS were obtained from the ATTRACT data. All future costs were discounted at 3% per year. All costs were approximated with gamma-distributions in Table I.

Analysis of cost-effectiveness and sensitivity analysis

Effectiveness was defined in QALYs. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) was calculated by dividing the difference in mean discounted costs by the differences in mean QALYs between any two treatment strategies. If a therapeutic option generated more QALYs at less cost than an intervention, the former dominated the latter. Willingness to pay (WTP) was set at $100,000 per QALY, as has been used in prior analyses.6, 7, 8

To explore the sensitivity of the analysis to changes in parameter values, a range of univariable sensitivity analyses were performed. We specifically analyzed changes in the cost of MVT, to better understand variation across different locations, and the probability of moderate to severe PTS after treatment on outcomes of the model. We also performed a probabilistic sensitivity analysis in which all parameters were randomly varied simultaneously over predefined probability distributions for 1000 iterations of the model. Probability distributions are noted in Table I. To evaluate the effect of different starting ages, we conducted a sensitivity analysis at advancing starting ages, while adjusting for competing risks between events and mortality.

We then performed multiple one-way sensitivity analyses to examine the impact of potentially modifiable parameters on the index results of our analysis. To examine the role MVT cost, we varied it from $0 to $80,000 from the base scenario cost $22,360. We found that, once the overall cost of providing MVT exceeded $71,000, it was no longer cost-effective (Fig 3). We also examined the incidence of moderate to severe PTS after MVT, and varied this value from 0.0% to 20.0% from a base case value of 6% to evaluate at what threshold the upfront costs of MVT are unable to offset the decreased QALYs due to PTS. Discussion

Thromboembolic disorders are a leading cause of mortality, with significant and long-lasting impacts and financial burden, with limited global awareness of this pathology spectrum.9 Published data suggest that interventional techniques may decrease the incidence and/or severity of PTS after the index event. Our study used a Markov model to analyze lifetime costs and benefits associated with treatment options for IFDVT. Our results indicate that MVT offers a cost-effective approach in treating IFDVT. MVT dominated CDL due to the bleeding risks associated with CDL. Our analysis showed that, despite increased overall costs, MVT continued to be economically favorable from a US payer perspective over a wide range of procedural costs and PTS risks and with simultaneous variation of multiple parameters in a probabilistic sensitivity analysis.

Beyond costs associated with index admissions for medically treated venous thromboembolism, which may account for US$7 to US$10 billion annually, long-term costs due to anticoagulation, follow-up care for cardiovascular concerns, wound care, and interventions for PTS, as well as costs associated with possible recurrence and bleeding complications, may occur.1 Given increased health care costs in patients with PTS, endovascular interventions offer an attractive option to reduce the initial clot burden and possibly reduce PTS incidence, despite the higher upfront costs of procedural intervention. A recent cost analysis by Pouncey et al10 in the UK suggests that the lifetime costs incurred by patients with DVT treated only with oral anticoagulation exceed those treated with intervention and afford fewer QALYs. Data from CaVenT suggest that CDL may produce a 14.4% lower risk of PTS, whereas data from the CLOUT registry found that MVT was associated with a lower PTS risk compared with MM (28% vs 17%; P < .001).11,12 A recent follow-up analysis of ATTRACT also suggests that, after an IFDVT, CDL may significantly reduce lasting symptoms of post-PE syndrome, characterized by persistent symptoms and reduced exercise tolerance after the index event (0.14 per patient receiving CDL vs 0.24 per patient not receiving CDL; P = .036).13

With the increasing utilization of endovascular approaches to treat IFDVT, there is a wide and expanding array of devices in the market. A discussion of costs is complex, because the overall cost with any given treatment includes not just the upfront costs of the device, but also several ancillary costs. Catheter-directed therapies can allow for the direct delivery of a thrombolytic agent to the area of concern; these patients often require intensive monitoring for prolonged infusion of the lytic, followed by repeat fluoroscopic evaluation of the clot burden.14 CDL may also require follow-up procedures and interventions and the use of adjuncts such as stents.15 In fact, additional adjuncts such as balloon angioplasty and stenting are needed in up to one-quarter of patients, but it is unclear whether this practice translates to improved patient satisfaction.16,17 Costs would also be further increased by the need to switch to an alternative therapy for bailout. A comparison of MVT and combined MVT with CDL shows that excellent iliac stent patency of 72.2% to 74.7% can be achieved, provided the initial treatment addresses the disease appropriately, suggesting that the choice of initial modality does not influence procedural follow-up costs.18 In contrast, published experience supports the economics of mechanical thrombectomy because it is a single-session and effective treatment option. The CLOUT registry found that 99.4% of patients were treated in a single session and only 2.2% required admission to the intensive care unit.19 A comparison with the pharmacomechanical catheter-directed thrombolysis arm of ATTRACT showed that MVT achieved a greater reduction in thrombotic burden (91.0% vs 67.7%; P < .001), almost four times higher rates of single-session treatment (97.7% vs 26.9%; P < .001) and a shorter overall length of stay (1.18 overnights vs 3.46 overnights; P < .001).4 MVT also generally reduced or eliminated the need for lytics; hence, it is attractive to interventionalists, because, per ATTRACT estimates, any bleeding event incurs significant cost and disutility, of nearly $12,000 for each incident.15 Our analysis supports that the cost of MVT likely translates to an improved patient experience.

The current literature suggests that interventional therapy can lead to greater symptom resolution. In fact, a subanalysis of ATTRACT suggests that the greatest effects on quality of life are enjoyed by those with greater severity on presentation.20 Patients who are more active at baseline and have a reasonable life expectancy are good candidates for therapeutic interventions given the significant long-term impact of PTS on future productivity and quality of life.21 Technically successful treatment with reduced postprocedure clot burden is associated with improved quality of life and reduced symptom severity. Beyond the index procedure, compliance with compression stockings has been shown to improve quality of life in patients with PTS, so it is comparable with their counterparts without PTS.22 As expected, any requirement for a repeat intervention or stent occlusion can also contributed to poor quality of life.23

In our analysis, we confirmed that MVT is cost-effective in the current US payer system. Given the high costs associated with CDL, including multiple sessions and intensive care unit admissions, MVT dominated CDL. We demonstrated that this remained the case across a wide range of index and follow-up costs. The current literature indicates that MVT can address significant clot burden and achieve excellent results and can achieve economic benefit up until a 16% chance of developing moderate to severe PTS. Our model was robust on probabilistic sensitivity analysis and demonstrated that MVT would remain cost-effective, unless there were significant improvements in the results achieved by CDL or substantially lower treatment costs.

There are several limitations inherent to any cost-effectiveness analysis that must be discussed. The models may not be applicable to the real-world scenario where patient care may be directed by various other factors. The devices used in ATTRACT have since been succeeded by different endovascular options, and hence the cost-effectiveness model may not apply. In the real world, bundled payments may also impact the costs to the institutions and make certain options more economically favorable. We attempted to rely on published data; however, where needed, we relied on expert opinion. We did not incorporate any disutility associated with procedures, which may potentially bias against anticoagulation alone. Moreover, given that there are no cost-effectiveness data from the CLOUT registry, we used hospital costs from our institution; however, the devices used may not coincide with those described in CLOUT. Last, this analysis is limited to US dollars and may not be applicable to other health care systems with a different payer structure.

Conclusions

MVT is a cost-effective treatment option for IFDVT treatment, compared with anticoagulation and CDL. Interventional approaches were associated with more QALYs gained compared with anticoagulation. Given the higher total costs associated with CDL, MVT provides not only a more economically attractive option, but also a safe treatment option for patients with contraindications to anticoagulation or at high risk of bleeding events.
