Randomized trial of self-adaptive computerized cognitive remediation in schizophrenia
Authors: Yan-ping Ren, Li-na Wang, Shuo Lin, Wei-gang Pan, Xue-quan Zhu, Yi Liu, Han Wu, Dan-di Zhu, Si-yuan Lian, Wen Wang, Wen-qing Jin, Zhi-ming Bian, Zi-heng Zhang, Yi-lang Tang, Long-jun Cai, Gang Wang, Xin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComputerized, self-adaptive cognitive training has emerged as a promising digital therapeutic for cognitive impairment; however, its underlying training mechanisms in schizophrenia remain poorly understood. This double-blind, randomized trial compared self-adaptive difficulty (SAD) with fixed-difficulty (FD) computerized cognitive training program, evaluating both domain-specific cognitive and clinical outcomes, and exploring difficulty-performance coupling as a potential active therapeutic mechanism. Seventy-two patients with schizophrenia (SAD = 41, FD = 31) completed 4 weeks of training, and 40 patients (n = 20 per group) were assessed at week 8. Cognitive outcomes were measured using the Cognitive Index (CI) and MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB), while clinical symptoms were also assessed. Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) revealed significantly greater and faster cognitive improvements in the SAD compared with the FD group in attention and agility, with faster improvement also observed in thinking. Robust difficulty-performance coupling was observed across all cognitive domains. Additionally, positive symptoms improved significantly in the SAD group compared to the FD group at week 4. Self-adaptive cognitive training enhances and accelerates cognitive improvements in schizophrenia, particularly in attention and processing speed, and also benefits positive symptoms. Difficulty-performance coupling may reflect dynamic load calibration as a key therapeutic mechanism in digital cognitive training. Introduction

Computerized cognitive training (CCT), the digital form of conventional cognitive training tasks, is an effective intervention for patients with cognitive impairment caused by diverse neurological disease1,2 and psychiatric diseases including schizophrenia3. Recently, a study suggested that the multidomain, self-adaptive, computerized cognitive training regimen led to a more significant improvement in global cognitive function than the effect of fixed, primary difficulty level tasks across the study in patients with subcortical vascular cognitive impairment4. Evidence for the efficacy of self-adaptive CCT in patients with schizophrenia is lacking.

Cognitive impairment is a core and disabling feature of schizophrenia5, fundamentally disrupting information processing, memory, attention, learning, executive function, and social cognition. These deficits are among the strongest negative predictors of psychosocial recovery6,7 and often weakly responsive to antipsychotic medications8. Although second-generation antipsychotics offer modest cognitive benefits, their clinical utility is limited by side effects such as metabolic complications or anticholinergic burdens that may further compromise cognitive function. CCT has emerged as a potentially effective intervention for cognitive impairment in patients with schizophrenia9,10, and is considered a promising approach for enhancing neuroplasticity11–14. A meta-analysis15 showed that cognitive training significantly improved the global cognition of schizophrenia with a moderate effect size (ES = 0.45), with particularly strong effects in social cognition (ES = 0.65), reasoning/problem solving (ES = 0.57), and social functioning (ES = 0.42), although no significant improvement was observed for clinical symptoms (ES = 0.18). Another systematic review and meta-analysis of 130 randomized clinical trials involving 8851 patients with schizophrenia16 similarly found that cognitive training was associated with improvement in cognition (d = 0.29) and functioning (d = 0.22). However, other studies have reported no meaningful cognitive benefits from cognitive training in schizophrenia17–20, highlighting substantial inconsistency in the literature. These discrepancies may reflect variations in participant characteristics, intervention methodological heterogeneity, and cognitive training components. With respect to patient-related factors, lower level of education, lower global functioning and premorbid IQ, higher baseline symptom severity are associated with diminished training response16. Intervention characteristics also appear to influence efficacy: active and well-trained therapists, structured cognitive strategies, integration with psychosocial rehabilitation, and longer treatment duration are often linked to better outcomes21. Interestingly, previous studies suggested that extending training duration or increasing the number of sessions may not bring additional cognitive benefits22, and there was a dose-dependent effect in CCT and age-specific optimal dosing for cognitive improvement measured weekly using Cognitive Index (CI) in participants with mild cognitive impairment23. Overall, although cognitive training demonstrates promise, further research is needed to clarify its efficacy and to optimize training algorithms for patients with schizophrenia24. Importantly, considering the significant heterogeneity of cognitive impairment profiles in schizophrenia, research must better evaluate domain-specific treatment effects. A particularly understudied issue is the dynamic nature of the training itself. Static interventions that fail to adapt in real time to a patient’s changing performance risk either understimulating the individual (leading to boredom) or overwhelming them, which often results in frustration and dropout.

To bridge this gap, research needs to utilize precision-based algorithms that optimize the “zone of proximal development” for each patient. For this study, we leveraged the cognitive training system, 66nao (www.66nao.com), a multidomain, self-adaptive CCT platform designed to improve neuroplasticity through continuous titration of task difficulty across attention, memory, perception, thinking, and agility. Preliminary data suggest this platform can improve global cognitive function as well as enhance brain plasticity4, but its clinical efficacy and mechanisms have not been rigorously examined in a controlled schizophrenia cohort. More importantly, explicit modeling of the training process allows us to move beyond outcome-only evaluation and identify a putative active ingredient: the coupling between task difficulty and real-time performance as a marker of optimal engagement and therapeutic dosing.

We conducted a randomized, double-blind, active-controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of a 4-week, self-adaptive, multidomain CCT program. Moving beyond traditional global outcome measures, we used the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB)25, along with a high-resolution Cognition Index23,26 to capture granular changes across attention, memory, and executive control. The primary objective of the study was to determine if an adaptive-difficulty algorithm provides a superior “therapeutic dose” compared to fixed-difficulty training. A secondary, mechanistic objective was to quantify difficulty-performance coupling using trial-level linear mixed-effects models, thereby elucidating how adaptive challenge drives learning and informing a precision framework for personalized cognitive rehabilitation in schizophrenia.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Of 108 patients screened, 90 were randomized to the SAD or FD groups (N = 45 for each). At post-training (week 4), 72 patients (41 in the SAD group, 31 in the FD group) completed the study protocol, and 40 completed the 8-week follow-up (20 per group, Fig. 1). The attrition rate differed significantly between groups (Supplementary Table S1), with a higher dropout rate in the FD group (χ² = 5.625, P = 0.018). As shown in Fig. 1, dropout reasons were multifactorial; differences in training characteristics may have contributed to attrition patterns. Baseline antipsychotic medication profiles are presented in Supplementary Table S2. There were no significant differences in sociodemographic information and clinical features at baseline between the two groups (Table 1).

Training exposure and engagement

Overall training exposure(min) was comparable between groups(SAD: 671.05 ± 134.45 vs. FD: 648.65 ± 111.85; t = 0.75, P = 0.455); however, the distribution of training time across cognitive domains differed as a function of protocol design (Supplementary Table S3). As expected by design, only the SAD group demonstrated progressive increases in task difficulty, indicating active engagement with the adaptive algorithm (Supplementary Table S4).

Primary outcome: cognition index change after 4-week training from baseline

There was no significant baseline difference in CI scores (all P > 0.05). LMM models revealed significant group × time interactions favoring SAD for Attention (adjusted B = 8.009, 95% CI [1.116, 14.902], P = 0.023), and Processing Speed/Agility (adjusted B = 14.148, 95% CI [2.492, 25.803], P = 0.018, FDR corrected, Table 2). Post hoc models using multivariable linear regression confirmed greater improvement in Agility (adjusted B = 9.787, 95% CI: 0.445 19.129, P = 0.040) and Attention (adjusted B = 9.320, 95% CI: 2.214 16.426, P = 0.011) in the SAD group, adjusted for training dose (Supplementary Table S5).

The score of MCCB changes after 4-week training and 8-week follow-up from baseline

LMMs showed that, compared with baseline, significant main effects of time were observed after 4 weeks of training for Symbol Coding (B = 2.62, t = 2.07, P = 0.042), Verbal Fluency (B = 2.83, t = 2.04, P = 0.045), Continuous Performance Test-Identical Pairs version (CPT-IP) (B = 2.37, t = 1.92, P = 0.014), and the MCCB total score (B = 1.71, t = 2.14, P = 0.036). In addition, significant Group × Time interactions were detected for CPT-IP (B = 4.02, t = 2.31, P = 0.024) and MCCB total score (B = 2.18, t = 1.97, P = 0.053). However, these improvements were not sustained at the 8-week follow-up. At week 8, significant main effects of time emerged for Verbal Learning (B = 6.64, t = 3.32, P = 0.002) and Visual Learning (B = 4.93, t = 2.12, P = 0.039), whereas no significant Group × Time interaction effects were observed. Missing data at the 8-week follow-up were addressed using a last observation carried forward (LOCF) approach in a sensitivity analysis, with the results presented in Supplementary Table S6.

The score of PANSS changes after 4-week training and 8-week follow-up from baseline

In terms of clinical symptoms, significant group × time interaction effects were observed for the PANSS positive symptoms. Compared to the FD group, the SAD group showed a significant reduction in positive symptoms at the end of the 4-week training (B = −1.99, t = −2.76, P = 0.007), though this specific benefit was not maintained at the 8-week follow-up. Difficulty-performance coupling as a mechanistic marker

Block-wise longitudinal analyses demonstrated earlier and steeper learning trajectories in the SAD group. Accelerated significant gains compared to baseline were observed in Agility (Block 2 vs. Block 16), Attention (Block 2 vs. Block 6), and Thinking (Block 2 vs. Block 6) after FDR correction (all P < 0.05, Fig. 2), while no such observation in Perception, Memory and Executive. Learning curves and stage-wise test results for all domains are provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Trial-level LMMs were utilized to explore the effects of difficulty-performance coupling of the intervention. For each cognitive domain, we fitted linear mixed-effects models to trial-level longitudinal trajectories. The performance function selection with the number of training sessions was selected in a data-driven manner based on the AIC/BIC, see Supplementary Table S7. Difficulty was decomposed into a within-person deviation component (cDiff) and a between-person mean component (Diff_bar) to distinguish momentary adaptation from stable individual differences. After controlling for group and time-related terms, there were significant difficulty-performance coupling effects in memory, attention, perception, agility, and thinking (all P < 0.05), and the within-person difficulty deviation (cDiff) was positively associated with concurrent performance scores, indicating better performance when task difficulty at a given stage exceeded an individual’s average level. There was no significant difficulty-performance coupling effect in executive control (P = 0.066). Nested model comparisons further demonstrated that difficulty parameters significantly enhance model fit and increased the marginal variance explained, with the strongest mechanistic effects for Memory (Beta = 0.276, P < 0.001, Cohen’s f² = 0.293, 95% CI [0.154 0.439]) and Agility (Beta = 0.903, P < 0.001, Cohen’s f² = 0.278, 95% CI [0.139 0.442]), confirming that adaptive titration is a primary driver of cognitive gain. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized, active-controlled trial to demonstrate that a multidomain, self-adaptive CCT program significantly accelerates cognitive improvement in schizophrenia, with the strongest and earliest effects observed in Attention and Agility. Beyond establishing efficacy, this study advances mechanistic understanding by isolating performance-contingent difficulty adjustment (e.g., maintaining an optimal challenge) as a candidate “active ingredient” driving early cognitive change, a priority in psychiatric research16.

Using the active-control framework, we observed significant main effects of time on both the MCCB and CIs, indicating that CCT, whether SAD or FD, can improve cognitive performance in schizophrenia. This pattern is consistent with prior evidence15, while also highlighting that the magnitude, timing, and domain specificity of improvement vary as a function of protocol characteristics and implementation features. In addition, the lower attrition observed in the SAD group suggests that adaptive difficulty may enhance participant engagement and adherence. Beyond conventional pre-post comparisons, an advantage of the SAD design is its capacity to capture training dynamics at a finer, process-oriented resolution, enabling a more nuanced characterization of how improvements unfold over time.

A landmark finding of this study is the accelerated improvement trajectory in the self-adaptive group27, whereas other cognitive domains tended to exhibit a more gradual trajectory. Specifically, agility demonstrated early gains followed by increased variability at later stages. In contrast, attention improved from the initial phase of training and exhibited a stronger coupling with adaptive difficulty. When task difficulty is held constant, a progressive mismatch between task demands and evolving capability may emerge, potentially attenuating training efficacy and constraining continued performance gains. Consistent with the learning-curve pattern of rapid early improvement followed by deceleration, these findings suggest that the primary incremental value of adaptive difficulty may lie in accelerating the emergence of observable benefits rather than simply increasing the overall magnitude of improvement.

Deficits in attention28 and agility constitute clinically salient features of schizophrenia and may exert broad downstream effects on higher-order cognition. Attention, as a core mechanism for allocating limited cognitive resources, supports early-stage selection, amplification, and binding processes29, and even subtle disruption may propagate into cascading biases in perception and memory. Accordingly, an expanding body of work has examined whether videogame-based or task-oriented training can enhance attentional functioning. Action videogames have been shown to improve attentional control and executive processes in healthy adults30. Related evidence suggests that videogame experience and training may promote adult neuroplasticity and more efficient allocation of attentional resources within frontoparietal networks31, with reported increases in functional connectivity between attention-related and sensorimotor networks32 and changes in visual ERP amplitudes33. Under appropriately calibrated task difficulty, improvements have been observed across sustained, selective, and divided attention34. These converging findings align with our observation of early attentional gains and support the view that attention may represent a particularly sensitive target for short-term training effects.

With respect to agility, prior studies35 indicate that it reflects not only speed processing but also adaptive adjustment to changing demands. Evidence suggests that attention and agility may be promising early intervention targets, potentially because schizophrenia is characterized by early compromise in foundational cognitive processes that, in turn, constrain higher-order regulation36. Consistent with this framework, auditory system-targeted training has been reported to enhance auditory agility and to shape individual learning trajectories, implicating early sensory processing as a determinant of training responsiveness37, with additional evidence of training-related benefits in electrophysiological indices such as the P30038. Shorter computerized remediation programs have also reported gains in agility39, although longer-term follow-up data remain comparatively limited. While these improvements may partly reflect increased engagement, such engagement is inherent to adaptive training paradigms. Moreover, the observed coupling between training dose, task difficulty, and performance suggests that the effects are unlikely to be attributable solely to reduced boredom, but instead reflect adaptive calibration of cognitive load. Importantly, review-level evidence indicates that performance-contingent, adaptive adjustment of task difficulty is more likely to yield beneficial outcomes40, which is directionally consistent with the earlier gains observed in the adaptive arm of our trial.

Mechanistically, the earlier benefits of adaptive difficulty training in attention and agility may be understood within a “sustained challenge-resource engagement-plasticity” pathway. Because adaptive protocols require participants to continuously recruit cognitive resources to meet performance-contingent demands, the training process itself may constitute high-frequency reinforcement of sustained attention and overall task engagement41. However, substantial heterogeneity in training modalities and game characteristics limits one-to-one mapping between specific training components and discrete neural substrates42; therefore, mechanistic inferences should be interpreted cautiously. The increased late-stage variability observed in agility is also consistent with the non-linear nature of training gains. Prior work suggests that cognitive training effects may follow an inverted-U pattern, with pronounced early improvement and slower growth thereafter43, and that experience-dependent structural changes may progress nonlinearly, particularly after longer training intervals44. Accordingly, early gains in agility accompanied by greater late-stage variability may reflect diminishing marginal returns or incomplete consolidation of transferable skills. Finally, neurobiological evidence indicates that improvements in attention and agility may be related to white matter microstructure within prefrontal-thalamic-sensorimotor pathways, and that appropriately calibrated training difficulty may induce plastic changes in prefrontal activation patterns such that increased prefrontal signaling tracks behavioral gains45.

Pre-post differences in thinking were not statistically significant; however, learning-curve analyses suggested a gradual increase over time. Better early performance under SD design may reflect differences in task–ability matching or nonspecific learning effects, whereas the later trajectory is consistent with the possibility that ongoing performance-contingent adjustment confers increasing advantage as proficiency evolves. Thinking is a higher-order cognitive function encompassing abstraction, reasoning, planning, and innovation, with prior work implicating the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex as a key neural substrate46. A 3-year study reported that targeted training can improve abstract and experiential thinking47, whereas prolonged fixed-format training does not necessarily yield sustained benefit, consistent directionally with our observation. Similarly, a 10-week adaptive cognitive training study48 using an active comparator observed significant time effects but no clear improvement in functional outcomes, cognitive measures, or ERP indices within the adaptive arm, highlighting the influence of protocol characteristics, sample size, and substantial inter-individual variability. Taken together, our findings of a process-level increase in thinking without a robust short-term pre-post effect are not isolated and may indicate that transfer in higher-order cognition is more contingent on sufficient dose, more precise task targeting, and longer observation windows.

For perception, memory, and executive functioning, we did not observe clear improvements after 4 weeks of training. Dose-response evidence offers a more direct interpretive framework. Fisher and colleagues49 suggested that ~20 h of training may be sufficient to improve auditory processing speed, whereas 40–50 h is more likely to yield stable transfer to memory and executive functioning. Gains often slow after 20 h, and the dose-response relationship is non-linear, with training beyond 100 h not necessarily conferring additional benefit. In line with this literature, the absence of robust change in these higher-order domains in our trial may be more plausibly attributable to limited training intensity or total dose rather than a lack of potential efficacy, and longer-term dose-response relationships for adaptive training warrant further study. More broadly, the cognitive training literature remains heterogeneous. Clinical trials50 and meta-analytic evidence51 typically report moderate overall effects (approximately 0.38), but not uniformly positive outcomes; moderators include training duration, training format, age, and educational background52. Accordingly, our findings support a strategy: increasing training weight on attention and agility early to accelerate observable gains, while higher-order domains may require more sustained adaptive training and greater cumulative exposure to achieve more stable transfer.

Clinically, improvements in positive symptoms may be in part attributable to pharmacotherapy and natural symptom variability; however, adaptive training may still contribute indirectly by sustaining engagement of relevant neural systems and functional integration53. The SAD group showed significant gains on the CPT-IP and the MCCB total score. Because the CPT-I is the MCCB subtest for the Attention or Vigilance domain25, these findings are consistent with a potential benefit of adaptive, difficulty-adjusted cognitive training, and suggest that the Cognitive Index may be a feasible outcome measure. However, the absence of significant effects in other domains may partly reflect the domain-level sensitivity precision of MCCB in repeated assessments, particularly in the context of schizophrenia heterogeneity and constrained transfer. In addition, MCCB subtests draw on multiple component processes, which may be less responsive to subtle, training-related changes than process measures. Accordingly, short-term RCTs may benefit from reporting global batteries alongside learning-curve and other process-level metrics to clarify not only whether training works, but also how and when benefits occur.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although our trial-level analysis captured difficulty dynamics, we did not explicitly quantify the cumulative domain-specific “dose” (total time per domain), which may have limited our ability to establish a dose-response relationship for higher-order functions like executive control. Second, the 4-week intervention and additional 4-week follow-up may have been insufficient to capture stable neurocognitive transfer into higher-order domains (e.g., memory and reasoning), which typically require longer engagement to show as clinical change, thereby limiting the interpretation of clinical effectiveness. Third, although the active-control design controlled for computer exposure, the control group did not implement a strictly fixed set of training domains or a fully standardized difficulty structure, potentially attenuating the contrast between the adaptive and fixed-difficulty conditions. Fourth, the trial was initiated before the most recent evidence on effective training ingredients was incorporated; consequently, the intervention did not include therapist-guided components, did not integrate psychosocial rehabilitation, and did not account for patient characteristics that may define optimal candidates for cognitive training. The absence of these components may explain why cognitive gains did not fully translate into sustained improvements in clinical symptoms at follow-up. Finally, although patients in both groups were maintained on stable doses of antipsychotic medications, it is possible that some of the cognitive and, more importantly, symptomatic improvements may reflect medication effects.

In this randomized, active-controlled trial, computerized cognitive training was associated with improvements in cognitive performance in individuals with schizophrenia, with the SAD design demonstrating clear advantages over the FD design. Performance-contingent difficulty adjustment of task difficulty emerged as a critical active component: by dynamically titrating cognitive load to maintain an optimal challenge level, the SAD design produces earlier and more pronounced cognitive improvement, most notably in attention and processing speed (Agility). The significant difficulty-performance coupling identified at the trial level suggests that the adaptive cognitive calibration is not merely a technical feature, but rather a primary “active ingredient” that engages neuroplasticity and accelerates the emergence of training effects.

In the meantime, while fundamental cognitive processes such as attention and processing speed (agility) responded rapidly to adaptive difficulty, higher-order domains (such as thinking, memory, and executive functioning) may require greater cumulative “dose” and longer intervention duration to achieve more substantial recovery. Overall, these findings provide actionable evidence to support domain- and phase-specific optimization of computerized cognitive training, and they offer directions for refining training protocols and allocating domain-specific training weights in future clinical translation.

Methods

Study design and ethical oversight

We conducted a 4-week, randomized, double-blind, active-controlled clinical trial with an 8-week follow-up phase. The study was conducted between November 2017 and January 2022 at Beijing Anding Hospital, Capital Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Anding Hospital (No. 2018-133) and registered at chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2000040326). All participants provided written informed consent before entering the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Participants

Eligible participants were patients with schizophrenia who met the following criteria: (1) Age 18–60 years; (2) diagnose of schizophrenia according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders 4th edition (text revision) (DSM-IV-TR); (3) ≥6 years of formal education; and (4) clinical stability, defined as being on stable dose of a single atypical antipsychotics for ≥1 month before and throughout the study. Exclusion criteria were: (1) presence of current or previous organic brain diseases, severe medical illness, or mental disorders caused by psychoactive substances (excepting nicotine) or intellectual disability; (2) received electroconvulsive therapy, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, other neuromodulation techniques, or other cognitive interventions within 6 months, and (3) agitation or behavioral instability precluding participation.

Participants were recruited from both inpatient and outpatient settings. To minimize the possibility of potential information sharing among inpatients on the same unit, we recruited participants from different units or at different times. Each inpatient completed training individually and was instructed not to share task content or training experiences with others. Outpatient participants also completed training independently. These procedures were implemented to reduce the risk of cross-participant contamination.

Sample size

Based on previous cognitive training trials in schizophrenia54, a sample size of 72 patients (36 per group) provided 80% power to detect a moderate between-group effect (two-tailed α = 5%). Dropout was defined as voluntary withdrawal by patients or loss to follow-up during treatment and follow-up periods. Allowing for 25% dropout rate, we set our enrollment target at 90 patients (45 per group).

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomized (1:1) into either the Self-adaptive Difficulty (SAD) training group or the fixed-difficulty (FD) control group using a computer-generated sequence prepared by an independent statistician. Allocation was concealed. To ensure robust double-blinding, study participants and all assessors were blinded to treatment assignment throughout the study. Trainers provided only technical support and had no access to outcome data.

Interventions

Participants engaged in a computerized, multidomain, adaptive training program (www.66nao.com) for 30 min daily (2-min task for 5 times and 3 rotations per day), 7 days per week, for 4 weeks. The training targeted six domains: memory, executive control, attention, perception, agility, and thinking. (1) Adaptive algorithm: At the beginning, task assignment within each domain was identical for all participants. Difficulty was continuously titrated using a performance-contingent algorithm. Progression to the next difficulty level required accuracy >80%, with adaptive adjustments implemented by varying a single task parameter per level (e.g., number of stimulus types, the presentation probability of each stimulus type, and the size and duration of stimuli) while holding others constant. (2) Personalization: When a participant’s performance exceeded 80% of the age-matched normative benchmarks, the task was replaced by a more complex paradigm within the same cognitive domain. Thus, the training is adaptive not only at the task level but also at the individual level, enabling personalized trajectories while maintaining a consistent structure framework across all participants.

To control for expectancy and computer exposure (the Hawthorne effects)55, participants in the FD control group completed four tasks targeting processing speed and attention. Their total training time was matched to that of the SAD group, but all tasks were maintained at a fixed, basic difficulty level irrespective of performance throughout the study. This condition was designed as an active control, focusing on lower-level cognitive processes while avoiding additional training effects in higher-order domains (e.g., memory and executive function), thereby preserving between-group contrast and enabling isolation of the effect of adaptive difficulty.

Cognitive training was delivered via tablet devices provided at enrollment. Participants completed daily sessions by logging into a secure web-based platform (www.66nao.com) either in the hospital or at home, under remote supervision by two trained research nurses. Adherence was monitored through daily review of backend training records, including session completion and task performance. For participants without valid training data by the end of the day, reminders were provided: inpatients were contacted through their physicians, and home-based participants were contacted directly by phone. Training validity was defined a priori as sessions lasting ≥120 s, containing at least one correct response, and showing non-zero response time and accuracy. This relatively permissive threshold was adopted to balance data validity with feasibility in a population with cognitive impairment and known adherence challenges4. Sessions not meeting these criteria were flagged, and participants were prompted to repeat the training. Invalid sessions were excluded during data preprocessing to ensure data quality. Each task generated an age-based Cognition Index (CI), which is sensitive to domain-specific cognitive impairment in schizophrenia26,56.

Outcome measures

Cognitive function was assessed using both the CI26 and the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Battery (MCCB) (Chinese version)25. Psychopathology was assessed using the Positive and Negative Symptom Scale (PANSS)57. Assessments were conducted at baseline, post-training (week 4) and follow-up (week 8).

To evaluate whether the adaptive difficulty algorithm produced superior effects across different cognitive domains, and to further examine the relationship between training difficulty and cognitive performance, the primary outcomes were defined as changes in global and domain-specific cognitive measures (CI and MCCB) from baseline to week 4. Secondary outcomes were changes in PANSS and MCCB from baseline to weeks 4 and 8.

Statistical analysis

Data management and analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and MATLAB R2018b. Baseline characteristics were compared using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Repeated outcomes (CI, MCCB, and PANSS) were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) for group, time points, and group x time, adjusted for the number of training sessions in training exposure. Trials were excluded if fewer than 10% of the total participants in the corresponding group and domain contributed data. Ultimately, 0.018% of the data was excluded.

To explore the difficulty-performance coupling, we first aggregated every session into one block, and the difficulty was mean-centered before model fitting. Learning curves were then modeled with random intercept models, with block as a categorical fixed effect; stage-wise gains were tested by linear hypothesis tests comparing the fixed-effect coefficients of each post-baseline block against the baseline block. For difficulty-performance coupling analyses, difficulty was decomposed into a within-subject deviation component (cDiff) and a between-subject mean component (Diff_bar), and we pay more attention to cDiff. The time form of performance change was selected using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The incremental contribution of difficulty was quantified by comparing the base and full models under maximum likelihood; Cohen’s f² for the added difficulty terms was computed from ∆R², and its 95% confidence interval was obtained via cluster bootstrap resampling by participant ID. All tests were two-sided, and the significance level was set at 0.05. FDR correction was used for multiple comparisons.
