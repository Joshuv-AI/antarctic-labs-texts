Centring Biodiversity in Nursing for Decolonial Planetary Health
Authors: Alysha T Jones, Melissa Vera, Jessica LeClair, Darryl Harsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEscalating biodiversity loss is tied to a global colonial‐capitalist order that treats human and other‐than‐human lives as resources for extraction. Sustained by logics of separation and hierarchies of value, this order creates grave risks for planetary health. Nursing is a profession and discipline grounded in relational caring praxis and a growing commitment to decolonizing perspectives. This positions nurses to resist these logics and instead act in recognition of the critical importance of biodiversity and the interconnectedness of human and other‐than‐human communities. In this discussion paper, we synthesize decolonial and ecological scholarship alongside nursing praxis. We engage five movements (Indigenous Consensus, Natural Law, Rights of Nature, Kincentric Ecology and Queer Ecology) that hold an ethics of kinship and resistance to colonial‐capitalist logics. We argue that centring biodiversity, especially in light of the current global biodiversity crisis, offers a pathway to decolonial planetary health. Drawing on this synthesis, we outline implications for nursing research, education, advocacy and policy, and practice, reorienting nursing's ethical commitments toward multispecies care and justice. This is the first nursing‐centred synthesis to integrate biodiversity care with decolonial ethics.Keywords: biodiversity, decolonization, Indigenous knowledges, multispecies justice, nursing, planetary health

Making peace with nature is the defining task of the 21st century.

Antonio Guterres (UNFCCC 2020)

Introduction

Interest in planetary health is growing within nursing around the world (Cleofas 2025; J. M. LeClair et al. 2024; J. LeClair et al. 2024; Martin et al. 2024; Matahela 2025; Moraes Filho and Tavares 2024; Vandenberg et al. 2025). Planetary health is an integrative field that focuses on the interdependence of human health and social systems with Earth's natural (e.g., ecological, geophysical) systems (Planetary Health Alliance 2026). This focus challenges nurses to move beyond individual and population health, and to consider the ecological systems and conditions within which health and ill‐health emerge. Here, ecological refers to the interconnected relationships among animals, plants, fungi, microorganisms, ecosystems, humans and other beings that make up our natural living world (Elliott‐Graves 2024).

Pollution, climate change and biodiversity loss are all central planetary health concerns. In nursing, the relationship between biodiversity and human health has been underexplored; however, overshadowed by the human health impacts of climate‐related disasters such as extreme heat events, wildfires, floods and displacement. Yet biodiversity is foundational to planetary health. The Convention on Biological Diversity (2006) defines biodiversity as the diversity of life on this planet, which includes terrestrial and aquatic organisms, and the ecosystems they form. Additionally, biodiversity includes diversity within species, between species and between ecosystems. As biodiversity erodes, the ecosystems that sustain food security, water quality, pathogen regulation and cultural relationships with land are disrupted, weakening the ecological foundations that underpin health (Intergovernmental Science‐Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services [IPBES] 2019; World Health Organization 2025). For this reason, biodiversity warrants far greater attention across nursing research, education, advocacy and policy, and practice (REAP) for planetary health. Centring biodiversity in nursing is not only an expansion of content; it reorients nursing's understanding of relationality and responsibility within multispecies and ecological worlds, opening a pathway toward decolonial planetary health.

For nursing, contributing to decolonial perspectives in planetary health means extending nursing's stance on relationality to its ecological conclusion. This means acknowledging the fundamental interconnectedness of human lives and health with the lives and health of animals, fungi, plants, microorganisms and whole ecosystems. This is also an ethical invitation to develop a praxis of care and justice for both. Nursing has a long tradition of ethics, historically unique but more recently overshadowed by bioethics (Fowler 2020). Yet as critical nurse scholar Jess Dillard‐Wright (2024) notes, there are other possibilities to speculate on ethics in nursing. Imagination is needed. Cultivating decolonial planetary health in nursing invites this kind of ethical reimagining. It invites expanding our understanding of care and justice to reimagine nursing as a practice embedded in Earth's ecosystems. This is so, even if unrecognized, like all other unfoldings on Earth.

Aim

This discussion paper emphasizes the vital role of biodiversity in planetary health and justice, inviting nurses to learn from and integrate insights from decolonial and ecological movements that emphasize relationality and care across species and ecosystems. We approach these movements as currents of praxis. They offer insights for reimagining nursing as a health‐ and equity‐promoting discipline acting amid a planetary health crisis. We share them in part through dialogue with our own nursing experiences and ethical commitments and focus on how eco‐relational ways of knowing can reorient nursing toward biodiversity care as contributing to decolonial planetary health.

We begin by outlining two key background sections. First, we explore decolonizing planetary health and nursing. Second, we describe dimensions of biodiversity loss. We then explore five movements that entail biodiversity stewardship. In the discussion, we explore how these movements centre human kinship with the other‐than‐human world and resist the colonial‐capitalist logics behind biodiversity collapse. We then draw out themes that deepen the understanding of nursing's ecological and ethical entanglements. Finally, we outline implications for nursing REAP and emphasize the cultivation of biodiversity literacy and care praxis as central to a decolonial planetary health approach. Before proceeding, we briefly situate ourselves as authors of this work.

Situating Ourselves

We are a group of four nurses writing from Indigenous territories in the lands now called Canada and the United States. Melissa (she/her) is Tsm'syen and Yaqui First Nations living on the ancestral lands of the Duwamish, Suquamish, Muckleshoot, Nisqually and other Coast Salish Tribes. She is a nurse scientist focused on the intersections of planetary health and Indigenous health. Darryl (he/him) is a citizen of the Haida Nation. He grew up on Vancouver Island away from his culture. He worked in forestry for many years before becoming a nurse and then worked in First Nations health across northern British Columbia and Manitoba, Canada. To deepen his understanding of Indigenous governance and leadership, he completed an MBA in Indigenous Business Leadership. This strengthened his connection to Indigenous teachings and perspectives. Alysha (she/her) is a second‐generation white settler living in the territories of the T'Sou‐ke and Sc'ianew Nations. She is of English ancestry on her maternal side. She is a nurse educator and wildlife rehabilitation volunteer whose work aspires to bridge a love for health, animals, forests and justice. Jessica (she/her) is a white settler of Scottish, Welsh and English descent, living on the ancestral lands of the Ho‐Chunk Nation, called Teejop since time immemorial. She is a nurse scientist and educator focused on integrating planetary health into public health nursing practice.

We write from a shared commitment to the ethical practice of walking together with different ways of knowing. This relational space invites nurses to engage with multiple worldviews through love and compassion (Sheppard 2020). It may also be understood as a borderlands space, in Gloria Anzaldúa's (2012) sense, as a site of possibility where identities, cultures and ways of knowing meet and are reworked (Strus et al. 2024). In this paper, we aim to honour the spirit of walking alongside one another, with respect for our different social locations and worldviews. At the same time, we intentionally uplift Indigenous perspectives in planetary health. This is because Indigenous knowledges have long recognized the profound interdependence of all life. Indigenous sciences, grounded in generations upon generations of ecological observation and spiritual law, constitute complete and rigorous knowledge systems that resist the extractivism of the colonial‐capitalist global order that is driving biodiversity loss, and are integral to enacting the protection and restoration of Earth's bioculturally diverse web of life (Redvers et al. 2024).

Decolonizing Planetary Health and Nursing

The biodiversity crisis, and the broader planetary health crisis of which it is a part, cannot be understood apart from colonial relations. Across contexts, colonialism has operated through hierarchies of value used to justify violent dispossession and exploitation (Wispelwey et al. 2023). In settler colonial contexts such as Canada and Aotearoa/New Zealand, this structure persists through efforts to secure land and power for settler society by dispossessing Indigenous Peoples and undermining their sovereignty (Wolfe 2006; Tuck and Yang 2012). In many other regions, franchise or exploitation colonialism has centred on the extraction of natural resources and labour for imperial benefit, often through regimes such as enslaved labour on plantations (Rodney 1972; Quijano 2000; Segal 1995). Extractive colonial relations continue today, for example, through global supply chains that externalize environmental and climate harms, along with associated health risks, onto the Global South (Mbembe 2003; Obasun 2025). Colonial domination, however, has long been resisted by Indigenous peoples and others subjected to it.

Against this historical and ongoing colonial backdrop, planetary health is growing into an influential movement. Yet the field has faced criticism for privileging Eurocentric, Western and high‐income country views of health and Nature (Redvers et al. 2024; Santos Baquero et al. 2021). More specifically, Planetary Health has been critiqued for reproducing Euro‐Western assumptions in how problems are framed, whose knowledges are prioritized and the kinds of interventions that are proposed. R. Jones et al. (2022) describe the contradiction of responding to the planetary crisis with the same way of thinking implicated in producing it. In response, a decolonial turn has emerged within Planetary Health. Efforts to decolonize the field span initiatives from Aotearoa New Zealand to British Columbia, Canada, centring Indigenous knowledges and positioning Land and Mother Earth as essential to health (Hoogeveen et al. 2023; R. Jones et al. 2022; Redvers, Poelina, et al. 2020; Redvers, Schultz, et al. 2020; Redvers et al. 2021 2022, 2024).

Parallel conversations and a decolonial turn are also occurring in nursing. Nursing scholarship, education and practice are increasingly reckoning with how colonial knowledge systems have shaped the discipline and profession in varied ways across global contexts; and how decolonizing nursing is an imperative (Ballout et al. 2025; Cleofas 2024; Iheduru‐Anderson and Waite 2024; McGibbon et al. 2014; Mkandawire‐Valhmu et al. 2025; Suárez‐Baquero 2024). A recent scoping review by Rosario et al. (2024) identifies the ways decolonization is conceived of in the nursing literature. Across their reviewed articles, it is seen as a sociopolitical project aimed at challenging Eurocentrism and colonial power structures (e.g., Adhikari and Smith 2023; Barton and Kahn‐John 2023); decentring whiteness; and the building of partnerships with Indigenous and racialized communities (Ajibade and Hayes 2022; Crosschild and Varcoe 2021). Epistemologically, decolonizing nursing is understood to be about validating and upholding Indigenous knowledges and challenging the epistemic dominance of biomedical paradigms (Chinn 2022; Bourque Bearskin 2023). It requires the undoing and unlearning of structures that erase and devalue other ways of knowing (Cleofas 2024). Decolonizing efforts therefore centre epistemic justice. This entails equity in whose knowledge is recognized, whose knowledge systems inform knowledge production, and how the historical privileging of Western knowledge alongside the marginalization of other epistemologies is addressed (Catala 2024).

While the concept of decolonization is increasingly used in nursing, Indigenous scholars caution against its symbolic use. In Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor, Tuck and Yang (2012) emphasize that decolonization is not synonymous with social justice or human rights. It is a material process. In settler colonial contexts, this materiality centres on the return of land and the restoration of Indigenous sovereignty and jurisdiction; without Land Back, decolonization remains symbolic. At the same time, decolonizing discourses in nursing go beyond settler colonial contexts to examine colonial legacies across geographies and from Global South and diasporic communities (Ballout et al. 2025; Cleofas 2024; Suárez‐Baquero 2024). Given concerns about the dilution of decolonization's meaning, some authors draw on related vocabularies—such as anti‐colonial praxis (Caxaj and Berman 2014) or unsettling perspectives and practices (A. T. Jones and Segal 2018). Unsettling has been described as a parallel process to decolonization. As a practice, unsettling requires settlers to critically examine their social position within settler colonial systems, including the privileges that sustain this position (Battell Lowman and Barker 2015; Regan 2010). It is important to note that within settler colonial states, people of colour may have limited or no settler privilege (Jafri 2012). With this context established, we now turn to biodiversity loss as a central expression of the planetary health crisis and a key site where colonial and extractive relations become materially visible in ecosystems and health.

Ecological, Structural and Health Dimensions of Biodiversity Loss

Biodiversity and Ecocentrism

In this paper, we use the terms other‐than‐human, more‐than‐human and multispecies to also mean biodiversity. Other‐than‐human, as described by environmental humanities scholar Sophie Chao (Price and Chao 2023), emphasizes the agency of non‐human beings without reinscribing anthropocentric binaries (as in human/non‐human). More‐than‐human refers to the wider community of life on Earth, plants, animals, waters, fungi, microbes, rocks, ecosystems, elemental forces and humans—of which humans are but one part (Abram 1996; Whatmore 2002). Other related terms carry distinct genealogies and emphases (Price and Chao 2023). The term multispecies draws from the work of posthumanist‐feminist scholar D. J. Haraway (2008). It draws attention to the species as opposed to the human. Multispecies justice, according to Celermajer et al. (2021), involves attending to the intersecting injustices experienced by human and other‐than‐human beings in the context of shared vulnerability (e.g., the impacts of colonialism and climate change on people and Land).

Understanding biodiversity through these different terms helps to surface the ethical and relational dimensions of biodiversity alongside ecological ones. Biodiversity is a critical planetary boundary upon which humans and other‐than‐human organisms depend for life, health and equity (World Health Organization 2025). Biodiversity is essential for the functioning of ecosystems and the many services they provide, including crop pollination, carbon storage, soil regeneration and the purification of air and water, all of which are foundational for human and other‐than‐human survival and well‐being. Healthy ecosystems also provide essential cultural, social, spiritual, material and economic resources (World Health Organization 2025).

From an ecocentric perspective (as opposed to an anthropocentric one), the other‐than‐human world has intrinsic value and the right to exist notwithstanding usefulness to humans (Taylor et al. 2020). Animals, for example, are thus seen to have their own telos, their own purpose and reason for being. Shelley Alexander (Raincoast Conservation Foundation 2023), an animal geographer who has studied wolves for more than 20 years, describes telos as the recognition that wolves exist for themselves, with their own ways of knowing and being, not as beings whose value is defined by humans. Wolves (and other animals) are not here on Earth for humans to make into property—to exploit, enslave and kill for expedience. Yet, anthropogenic biodiversity and ecosystem destruction, driven by a view that sees humans as more valuable than all other organisms on Earth combined with the consumptive greed of extractive capitalism, violates ecocentric ethics (Redvers et al. 2022; Batavia and Nelson 2017). Ecocentrism shifts moral attention from human‐centred interests to a recognition that other‐than‐humans have interests, and rights to life too.

Although ecocentric thinking has been rare in contemporary nursing scholarship (shaped as it has been historically by white, Western and anthropocentric views), a small number of nurse theorists have laid important groundwork for reimagining the profession's relationship with and responsibilities to the more‐than‐human world. Eleanor Schuster (1990) was an early advocate for an ecocentric paradigm in nursing. Schuster emphasized that relational awareness leads nursing theory to naturally extend care beyond the human world to other‐than‐humans and the Earth as well. Building on this vision, Dorothy Kleffel (1996) developed a taxonomy of environmental paradigms for nursing to move beyond egocentric (individual‐focused) and homocentric (human society‐focused) approaches toward an ecocentric paradigm that views the environment as “whole, living, and interconnected” (p. 1). More recently, Anderko (2014) introduced the Ecocentric Values‐Based Caring Model, an explicitly ecocentric framework for nursing practice. Values inherent in this model include social justice, described as a commitment to addressing inequities for all beings on Earth, and the common good, described as a commitment to regarding all living things as part of a larger community sharing in a “commons” upon which their health and well‐being coexist. According to an ecocentric view within nursing, then, biodiversity becomes part of nursing's domain of caring.

Colonial‐Capitalist Drivers of Biodiversity Loss

Biodiversity loss, however, is a crisis occurring at a jaw‐dropping rate. Quite literally, the Earth is haemorrhaging biodiversity. According to the World Wildlife Fund's (WWF) 2024 Living Planet Report, there has been a 73% decline in monitored wildlife populations in the past 50 years (1970–2020). Since the start of the Industrial Revolution in the 18th century, forests, grasslands, watersheds, wetlands and other vital ecosystems have increasingly been destroyed and degraded (World Wildlife Fund 2020). At present, an estimated 75% of the Earth's ice‐free land has been substantially altered, mostly for agriculture; in addition, Earth's oceans are becoming heavily polluted, and more than 85% of wetlands have been destroyed (World Wildlife Fund 2020). Rampant biodiversity loss undermines the capacity of ecosystems to function and provide essential life‐supporting services, such as the purification of air and water. Pollination is one of these most essential services: insect pollinators service more than 80% of the world's flowering plants (including food crops), and a steep decline (∼20%–40%) in their populations is profoundly undermining global biodiversity (Nath et al. 2020). The implications for food security for animals are significant, as Southern resident orca and boreal caribou face imminent extinction due to food insecurity. These include diminishing salmon populations and the loss of boreal forests that house lichen, a primary food source for these caribou (Environment and Climate Change Canada 2024, 2025).

Biodiversity is thus both a major planetary health emergency and a multispecies justice issue. Proximally, land‐use change is the greatest direct driver of biodiversity loss, destroying or fragmenting the natural habitats of many diverse species on land and in the world's waters (World Wildlife Fund 2024). However, the World Wildlife Fund (2024) has warned that if we cannot limit global warming to 1.5°C, a threshold that has already been surpassed, climate change will become the leading cause of biodiversity loss in the coming decades. The climate‐fuelled Australia bushfire crisis of 2019, for example, was one of the worst wildlife disasters in modern history, resulting in the death/displacement of 3 billion animals (World Wildlife Fund 2020). Writing in the aftermath, Celermajer et al. (2021) argue that the bushfires reveal the inadequacy of our existing moral and institutional frameworks. In particular, the authors call for a transformation in how we understand and practise justice. They advocate for a multispecies justice approach that recognizes animals, for instance, as beings whose lives matter in and of themselves. Best et al. (2025) echo this call from an Indigenous perspective, describing how for many Aboriginal Peoples the bushfires were not only an ecological catastrophe but also a spiritual one: “What they [non‐Indigenous peoples] see as the death of a national emblem, we see as the death of totems and ancestors” (p. 266). Such testimony underscores that biodiversity loss is inseparable from cultural and spiritual loss, requiring justice frameworks that centre Indigenous worldviews and responsibilities to multispecies kin.

To more fully grasp the biodiversity crisis, we must look beyond proximal drivers (as noted above) to the structural forces that produce and intensify it. Central among these is anthropocentrism—also described as human exceptionalism—which positions human interests as paramount and casts humans as separate from and superior to the other‐than‐human world (Plumwood 2002). Colonialism, imperialism (Brubacher et al. 2024) and racial capitalism (Gonzalez 2022) entrench this worldview, embedding it in economic systems, governance and land relations. The crisis is further co‐created by colonial policies enacted against Indigenous peoples and territories, including those that have enabled the dispossession of Indigenous lands for resource extraction and the erasure of Traditional Ecological Knowledge and practices (Layden et al. 2025). From a systems perspective, this “war on nature” (United Nations Environment Programme 2021), or ecocide, is an attack on animals and ecosystems as well as multiple forms of diversity—racial, ethnic, gender, biological, linguistic, cultural and epistemic. The erosion of life is bound up with logics of domination that sustain “imperialist white supremacist capitalist patriarchy” (hooks 1984, p. 51), ecological collapse and intersecting injustices.

These structural critiques are also reflected in how scholars have named the present epoch. Frequently dubbed the Anthropocene, this epoch has also been called the Plantationocene (D. Haraway et al. 2015) to emphasize the totalizing force of monocultures of global colonial‐capitalism; the Wastocene, as articulated by Armiero (2021) to describe an era of wasted social and ecological relationships and toxic ecologies; and the Capitalocene, to emphasize capitalism's role in driving oppressions and extinctions worldwide (Moore 2017). Moore (2017) calls capitalism a world‐ecology, a system of accumulating resource power from “cheap natures,” that is, cheap labour (women and people of colour, peoples of low‐middle income countries), food, energy, forests, minerals, animals (think factory farms) and more. In this framing, capitalism is a predatory “economi[c]s of dispossession and disturbed relationalities” (Byrd et al.2018, 1).

Neoliberal practices offer a more recent intensification of these logics (Labonté 2020). This is particularly evident in food production as discussed by McGibbon (2025) in her work on root determinants of health. Otero (2018) explains how neoliberal governance leads to the replacement of traditional agriculture with globalized commodity/corporate agriculture, alongside a shift from healthy diets to cheap, convenient foods that cost public health. This cheap food paradigm, organized around keeping food prices low while externalizing health, labour, and ecological harms, undermines small‐scale farming, reduces farmed animals to mere production units, and greatly damages biodiversity. Commodity agriculture, particularly animal agriculture, is one of the greatest threats to biodiversity on the planet (United Nations Environment Programme 2021).

Mbembe's (2003) concept of necropolitics adds another dimension to this conceptualization. Necropolitics describes “the capacity to define who matters and who does not, who is disposable and who is not” (Mbembe 2003, 27) in service to colonial‐capitalist wealth and power. Necropolitics is a concept that illuminates how state governments and corporations determine whose lives are expendable, and how colonialism and capitalism make a “deathworld” for biodiversity and for peoples who have historically been constructed as closer to Nature within colonial hierarchies of human and ecological value (Mbembe 2003; Moore 2017).

Capitalism and colonialism are therefore co‐constituted systems that both operate through the exploitation and expendability of peoples and Nature. Their combined forces result in persistent, intensive resource exploitation that fragments habitats, homogenizes human and other‐than‐human communities, shifts species ranges, undermines food security for all beings and fuels extinctions (Moranta et al. 2022). Beliefs in unlimited economic growth and the concentration of wealth/power, the driving forces of capitalism's logics, have reshaped society and Nature, contributing to extreme biodiversity loss (Moranta et al. 2022) and cultural erosion (Best et al. 2025).

Biodiversity and Health

While biodiversity loss threatens the survival of innumerable species and ecosystems—2 million plant and animal species may currently be at risk for extinction (Hochkirch et al. 2021)—it also has far‐reaching consequences for human health. A recent scoping review identified five pathways through which Nature and biodiversity influence human health: Biological, psychological, social, physical activity and environmental buffering (Robinson et al. 2024). Being in biodiverse environments, for instance, particularly those abundant in diverse microbial communities, including bacteria and fungi for example, promotes immune regulation and may lower the risk of inflammatory diseases. The “biodiversity hypothesis” is gaining recognition as evidence links diminished environmental microbial exposure to higher rates of asthma, allergies and other immune‐mediated conditions (Boulund et al. 2025; Hanski et al. 2012; Prescott et al. 2022).

The emergence of infectious diseases is also exacerbated by biodiversity loss. Disruptions to ecosystems caused by deforestation, species loss, and habitat loss and fragmentation promote zoonotic spillover events as seen in the outbreaks of Ebola, Zika and COVID‐19 (Landrigan et al. 2024; Brubacher et al. 2024). In contrast, healthy ecosystems with rich species diversity have a “dilution effect” which mitigates the transmission of vector‐borne diseases (Robinson et al. 2024; Landrigan et al. 2024).

Biodiversity also has meaningful implications for mental health. Research continues to show that diverse green and blue spaces can help reduce stress and anxiety, enhance emotional well‐being and promote social connections (Robinson et al. 2024; Cuenca et al. 2025). For instance, access to nearby natural environments during the COVID‐19 pandemic was associated with improved general and mental health among urban women, particularly those experiencing economic hardship (Cuenca et al. 2025). However, these advantages are not equally available to people. Urban biodiversity is often limited in historically marginalized communities due to a history of discriminatory planning policies, such as redlining, and a lack of investment in green infrastructure such as parks and trails (Schell et al. 2020; Robinson et al. 2024). These inequities can lead to reduced exposure to Nature and contribute to what Louv (2005) calls nature‐deficit disorder, particularly harmful for children and their developmental needs. Reduced access to Nature can exacerbate health disparities by limiting access to beneficial environmental microbiota and restorative green space such as urban forests so vital for shade during extreme heat events. The resulting differences in mental and physical health highlight the connection between biodiversity loss and structural injustice.

Indigenous Peoples, whose health and wellness may be closely connected to the health of local ecosystems, face greater disadvantages due to biodiversity loss. A recent umbrella review revealed a range of health impacts on Indigenous communities, including disruptions to food systems, land‐based healing practices and cultural identity (Brubacher et al. 2024). These results emphasize the need for relational, rights‐based health approaches that respect Indigenous leadership and knowledges.

At a global policy level, biodiversity's importance for health is recognized. At the 2024 Convention on Biological Diversity (COP16) held in Cali, Colombia, member countries adopted a Global Action Plan on Biodiversity and Health, which details strategies for collaborative governance in both environmental and health sectors (Willetts 2025). According to Willetts et al. (2025), biodiversity should be viewed not only as a scientific issue but as fundamental to achieving the right to a healthy environment, recognized as part of human rights by the United Nations in 2022. They urge health care professionals to advocate for biodiversity conservation as a public health imperative and to cultivate future leaders through interdisciplinary education and training. For nurses, there is an urgent need to engage with biodiversity as a critical determinant of health and to integrate biodiversity literacy across REAP.

Movements for Biodiversity

Amid a bewildering rate of biodiversity loss, several intellectual and social movements are reshaping how humans understand and relate to the living world. This reorientation is also a process of remembering. Such a remembering is a return to the relational ecologies present in human cultures worldwide but eroded over the past four hundred (and more) years (Merchant 1990). Each movement challenges dominant anthropocentric and extractive worldviews by offering alternative ethical and relational foundations for multispecies survival and flourishing amidst crisis and precarity. They serve as both medicine and antidote. In this section, we highlight five such movements: Indigenous Consensus on Planetary Health, Natural Law, Rights of Nature and more‐than‐human rights, Kincentric Ecology and Queer Ecology. We engage these movements as currents of praxis that each offer distinct guidance for nursing, such as understanding Indigenous rights and governance; how to practise kinship, and how to unsettle dualism for planetary health. In what follows, we move from diagnosis to counter‐praxis. These movements expand the possibilities for nursing and planetary health by reframing biodiversity as not only a scientific concern but also a matter of care and justice (see Table 1 for a summary of their ethical and relational orientations). We begin with the Indigenous Consensus because it most directly challenges planetary health's epistemic centre and reasserts Mother Earth and Indigenous governance as foundational to planetary health.

Indigenous Consensus on Determinants of Planetary Health

Indigenous Peoples are severely overburdened with the compounded effects of colonialism and environmental degradation, despite contributing very little to global ecological crises. In response, Redvers et al. (2022) present a groundbreaking Indigenous‐led consensus on what constitutes the determinants of planetary health, shifting the discourse away from the dominant Western paradigm. Within this work, Redvers et al. (2022) articulate planetary health through Indigenous worldviews that are grounded in Land‐based knowledges, long rooted in relational ways of understanding life. A diverse collective of Indigenous scholars, Elders, Land defenders and practitioners from around the globe engaged in a consensus process to identify 10 core determinants of planetary health, grouped into the following three interrelated levels: (1) Mother Earth level, (2) Interconnecting level and (3) Indigenous Peoples' level (see Figure 1).

At the Mother Earth level, two determinants are emphasized. First is respect for the feminine, recognizing the spiritual and generative power of women and the Earth, both of whom face systemic violence. Second is the ancestral legal personhood of natural entities, challenging the legal and cultural norms that commodify and extract Nature and advocating for Indigenous legal systems that recognize rivers, Land and other‐than‐human life as relatives, not resources.

The interconnecting level explores four more determinants. These include the intrinsic interconnectedness of humans within Nature, the quality of self and community relationships, the limitations and reductionism of the modern scientific paradigm, and the role of governance and law. The authors argue for systems that embed reciprocity, love and long‐term sustainability, values foundational in Indigenous Natural or First Law.

At the Indigenous Peoples' level, four additional determinants are outlined: Land tenure rights, languages, health and the roles of Elders and children. These elements are deeply intertwined. For instance, without secure Land rights, Indigenous Peoples lose access to the ecosystems, languages and spiritual teachings that sustain their knowledge systems and practices. Language loss is not merely cultural; it erases Land‐based knowledges and ecological wisdom. Elders are seen as living libraries of knowledge, while children are viewed as the future stewards of culture and land.

Redvers et al. (2022) emphasize that planetary health is not just about restoring ecosystems; it is also about restoring relationships. This means recognizing that violence against the Earth is inseparable from violence against women, against Indigenous Peoples and against future generations.

In co‐authoring this article for The Lancet Planetary Health, I (Melissa) was able to be part of re‐Indigenized research for the very first time as a PhD student, and it was deeply moving and changed the course of my research career trajectory. This group of authors and knowledge‐keepers embraced and embodied this work, even in the writing of it. We opened the consensus sessions in ceremony and prayer, were guided by the Elders in the group for the entire duration from brainstorming to publication and we centred our own Indigenous ways of knowing and being, unapologetically. The result was a truly Indigenous‐centred piece; a call to action and uplifting of Mother Earth within climate change discourse. It also changed the way I viewed knowledge creation and transmission and helped me see that we are in a time of a great remembering of our relationship to Mother Earth and how to truly thrive with all life on the planet in reciprocity.

Natural Law—Insights From Haida Matriarch Kii'i'juus Barbara Wilson

The Indigenous Consensus on Planetary Health (Redvers et al. 2022) affirms that restoring planetary health requires repairing relationships with the more‐than‐human world. Central to this vision is the resurgence of Indigenous legal orders, including Natural Law, which flow from the Land and guide relational responsibilities (Redvers, Poelina, et al. 2020; Redvers et al. 2022). As Redvers, Poelina, et al. (2020) explain, “Indigenous Peoples associate their own laws with the laws of the natural world, which are formally known as or translated as Natural or First Law. These laws come from the Creator and the Land through our ancestral stories and therefore, they are sacred” (p. 1). As shown in Figure 2, Natural or First Law form the outer foundation within which Indigenous traditional knowledge, Indigenous applications for planetary health and ultimately healthy communities and ecosystems are situated.

These laws reject the colonial‐capitalist logics that treat Nature as property, that impose separations of all kinds and instead uphold the inherent value of the more‐than‐human world. According to John Borrows (2010), member of the Chippewas of Nawash First Nation and leading Indigenous legal scholar, Indigenous Natural Law is rooted in lived experience and observation of the natural world. He describes Natural Law as found in Land, in stories, songs, ceremonies and in the lifeways of plants and animals.

I (Darryl) spoke with Haida Matriarch Kii'i'juus Barbara Wilson (Haida Nation, lives on Haida Gwaii, British Columbia Canada) who shared that modern approaches to economics and resource management are rooted in colonial frameworks of private property, separation and profit maximization (personal communication, September 2025). These systems, entrenched since the Doctrine of Discovery and terra nullius, prioritize financial returns from extraction over everything else, including any kind of balance with the natural world. In contrast, Indigenous Natural Law is based on values of transparency, reciprocity, collective care “not taking more than you need,” looking after each other and living one's words with integrity.

Kii'i'juus explained that, before colonization, safety and accountability were maintained through openness rather than secrecy. Transparency, knowing one another's lives and movements, ensured children were safe and supported, people's needs were met and promises were honoured. She described how colonial systems replaced this way with privacy and hierarchical decision‐making, which results in exclusion and creates favouritism and mistrust. For planetary health stewardship, she advocates returning to transparency and collective responsibility.

In the discussion, I (Darryl) reflected on how I have witnessed Indigenous values contrasting with Western practices in my past forestry work. For many Haida, healthy oceans and forests are central to well‐being and identity. Yet, urban‐based state decision makers often view “nature” through recreational or monetary lenses, failing to see the interconnections that sustain community health. This disconnect reflects broader tensions between Indigenous relational values and Western economic logics.

Kii'i'juus noted that Indigenous laws emphasize respect, balance, interconnectedness and care across all relationships: if one person is hungry, everyone is hungry; if one person suffers, all are responsible. By applying these principles, communities can also learn how to care for the Earth. She connected this to current health inequities, such as unequal housing repairs or healthcare access, and emphasized that decisions must be based on need, not favouritism or bureaucratic exclusion.

These teachings resonate with nursing' relational ethics, but they also press further by locating health within collective responsibilities to an ecology of place. From Indigenous legal orders, we now consider how other jurisdictions are being pushed to recognize ecosystems as more than property through the Rights of Nature movement.

Rights of Nature and More‐Than‐Human Rights

The Rights of Nature movement resonates with Indigenous Natural Law within the context of Western legal systems. It involves a shift from viewing Nature as property to recognizing Nature as a community of rights‐bearing beings. This shift challenges the anthropocentric foundations of Western legal systems and can ally with Indigenous rights and sovereignty (More‐Than‐Human Life [MOTH] Program 2025). Rodríguez‐Garavito (2024), founder of MOTH Program at New York University's School of Law, states that while the language of rights is problematic in some ways, it is a compelling narrative. A human rights lawyer by training, Rodríguez‐Garavito became involved in the Rights of Nature movement when working with the Sarayaku Indigenous peoples in Ecuador in the context of their human rights.

Ecuador was the first country to enshrine the Rights of Nature in its constitution in 2008, specifically Articles 71–74 in Chapter Seven, recognizing Pachamama (Mother Earth) as a legal subject with inherent rights to exist and regenerate (Asamblea Constituyente del Ecuador 2008). These Articles were upheld in a 2021 Constitutional Court ruling to protect the Los Cedros cloud forest from mining exploration, prioritizing biodiversity and Indigenous rights over extractive interests (Corte Constitucional del Ecuador 2021). Elsewhere similar legal changes have occurred. The Whanganui River in Aotearoa (New Zealand) was granted legal personhood in 2017 after over a century of Māori advocacy, recognizing the river and the iwi (Māori tribes) as one being (New Zealand Parliament 2017). In Canada, Kawinawàw (Magpie River) in Quebec was declared a legal person in 2021 through a resolution by the Innu Council of Ekuanitshit and the Minganie Regional County Municipality. This resolution acknowledges the river's right to flow freely and to be free from pollution (MRC de la Minganie & Conseil de la Nation Innue d'Ekuanitshit 2021). According to Lindsay Borrows (Berge 2022), “In the case of the Magpie River, Indigenous law is showing up in a language that Canadian law can understand” (para. 8).

Recently, I (Alysha) had the chance to participate in a More‐than‐Human Rights program alongside a group of lawyers, scientists, artists, musicians and filmmakers. As one of two healthcare professionals present, I was aware of my disciplinary location. Nursing has trained me to think critically about health and its determinants, though when I was a newer nurse, colonialism and racial injustice were not taught about as fundamental health determinants. Furthermore, health, as I was taught, was situated firmly within anthropocentric boundaries, leaving little room to consider human responsibilities for the health of ecosystems and biodiversity. Being in the MOTH space, a prefigurative space where animals and ecosystems were also imagined as participants in a fundamental structural determinant of health—political governance—offered a sense of fresh possibility for integrity with holistic health and justice.

Kincentric Ecology

Scholarship on kincentricity offers guidance for transforming nursing in the service of decolonial planetary health. Salmón (2000) introduced the concept of kincentric ecology to describe Indigenous ways of knowing that understand humans as part of an extended ecological family, where plants and animals are regarded as relatives and kin. This relational ontology entails reciprocal responsibilities, such as caring for the Land and other‐than‐human kin as part of sustaining community and well‐being. In the context of education, Salmón (2015) describes how kincentricity can be cultivated through practices such as direct engagement with place, storytelling and ceremony, which together foster ecological identity and intergenerational accountability. J. LeClair's (2021) rapid evidence review affirms the importance of such experiential and affective pedagogies, highlighting how kincentric awareness deepens students' capacity to perceive ethical relationships with more‐than‐human (including humans) communities. Together, these works challenge dominant anthropocentric and extractive logics embedded in Western nursing frameworks (which are themselves shaped by the dominant culture), offering instead a pathway toward eco‐relational nursing praxis.

For nurses working at the intersection of planetary and public health, kincentricity provides a decolonial epistemological foundation that reorients care from individualistic intervention to collective responsibility, and from disconnection to Right Relationship with the living world. For example, I (Jessica) am a public health nurse scientist who is monitoring the health impacts of water contamination while also practising Etuamptumk (Two‐Eyed Seeing) by engaging with Indigenous and white settler colleagues. Through ceremony, storytelling and advocacy, this group collaborates to protect watersheds, thereby enacting kincentric care by honouring River, Inlet and Lake as living relatives whose health is inseparable from the community's own (J. M. LeClair et al. 2024).

Queer Ecology

Queer ecology is an interdisciplinary framework that brings together queer theory, ecology, environmental studies and feminist science studies to interrogate dominant understandings of Nature and sexuality. Queer ecology challenges heteronormative assumptions embedded in environmental science and discourse, arguing that what is often considered “natural” is shaped by social and cultural norms (Mortimer‐Sandilands and Erickson 2010). Drawing from queer theory, queer ecology questions binary thinking, such as male/female, natural/unnatural or human/non‐human, and emphasizes fluidity, multiplicity and relationality across bodies and environments. In essence, queer ecology disputes the idea that heterosexual reproduction is the default or most “natural” mode of life. Instead, what is highlighted is the diversity of reproductive and sexual behaviours found in the natural world, offering empirical evidence that same‐sex behaviours and non‐reproductive intimacy are widespread among animal species (Mortimer‐Sandilands and Erickson 2010).

In addition to critiquing heteronormative assumptions in biology and ecology, queer ecology also interrogates how the category of “Nature” is mobilized as a moral standard to regulate bodies and identities (Alaimo 2010). For instance, environmental rhetoric has historically been used to reinforce colonial, racialized and gendered biopolitics, portraying certain bodies and relationships as “unnatural” or out of place. Scholars like Alaimo (2010) introduce concepts such as trans‐corporeality, which highlight the material entanglement of human and other‐than‐human bodies and emphasize the porosity between organisms and environments. Similarly, Chen (2012) explores the intersections of race and toxicity, illustrating how bodies are differently exposed to environmental harm depending on their social and political locations. Queer ecology, therefore, is not only about rethinking sexuality in the context of ecology, it also calls attention to how queerness itself is ecological, in the sense of resisting norms and imagining alternative ways of relating to more‐than‐human worlds.

Furthermore, queer ecology expands notions of kinship and care by embracing relational forms that extend beyond biological reproduction. Influenced by both ecofeminism and queer theory, scholars such as Gaard (2010) advocate for modes of ecological care and responsibility that reflect queer values like chosen family and multispecies kinship. In practice, queer ecology informs a range of research areas: from reinterpreting seminal environmental texts through a queer lens to addressing how queer and trans communities, especially those of colour, are disproportionately impacted by environmental injustice (Alaimo 2010; Chen 2012; Mortimer‐Sandilands and Erickson 2010). Ultimately, queer ecology asks not only who belongs in Nature, but also what kinds of relationships within Nature are possible. It opens space for imagining more‐than‐human ecologies that are diverse and just.

Queer ecology offers important implications for nursing by expanding how the profession conceptualizes care and justice in the context of planetary health. By challenging binary thinking and heteronormative notions of what is “natural,” queer ecology pushes nursing to move beyond narrow, biomedical understandings of bodies and environments toward more inclusive and fluid approaches to health. Integrating queer ecological perspectives invites nursing to foreground diversity as a principle of ecological and social resilience, to affirm chosen kinship networks as legitimate sites of care and to advocate for multispecies forms of justice. Queer ecology can help nurses cultivate critical ecological literacy that embraces multiplicity and fluidity, positioning nursing as a discipline capable of reimagining care in solidarity with all our relations.

I (Melissa) have fully embraced the fairly new term Indigiqueer, which captures the unique experience of being queer and Indigenous. Alongside Indigiqueer, queer ecology most resonates with the theoretical underpinnings of the intersections of my identities and research. The diverse and fluid approach that queer ecology offers resonates deeply with the selves I bring to the research table: Indigenous, lesbian, Mother Earth warrior, equity and justice advocate, artist and poet, creatrix in the matrix. The expansiveness of ecologies mirrors the expansive creativity inherent in all of us to address ecological collapse.

Discussion

In this discussion, we bring together themes from the five interrelated movements to support the ecologizing and decolonizing of nursing's relational praxis. Together, these movements frame biodiversity loss as a profoundly relational‐ethical concern, entangled with colonial‐capitalist epistemic and ontological injustices. Each movement orients nursing toward different knowledge systems and ethics that foreground more‐than‐human relations and responsibilities that include the human but do not make humans so harmfully superior.

To guide this exploration, we draw on three living beings: River, which represents the current of decolonizing nursing and planetary health; Salmon, which symbolizes the ecological epistemic diversity necessary for protecting and restoring biodiversity, and Cedar, which evokes the need to re‐root our relational commitments, both outwardly in dialogue with other knowledge traditions and inwardly within nursing's own theoretical foundations. Each of these living beings is severely threatened by the pollution and climate change of colonial‐capitalist extractivism. In the sections that follow, we use these beings to orient our discussion before outlining implications for REAP, with biodiversity literacy and care at the centre of a decolonial planetary health approach (see Figure 3).

Current of Decolonizing Nursing and Planetary Health: River

Rosario et al. (2024) show that there is a growing momentum for decolonizing nursing knowledge. Yet there remains a need to expand decolonizing to include the ecological and more‐than‐human dimensions of planetary health. Current nursing conversations about decolonization focus on equity, curriculum reform, Cultural Safety and anti‐racist practice (Rosario et al. 2024). These are necessary and vital directions. Yet they, to date, appear to have overlooked how colonialism is inseparable from ecological degradation and species extinctions, which undermines planetary health for all, and disproportionately for Indigenous communities.

The river offers a guiding principle here. Rivers connect, feed salmon and cedar and sustain countless plants, animals, fungi, microbial communities and humans. Indigenous teachings remind us that rivers, salmon, cedar and other beings are kin, relations to whom we owe respect and care (Redvers et al. 2022; Salmón 2000). Queer ecologies unsettle heteronormative and anthropocentric assumptions, affirming diverse forms of kinship. Together, these knowledges open nursing toward what Puig de la Bellacasa (2017) calls “matters of care,” ethical obligations that extend to the more‐than‐human, including rivers. This approach calls on nursing to reorient praxis toward decolonization and ecocentrism.

McGibbon's (2025) work on root determinants of health highlights that nursing must also become literate about, grapple with, talk about and act against the global structures that devastate ecosystems and deepen inequities such as neoliberal, necropolitical capitalist economic systems. This is going way upstream. Naming these forces is essential for nursing to become a site of resistance to the “deadening of our response” (Macy 2011, para. 2) to biodiversity loss. Nursing for decolonial planetary health can instead build resistance to the hypernormalization of extinctions and “war on nature,” (United Nations Environment Programme 2021) and name it for what it is, the making of a death world, not the collateral damage of a growing economy. Nursing for decolonial planetary health thus enacts an ethic of sobriety (Gesturing Toward Decolonial Futures n.d.) and care and reparative praxis that honours human interdependence with biodiversity on multiple levels, across health, social and political domains.

Ecocentric Epistemic Alternatives: Salmon

At their heart, the five movements are epistemic alternatives to dominant anthropocentric worldviews. Each offers a distinct way of knowing that resists colonial‐capitalist logics of separation and superiority. The Indigenous Consensus on Determinants of Planetary Health, Natural Law as reciprocity with land and waters, the personhood and rights of rivers, kincentric ethics and the queering of dualism (human/Nature), these movements are epistemic alternatives to dominant Western/Eurocentric paradigms. What makes them epistemic is that each redefines the grounds of knowing. The Indigenous Consensus shifts determinants from a human‐only social framing toward land‐based and spirit‐level relationships. Natural Law positions the land, waters and stories themselves as sources of law, challenging assumptions that law and knowledge originate only in human institutions, and from the reasoning human mind. The Rights of Nature unsettles Western legal epistemology by redefining who can hold rights and therefore who is recognized as having a say in the governance that ultimately determines planetary health. Kincentric ecology reframes family, understanding humans as part of an extended ecological family which is shared among species and ecosystems. And, queer ecology challenges the binaries (e.g., human/animal; male/female; Nature/culture) that have created a ubiquitous hierarchy of dominance; opening space for what is more democratic and inclusive and for a plurality of perspectives and knowledges to thrive.

Collectively, these movements uphold epistemic diversity. They uphold multiple, coexisting ways of knowing health and relationality that resist Eurocentric, anthropocentric epistemic dominance and reductionism. Salmon lifeways provide a metaphor for this diversity. Across watersheds, rivers, estuaries and oceans, salmon embody diversity: diverse species, places of birth, growth and living, and migratory routes that sustain an incredible diversity of other‐than‐humans in forest and riverine ecosystems. It is this diversity that enables resilience. When salmon lifeways collapse into farmed monoculture, ecological and Indigenous cultural systems are harmed (Atlas et al. 2020). Knowledge systems perhaps work in a similar way. When nursing draws only from a narrow anthropocentric framework, it is brittle and unable to respond adequately to the planetary health crisis. But when nursing engages with multiple epistemic alternatives, it gains a stronger capacity to respond with integrity to its calling ‐ to promote, protect and help repair health.

Re‐Rooting Our Relational Commitments: Cedar

Relationality is at the theoretical core of both nursing and the movements we are engaging with in this paper. Redvers et al. (2021) emphasize that planetary health is not just about restoring ecosystems. It is also about restoring relationships. This means recognizing that violence against the lands and waters is inseparable from violence against many including women, Indigenous Peoples and future generations (Laboucan‐Massimo 2018). The Indigenous Consensus offers a vision of planetary health that is redefined through Indigenous ways of knowing and being. These determinants, though rooted in specific Indigenous contexts, offer guidance for all humanity. They call on nurses to consider land tenure, language revitalization and Indigenous governance as vital determinants of health; to recognize the interconnectedness of ecological and gender‐based violence, and to actively support Indigenous legal systems that recognize the rights and personhood of lands, waters and other‐than‐human beings. This orientation aligns with nursing's long‐standing commitment to relational caring, and perhaps to critical caring (Falk‐Rafael 2005) but expands the reach and relevance in a time of ecological collapses and planetary ill‐health.

For nursing, re‐rooting in relationality requires looking outward and inward at the same time. Outwardly, Indigenous, feminist, queer, kincentric and ecological epistemologies invite nursing to expand relational ethics beyond anthropocentric limits. Inwardly, nursing must also recover the relational resources already within its own discipline. Ecocentric theorists such as Schuster (1990), Kleffel (1996) and Anderko (2014) remind us that nursing's long‐standing emphasis on holism and caring is inherently relational, yet this insight has too often been reduced to the human sphere by Eurocentric and biomedical dominance. More recent scholarship (e.g., Best et al. 2025; J. LeClair 2021; J. M. LeClair et al. 2024; J. LeClair et al 2024; Kalogirou et al. 2020; Sanderson et al. 2020) continues and strengthens this thread in nursing.

For nursing, engaging these epistemic alternatives is a work of repair: unlearning anthropocentrism and dangerously, dualistically conceived liberal humanism, recovering and expanding on the discipline's own relational heritage and expanding its epistemic horizons to include more‐than‐human knowledges. Re‐rooting our relational commitments, then, is a project of repair and expansion. Like the cedar tree, which grows in relation to salmon, water, soil and sun, offering roots for stability, branches for shelter, wood for building and clothing and medicines for healing, nursing relationality gains strength and meaning when it is understood as rooted in its own disciplinary soil and fed by other tributaries of thought and practice.

Darryl's conversation with Kii'i'juus (Haida Nation, lives on Haida Gwaii, British Columbia Canada, personal communication, September 2025) highlights that Western laws and policies often exclude and narrow, while Indigenous law offers inclusive, needs‐based and holistic frameworks. For nursing and healthcare, this can translate to treating patients as part of the ecosystem, ensuring equitable access to care and grounding practice in collective responsibility. Kii'i'juus compared this to how species in Nature, such as wolves, care for their most vulnerable, an example humanity should follow.

Whereas English simplifies a “tree” into a noun, Haida and other Indigenous languages describe trees as verbs, beings defined by their roles and relationships such as shade for salmon fry and riverbank stabilization. For nursing, ecologizing our holistic and relational thinking offers a pathway to decolonizing care and aligning health practices with anti/decolonial understandings.

Nursing REAP for Decolonial Planetary Health

Nursing for decolonial planetary health, emphasizing the development of biodiversity literacy and care practices, calls for action across multiple domains of nursing. These actions must centre and credit Indigenous knowledges, and begin from epistemic and cultural humility, and relationship‐building. When engaging with Indigenous worldviews, nurses and educators must ensure transparency and proper attribution in any sharing of knowledge. In REAP, nurses need to critically reflect on their positionality and privilege. Conclusion

The current global biodiversity crisis is a planetary health emergency. It is tangled in colonialism, capitalist extractivism and anthropocentrism. Multispecies justice asks us to attend to these intersecting injustices experienced by human and other‐than‐human beings in the context of shared vulnerability to these “isms.” River, salmon and cedar are all under threat from a necropolitical system turning life toward depletion; a grey diminished world, with diminished opportunities for health. For nurses, responding to this requires ecologizing relational practice and daring an act of imagination: envisioning a multispecies and decolonial ethics of care that stretches beyond what nursing has yet named. Attending to the interdependence of life expands nursing's ethical horizons beyond anthropocentrism. In turn, centring biodiversity within nursing strengthens the foundation for a decolonial planetary health paradigm that honours reciprocity, relationality, respect and the health of more‐than‐human communities. This shift toward ecocentric nursing for decolonial planetary health expands the moral horizon of care. In doing so, we can challenge the view that Nature, and animals, are expendable and separate from or subordinate to human health and instead position biodiversity as fundamental to health and justice on Earth for all.
