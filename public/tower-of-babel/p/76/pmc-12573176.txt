Artificial Synapse Based on Black Phosphorus/SnS2 Heterostructure Transistor for Neuromorphic Computing with High Accuracy
Authors: Wenxing Lv, Yuxuan Zeng, Xiaobo Wang, Weiming Lv, Xiaojing Wu, Hongmei Tian, Baoshun Zhang, Li Huang, Zhongming Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Artificial synaptic devices have garnered significant attention for overcoming the limitations of conventional von Neumann architecture in neuromorphic computing. However, the reported nonlinear weight update of artificial synapses prevents a hardware neural network from achieving the same high training and learning accuracy levels as those attained by a soft neural work. Despite the large-area fabrication constraints, two-dimensional (2D) materials that have tunable electronic band alignment and optoelectronic properties have emerged as promising candidates for artificial synapses. Here, we demonstrate an artificial synaptic device based on a black phosphorus (BP)/SnS2 van der Waals heterostructure. This device achieves a large memory window and successfully emulates various synaptic functionalities. Notably, the nonlinearity in the synaptic weight update of this device can be electrically modulated, and the underlying mechanism is analyzed by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. Furthermore, we successfully integrate BP/SnS2-based artificial synapses into an artificial neural network, achieving a high recognition accuracy of 96.3% on the MNIST data set for handwritten digit recognition. These findings introduce an effective method for high-accuracy neuromorphic applications through the band structure engineering of 2D materials.

Introduction

Modern computing systems predominantly rely on the von Neumann architecture; however, its inherent separation of memory and processing units imposes critical energy and efficiency bottlenecks for big data processing. , To address these limitations, neuromorphic computing, inspired by the human brain’s parallel architecture, has emerged as a transformative paradigm for next-generation hardware. Neuromorphic systems promise ultralow-power, high-speed, and fault-tolerant operations by emulating biological neural networks, making them well suited for the demands of modern computing. , In neuromorphic systems, artificial synapses play a crucial role by enabling rapid, specific information transfer between adjacent neurons through dynamic synaptic weights regulation. Among various implementations, three-terminal synaptic transistors based on two-dimensional (2D) materials have gained prominence due to their intrinsic advantages: atomically sharp interfaces for robust electrostatic control, exceptional mechanical flexibility, and sensitivity to multiple stimuli (electrical/optical/mechanical). − Specifically, black phosphorus (BP)-based synapses have demonstrated exceptional potential owing to their thickness-dependent bandgap (0.3–2 eV), high hole mobility (>1000 cm2/V·s), and anisotropic optoelectronic properties. − Nevertheless, most of these reported BP-based artificial synapses exhibit a nonlinear weight update, impeding their practical application in the field of neuromorphic computing. −

Recently, van der Waals heterostructure, formed by stacking different 2D materials with specific properties and band alignments, offer promising solutions to overcome these limitations. − For example, graphene/h-BN/SnS2 floating-gate transistors achieve high linearity and conductance ratios in long-term potentiation/depression (LTP/D) due to a low hole-injection barrier at the SnS2/h-BN interface. Graphene/MoS2/SiO x memristive synapses attain ultrahigh linearity in conductance weight update via defect-mediated conductive filaments, correlating with extracted trap levels in SiO x and MoS2. MoS2/In4/3P2Se6 heterostructures exhibit low nonlinear factors (0.014/0.042) enabled by a intrinsically low conduction band offset (0.86 eV). Recent advances in BP-based heterostructures further enable novel mechanisms, including gate-tunable charge transfer in BP/SnSe, filamentary switching in BP/HfO x bilayers, ferroelectric polarization modulation in BP/P­(VDF-TrFE) systems, and coupled quantum confinement effect in low-power BP/graphene synapses. These advances highlight the potential of BP heterostructures for energy-efficient neuromorphic computing. However, the influence of operational regimes on conductance-update linearity, which is critical for learning accuracy, remains largely unexplored.

In this work, inspired by the efficient modulation of interfacial band alignment on the weight update of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)-based synaptic device, , we propose a synaptic transistor based on the BP/SnS2 heterostructure. Here, BP serves as the functional layer emulating synaptic behavior, while the SnS2 layer forms a broken-gap band alignment with BP to regulate synaptic performance. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirm this broken-gap band alignment at the BP/SnS2 interface, corroborating our experimental observations and elucidating the mechanisms underlying the enhanced synaptic performance.

In the first part of this work, we systemically characterize the electrical transport properties of the BP/SnS2 heterostructure, achieving a memory window of 55 V under ± 60 V of the back-gate sweeps, establishing a foundation for synaptic emulation. Then synaptic functionalities are demonstrated under various back-gate-voltage pulses, where long-term potentiation/depression (LTP/D), and short-term plasticity (STP) were successfully emulated, and the nonlinearity of weight update can be modulated by tuning the amplitude of the back-gate pulse. Finally, an artificial neural network (ANN) simulation employing these BP/SnS2 synapses achieves a pattern recognition accuracy of 96.3%. This work establishes interface band engineering as a critical design paradigm for high-precision neuromorphic devices, advancing the practical implementation of 2D material-based neuromorphic computing systems.

Results and Discussion

Prior to the synaptic characterization, we first investigated the electrical transport properties of the BP/SnS2 field-effect transistor (FETs). Figure a displays the output (I ds–V ds) curves of the BP/SnS2 FET. Diverse functional diode behavior, including backward rectifying diode (−60 V ≤ V g < 0 V) and forward rectifying diode (0 V ≤ V g ≤ 60 V), was observed by varying the gate bias. Discrepancies in current conduction under reverse V ds biases between these different functional diodes can be easily discriminated by their output curves shown in a logarithmic scale (see Figure S1). The forward to reverse rectification ratio (I 1 V/I –1 V) approached 1.5 for a forward rectifying diode configuration while the reverse-to-forward rectification ratio (I –1 V/I 1 V) approached 1.4 for a backward rectifying diode configuration. Similar multifunctional diodes have also been reported in BP/MoS2, , BP/InSe, and BP/SnSeS heterostructures. In contrast to the prior mechanisms (BP thickness-dependent band alignment in BP/MoS2 or BP/InSe or length-dependent band alignment in the BP/SnSeS heterostructure), the multifunctionality within our BP/SnS2 device is attributed to the oxidization-modulated band alignment of the heterostructure, which is discussed in detail below.

To elucidate the underlying physics of such multifunctionality, we systematically investigated the electrical transport properties of individual BP and SnS2 FETs (Figure b,c). The I ds–V ds curves of BP FETs show a nonlinear feature, whereas SnS2 FETs demonstrated near-Ohmic contact with Cr/Au electrodes. This contrasts theoretical expectations given Cr’s larger work-function mismatch with SnS2 than with BP. The apparent discrepancy can be explained by considering surface oxidation in both BP and SnS2. − , Both materials readily adsorb ambient oxygen, forming charge-trapping oxide species that can alter their electrical transport properties. The I ds–V g curves of the SnS2 FETs show a typical n-type conduction feature with a small hysteresis during dual-sweeping of the gate voltage (V g), as shown in Figure S2a. However, the I ds–V g curves of the BP device (Figure S2b) show a p-type conduction feature with a large hysteresis. Notably, BP FET’s threshold voltage V th is close to that of the BP/SnS2 heterostructure (Figure d), indicating that the BP channel dominates the conductive characteristics of the heterostructure in the cutoff state. The memory window of individual BP, individual SnS2, and BP/SnS2 heterostructure FETs is summarized in Figure S2c, which is quantified by the threshold voltage shift (ΔV th) between forward (negative-to-positive) and reverse (positive-to-negative) sweeps. It is found that the BP/SnS2 heterostructure achieves a large memory window of 55 V at V gmax = 60 V, comparable to that of individual BP FETs but significantly exceeding that of SnS2 FETs at equivalent V gmax. Thus, we can infer that the nonvolatile memory effects of the BP/SnS2 heterostructure originate from the BP channel, while the gate-dependent multifunctional diode behavior arises from the band alignment of BP and SnS2 with a tunable Fermi level. All of these are related to the oxidization within the BP/SnS2 heterostructure.

To clarify the effect of oxidation on the electrical transport properties of the BP/SnS2 heterostructure, we combined a series of experimental characterizations with DFT calculations. Previous studies have systematically investigated the ambient oxidation behavior of BP. ,− These investigations reveal that oxygen dissociation on BP surfaces exhibits both low activation energy barriers and high exothermic characteristics. In contrast, theoretical calculations by Yu et al. suggest that SnS2 undergoes surface oxidation through the formation of Sn–O bonds under ambient conditions. Cross-sectional TEM images of the respective BP and SnS2 regions in our BP/SnS2 heterostructure (Figure S3a,b) provide direct evidence of surface oxidation in both materials. Complementary EDX analysis (Figure S3c,d) further demonstrates a higher oxygen concentration on SnS2 surfaces compared to BP, and the presence of ∼3 nm amorphous PO x layers at both top (BP/Cr) and bottom (BP/SiO2) interfaces. Based on these experimental observations, our computational models incorporate one and three oxygen adatoms on BP and SnS2 surfaces, respectively, to simulate realistic interface conditions.

Figure a presents the atomic configuration of the oxygen-incorporated BP/SnS2 heterostructure with engineered defects. To elucidate the individual contributions of BP and SnS2 within the heterostructure, we performed band unfolding calculations with wave function decomposition along the z-direction. To accurately separate the electronic states of the BP and SnS2 layers while retaining their mutual interactions, we carefully selected the wave function cutoff to encompass regions extending ±1.715 Å from the surfaces of the BP and SnS2 layers along the z-direction, corresponding to half of the interlayer spacing of 3.43 Å. This approach allows us to isolate the electronic states of BP and SnS2 effectively. The light-gray planes in Figure a illustrate these cutoff regions in real space. Comparative band structure analyses of the pristine and oxidized heterostructures, focusing on the individual BP and SnS2 layers, are displayed in Figure b. For the pristine BP/SnS2 system (upper panel of Figure b), distinct bandgaps of 0.224 eV for BP and 2.192 eV for SnS2 reveal a characteristic broken-gap alignment. Notably, upon oxidation, the BP layer (lower panel of Figure b) exhibits a reduced bandgap of 0.120 eV, attributed to a downward shift of the conduction band minimum (CBM) and an upward shift of the valence band maximum (VBM). The oxidation of the pristine BP/SnS2 structure introduces substitutional defects, with oxygen atoms replacing the phosphorus atoms in BP and sulfur atoms in SnS2, respectively.

Importantly, three dispersion-less impurity states emerge around 0.480 eV in the band structure of the oxidized BP layer, similar to the defect-induced states observed in oxidized BP multilayers. These impurity states are proposed to be the physical origin of the nonlinear transport behavior observed in BP FETs, consistent with our experimental findings shown in Figure b. While the oxidized SnS2 maintains a comparable bandgap (2.184 eV), its band morphology and energy levels exhibit only slight changes after oxidation. This is primarily because in SnS2, the substitutional defects involve oxygen atoms replacing sulfur atoms. Although the doping concentration of oxygen in SnS2 is higher than that in BP, both oxygen and sulfur belong to group VI of the periodic table and have similar outer electron configurations. As a result, the formation of compounds with these substitutional defects does not significantly alter the electronic structure of SnS2. In contrast, in the BP layer, oxygen atoms replace phosphorus atoms, introducing foreign impurities that substantially affect the electronic structure. Despite a lower doping concentration, these substitutions lead to significant changes, such as the emergence of the dispersion-less impurity states. Therefore, compared to the significant bandgap reduction and impurity states in BP, the SnS2 layer experiences less variation. Furthermore, SnS2 exhibits signatures of CBM pinning at the Γ-point due to oxidation. This CBM pinning, combined with electron doping under the gate voltage (resulting in the Fermi level moving upward), maintains the metallic character of SnS2. This electronic restructuring provides a mechanistic explanation for the anomalously linear output characteristics observed in Figure c. Additionally, it is worth noting that in the oxidized BP within the heterostructure, the VBM lies slightly above the Fermi level by a small amount, effectively placing it near the Fermi level. This suggests that under hole doping conditions, oxidized BP also exhibits metallic behavior.

When applying positive V g, positive gate voltage induces upward Fermi level shifts in both BP and SnS2, leading to the depletion of BP and electron accumulation in SnS2, thereby resulting in nearly no charge flow through the heterostructure. In this case, the ultralow current under reverse V ds primarily stems from the drift of minority carriers (electrons) from BP to SnS2 , (as depicted in Figure b), which is similar to the electrical transport observed in the WSe2/SnS2 heterostructure with a broken-gap alignment. Conversely, the relatively large current under positive V ds can be attributed to the direct tunneling of electrons from SnS2 to BP, as confirmed by the relationship ln­( IV2 ) ∝ ln­( 1V )­ −4πd2m*φh shown in Figure S4. , Here, the Planck constant, tunneling thickness, tunneling barrier, and effective electron mass are respectively referred to by the characters h, d, φ, and m*. The positive slope of direct tunneling (DT) fitting indicates the presence of direct tunneling in our device, consistent with previous reports. When a negative V g is applied, the Fermi levels of both BP and SnS2 shift downward. This causes BP to become conductive, while SnS2 enters a depleted state. As a result, at a small negative V ds, electrons in the valence band of BP can tunnel to the conduction band of SnS2 via band-to-band tunneling , (Figure d). Consequently, the reverse current begins to increase at a small reverse V ds, which is consistent with the I ds–V ds curves of a backward rectifying diode observed in Figure a. Similarly, with a gradual increase in the positive V ds, the current also arises from the direct tunneling effect (Figure e). It should be noted that when a positive V g is applied, electrons tunneling from SnS2 to the BP channel are trapped by the amorphous PO x layers. This trapping effect leads to a reduction in I ds, as is clearly shown in Figure a.

Based on the electrical transportation established above, we further investigated the capability of the devices to emulate synaptic behavior. In biological neural systems, synapses connect presynaptic and postsynaptic neurons, transmitting signals by modulating neurotransmitter content (Figure a). , The connection strength between neurons, known as synaptic weight, is reflected by the postsynaptic current (PSC) intensity. , The PSC can be further classified as excitatory (EPSC) or inhibitory (IPSC), corresponding to synaptic weight potentiation or depression, respectively. In our three-terminal FET architecture, the gate terminal functions as the presynaptic input, while the channel current emulates the PSC, enabling gate-controlled modulation of synaptic behavior (Figure a). Synaptic plasticity, which governs information processing in neural networks, can be categorized into short-term plasticity (STP) and long-term plasticity (LTP) based on retention times. STP, typically lasting tens of seconds, is essential for transient neural computations, whereas LTP, which persists from minutes to hours, forms the foundation for learning and memory formation.

Figure b shows the excitatory synaptic response to a positive input pulse (60 V amplitude) with various widths (10–500 ms) applied at the gate of the BP/SnS2 device. When a positive pulse is applied to the gate, electrons are injected from the BP/SnS2 channel to the PO x layer. Some injected electrons are trapped into the PO x layer and decay slowly, resulting in a positive shift of the minimum conductivity voltage and thereby a higher postsynaptic current in the BP/SnS2 channel after the gate pulse returns to zero. That is, a positive gate pulse can induce EPSC in the BP/SnS2 channel, as shown in Figure b, and the EPSC value increases markedly with an increasing pulse width. The relaxation process was analyzed by fitting the EPSC with a double exponential function, as shown in Figure S5a,b. The results show that the relaxation time increases with pulse width, likely because wider pulses trap more electrons into the PO x layer, resulting in a longer relaxation time. Additionally, we examined the impact of pulse amplitude on the EPSC. As shown in Figure c, the EPSC increases with a positive input pulse amplitude; however, at amplitudes of 20 and 40 V, it decays rapidly (within 15 s) with the feature of STP. In contrast, at 60 V, the EPSC remains significantly higher than the baseline current measured before pulse application. Similarly, the relaxation time was calculated by fitting the EPSC with a double exponential function (see Figure S5c,d). The results reveal the feature of LTP for the 60 V case. When the negative input pulses (−20 V to −60 V) were applied to the gate (Figure d), the removal of trapped electrons results in a lower current, generating an inhibitory synaptic response. Similarly, the IPSC at −60 V shows a larger magnitude and longer retention time than those at −20 V and −40 V.

To further understand the physical origin of the artificial synaptic performance of the BP/SnS2 device, we measured synaptic behaviors of individual BP and SnS2 synaptic devices. As illustrated in Figure S6, individual BP devices can simulate artificial synaptic properties via gate pulse application with the performance similar to that of BP/SnS2 devices. However, SnS2 devices do not exhibit artificial synaptic performance, revealing that the synaptic characteristics in the BP/SnS2 heterostructure are primarily governed by field-driven electron trapping/detrapping dynamics at the amorphous PO x interface.

The long-term potentiation (LTP) and long-term depression (LTD) behaviors of our BP/SnS2 synaptic device were investigated by applying 20 consecutive gate-voltage pulses with alternating polarities. Figure e demonstrates exceptional cycle-to-cycle reproducibility through 120 successive pulses, exhibiting negligible intercycle variation. Herin, each reversible regulation of PSC was measured by means of 20 positive gate-voltage pulses and 20 negative gate-voltage pulses, and the PSC values were measured 250 ms after each gate pulse. However, the endurance of such a BP/SnS2 synaptic device needs to be further enhanced since it just demonstrates a relative stable performance if applying more cycles (evidenced by Figure S7), which is attributed to the inherent environmental sensitivity of BP and could be addressed by employing h-BN as the capping layer. Moreover, as shown in Figure e, when positive pulse amplitudes were applied, the ΔPSC gradually increased with an increasing amplitude. Conversely, for negative pulse amplitudes, the ΔPSC decreased as the amplitude increased. This amplitude-dependent response under both polarities indicates that higher V g amplitudes induce the movement of a greater number of electrons into or out of the PO x layers.

Finally, to validate the potential of our BP/SnS2 synaptic devices for neuromorphic applications such as pattern recognition, we integrated them into a multilayer perceptron artificial neural network (ANN), which is formed by three layers with 28 × 28 input neurons, 400 hidden neurons, and 10 output neurons, respectively, as shown in Figure a. Each layer of neurons was fully connected by synapses, with weights updated through feedforward and backpropagation processes. Here, synaptic training inputs correspond to the synaptic plasticity at a pulse amplitude of 60 V (shown in Figure f). The ANN model is trained and tested on the Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (MNIST) data set, where the 784 neurons in the input layer correspond to the 28 × 28 (pixels) of the MNIST image, while the 10 output neurons correspond to 10 classes of digits (from 0 to 9). , The experimental result is shown in Figure b. After 100 training epochs, the accuracy becomes stable at ∼96.3%, surpassing other reported synaptic devices. Therefore, the ANN simulation at the system level demonstrates the outstanding performance of our BP/SnS2 synaptic devices in pattern recognition tasks. Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated an artificial synapse based on the BP/SnS2 heterostructure, capable of effectively emulating a wide range of biological synaptic properties, including EPSC/IPSC, short-term plasticity, long-term plasticity, long-term potentiation, and depression (LTP/D). The nonlinearity of BP/SnS2-based synaptic device in LTD can be significantly reduced by decreasing the gate-voltage pulse amplitude. This reduced nonlinearity is lower than that observed in the BP-only artificial synapse and is attributed to the direct tunneling effect inherent at the BP/SnS2 interface. Furthermore, we explored the potential of the BP/SnS2-based synaptic device for pattern recognition tasks using a multilayer perceptron artificial neural network (ANN). Specifically, we applied this device to the MNIST handwritten digit recognition task and achieved a high accuracy of 96.3%, comparable to or exceeding other reported synaptic devices. Our work demonstrates the promise of band engineering in van der Waals heterostructures as a promising strategy for designing synaptic devices and provides a viable pathway for implementing next-generation neuromorphic systems with improved learning accuracy and energy efficiency.

Methods and Experimental Section

Device Fabrication

The BP/SnS2 heterostructure-based synaptic transistor was fabricated on a heavily p-doped Si wafer covered with a thermally grown 280 nm thick SiO2 dielectric layer. The substrate was cleaned with acetone, isopropanol, and deionized water in sequence, and dried with pure nitrogen. First, thin BP flakes were mechanically exfoliated from their bulk crystals (HQ graphene) via standard scotch tape to the poly­(dimethylsiloxane) film and then transferred onto the SiO2 (280 nm)/Si substrate with prepared electrical pads. Then, few-layer SnS2 was exfoliated onto the poly­(dimethylsiloxane) film and artificially stacked atop the BP flake under the optical microscope assisted by an aligned transfer system. Finally, multiple metal electrodes were patterned by standard electron-beam lithography and deposited with metal Cr/Au (10/60 nm) by electron-beam evaporation to fabricate source and drain electrodes.

Device Characterizations

Morphology of the BP/SnS2 vdW heterostructure was investigated with an optical microscope (BX51, OLMPUS). The thickness was conducted by an atomic force microscope (Dimension 3100, Veeco). Raman spectra were taken from pristine BP and SnS2, and the overlapped BP/SnS2 region, respectively, on a Horriba-JY LABRAM HR Raman spectrometer with 532 nm laser. The atomic structure features of the BP/SnS2 heterojunction structure were investigated by using a high-resolution transmission electron microscope (Talos F200X, FEI), and the composition and elemental distribution of the heterojunctions were analyzed by EDX mapping on a high-resolution transmission electron microscope. All electrical measurements were carried out with two high-precision digital source meters (Keithley 2612B and 2400).

DFT Calculations

First-principles calculations within the density functional theory (DFT) framework were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP), based on the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) type and employing projector-augmented-wave (PAW) pseudopotentials. A rectangular unit cell was utilized to model the heterostructure, consisting of three layers of BP and a single layer of SnS2. Specifically, the heterostructure was constructed by stacking a 6 × 4 BP supercell with a 3√3 × 5 SnS2 supercell, resulting in a minimal lattice mismatch of less than 1%. To simulate the oxidation observed experimentallywhere SnS2 exhibits more pronounced oxidation than BPwe introduced substitutional oxygen defects by replacing one phosphorus atom with an oxygen atom in the BP layer and substituting three sulfur atoms with oxygen atoms in the SnS2 layer. This approach aligns with our energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) measurements (see Figure S2), which indicate a higher oxygen concentration in SnS2. The plane-wave energy cutoff was set to 520 eV, and the Brillouin zone was sampled using a Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack 5 × 5 × 1 k-point mesh. All atoms were fully relaxed until the forces converged to less than 1 × 10–2 eV/Å. A vacuum layer of 20 Å was included along the z-direction to prevent interactions between periodic images. To analyze the electronic structures of the individual BP and SnS2 layers within the heterostructure, we employed k-projection-based band unfolding methods with wave function decomposition along the lattice z-direction. This technique allows us to decompose the total wave function of the heterostructure and extract the contributions from BP and SnS2 separately, despite their differing lattice symmetries. Specifically, the wave function cutoff was carefully selected to encompass regions extending ±1.715 Å from the surfaces of the BP and SnS2 layers along the z-direction, corresponding to half of the interlayer spacing of 3.43 Å (1.715 Å2). For both the three-layer BP and the single-layer SnS2, this cutoff includes their respective thicknesses plus an additional ±1.715 Å on either side along the z-axis. This selective projection enables us to obtain the local band structures of BP and SnS2 while preserving the effects of their interactions, facilitating a detailed analysis of the electronic properties relevant to our experimental observations.
