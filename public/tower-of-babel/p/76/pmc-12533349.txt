Tropomodulin 1 is essential for chemotherapy sensitivity and associated with better outcome in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Yijie Li, Xinyi Long, Yiting Xie, Xiaorong Zhong, Shiyu Cao, Fei Chen, Mengjia Shen, Lin Xiao, Lijuan Yin, Feng Ye, Ting Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer (BC), characterized by poor clinical behaviors and outcomes. TMOD1 was reported as a downstream target of NF-κB contributing to the growth of cancer cells. However, it’s impacts on TNBC patients’ prognosis and treatment response remain unclear.MethodsThe prognostic value of TMOD1 was analyzed by data from the Kaplan–Meier database and verified through immunohistochemical evaluation from 190 TNBC patients in West China Hospital (WCH). Cell Counting Kit-8 assay, Transwell cell migration assay and Matrigel invasion assay were used for in vitro study. TNBC cells treated with gradient dosage of doxorubicin (Dox), paclitaxel (PTX), and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) for chemotherapy sensitivity assay. The chemotherapy sensitivity of Dox in vivo was further confirmed by cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model.ResultsTNBC patients with high TMOD1 expression had longer overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in both Kaplan–Meier plotter database and in cohort from WCH. In cellular functional study, overexpression of TMOD1 promoted TNBC cells’ proliferation, migration and invasion. It also enhanced TNBC sensitivity to Dox treatment both in vivo and in vitro.ConclusionsHigh TMOD1 expression in TNBC patients is associated with better prognosis. Although TMOD1 expression promotes TNBC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, TMOD1 enhances the sensitivity to Dox therapy both in vivo and in vitro, which might contribute to improve the prognosis for TNBC patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-14961-9.

Introduction

Tropomodulin 1 (TMOD1), an actin-capping protein that binds to the pointed end of actin filaments, preventing monomer addition or loss. This capping action stabilizes actin structures, ensuring cytoskeletal integrity and controlling filament length [1]. TMOD1 also enhances tropomyosin’s binding to actin filaments in order to regulate cell shape and migration [2]. It was reported that overexpression of TMOD1 can rigidify actin networks, reducing cell motility, while its loss destabilizes protrusions like lamellipodia [3]. Knockout of TMOD1 leads to embryonic lethality in null mice model, and expression of TMOD1 in the heart rescues embryonic lethality but results in a mild hemolytic anemia due to absence of TMOD1 in red blood cells [4].

In recent years, increasing evidence indicated that TMOD1 plays an important role in cancers. In thyroid cancer, TMOD1 SNP (Rs10982622) was reported correlated with a remarkable decreased carcinoma risk [5]. In cervical cancer, downregulation of TMOD1 promotes cell motility and proliferation [6]. For oral cancer, overexpression of TMOD1 enhanced regional lymph node metastasis, and associated with the prediction of patient prognosis [7]. Furthermore, TMOD1 has been reported as a novel NF-κB target gene and may contribute to the enhanced growth of Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) in vivo [8]. Among these cancers involved, TNBC exhibits relatively higher malignancy and invasiveness. Also, TMOD1 is a key regulator of F-actin stability and may affect tumor cell migration, while the high aggressiveness of TNBC also relies on cytoskeletal remodeling. And, TMOD1 is an NF-κB target in TNBC [8] it suggests that its immune-related role in TNBC may be associated with its therapeutic effects, while no studies have systematically correlated TMOD1 expression with TNBC and its therapeutic effects, limiting its translational potential.

Plenty of factors effected the outcomes of TNBC patients, such as size of tumor, status of lymph node, therapy response, etc. As TNBC is the most aggressive subtypes of breast cancer with very limited therapeutic options, their treatment response and prediction of curative effect are particularly important for patients. Given the lack of effective targeted therapies, systemic chemotherapy (e.g., paclitaxel, doxorubicin, or platinum-based agents) remains the cornerstone of current TNBC treatment. However, although approximately 30–65% of patients receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy achieve pathological complete response (pCR), those failing to attain pCR face a 3-year recurrence risk as high as 27% (compared to only 9% in pCR patients), with a median survival of merely 18–28 months after recurrence [9]. Consequently, a deeper understanding of TNBC chemoresistance mechanisms and the development of strategies to overcome them are urgently needed to improve patient prognosis.

TMOD1 play essential roles for the integrity of the cytoskeleton and this is crucial for cellular response to chemotherapy drugs, thus we also hypothesize that TMOD1 may effect TNBC patients prognosis through chemotherapy responses. This study aimed to investigate the clinical significance of TMOD1 in TNBC. We evaluated the correlation between TMOD1 expression and the clinicopathological characteristics, prognosis significance, as well as treatment response.

Materials and methods

Survival prognosis analysis

In the “pancancer RNA-seq” modules of the Kaplan–Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) [10], we analyzed the correlation between Tmod1 mRNA expression and survival, including OS and RFS in TNBC. RFS was defined as the time from the start of surgery to the first observation of local/distant recurrence or death due to any cause. Only the first 60 months of follow-up were used to define survival status. OS was defined as the time from the start of surgery to death from any cause [11].

Clinical data resource

Paraffin sections were obtained from 190 primary TNBC patients who received chemotherapy, without receiving neoadjuvant therapy at WCH from 2010 to 2021. Approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee of WCH (2017 (No.243)), and informed consent was obtained from patients. Clinicopathological characteristics of these patients, including age, BMI, TNM staging, menopause status, WHO grade, and survival time (OS and RFS), were collected. The definitions of RFS and OS are the same as above.

Immunochemistry (IHC) and interpretation

IHC of 4-µm sections on formalin fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens was performed. According to the instructions of the EnVision two-step kit (Dako, Denmark), tissue sections were dewaxed and rehydrated with xylene (Sigma, St Louis, USA) and gradient ethanol. Then, antigen repair was performed with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; pH 9 (Sigma, St Louis, USA)) in a microwave oven. The primary antibody (rabbit, anti-TMOD1, Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) was diluted at a concentration of 1:80 and incubated at 4 °C overnight. The secondary antibody (Kangwei Century Biotechnology, Beijing, China) was incubated at room temperature for 50 min. Finally, slides were stained with diaminobenzidine (DAB) (4 A Biotech, Beijing, China) for 3–5 min. Hematoxylin (MedChemexpress, NJ, USA) was used for contrast staining, followed by dehydration and sealing. The images of the sections were collected using a pathological biopsy scanner PRECICE 600X (unic-med, China).

Two experienced pathologists examined the stained slides and scored the TMOD1 signal in invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) cells. The Fromowitz comprehensive scoring method [12] was used to make judgments based on staining intensity and positive cell percentage. The TMOD1 staining intensity was interpreted as follows: 0 points (colorless), 1 point (light yellow), 2 points (brownish yellow), and 3 points (brownish brown). The percentage of positive cells was scored as follows: 1 point (< 25% cells), 2 points (25–49%), 3 points (50–74%), and 4 points (> 75%). The staining degree was multiplied by the percentage of positive cells as the final IHC staining score of TMOD1: 0 ~ 1 is negative (-), 2 ~ 4 is weakly positive (+), 5 ~ 8 is medium positive (++), and 6 ~ 12 is strongly positive (+++). Negative and weakly positive were defined as low expression (0–4 points); medium positive and strongly positive were defined as high expression.

Cell culture

Breast cancer cell lines (MCF-7, BT474, SK-BR-3, MDA-MB-231) and the normal breast epithelial cell line MCF-10 A were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells used in this study were identified using short tandem repeat (STR) sequencing and were free of cross-contamination or misidentification. The SK-BR-3 cell line was cultured in McCoy’s-5 A medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and the MCF-10 A cell line was cultured in special medium for mammary epithelial cells (Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% horse serum (HS), 1% penicillin‒streptomycin solution (PS) and 1% breast epithelial cell growth factor (EGF). Other cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Overexpression plasmid cell transfection

In each well of a 6-well plate, lentivirus packaging plasmids (1.5 µg PsPAX2, 0.5 µg pMD2. G, Genechem, Shanghai, China) and the overexpression plasmid of TMOD1 (2 µg, GV365, BamHI/AgeI digestion, Genechem, Shanghai, China) and the empty vector (has the same backbone of TMOD1 plasmid) were co-transfected into 293 T cells with lipo3000 transfection reagent (Genechem, Shanghai, China). After 48 h of transfection, we harvested viruses from the supernatant of wells. Next, we infected MDA-MB-231 cells with the collected viruses for 72 h and then treated the infected MDA-MB-231 cells with puromycin (6 µg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 5–7 consecutive days.

RNA extraction and polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from cells using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (YiShan Biotech, Shanghai, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNAs using EasyQuick RT MasterMix (Kangwei Century Biotechnology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed in a 10 µL reaction volume using a LightCycler 480 apparatus (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany). The designed PCR primers as shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting analysis

Total protein was extracted from the logarithmic cell lines using RIPA lysis buffer (Biyuntian Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), and the protein concentration was measured using a Pierce bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). A total of 3–10 µL protein/lane was separated via SDS‒PAGE on a 10% gel. The separated proteins were subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and blocked with 5% skim milk (Yili, Inner Mongolia, China) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against TMOD1 (1:1000; anti-rabbit. Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) and GAPDH (1:1000; anti-rabbit. Kangwei Century Biotechnology, Beijing, China) overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed three times with TBST (1% Tween‑20 in 1×TBS). Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase‑conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000; anti-rabbit. Kangwei Century Biotechnology, Beijing, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were washed three times again with TBST, and protein bands were visualized using ECL Western blotting substrate (4 A Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell proliferation assay

The transfected cells were reseeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3 × 103 MDA-MB-231 cells/mL. Cell viability was measured using CCK-8 (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Rockville, MD, USA) after incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, and then the absorbance value at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay plate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) every day for 4 consecutive days.

Clone formation assay

The transfected cells were reseeded into 6-well plates at a density of 500 MDA-MB-231 cells/mL and allowed to adhere overnight. Following incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 14 days, the wells were washed three times with PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), immobilized with 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 15 min at room temperature and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 20 min. Finally, stained cells were counted in all fields of a well.

Cell migration assay

Before the preparation of the cell suspension, the medium was changed, and the cells in each group were cultured in a serum-free environment for 12–24 h. A total of 3 × 104 MDA-MB-231 cells in 200 µL RPMI-1640 medium were plated in each upper chamber of transwell plates. In addition, 600 µL of 10% FBS in RPMI-1640 medium was plated in the lower chambers. Following incubation at 37 °C for 48 h, the nonmigratory cells in the upper chambers were removed, and the migratory cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with crystal violet for 20 min. Stained cells were counted in 5 fields, photographed and counted under a microscope randomly.

Cell invasion assay

Transwell chambers were used to detect the invasion ability of cells, and 50 mg/L Matrigel (1:8 dilution) was used to coat the upper surface of the bottom membrane of transwell chambers to make it evenly distributed. The 24-well plates with chambers were placed in the cell incubator and incubated for 4 h until the gel solidified. Before use, the chambers lined with Matrigel were hydrated with 50 µL of serum-free medium and placed at 37 °C for 30 min. The rest of the operation was the same as the migration experiment.

Chemotherapeutic sensitivity assay

The transfected cells were reseeded into 96-well plates in 100 µL of culture medium at a density of 5 × 103 MDA-MB-231 cells/mL. The cells were then treated with Doxorubicin (Dox, 0.2 µM, 0.4 µM, 0.6 µM, 0.8 µM, 1 µM), paclitaxel (PTX, 2.5 µM, 5µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, 40 µM), and fluorouracil (5-FU, 0.2 µM, 0.4 µM, 0.6 µM, 0.8 µM, 1 µM) and cultured in a normal incubator as usual for 48 h. RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% CCK-8 was freshly added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay plate reader, and the curve was drawn according to the growth inhibition rate.

TMOD1-Related gene enrichment analysis

The GSCALite database (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/web/GSCALite/) offers multiple types of cancer gene set analyses, including mRNA expression, single nucleotide variation (SNV), methylation, cancer-related pathways, and miRNA networks [13]. We analyzed the effect of TMOD1 on cancer-related signaling, the expression of some genes of interest, and the miRNA network between them.

TMOD1 expression analysis in Dox-resistant cells

The GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) provides comprehensive transcriptomic datasets, including RNA-seq profiles of drug-treated cancer cell lines. We analyzed dataset GSE216137 containing MCF7 cells treated with doxorubicin (1-100 nM), with three biological replicates per concentration and DMSO controls. TMOD1 mRNA expression levels (TPM) were visualized using box plots generated by GraphPad Prism 9.0, demonstrating its concentration-dependent downregulation.

CDX model experiment

A total of 20 male BALB/c nude mice (immunodeficient mice) were acquired from GemPharmatech company and raised in pathogen-free conditions. NC cells and OE cells (1 × 107 cells) were suspended in 100 µL of PBS and subcutaneously injected into mice (n = 5). After almost 3 weeks, tumors were collected and cut into almost 4–5 mm3 cubes and inoculated subcutaneously into nude mice (NC group and OE group, n = 5). 12 days after injection of the tumor mass, Dox therapy started. Dox was dissolved in PBS and given by intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at 5 mg/kg [14] every three days for 15 days. Every 3 days, tumor volume size was measurement. On the 15th day after the initiation of therapy, all mice were euthanized. The tumor growth inhibition rate (TGI) was calculated using the formula TGI(OE)= (1–RTV(OE-Dox)/RTV(OE)) ×100%, TGI(NC)= (1 –RTV(NC-Dox)/RTV(NC)) ×100% and RTV = Vt/V0 (V0: the average volume of the tumor at the beginning of the experiment, Vt: the average volume of the tumor at the end of the experiment). All animal experiments were approved and performed in accordance with the West China Hospital of Sichuan University Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (2022 − 1056).

Statistical analysis

Clinical survival statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA), and the association between the clinical variables and the five-year survival period was assessed by the log-rank test, and survival curves were plotted according to the Kaplan–Meier method. The association between the expression of TMOD1 and clinical variables was assessed by the Cox test with GraphPad. Other statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad by student t-test. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were tested by a two-sided test. P < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

Results

High TMOD1 expression was associated with better prognosis in TNBC

To check the overall effection of TMOD1 on TNBC patients, we first performed survival analysis using the Kaplan-Meier plotter database. Our results revealed that the higher expression of TMOD1 contributed to better prognosis in both overall survival (OS, Fig. 1A, P = 0.011, HR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.28–0.86) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) (RFS, Fig. 1B, P = 0.0045, 95% CI: 0.54–0.89) in TNBC patients. To further confirm this observation, we utilized FFPE specimens from 190 WCH TNBC patients to explore the correlation between TMOD1 protein expression in tumor tissue and patient survival outcomes. The clinical and pathological characteristics of 190 patients is presented in Table 1. We utilized the chi-square test to analysis the correlation degree between TMOD1 expression and demographic and clinic-pathological features. Among these characteristics, only body mass index (BMI) was significantly associated with TMOD1 expression, while no significant differences were found between the low and high TMOD1 expression groups in variables for age, tumor size (T), N Lymph Node (N), menopause status at diagnosis, or WHO grade (P > 0.05) (Table 1). TMOD1 promotes TNBC cell proliferation, migration and invasion

In recent years, increasing data demonstrated that TMOD1 plays a critical role in several cancers. A Pan-cancer analysis was used to investigate TMOD1 expression between tumor and normal tissues by UCSC database (https://xenabrowser.net/). In the 34 cancer types, TMOD1 expression was significantly increased in 6 tumor types and decreased in 26 tumor types (supplementary Fig. 2). The previous data indicated that high expression of TMOD1 led to a better prognosis in TNBC patients (Fig. 1). However, the mechanism of TMOD1 in TNBC patients’ prognosis is unclear. We first investigated the effect of TMOD1 on the proliferation, migration, and invasion in TNBC cell line(MDA-MB-231). To explore the role of TMOD1 in TNBC, we overexpressed TMOD1 in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 2A, P < 0.0001). We found that the proliferation of TMOD1-overexpressing cells (OE) was significantly higher than that of control cells (NC) in CCK-8 proliferation assay and colony formation assay (Fig. 2B and C, P < 0.05). Furthermore, the results of trans-well migration and trans-well invasion assays revealed that migration and invasion abilities of OE cells also significantly increased compared to NC (Fig. 2D and E, P < 0.001 and P < 0.01, respectively). TMOD1 enhances the sensitivity of TNBC cells to Dox treatment

According to previous findings, we found that higher TMOD1 expression correlates with a better prognosis for TNBC patients. However, TMOD1 indeed stimulates the proliferation, migration, and invasion of TNBC cells. These seemed controversial data forced us to consider other events that TMOD1 may contribute to better prognosis. The integrity of the cytoskeleton is crucial for cellular response to chemotherapy drugs, as it affects drug entry into cells, intracellular drug concentration maintenance, and drug-induced cell death processes [16]. As TMOD1 stabilizes actin structures, ensuring cytoskeletal integrity contributing to cytoskeleton integrity, we hypothesize that it might influence cellular sensitivity to chemotherapy treatment by modulating the cytoskeleton, thus playing a typical role in TNBC prognosis.

Firstly, we analyzed the GSE216137 from GEO database and the result revealed that drug resistant cells showed lower TMOD1 expression during treatment after Dox treatment (supplementary Fig. 3). When the concentration of Dox is greater than 10 nM, the expression of TMOD1 begins to decrease significantly (supplementary Fig. 3). These results suggested that high expression of TMOD1 might be more sensitive to Dox. Then, we performed a cell viability assay on TNBC cells following a panel of commonly used chemotherapy drugs, including microtubule stabilizer (paclitaxel; PTX), DNA topoisomerase IIα (TOP2A) inhibitor Dox, antimetabolite (5-fluorouracil; 5-FU). The results indicated that the drug inhibition of OE cells significantly increased after Dox treatment comparing to the NC cells (Fig. 3A). TMOD1 enhances the sensitivity of TNBC to Dox in vivo

To further reveal the role of TMOD1 in the process of Dox treatment in vivo. We constructed a cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model in BALB/c nude mice by using OE and NC cells. The experimental design and Dox treatment regimen were shown in Fig. 4A. And the growth curves of each tumor from four groups were shown in Fig. 4B and C. After 27 days post tumor implantation, tumors were harvested and photographed (Fig. 4D and E). We found that tumor volumes of the OE group were obviously increased compared to those in the NC group (Fig. 4D and E). With Dox treatment, the tumor volume in OE group was significantly reduced, while the effect in NC group was not obvious (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, the tumor growth inhibition rate (TGI) of the OE group is 47% compared with 12% in the NC group (Fig. 4F). TMOD1 involved signal pathways in TNBC

Since our results indicated that TMOD1 promoted the proliferation, migration, invasion of TNBC in vitro, and enhanced the sensitivity of TNBC to Dox. And firstly we analyzed the expression of classical markers for these phenotypes using RT-qPCR. The results indicated that TMOD1 overexpression could significantly up-regulate the proliferation and migration relevant signaling pathways compared to NC (Fig. 5A). Discussion

TNBC is a highly aggressive subtype of breast cancer (BC), which lacks a therapeutic target for precision therapy, making chemotherapy still the main treatment, but drug resistance often occurs. Identifying the corresponding biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets of chemoresistance can help improve the prognosis of patients with TNBC. In this study, we found that TMOD1 promotes TNBC cells proliferation, migration, as well as invasion, however, high expression of TMOD1 leads to a better prognosis for TNBC. Furthermore, we found that TMOD1 plays a critical role in chemotherapy resistance—higher TMOD1 expression, more sensitive to Dox treatment both in vitro and vivo.

In recent years, there has been increasing evidence that TMOD1 plays an important role in certain cancers. In cervical cancer, TMOD1 inhibited cell motility and cell proliferation [6]. While in oral cancer and TNBC, TMOD1 promoted tumor progression [7, 8]. TMOD1 is also reported involved in predicting prognosis in some cancers, e.g. oral cancer. Taku Ito-Kureha reported that TMOD1 can promote TNBC tumor growth, but other impact of TMOD1 on TNBC remains unclear, including its impact on prognosis [7]. First, we checked the role of TMOD1 on TNBC cells for basic cell and tumor effection, and found that it promotes TNBC cells proliferation, migration and invasion. This phenomenon is consistent with previous reports [7, 8, 14].

Beyond on this, we further explored the prognostic value of TMOD1 in TNBC at first by using the Kaplan-Meier plotter database and WCH clinical cohort. In this study, Table 1 shows a significant correlation between BMI and the expression of TMOD1 (P < 0.0001). This finding underscores the potential clinical significance of BMI as a modulator of TMOD1 expression. Previous research has identified TMOD1 as one of the genes associated with BMI, indicating its potential role in metabolic processes and obesity-related conditions. Specifically, a study by Wang et al. reported that TMOD1 expression differences are positively associated with BMI, further supporting our findings [17]. This correlation may have implications for understanding the pathophysiology of obesity and related disorders, as well as for identifying potential therapeutic targets. Future studies should explore the mechanistic links between BMI and TMOD1 expression to elucidate their combined impact on health outcomes.

The Kaplan-Meier plotter database and WCH clinical cohort confirmed that high TMOD1 expression was associated with better prognosis in TNBC (Fig. 1). This observation is very interesting but seems to be contrary to the finding that patients with high expression of TMOD1 had a good prognosis, which arouse our interests to further study to fill the gap. Plenty factors effecting the prognosis of TNBC patients, such as tumor size, lymph node status, chemotherapy response, etc. It has been reported that chemotherapy resistance is one of the main factors affecting survival and prognosis in patients [18] especially for TNBC patients as their treatment options is very limited. We tested 3 chemotherapeutic drugs (Dox, PTX, 5-FU) in TNBC cells. Accordingly, MDA-MB-231 cells with high TMOD1 expression were more sensitive to Dox, even though there was no significant difference for the other two chemotherapy drugs. The three different chemotherapeutic drugs have different action mechanisms. Dox intercalates DNA and inhibits topoisomerase II, while 5-FU disrupts thymidylate synthase and PTX stabilizes microtubules [19]. Our data further indicated that TMOD1 enhances the sensitivity of Dox treatment in TNBC tumors in vivo (Fig. 4). These results explain that TMOD1 high expression leads to better prognosis in TNBC patients, though TMOD1 promote TNBC cell proliferation, migration, as well as invasion. Actually, TMOD1 shows very contradictory roles in different cancer types: it promoting tumor progression in oral cancers [7, 8] while inhibiting cancer cell motility and proliferation in cervical cancer [6]. Our data shows its role in TNBC is even more complicated.

Our data also indicated that TMOD1 overexpression upregulated Cyclin D1 and Ki67 expression (Fig. 5A), consistent with its role in promoting TNBC cell proliferation (Fig. 2B and C). It has also been reported that elevated levels of TMOD1 correlate with increased breast tumor growth both in a mouse xenograft model and in a three-dimensional type I collagen culture system [8]. However, within Dox treatment, the OE cells showed significantly decrease in the expression of CDK4 and Ki67 comparing to NC cells (Fig. 5B). Ki-67 is a marker of cell proliferation, and a decrease in its expression typically indicates a reduction in the proliferative capacity of tumor cells. In TNBC patients who have undergone neoadjuvant chemotherapy, a significant reduction in Ki-67 expression is usually associated with a better treatment response [20]. Finn et al. also found that CDK4 inhibitors can inhibit the proliferation of multiple TNBC cell lines [21]. The decrease of these marker genes suggested that TNBC cells with high expression of TMOD1 may be more sensitive to Dox. Our RT-qPCR results also showed TMOD1-mediated upregulation of migration markers (CDH1, CTSL, SNAIL) (Fig. 5A) implies its role in facilitating the binding of tropomyosin to actin filaments, thereby modulating cell shape and movement [2]. Contrarily, under the treatment of Dox, the migration marker genes (CDH1, CTSL, SNAIL) significantly decrease in TMOD1 OE cells compared with NC cells (Fig. 5B). According to reported studies, inhibition of circRNA_0044556 enhances the sensitivity of TNBC cells to Dox through decreased cell viability, increased apoptosis, and decreased migration [22].

Actually, there are many other signaling pathways associated with chemotherapy drug resistance. Among them, NF-κB signaling pathway is particularly important, as it is a crucial regulator of TNBC and is associated with poor prognosis [23]. In TNBC, TMOD1 is overexpressed and driven by the NF-κB signaling pathway, enhancing tumor growth [8]. In macrophages, overexpression of TMOD1 significantly enhances the activation of NF-κB, as evidenced by increased nuclear translocation of p65 and elevated expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Conversely, TMOD1 deficiency suppresses NF-κB activation and the production of these inflammatory mediators [24]. Furthermore, highly activated AKT is associated with chemoresistance in breast cancer, while mTOR inhibition sensitizes resistant cells to cytotoxic agents [25]. There are currently no studies directly regarding the direct relationship between TMOD1 and the AKT signaling pathway. Additionally, TMOD1 overexpression may inhibit RTK, and EGFR is a transmembrane glycoprotein that belongs to the ErbB/HER family of RTKs [26] which is associated with worse outcomes in TNBC [27, 28]. It was also reported that TMOD1 and its related genes may activate the DNA damage response, which is important for cancer cell death and patient survival [15]. And we speculated that above signal pathways may be important reasons why TMOD1 regulates TNBC chemosensitivity.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrate that TMOD1 high expression is associated with both longer OS and RFS time in TNBC patients. However,

TMOD1

Expression promotes TNBC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, but it enhances the sensitivity of TNBC to Dox therapy both in vivo and in vitro, which contribute to improving TNBC patients’ prognosis.
