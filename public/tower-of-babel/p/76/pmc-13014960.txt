Design of species-specific molecular markers for PCR-based differentiation of bird louse flies Ornithomya avicularia, Ornithomya biloba and Ornithomya chloropus (Diptera: Hippoboscidae)
Authors: Ľudmila Juhásová, Ivica Králová-Hromadová, Jozef Oboňa, Alžbeta Radačovská, Alexandru-Mihai Pintilioaie, Martin Hromada, Eva Čisovská Bazsalovicsová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLouse flies of the genus Ornithomya (Hippoboscidae) are widespread avian ectoparasites of ecological and epidemiological importance, acting as blood-feeding parasites and potential vectors of avian and zoonotic pathogens. Traditional taxonomic identification can be challenging due to overlapping phenotypic traits, broad host associations, and the frequent occurrence of damaged specimens. This study aimed to develop a reliable, species-specific PCR-based molecular tool for differentiating three European louse fly species: Ornithomya avicularia,Ornithomya biloba, and Ornithomya chloropus. Mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 gene (cox1) sequences from different geographical populations of the studied species, both newly sequenced and retrieved from GenBank, were analysed to assess intraspecific variation and interspecific divergence. The cox1 regions characterised by high interspecific polymorphism and no intraspecific variability were selected for the design and validation of nine species-specific primer sets. Four of these produced PCR products exclusively for their target species, demonstrating their effectiveness for species identification. This new single-step PCR-based approach for accurate and cost-effective molecular identification can efficiently supplement taxonomy methods based solely on morphology. The findings highlight the necessity of combined approaches integrating morphological and DNA-based methods to resolve the taxonomy of louse flies and underscore the importance of developing novel molecular tools to improve species delimitation.Keywords: Louse flies, Cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1, cox1, Primer design, Identification

Introduction

Louse flies of the subfamily Ornithomyinae Bigot, 1853 (Diptera: Hippoboscidae) are obligate haematophagous ectoparasites primarily of birds (Nartshuk et al., 2022; Janiszewska et al., 2023). These hippoboscids are generally characterised by low host specificity, with a single species parasitising multiple bird species across different avian families and orders, although there are some exceptions, such as Ornithomya biloba Dufour, 1827, which is largely restricted to swallows (Hirundininae) (Maa, 1969a; Graciolli and Laps, 2024).

The genus Ornithomya Latreille, 1802 currently includes one fossil and 34 extant species (Dick, 2018; Nartshuk et al., 2022; Matyukhin et al., 2023; Yatsuk et al., 2024, 2025). Ornithomya louse flies can negatively affect the health of birds, although their potential as vectors of pathogens has been largely unexplored. Ornithomya species have been implicated in the transmission of several pathogens of medical and veterinary relevance (Keve et al., 2025), such as parasites of the genus Babesia (Piroplasmida), specifically Babesia canis, Babesia venatorum, and Babesia microti (Čisovská Bazsalovicsová et al., 2023); parasitic protozoans Trypanosoma spp., namely Trypanosoma corvi, Trypanosoma culicavium, and Trypanosoma bennetti groups (Keve et al., 2025); bacteria Anaplasma phagocytophilum (Rickettsiales) and Haematospirillum jordaniae (Rhodospirillales) (Keve et al., 2025), and Avipoxvirus (Wawman et al., 2025a).

In Europe, six Ornithomya species have been confirmed to date: Ornithomya rupes Hutson, 1981; Ornithomya comosa (Austen, 1930); Ornithomya avicularia (Linnaeus, 1758); O. biloba; Ornithomya chloropus Bergroth, 1901; and Ornithomya fringillina Curtis, 1836. Ornithomya rupes and O. comosa have rather limited distributions. To date, O. rupes has been found only in Europe, specifically in France, Greece, Spain, and Switzerland (Le Guillou and Chapelin-Viscardi, 2022), while O. comosa has been recorded in northwestern Russia (Kaliningrad Province), Belgium, France, and Slovakia (Le Guillou and Chapelin-Viscardi, 2022; Oboňa et al., 2022).

In contrast, the other four species of Ornithomya are more widely distributed in Europe and across the Palaearctic region. The most common louse fly in Europe is O. avicularia, which has also been found in Asia and North Africa, and was even introduced to Australia and New Zealand through the translocation of its avian hosts (Maa, 1962, 1969a; Krištofík, 1998; Oboňa et al., 2019a). This louse fly parasitises a wide range of avian hosts, having been reported from 65 genera, 23 families, and 10 orders (Oboňa et al., 2019b, 2022). Ornithomya biloba occurs mainly in the Western Palaearctic region and primarily parasitises barn swallows (Hirundo rustica Linnaeus, 1758), only occasionally infesting other hirundines and raptors that prey on them (Maa, 1969b; Krištofík, 1998; Oboňa et al., 2019a). Ornithomya chloropus exhibits the northernmost range among bird louse flies (Theodor and Oldroyd, 1964) and shows considerable variation in host preferences, as it can parasitise 72 genera, 25 families, and 10 avian orders (Maa, 1969b; Lehikoinen et al., 2021).

Another widely distributed louse fly across the Palaearctic realm is O. fringillina, which also extends into North Africa and East Asia (Maa, 1964; Iwasa and Choi, 2013). It mainly parasitises Passeriformes, although occurrences in other avian orders have also been documented (Krištofík, 1998; Oboňa et al., 2019b). Since O. fringillina displays a rather high level of intraspecific morphological variation, it has been the subject of speculation regarding the presence of more than one species. The latest data published by Wawman et al. (2025b), based on robust analyses of morphological, ecological, and biogeographical data, as well as sequences of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 gene (cox1), revealed that the three patterns of microtrichia identified in different O. fringillina individuals corresponded to various morphotypes of this species and do not represent morphological features specific to different Ornithomya species.

The taxonomy of the genus Ornithomya has been debated among entomologists during the 20th century and remains somewhat uncertain till now (Wawman et al., 2025b). The principal methodological approach for identifying bird louse flies is traditional alpha taxonomy based on species-specific markers. The distribution patterns of small wing hairs (microtrichia or setulae) and the shape of the female tergites (Supplementary Fig. S1), which are often poorly preserved in specimens, are the two main morphological features used to identify Ornithomya species (e.g. Maa, 1964, 1969b; Hutson, 1984; Levesque-Beaudin and Sinclair, 2021).

However, morphological identification can be problematic for several reasons: (i) the overlap of certain diagnostic traits among different taxa (e.g. presence or absence of microtrichia in wings, coloration of the scutellum and ventral side of the head, and the presence and arrangement of bristles and hairs on the thorax and scutellum); (ii) missing or poorly developed morphological features (e.g. the number of scutellar bristles); (iii) altered morphological markers resulting from partially damaged biological material obtained during sample collection (e.g. broken setae, missing wings, or other missing body parts) or fixation procedures (e.g. faded dark marks on the ventral surface of the head and thorax in specimens preserved in ethanol) (Hill et al., 1964; Wawman et al., 2025b); and (iv) the sharing of common bird hosts among several Ornithomya species. In addition, insufficient taxonomic resolution of bird louse flies may lead to serious gaps in our understanding of the complex interactions between Ornithomya spp. and their hosts, possibly also overlooking some of the pathogens transmitted by these blood-sucking ectoparasites (Janiszewska et al., 2023).

Complications in identifying bird louse flies can be reliably addressed by the parallel application of molecular taxonomy methods. Effective use of DNA-based markers requires detailed analysis within the studied taxa to determine intraspecific variability and interspecific differences in selected DNA regions within and between species, respectively. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) genes, especially the cox1 gene, are among the most common barcodes used for identifying bird louse flies. In the past decade, alpha taxonomy combined with cox1 sequences has been successfully applied for accurate taxonomic identification of Ornithomya species (e.g. Šochová et al., 2017; Lehikoinen et al., 2021; Levesque-Beaudin and Sinclair, 2021; Čisovská Bazsalovicsová et al., 2023; Janiszewska et al., 2023; Martin Říhová et al., 2023; Yatsuk et al., 2024). However, the primers used for PCR amplification of different species of Ornithomya were universal and resulted in non-specific amplification of the cox1 product in all studied species, regardless of the target DNA. Consequently, exact taxonomic identification of each specimen required sequencing each PCR product, thus increasing the financial costs of the analyses.

This study aimed to design species-specific mitochondrial cox1 primers to enable reliable single-step PCR-based differentiation of three selected Ornithomya species, O. avicularia, O. biloba, and O. chloropus. To achieve this, cox1 sequences from different geographical populations of the studied species, retrieved from GenBank and newly generated, were analysed in detail to determine intraspecific variability and interspecific differences within and between the species, respectively. Regions of the cox1 gene lacking intraspecific polymorphism but characterised by interspecific sequence heterogeneity were selected as target regions for designing species-specific primer sets, which specificity was tested on genomic DNA of O. avicularia, O. biloba and O. chloropus. Primers that produced PCR products exclusively in the species for which they were designed and validated as species-specific were recommended for further use in the molecular taxonomy of the studied Ornithomya species.

Materials and methods

Material

The specificity of primers, designed as potential candidates for species-specific PCR-based identification of the studied Ornithomya spp., was tested on DNA from three specimens each of O. avicularia (OA1–3), O. biloba (OB1–3), and O. chloropus (OC1–3). Three O. avicularia and two O. biloba specimens (OB1, OB3) were collected from the barn swallow H. rustica, and one O. biloba (OB2) sample was obtained from the sand martin Riparia riparia (Linnaeus, 1758) (both Hirundinidae). The birds were examined for ectoparasites as part of regular bird monitoring at the Uzovský Šalgov (Slovakia) ringing station. Ornithomya chloropus specimens were collected from the tree pipit Anthus trivialis (Linnaeus, 1758) (Motacillidae) at Drienovec (Slovakia; OC1) and from the Dunele Marine de la Agigea Nature Reserve, Romania (OC2, OC3) during regular seasonal bird ringing procedures. The louse flies were initially identified according to species-specific morphological criteria under a stereomicroscope using the keys published by Oboňa et al. (2022) and Maa (1969a) and were preserved in 96% ethanol for further molecular analyses.

PCR and sequencing

The taxonomic identification of all nine bird louse flies was confirmed by sequencing. Flies were rinsed three times in sterile distilled water for 1–2 min each to remove residual ethanol, placed on filter paper and air-dried for 10–15 min. The whole body of each louse fly was then individually treated in a mechanical homogeniser. Genomic DNA was extracted using the alkaline hydrolysis method (Rijpkema et al., 1996) and stored at −20 °C. The partial cox1 mtDNA gene was amplified using the forward primer mtd4 (5′-TAC AAT TTA TCG CCT AAA CTT CAG CC-3′) and the reverse primer mtd12 (5′-TCC AAT GCA CTA ATC TGC CAT ATT A-3′) (Petersen et al., 2007a) under the conditions described in Savage et al. (2004). As PCR products of the same size (∼1500 bp) were obtained for all individuals of the three Ornithomya species, exact taxonomic identification had to be performed by sequence analysis.

The PCR products were purified using ExoProStar™ 1-step (Illustra™, GE Healthcare, Little Chalfont, UK) and sequenced from both sides using the corresponding pairs of PCR primers and two internal primers, mtd8 (5′-CCA CAT TTA TTT TGA TTT TTT GG-3′) and mtd9 (5′-CCC GGT AAA ATT AAA ATA TAA ACT TC-3′) (Petersen et al., 2007a). Sequencing was performed at a 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Contiguous sequences were assembled and analysed for errors using Geneious software v.10.0.5 (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand).

The newly obtained cox1 sequences for O. avicularia (1457 bp), O. biloba (1491 bp), and O. chloropus (1480 bp) were compared with reference cox1 sequences deposited in GenBank. The sequences of the three O. avicularia specimens were 99.8–99.9% similar to each other and showed 99.9% similarity to the reference sequence of O. avicularia isolated from the common blackbird Turdus merula in the UK (GenBank: PQ066336; Wawman et al., 2025b). The sequences of O. biloba specimens (99.9–100%) showed high similarity (99.9–100%) to the reference sequence of O. biloba collected from the barn swallow in the Czech Republic (GenBank: MF496010; Šochová et al., 2017). The three sequences of O. chloropus exhibited 99.9% pairwise similarity and corresponded (99.9%) to the reference sequence of O. chloropus isolated from the house sparrow Passer domesticus in the UK (GenBank: PQ066335; Wawman et al., 2025b). Except for two sequences (O. avicularia: OP035933; O. biloba: OP036771) previously published by Čisovská Bazsalovicsová et al. (2023), the seven newly obtained sequences were deposited in the GenBank, EMBL, and DDBJ databases under the following accession numbers: PX890093-PX890094 (O. avicularia); PX893059-PX893060 (O. biloba); and PX890103-PX890105 (O. chloropus). The sequencing results corresponded with the data based on morphological criteria and confirmed the identification of all nine individuals of the three studied Ornithomya spp.

Data on cox1 sequences for O. avicularia, O. biloba and O. chloropus from GenBank

Data on complete and partial sequences of the mitochondrial cox1 gene for O. avicularia, O. biloba and O. chloropus were retrieved from the GenBank database and are summarised in Table 1, Table 2. The largest number of cox1 data is available for O. avicularia, in which the length of the sequenced gene varied substantially, from a 202 bp partial fragment to the complete cox1 gene (1531 bp), most often identified within the complete mitochondrial genome (see Table 1 and references therein). Most cox1 data for O. avicularia were obtained from different countries across Europe, mainly Poland and the Czech Republic, with several accessions also available for flies from South Africa and Canada. Considerably less data are available for O. biloba and O. chloropus, for which the length of the sequenced cox1 fragment ranged from 207 bp (O. biloba) and 421 bp (O. chloropus) up to the complete cox1 gene (see Table 2 and references therein). Data on O. biloba originated mainly from Europe, mostly from the Czech Republic and Slovakia, with one submission related to South Africa, while data on O. chloropus were available only for louse flies collected in different European countries (Table 2). The cox1 sequences were assembled and analysed in detail using Geneious software v.10.0.5 and Clustal Omega Multiple Sequence Alignment (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo).

Design and validation of species-specific primers

The cox1 sequences of the three species differed substantially in both length and position within the complete gene. Therefore, the initial analysis focused on determining the exact positions of the sequenced cox1 regions within the complete gene and identifying the common cox1 fragment overlapping in as many individuals as possible. The selected common cox1 region was then subjected to sequence analyses and comparisons aimed to: (i) characterise intraspecific variation within the selected cox1 fragment of O. avicularia, O. biloba, and O. chloropus; (ii) assess interspecific differences among all three Ornithomya spp.; and (iii) identify DNA regions with substantial interspecific differences and no intraspecific polymorphism suitable for designing of primers.

Primers designed as potential candidates for species-specific PCR identification of O. avicularia, O. biloba and O. chloropus were tested on DNA isolated from three specimens of each species (see Table 3 for details). PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 20 μl containing 10–20 ng of genomic DNA, 10 pmol of each of the two newly designed primers, and 1× PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Initially, a PCR gradient was performed to determine the optimal annealing temperature for the designed primers, allowing maximal specificity and yield of the target sequence while minimising non-specific amplification. The PCR amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 94 °C for 40 s, annealing at the optimised temperature (see Table 4 for details) for each newly designed primer set for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min. A final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were visualised on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Primer sets that produced PCR products in all three individuals of the corresponding species for which they were designed, and at the same time failed to produce PCR products in any individual of the two remaining species, were validated as species-specific and recommended for further identification of the studied species. Results

Analysis of cox1 sequences of O. avicularia, O. biloba and O. chloropus retrieved from GenBank

The analyses of partial and complete cox1 sequences of the Ornithomya species studied and retrieved from GenBank were based on the data summarised in Table 1 (O. avicularia) and Table 2 (O. biloba and O. chloropus). Their graphical schemes are displayed in Fig. 1 (O. avicularia) and Fig. 2 (O. biloba and O. chloropus). The data in Table 1, Table 2 showed that complete cox1 gene sequences (1531–1534 bp) were available for all three species. In contrast, partial cox1 sequences of varying lengths were scattered across different regions of the complete gene (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

A substantially higher number of the longer cox1 regions (639–710 bp) was analysed at the 5′-end of the gene, while fewer and mostly shorter cox1 regions were sequenced in the central part and at the 3′-end of the gene (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Based on this, the 551 bp cox1 region located at the 5′-end of the gene was selected as the target region for further analyses. This region included most of the partial cox1 sequences from GenBank, as well as the corresponding part of the complete cox1 sequences, all trimmed to a unified length of 551 bp (see Fig. 1, Fig. 2 and sequences labelled with codes A, C, and E for O. avicularia, O. biloba, and O. chloropus, respectively).

Sequences that only partially overlapped or did not overlap with the selected 551 bp region, or sequences containing ambiguous nucleotides, were excluded from analyses (see Fig. 1, Fig. 2 and sequences labelled with codes B, D, and F for O. avicularia, O. biloba, and O. chloropus, respectively). Of note, the sequences designated as O. biloba (GenBank: NC061211 and MZ379837; Li et al., 2022) were probably incorrectly annotated, given their high sequence similarity (96.8–97.0%) to O. avicularia (GenBank: OR348034 and PQ066336, respectively), and thus were excluded from analyses.

The sequence similarity/identity of the selected 551 bp cox1 region was significantly higher within each of the studied species (97.5–100% in O. avicularia; 99.6–100% in O. biloba and O. chloropus) than the similarity detected between species, namely between O. avicularia and O. biloba (91.3–92.0%), O. avicularia and O. chloropus (92.4–93.5%), and O. biloba and O. chloropus (90.2–92.4%).

Design and validation of novel cox1 primers

A total of 79 sequences were included in the alignment of the selected 551 bp cox1 fragment from O. avicularia (n = 56), O. biloba (n = 11) and O. chloropus (n = 12), revealing 82 mutation sites. The alignment enabled the identification of short cox1 regions (up to 24 bp) that exhibited sequence uniformity within all individuals of the corresponding species (without intraspecific variation), while displaying substantial interspecific differences (with a greater number of species-specific mutations). These regions were selected for the design of primers: two forward and one reverse O. avicularia-specific primers (AVI_cox1_F1, AVI_cox1_F2, and AVI_cox1_R), one forward and two reverse O. biloba-specific primers (BILO_cox1_F, BILO_cox1_R1, and BILO_cox1_R2) and one forward and one reverse O. chloropus-specific primers (CHLORO_cox1_F and CHLORO_cox1_R). In addition, one forward primer (Ornitho_UNI_cox1_F) was designed as the universal primer for all three studied Ornithomya spp., as its sequence was identical in all 79 individuals analysed. The sequences of the primers are summarised in Table 3, and their positions are graphically displayed in Fig. 3.

Discussion

The present study aimed to develop a species-specific PCR-based method for reliable differentiation among O. avicularia, O. biloba and O. chloropus, parasitic louse flies that infest birds. Four of nine originally designed and validated sets of cox1 primers were suitable for species-specific PCR, providing a new molecular approach for accurate and cost-effective molecular identification, which can efficiently supplement traditional morphology-based methods. The broader applicability of the designed primers is currently being tested on a wider range of Ornithomya specimens from different localities (data not shown). Preliminary results showed that identification of Ornithomya species using designed species-specific primers were consistent compared with morphological identification of the studied louse flies, confirming the specificity of these discriminatory markers.

Besides overlapping and extensive variability of some morphological features (Maa, 1967, 1969b; Doszhanov 1980, 2003; Oboňa et al., 2022; Yatsuk et al., 2023), another complication that can lead to misidentification of O. avicularia, O. biloba and O. chloropus is their sharing of the same avian hosts. In particular, all three species have been found in birds of the Hirundinidae, with the barn swallow being one of the most common mutual hosts. Besides, O. avicularia and O. chloropus are polyphagous and prefer a wide range of bird hosts from the orders Galliformes, Ciconiiformes, Cuculiformes, Piciformes, and Passeriformes (Nartshuk et al., 2024).

Seasonal migration of birds across vast geographical distances between continents plays an important role in the dispersal of their invertebrate ectoparasites (Viana et al., 2016), thus substantially increasing the probability of introducing non-native or previously unrecorded louse fly species into new areas. The optimal strategy for documenting newly introduced or recorded species in a particular location is a combination of morphological and molecular data. This integrative approach has recently been essential for the correct identification of Ornithoctona laticornis (Macquart, 1835) (Ornithomyinae), a sub-Saharan louse fly species recorded for the first time in Europe, particularly in Hungary (Keve et al., 2024) and later also in Romania (Oboňa et al., 2025). While certain morphological features of O. laticornis overlapped with those of Ornithomya candida Maa, 1967, other body structures were substantially different from all other members of the genus Ornithomya. Mitochondrial cox1 sequences helped to identify O. laticornis and confirmed its first record in Romania (Oboňa et al., 2025).

As the most frequent species of Ornithomya in Europe are O. avicularia, O. biloba, O. chloropus, and O. fringillina, we attempted to design species-specific primers also for O. fringillina based on the available GenBank data. However, designing a forward primer specific to O. fringillina was not feasible due to the lack of conserved regions unique to this species. Only one O. fringillina-specific reverse primer (FRING_cox1_R) could be designed, which was used with the universal forward primer (Ornitho_UNI_cox1_F) in the PCR reaction. Despite optimising PCR amplification conditions, this combination consistently produced non-specific PCR products in O. biloba and O. chloropus DNA. This may be related to greater morphological plasticity and sequence variation within the morphotypes of O. fringillina, which have even been speculated to represent distinct species (Wawman et al., 2025b).

Even though O. fringillina-specific primers could not be designed, the broader specificity of O. avicularia-, O. biloba-, and O. chloropus-specific primers was tested on genomic DNA from three O. fringillina specimens from Slovakia. All four sets of species-specific primers failed to anneal or amplify a PCR product in O. fringillina (data not shown). This confirmed their high specificity, as no unspecific cross-reactivity was observed in any of the tested non-target species. Further comprehensive studies, likely focusing on the complete cox1 sequence, are necessary to develop molecular markers capable of distinguishing O. fringillina from its congeners.

The specificity of the newly developed primers was also tested on genomic DNA of several other representatives of the Hippoboscidae, i.e. O. laticornis, Hippobosca equina, and Lipoptena fortisetosa. Neither of O. avicularia-, O. biloba-, and O. chloropus-specific primers produced a PCR product on any of the tested hippoboscid species (data not shown).

The sequences of the mitochondrial cox1 gene have been the most frequent and effective molecular markers used for the taxonomic identification of louse flies (e.g. Šochová et al., 2017; Lehikoinen et al., 2021; Levesque-Beaudin and Sinclair, 2021; Čisovská Bazsalovicsová et al., 2023; Janiszewska et al., 2023; Martin Říhová et al., 2023; Yatsuk et al., 2024). The broad utility of the cox1 gene is underlined by the fact that this barcoding gene has also been applied in studies of several other Ornithomya spp., including Ornithomya anchineuria Speiser, 1905, Ornithomya bequaerti Maa, 1969, O. comosa, Ornithomya palmae Wawman and Milá, 2025, O. fringillina, and the newly described Ornithomya panovi Yatsuk, Matyukhin, Shokhrin, Triseleva & Nartshuk, 2025; Meißner et al., 2020; Lehikoinen et al., 2021; Levesque-Beaudin and Sinclair, 2021; Wawman et al., 2025b Wawman and Milá, 2025; Yatsuk et al., 2025). However, sequencing was required in all the above-mentioned studies, and a PCR-based approach, as designed in the present study has not been applied before.

Regarding other mitochondrial genes, only 16S rRNA gene sequences have been sparsely present in GenBank, with five records for O. avicularia, two for O. biloba, and one for O. chloropus. These data have previously been used to reconstruct phylogenetic relationships and identify key evolutionary events within the Hippoboscoidea (Petersen et al., 2007a; Šochová et al., 2017). The 16S rRNA gene sequences could not be included in the present study due to the insufficient number of available records.

Nuclear ribosomal DNA (rDNA) sequences have been widely applied in molecular phylogenetics, enabling the investigation of evolutionary relationships across various taxonomic levels due to their diverse rates of substitution (Hillis and Dixon, 1991). For the louse flies species studied here, nuclear 28S rRNA records were limited to one sequence each for O. avicularia and O. chloropus (Petersen et al., 2007a) and two for O. biloba (Petersen et al., 2007a; Kutty et al., 2008), while 18S rRNA was available only for O. avicularia (Nirmala et al., 2001). The sequences of these genes have contributed to resolving louse fly lineages within the Hippoboscidea, Oestroidea and Muscoidea (Nirmala et al., 2001; Kutty et al., 2008). Although the more conserved structure of rDNA subunits has been valuable in studies of phylogenies and evolutionary relationships at higher taxonomic levels, it makes them unsuitable for reliable discrimination among closely related taxa, such as species and populations (Hillis and Dixon, 1991).

Conclusions

Development of a PCR-based cox1 assay for three Ornithomya spp. is an important first step towards reliable molecular identification without sequencing. The present findings highlight the necessity of using genetic approaches to resolve the taxonomy of louse flies and underscore the importance of developing novel molecular tools to improve species delimitation and fill gaps in knowledge of their biodiversity. Future research should prioritise extending the study to additional Ornithomya spp., namely O. fringillina, O. comosa and O. rupes, incorporating longer cox1 sequences from a wider range of populations. For O. comosa and O. rupes, there are limited data on their occurrence in Europe (Le Guillou and Chapelin-Viscardi, 2022; Oboňa et al., 2022). These species have not been studied using molecular methods, since no data are available for O. rupes and only one sequence is present in GenBank for O. comosa (GenBank: OR064833; 680 bp cox1 sequence). Sequences obtained from a wider range of populations of understudied species would provide essential input data for robust molecular comparative analysis in the future. Building a comprehensive multi-species diagnostic panel would enable broader ecological and epidemiological studies and reduce misidentification in areas with overlapping species distributions.
