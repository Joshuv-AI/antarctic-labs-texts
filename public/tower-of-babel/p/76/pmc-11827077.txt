A Handheld, Portable Image-Based System May Outperform Computer Navigation or Robotic Platforms in Providing Accurate Acetabular Component Positioning
Authors: Eric M. Slotkin, Francesca Coxe, Tristan Jones, Thomas Morton, Stefan Kreutzer, Alejandro Della Valle
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalposition of the acetabular component during total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a leading cause of complications and need for revision. Robotic-assisted THA purports to improve accuracy of component positioning with many reports demonstrating over 92% of components within 10° of inclination and anteversion compared to intraoperative system output. This study aimed to evaluate the intraoperative accuracy output of acetabular cup position values using a handheld miniaturized portable navigation system (Naviswiss, AG) compared to postoperative computed tomography (CT) scans.MethodsA total of 108 direct anterior approach THA surgeries using the intraoperative navigation device were performed over a 6-month period. Intraoperative device output for measured acetabular component inclination and anteversion were recorded and compared with values derived from postoperative CT scans.ResultsPostoperative CT analysis of acetabular component positioning demonstrated 97.22% and 94.44% were within 5° of intraoperative inclination and anteversion, respectively, compared to intraoperative values from the navigation unit. No CT demonstrated an absolute inclination or anteversion measurement difference more than 8° from the intraoperative navigation unit value. Overall, 92.59% of components were within 5° and 100% were within 8° for both inclination and anteversion compared to postoperative CT measurements.ConclusionsThis handheld portable navigation system yielded highly accurate intraoperative component positioning values confirmed by postoperative CT scans during direct anterior THA, possibly superior to reported robotic-assisted THA values. These smaller, portable, and more accessible intraoperative units may provide surgeons improved accuracy and availability in a number of surgical settings for use in THA.

Introduction

Direct anterior total hip arthroplasty (DA THA) has become increasingly popular due to its potential benefits of faster recovery, less pain, and improved mobility [1]. Accurate placement of the acetabular component is crucial for successful outcomes and longevity of the prosthesis [2]. Proper acetabular component alignment is a critical surgical step to improve the function and durability during THA [3]. Malposition of the acetabular component in THA is the most common cause of dislocation and can lead to impingement, increased and premature wear, and early revision [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Dislocation and component loosening remain leading factors for revision surgery following THA [7].

Many studies have been performed to evaluate the ideal position of the acetabular component in THA [5,8,9]. Historically, Lewinnek et al. described a universal optimal “safe-zone” and target ranges for acetabular component anteversion and inclination (5°-25° and 30°-50°, respectively) [8]. More recent studies have made the case for patient-specific approaches to incorporate spinopelvic biomechanics for acetabular component positioning [10]. Intraoperative assessment of true anteversion and abduction measurements is therefore essential. However, placement of the acetabular component within the safe zone with an inclination of 40° ± 10° and anteversion of 15° ± 10° as defined by Lewinnek et al. is not guaranteed when using a freehand surgical technique, even in the hands of experienced surgeons [11].

The use of enabling technology during THA surgery has revolutionized the procedure, improving surgical accuracy, reducing complications, and enhancing patient outcomes [12,13]. Simple fluoroscopy has been shown to improve acetabular component orientation; however, intraoperative analysis and determination have been demonstrated to be inaccurate and based on surgeon interpretation [11,14]. Several computer-assisted or robotic systems have been shown to improve component positioning as compared to traditional freehand techniques, but the increased financial costs as well as documented increased time requirements to implement such systems have prevented their widespread use [15,16]. Computer-assisted navigation has several advantages over traditional THA surgery including improved implant positioning precision [15]. Robotic-assisted THA surgery has been shown to allow for greater accuracy and precision in implant placement [17]. Despite advancements in various technologies, the method each system uses to register various pelvic referencing points creates variability in achieving the functional positioning goal [18].

Handheld navigation devices have been developed as a cost-effective, user-friendly, minimally invasive alternative tool; however, clinical evidence of their performance is yet not available [19]. These miniaturized, portable, inexpensive, and accessible devices may be the next evolution in intraoperative technology; however, their use needs to be validated. The primary purpose of this study aimed to evaluate the intraoperative accuracy output of acetabular cup position values using a handheld miniaturized portable navigation system (Naviswiss, AG) compared to postoperative computed tomography (CT) scans for a patient-specific image-based functional acetabular component target (Fig. 1).Figure 1NaviSwiss device unit. Surgical procedure

All surgeries were performed through the DA approach for THA using a Hana operating table (Mizuho OSI, Union City, CA) patient positioning device by a single surgeon (E. S.). All patients received standard instrumentation and implants from either of 2 implant companies (Corin Group, LTD: Metafix femoral stem, Trinity acetabular component, highly cross-linked polyethylene, and ceramic femoral head [N = 61] or Ortho Development LLC: Entrada femoral stem, Legend acetabular component, highly cross-linked polyethylene, and ceramic femoral head [N = 47]) based on the surgeon preoperative templating. Prior to the start of the procedure, registration array placement followed the recommended Naviswiss surgical technique instructions with the ipsilateral pelvic array positioned and attached to the pelvic rim superior to the anterior superior iiac spine (ASIS) with 2 fixation pins (diameter, 3.0 mm). Importantly, these fixation pins come in multiple lengths, enabling the surgeon to select the appropriate size to achieve unobstructed access to the arrays, even in patients with an elevated BMI. Using the Naviswiss device, the patient pelvic position was registered according to the directed Naviswiss system protocol using a series of intraoperative anatomic landmarks (ipsilateral ASIS, contralateral ASIS, pubic symphysis, and a registration of 36 points around and within the acetabulum prior to reaming). Implantation of the acetabular component was performed according to the surgeon’s standard protocol. Additional fixation of the acetabular component with cancellous dome screws was used in 44/108 (40.1%) cases and was based on surgeon determination at the time of final implant placement. In each surgery, the intraoperative assessment of acetabular component inclination and anteversion relative to the patient’s anatomy was performed using the Naviswiss device and the values for component inclination and anteversion were recorded. The target inclination and anteversion values were determined using a combination of preoperative 3-dimensional imaging and functional lateral spinopelvic radiographs (standing and seated) to identify the individual patient-specific orientation basis using dedicated planning software (Optimized Positioning System, OPS Insight, Corin Group, Ltd.). This software allows for various component orientations to be simulated (based on patient-specific kinematics) until an appropriate impingement-free range of motion (ROM) can be customized for each patient. Furthermore, the preoperative CT scan allows the surgeon to evaluate anatomical considerations for cup position, by referencing key landmarks including the anterior and posterior walls and the medial teardrop. Once confirmed by the surgeon, the preoperative plan was paired with the Naviswiss device, enabling the patient anatomy to be accurately registered intraoperatively using an image-based workflow, incorporating known pelvic and periacetabular anatomical landmarks. The acetabular component was then aligned during implantation to match these targeted parameters for anteversion and inclination. While the femoral component orientation can also be assessed within the software, this element was outside the scope of this study and therefore not included in the discussion.

Evaluation

Postoperatively, all patients received standard CT imaging of the pelvis within 24 hours of surgery. All postoperative CT scans were performed on a GE Revolution 2021 64-slice CT Scanner, acquiring 1 mm contiguous axial images. The images were then manipulated using a multiplanar reformatting function in the pictures archiving communication system (Merge RadSuite, Chicago, IL) into coronal and sagittal orthogonal images to accurately measure acetabular component inclination and anteversion (Fig. 2). A single author (T. M.) interpreted all postoperative images to ensure reproducibility. Previous studies have investigated the intraobserver and interobserver reliabilities in these measurements [[19], [20], [21]]. Results

One-hundred and eight DA total hip replacement procedures using intraoperative Naviswiss handheld navigation met all inclusion criteria and were included in analysis. The average inclination and anteversion angles for the 108 hips included in the study were 39.8 ± 2.2 and 21.7 ± 2.7, respectively. Inclination angles ranged from 36° to 46°, while anteversion angles ranged from 17° to 27°. 91.7% (99/108) of acetabular cups were placed within the Lewinnek safe zone and 90.7% (98/108) of cups were placed within the Callanan modified safe zone for both inclination and anteversion [8,22]. Discussion

Proper orientation of the acetabulum during THA is critical in improving patient function and surgical outcomes [[1], [2], [3], [4],13,23,24]. Malposition has been identified as a key factor in numerous reports focusing on complications such as impingement, dislocation, wear, and revision surgery [1,[3], [4], [5], [6], [7],13,23,24]. Many factors contribute to the proper placement of the acetabular component and can be influenced by patient positioning, distorted anatomy, spinopelvic mobility, BMI, and surgeon factors [13,23]. Likewise, many studies have demonstrated differences of opinion regarding the true “safe-zone” as it applies to patients universally [8,10]; however, cup placement outside the safe zone has been associated with higher rates of dislocation and revision [25,26]. Additionally, a mispositioned cup can lead to edge-loading and impingement, as well as accelerated poly wear [13,24,27,28]. Thus, optimal positioning of the acetabular component is crucial for THA stability and longevity.

Intraoperative enabling technologies have been shown to improve component positioning accuracy, precision, and reproducibility regardless of surgical approach or experience [9,10,12,15,[17], [18], [19]]. A recent meta-analysis found that navigated THA has statistically greater accuracy and precision in cup placement, both for anteversion and inclination, when compared to freehand technique [28]. Moreover, Domb and Redmond et al. found that robotic-guided and navigation-guided techniques were more consistent than other techniques in placing the acetabular cup into Lewinnek’s safe zone for both anterior and posterior approaches [29]. The placement rate of 92% in this investigation within the safe zone for both inclination and anteversion is comparable or better with regard to recently published results by others using various techniques (Table 5). Compared to other studies investigating imageless portable navigation, such as HipAlign (OrthoAlign Inc., Aliso Viejo, CA, USA), our results show similar or even improved accuracy of cup placement in the supine position during DA THA [35]. Compared to traditional acetabular alignment guides, HipAlign had significantly more acetabular components (72.2%) placed within a 5° margin of error [21]. In the study at hand, 97% of cups were placed within a 5° margin of error using Naviswiss. Our study also corroborates the results of 2 recent studies in the literature on the use of Naviswiss in DA THA [19,20]. Specifically, our results confirm that cup position is accurately registered by Naviswiss intraoperatively as established by postoperative CT scans. The image-based handheld navigation device system in this article offers the capability to reliably reduce outliers in acetabular cup orientation.

Additionally, Vigdorchik et al. demonstrated that targeting a patient-specific functional safe zone using various technologies was highly variable in method and success [18]. Recent literature has questioned the clinical utility of Lewinnek and Callanan’s safe zones as targets for ideal acetabular component orientation [36]. Our study, however, used image-based patient-specific functional targets for acetabular component positioning and demonstrated highly accurate intraoperative delivery of those targets with the used of the handheld navigation unit.

While the data are plentiful in comparing robotic-assisted THA to manual technique, more limited data exist regarding how image-based handheld navigation devices during DA THA compare. Robotic-assisted THA purports to improve accuracy of component positioning with many reports demonstrating over 92% of components within 10° of inclination and anteversion compared to intraoperative system output [2,17]. Specifically, Kanawade and Dorr et al. evaluated the postoperative CT validation of the intraoperative MAKO robot measurements and showed 12% and 18% of patients had outlier inclination and anteversion angles between 5° and 10°, respectively [37]. Our study showed greater accuracy with fewer outliers using Naviswiss, with 100% of components within 8° of inclination and anteversion compared to intraoperative system output. With regards to safe-zone placement, Stewart et al. reported Lewinnek safe-zone placement of 87% and Callanan safe-zone placement of 75% using robotic assistance [33]. The study at hand showed 92% and 91% of components were placed in Lewinnek and Callanan safe zones, respectively. However, when evaluating component position in relation to a functional patient-specific target, our data demonstrated accuracy of 92.6% of acetabular components placed within 5° and 100% within 8°. Finally, the Naviswiss performs similarly to robotic-assisted THA with regards to the variation or discrepancy between postoperative and target anteversion and inclination. Robotic THA studies report a variation of 2.0 to 3.3 and 1.9 to 2.9 for inclination and anteversion, respectively [2,33]. Our study showed a variation of 1.7 ± 1.2 for inclination and 2.1 ± 1.2 for anteversion, suggesting similar to improved accuracy compared to existing robotic THA literature.

Moreover, as the robotic-assisted THA workflow significantly increases costs and space requirements due to the need for access to a robot (such as MAKO [Rio Robot, Ft. Lauderdale, FL] or ROSA Hip System [Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw IN, USA]), and, typically, a surgical sales representative to manage the robot present, it is less accessible and makes up less than 4% of all THAs performed in 2018 [[36], [38], [39], [40]]. In comparison, the Naviswiss image-based workflow is an open platform, allowing more ubiquitous usage that helps avoid single-vendor implant agreements. Furthermore, it is available at a fraction of the capital cost as compared to most robotic systems on the market and requires only the handheld device intraoperatively, which has a much smaller intraoperative footprint and is surgeon-operated. Furthermore, the advantages to Naviswiss over other portable navigation devices include its smaller pin diameter (3 mm) and one-time registration, saving time intraoperatively and shortening the workflow [20].

The study at hand had several limitations. First, the operating surgeon was aware of the use of the data for possible publication; however, as the study only evaluated the device accuracy intraoperative as compared to postoperative CT scan analysis of acetabular component positioning, there was limited surgeon investigational bias. Second, our sample size was small, although larger than other studies investigating Naviswiss. Third, the primary purpose of this study is to validate the performance of the handheld image-based navigation device and therefore does not address if its use affects clinical results. Fourth, as this study only focused on Naviswiss device output using an image-based workflow requiring a preoperative CT scan for analysis, and did not evaluate an imageless workflow capability using solely intraoperative landmarks, additional studies are needed to demonstrate similar accuracy in an imageless workflow. Moreover, clinical results were not included, which could add to the value of the study of Naviswiss in the future. Additionally, this cohort of patients is from a single surgeon, possibly limiting the generalizability of the results. Nevertheless, all procedures were performed during the same time period, by the same surgeon with the same approach, technique, and aftercare, thus limiting confounders.

Conclusions

In conclusion, Naviswiss, a handheld navigation system, performed at least as well as robotic and CT-based navigation systems when determining acetabular component cup placement, specifically inclination and anteversion, in DA THA. Thus, these smaller, portable, and more accessible intraoperative units may provide surgeons improved accuracy and availability in a number of surgical settings for use in THA.
