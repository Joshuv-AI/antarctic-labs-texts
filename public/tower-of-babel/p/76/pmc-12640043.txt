Eloralintide (LY3841136), a novel amylin receptor agonist for the treatment of obesity: From discovery to clinical proof of concept

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesEloralintide (LY3841136), a novel amylin analog, was evaluated in translational studies to characterize its therapeutic potential for treating obesity.MethodsIn vitro assays were performed in cell lines selectively expressing rat or human amylin 1 receptor (AMY1R), amylin 3 receptor (AMY3R), or calcitonin receptor (CTR). In vivo studies were conducted in rats and monkeys. A phase 1, randomized, placebo-controlled, participant/investigator-blinded trial evaluated the safety and tolerability of single-ascending eloralintide doses (0.04–12 mg) in healthy participants (NCT05295940).ResultsIn vitro, eloralintide preferentially activated human AMY1R (12-fold > CTR, 11-fold > AMY3R), while in rats, both AMY1R and AMY3R were activated more potently than CTR. Eloralintide induced significantly less conditioned taste avoidance in lean rats than cagrilintide, a non-selective amylin receptor agonist (p < 0.05). Eloralintide dose dependently reduced food intake and lowered body weight, primarily through fat mass loss, in diet-induced obese rats. Eloralintide demonstrated favorable pharmacokinetics in both rats and monkeys. In the phase 1 trial, 48 healthy participants had a mean body mass index of 27.5 kg/m2. Nine participants in the eloralintide cohorts reported 16 adverse events, with most being mild (n = 15/16). Two participants reported 4 gastrointestinal events, including one moderate vomiting event. The pharmacokinetic profile of eloralintide supports once-weekly dosing. In eloralintide cohorts receiving single doses of 4 or 12 mg, week-4 mean percent change from baseline in body weight was −2.5% (p < 0.01) and −4.4% (p < 0.001), respectively, vs placebo (+0.6%).ConclusionsOnce-weekly dosing with eloralintide, an AMY1R-selective agonist, may offer a promising new therapeutic with favorable gastrointestinal tolerability for the treatment of obesity.

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic and complex metabolic disease with an underlying pathology of excess adiposity [1]. Recent advances in pharmacotherapy have led to the development of new and highly effective therapies for weight management in people living with obesity, including semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, and tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 receptor agonist [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. These therapies are generally regarded as safe and effective, but they do not result in optimal weight loss and are not well tolerated by all patients. New therapies with alternative mechanisms of action could be used as monotherapy or in combination with incretin-based therapies to further improve weight loss, achieve additional therapeutic outcomes, or offer other therapeutic options for these populations.

Amylin and calcitonin receptor agonists are emerging as a novel class of therapeutics for obesity [6,7]. Amylin is a 37-amino acid peptide co-secreted with insulin from pancreatic β-cells in response to nutrient intake [[8], [9], [10]]. Amylin interacts with the amylin receptors, which are heterodimers formed by combining the calcitonin receptor (CTR) with three different receptor activity-modifying proteins (RAMP1, RAMP2, and RAMP3) to form amylin receptor subtypes (AMY1R, AMY2R, and AMY3R) [11]. Amylin regulates gastric motility, post-meal glucagon secretion, and appetite and has been shown to lower body weight by promoting satiation and appetite suppression [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Calcitonin binds the calcitonin receptor with highest affinity when it is not combined with RAMPs. Known primarily for its role in calcium and bone metabolism [16], calcitonin may also have a role in glucose homeostasis [17]. Amylin and calcitonin both bind to amylin receptors and CTR. However, amylin has a higher affinity for amylin receptors than CTR, while calcitonin has a higher affinity for CTR than amylin receptors.

Similar to native amylin and calcitonin, amylin receptor agonists can be selective or non-selective, depending on their affinities for amylin and calcitonin receptors. Several agonists are in clinical development, each with varying degrees of activity at amylin and calcitonin receptors. Pramlintide, a synthetic analog of human amylin approved for use in patients with diabetes, is an agonist that primarily activates amylin receptors over CTR. Early phase clinical trials of pramlintide demonstrated weight loss in people with obesity [18,19]. The efficacy of pramlintide was limited by its short plasma half-life, which required 2 to 3 daily doses. In contrast, cagrilintide has a plasma half-life that supports once-weekly dosing; however, it is a non-selective amylin receptor agonist [20,21]. In a phase 3 trial in people with overweight or obesity, once-weekly cagrilintide (2.4 mg) monotherapy over 68 weeks reduced mean body weight from baseline by 11.5% but also led to nausea in 24% of participants [22]. Although the role of CTR in weight loss in humans remains unclear, calcitonin is used in humans as a treatment for various bone conditions [23,24].

Developing selective amylin receptor agonists may be an effective strategy for enhancing therapeutic benefits while minimizing unwanted side effects. Other synthetic amylin receptor agonists with varying degrees of activity at amylin or calcitonin receptors are in clinical development (petrelintide, AZD6234, GUB014295, amycretin, amylin 355/NN355, amylin 1213/NN1213, and MET-233i) and preclinical development (ACCG-2671, PTT-A, VRB-103) [25]. Eloralintide (LY3841136) is a synthetic analog of human amylin with modifications to its amino acid polypeptide chain that may enable once-weekly subcutaneous administration, reduce immunogenicity risk, improve developability properties, and provide amylin receptor selectivity [26]. Here, we characterized eloralintide's receptor affinity and agonistic activity, pharmacokinetics (PK), weight loss efficacy, gastrointestinal tolerability, and effects on body composition in preclinical models. On the basis on these preclinical findings, a first-in-human phase 1 trial was conducted to evaluate the safety, tolerability, PK, and pharmacodynamics (PD) of a single subcutaneous dose of eloralintide in healthy participants.

Materials and Methods

Preclinical methods

Eloralintide (LY3841136) and other synthetic peptides (i.e., pramlintide, cagrilintide, petrelintide, and NN1213) were synthesized at Eli Lilly and Company using solid-phase peptide synthesis methods. Reference peptides, purchased from Bachem (Bubendorf, Switzerland), included native peptides selective (rat amylin) and non-selective (salmon calcitonin [sCT]) to amylin and calcitonin receptors. Rat amylin and pramlintide were used as surrogates for human amylin, which forms unstable fibrils in vitro [27], and are commonly used in amylin in vitro pharmacology assessments [21,28]. The receptor selectivity of each peptide was characterized by its ability to preferentially bind and activate CTR, amylin 1 receptor (AMY1R), and amylin 3 receptor (AMY3R). In vitro cellular potency (half maximal effective concentration [EC50]) and cellular efficacy (maximum percent efficacy [Emax]) were measured using 3′,5′-cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) accumulation as a secondary messenger of receptor activation. In vitro binding affinity was measured using radioligand binding assays to determine the inhibition constant (Ki), which represents the concentration needed to occupy half the receptor binding sites. For binding and cAMP assays, peptides were solubilized in DMSO. UMUC3 cells stably expressing genes for rat or human CTR, AMY1R, and AMY3R were used for functional assays. Binding and cAMP assays were performed in the absence of albumin to allow direct comparison to native peptides without the confounding influence of albumin binding. In vivo studies were then conducted comparing eloralintide to the non-selective amylin receptor agonist, cagrilintide, which is in the advanced phases of clinical development. The PK of eloralintide and cagrilintide were characterized in male Sprague–Dawley rats and cynomolgus monkeys. Plasma concentrations were measured using a qualified liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method. For PD studies, weight loss, food intake, conditioned taste avoidance, and body composition were assessed in lean Sprague–Dawley rats or diet-induced obese (DIO) Long Evans rats. Conditioned taste avoidance was assessed in lean rats given ad libitum access to a 0.15% saccharin solution for 40 min before being subcutaneously treated once with synthetic peptides (eloralintide or cagrilintide) or vehicle. Lean and fat mass were quantified using quantitative nuclear magnetic resonance (QNMR). Separate studies of eloralintide and cagrilintide at various doses were initially conducted to identify effective doses for subsequent head-to-head comparisons. Cagrilintide was formulated in pH 4 buffer, while eloralintide was formulated in pH 8 buffer. Additional experimental details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Phase 1 study

This randomized, participant/investigator-blinded, placebo-controlled phase 1 trial was conducted from March 2022 to January 2024 to evaluate the safety, tolerability, PK, and PD of a single subcutaneous dose of eloralintide. Healthy adults aged 18–65 years in the US were eligible if they had a body mass index (BMI) of 18.5–32.0 kg/m2 and stable body weight for at least three months prior to screening, defined by less than 5% change in body weight. Exclusion criteria included a history of chronic heart, liver, or kidney disease; type 1 or 2 diabetes; and a fasting plasma glucose concentration of ≥126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥6.5% (≥48 mmol/mol) at screening. Up to 6 single-ascending dose (SAD) cohorts were planned at escalating doses of 0.04 mg, 0.12 mg, 0.4 mg, 1.2 mg, 4 mg, and 12 mg. Cohorts were dosed sequentially, starting with the lowest dose. Dose-escalation decisions were made based on safety and tolerability data and any available PK or PD data from preceding cohorts. A total of 48 participants were enrolled. Eight participants were enrolled in each cohort, with 6 randomly assigned to receive eloralintide and 2 to receive matching volumes of placebo. Participants were randomly assigned a unique number encoding the participant's treatment assignment (eloralintide or placebo). Participants and investigators were blinded to treatment allocation by assigning each participant a study intervention labeled with their unique randomization number. Participants received a single subcutaneous injection of eloralintide or placebo on day 1 (week 1) during an inpatient visit, followed by 4 weekly outpatient visits to day 29 (week 5) for safety assessment and collection of blood samples for PK measurements. A safety follow-up visit occurred on day 60 (week 9). The primary objective was to evaluate the safety and tolerability of eloralintide. Safety endpoints included the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs) and changes from baseline in vital signs. The PK of eloralintide was characterized based on plasma concentrations that were measured using a validated LC-MS/MS method. Preliminary efficacy was assessed by absolute change and percent change in body weight from baseline longitudinally through day 29.

Statistical analysis

Preclinical in vitro data are presented as the geometric mean and the standard error of the mean (SE) using the delta method, and preclinical in vivo data are presented as the mean ± SE. Preclinical in vivo data in separate studies of eloralintide and cagrilintide were compared using the dose of peptide required to produce 50% of the peptide's maximal effect (ED50), calculated from dose–response curves using a four parameter logistic equation. Preclinical in vivo data in a head-to-head experiment of eloralintide and cagrilintide were compared using Dunnett's one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with vehicle as the comparator unless noted.

In the clinical trial, descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic data, adverse events, and safety data. Plasma concentrations of eloralintide were analyzed using non-compartmental analysis methods in Phoenix WinNonlin Version 8.3.5. The degree of dose proportionality was assessed by fitting a power model to log-transformed area under the curve from time zero to infinity (AUC[0-∞]) and maximum concentration (Cmax) versus log-dose for each dose level. The log-transformed Cmax and AUC least squares (LS) means and 90% confidence intervals (CI) were then back-transformed to provide geometric means. Change and percent change from baseline in vital signs and body weight were analyzed by using a mixed model repeated measures (MMRM) approach. The model for body weight assumed an unstructured covariance matrix, and the model for vital signs assumed a compound symmetry covariance matrix. The model included the following terms: baseline value as a covariate and treatment, timepoint, and treatment-by-timepoint interaction. Baseline values were defined as the day 1 predose value for vital signs and as the day −1 measurement for body weight. Only participants with an available baseline assessment and at least one available post-baseline assessment of the response variable were included in the MMRM analysis. Sample size for this phase 1 study is considered adequate for evaluating the primary safety endpoint and was not determined by power calculations.

Ethics statement

Animals were studied and maintained in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Eli Lilly and Company and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The clinical trial was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines and was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05295940, Part A). The clinical trial protocol was approved by Salus IRB, and participants provided written informed consent.

Preclinical studies

Chemical structure of eloralintide

Eloralintide is a single fatty acid acylated amylin analog (Figure 1). The main polypeptide chain contains 37 amino acids including 3 non-coded amino acid residues at positions 11, 15, and 22. The disulfide bridge in native amylin is replaced with a methylene thioacetal bridge, which enhances chemical stability. Eloralintide selectively activates AMY1R in vitro

To assess eloralintide's intrinsic receptor activity, UMUC3 cells expressing human AMY1R, human AMY3R, or human CTR were used for ligand binding and cAMP activation assays. In cAMP activation assays, eloralintide was approximately 12-fold more potent at stimulating human AMY1R (EC50: 23.9 pM, SE: 1.1) than human CTR (EC50: 291.0 pM, SE: 27.0; Figure 2A–C, Table S1). Eloralintide also had approximately 11-fold greater potency at human AMY1R than human AMY3R (EC50: 253.8 pM, SE: 9.7) and similar potency at human AMY3R and human CTR. Across all three human amylin and calcitonin receptors, eloralintide achieved full agonism (Emax >80%, Table S1). To assess the impact of eloralintide's albumin binding on the observed in vitro cAMP activity, we tested eloralintide and several other lipidated and non-lipidated peptides in the presence of 1% human serum albumin (HSA). As expected, albumin binding reduced the potency of lipidated peptides eloralintide and cagrilintide at human AMY1R, AMY3R, and CTR when tested with 1% HSA, while non-lipidated rat amylin and sCT showed no significant potency shifts. Importantly, eloralintide maintained amylin selectivity over human CTR despite albumin binding (Table S2). In ligand binding assays, eloralintide had a similar selectivity ratio as in the cAMP activation assays and bound human AMY1R with approximately 8-fold higher affinity than human CTR (Ki: 478.1 pM, SE: 37.5 vs Ki: 3896.1 pM, SE: 804.6; Table S3). Eloralintide also had approximately 8-fold higher affinity for human AMY1R than human AMY3R (Ki: 3608.9 pM, SE: 356.8). Eloralintide's intrinsic receptor activity was also characterized in UMUC3 cells expressing rat AMY1R, rat AMY3R, or rat CTR. Eloralintide was approximately 45-fold more potent at stimulating rat AMY1R (EC50: 5.4 pM, SE: 0.5) than rat CTR (EC50: 243.2 pM, SE: 27.5; Figure 2D–F, Table S4) in the cAMP activation assay. Eloralintide's rat receptor activity profile was unique compared to other native and synthetic peptides (Table S4 and S5).

Pharmacokinetics in rats and monkeys

Eloralintide and cagrilintide were administered at a dose of 30 nmol/kg in male Sprague–Dawley rats and 20 nmol/kg in Cynomolgus monkeys (Table S6; Figure S1). The PK profiles of eloralintide in both rats and monkeys demonstrated higher exposure, longer half-life, and flatter concentration vs time curves as compared to cagrilintide. These PK data in preclinical species support clinical studies of eloralintide with once-weekly dosing in humans.

To evaluate repeated dosing in an animal model of obesity, DIO rats were subcutaneously treated every three days (Q3D) with vehicle or eloralintide at doses ranging from 0.1 to 100 nmol/kg. Repeated dosing of eloralintide in DIO rats resulted in dose-proportional reductions in food intake and body weight loss compared to vehicle (Figure 3C,D). At the highest eloralintide dose of 100 nmol/kg, the mean percent change in body weight was −12.3% (SE: 0.4) at day 13 post-dose. Most body weight loss was due to loss of fat mass rather than lean mass. Fat mass loss accounted for approximately 85%, 77%, and 68% of total body weight loss at eloralintide doses of 10, 30, and 100 nmol/kg Q3D (Figure 3E). In a separate study, cagrilintide in DIO rats also resulted in dose-proportional reductions in food intake and body weight loss compared to vehicle (Figure 2C,D). At the highest cagrilintide dose of 100 nmol/kg, the mean percent change in body weight was −13.3% (±SE: 0.3) at day 13 post-dose. Approximately 71%, 64%, and 62% of this body weight loss was due to loss of fat mass rather than lean mass at cagrilintide doses of 10, 30, and 100 nmol/kg Q3D (Figure S2E). ED50s were calculated for changes in food intake, body weight, and fat mass from baseline to day 13 after the first dose (Table S9). There were no significant differences observed in DIO rats treated with eloralintide or cagrilintide for changes in food intake (ED50: 0.9 nmol/kg [SE: ±1.3] vs 2.4 nmol/kg [SE: ±1.5]) or body weight (ED50: 2.8 nmol/kg [SE: ±1.5] vs 4.9 nmol/kg [SE: ±1.3]). However, eloralintide had a significantly lower ED50 than cagrilintide for loss of fat mass (ED50: 0.8 nmol/kg [SE: ±1.3] vs 2.7 nmol/kg [SE: ±1.2]).

Phase 1 single-ascending dose trial

Baseline participant characteristics

Forty-eight participants were enrolled and randomized in six cohorts to receive either eloralintide or placebo (Table 1). Participants had a mean age of 45.5 years, were 66.7% male, and were 56.3% Hispanic or Latino. Safety and tolerability

All participants completed the study. No treatment discontinuations or serious adverse events occurred. A total of 17 treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were reported by 10 of 48 (20.8%) participants receiving placebo (n = 1) or eloralintide (n = 9) (Table S10). The most common TEAEs, reported by at least 4% of participants receiving eloralintide, were COVID-19 and decreased appetite (Table 2). Nearly all TEAEs were mild in severity (n = 1/17; Table S10). Two participants reported 4 gastrointestinal TEAEs. One participant at the 12 mg dose experienced 2 episodes of vomiting: 1 moderate event on the day of dosing and 1 mild event 2 days after dosing; both events were deemed to be treatment related; this participant also experienced mild nausea. One other participant experienced a gastrointestinal event (dyspepsia) at the highest 12 mg dose. All TEAEs subsequently resolved. Pharmacokinetic analyses

Eloralintide was absorbed with a median time to maximum concentration (tmax) of 72.0–132.2 h across the dose range studied (Table S11). Plasma concentrations declined in a monophasic manner after tmax, resulting in a terminal geometric mean t1/2 of 310–366 h (12.9–15.3 days) across the 0.4 mg–12 mg dose range (Figure S4; Table S11). For the 0.04 and 0.12 mg doses, the t1/2 values could not be calculated reliably as they were determined over a time period that was less than 2 half-lives. Eloralintide showed dose proportionality between 0.4 and 12 mg for AUC(0-∞), with a dose-normalized geometric mean ratio of 0.975 (90% CI: 0.803–1.18) across this dose range. Cmax increased less than proportionally with dose; the dose-normalized geometric mean ratio for Cmax was 0.792 (90% CI: 0.657–0.956). Variability estimates for AUC(0-∞) and Cmax were low to moderate, with a geometric mean coefficient of variation percentage (CV%) of 14–28% and 19–34%.

Pharmacodynamic analyses

Forty-eight healthy participants were included in the PD efficacy analyses. Analyses of absolute and percent change in body weight from baseline to days 8, 15, 22, and 29 are shown in Table S12. Body weight in the placebo cohort remained stable from baseline to day 29. A single dose of eloralintide at lower doses (0.04 mg–1.2 mg) showed no significant difference in mean percent change from baseline in body weight compared to placebo. However, a single dose of eloralintide at the highest tested doses (4 mg and 12 mg) was associated with a significant mean percent change from baseline in body weight compared to placebo on day 8, with significant body weight reduction persisting through day 29 (Figure 6; Table S12). For eloralintide 4 mg, the mean percent change from baseline in body weight was −2.0% (SE: 0.6) on day 8 and -2.5% (SE: 0.9) on day 29. Discussion

Eloralintide is being developed as a subcutaneous once-weekly treatment for obesity and related conditions. In vitro studies showed that eloralintide selectively activated human AMY1R over human AMY3R or CTR. In vivo studies in rats demonstrated that eloralintide dose dependently reduced food intake and body weight, which occurred primarily through loss of fat mass. Eloralintide also resulted in less conditioned taste avoidance than the non-selective amylin receptor agonist, cagrilintide. On the basis of these preclinical findings, eloralintide advanced to a phase 1 trial, where it was well tolerated at all evaluated dose levels, including the highest dose of 12 mg, in healthy participants. No serious AEs were reported. The PK of eloralintide was approximately dose-proportional, and its half-life supports once-weekly dosing. In cohorts receiving a single dose of eloralintide at 4 mg or 12 mg, observed reductions in body weight support the potential utility of eloralintide for weight management. Given its selective agonism of AMY1R relative to AMY3R and CTR, eloralintide may offer a novel approach to treating obesity with potential for improved tolerability.

Eloralintide demonstrated a distinct PK profile in the phase 1 trial. It was absorbed with a median tmax of 72–132 h across the doses ranging from 0.04 to 12 mg. Moreover, it had a half-life of 310–366 h (12.9–15.3 days) across doses. Cagrilintide at doses ranging from 0.16 to 4.5 mg has a half-life of 159–195 h (6.6–8.1 days), with a median tmax of 24–72 h [20]. The implications of eloralintide's longer half-life on its efficacy and side effect profile are yet to be fully understood and warrant further investigation.

A key finding from the phase 1 study was that only 2 of 36 participants treated with eloralintide experienced gastrointestinal adverse events. In populations being treated for weight management, incretin class medications are well recognized to produce gastrointestinal adverse events [29,30]. The most common adverse events in patients treated with GLP-1 receptor agonists include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation, with nausea being the most frequent [29]. In the phase 3 STEP trial, 44% of participants with obesity but without diabetes experienced nausea when treated with the GLP-1 receptor agonist, semaglutide, which was titrated to 2.4 mg over 16 weeks [31]. Gastrointestinal adverse events have also been reported with the use of GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists, with nausea occurring in in 25–33% depending on the dose [32]. Likewise, cagrilintide, a non-selective amylin receptor agonist that activates both amylin and calcitonin receptors, has been associated with gastrointestinal adverse events [22,33]. In adults with overweight or obesity without diabetes, cagrilintide at a dose of 2.4 mg led to reported rates of nausea of 24–31% with monotherapy and up to 55% in combination treatments with semaglutide [22,33]. Developing novel agents with improved tolerability compared to existing agents may enhance treatment adherence, enable higher dosing, and ultimately improve both the efficacy and long-term sustainability of weight loss therapies.

Findings from our preclinical conditioned taste avoidance experiments indicate that eloralintide elicited taste avoidance in rats, but it elicited significantly less taste avoidance than cagrilintide. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that rat amylin reduces food intake without inducing taste aversion in rodent models as compared to the non-selective amylin and calcitonin receptor agonist, sCT [34,35]. Eloralintide's tolerability profile may differ from other amylin receptor agonists because of its unique biochemical structure or receptor selectivity. Preclinical findings reported here, including cAMP activation and radioligand binding assays, demonstrate that eloralintide selectively activates AMY1R in human assays, while exhibiting significantly lower affinity and activation of AMY3R and CTR. In rat assays, eloralintide is a selective amylin receptor agonist that activates both AMY1R and AMY3R while remaining selective from CTR. Cagrilintide is a non-selective amylin receptor agonist, activating AMY1R, AMY3R, and CTR with comparable relative potency, in both human and rat assays. A recent study showed that cagrilintide lowers body weight in rodents through AMY1R and AMY3R; however, this study did not investigate taste aversion in their receptor knockout mouse models [36]. Therefore, differences in the effects between eloralintide and cagrilintide in rats may reflect CTR engagement rather than distinguishing between AMY1R and AMY3R activation. Previous studies in rodents have shown a role for AMY1R in mediating adiposity and a role for AMY3R in glucose homeostasis and appetite suppression [37]; however, the role of amylin receptor subtypes remains poorly understood [12]. Our findings in rat models suggest that selectively targeting AMY1R and/or AMY3R, without significant engagement of CTR, may reduce food intake and improve body weight loss, while also improving gastrointestinal tolerability. Further research is needed to characterize the in vivo selectivity profile of eloralintide and cagrilintide to fully understand the mechanisms underlying their physiological effects.

In addition to differences in taste aversion, our head-to-head DIO rat studies showed that eloralintide matched the fat mass loss of cagrilintide, without the same degree of lean mass loss. Lean body mass loss has also been observed with incretin-based receptor agonists, such as GLP-1 analogs [38,39]. Although eloralintide led to less lean mass loss than cagrilintide in rats, it is important to note that lean mass could include muscle mass, bone, water, or other tissues. Given that eloralintide and cagrilintide reduced food intake and body weight similarly in rats, the reasons for less lean mass loss with eloralintide is unclear. Further research to better understand the differential effects of amylin receptor and/or calcitonin receptor agonist activity in vivo will help translate these rodent findings into clinical advances for treating obesity and related metabolic disorders.

The limitations of preclinical experiments include the use of animal models that may not fully replicate human physiology, including eloralintide's selectivity for both AMY1R and AMY3R in rats vs only AMY1R in humans. In addition, our data demonstrate that eloralintide has approximately 12-fold higher potency for AMY1R over CTR and 11-folder higher potency over AMY3R in humans. Although this selectivity profile is unique compared to other peptides, such as rat amylin, pramlintide, and NN1213, there is no established minimum selectivity threshold to define biologically relevant selectivity. Further research is needed to characterize the in vivo selectivity profile of eloralintide and its biological relevance. Another limitation is the acute nature of the food intake and saccharin preference studies. Differences in potency between eloralintide and cagrilintide could result from differences in PK, particularly the shorter Tmax and half-life of cagrilintide relative to eloralintide. Additionally, the body weight evaluation in the phase 1 trial is limited by small sample size, which is intrinsic to such studies, and by a study population that was not limited to individuals with overweight or obesity. Thus, although we observed weight loss at the highest two doses, the efficacy for weight loss more generally was not well evaluated. Similarly, the tolerability of the 12 mg dose was notable for only 2 of 6 participants experiencing gastrointestinal adverse events, but the underlying event rate is poorly estimated with this small sample size. Further studies with larger sample sizes, and in people with overweight or obesity, will be needed to address these limitations.

Conclusion

Eloralintide is being developed as a once-weekly subcutaneous injection for the treatment of people with obesity. Preclinical studies demonstrated that eloralintide has a distinct receptor activation profile, with selectivity for AMY1R over AMY3R or CTR in human assays. In vivo studies in rats showed favorable findings in both conditioned taste avoidance and dose proportional reductions in food intake and weight loss. In the phase 1 single ascending dose trial, eloralintide was well tolerated, demonstrated a low incidence of gastrointestinal adverse events, and a PK profile that supports once-weekly dosing. Taken together, these results suggest that selective amylin receptor agonists, such as eloralintide, may be effective and well tolerated, with potential therapeutic applications for obesity and/or related metabolic conditions. Additionally, the distinct mechanisms of action of amylin receptor agonists vs incretin receptor agonists may offer promising therapeutic options for monotherapy or combination therapies. Several clinical trials are evaluating eloralintide as a monotherapy (NCT06230523, NCT06297616, NCT06916091) or in combination with the dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist tirzepatide (NCT06603571, NCT06345066, NCT06916065) in adults with overweight and obesity, with or without type 2 diabetes.
