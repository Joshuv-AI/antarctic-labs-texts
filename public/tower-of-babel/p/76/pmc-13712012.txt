Oral health skills framework for pregnant women: a professional consensus
Authors: Annabelle Tenenbaum, Mehrnoosh Yazdanbakhsh, Iris Cerdan, Johann Omiya, Vincent de Andrade, Aurélie Maurice, Thomas Marquillier, Aurore Margat, Pierre Lombrail, Rémi Gagnayre, Sébastien Riquet, Sylvie Azogui-Levy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral health during the perinatal period is essential for maternal and child oral health. Pregnancy increases vulnerability to oral conditions (periodontal disease and dental caries) associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, while children’s oral health is strongly influenced by parental—particularly maternal—knowledge, attitudes, practices, and oral health status. Although oral health education interventions may be offered during pregnancy follow-up, their effectiveness is difficult to evaluate, due to a lack of validated framework. This study aimed to develop a competency framework for oral health education during the perinatal period.MethodsA Delphi study assessed expert opinion on the competencies pregnant women should acquire to ensure good oral health for themselves and their children through oral health education interventions. The questionnaire comprises two sections (pregnant woman and child) and covered core skill domains—preventing oral diseases, monitoring oral health conditions, and seeking dental care— subdivided into 97 contributory learning objectives across knowledge, practices and attitudes. Objective were rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with consensus defined as ≥ 70%. The expert panel included 21 perinatal healthcare professionals (midwives, pediatricians, gynecologists, general practitioners, dentists, and pharmacists).ResultsIn the first round, 59% of objectives were rated as very useful, and 40 were revised the second round due to lack of consensus. After three rounds, consensus was reached for all objectives except four: the links between periodontal disease and preeclampsia, impact of breastfeeding on oral health, the protective role of saliva and women’s active search for oral health information. The discussion of the unresolved areas highlighted gaps in healthcare professionals’ understanding of oral-general health links in the perinatal period, and in perceptions of individuals’ autonomy in health information seeking.ConclusionsThis study resulted in a consensus-based competency framework for oral health education during pregnancy, structured around three key competencies: preventing oral diseases, participating in care plans, and asserting health-related rights. Establishing consensus on women’s oral health competencies supports a cross-disciplinary approach to prevention and health education during the perinatal period. Background

Dental caries is a highly prevalent disease, affecting approximately 40% of the global population, with a disproportionate burden among children and socially vulnerable populations. It results from complex interactions between individual and collective determinants, including biological, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors as well as life-course conditions [1–9]. Children’s oral health is closely linked to that of their parents particularly their knowledge, attitudes, and practices —as well as to maternal oral health as early bacterial transmission from mother to child can occur shortly after birth and increase the risk of caries development [10–12].

Pregnancy represents a period of increased susceptibility to oral diseases, particularly dental caries and periodontal disease. Physiological and behavioral factors associated with pregnancy, especially dietary modifications, contribute to a higher risk of caries, and gingivitis is more frequent in this time. Periodontal disease has also been associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes, including prematurity, preeclampsia, chorioamnionitis, and intrauterine growth restriction, likely mediated by systemic inflammatory pathways [13–24].

Consequently, improving children’s oral health, as well as maternal oral health and pregnancy outcomes, requires preventive interventions during the perinatal period. This critical window is also emphasized by Santé Publique France and UNICEF’s First 1000 Days of Life program of, which identifies early pregnancy as a foundation for lifelong health and well-being [25–27].

In recent years, oral health education interventions have increasingly been integrated into pregnancy follow-up. While some evidence suggests modest benefits from these interventions —for example, a meta-analysis from Gormesall et al. reported a reduction in early childhood caries following maternal education on infant feeding practices [28]—several studies have questioned their effectiveness in improving maternal and child oral health, dental care utilization, and women’s quality of life. Limitations include a predominant focus on the child, rather than a comprehensive approach addressing both maternal and child needs, insufficient consideration of living environments and health literacy, poor description of intervention objectives and targeted skills, and substantial heterogeneity in outcomes and assessment tools [29–36].

To address these gaps, this study aimed to develop a reference competency framework for oral health education during pregnancy to harmonize healtcare professionals’s educational interventions and support pregnant women in oral health promotion.

Methods

A research group dedicated to the oral health of mothers and children called Promoting Oral Health during Pregnancy and ChiLdhood (POHPiL) was formed. Its aim was to bring together professionals involved across the perinatal care pathway, including two public health physicians, two midwives, three dentists specializing in oral public health, one nutritionist, and two hospital pharmacists with expertise in health education, thereby ensuring a multidisciplinary and multiprofessional perspective. These group members are referred to as “researchers” in this report. The group’s work was performed in two phases. First, a literature review was undertaken to identify oral health recommendations for pregnant women. Second, a questionnaire based on this review was presented to a panel of professional experts specializing in perinatal care. These experts then participated in a classic Delphi study to reach a consensus regarding the oral health skills expected of pregnant women for themselves and their children (Fig. 1).

Literature review

A literature review was undertaken to identify oral health guidelines during pregnancy for healthcare professionals and pregnant women. The search methodology, including search terms and choice of databases, was developed in collaboration with a research librarian. The chosen databases selected to specifically target resources written in the form of recommendations were Trip Database (the only one to propose the term “guidelines” as a filter), PubMed, Web of Science, Dentistry & Oral Sciences Source (DOSS), and CISMeF (health database for relevant French-language resources). This approach was complemented by a grey literature search to identify material published by scholarly societies, institutional websites and Google Scholar. The inclusion criteria specified that the study population should comprise pregnant women and that the subject should be oral health recommendations. Care protocols and research articles containing no recommendations were excluded. The literature review was conducted between January and February 2022 and was updated in May 2026. Selected references were read in full and analyzed independently by two authors (AT and SAL). In the event of disagreement, the opinion of a third researcher was sought (Fig. 2 and Supplementary File 1).

Literature review flow diagram

The search strategy enabled us to identify 40 references (Table 1), including 28 educational guides or brochures, seven scientific articles or reports, and six resources from institutional sites or scholarly societies. Of these references, 28 were aimed at healthcare professionals, and 12 were aimed at pregnant women. In addition, 17 references provided information regarding children’s oral health. Some guides for professionals adopted a multi-professional approach, simultaneously addressing perinatal and early-childhood caregivers and dental professionals. Six guides for pregnancy care included a chapter dedicated to oral health, while the other guides were monothematic, dealing solely with oral health. Data extracted from all resources included organization, authority or author, year, country, format, target audience, and oral health-specific content. The material was categorized into recommendations on preventive behaviors (hygiene, diet) and prevention pathways, use of healthcare professionals, and treatment of oral diseases.

Pedagogical positioning

This work lies at the interface between a competency-based approach and the principles of behaviorist-inspired objective-based pedagogy [37]. The latter justifies using concepts such as contributory pedagogical objectives and the various types of knowledge mobilized (knowledge, practices, attitudes) to better characterize skill development. This choice reflects the desire to adopt an approach that articulates knowledge and skills while facilitating the collection of elements contributing to the evaluation of skills based on experts’ pedagogical experience. Indeed, the use of a method purely focused on the skills approach, such as analyzing skills components, reference situations, or development levels, could have introduced bias into the responses.

Delphi survey

The classic Delphi method is a qualitative research approach that measures the degree of consensus and divergence among experts’ opinions on a specific topic, gradually facilitating the emergence of a consensus [38–40]. This method involves well-defined steps: choosing a topic and identifying the relevant experts, soliciting participation, formulating the problem as an initial question; developing the questionnaire, launching the first round of consultations by administering the questionnaire, collecting the ratings (level of agreement), analyzing the ratings, providing feedback to the experts, adapting the questionnaire accordingly and organizing a new consultation, and repeating this process until the expert reach a consensus. The study also follows Delphi reporting guidelines in DELPHI studies in social and health sciences- recommendations for a STAndardized Reporting (DELPHISTAR) (Supplementary File 2).

Expert panel

Participant selection followed a four-stage process: elaborating criteria for expert selection, establishing a list of potential experts, contacting selected experts, and inviting participants [39]. Participants were initially recruited through purposive sampling among professional contacts of the research team, targeting experts in perinatal care with experience in pregnancy follow-up within their clinical practice. This approach ensured representation across key healthcare disciplines, including physicians, midwives, obstetricians–gynecologists, general practitioners, dentists, and pharmacists. In a second stage, a snowball sampling approach was employed, whereby participants were invited to identify additional eligible professionals within their professional networks. Twenty-one professional perinatal experts were invited (Fig. 1).

Questionnaire design

The literature review results were used to identify three key competencies: preventing, monitoring, and seeking dental care. These competencies are associated with specific pedagogical objectives, defining the critical learning to be achieved to enable optimal activation of each competency. A questionnaire was developed specifically for this study. It was structured into two sections (pregnant woman and child) and covered the three main families of skills (preventing, monitoring, seeking dental care), divided into 97 contributory learning objectives categorized into knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Participants were asked to rate the usefulness of the contributory educational objectives on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from “not at all useful” to “very useful.” (Supplementary File 3).

Questionnaire distribution

The questionnaire was distributed electronically and included a cover letter introducing the study, soliciting participation, and guaranteeing the anonymity of responses. The study ran from April 2022 to July 2022. A period of ten days was allowed between each round, with a reminder sent after one week. The experts were asked to rate their level of agreement with the proposed objectives on a discrete quantitative scale ranging from 1 (not at all useful) to 4 (very useful). The objectives given a score of 4 by at least 70% of participants were retained (total agreement), those given a score of 1 by at least 70% were eliminated (definitive rejection), and the remaining items were reformulated for the next round. Between rounds, the researchers reviewed the results and redefined the objectives lacking consensus. In this way, responses were consolidated through successive questionnaire rounds to achieve a satisfactory degree of consensus [40]. The final report was presented to the experts at a meeting to summarize the results and served as the basis for constructing the competency framework.

Participation

Of the 21 experts invited to form the expert panel, 19 agreed to participate. Three questionnaire’s rounds were conducted. Nineteen experts completed the first Delphi round (n1 = 19). Participant drop-out occurred in subsequent rounds, with 15 experts completing the second round (n2 = 15) and 11 completing the final round (n3 = 11) (Fig. 1).

In the first round, 57 of the 97 contributory learning objectives (59%) were rated as “very useful.” For objectives related to the mother’s competencies for her own oral health, consensus was distributed across the three competencies as follows: 52% for preventing, 66.7% for monitoring, and 88.9% for seeking dental care. For objectives related to oral health competencies for pregnant women concerning the child, consensus was distributed across the three competencies as follows: 54.5% for preventing, 41.7% for monitoring, and 33.3% for seeking dental care. Forty objectives failed to achieve consensus and were reformulated for thesecond round.

In the second round, 12 of the 33 (36%) resubmitted objectives were rated as “very useful.” These were distributed across competencies as follows: 30% for preventing, 0% for monitoring, and 100% for seeking dental care. For objectives related to oral health competencies for pregnant women concerning the child, consensus was distributed across the three competencies as follows: 45.45% for preventing, 14.28% for monitoring, and 50% for seeking dental care. Twenty-one objectives failed to achieve consensus and were reformulated for the second round.

In the third round, 8 of the 12 (66.66%) remaining objectives were deemed a “very useful”, with 40% related to preventing and 100% related to monitoring. No seeking dental care objectives were included in the third round, as full consensus for this competency had already been achieved in the previous round. For objectives related to oral health competencies for pregnant women concerning the child, consensus was distributed across the three competencies as follows: 66.66% for preventing, 100% for monitoring, and 100% for seeking dental care. Four objectives failed to achieve consensus.

Mother’s competencies related to her own oral health

Regarding maternal competencies related to women’s own oral health, 67.3% of the objectives reached a “very useful” consensus after the first Delphi round, with consensus rates of 52% for preventing, 66.7% for monitoring, and 88.9% for seeking dental care (Tables 2 and 3).

Results of the three Delphi rounds

Within the preventing competency, more than 50% of the objectives related to oral hygiene reached consensus, making this the most widely agreed-upon application area. In contrast, more than 50% of nutrition-related objectives did not reach consensus. The only nutrition-related objectives achieving consensus concerned beverages—prioritizing water (100% rated as “very useful”) avoiding sugary drinks outside mealtimes (73.68% rated as “very useful”)—as well as choosing whole fruit rather than fruit juice (89.47% rated as “very useful”). No consensus was reached for two objectives: awareness of the risk of preeclampsia associated with periodontal disease (57.89% rated as “very useful”) and independently seeking oral health information online (15.79% rated as “very useful”).

For the monitoring competency, consensus was reached for identifying signs of caries or dental abscess and remaining alert to discomfort or pain; however, objectives related to recognizing signs of gingivitis and observing the mouth did not reach consensus (63.16% and 47.37% rated as “very useful,” respectively).

The seeking dental care competency achieved the highest overall consensus across knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Most care-related objectives (e.g., the importance and modalities of dental care, anesthesia, X-rays, treatment options, prevention programs, seeking care, and avoiding self-medication) received favorable utility ratings. Only two objectives failed to reach the ≥ 70% consensus threshold: reassurance regarding the absence of risk of preterm delivery associated with dental care (68.42% rated as “very useful”) and the need to claim reimbursement (63.16% rated as “very useful”).

In the first round, consensus was not reached for 13 objectives related to the mother’s competencies for her own oral health; these objectives were partly reformulated and resubmitted in the second round (13 objectives). In the second round, the only that objectives reached consensus were those related to seeking dental care. Objectives that still failed to reach consensus after the second round were completely reformulated and resubmitted in the third round (6 objectives). After the final round, consensus was still not reached for three prevention-related objectives: awareness of the risk of preeclampsia associated with periodontal disease (63.64% rated as “very useful”%), awareness of the protective role of saliva (63.64% rated as “very useful”%), and search for information about oral health on your one’s (45.45% rated as “very useful”%).

Maternal competencies related to the child’s oral health

Regarding maternal competencies related to the child’s oral health, 50% of the objectives reached a “very useful” consensus after the first Delphi round. Consensus levels differed across competencies, with 54.5% for preventing, 41.7% for monitoring, and 33.3% for seeking dental care (Tables 2 and 3).

Within the preventing caries and periodontal disease competency, all hygiene-related objectives achieved consensus and were rated as “very useful,” whereas nutrition-related objectives reached only a moderate level of consensus. Several areas of application failed to reach consensus, including understanding the masticatory and social functions of the oral cavity (with “very useful” ratings ranging from 31.58% to 63.16%), informing caregivers about caries prevention ( 52.63% rated as “very useful”%), reducing discomfort during teething ( 42.11% rated as “very useful”%), and adopting specific feeding-related practices suche as number of daily intakes, use of a cup from the age of 1, and use of different utensils (with “very useful” ratings ranging from 26.32% to 63.16%). The breastfeeding objective generated the most divergent opinions among experts: it was the only objective for which the “not at all useful” rating (36.8%) exceeded the “quite useful” rating (26.3%), and it remained the only preventing objective without consensus after the final round.

For the monitoring competency, more than 70% of experts considered it “very useful” for mothers to monitor visible signs of caries and pain in children. However, recognition of more technical clinical signs (e.g., dental abscesses, gingival inflammation, malposition, eruption disorders, or refusal to eat) received lower levels of agreement, with “very useful” ratings below 60%.

Within the seeking dental care competency, preventive dental care through health insurance programs (e.g., the French program M’T Dents) was rated as “very useful”. However, no consensus was reached during the first round for proposed attitudes such as requesting a dental examination by a general practitioner or scheduling a dental visit from the age of 1 year (36.84% rated as “very useful”).

In the fisrt round, consensus was not reached for 24 objectives related to the child’s oral health. These areas were reformulated and, when appropriate, merged, resulting in 20 objectives resubmitted in the second round. Similarly, six objectives that still failed to reach consensus after the second round were reformulated and resubmitted in the third round. After the final round, breastfeeding remained the only area of application for which consensus was not reached.

Following the third consecutive rounds, four objectives did not reach consensus and where further discussed: knowing the risk of preeclampsia associated with the presence of periodontal disease, identifying the effects of breastfeeding on oral health, seeking information on oral health and the protective role of saliva. The responses in the free-text comments were analyzed by two independent reviewers.

Providing feedback to the experts and outcome of the consensus discussion

Following the Delphi rounds, the research group (POHPiL) organized a feedback session with the experts to discuss objectives lacking consensus, using results from the previous rounds.

Regarding the oral health–related risk of preeclampsia, participants expressed concerns about the prioritization and timing of messages delivered to pregnant women. Experts highlighted the risk of information overload during pregnancy, noting that excessive or poorly timed information could be counterproductive. Several professionals also emphasized that preeclampsia is a complex condition that is difficult to explain, making its association with oral health particularly challenging to address. As a result, no consensus was reached on the usefulness of directly informing pregnant women about the risk of preeclampsia related to periodontal disease. Instead, experts agreed to address this issue more broadly within the context of the links between oral health and pregnancy outcomes.

Regarding breastfeeding, experts highlighted inconsistencies in the messages delivered by perinatal professionals (midwives, obstetricians–gynecologists, nursery nurses) and other healthcare providers (general practitioners, pharmacists, dentists, pediatricians). Practitioners reported being frequently confronted with discrepancies between breastfeeding promotion messages and dietary recommendations related to oral health. For example, dentists recommend limiting food intake to four times per day from the introduction of complementary feeding in order to stabilize oral pH and promote tooth remineralization [41]. This inconsistency among professionals reflects a lack of clarity regarding nutritional messages and an incomplete understanding of the links between milk intake (breastfeeding or infant formula), frequency of food intake, and oral health. As a result, risk factors for early childhood caries are not clearly identified or consistently addressed by professionals. The discussion did not resolve this disagreement, as experts expressed concern about delivering contradictory messages that might undermine breastfeeding promotion, particularly given that rates in France remain among the lowest in Europe. However, consensus was reached on the importance of clearly structuring food intake from the time of dietary diversification.

The issue of women independently searching for oral health information online was also discussed. Experts raised concerns regarding users’ capacity to critically appraise the reliability of online sources and navigate conflicting information. At the same time, they highlighted a preference for information provided directly by healthcare professionals, underscoring the central role of professional counselling in ensuring accurate, consistent, and context-appropriate health education during the perinatal period.

Knowledge of the protective role of saliva did not generate dissenting opinions; however, this issue highlights a broader concern regarding oral health professionals’ training, which experts considered to be almost nonexistent in initial education.

As a final outcome of the study, consensus was reached after three rounds, taking into account the direction of the discussions. Specifically, the risk of preeclampsia was addressed within the broader framework of the links between oral health and pregnancy, and the relationship between milk intake and oral health was integrated into the broader concept of food intake frequency.

This expert consensus process resulted in the development of a competency framework designed to be mobilized among pregnant women through oral health education programs and to serve as a resource for healthcare professionals. The framework is structured around three core competencies addressing pregnant women’s own oral health and that of their child: preventing oral diseases (caries and periodontal diseases) during the perinatal period, participating in the implementation of the care plan, and empowering women to assert their rights. For each competency, essential components and corresponding educational objectives were identified (Table 4).

Discussion

Oral health education during pregnancy and in preparation for birth and parenthood remains uneven and, in some contexts, largely absent [42, 43]. According to Kumat et al., the lack of systematic integration of oral health into general health monitoring and early prevention programs limits families’ access to preventive services, the adoption of health-promoting behaviors, and timely access to care [44]. Moreover, although oral health education initiatives targeting pregnant women exist, they often lack scientific grounding in terms of both content and demonstrated effectiveness, thereby limiting their impact as genuine preventive strategies at individual and community levels [28]. In this context, the present study sought to address an important gap by developing a reference competency framework for oral health education during pregnancy based on expert consensus to support healthcare professionals in their educational practices.

The results of the Delphi process highlight a clear tendency among professionals to prioritize competencies related to monitoring oral disease and seeking dental care over preventive competencies for pregnant women. This finding reflects a predominantly care-oriented and biomedical approach, consistent with the highly medicalized model that still characterizes perinatal care. In contrast, prevention-related competencies were more readily endorsed when addressing the child’s oral health, revealing that healthcare professionals are less familiar with pediatric oral healthcare. This discrepancy underscores the persistent difficulty of positioning pregnancy as a key window for both preventive and curative oral health interventions [27].

Discussions during the feedback phase further revealed tensions and inconsistencies in professional discourses, particularly regarding the links between periodontal disease and preeclampsia, as well as between breastfeeding, feeding practices, and oral health [45, 46]. These topics exposed discrepancies between medical specialties and uncertainties about how to prioritize and communicate preventive messages during a period already marked by a high informational burden for pregnant women. Professionals expressed concern that poorly coordinated or overly complex messages could be counterproductive, especially when addressing conditions such as preeclampsia, which are difficult to explain and emotionally sensitive [47–51]. Consequently, consensus was ultimately reached by integrating these topics into broader thematic frameworks—namely, the links between oral health and pregnancy outcomes, and the role of food intake frequency—rather than addressing them as isolated educational objectives.

These findings reflect wider structural issues in perinatal prevention. Previous studies have demonstrated that dentists rarely address oral health prevention specific to pregnancy, early childhood, or breastfeeding, while perinatal care professionals (e.g., midwives, maternal and child protection services, early childcare professionals) often lack the training and confidence to integrate oral health prevention into their practices [52, 53]. Oral hygiene is seldom included among the first preventive practices addressed in maternity settings, and pregnancy-related oral health risks are infrequently discussed. This compartmentalization of prevention messages contributes to a siloed approach, whereby professionals focus on discipline-specific or pathology-oriented messages rather than adopting a holistic, life-course perspective [54]. More broadly, delivering coherent and consistent educational messages across the perinatal care pathway remains challenging due to the involvement of multiple healthcare professionals with heterogeneous training, roles, and reference frameworks. A recent rapid review by Raghunathan et al. emphasized that fragmentation of care can lead to inconsistent or conflicting counselling and constitutes a major barrier to implementing multidisciplinary educational interventions during pregnancy [55].

The limited endorsement of objectives related to women independently seeking oral health information—particularly online—further illustrates prevailing professional representations of the practitioner–patient relationship [56]. The lack of consensus on this objective reflects concerns about misinformation, but also a reluctance to recognize women as active agents in managing their own health. These findings echo longstanding critiques of health education approaches based solely on information transmission, which assume that knowledge automatically leads to behavior change. As highlighted by Nutbeam, health education must instead be understood as an active process that enhances individuals’ ability to access, understand, appraise, and use information within their social and environmental contexts—that is, their health literacy [57]. From this perspective, supporting women in critically navigating health information, including online resources, represents an opportunity to strengthen autonomy, empowerment, and informed decision-making rather than a risk to be avoided [58, 59].

According to Bernard and Eymard, health education in perinatal care can be defined as a process centered on women, aimed at increasing their autonomy and facilitating the acquisition or maintenance of the skills they need to manage and adapt to the changes inherent in motherhood [60]. This health education is “an active process of reinforcing self-esteem and critical thinking skills, and the ability to make decisions and take action to act and improve one's health; in this case, to succeed in a health experience by welcoming the child and making the transition to parenthood” [61].

The competency framework developed through this study aligns with contemporary health promotion principles by shifting the focus from advice-based information delivery toward an educational approach centered on skills development, autonomy, and empowerment as outlined in the health education competency framework of the French National Institute for Prevention and Health Education [62–64]. By structuring oral health education around three core competencies—preventing oral diseases during the perinatal period, participating in the care pathway, and asserting one’s rights—the framework provides a coherent and shared reference for healthcare professionals from different disciplines. Unlike existing guides that primarily function as informational tools, this framework is designed to support educational interventions that actively engage women in maintaining or improving their oral health and that of their child.

To our knowledge, this is the first reference framework of its kind specifically dedicated to perinatal oral health education. It offers a common foundation for interdisciplinary practice and could contribute to greater coherence in prevention discourse, provided it is accompanied by appropriate initial and continuing professional training. Strengthening interdisciplinary education—particularly in educational methods and psychosocial skills—appears essential to move beyond paternalistic and curative models and to support professionals in adopting a genuinely health-promoting approach. Ultimately, integrating this framework into perinatal care pathways may help foster a shared prevention culture, enhance professionals’ collective capacity for action, and support women’s power to act in line with the principles of health promotion and health democracy [65, 66].

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. The number of professionals meeting the criteria of the Delphi method decreased significantly during the third round (due to vacation period, retirement, etc.). Nevertheless, the careful selection of experts resulted in a representative panel of professionals working in the field of maternity and early childhood. In retrospect, the 97-question questionnaire may have been perceived as excessive, which could have contributed to participants attrition. Furthermore, categorizing skills into knowledge, attitudes, and practices may have led to misunderstandings or a perception of redundancy, as some areas were addressed multiple times across different categories. Finally, the proposed competency framework should be validated in different sociocultural and linguistic contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, enabling pregnant women to acquire oral health skills for themselves and their children is a key public health goal. This study represents a first step toward a common, interdisciplinary approach to perinatal oral health by establishing a consensus among experts on the fundamental skills required. The resulting single reference framework establishes a common foundational core structured around three coherent and evidence-based key competencies, supporting the harmonization of oral health education interventions for pregnant women and promoting the development of women’s skills and autonomy. The study also highlights the need for interprofessional collaboration to address patients’ health education needs and autonomy, particularly as this area of intervention lies at the intersection of several medical disciplines. Further research is needed to determine the most appropriate modalities, contexts, and health professionals for implementing brief and effective oral health interventions within prenatal care pathways.
