Rural community pharmacists' willingness to receive, administer, and recommend COVID-19 vaccines after the First U.S. vaccine rollout
Authors: Sura O. AlMahasis, Brent Fox, David Ha, Jingjing Qian, Chih-hsuan Wang, Salisa C. Westrick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionVaccines remain the cornerstone for prevention of COVID-19. Particularly in rural areas, community pharmacists are among the most accessible healthcare professionals. Their advocacy for immunization can help overcome patients' vaccine hesitancy and close the disparity gap between rural and urban regions. This study explored factors influencing the willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines among rural community pharmacists after the first U.S. vaccine rollout.MethodsA convenience sample of rural community pharmacists completed a selfadministered, validated paper/online survey (n = 86, 23.4% response rate). Measures included COVID-19 vaccination status, perceived benefits of offering COVID-19 immunization services at the pharmacy, and willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines. Descriptive, bivariate, and multivariate statistics were used to analyze potential nonresponse bias, survey responses, and associations between variables as appropriate. Alpha was set at 0.05.ResultsThe proportion of pharmacists who were vaccinated against COVID-19 was 79.1%. Over 75% of participants were in favor of recommending COVID-19 immunizations to eligible family members and patients and almost 85% agreed that they would administer COVID-19 vaccines to their patients. Most pharmacists perceived COVID-19 immunization services to be beneficial, such as attracting more patients into the pharmacy (81.2%), increasing pharmacy revenue (75.3%), and enhancing relationships with local clinics/public health agencies (74.1%). COVID-19 vaccination status and the perceived benefit of offering immunization services at the pharmacy were significantly associated with pharmacists' willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines.ConclusionThe relatively high rates of COVID-19 vaccination uptake and the strong willingness of rural community pharmacists to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines, after the first national vaccine rollout, were encouraging. The findings underscore the importance of engaging rural community pharmacists in efforts to prepare for and respond to future pandemics and public health crises.

Introduction

Vaccines have long been the most effective strategy for controlling and eradicating numerous infectious diseases. COVID-19 vaccines, in particular, have proven to be highly effective in preventing COVID-19 infections, hospitalizations, and deaths.1 However, evidence demonstrates a large gap in COVID-19 vaccination coverage between residents of rural and urban areas in the United States (U.S.).2, 3, 4 One Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) report showed that between December 14, 2020 and January 31, 2022, around 59.0% of rural residents received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine compared to more than 75.0% of urban residents.3 Data from the National Immunization Survey (NIS) of adults— a telephone survey designed to monitor nationwide vaccination coverage— from May 28 to June 30, 2023, revealed that a higher rate of rural residents are completely unvaccinated against COVID-19 compared to urban residents (20.5% vs. 11.0%).15 Further, data from the NIS-Child for the same period showed that 56.0% of teens 12–17 years old in rural areas are completely unvaccinated against COVID-19 compared to urban teens (33.0%).5 Additionally, COVID-19 vaccination series completion rates were lower in rural teens compared to urban teens (39.9% vs. 62.3%). The difference can be attributed to various factors inherent in rural areas, including inadequate health infrastructure, a shortage of healthcare providers, and the lack of a reliable transportation system where patients often travel long distances to seek care.2,6 Furthermore, attitudes about the severity of COVID-19 infection and the COVID-19 vaccines are driven by various socioeconomic and political factors that can differ between urban and rural residents.5,7

To achieve vaccine equity, reduce rural-urban disparities, and reduce the burden of COVID-19, it is crucial to eliminate barriers to vaccination in rural areas. Community pharmacists, often the first line of contact for patients in rural areas, are ideally positioned to play a significant role in promoting vaccine equity.8 Community pharmacies are open for extended hours, including on weekends and holidays, and offer services without the need for an appointment or visit fees.9,10 Pharmacists educate communities about vaccine benefits and risks, dispel misconceptions and misinformation about COVID-19 and its vaccines, and address pertinent queries from the general public, all while making the vaccines available at the pharmacy.11., 12, 13., 14 Further, research indicates that patients' acceptance of vaccines is strongly influenced by pharmacists' recommendations, underscoring pharmacists' significant role in promoting vaccine uptake.15

Pharmacists' recommendations are greatly important to increase vaccine uptake among rural residents. However, little is known about the attitudes of American rural community pharmacists towards COVID-19 immunization and its association with their willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines. As such, this study explored: 1) rural community pharmacists' COVID-19 vaccination status, 2) rural community pharmacists' willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines to family members and patients, 3) rural community pharmacists' perceived benefits of offering COVID-19 immunization services at their practice site, and 4) factors associated with rural community pharmacists' COVID-19 vaccination status (4.1) and willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines to family members and patients (4.2).

Methods

Study design and setting.

The current study is part of a master's thesis that explored the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on pharmacy-based immunization practices in rural areas.16 A cross-sectional study was conducted from May to August 2021, utilizing a self-administered mixed-mode survey (paper and electronic). The study focused on rural community pharmacies located in five southeastern states (Alabama (AL), Arkansas (AR), Mississippi (MS), North Carolina (NC), and South Carolina (SC)). Pharmacies were identified through two different sampling frames: 1) the Rural Research Alliance of Community Pharmacies (RURAL-CP) network and 2) the Hayes Directories of Alabama.17,18 The RURAL-CP is a multi-state research network dedicated to reducing rural health disparities in seven southern states (Georgia (GA), Tennessee (TN), and the five states mentioned above). Its mission is to support high-quality implementation research in rural pharmacies to improve healthcare delivery and outcomes. The Hayes Directories of Alabama is an online database that provides contact information for community pharmacies in AL.

Eligible pharmacies met two criteria: operating in a rural area (indicated by a Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) code of 4–10) and providing at least one vaccine in both 2019 and 2020. Invitations to participate were extended via email or first-class mail. Respondents (i.e., pharmacists) were incentivized with a $20 Amazon electronic gift card. The study protocol received approval from the Auburn University Institutional Review Board (IRB). Comprehensive details on sampling, sampling frames, pharmacy selection (rurality verification), survey development, and data collection can be found in AlMahasis et al., 2023.16

Measures

The questionnaire was pre-tested with seven individuals (three pharmacists and four student pharmacists) to ensure its content validity. It was then pilot-tested with twenty urban pharmacists identified using the Hayes Directories of Alabama. The full questionnaire is available in Appendix 1. This paper reported on specific questionnaire questions, including (1) pharmacists' COVID-19 vaccination status– question 1.2 (two response options; Yes or No) and question 1.3 (eight response options; multiple-choice question), (2) pharmacists' willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines– question 1.4 (three items with response options ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree on a 5-point Likert scale), (3) pharmacists' perceived benefits of offering COVID-19 immunization services at the pharmacy– question 1.5 (five items with response options ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree on a 5-point Likert scale), and (4) pharmacy and pharmacist characteristics – section 4.

Data analysis

To evaluate potential nonresponse bias, demographic and pharmacy characteristics including age, sex, education, race, previous influenza vaccination, COVID-19 vaccination status, pharmacy immunization experience, pharmacy ownership type, and pharmacy enrollment in an immunization information system (IIS), were compared between early respondents and late respondents. These factors were compared using the Chi-square test, Fisher's exact test, and the independent two-sample t-test.

The analysis of survey responses involved both basic descriptive statistics and bivariate statistics. The Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was applied for categorical variables, while the independent two-sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, Spearman correlation, and one-way ANOVA were used for continuous variables. Specifically, associations between demographic variables (age, sex, race, education, and previous influenza vaccination) and pharmacists' COVID-19 vaccination status were examined.

The construct validity of the Willingness scale and Benefit scale (composed of items from questions 1.4 and 1.5 in Appendix 1) was assessed using exploratory factor analysis, employing principal components and varimax rotation.16 Components with eigenvalues ≥1 and scale items with factor loadings ≥0.4 were retained in the analysis. Appropriateness of the factor analysis was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy (KMO) (KMO ≥ 0.5) and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity (p < .0001).19,20 Internal consistency reliability of the scales was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha, with a threshold of 0.70 considered acceptable.

Associations between independent variables (pharmacist and pharmacy characteristics, COVID-19 vaccination status, and the Benefits scale) and pharmacists' willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines (Willingness scale) were assessed using bivariate analyses. Significant variables from these bivariate analyses were then included in a multivariate linear regression model to predict pharmacists' willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines. The alpha level for significance was set at 0.05. All analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4.

Results

Response rate and potential nonresponse bias

A total of 385 pharmacies were identified and 102 surveys were received. The final sample consisted of 86 eligible pharmacies resulting in a response rate of 23.8% (95% C.I.: 14.8% to 32.8%). Of the 86 pharmacies, 29 (33.7%) were members of the RURAL-CP network, and 57 (66.3%) were identified from the Hayes Directories. A detailed description of response rate and response distribution by state was published in AlMahasis et al., 2023.16 In investigating non-response bias, no significant differences were found between early and late respondents, except for the mean age. Late respondents, on average, were older than early respondents (49.4 vs. 37.0 years) (Appendix 2).16 Because no differences were found between pharmacies identified via RURAL-CP vs. Hayes Directories, except for chain pharmacy ownership (3.5% RURAL-CP group vs. 25.5% Hayes group; p < .05), this paper presents results from both groups combined.16

Pharmacist demographics and pharmacy characteristics

Respondents had a mean (SD) age of 42.2 (± 12.4) years; nearly 59.0% were females; 94.1% were white; most (63.3%) held a PharmD degree; and approximately 85.0% had received the seasonal influenza vaccine in the past year. About half (49.0%) of the pharmacies where respondents worked were single-independent pharmacies.16 On average, these pharmacies dispensed 278.7 (± 138.7) prescriptions daily and employed two pharmacists and three pharmacy technicians.16 Pharmacies had a median of 10 years of immunization experience with a median of two pharmacists trained for vaccine administration.16 The majority of pharmacies (71/83; 86.0%) were participants in an IIS, with 11 and six pharmacies enrolling in 2020 and 2021, respectively.16

Pharmacists' COVID-19 vaccination status

At the time of the study, the majority of respondents (79.1%; 68/86) were vaccinated against COVID-19, while 20.9% (18/86) were not vaccinated. Notably, 17.4% (15/86) of respondents were against COVID-19 vaccination (i.e., expressed no intention to get vaccinated) and the remaining 3.5% (3/86) did not report their vaccination intentions.

Pharmacists' willingness to recommend and administer the COVID-19 vaccines

Over 75.0% of respondents strongly agreed or agreed that they would recommend COVID-19 immunization to eligible family members, and nearly 78.0% strongly agreed or agreed that they would recommend it to their patients. Around 85.0% also strongly agreed or agreed that they would administer COVID-19 vaccines to their patients (Table 1). A composite score, the Willingness score, was developed consisting of the three items listed in Table 1 using five answer options (Strongly Disagree = 1, Disagree = 2, Undecided = 3, Agree = 4, and Strongly Agree = 5) with scores ranging from 1 to 5. The mean (SD) Willingness score was 4.2 (± 1.2) with 44 respondents having a score of 5. Higher scores reflect a greater willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines. Exploratory factor analysis showed that all three Willingness score items fit into one construct (KMO = 0.57, Bartlett's Test of Sphericity p < .0001). Factors associated with community pharmacists' COVID-19 vaccination status and willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines

When examining associations between demographics and pharmacists' COVID-19 vaccination status, a statistically significant association was observed with seasonal influenza vaccination history. Pharmacists with a history of seasonal influenza vaccination were more likely to have received a COVID-19 vaccine (Fisher's Exact test; p = .0006). Other variables studied (age, sex, race, and education) were not significant.

A series of bivariate analyses were conducted to examine the association between independent variables (pharmacist demographics, pharmacy characteristics, and the Benefit score) and the dependent variable (the Willingness score). Bivariate analyses showed that three out of nine variables studied (age, sex, race, education, previous influenza vaccination, COVID-19 vaccination status, pharmacy immunization experience, pharmacy ownership, and the Benefit score), showed statistically significant associations with the Willingness score. These variables were: 1) previous seasonal influenza vaccination (Mann-Whitney U test; p = .0015), 2) COVID-19 vaccination status (Mann-Whitney U test; p = .0001), and 3) the Benefit score (r 0.52, p < .0001). Significant variables identified in bivariate analyses were included as predictors in a multivariate linear regression model, with the Willingness score as the dependent variable. The multivariate linear regression analysis (Table 3) found only the Benefit score and COVID-19 vaccination status to be significant predictors of pharmacists' willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines (p < .05). Specifically, with every 1-unit increase in the Benefit score, there is an expected average increase of 0.57 units in the Willingness score. Community pharmacists who received a COVID-19 vaccine had, on average, a Willingness score that was 0.83 units higher compared to those who had not received the vaccine. Discussion

The current study is the first known to explore COVID-19 vaccine acceptance among rural community pharmacists following the first U.S. vaccine rollout in mid-December 2020, and its association with willingness to make strong vaccine recommendations. Overall, a high percentage of respondents reported being already vaccinated against COVID-19, which is consistent with a nationwide survey of pharmacists launched by the American Pharmacists Association (APhA) in May 2021.21 Despite the high self-reported COVID-19 vaccine coverage among pharmacists found in the current study, hesitancy was observed as more than 17.0% indicated having no plans to get vaccinated. Vaccine hesitancy among healthcare providers (HCPs) is not new, and the reasons for this hesitancy are often similar in nature to those of patients.22 Numerous studies have highlighted reasons contributing to HCPs' delay or refusal of COVID-19 vaccines such as fear of side effects, concerns that vaccines were not being tested clinically, and the rapidly changing real-world data during the pandemic.23, 24, 25, 26 Most literature on healthcare professionals' acceptance of COVID-19 immunization is generally limited to studies of intention rather than actual uptake of the vaccines and was mostly conducted prior to vaccine approval.27, 28, 29, 30, 31 However, compared to the few studies sharing a similar timeframe and capturing the actual uptake of COVID-19 vaccines by HCPs, a higher proportion of pharmacists in the current study had no plans to get vaccinated. Those studies showed 0%, 8%, 9%, and 10% hesitancy among different HCPs, including nurses, clinicians, doctors, dentists, and pharmacists.26,32, 33, 34 Also, three states included in this study (AL, MS, AR) rank among the 10 lowest states for the number of fully vaccinated people against COVID-19.35 As such, perhaps, vaccine hesitancy in southern states is not limited to the general public; but rather affects pharmacists as well. While this study did not specifically inquire about reasons why pharmacists were unvaccinated against COVID-19 or undecided about vaccination, it is important to consider the timeframe, which was conducted within 6 months of the first U.S. vaccine rollout. Pharmacists' concerns about the vaccines may have been more prominent at that time.

Consistent with previous literature linking previous seasonal influenza vaccination with increased acceptance of other vaccines, this study revealed that pharmacists who had previously received the influenza vaccine were more likely to receive the COVID-19 vaccine.27,36, 37., 38, 39 However, it is important to note that the context of the pandemic likely influenced pharmacists' vaccination attitudes. In 2020, heightened concerns about the potential co-occurrence of flu and COVID-19 and severity of symptoms, may have driven pharmacists to seek the seasonal influenza vaccine.40

Overall, pharmacists have shown a high willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines. This willingness was notably higher among those who perceived significant benefit from offering COVID-19 immunization services at their practice sites and who have already been vaccinated against COVID-19. This is similar to findings from a study on vaccine intention and advocacy among pharmacists and pharmacy technicians in Qatar.37 Community pharmacies play a pivotal role as the most accessible healthcare setting for most people in the U.S., especially those residing in rural areas where pharmacies may be the only healthcare facility available. Thus, pharmacists' views on vaccines can significantly influence patients' acceptance and uptake.41 Survey data from APhA conducted between May 1–8, 2021, revealed that 88.0% of pharmacists were fully vaccinated against COVID-19, with 98.0% expressing comfort in discussing vaccine confidence and addressing vaccine hesitancy concerns with patients and others.21 Furthermore, HCPs' confidence in vaccines often correlates with their own vaccination behaviors. A national survey of HCPs (doctors, nurses, public health employees, medical technicians, and hospital administrators) from China found that HCPs' high confidence in COVID-19 immunization was associated with their intention and actual uptake of the vaccines.42 Similarly, a survey of HCPs from Kuwait (20.2% pharmacists) showed a positive association between HCPs' confidence in the COVID-19 vaccine's effectiveness and their acceptance of the vaccine.33 As a result, it can be problematic if rural pharmacists have concerns about COVID-19 vaccines, as they may convey their concerns to their patients, not discuss the vaccines, and/or not recommend them to their patients or people in their social networks.

The positive perceived benefit of offering COVID-19 immunization services at the pharmacy for both the pharmacy and pharmacists was associated with willingness to recommend and administer COVID-19 vaccines. Immunization serves as a valuable source of additional revenue for pharmacies, derived not only from vaccine administration but also from other services that customers may request during their pharmacy visits for immunization.43,44 Additionally, offering immunizations allows pharmacies an opportunity to distinguish themselves from other pharmacies and enhance their contributions to improved public health.44, 45., 46 Pharmacists' strong perception of the benefits of offering COVID-19 vaccines in the pharmacy is also tied to pharmacists' confidence and advocacy of the vaccines, as discussed previously.

Limitations

Limitations of this study should be considered. The generalizability of findings is restricted by convenience sampling. The sample size was small. The questionnaire was pilot-tested with urban pharmacists to maintain the sample size. Furthermore, RURAL-CP network pharmacists (34.0%) may differ from those outside the network in their familiarity with research participation and access to information. Additionally, the Hays Directories may not contain the contact information of all pharmacies leading to mistakenly excluding eligible pharmacies. Results are prone to recall bias, as self-reported responses were collected, and social desirability bias. This study measured COVID-19 vaccine acceptance and can not be generalized to other vaccines. Lastly, policies about COVID-19 are evolving quickly, and changes to the current findings are to be expected.

Conclusion

This study adds to the growing body of literature on COVID-19 immunization. Specifically, the current study reports on rural community pharmacists' acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccines after vaccine approval. Pharmacists who were vaccinated against COVID-19 themselves, representing the majority, were more willing to recommend and administer the COVID-19 vaccines to others. Pharmacists' confidence in the COVID-19 vaccines can influence the public's vaccination decisions in rural communities helping close the rural-urban vaccination gap. These results emphasize the importance of engaging community pharmacists in preparedness and response efforts for future pandemics and public health crises.

Future research could expand on these findings by comparing vaccine acceptance among rural pharmacists nationwide with that of their urban counterparts. Finally, to address the immunization disparity gap, valuable contributions could be made by exploring how rural community pharmacists' acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccines affects their advocacy for the vaccines and designing and evaluating interventions to enhance pharmacists' vaccine confidence.
