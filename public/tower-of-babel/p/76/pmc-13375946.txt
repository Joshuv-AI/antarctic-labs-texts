Periodic limb movements of sleep: A comparison of REM and NREM
Authors: Daniel A Barone, Bella Pathak, Haoyang Li, Ana C Krieger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIt is known that periodic limb movements of sleep (PLMS) impact various sleep parameters. Utilizing polysomnographic (PSG) data from a large set of retrospective records, we aimed to identify and describe differences in sleep efficiency comparing PLMS occurring in both rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and non-REM (NREM) sleep with PLMS occurring only in NREM sleep.MethodsThis was a retrospective analysis of consecutive full-night PSGs performed on adults (≥18) presenting for the clinical evaluation of insomnia, snoring, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), PLMS, rapid eye movement (REM) behavior disorder (RBD), and other sleep disorders, at one institution, over a period of three years. The database of records was queried and the records were assembled into one of three groups: 1. Controls (those without notated sleep disorder diagnoses), 2. PLMS in NREM (those with PLMS found only in NREM sleep), and 3. PLMS in REM/NREM (those with PLMS found in both REM and NREM sleep). The PSG parameters of each group were then compiled and statistically evaluated.ResultsA total of 4110 records were examined; 1635 were labeled as Controls, 1012 as PLMS in NREM, and 528 as PLMS in REM/NREM. Compared to PLMS in REM/NREM (76.1 ± 16.0%) and Controls (79.5 ± 17.3%), there was a significant reduction in sleep efficiency in PLMS in NREM (70.1 ± 19.5%) (p < 0.05). There were significantly more PLM arousals in both groups compared to Controls (0.5 ± 1.7/hr); the PLMS in NREM group demonstrated the highest index (2.5 ± 4.2/hr), followed by PLMS in REM/NREM (2.1 ± 3.8/hr).ConclusionsIt seems that the PLMS in which occur in NREM sleep may play a role in reducing sleep efficiency, perhaps through elevated PLM arousals, as compared to PLMS that occur in both REM and NREM sleep. Future research should consider these factors and explore longitudinal outcomes to understand better the long-term impact of PLMS.

Introduction

Periodic limb movements of sleep (PLMS) are sleep-related phenomena described as periodic episodes of repetitive, stereotypical movements of the limbs [1,2]. The presence of PLMS increases with age but are frequently comorbid with other sleep disorders and other medical, neurologic, and psychiatric disorders, and can be triggered by medications and psychoactive substances [1,2]. PLMS typically occur during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep; while generally absent during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, PLMS are still sometimes noted, without a clear explanation or etiology [3].

Despite the fact that PLMS can disrupt sleep architecture and lead to daytime fatigue and cognitive impairments [4], their clinical relevance is somewhat controversial: they may be seen in various sleep disorders, such as narcolepsy, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) [5,6], excessive fragmentary myoclonus [7], NREM sleep parasomnias, sleep-related eating disorder, and REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) [5,8] but can also be found in healthy participants.

However, studies have indicated that patients with elevated PLMS often have elevated arousal indices and shorter sleep durations than healthy controls [9]; additionally, the presence of PLMS is associated with increased risks of hypertension and cardiovascular events [10,11]. Furthermore, the presence of PLMS in REM sleep may be related to a worsening neurologic outcome (i.e. in Parkinson's disease or multiple sclerosis [MS]) or simply an unexplained phenomenon which belies current understanding [3].

Our study was designed to evaluate polysomnographic (PSG) parameters that may differentiate PLMS occurring in NREM sleep only versus those found in both REM and NREM sleep. We hypothesize that the presence of PLMS in both REM and NREM sleep will reduce sleep efficiency more than that seen with PLMS in NREM alone. Utilizing a large set of retrospective data, we aimed to identify PSG changes that may provide a further understanding of PLMS in general and add to the literature regarding PLMS in other disease states specifically.

Methods

The institutional review board at Weill Cornell Medical College approved this protocol. This was a retrospective analysis performed on consecutive full-night PSG data taken from adults (≥18) at the Weill Cornell Medicine Center for Sleep Medicine, Jan 2021 through Jan 2024, as part of the clinical evaluation of insomnia, snoring, OSA, PLMS, RBD, and other parasomnias.

Sleep stages, respiratory events, arousals, and PLMS were scored by a certified sleep technologist following the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) criteria [12], utilizing electroencephalogram (EEG; two frontal, two central, and two occipital channels, referred to as the contralateral mastoid); electrooculogram, electromyogram (EMG) of the submentalis muscle, EMG of the right and left tibialis anterior muscles, respiratory signals, effort signals for thorax and abdomen, oximetry, capnography, a single-lead electrocardiogram, video, and audio recording.

Parameters measured included apnea–hypopnea index (AHI), representing the number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep; periodic limb movements of sleep index (PLMS index), representing the total number of periodic limb movements per hour of sleep; periodic limb movements of sleep arousal index (PLMS arousals index), representing the number of arousals caused by PLM events per hour of sleep; sleep efficiency (%); and total sleep time (TST), in minutes.

Once compiled, the database of records was queried for sleep-disorder-related terms, including the following: “PLMS,” “PLMS in REM,” “OSA,” and “RBD.” From there, the records were assembled into one of three groups: 1. Controls (those without notated OSA, PLMS, or RBD), 2. PLMS in NREM (those with PLMS found only in NREM sleep), and 3. PLMS in REM/NREM (those with PLMS found in both REM and NREM sleep). The PSG parameters of each group were then statistically evaluated including sleep efficiency and PLM arousal index.

Statistical testing strategy

1.Shapiro–Wilk Test (Normality Check) [13].

Shapiro–Wilk Test (Normality Check) [13].

Before selecting between parametric and non-parametric tests, we used the Shapiro–Wilk test to evaluate whether the data in each group followed a normal distribution. This is necessary because ANOVA assumes normality in each group. If any group exhibited non-normality (p < 0.05), the assumption was considered violated.

2.Levene's Test (Homogeneity of Variance) [14].

Levene's Test (Homogeneity of Variance) [14].

We performed Levene's test to assess whether the variance of each variable was equal across the three groups. This is another core assumption of ANOVA, and if violated (p < 0.05), the robustness of ANOVA results may be compromised.

3.One-Way ANOVA (Parametric Global Test)

One-Way ANOVA (Parametric Global Test)

The one-way ANOVA test was used to compare the mean values of each variable across the three groups. This test assumes that the data are approximately normally distributed within each group and that group variances are equal.

4.Kruskal–Wallis Test (Non-parametric Global Test) [15].

Kruskal–Wallis Test (Non-parametric Global Test) [15].

Given the violations of normality and variance homogeneity in our data (as indicated by Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests), we primarily relied on the Kruskal–Wallis test, a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA that does not require normality or equal variances. Kruskal–Wallis tests whether the median values differ across groups and is more robust to skewed distributions.

5.Dunn's Test with Bonferroni Correction (Pairwise Post-hoc Testing) [16,17]

Dunn's Test with Bonferroni Correction (Pairwise Post-hoc Testing) [16,17]

When the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated significant group-level differences (p < 0.05), we conducted Dunn's test for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Dunn's test is the non-parametric equivalent of Tukey's HSD and is suitable after a significant Kruskal–Wallis result.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the largest retrospective analyses of PLMS in the medical literature comparing their presence across sleep stages. in NREM sleep with REM and NREM sleep. The PLMS in NREM sleep demonstrated the lowest sleep efficiency and had the most PLM arousals when compared to both Control group the PLMS in NREM/REM group. Extrapolating this data, it seems that the presence of PLMS in REM may serve to preserve sleep efficiency to some degree. Thus, our hypothesis that the presence of PLMS in NREM/REM sleep would reduce sleep efficiency more than PLMS in NREM was incorrect. These findings are somewhat surprising and inexplicable, and serve to highlight the degree with which PLMS are incompletely understood.

PLMS occurring in REM sleep has been described sparsely in the medical literature [18]. In one notable study, patterns of EEG, and cardiac activity associated with PLMS in both NREM and REM sleep were ascertained from PSG recordings of 14 participants [19]. Autonomic activation, expressed by heart rate increase, took place before the motor phenomenon both in REM and NREM sleep, but it was significantly earlier during NREM. In NREM sleep, PLM onset was heralded by a significant activation of delta EEG, but no significant activations of delta EEG were found in REM sleep [19]. Differences in HR activation and EEG between REM and NREM sleep suggested a differentiation in arousal response in these two groups [19]. Other data regarding the comparison of PLMS in NREM versus PLMS in REM sleep has been described in patients with spinal cord injury [20], cervical spondylosis, MS, and spinal vascular attack [21]. Typically, the presence of PLMS in REM sleep is thought to be related to a worsening neurologic outcome (i.e. in Parkinson's disease or MS) [3].

One factor that bears mentioning is the difference in AHI between the groups. A connection between PLMS and low arousal threshold has been identified [9], in which low arousal threshold was defined by AHI, minimal oxygen saturation, and hypopnea fractions [22]. In a recent study of 1008 adults with moderate-to-severe OSA, the association between OSA-PLMS demonstrated a less collapsible upper airway, better muscle recruitment ability, and lower arousal threshold compared to OSA-only [23]. This may have impacted our findings insofar that the PLMS in NREM group, which demonstrated the highest AHI (although not statistically significant when compared to the PLMS in NREM/REM group), had the most PLM arousals and the lowest sleep efficiency. (See the Limitations section for more information.)

Aside from this potential link, there does not exist a satisfactory explanation in the medical literature for these novel findings, and future prospective studies will be needed to provide further insight.

Limitations

There are several important limitations regarding this study. Firstly, the retrospective nature of this analysis may mask other potential confounders; medication use (dopamine agonists, benzodiazepines, or antidepressants), and comorbid conditions (metabolic or neurological disorders) were not explicitly controlled for, which may influence the results as these factors could have a significant impact on multiple sleep parameters. There is growing evidence that antidepressants can increase sleep-related movements, including PLMS, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) [24]; worsening sleep quality may be related to the impact of antidepressants on induction of PLMS [25]. Also, there were no measures of subjective sleep quality, such as Epworth Sleepiness Scale or Insomnia severity scale.

Our grouping was based on diagnostic criteria entered into our database; the absence of clinical evaluation may have masked the presence of other important sleep disorders such as insomnia [26]. Furthermore, the PSG parameters reported on in this study were somewhat limited. However, a recent descriptive, comparative cross-sectional study including 70 participants diagnosed with PLM disorder (PLMD, defined as greater than 15 PLMS/hr) evaluated similar parameters including sleep efficiency, total sleep time, respiratory disturbance index RDI, PLMS index and PLMS arousals index [27].

While research describing the relationship between PLMS and OSA certainly exists, the relationship is not completely understood [28]. In our cohort, the presence of OSA may have impacted these results regardless of the presence or absence of PLMS. A recent retrospective analysis of 581 adults found that the coexistence of PLMS and OSA was associated with a significant reduction in total sleep time and a marked increase in awakenings [29]. A recent paper highlights this phenomenon: the authors conclude that the presence of OSA in patients with PLMD does not substantially worsen sleep fragmentation, sleep efficiency, and sleep architecture, beyond the disruption already attributed to PLMD alone [27]. Other studies have found the relationship to be more confounding, particularly when treatment of OSA with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is applied; for example, an increase [30], decrease [31], or no change in the number of PLMS [32] have all been described. Furthermore, there have been descriptions of an increase in PLMS being more likely among patients with more severe OSA [33], and among those with a lower baseline body mass index or baseline level of PLMS [34].

Importantly, the presence of sleep disordered breathing (SDB) was only measured by the presence of AHI; this is a relevant limitation because of the association that exists between PLMS and upper airway resistance syndrome (UARS) [35]. In a prior study, 70% of PLMS with arousals correlated with breathing events due to increased effort in UARS [35]. Therefore, our measurement of SBD being limited to AHI may have impacted the results. In short, while both SDB (treated and untreated) and PLMS can impact sleep quality [29], the impact in our cohort is not entirely clear, and future prospective studies will be required.

Our analyses were based on single-night PSG recordings; although considered reliable for diagnostic purposes, it may fail to capture night-to-night variability in individual patients [36]. Additionally, the presence of PLMS associated with obstructive respiratory events (respiratory-related leg movements [RRLM]), can mimic true PLMS, potentially confounding the findings, particularly in severe OSA [37].

A final potential issue is absence of a “true” control group. This omission restricts the ability to fully contextualize the impact of PLMS on sleep efficiency and arousal patterns. A non-PLMS, non-OSA group was not included in the original design, and future studies including a “true” control group would help better characterize the sleep-related burden attributable to REM vs NREM PLMS [27]. Ultimately, there were multiple factors not explicitly controlled for, which potentially confounds the results of our study. However, this was designed to be a preliminary evaluation, with large numbers of participants, and the novel findings warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

Our present study adds to the growing literature regarding the impact that PLMS, both in NREM and REM, may have on sleep health. Future studies may be able to further demarcate when exactly the presence of PLMS in REM sleep is a concerning sign versus a potentially protective one [3]. Research should explore longitudinal outcomes to understand better the long-term impact of PLMS, both in REM sleep and in all sleep stages.
