Predictors of pediatric tuberculosis disease in a pastoralist community, Turkana County, Kenya: a case-control study
Authors: James Marcomic Maragia, Jepchirchir Kiplagat, Onyango Dickens, Lazarus Odeny, Stephen Olubulyera, Ongaki Dominic, Vallerian Karani, John Jamada, Job Okemwa, Jeremiah Okari, Benson Ulo, Fredrick Odhiambo, Maurice Owiny, Abade Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global public health challenge, with 10.8 million cases and 1.23 million deaths reported in 2023. Children comprised 11% of these cases and deaths globally. In Kenya, approximately 77,000 TB cases were reported, with pediatric TB contributing to 9.6% of all cases and 4.8% of TB-related deaths. Turkana County, a pastoralist region, accounted for 19% of the national pediatric TB burden. The specific drivers of this high pediatric TB rate in Turkana remain unclear. Therefore, we conducted a study to identify predictors of pediatric tuberculosis in this pastoralist community.MethodWe conducted an unmatched case-control study among children aged 0–14 years in Turkana West Subcounty, Turkana County, Kenya, from January to May 2023. A total of 106 pediatric TB cases were randomly selected from local healthcare facilities, with 212 neighborhood controls enrolled. Data collection involved structured interviews using a questionnaire covering socio-demographic characteristics, clinical history, and socioeconomic and behavioral factors. Predictors of pediatric TB were evaluated using both bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses.ResultsAmong the 318 respondents, 150 (47%) were male, and 168 (53%) were female. The mean age was 5 years (SD = 4.3) for cases and 6 years (SD = 4.3) for controls. Children with a history of household contact with TB patients in the previous year had significantly higher odds of developing TB (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 21.09; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 7.86, 56.59) compared to those without such contact. Similarly, children with a previous history of hospital admissions had three times higher odds of TB (aOR = 3.26; 95% CI: 1.30, 8.24) than those who had not been admitted. The odds of TB diagnosis were markedly elevated among children whose caregivers consumed alcohol (aOR = 6.50; 95% CI: 2.41, 17.53) compared to those whose caregivers did not. Conversely, children with caregivers knowledgeable about TB prevention measures had significantly lower odds, with a 96% reduced likelihood (aOR = 0.04; 95% CI: 0.01–0.12), of TB diagnosis compared to those whose caregivers lacked such knowledge.ConclusionPediatric TB in Turkana West Subcounty, Kenya, was significantly associated with household contact with TB patients, previous hospital admissions, caregiver alcohol consumption, and inadequate knowledge of TB prevention among caregivers. These findings highlight the nature of pediatric TB risk factors in pastoralist communities, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions. Effective strategies should focus on improving healthcare access, enhancing caregiver education on TB prevention, and strengthening infection control measures in healthcare settings to mitigate pediatric TB burden and contribute to global TB elimination efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11470-9.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a major global health issue, ranking as the 13th leading cause of mortality [1] and the second leading infectious killer after COVID-19 [2, 3]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines pediatric TB as TB infection in children aged 0–14 years [4]. According to the 2024 Global TB Report, children accounted for 12% of the estimated global TB cases and 15% of TB-related deaths in 2023. This trend, where children represent a higher proportion of TB deaths than cases, has been consistently reported across multiple years [1]. Despite accounting for 15–20% of TB cases in many countries [5, 6], pediatric TB remains underdiagnosed due to non-specific symptoms and limited sensitivity of diagnostic tools [4–9].

In Kenya, the burden of TB is high, with 77,000 reported cases in 2021, of which pediatric TB accounted for 9.6% [10]. In Turkana County, a region predominantly inhabited by pastoralist communities, the situation is particularly severe. The county reported 2,632 TB cases, with pediatric TB constituting 19% [10], significantly above the national average. The high burden of pediatric TB in Turkana County suggests active TB transmission within the community [11], potentially due to the unique lifestyle and socio-economic challenges faced by pastoralist populations.

Pastoralist communities in Turkana County are characterized by mobility, communal living, and limited access to healthcare and education, creating an environment conducive to TB transmission. Turkana is also among Kenya’s most economically marginalized regions, with over 79% of the population living below the poverty line and average monthly household income estimated at KES 2,500–3,000 (USD 20–25) (KNBS 2019). Previous studies have identified various predictors of pediatric TB, such as malnutrition, close contact with TB patients, and socio-economic factors [7, 12–20]. However, these predictors may differ in pastoralist settings due to the distinct lifestyle and healthcare barriers.

Methods

Study design and settings

This study employed an unmatched 1:2 case-control design to investigate predictors of pediatric TB in Turkana West sub-county, Turkana County, Kenya. The case-control approach was selected due to its efficiency in examining rare outcomes and its suitability for retrospective assessment of exposures. Turkana West is an arid and semi-arid region in northwestern Kenya, inhabited predominantly by pastoralist and nomadic populations. Turkana West sub-county alone accounts for more than 45% of the reported pediatric TB cases in Turkana County [21]. Environmental factors such as drought, prolonged heat exposure, and limited healthcare infrastructure contribute to high TB prevalence in the area. Immunization coverage in the county is suboptimal, with 71.9% of children reported as fully immunized and 97.9% receiving BCG vaccination [22]. Additionally, the HIV/TB co-infection rate among children is approximately 15.7%, and malnutrition remains a critical public health challenge [10, 23].

The study received ethical approval from the Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital/Moi University Institutional Research Ethics Committee (FAN: 0004355) and research clearance from the Kenya National Council for Science Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI/P/23/24082). Given the low literacy levels in the community, verbal informed consent was obtained from caregivers following a comprehensive explanation of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits.

Study population

The study population comprised children aged 0 to 14 years and their caregivers, all residing in Turkana West sub-county. A TB case was defined as any child aged 0–14 years diagnosed with TB (clinically or bacteriologically) and actively on TB treatment between January and May 2023, consistent with WHO pediatric TB definitions. As this was a retrospective study, cases were ascertained through routine facility records: bacteriologically confirmed TB from laboratory registers (GeneXpert, smear microscopy, or culture) and clinically diagnosed TB from clinician documentation in TB treatment registers and patient charts. Active treatment status was verified from the treatment register. A control was defined as a child in the same age range and location who did not present with cardinal TB symptoms, including persistent cough, fever, failure to thrive, or night sweats.

Children who were seriously ill at the time of data collection were excluded to prevent additional health risks and to ensure the reliability of responses. Additionally, children without a caregiver present to provide consent were excluded.

This approach assumed an odds ratio of 2.1, a 20% prevalence of exposure among controls [9], a 95% confidence level, and 80% power. Sampling and recruitment procedure

Case recruitment

Cases were identified from the TB treatment registers of the five selected facilities in Turkana West during the abstraction period (January–May 2023). A total of 178 pediatric TB cases were recorded across the facilities. From these, 106 cases were included in the study based on proportional allocation per facility to ensure representation across the sub-county. To achieve the sample size of 106 cases, a line list for all paediatric TB was generated, each case in the line list was assigned a number then a random number was generated using open epi. Research assistants, accompanied by community health promoters, explained the study in detail and obtained verbal consent and assent from caregivers and minors respectively before enrolling participants. A structured questionnaire was administered to the respondents to ascertain the predictors for paediatric TB; however, clinical information (for cases) was collected from the registers.

Control recruitment

Controls were recruited from the same communities as the cases using a systematic household sampling method. For each case, a random direction was determined using the pen-spinning technique. Households in the selected direction were visited sequentially. If a household did not have an eligible child or declined participation, the next household in the same direction was approached. In cases where multiple eligible children lived in a household, one was randomly selected by casting lots. An adult household member aged 18 years or older provided permission for the selection of a child participant. This approach ensured that controls were comparable to cases in terms of geographic location and community context.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire, which had been pre-tested for clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehensiveness. The tool was administered by trained research assistants fluent in English, Kiswahili, and the local dialect, Nga’turkana. Community health volunteers introduced the research team to households to facilitate trust and engagement.

For children unable to respond due to age or health status, caregivers provided responses on their behalf. For older children, data were collected directly with caregiver guidance when needed. Clinical information for cases was abstracted from TB treatment registers and cross-verified with medical records to ensure accuracy.

The questionnaire captured a range of variables, including sociodemographic, clinical, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors. Sociodemographic data included age, sex, caregiver education level, household size, and healthcare access. Clinical factors such as HIV status, malnutrition, and immunization history were documented. Behavioral exposures included caregiver alcohol consumption and smoking, both of which were categorized as binary variables; alcohol use was recorded as “Yes” if any consumption was reported, regardless of quantity, while smoking was categorized as current, former, or non-smoker without recording the number of cigarettes or duration of use.

Knowledge of TB transmission and prevention was assessed through structured questions exploring awareness of aerosol transmission, respiratory hygiene, vaccination, and common misconceptions. To ensure data quality, completed questionnaires were checked daily for completeness, and follow-up visits were conducted when data were incomplete.

Data management and analysis

Data were coded and cleaned prior to analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA version 14.1. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the study population characteristics. Differences between cases and controls were examined using appropriate comparative statistics.

To identify predictors of pediatric TB, stepwise logistic regression was employed. Variables with a p-value of less than 0.05 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounding. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify associations between exposures and TB status. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, both cases and controls were recruited from the same community, focusing the analysis on individual and household-level predictors rather than community-level or structural determinants of pediatric TB. Factors such as differences in healthcare infrastructure, environmental exposures across communities, or broader socioeconomic disparities could not be evaluated in this design. Future research employing multi-community or regional comparisons is recommended to better understand the role of broader contextual and structural risk factors for pediatric TB.

Additionally, the study excluded severely ill children and those without caregivers, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. These excluded groups could have unique socioeconomic, clinical, or demographic characteristics that differ from those included in the study. As a result, our analysis primarily reflects risk factors among non-severely ill children with available caregivers, potentially underrepresenting the most vulnerable pediatric populations, who may be at an even higher risk for TB or associated complications. Children without caregivers were excluded to ensure accurate reporting of health and TB exposure history. While this may have introduced some selection bias, as these children might differ in exposure risk, it was essential for data reliability and internal validity.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 318 children aged 0 to 14 years were enrolled in the study, including 106 cases of pediatric TB and 212 community controls. Among the cases, 58% were male, while the control group was predominantly female (58%). The mean age was comparable between cases and controls, with cases averaging 5 years (SD ± 4.3) and controls 6 years (SD ± 4.3). Caregivers of children with TB were more likely to be unmarried (73%) compared to caregivers of controls (25%). Hospitalization in the previous year was also more common among cases, reported by 56% of participants in this group versus 24% of controls.

Characteristics of pediatric TB participants

Behavioral and socioeconomic factors

Almost all respondents (97%) reported that they sought medical care when feeling ill. However, significant differences were observed in knowledge about TB. Only 15% of cases correctly identified airborne transmission as the primary route of TB spread, compared to 79% of controls. In contrast, 89% of cases reported knowledge of TB prevention measures compared to 25% of controls. This apparent discrepancy reflects how prevention knowledge was assessed in the questionnaire; participants commonly cited actions such as BCG vaccination, good nutrition, prompt health-seeking behavior, and avoiding contact with individuals who are coughing as preventive measures, even if they did not fully understand the mechanics of TB transmission.

Caregiver behaviors also differed between groups. Alcohol consumption among caregivers was reported in 49% of cases compared to just 8% of controls. Smoking was similarly more prevalent among caregivers of cases, reported by 20%, compared to 5% of controls. The majority of participating households (89%) reported a monthly income above USD 10, although the overall socioeconomic status remained low across both groups (Table 2; Fig. 1).

Independent predictors of pediatric tuberculosis

In bivariate analyses, multiple factors were found to be associated with pediatric TB, including male sex, contact with a TB case in the past year, recent hospitalization, lack of knowledge about TB transmission and prevention, caregiver alcohol use, caregiver smoking, and sleeping outside the house.

After adjusting for confounding factors in multivariate logistic regression, three variables remained significant independent predictors of pediatric TB. Children who had contact with a known TB case in the past year were over twenty times more likely to develop TB compared to those with no known exposure. Recent hospitalization also emerged as a strong predictor, suggesting either increased vulnerability to TB due to underlying health conditions or the possibility of healthcare-associated exposures. Additionally, children whose caregivers consumed alcohol had a substantially higher risk of TB, highlighting the role of household-level behavioral and social factors in shaping pediatric TB risk.

These findings indicate that pediatric TB in Turkana West sub-county is driven by a combination of clinical exposures, caregiver behaviors, and household living conditions. Addressing these determinants is essential for developing targeted interventions and improving TB control efforts in similar high-burden, pastoralist settings.

Discussion

This study examined factors associated with pediatric tuberculosis among children aged 0 to 14 years in Turkana West sub-county, Kenya, a setting characterized by pastoralist livelihoods, high mobility, and limited access to healthcare. The findings provide important insights into the social, behavioral, and clinical factors linked to TB in this unique population.

A history of close contact with a TB case was strongly associated with pediatric TB, consistent with prior research emphasizing household exposure as a primary risk factor for TB disease in children [9, 12, 25, 26]. This highlights the need to strengthen contact tracing and preventive treatment strategies [25, 27, 28], particularly in pastoralist communities where frequent movement complicates follow-up and continuity of care. Enhanced community-based TB screening and outreach could help address these challenges [29–33].

Recent hospitalization also emerged as a factor associated with pediatric TB, reflecting potential exposure to TB in healthcare settings. This aligns with studies from Ethiopia [34] and Kenya [35], which have documented nosocomial TB transmission in overcrowded, poorly ventilated facilities. Improving infection prevention and control measures in healthcare settings is critical to mitigate this risk [35–37], especially in resource-constrained regions where healthcare-associated exposures may be overlooked.

Caregiver alcohol consumption was significantly associated with pediatric TB. This finding suggests that caregiver behaviors may indirectly increase children’s TB risk by affecting health-seeking patterns, delaying diagnosis, or increasing household exposure to TB through high-risk social interactions. Alcohol use is also linked to weakened immunity, which may contribute to disease susceptibility within the household environment. These results point to the importance of integrating TB prevention with interventions addressing caregiver substance use in similar settings [38–40].

Knowledge of TB transmission was notably lower among caregivers of cases than controls, particularly regarding airborne spread. However, knowledge of general TB prevention measures—such as vaccination, nutrition, and early treatment-seeking—was higher among caregivers of cases. This paradox likely reflects post-diagnosis education at TB clinics, where caregivers of cases received information after their child’s diagnosis. In our multivariable analysis, knowledge of TB transmission was not retained as an independent factor associated with TB, indicating that other exposures had stronger predictive value. Nonetheless, community education programs focusing on transmission routes remain essential for early identification and prevention [41–44].

This study has several limitations. First, both cases and controls were sampled from the same community, which limits the ability to assess broader community-level determinants of TB, such as structural barriers to healthcare access or regional environmental exposures. However, this approach was chosen to maximize internal validity and ensure that controls were geographically and contextually comparable to cases, minimizing selection bias. Second, the study excluded severely ill children and those without caregivers, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to non-severely ill children living with caregivers. These exclusions, while necessary for ethical and logistical reasons, could have led to underrepresentation of particularly vulnerable subpopulations. However, to reduce potential underrepresentation, we included all eligible children with caregivers across the five TB treatment facilities and their surrounding communities. Third, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall bias, particularly for behavioral factors such as alcohol use or knowledge assessments. However, we used a standardized, caregiver-administered questionnaire and cross-verified clinical and treatment information with TB registers, improving data reliability.

Future research should consider longitudinal studies to better examine causal pathways linking caregiver behaviors, healthcare exposures, and TB outcomes in children. Incorporating advanced diagnostic tools, such as molecular testing for latent TB infection, would also improve accuracy in identifying pediatric TB cases and contacts. Additionally, expanding studies to include multiple communities would help assess the role of structural and environmental factors beyond the household level. Future studies should consider incorporating qualitative methods, such as Focused Group Discussions or In-depth Interviews, to provide deeper insights into caregiver behaviors that may not be fully captured through questionnaires.

In conclusion, this study identifies household TB contact, recent hospitalization, and caregiver alcohol consumption as key factors associated with pediatric TB in Turkana West sub-county. These findings suggest that targeted interventions—such as strengthening contact tracing, improving infection control in healthcare settings, integrating caregiver support programs, and enhancing TB education—are crucial for reducing the pediatric TB burden in pastoralist communities. Addressing these factors is essential for advancing toward national and global TB control goals, particularly in underserved and high-risk populations.
