A fuzzy logic model for taxi service assessment using passenger survey data from Ukraine and Poland
Authors: Ievgen Medvediev, Yuliia Kyrii, Olena Smyrnova, Dmitriy Muzylyov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTaxi service quality is traditionally assessed using objective metrics like waiting time, cost, and comfort. However, contemporary research confirms that subjective psychological factors significantly influence service perception. This study develops an interpretable Mamdani fuzzy inference system (TEC model) that maps three passenger psycho-emotional factors—Trust, Empathy, and Control—into an interaction level (0–100) and a corresponding 1–5 star rating for taxi trips. The model is parameterized using a questionnaire survey in Ukraine (n = 118) and Poland (n = 101) and examined through a pilot case study of 10 real trips in Kharkiv and Gdansk. In the pilot, the model-based star ratings were largely consistent with app ratings; the relative error was 6.67% and is reported as a descriptive indicator only. The framework is intended for offline diagnostic assessment and scenario-based improvement planning rather than real-time prediction. Further validation on larger and more diverse samples is required to quantify generalizability. The novelty of this research lies in translating a passenger’s subjective psycho-emotional perceptions into quantitative indicators, which increases the reliability of taxi service quality assessment. The results are significant for taxi services and the logistics of their operations, providing a deeper understanding of the psychological factors that influence ratings. Furthermore, this approach can be used by digital taxi platforms for more effective service management. The work demonstrates the potential of fuzzy logic to improve predictive accuracy, develop personalized strategies, and create a foundation for intelligent decision support systems to enhance taxi services in European cities.Keywords: Fuzzy logic, Passenger-driver interaction, Taxi service quality assessment, Psychological human factors in transportation, Trust, Empathy, Control, Ride-hailing platformsSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The demand for increased urban mobility is a growing trend, with both public passenger transport and various taxi services playing a significant role in addressing this need1. The role of taxis becomes especially crucial in extreme conditions2, where public transit is unable to operate at full capacity. This can be due to a variety of factors, including a carrying capacity that falls short of demand during large-scale events, restrictions related to public health regulations like those for COVID-19, or security concerns such as missile and UAV threats3.

Beyond their role under disrupted public transport, taxis and ride-hailing services are unique alternatives, offering door-to-door accessibility and privacy with minimal transfers. This makes them comparable to private car travel, appealing to users who prioritize convenience, privacy, and reliable timing. Research from Eastern Europe indicates that private vehicle users value security, relaxation, flexibility, and comfort4,5, highlighting the importance of psycho-emotional factors in taxi services.

Eastern Europe is a particularly informative setting for studying taxi trust because institutional legacies, regulatory heterogeneity, and market informality shape how passengers form and rely on trust. In such societies, people often evaluate institutions based on observable performance and interpersonal cues rather than on automatic confidence6. In taxi services, this increases the role of driver-level signals and platform safeguards such as identity verification, ratings, and visible safety practices when assessing safety and service quality7,8. As a result, trust, empathy, and perceived control become especially important drivers of perceived safety and willingness to use taxi services in this context9.

In normal circumstances, a key aspect of a taxi ride is not merely its cost and comfort but also its psychological component. It is this psychological dimension that is crucial for establishing the link between service level and the nuanced interaction between the driver and the passenger10. Ultimately, the result of this interaction is reflected in the rating the passenger gives for the trip, while the driver, in turn, evaluates the passenger’s behaviour as either “appropriate” or “inappropriate”11. Studying these interactions is vital, as it allows for a deeper understanding of the behavioural dynamics of both parties and the factors that contribute to friction during the trip.

The very existence of these open questions highlights the complexity of the issue, particularly given the dynamic psychological factors that can differ significantly from one trip to another.

Previous research has repeatedly addressed this topic. For example, Askari et al.12 evaluated drivers’ behavioural conduct from a passenger’s perspective, focusing on driving practices and professionalism. This focus on evaluation suggests a desire to understand how the level of passenger safety contributes to the overall quality of the service provided. Similarly, Verma et al.13 used communication levels to assess not just a single trip, but the overall service quality of an entire taxi service, specifically Uber and Lyft.

Previous studies have shown a somewhat limited psychological perspective when evaluating the driver-passenger interaction. For instance, the work of Isaeva et al.14 primarily prioritized the role of trust, which is only one part of the psychological dynamic during a trip. In a broader context, recent research across several European countries—including Finland15,16, Germany17, and others18—indicates a general decline in trust toward the taxi industry. This trend can be attributed to both misunderstandings that arise during the trip itself19 and a perceived deterioration in the overall service quality provided by taxi companies20–22.

The true uniqueness of each taxi ride stems not from the random vehicle or driver, but from the trip’s underlying psychological dynamics. The interaction dynamics are shaped by psycho-emotional characteristics of both participants, which are ultimately reflected in the passenger’s post-trip rating of service quality23. While the collection of such statistical information is standard practice for modern taxi services to track passenger satisfaction24. However, the utility of this data is often limited, as its analysis rarely moves beyond a cursory overview. The collected information is typically not leveraged to model the underlying relationships between a trip’s psychological components and the dynamics of the driver-passenger interaction. Yet, finding such a relationship may hold the key to understanding the erosion of trust in taxi services; more broadly, to defining what truly constitutes a high-quality service.

Our research synthesizes the results of a survey conducted among taxi service users in Poland and Ukraine. Through this approach, we objectively evaluated the psycho-emotional dynamics of a taxi ride from the perspective of a representative passenger sample. The data collected in this manner provides a more accurate dataset for developing our fuzzy model. This methodology enhances the model’s precision, allowing it to perform a proactive diagnostic assessment the interaction level within the “driver-passenger” system and, consequently, predict a passenger’s service quality rating. In this paper, “proactive assessment” is used in an ex-ante and preventive sense rather than a real-time one. After the mapping is calibrated, it can be applied offline to anticipate the risk of low ratings in future trips and to support targeted improvements (training, safety practices, platform safeguards).

Although previous studies have linked service quality and ratings to behavioral and psychological factors, they often consider these constructs separately and rely on post-hoc associations. This approach lacks an interpretable rule-based model that can translate passengers’ psycho-emotional perceptions into quantitative indicators for ex-ante diagnostic assessment. To address this gap, an interpretable Mamdani fuzzy inference system was proposed. It maps three normalized passenger constructs (Trust, Empathy, and Control) onto an Interaction Level and an expected rating. This mapping was also validated through a pilot-trip case study.

Hence, the contribution of this paper is an interpretable, rule-based TEC mapping from passenger perceptions to an Interaction Level and expected rating profile, designed for proactive diagnostic use after calibration.

The study addresses the knowledge gap in the field by using a new fuzzy model to establish a mathematically sound relationship between the psychological factors of a trip (trust, empathy, and control) and the level of driver-passenger interaction.

Literature review

This literature review analyzes current research by focusing on the influence of three key psychological components on taxi service and the interaction within the “passenger-driver” system. This focused review aims to identify existing knowledge gaps regarding how these emotional factors impact the level of interaction and, consequently, a passenger’s perceived service quality.

To systematize the review, a structured search and screening process was employed, focused on taxi and ride-hailing and passenger-driver interaction, and psychological factors influencing perceived service quality and ratings. We searched Scopus and Web of Science, supplemented by Google Scholar for recent “online-first” items, using keywords like taxi, ride-hailing, rating, trust, empathy, and service quality. Peer-reviewed articles and high-quality policy reports were included that operationalize at least one TEC construct in a passenger context, excluding studies limited to operational metrics without a behavioral component. Finally, backward and forward snowballing were utilized from relevant papers to ensure comprehensive coverage of current research.

Trust during the trip

When traveling by taxi, trust between the passenger and driver is a key psychological factor that influences comfort, service quality, and safety25,26. This is especially relevant in Eastern Europe, where passengers may rely more on interpersonal and platform-based cues when institutional assurance is perceived as less predictable. A recent Ukraine-focused study on ridesharing during crisis conditions explicitly identifies trust, perceived behavioural control, perceived safety, and emotions as key factors and uses SEM to test causal relations among them, supporting the general relevance of psychological constructs for mobility modelling under uncertainty27. Such trust dynamic also affects trip cost, driver behavior, and passenger loyalty, particularly in the context of events like the COVID-19 pandemic10,28,29. Some studies have used Structural Equation Modelling (SEM) to assess service quality based on trust30. However, these analyses often rely on online application data, which omits the direct emotional component that emerges during the trip itself. Despite this limitation, the use of online taxi services is projected to increase, largely due to a loss of public trust in mass transit31.

While SEM is widely used to test confirmatory causal structures among latent constructs in transportation planning4,32, we use these SEM-based findings here to motivate the selection of key psycho-emotional constructs (e.g., trust) rather than to estimate a parametric SEM in this study. The proposed links are therefore treated as a conceptual structure that informs the rule base, whereas the mapping is implemented via a fuzzy inference system to preserve interpretability under linguistic and nonlinear relationships.

Trust, as a key emotional component, is not solely the prerogative of the passenger; it is a bidirectional psychological category also evaluated by the driver. This is particularly relevant in the context of autonomous driving, where a driver’s trust can be assessed in relation to situational awareness33 or their interaction with autonomous vehicles (AVs)34,35. Furthermore, the list of parameters influencing trust expands significantly with emerging innovations in the taxi industry, such as automated mobile services36 and robo-taxis37. These technologies introduce new variables that complicate the psychological characteristics of a trip, requiring a broader assessment of trust.

Trust is a multidimensional psychological construct in the context of taxi services. It’s evaluated not only in relation to the driver and the trip itself but also to the ride-hailing service (RHS) that provides the booking38. In this regard, trust is a critical factor that determines whether a client will continue to use a specific service39.

Despite its importance, measuring trust in a taxi context is inherently challenging due to its fuzzy nature40,41, as it’s heavily influenced by the emotional states of both the passenger and the driver. Trust is a powerful predictor of future passenger behaviour, determining whether a customer becomes a repeat or one-time user11,42. A high level of trust can lead a client to remain loyal to a specific service, while low trust may cause them to switch platforms entirely43.

Several studies have investigated the parameters of trust that influence a passenger’s willingness to take a trip, particularly in ride-sharing scenarios with other clients44. Furthermore, the significant role of social systems in influencing trust has been emphasized45. However, a major limitation is that the reviewed works fail to establish a fundamental mathematical relationship between trust parameters and the level of trust itself. This prevents existing approaches from being universally applicable for assessing a passenger’s trust in a taxi driver.

Empathy during the trip

The SERVQUAL model46 is a foundational framework for studying service quality, identifying empathy as one of the key dimensions. In the context of taxi and ride-sharing services, this factor reflects a driver’s ability to understand and address a passenger’s individual needs with personal attention. A driver’s capacity for empathy is directly correlated with the passenger’s overall service perception and satisfaction.

A number of studies demonstrate that empathy plays a central role in the perception of service quality. For example, Frinaldi et al.47 found that a driver’s behaviour directly impacts passenger satisfaction. Furthermore, elements of interpersonal interaction, including emotional engagement during conversation, can strengthen trust in the service provider. These findings are also supported by research on e-ridesharing, where empathy and personalized attention significantly increase user satisfaction with digital platforms48.

However, the significance of empathy in the transportation process remains a subject of scholarly debate. For instance, a study in China by Yao and Ding49 found that passengers rated empathy as the least significant service quality criterion when compared to factors like reliability or assurance. This suggests that the perception of empathy’s importance may depend on an individual’s cultural background and their understanding of this emotional category. Similarly, Andreassen50 highlighted that despite the acknowledged need for individual attention, taxi operators in practice often minimize or completely disregard empathy.

Recent scholarly works link empathy to distinct cognitive and emotional mechanisms. Kissi51 notes that cognitive empathy indirectly moderates the relationship between data analysis and service quality, whereas emotional empathy directly strengthens this connection. This finding redefines empathy not merely as an interpersonal skill, but as a critical element of strategic quality management in the context of a taxi ride.

The psychological aspect of driver behaviour is another crucial area of focus. Studies indicate that a driver’s emotions are directly linked to their on-road behaviour52, and a lack of empathy can increase the likelihood of aggressive reactions in hazardous road situations53–55. Concurrently, the concept of the “empathetic car” has emerged56, where emotion analysis is used to enhance trip comfort and safety. This development intersects with the growing field of “Artificial empathy,” which is already being applied in technologies like chatbots57,58.

The socio-communicative context is also a factor: studies show that a driver’s openness and empathetic behaviour foster deeper passenger trust59. However, as noted by Wieseke et al.60, research on empathy in service interactions is still limited, which creates practical methodological difficulties.

The analysis of current research on the influence of empathy in taxi travel confirms its dual role. On the one hand, empathy enhances a passenger’s perception of service quality and fosters a degree of loyalty toward the taxi service. On the other hand, the significance of empathy as an emotional component of a trip varies greatly depending on an individual’s cultural background and understanding of its context. Crucially, there are no established mathematical models for quantifying empathy’s influence on the level of interaction between the passenger and driver. This key finding validates the chosen research direction.

Control during the trip

The psychological factor of control during a taxi ride is a critical dimension that directly influences a passenger’s trust, sense of safety, and overall satisfaction. According to research, the ability to exert control over a trip reduces anxiety, enhances the perception of comfort, and fosters positive interactions with the service provider61–63. Indirect forms of control compensate for the passenger’s lack of direct influence over the vehicle’s operation: route selection, real-time trip tracking, driver communication, and the ability to monitor driving style. This ensures informational transparency of the driver’s actions throughout the journey, thereby reducing feelings of uncertainty and discomfort, a concept consistent with the cognitive model of passenger discomfort64. Moreover, evidence from app-based ride-hailing shows that inbuilt security features (driver and vehicle identification, trip traceability, and journey-sharing options) can increase perceived safety and trust, effectively strengthening passengers’ perceived control in uncertain contexts65. Recent evidence from Poland indicates that taxi service development is strongly shaped by platformization and user-facing determinants such as comfort, safety, and the availability of mobile applications and cashless payment options, which are directly linked to perceived control and reassurance in the service experience66.

A passenger’s psycho-emotional discomfort is often linked to a perceived loss of control and insufficient information67. This is particularly critical for vulnerable groups, such as women, for whom the perception of safety is directly correlated with a sense of control68. However, most existing models fail to account for how information about a driver’s intentions and behaviour can reinforce a passenger’s sense of control and, consequently, reduce anxiety.

A key issue in existing research is their predominant focus on drivers’ behaviour and technological aspects, often neglecting the subjective experience of passengers69,70. Drivers, for example, have limited control over external factors like traffic jams71,72, signal synchronization (a “green wave”)73, and frequent changes in traffic flow74,75. On the other hand, a lack of information about a driver’s profile diminishes passenger trust and their sense of control during both the service selection process and the trip itself76. This suggests that many service providers do not give enough attention to the transparency of driver profiles, including their ratings and biographies, which ultimately impairs the passenger’s perception of control.

A taxi trip in an unfamiliar city presents a unique challenge, often generating negative emotions for tourists due to their limited local knowledge and a subsequent lack of control over the journey. When drivers are dishonest or impolite (e.g., charging an inflated fare or taking a longer route), tourists experience negative emotions that lead to unfavourable future behaviours, such as avoiding repeat trips or leaving negative reviews77. However, the influence of these negative risks—stemming from the passenger’s lack of control—on the driver’s own behaviour during the trip remains largely unexplored.

Most existing models view the perception of control as a static construct, while in reality, it is dynamic, changing based on the driver’s behaviour, the quality of communication, and external environmental conditions78. Furthermore, the field of psychology suggests that empathy and trust can mitigate stress and strengthen the perception of control, even in the face of an objective lack thereof79.

Summary of literature review

Given the lack of established mathematical relationships between interaction levels and emotional categories, the use of a fuzzy model is a promising approach. From an SEM perspective, we can create a measurement model linking questionnaire items to latent constructs (TEC) and a structural model for predicting relationships among constructs, including direct and indirect effects. In our framework, Trust, Empathy, and Perceived Control serve as input factors, Interaction Level is the intermediate link, and the rating is the outcome. This aligns with recent SEM applications in transportation after establishing a measurement model and estimating structural relationships. However, our focus is on interpretable, rule-based mapping using fuzzy inference rather than linear path coefficients. Therefore, we operationalize constructs through normalized factor scores, with SEM estimation planned for future extension. Hence, the fuzzy model proposed in this research accounts for the influence of trust, empathy, and subjective control on the passenger-driver interaction level. This approach is particularly well-suited to describing vague psycho-emotional categories, such as “moderate trust” or “low empathy,” which are challenging for classical models80.

A critical analysis of existing research demonstrates that, without accounting for key psychological factors, such studies remain incomplete. A fuzzy model based on trust, empathy, and control provides a more accurate assessment of interaction quality by including both the rational and emotional aspects of a trip. This research is particularly relevant as most existing work considers the psychological dimensions of a trip primarily from the driver’s perspective. In our study, the main focus is on accounting for the passenger’s emotional categories during their journey.

Moreover, prior evidence from Eastern Europe shows that private vehicle user satisfaction stems from security, relaxation, flexibility, and comfort5. These factors align with psycho-emotional constructs, where security and relaxation relate to trust and perceived control, while flexibility and comfort are enhanced through empathetic service and transparent information that foster perceived control in travel.

The interaction level (Ilevel) is therefore based on three primary psychological factors: Trust (T), Empathy (E), and Control (C). These three factors were chosen for their direct impact on taxi service encounters. Trust reflects safety and reliability; empathy addresses interpersonal treatment; and perceived control conveys passenger agency through information and options. This streamlined set aids in transparent rule design, while broader factors like satisfaction, mood, and pricing are seen as contextual influences rather than core drivers. These factors will be used to construct the TEC Model, which is designed to determine the level of interaction between a passenger and a taxi driver. Additionally, recent studies provide valuable insights into the quality of taxi and platform services, but they typically view ratings either as a direct evaluation after a trip or as influenced by specific service attributes. They often overlook the psychological and emotional factors that play a role during the trip itself. In contrast, the TEC framework offers a compact explanatory structure that connects three key elements:

Participants and ethical considerations

The empirical data were collected using an anonymous questionnaire (Google form) administered to taxi passengers in Ukraine and Poland. Participation was open to adults (≥ 18 years) who had used taxi services at least once during the previous 12 months. No direct personal identifiers (such as names, phone numbers, e-mail addresses, vehicle registration numbers or ride identifiers) were collected. The dataset analyzed in this study contains only survey responses and general, non-identifying demographic information (age group, gender, city of residence, and employment status), which were recorded on an optional basis to characterize the sample. Thus, the sample represents taxi users (passengers) reporting their own service experience rather than a panel of domain experts.

The information statement clarified that participation was entirely voluntary, responses would remain anonymous, and the data collected would be used exclusively for research purposes. Additionally, it was mentioned that the questionnaire was an original instrument developed by the authors. Contact details for the corresponding author were provided so that potential participants could ask questions before or after completing the survey.

Before the survey started, participants were informed that this study was a collaboration between Gdańsk University of Technology in Poland, Volodymyr Dahl East Ukrainian National University in Kyiv, and State Biotechnological University in Kharkiv. The aim was to analyze interactions between taxi drivers and passengers to improve taxi services.

The study protocol was approved by the Rector’s Committee for Research Ethics at Gdańsk University of Technology (reference no. RN 143/2025; approval issued on 3 November 2025). It involved a fully anonymous, non-interventional questionnaire survey of adult taxi passengers in Poland and Ukraine, without medical procedures or personal health data collection. All procedures followed relevant institutional and national guidelines.

Additionally, the study complied with the Ukrainian legal framework for non-interventional social and economic research (Law of Ukraine “On Scientific and Scientific-Technical Activities,” No. 848-VIII, adopted on 26 November 2015). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was implied by the completion of the questionnaire.

At the beginning of the survey, all potential participants were provided with an information statement and indicated their willingness to participate by proceeding to complete the questionnaire. Therefore, the completion and submission of the questionnaire implied informed consent. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Advantages of a fuzzy logic approach

Fuzzy logic represents the most suitable approach for analyzing psycho-emotional factors. This is because fuzzy models describe data using linguistic categories like “high trust” or “moderate empathy” through membership functions and expert rules81. This mathematical framework does not require strict statistical data and accommodates the overlapping nature of subjective states. It also maintains a high degree of interpretability, which is crucial for studies with small, subjective datasets.

Unlike neural networks, which excel in accuracy with large volumes of data82, fuzzy logic offers a distinct advantage in transparency (a “white-box” approach) and stability when dealing with limited samples83.

Probabilistic models, such as Bayesian networks and regression analysis, are effective tools for formalizing stochastic uncertainty84–87. However, they come with several limitations. These models require a priori assumptions at the axiomatic level and can be challenging for experts to interpret, as they are based on a statistical sample rather than expert input. Additionally, they are highly sensitive to the quality of the initial data.

Therefore, fuzzy logic represents an optimal choice for determining the passenger-driver interaction level. This is due to its use of linguistic variables, the robustness of fuzzy models with limited datasets, and the high explainability of the input parameters88.

Passenger survey sample characteristics

The passenger survey was conducted from May to June 2025 (Appendix A). It included two groups of taxi passengers: Ukraine (n = 118) and Poland (n = 101). The age range for Ukrainian respondents was 18–69 years, while Polish respondents ranged from 18 to 70 years. The primary geographical locations for the Ukrainian group included Kharkiv, Dnipro, Kyiv, Kryvyi Rih, and Lviv. The Polish respondents were primarily from the cities of Wloclawek, Gdansk, Gdynia, Poznan, and Tczew. Although the sample includes students (including transport-related students), all respondents were treated as passengers (end-users) rather than expert judges.

The survey findings from the Trust section reveal a high level of confidence among passengers toward both the service and its drivers, although the degree of trust varies depending on the specific context.

A significant majority of respondents demonstrated a high degree of confidence in the service’s operational aspects. Specifically, 68.64% expressed trust in the proposed route, which suggests a positive perception of the service’s benevolence, competence, and integrity. A similarly high percentage (69.5%) believed that most drivers perform their work with honesty and professionalism. This trust extends to flexibility, as over 74% of respondents agreed to a driver’s proposal for an alternative route when necessary, indicating a low level of resistance to change and an openness to collaboration.

This pervasive trust is further evidenced by a general feeling of security, with 75.42% of respondents agreeing they feel safe during their trip. The core of such trust is the willingness of one party to be vulnerable to the actions of another, based on the subjective expectation that the other party will act with good intentions. In this context, passengers demonstrate a high level of vulnerability, believing the driver will act in their best interest regardless of direct control.

While foundational trust is high, the data on managing delays presents a critical area of weakness. A substantial minority (39%) of respondents reacted negatively to trip delays, a finding that stands in stark contrast to the higher trust levels observed elsewhere. This may be attributed to a low tolerance for uncertainty, a high value placed on time, or negative prior experiences. Conversely, 32.20% of respondents demonstrated a calm reaction, suggesting high stress resilience and psychological adaptability. The high percentage of neutral responses (28.81%) indicates a segment of passengers whose reaction is not clearly defined and may be influenced by the quality of communication during the delay. The low tolerance for delays represents the most significant challenge to overall passenger loyalty, making it a priority for service improvement.

The findings from the Empathy Section demonstrate a prevailing positive attitude toward drivers and the service. The data reveals a high degree of empathy among respondents, though the extent to which this empathy is actively expressed varies depending on the specific behaviour. A significant majority of respondents (92.37%) consistently exhibit a polite attitude toward the driver, regardless of their personal mood. This suggests a high level of self-control and a strong inclination toward courteous communication, which is a foundational aspect of professional interaction. This is supported by the fact that 83.9% of respondents show understanding toward the driver when road circumstances change, indicating a high degree of receptivity to external factors. Furthermore, a vast majority (83.05%) agree that a driver’s job is demanding and strenuous, which points to a high level of cognitive empathy and an understanding of the profession’s nature.

While a majority of respondents show passive empathy through understanding and politeness, the readiness for active, direct communication is more varied. A significant majority (69.49%) of respondents indicate a tendency to pay attention to the driver’s emotional state, but a substantial neutral group (26.27%) suggests that this awareness is not universal. Furthermore, only 56.8% of respondents actively maintain a conversation with the driver, while a considerable number (35.6%) remain neutral or prefer not to engage (7.6%).

Overall, the results indicate that passengers possess a strong baseline of empathy, which is most consistently expressed through behaviours such as politeness and situational understanding. While direct conversational engagement is less prevalent, the collective findings suggest a general willingness to cooperate and a high level of psychological awareness, both of which are critical for fostering a more harmonious passenger-driver relationship.

The survey results from the Control Section demonstrate that passenger behaviour is not governed by a single model but rather by a spectrum of approaches, with a clear preference for direct, communicative control over passive or digital methods.

A strong majority of respondents actively engage in behaviours that assert control during a trip. This is most evident in situations involving direct interaction with the driver and financial transparency. Specifically, a remarkable 61.86% of respondents clarify the trip cost or payment method beforehand, underscoring a strong desire for predictability and a high level of responsibility in managing financial terms. Similarly, a high percentage of users proactively address uncertainties: 72.88% seek clarification from the driver in unclear situations, and 69.49% inquire about sudden route changes. These findings indicate that passengers prefer to mitigate risks in real-time through direct communication, viewing it as a primary mechanism for ensuring their safety and avoiding conflict.

In contrast to the high levels of communicative control, passenger attitudes toward technical monitoring and pre-trip verification are more divided. A significant portion of respondents (38.98%) actively follows the route on a navigator, a behaviour often linked to a lack of trust or a preference for personal orientation. However, an almost equal number (27.12%) do not follow the route, relying instead on a high level of trust in the service.

The survey findings from the Trust section reveal a high level of confidence among passengers toward both the service and its drivers. This trust is evident across several key indicators.

Specifically, 75.25% expressed trust in the proposed route, while 71.29% believed that most drivers perform their work with honesty and professionalism. The low percentage of negative responses across these areas (ranging from 4.95 to 8.91%) further validates a high baseline of trust in the competence, integrity, and professionalism of the service and its drivers.

The neutral group, in particular, represents a segment whose trust is potentially vulnerable to poor communication and a lack of additional information.

Overall, the results confirm that passenger trust is built on three key components of Mayer’s trust model: competence, integrity, and benevolence. The findings underscore the importance of transparency, reliability, and clear communication in maintaining and strengthening passenger trust in taxi services.

The survey findings reveal a high degree of empathy among respondents, although their readiness for active engagement varies. The results show that passengers demonstrate a strong capacity for understanding and psychological awareness in the context of taxi services.

Specifically, a vast majority of respondents (86.14%) showed understanding toward a driver when road circumstances changed, and 76.23% recognized that a driver’s job is both challenging and fatiguing. This indicates a high level of cognitive empathy, where passengers can mentally and emotionally grasp the driver’s professional realities. Furthermore, a remarkable 88.11% consistently reported treating the driver with courtesy, regardless of their own mood. This finding points to a high degree of self-control and a strong preference for pro-social behaviour in the passenger-driver relationship.

Despite this high level of passive empathy, the data suggests a more nuanced approach to active engagement. Only a bare majority of respondents (55.44%) pay attention to the driver’s emotional state, while 28.71% remained neutral and 15.84% did not value this aspect. Moreover, only a minority of respondents (25.74%) are willing to engage in conversation, with a significant 41.58% actively choosing not to communicate. This suggests that while passengers may possess empathy, they do not always feel the need to express it through direct interaction. The large neutral groups in these areas indicate a segment of the population whose behaviour is likely situational and could be influenced by external factors or a deliberate decision to maintain personal space.

Overall, the findings collectively point to a high level of emotional intelligence among respondents, who are able to understand and appropriately react to the emotional states of others and to changing circumstances. However, the data also highlights a key area for future development, as a significant portion of respondents exhibits a limited readiness for direct communication and emotional interaction.

The survey results demonstrate that taxi service users exhibit varying degrees of proactive behaviour and control during their trips. The findings reveal a clear preference for a high level of communicative and financial control, while attitudes toward technical monitoring are more varied.

A strong majority of respondents actively engage in proactive control measures. Specifically, 78.22% of respondents verify the trip cost or payment method beforehand, while 80.20% seek clarification from the driver in unclear situations. This behaviour underscores a strong desire for predictability and a high level of responsibility in managing service conditions. Similarly, 63.36% of users proactively inquire about sudden route changes, indicating a preference for open communication to mitigate the risk of misunderstandings.

In contrast, passenger attitudes toward technical monitoring and pre-trip verification are more divided. Only 21.78% of respondents trust the proposed route and do not monitor it via a navigator, with a substantial 51.48% believing that a potential issue with route control needs to be addressed. This divergence is also evident in attitudes toward pre-trip checks: 36.63% of users check a driver’s reviews and rating to mitigate risks, while a nearly equal number (35.64%) disregard these checks entirely. This heterogeneity suggests a lack of a single, unified approach to risk assessment and highlights a potential area for service improvement.

Overall, while most users display active control behaviours, a significant minority exhibits a passive approach. This passivity, particularly in situations involving route monitoring and driver verification, could expose them to a higher risk of misunderstandings or conflict, reinforcing the need for both services and users to be aware of and adapt to these behavioural differences.

Trip evaluation based on interaction level

The results of our fuzzy model are formalized into a practical service rating, which directly correlates with how taxi services establish their own ratings or a driver’s specific rating.

Our proposed approach to trip evaluation is based on integrating three factors of passenger-driver interaction: Trust, Empathy, and Control. An integrated interaction score, expressed as a percentage, is derived from a survey of 15 questions grouped into three blocks. This score is then interpreted using a system of defined linguistic levels: Low (up to 20%), below medium (up to 40%), medium (up to 55%), Above medium (up to 70%), High (85% and above).

The results from the fuzzy model are then mapped to a familiar star-rating system, where the number of stars corresponds to the level of passenger-driver interaction. This methodological approach not only formalizes a passenger’s psycho-emotional state during a trip but also enables the use of these insights for the practical evaluation of taxi service quality (see Fig. 18).

Pilot case study and proof-of-concept validation for the TEC model

Pilot case-study

Increasingly, taxi companies use ratings as a key performance indicator to ensure high service quality. After each trip, a mutual rating system is used, allowing for evaluations between both the passenger and the driver. This system is said to foster an atmosphere of mutual respect on platforms like Uber95, FREE NOW96, and Bolt97.

Comparing service-level ratings with the model’s output allows us to validate the fuzzy model’s predictive accuracy. This comparison helps determine how well the model can predict service ratings based on passenger-driver interaction and the psycho-emotional factors of the trip: Trust, Empathy, and Control. Consequently, our field research in this case study is crucial for validating the proposed fuzzy model, which assesses the level of passenger-driver interaction by accounting for these emotional aspects.

For the case study, we selected ten taxi users, evaluating five trips in Ukraine (Kharkiv) and five in Poland (Gdańsk) to ensure a diverse sample. This case study is designed as a pilot proof-of-concept. Its purpose is to verify the feasibility of the full algorithm (survey-based inputs—fuzzy inference—mapping to a star rating) across two urban contexts, rather than to provide statistically significant estimates of predictive performance. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Consistent with the methodology described in Section “Framework and methodology", a survey was conducted after each trip. This survey consisted of five questions for each of the three psychological categories: Trust, Empathy, and Control. The survey results were used to determine the percentage values for each factor, which then served as quantitative input data for our fuzzy model. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale (Appendix A). To obtain adequate inputs for the fuzzy model, each response x was linearly rescaled to a percentage p using p = (x–1)/4·100, where 1 corresponds to 0% and 5 maps to 100%. The input value for each factor (trust, empathy, control) equals the arithmetic mean of the five rescaled items in the corresponding block. For presentation, Table 1 reports these inputs rounded to the nearest 5%. Here, percentages are used only to place the ordinal 1–5 responses on a common 0–100 scale required by the fuzzy membership functions. The mapping is monotonic and preserves the original ordering and equal spacing of the 5-point scale. It does not imply a physical ‘percentage’ measurement. The rating for each trip, calculated by the fuzzy model, was based on the methodology outlined in Section “Trip evaluation based on interaction level". The user’s actual trip rating was provided independently through the taxi app. To prevent any influence on the passenger’s actual rating, the survey was administered only after the user had already submitted their rating, ensuring the integrity of the field experiment. Accordingly, the present study evaluates the model in a post-trip diagnostic mode. Proactive use would rely on the calibrated mapping to assess future trips before the final rating is submitted.

Discussion

In this section, we discuss the modelling results for passenger-driver interaction based on Trust, Empathy, and Control. We also address the limitations of our proposed approach and suggest directions for future research.

Result and validity discussion

The results presented in Section “Pilot case study and proof-of-concept validation for the TEC model” provide a preliminary comparison, using a pilot sample of 10 cases, between the passenger-driver interaction level calculated by the proposed fuzzy model (FM) and the actual rating for each trip. This comparison allows us to validate the model’s performance and determine its predictive accuracy.

First, a clear correlation exists between the final interaction level and the percentage scores for Trust, Empathy, and Control, which were determined by a supplementary survey. For instance, trips with high Trust and Empathy scores (e.g., #2, #4, #9, and #10) yielded Fuzzy Model values above 88%, which matched the maximum actual ratings of five stars (*****). This confirms that emotional factors are key predictors of service quality from a passenger’s perspective.

Secondly, average input values led to corresponding average Fuzzy Model outcomes and satisfactory user ratings. For example, in trips #3, #6, and #8, where Trust, Empathy, and Control were in the 45–70% range, the resulting average interaction values (56–63%) aligned with three-star ratings (***). This demonstrates the model’s consistent ability to accurately identify the relationship between psycho-emotional factors and the final perception of a trip.

Third, the results from two different countries—Kharkiv (Ukraine), and Gdańsk (Poland)—reveal similar patterns. A high level of Trust and Empathy consistently led to high actual ratings, while average factor values correlated with more moderate scores. While the pilot patterns are directionally consistent across Kharkiv and Gdańsk, this should not be taken as evidence that passengers have the same understanding of “safety” in both locations. In Kharkiv, due to wartime insecurity, the concept of “safety” may expand to encompass personal security and predictability. This can lead to a greater reliance on observable cues from the driver and platform safety measures when establishing trust and a sense of control. Therefore, we view the comparison between the two cities as preliminary and generating hypotheses. To properly assess the effects of context and the comparability of measurements, a larger follow-up study is necessary.

One pilot trip (Trip No. 1) shows disagreement between the model-based rating and the user’s app rating. This discrepancy may reflect normal noise in subjective ratings and/or contextual factors not captured by the current inputs. We report this case transparently as part of the pilot evidence and treat it as motivation for a larger validation study. In addition, the current proof-of-concept design does not include explicit war-related context variables (e.g., exposure to air-raid alerts, curfews, or recent security incidents) that may shape perceived control and trust. Future validation will incorporate such moderators and test whether the meaning and strength of TEC relationships vary across stable vs. conflict-affected environments.

In conclusion, the conducted case study confirmed the theoretical adequacy and practical applicability of the proposed fuzzy model. This model provides an interpretable mapping that is useful for diagnosing passenger-driver interaction quality and supporting forward-looking (offline) proactive improvement in the taxi service markets of Ukraine and Poland.

Survey results discussion

In this section, we discuss the quality of the survey based on the statistical results presented in Section “Statistical analysis: respondents in Ukraine". To assess this, we use the coefficient of variation (CV), a metric that shows the degree of dispersion of the respondents’ answers relative to their mean100. This evaluation is logical because the survey was conducted among taxi users in two different countries. The coefficient of variation (CV) will provide insight into how the opinions of respondents in Poland and Ukraine correlate with each other. To determine the degree of opinion divergence for each question, we use seven predefined categories of agreement based on the CV values101

Based on the101: «Definition 1: A data (population) will be called as consistent iff 0 < CV < 33 for that data (population)» we observed a high degree of consistency for all five questions in the Trust block among both Ukrainian and Polish respondents. This suggests that the questions were clearly understood in both countries and that the measured level of Trust is comparable between the two groups. This finding may be attributed to the similar mentalities of these two neighbouring nations.

The Empathy block also falls within the acceptable range for response consistency. Specifically, all five questions for Ukrainian taxi users and four questions for Polish respondents in this block showed a satisfactory level of agreement. For Polish passengers, the Empathy question “I maintain a conversation with the driver” had a CV of 41.37%. According to Bindu et al.101, this corresponds to “weak inconsistency” (Definition 5: A data will be called as weakly inconsistent (WI) iff 33 < CV ≤ 66). This low consistency is likely due to the fact that Polish passengers are generally less talkative during trips than their Ukrainian counterparts, who tend to engage in conversation with the driver.

For Polish respondents, four of the five questions in the Control block had a CV below 33%, indicating response consistency. However, one question from this block—“I check the driver’s reviews and ratings before ordering a taxi”–had a CV of 43.78%. This is likely due to the varied psychological attitudes toward financial and material aspects among the respondents, as the sample included both students and mature individuals with different social statuses.

For Ukrainian survey participants, the questions in the Control block showed less correlation than those from their Polish counterparts. Only two of the five questions in this block demonstrated a sufficient level of response consistency. While two additional questions had CV values of 33.66% and 33.01%, respectively, placing them at the upper limit of acceptable consistency, a single question had a minor inconsistency (CV = 37.86%): “I check the driver’s reviews and rating before calling a taxi”. This is because some passengers order a taxi via phone call rather than through a dedicated app, which prevents them from accessing and reviewing driver information.

It’s important to note that achieving 100% consistency in survey responses is practically impossible due to the varied conditions of each trip. These conditions include different drivers and levels of comfort, the individual psycho-emotional state of the passenger and driver, the purpose and time of the trip, weather conditions, and other factors that directly or indirectly influence the consistency of responses.

The survey was conducted in only two countries with similar mentalities and social behaviours. This limits the generalizability of the findings to a wider range of cultural and social conditions.

An additional limitation is the subjective nature of perception; the psychological states of the respondents were not controlled or measured. Furthermore, important aspects such as trip purpose, frequency of use, and prior service experience were not analyzed in detail, all of which could also impact the perception of Trust, Empathy, and Control. To address scale adequacy, a consistency check was conducted using the CV for items within each block (Trust, Empathy, Control), confirming the coherence of the response scale in both samples. These empirical response distributions also serve as model inputs for parameterizing TEC.

Ultimately, while the model effectively captures the passenger’s perception of the trip and their interaction with the driver by focusing on key psycho-emotional factors, it doesn’t account for the multidimensional context of the trip or behavioural differences that may arise in non-standard situations.

While the proposed fuzzy model shows promising performance in the analysed sample, these limitations mean that the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive. The validation on 10 pilot trips provides an initial proof of concept, but further large-scale and long-lasting studies in diverse cultural and operational settings are required before the model can be used as a robust decision-support tool in real-time taxi service management.

Addressing these unexamined aspects will be a focus for future research.

Discussion of findings in relation to previous studies

This section examines how the findings of the current study relate to, differ from, and expand upon previous research in the areas of transportation service quality, mobility management, and logistics performance assessment. The analysis underscores how the proposed proactive assessment model enhances our understanding of taxi service performance by incorporating psychological dimensions into a quantitative evaluation framework.

The study extends existing knowledge in transportation and service logistics by addressing a fundamental gap in how taxi service performance is evaluated. Previous research has primarily concentrated on operational and service attributes such as cost, waiting time, comfort, and professionalism12,13, often leaving psycho-emotional mechanisms under-formalised. In contrast, this work incorporates emotional and cognitive dimensions—such as trust, empathy, and control—into a quantitative framework. This approach broadens the methodological foundation for assessing service quality from a human-centered perspective.

This research also complements previous studies that have applied fuzzy logic in transportation systems for demand forecasting, driver behavior analysis, and service optimization. However, unlike those works, which typically focus on operational uncertainty, the proposed proactive assessment model captures psychological uncertainty—a dimension rarely formalized in transport studies. By translating subjective passenger perceptions into measurable indicators, the study extends the application of fuzzy logic beyond technical optimization to encompass emotional and behavioral aspects of service evaluation.

In relation to studies on mobility management and intelligent transport systems, the obtained results extend prior work on digital feedback and rating mechanisms by offering an interpretable, forward-looking assessment layer10,11. While earlier studies largely rely on post-trip ratings as descriptive outcomes, our fuzzy inference framework maps psycho-emotional factors (trust, empathy, and perceived control) into an interaction index and an expected rating profile that can be used ex-ante after model calibration. In this sense, “proactive” refers to anticipating rating risk for future service scenarios and supporting targeted improvements (e.g., driver training, communication standards, and platform assurance features), rather than an in-trip real-time intervention. This interpretation aligns with findings that platform safeguards and safety features enhance perceived safety and trust by increasing passengers’ sense of control in uncertain situations65,66.

Moreover, the inclusion of empirical data from Ukraine and Poland contributes to the limited body of evidence from Eastern Europe—a region often underrepresented in behavioral transportation research. The observed consistency of passenger responses across both countries supports the cross-cultural validity of the model and demonstrates its potential applicability in other European urban mobility contexts. Hence, the application pathway using the proposed FM consist from calibration the mapping on post-trip survey data (as shown in this paper) and applying the calibrated mapping ex-ante in scenario form to identify combinations of service conditions and driver practices that are most likely to lead to low perceived interaction quality, and implement preventive measures. Real-time prediction would require collecting equivalent indicators during the trip or before rating submission and is beyond the scope of this pilot. This limitation is consistent with recent transport research showing that survey-based psychological measures are often too slow and resource-intensive to reflect rapid perception shifts. More time-sensitive proxies can be derived from digital text streams using NLP (e.g., sentiment and dynamic topic modelling) as a pathway for future real-time extensions102,103.

In summary, the present findings reaffirm earlier conclusions regarding the importance of trust and empathy in transport service perception while advancing the field through a quantitative, predictive, and psychologically grounded approach to taxi service performance assessment. This integrative methodology provides a novel contribution to the literature on transportation service quality, digital mobility management and human-centred logistics, particularly in the underexplored context of Eastern European taxi services. However, its implications should currently be understood as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a novel fuzzy-logic-based approach to assessing the level of interaction between a taxi passenger and driver by accounting for psycho-emotional factors: trust, empathy, and subjective control. Unlike traditional methods based on behavioural and service metrics, the proposed fuzzy model formalizes a passenger’s subjective perceptions of a trip and translates them into quantitative indicators that determine the trip’s rating. This approach provides a deeper understanding of interaction quality within the passenger-driver system and allows for the consideration of the variability in users’ emotional states.

The fuzzy model was developed in a MATLAB environment, which ensured accurate data processing and the effective application of fuzzy logic tools. Input data were based on passenger survey responses from Ukraine and Poland, normalized to a 0–100 scale for fuzzy-model inputs. Internal consistency checks confirmed the response scale’s adequacy for the three factors. This method ensured data comparability and its proper use in constructing the fuzzy model. The coefficient of variation (CV) values for each block of questions confirmed high consistency among respondent answers. For Polish respondents, only one question in the Empathy block had a CV of 41.37% and one in the Control block had a CV of 43.78%. For Ukrainian respondents, only one question in the Control block showed a slight inconsistency (CV of 37.86%). Overall, this validates the survey’s quality and the sufficient reliability of the results for the psycho-emotional factors. The percentage values of these factors were subsequently used as quantitatively defined input parameters for building the fuzzy model.

A case study was used to validate the developed fuzzy model based on the results of the main respondent survey. A small real-trip case study was used to demonstrate feasibility and proof-of-concept performance. Given the limited number of trips and the exploratory design, this should be viewed as preliminary evidence of the model’s predictive capability rather than a definitive validation. The sole exception was Trip No1, where the model’s value (59.47%) was lower than the actual rating (vs.*****). However, a follow-up analysis revealed that the passenger mistakenly gave the maximum rating due to being in a hurry, with the intention of giving four stars. Given the limited number of trips and the exploratory design, these results should be treated as preliminary proof-of-concept evidence rather than a definitive validation. Future work will extend validation to a larger sample and a broader range of operating conditions to quantify predictive performance more robustly.

The proposed approach has significant potential for expansion. Therefore, future research should focus on broadening the cultural and geographical scope to include regions with different social characteristics, which will help to reveal how mentality influences the perception of trust, empathy, and control. It is also crucial to integrate drivers into the sample to gain a comprehensive understanding of the passenger-driver system. A particularly promising direction is the development of evolving models that can account for the dynamic nature of interaction perception. This could involve integrating the fuzzy model with neural networks to increase the accuracy of service quality predictions.
