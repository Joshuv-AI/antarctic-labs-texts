Narrow band gap and room-temperature ferromagnetism in Cr-doped ZnO nanorods fabricated by electrochemical deposition
Authors: Mansour Mohamed, Emad K. Jaradat, Sharif Abu Alrub, Abdullah S. Alshammari, Marzook S. Alshammari, Mohammed A. Alkhunayfir, A. Sedky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study reports on the structural, optical, and magnetic properties of Cr-doped ZnO nanorods (NRs) fabricated via a one-step electrochemical deposition process. The impact of Cr3⁺ doping was systematically investigated across concentrations of 0.00, 0.1, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 mM, with the resulting samples labelled S0, S1, S2, S3, and S4, respectively. The XRD confirmed the successful substitution of Cr3⁺ into the wurtzite ZnO lattice, but with a few amounts of the ZnCr₂O₄ secondary phase obtained at 2θ = 43.5° for S3 and S4. The SEM micrographs show a significant morphological evolution from high-aspect-ratio nanorods to stubby, thickened structures. In general, the crystallite size and the ratio of diameter/length increase against Cr doping up to S3 and then decrease. The Cr-doped NRs (S1-S4) show enhanced absorption in the visible region (400–600 nm) compared to undoped S0. Additionally, the increase in the Cr leads to a decrease in the optical band gap from 3.42 eV for S0 to 3.23 eV for S4, as determined by Tauc’s plot. All NRs exhibit ferromagnetic and diamagnetic behavior at 300 K, whereas the behavior changes to ferromagnetic and paramagnetic with decreasing temperature to 10 K. In addition, the M-H curve develops a little bit of curvature between 0.00 and 3000 Oe to be likely similar to the well-known hysteresis loop of ferromagnetism. To isolate the intrinsic ferromagnetic signal, we subtracted the linear diamagnetic or paramagnetic background from the raw M-H data. The extracted ferromagnetic parameters of (Ms), coercive field (Hc), squareness (Sq), and anisotropy (γ) are obtained, and it is found that they are dependent on the chosen Cr concentration and temperature. Although most of them are affected by Cr doping, the values of Ms and γ at 10 K are higher than those of 300 K, whereas the vice is true for the Hc and Sq. The field cooling (FC) and zero-field cooling (ZFC) curves of S0 and S1 splitting across the temperature range (10–300 K). In contrast, introducing Cr drastically changes this picture since FC and ZFC curves completely overlap with each other. The Curie temperatures are between 299.95 and 299.99 K for the NRs (S0-S4). These findings establish electrochemical deposition as a viable route for fabricating Cr:ZnO NRs with narrow band gaps; paramagnetic and RTFM behaviors are tuneable multifunctional properties in visible-light optoelectronics, medical treatments, and spintronics.

Introduction

A wurtzite ZnO n-type nanoparticle (NP) of the P63mc space group is formed when the Zn and O atoms are formed in an octahedral structure1–3. The non-stoichiometry of ZnO NPs exhibits strong excitonic effects due to the introduction of some donor states of 60–100 meV below the conduction band (CB), which is usually called the Urbach tail. In addition, it reduced its wide energy gap (Eg) to about 3.37 eV4–6. However, tuning the Eg of ZnO NPs is necessary because lowering the Eg, enhancing optical absorption towards the visible region, a red shift in the absorption band edge, and increasing the carrier density are required for light-emitting diodes (LEDs), solar cells, and filters. The vice is true for devices with higher breakdown fields, lower electronic noise, and high-power operation7,8.

Numerous studies have been conducted for pure and 3D-doped ZnO NPs, demonstrating a Curie temperature of ferromagnetism at or above room temperature (RTTM). The RTFM has potential applications in spintronic devices such as magnetic memories, optical isolators, and quantum computers9–11. However, to date there is a lack of agreement regarding the origin of FM in ZnO NPs. Usually ZnO NPs show diamagnetic or poor ferromagnetic behavior at RT. Preliminary research indicated that the RTFM is due to the free carriers, oxygen vacancies (Ov), and secondary phases formed by ZnO and dopants during synthesis, which forms bound magnetic polarons12,13. Few studies show that OV creates deep-level defects at about 1.0 eV below the CB rather than being shallow donors. Some other reports attributed the weak FM of ZnO NPs to the magnetic exchange interaction between the unpaired electron spin of ZnO and OV14,15. Besides FM, paramagnetic has unpaired electrons and an orbital magnetic moment that possesses a magnetic dipole moment to be weakly aligned with the applied field and follow the well-known Langevin’s theory.

So far, lot of applications have been introduced for paramagnetic NPs, including medical imaging as MRI contrast agents, material separation in mining, and low-temperature magnetic refrigeration. They are also used in electronics and sensors, detectors and sensors, and as structural catalysts16. Diamagnetic materials are weakly repelled by magnetic fields; they have practical applications including magnetic levitation (maglev) trains, MRI contrast, sensitive sensors, and material separation. They are crucial in high-tech fields for creating stable, frictionless environments, shielding sensitive equipment, and enabling advanced material processing16,17.

Additionally, hard magnetic material with a high coercive field (Hc) can be used as permanent magnets, whereas, in contrast, the soft material of low Hc is useful in transformers, recording heads, and magnetic shielding18. Furthermore, the impact of cooling from RT to 10 K in both field cooling and zero field cooling (FC and ZFC) on the type of magnetic behavior of ZnO should be evaluated to check the Curie and blocking temperatures, if any. In addition, the shape of the curves can provide insight into the magnetic structure of the material.

The appropriate selection of transition metals (TMs) to ZnONPs for tuning their optical and magnetic properties is mainly affected by the ionic size, valence state, and magnetic moment of TMs19–23. Among various dopants, chromium (Cr) has attracted considerable attention because it usually improves the ZnO overall properties, which directed it toward more appealing technological material24,25. For example, Cr (0.62 nm) incorporation alters defect chemistry in place of Zn (0.72 nm) in ZnO NPs, influencing its optical absorption, photoluminescence, and electrical conductivity26. However, possible different oxidation states of Cr (Cr2⁺, Cr3⁺, and Cr4⁺) have different electron configurations, directly affecting its unpaired electrons, which can give different behaviors. For example, the magnetic moment of the ferromagnetic Cr ion is ~ 3.87 µB for Cr3⁺, 4.90 µB for antiferromagnetic or paramagnetic Cr2⁺ (spin-only), and 2.83 µB for half-metallic ferromagnetic Cr4⁺, reflecting changes from ideal ionic models due to metallic character or crystal field effects27. Despite these exciting possibilities, the exact role of Cr in modifying ZnO NPs’ properties remains debated, particularly concerning how different doping concentrations affect its structure, optical performance, and magnetic properties11,28,29.

It has been reported that Cr-doped ZnO NPs reduce the Eg for visible light absorption through introducing localized impurity states within the forbidden gap, creating new electronic pathways that facilitate electron transitions and then improving photocatalytic activity30,31. This happens because Cr ions substitute Zn ions, altering the local electronic structure, with the Cr 3 d orbitals mixing with O 2p and Zn 3 d states, creating a new valence band (VB) and/or conduction band (CB) edge. In contrast, the theoretical analysis of the UV–vis spectra of Cr-doped NPs reveal that the wavelength of the absorption maxima blue-shifted to a lower wavelength with increases in the Cr content32. They have attributed the increase of Eg to the decrease in the particle size of ZnO NPs to the quantum confinement effect.

It has been found that Cr-doped ZnO NPs often show diamagnetic behavior at RT because of competing magnetic exchange interactions, where the diamagnetic response of the host ZnO NPs and the possible creation of antiferromagnetic (AFM) or spin-glass-like states from Cr-Cr interactions can overwhelm weak ferromagnetic (FM) signals, especially with either higher doping of Cr or increased defects or both. This leads to a net diamagnetic or paramagnetic signature, but with few ferromagnetic domains appearing only at low fields not exceeding 5 kOe. The high doping, for example, can increase structural disorder, secondary phases of ZnCr2O4, and defects, promoting antiferromagnetic coupling or spin freezing, which can mask ferromagnetism even at higher fields and result in a diamagnetic-dominated signal at RT, as reported33–36.

The mechanism behind RTFM in Cr-doped ZnO NPs continues to be actively debated in the literature, and this ongoing disagreement highlights critical gaps in our understanding of how synthesis conditions and defect chemistry influence their magnetic ordering. This study pioneers the use of electrochemical deposition for synthesizing Cr-doped ZnO nanorods, leveraging this method’s unique advantages for controlled doping, precise potential-dependent control of Cr3⁺ incorporation, low-temperature processing that preserves defect complexes, and tunable nanorod morphology through electrolyte engineering. As the first electrochemical growth of this material system, we systematically characterize how these synthesis advantages translate to enhanced structural, optical, and magnetic properties. For instance, Cr-doped ZnO NPs prepared by the facile co-precipitation method show RTFM36, whereas plasma-deposited films reveal oxygen partial pressure-dependent ferromagnetism37. Hydrothermal synthesis further suggests Zn interstitials mediate ordering via bound magnetic polarons (BMPs) with Cr3⁺38.

However, the previous results of magnetism based on Cr-doped ZnONPs are in conflict with each other, but in general, most of them indicated that Cr-doped ZnO NPs should exhibit weak RTFM, which is mainly useful insights into the discipline of spintronics. Therefore, the present study aims to expand the particular studies on the optical and magnetic properties of Cr-doped ZnO nanorods (NRs). We have chosen Cr as our dopant material for the host ZnO, and electrochemical deposition was used for pure and Cr-doped ZnO NRs. As the first electrochemical growth of this material system, we systematically characterize how these synthesis advantages affect the structural, optical, and magnetic properties of pure ZnO NRs.

While our recent work focused on co-doped ZnO nanorods39, the present study on Cr doping reveals distinctly different behavior, including lattice contraction, formation of a secondary ZnCr₂O₄ phase, surface roughening, and non-monotonic ferromagnetism, highlighting the dopant-specific nature of the structure–property relationships.

Experimental

The pure and Cr-doped ZnONRs were synthesized via electrochemical deposition using a three-electrode cell (ITO substrate as the working electrode, Ag/AgCl reference electrode, and Pt counter electrode) controlled by an Autolab PGSTAT101 potentiated with NOVA software. Prior to deposition, the ITO substrates were ultrasonically cleaned in isopropanol and deionized water. The growth solution consisted of 0.1 M zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O), 0.1 M hexamethylenetetramine (HMT), and chromium(III) nitrate nonahydrate (Cr(NO₃)₃·9H₂O) of 0.00, 0.1, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 mM concentrations, respectively, dissolved in 100 mL deionized water. Deposition was carried out at −1.0 V and 80 °C for 1 h under continuous stirring, after which the samples were rinsed with water and air-dried. The crystal structure of the samples was examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Philips PW1710) with Cu-Kα radiation of 0.15405 nm and scanning from 20° to 80° at 0.06°/s. Structural morphology of the samples was carried out using FEI Quanta 250 scanning electron microscopy (SEM). For optical measurements, suspensions of each sample (100 mg/L) were prepared in double-distilled water by sonicating for 2 min, then recorded UV–Vis spectra from 200 to 900 nm using a Shimadzu UV-2600. Magnetic properties were investigated using a SQUID magnetometer (Quantum Design MPMS 3), with field-dependent magnetization (−20 to + 20 kOe) at different temperatures of 10 K and 300 K. In addition, the magnetization against temperature is done in both field cooling (FC) and zero-field cooling (ZFC) at a field of 100 Oe and a temperature between 10 and 300 K. More precisely, the samples are first cooled down to 10 K in the absence of the field, then the field of 100 Oe is switched on, and the data are acquired for increasing temperature (ZFC) up to 300 K. After that, the samples are cooled down, and FC magnetization is acquired in the presence of the field. For simplicity, the samples are arranged relative to Cr content and called S0, S1, S2, S3, and S4, corresponds to 0.00, 0.1, 0.5, 0.7, and 0.9 mM concentrations, respectively.

Results and discussion

Morphological and structural properties

Figure 1 shows a clear evolution from uniform hexagonal NRs in the S0 sample to thicker, more densely packed, and ultimately shorter structures for S1, S2, S3, and S4 samples. This pronounced morphological evolution in one-dimensional nanostructures contrasts with the typical isotropic NPs obtained via common chemical routes like solvothermal synthesis40, underscoring the sensitivity of morphology to the specific growth kinetics of electrochemical deposition. In general, the diameter of the rods increases from 500 nm for S0 to 887 nm for S4, while the length of the NR contracts from 1730 to 1560 nm (see Table 1). This shift in aspect ratio—thickening at the expense of elongation—suggests that Cr3⁺ ions are not merely passive dopants but active modifiers of the growth kinetics. The transformation in nanorod morphology by increasing Cr concentration is not merely a surface-level alteration but a direct consequence of doping-induced modifications to the nucleation and growth kinetics during electrochemical deposition. A detailed analysis of the changes in diameter and length provides insight into the underlying mechanisms and their functional implications. Table 1 shows a clear inversion of aspect ratio with increasing Cr concentration, since S0 exhibits an aspect ratio (length/diameter) of approximately 3.46 (1730 nm/500 nm), whereas S4 has an aspect ratio of about 1.76 (1560 nm/887 nm). The aspect ratio (length/diameter) increases up to S2 and then decreases for S3 and S4. The morphological shift can be understood through classical crystal growth theory, where the final shape is determined by the relative growth rates along different crystallographic directions. For wurtzite ZnO, growth along the polar [0001] direction (c-axis) is typically favoured due to higher surface energy and faster kinetics. The introduction of Cr3⁺ ions appear to selectively suppress this directional preference while enhancing lateral growth. Several interrelated factors contribute to this doping-induced reshaping: (i) Cr3⁺ incorporation alters the surface charge distribution along different crystallographic planes. The smaller Cr3⁺ ions (0.62 Å) replacing Zn2⁺ (0.74 Å) create localized positive charge imbalances that may preferentially stabilize the non-polar (100) side facets through electrostatic interactions with growth species in solution. This stabilization reduces the surface energy differential between axial and radial directions, promoting lateral expansion. (ii) Cr species may adsorb more strongly to the polar (002) surfaces, acting as growth inhibitors along the c-axis. This preferential adsorption creates a kinetic barrier that slows longitudinal growth while radial growth continues relatively unimpeded. The increased surface roughness observed at higher concentration levels (S3, S4) further supports this mechanism, suggesting that adsorbed Cr species create nucleation barriers that disrupt smooth layer-by-layer growth. (iii) The significant lattice mismatch between Cr3⁺ and Zn2⁺ introduces compressive strain, particularly along the c-axis where the ionic substitution directly affects interplanar spacing. This strain energy may be partially relieved by expanding laterally, effectively redistributing the strain over a larger cross-sectional area. This explains why the diameter of S4 decreases to about 90% of S0 while the length increases to about 177.4%. (iv) The presence of Cr(NO₃)₃ modifies the local electrochemical environment during deposition. Cr ions can complex with HMTA or Zn precursors, changing their diffusion coefficients and adsorption characteristics. Such complex, facet-dependent interactions leading to a systematic shape transformation are characteristic of anisotropic crystal growth in nanorods and are not typically resolved in studies of isotropic NPs40. This altered mass transport likely affects the supersaturation gradients near different crystal faces, modifying the relative growth rates. Consequently, our electrochemical method provides a direct pathway to tune NRs’ aspect ratio through dopant concentration, a level of morphological control distinct from that achieved in NPs’ synthesis40.

The XRD patterns of S0, S1, and S2 confirm the hexagonal (wurtzite) structure of JCPDS 36–1451 card number without any secondary phases, indicating successful Cr3⁺ ion substitution into the ZnO lattice (see Fig. 2). The peaks labelled (*) correspond to the ITO substrates. In addition, the dominant (100), (002), and (101) line peaks exhibit a consistent shift toward higher 2θ angles with increasing Cr concentration. This confirms a contraction of the ZnO unit cell, directly resulting from the substitution of the larger Zn2⁺ ion with the smaller Cr3⁺ ion. The observed shift in the (002) line peak is especially significant, indicating a reduction in the c-axis lattice parameter. This lattice contraction is the direct source of the compressive strain hypothesized from the morphological analysis. Optical studies

The optical absorbance spectra of the pure and Cr-doped ZnO NRs are shown in Fig. 4. It is seen that the S0 sample exhibits a sharp absorption edge near the exciton wavelength of 366.1 nm for S0, which further increases till it reaches 385.4 nm for the S4 of Cr-doped samples. These values correspond to exciton energy (Eexc) between 3.22 and 3.39 eV. The Eexc is usually given by the band gap energy minus the binding energy of the electron–hole pair (Eexc = Eg – Eb), such that Eb is in the range of 0.5 eV44. The pair usually created during exciton formation attracts each other, forming a bound state (exciton) that lowers the total energy required below that needed for free charge carriers. Additionally, energy relaxation through phonons often removes excess energy, making the observed Eexc smaller than the Eg, as obtained. The observed progressive red shift in the absorption edge can be considered clear evidence for the narrowing of Eg as a result of the introduction of Cr to ZnO NRs. The observed redshift with increasing Cr concentration indicates a reduction in particle or crystalline size, which accounts for the decrease in the band gap (Eg) upon Cr incorporation into the ZnO NRs33.Fig. 4The optical absorbance and Tauc plots of pure and Cr doped ZnO nanorods.

The optical absorbance and Tauc plots of pure and Cr doped ZnO nanorods.

Additionally, the doped samples (S1 through S4) show enhanced absorption in the visible region (400–600 nm) compared to the S0, which can be attributed to d-d transitions of the incorporated Cr3⁺ ions of the defect states introduced by Cr. The absorption in this visible range strengthens with higher Cr content, with sample S4 demonstrating the strongest and broadest absorption spectrum. This suggests that a higher concentration of Cr introduces more mid-gap states and potentially greater structural disorder, significantly modifying the optical response of the material. The extended absorption into the visible light region is particularly advantageous for applications such as photocatalysis and photovoltaics, where efficient utilization of the solar spectrum is required.

This bandgap engineering is further supported by the observed morphological changes. As discussed in the structural analysis, the average diameter of the NRs increases against Cr. Larger NR diameters crystallite sizes reduce the quantum confinement effects responsible for increasing the Eg in nanostructures, where the reduction typically leads to a decrease in Eg51. Therefore, the observed Eg narrowing is likely a combined effect of the electronic modifications from Cr doping and the morphological evolution of the NRs. The introduction of defect states, such as Ov which are often associated with charge compensation for Cr3⁺ substitution, may also contribute to sub-bandgap absorption and influence the perceived bandgap. The trend suggests that Cr doping is an effective method for tuning the optical properties of ZnO NRs for specific applications. However, it is important to consider that beyond an optimal point, excessive doping could lead to the formation of defect clusters or secondary phases that might act as recombination centers for charge carriers, potentially counteracting the benefits of enhanced visible light absorption in optoelectronic applications.

Magnetic studies

The magnetization versus field (M-H) curves of pure and Cr-doped ZnO nanorods are shown in Fig. 6(a, b) at 300 and 10 K, respectively. It is seen that all samples exhibit diamagnetic behavior at 300 K such that the total magnetization at 20 kOe (Mt) slightly increases with Cr concentrations; see Fig. 6(c). The Mt is 0.013, 0.015, 0.017, 0.018, and 0.019 emu/g for S0, S1, S2, S3, and S4, respectively. In addition, the M-H curve develops a little bit of curvature between 0.00 and 3000 G to be likely similar to the well-known hysteresis loop of ferromagnetism52. This tells us that introducing Cr is adding a new magnetic component that can be switched by a field. However, even for S4, the magnetization never flattens out; it keeps increasing in its negative value of Mt. This means the underlying diamagnetism of ZnO NRs is still strong, and the new magnetic signal of Cr is relatively weak but distinct at RT. However, Cr-doped ZnO often shows diamagnetic behavior at RT because of competing magnetic interactions, where the diamagnetic response of the host ZnO NRs and the creation of antiferromagnetic (AFM) or spin-glass-like states from Cr-Cr interactions are due to unpaired electrons in their 3 d orbitals. This can overwhelm weak ferromagnetic (FM) signals, even with lower doping of Cr concentration, leading to a weak FM mix with a strong diamagnetic background, rather than strong RTFM11,29. It has been investigated that higher doping of Cr to ZnO NPs could induce secondary phases of ZnCr₂O₄ and defects for promoting antiferromagnetic (AFM) coupling or spin freezing, which can mask ferromagnetism and result in a diamagnetic-dominated signal at 300 K, which is consistent with those obtained by XRD, since some of ZnCr₂O₄ could be observed for S3 and S4.Fig. 6(a,b) Magnetization against magnetic field at 300 and 10 K for pure and Cr doped ZnO nanorods.(c) Total magnetization at 20 kOe Cr concentration for pure and Cr doped ZnO nanorods.

(a,b) Magnetization against magnetic field at 300 and 10 K for pure and Cr doped ZnO nanorods.(c) Total magnetization at 20 kOe Cr concentration for pure and Cr doped ZnO nanorods.

In contrast, with decreasing temperature to 10 K, the behavior changed to paramagnetic with enhancement of Mt values to 0.351, 0.429, 0.483, 0.535, and 0.497 emu/g, respectively, but the magnetization curves keep rising linearly at the highest fields. In addition, the hysteresis loops’ area and M-value become much larger for all Cr-doped samples (S1-S4), compared to S0. Surprisingly, even the undoped S₀ shows a small measurable hysteresis at 10 K, which is ascribed to the defects or surface states common in nanomaterials. This is a key observation: it means our samples contain two types of magnetic contributions, ferromagnetic and diamagnetic, at 300 K and ferromagnetic and paramagnetic at 10 K. The mixed magnetic contributions occur because some Cr3⁺ ions remain isolated due to low doping levels and uneven distribution. Additionally, defects like Ov create their own paramagnetic centers almost at a low temperature of 10 K, as obtained30. These vacancies act as intrinsic point defects that, when trapping electrons, form paramagnetic centers called F⁺-centers-mediated coupling in NPs oxides, such that they contain unpaired electrons responsible for paramagnetic behavior. Anisotropic surface F- centers are so unstable as to be destroyed with higher temperatures. Even at 300 K, the behavior is diamagnetic53,54. The ferromagnetic order at low fields may be attributed to the undestroyed or residual Ov, since the ferromagnetic behavior is mainly controlled by these vacancies in metal-oxide NPs. These factors even at 10 K prevent the complete spin alignment, maintaining some disordered moments alongside the dominant ferromagnetic response. The system’s complexity with competing magnetic interactions and defect sites naturally leads to this coexistence of ordered and disordered magnetic states. This contrasts with the purely ferromagnetic behavior at RT reported for Cr-doped ZnO NPs synthesized by co-precipitation45. Similar behavior has been obtained in Zn₁₋ₓCrₓO NPs with x ranging between 0 and 0.05 synthesized by the sol–gel technique36. Although ZnO NPs exhibit diamagnetic behavior, Cr-doped ZnO samples exhibit weak ferromagnetism to anti-ferromagnetism of 0.004 emu/g optimum maximum magnetization, relative to the content x. Another reported dataset based on Cr-doped ZnO NPs has indicated that Cr-doped samples exhibit ferromagnetic properties11,46,55. One of them found RTFM of 0.06 emu/g at 10 kOe, whereas the other exhibited RTFM with an increase of Ms from 0.0078 to 0.0092 emu/g, corresponding to an increasing coercive field (Hc) from 64 to 85 Oe. Our strange point here was directed to their lower values of Ms of 0.0011, 0.0016, and 0.012 emu/g obtained at RT for 0, 0.02, and 0.04 of Zn₁₋ₓCrₓO NPs. However, we found that their M-H curves were limited up to a maximum field of 5 or 10 kOe. In addition, the M-H curves show a curvature towards negative values once the field exceeds the 3 kOe, which is evidence for diamagnetic behavior, as we obtained. These previous data prompted us to examine the M-H curves from zero up to 20 kOe, which are available for us here. Our interesting point here is the appearance of a hysteresis loop at low fields ​​not exceeding 3 kG, as reported.

To isolate the intrinsic ferromagnetic signal, we subtracted the linear diamagnetic (and paramagnetic) background from the raw M-H data and showed it in Fig. 7 (a, b). The extracted parameters of Ms, remanent magnetization (Mr), coercive field (Hc), squareness (Sq = Mr/Ms), and anisotropy (γ = HcMs/0.98) are shown in Fig. 8 (a-d) against Cr concentration and also compiled in Table 456,57. At 300 K, S1 exhibits the highest Ms value of 0.0023 emu/g, but it drops to 0.0017 emu/g for S2 and then plateaus around 0.0012 and 0.0013 emu/g for S3 and S4. This trend indicates that the ferromagnetic coupling between Cr3⁺ ions is most effective at the lowest value of Cr concentration. The initial decrease in Ms suggests that higher doping introduces factors that disrupt optimal magnetic exchange. This could be due to the onset of competing antiferromagnetic interactions between Cr ions that are closer together or an increase in structural disorder that hinders the long-range mediation of magnetism by defects like Ov58. This phenomenon has been reported in Cr-doped ZnO nanocrystals, where a coexistence of FM and AFM ground states is observed58. In contrast to Ms, the Hc shows a clear and consistent increase with Cr doping at 300 K, rising from 66.66 Oe for S1 to 151.35 Oe for S4 to be hardly magnetic materials, as well as reported for Cr-doped ZnO microrods59. But it contrasts with the decreasing Hc reported for Cr-doped ZnO NPs, where Hc dropped from 300 Oe for ZnO NPs to 160 Oe for 4% Cr doping36. In addition, this trend in magnetic hardness can be directly correlated with our structural findings since the doping of Cr3⁺ ion for Zn2⁺ is confirmed by XRD peak shifts, inducing compressive lattice strain. This strain, combined with the point defects generated for charge compensation, creates energy barriers that pin magnetic domain walls. As the density of these pinning centers increases with Cr concentration, it becomes progressively harder to reverse the magnetization, leading to the observed rise in Hc. On the other hand, the hysteresis loops at 10 K are much larger in magnitude than those at 300 K, confirming that the ferromagnetic component strengthens significantly when thermal fluctuations are reduced. The Ms at 10 K increase from 0.025 emu/g for S0 to 0.029, 0.033, 0.036, and 0.034 emu/g for S1, S2, S3, and S4, respectively. In contrast to Hc at 300 K, the Hc is 217.5 Oe for S0, but it decreases to 94.45 for S1 and further increases to 132.12, 149.33, and 177.01 for S2, S3, and S4, indicating that Cr shifts the NRs to soft magnetic material. The Sq values at 10 K are almost unchanged and between 0.023 and 0.025, whereas the values of γ generally increased from 0.009 emu. Oe/g for S₀ till reaching 5.96 emu. Oe/g for S4. The values of Ms and γ are at least 20 times more than obtained at 300 K, and the vice is true for Sq. This is the definitive signature of a system with a substantial paramagnetic contribution coexisting with ferromagnetic order. At 10 K, isolated Cr3⁺ ions that are not coupled into the ferromagnetic network can still align with a high external field, contributing to a large Mₛ. However, these moments randomize immediately when the field is switched off, causing Mr to remain small and driving Sq to near-zero values.

The magnetic properties we measure are not governed by the Cr concentration alone but emerge from a delicate balance of factors intimately linked to the material’s structure. XRD confirms that Cr3⁺ substitutes for Zn2⁺, providing the source of localized 3 µB magnetic moments. This very substitution process is responsible for the lattice contraction and strain observed, and it necessitates the creation of charge-compensating point defects, most plausibly Ov. These vacancies are not mere imperfections; they are likely the critical mediators that enable long-range ferromagnetic coupling between otherwise isolated Cr3⁺ ions, potentially through a bound magnetic polaron mechanism. This model, strongly supported by recent combined experimental and theoretical studies on Cr-doped ZnO, proposes that electrons trapped at oxygen vacancies interact with neighboring Cr3⁺ spins, aligning them ferromagnetically61. The critical role of Ov as a source and mediator of ferromagnetism is strongly supported by the previous work46, which used XPS and PL spectroscopy to demonstrate a direct correlation between higher oxygen vacancy concentration in 4% Cr-doped ZnO nanoparticles and their significantly enhanced saturation magnetization36. Furthermore, the evolving nanorod morphology plays an equally critical role. The systematic change from high-aspect-ratio rods to thicker, shorter structures indicates that Cr actively interferes with the electrochemical growth kinetics, likely through surface adsorption or altered charge transport. This pronounced morphological evolution stands in contrast to reports on spray-pyrolyzed Cr:ZnO microrods, where morphology remained essentially unchanged with doping59, highlighting the sensitivity of growth kinetics to the synthesis method. It also differs from the granular NPs’ morphology reported for co-precipitated samples36, further emphasizing the method-dependent control over structure. This changing geometry has direct magnetic consequences. The increased rod diameter may facilitate multi-domain states, consistent with the low Sq values. More importantly, the inherently high surface-to-volume ratio of the NRs means a significant fraction of incorporated Cr3⁺ ions reside in surface or near-surface environments. These sites have different coordination, symmetry, and crystal fields compared to the bulk-like core. Ions in these positions often experience spin frustration or weak coupling, making them more likely to contribute to the persistent paramagnetic signal that characterizes our low-temperature data, rather than to the ordered ferromagnetic network.

The field cooling (FC) and zero-field cooling (ZFC) curves measured at 100 Oe for the pure and Cr-doped ZnO NRs are shown in Fig. 9 (a-e), in which a gradual increase in magnetization upon cooling close to 10 K is obtained. This is typical for systems containing ferromagnetically coupled regions or spin clusters. However, the lack of saturation in the curves, even at 10 K, echoes what we saw in the M-H loops: a significant portion of the magnetic moments remains loosely coupled, contributing a paramagnetic-like tail. The curves of S0 and S1 show a splitting between the FC and ZFC curves such that FC magnetization is higher than those of ZFC. In contrast, the FC and ZFC curves of the higher Cr-doped samples (S2-S4) change this picture since they nearly overlap across the temperature range (10–300 K), showing nearly zero difference between them. This behavior indicates a simple, ordered magnetic system without significant Mr, suggesting a single magnetic phase, often seen in materials where field cooling doesn’t introduce complex pinning or freezing62,63. This weak and reversible signal is consistent with a simple antiferromagnet or paramagnet of uniform magnetic structure. A second critical feature is the visible sharp upturn in magnetization observed below approximately 40 K in all NRs, such that it becomes more pronounced with increasing Cr concentration. This is attributed to paramagnetic contributions from isolated Cr3⁺ ions, which would cause magnetization to increase as temperature drops; the steepness and magnitude of this rise point to effects beyond simple paramagnetic. It likely signals the collective freezing or alignment of spins associated with Cr clusters, defects, or strongly interacting surface spins that become active at low thermal energies. The hysteresis between FC and ZFC curves indicated that the Curie temperature for RTFM (Tc) is close to 300 K for Cr doped ZnO NRs. As listed in Table 4, the values of Tc for zero magnetization are between 299.95 and 299.99 K for the pure and doped samples (S0-S4), which indicating RTFM for the doped NRs.

The total magnetization of our samples contains two contributions: a ferromagnetic part and a paramagnetic part. Under a high field of 5 T, the paramagnetic component dominates and makes the ferromagnetic part negligible. This is why the high-field M-T data follow the modified Curie–Weiss law. At low fields (≤ 3 kOe), however, the paramagnetic background is weaker and a small hysteresis loop from ferromagnetic clusters becomes visible. Therefore, paramagnetic and ferromagnetic phases coexist in these samples. This behavior is typical for dilute magnetic semiconductors and has been reported in Cr-doped ZnO nanostructures58,64,65.

However, the coexistence of paramagnetic and ferromagnetic phases has been observed and confirmed by fitting the ZFC curve to Curie–Weiss and spin-wave models in La-doped ZnO nanorods synthesized by a simple chemical method66. They attributed the occurrence of RTFM in all samples with the defects, and the origin of ferromagnetism was explained on the basis of an isolated bound magnetic polaron model (BMP) through non-interacting magnetic moments of La3⁺ as compared to Zn2⁺. Properties of chemically synthesized pure and Gd-doped ZnO nanorods have been explored65. A systematic evolution of stronger enhancement of RTFM of ZnO nanorod synthesis by a simple chemical route with increasing Gd doping content was observed. They approved that oxygen vacancy mainly controlled RTFM in the samples. In addition, the co-existence of both paramagnetic and ferromagnetic phases was noticed, as we obtained at present. A surfactant-free wet chemical route is employed to synthesize pure and Ce-doped ZnO nanorods67. A very weak ferromagnetic ordering was observed in the background of the diamagnetic phase of all samples at RT. They have well correlated the ferromagnetic signature with the oxygen vacancies. Based on the above, the co-existence of two successive magnetic phases present in the Cr-doped ZnO nanorods, relative to the value of the applied field, is well consistent with those reported, which highlight the present investigation. Connecting these magnetic observations to the material structure is crucial. The high surface-to-volume ratio of the nanorods, which increases as the rods become shorter and thicker with Cr doping (S3, S4), places a substantial fraction of Cr3⁺ ions in surface or near-surface sites. These ions experience different crystal fields and coordination compared to those in the bulk-like core, leading to spin frustration and a range of magnetic energies. Furthermore, the one-dimensional geometry may channel magnetic exchange preferentially along the rod length, while inter-rod interactions contribute to the overall magnetic irreversibility. Finally, the inherent point defects in our system, particularly Oᵥ generated to compensate for the charge imbalance when Cr3⁺ replaces Zn2⁺, are not passive to mediate magnetic exchange interactions. In our nanorods, they likely serve as the hubs that facilitate coupling between otherwise isolated Cr moments, helping to establish the magnetic irreversibility we observe. The trend of increasing with Cr concentration supports this, as a higher density of both magnetic ions (Cr3⁺) and mediating defects (Ov) should strengthen the network of interactions.

The Curie–Weiss fits support this picture too the negative Tc values (between −0.7 and −0.23 K) tell us that antiferromagnetic interactions are also present, especially when Cr ions get close to each other or form ZnCr2O4 clusters. And the non-monotonic change in the Curie constant C (highest for S1 and S3) matches what we saw in the M-H loops, confirming that not all Cr adds equally to the magnetic response. The µeff values in Table 6 are almost the same for all samples. The undoped S0 already gives 0.041, which comes from defects. The doped samples show a small nonmonotonic change where S1 and S3 are a bit higher, and S2 is lower matching the trend in the Curie constant.

Conclusion

The structural, optical, and magnetic properties of Cr-doped ZnO nanorods (NRs) synthesized via a one-step electrochemical deposition method have been investigated. We have shown a successful doping of Cr into the wurtzite ZnO, but with very few amounts of ZnCr₂O₄ secondary phase obtained at 43.5° for S3 and S4. In general, the crystallite size and the ratio of diameter/length increase against Cr doping up to S3 and then decrease. As compared to S0, the S1-S4 show enhanced absorption in the visible region (400–600 nm) and lead to a decrease in the Eg from 3.53 to 3.25 eV. All NRs exhibit coexisting ferromagnetic and diamagnetic behavior at 300 K. In contrast, the behavior changes to ferromagnetic and paramagnetic at 10 K. The magnetic parameters of Ms, Hc, Sq, and γ are found to be dependent on the chosen Cr concentration and temperature. The The FC and ZFC curves indicate that the Curie temperature is close to room temperature for the doped nanorods. The coexistence of paramagnetic and weak ferromagnetic contributions is evident from the high-field Curie–Weiss behavior and the low-field hysteresis loops, respectively. The findings indicate that these materials are promising for applications in spintronics, medical treatments, and visible-light optoelectronics, where the balance between optical absorption and magnetic functionality can be optimized by controlling the Cr doping concentration.
