Effect of 12-week dynamic stretching of hamstrings on joint flexibility and musculoskeletal properties
Authors: Naoki Ikeda, Kazuya Hiratsuka, Takayuki Inami, Tadao Isaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to examine the effects of a 12‑week hamstrings dynamic stretching (DS) program on joint flexibility, muscle strength, and musculoskeletal properties in the stretched limb, with the non‑stretched limb assessed for comparison.MethodsThe study comprised 18 healthy young adults (12 males, 6 females) who performed hamstrings DS on one leg three times per week for 12 weeks, with the contralateral leg serving as the control. Pre- and post-intervention assessments included knee extension range of motion (KEROM), peak passive torque during KEROM (stretch tolerance), muscle stiffness, and knee flexor strength. Muscle stiffness was evaluated using shear-wave elastography targeting the biceps femoris (BF). Knee flexor strength was assessed using the rate of torque development (RTD) and isokinetic knee flexion torque.ResultsKEROM significantly increased in the DS condition (p = 0.025; d = 0.61), with no significant change in the control condition (p = 0.55; d = 0.15). Peak passive torque significantly increased following DS (p = 0.041; d = 0.68), with no change in the contralateral limb (p = 0.21; d = 0.21). BF stiffness significantly decreased in the DS condition and remained unchanged in the contralateral limb (70° knee flexion: DS, p < 0.01, d = 0.79; control, p = 0.30, d = 0.30; 50° knee flexion: DS, p = 0.044, d = 0.55; control, p = 0.18, d = 0.34. RTD and isokinetic knee flexion strength did not change significantly in either condition.ConclusionTwelve weeks of hamstrings DS enhanced stretch tolerance, reduced muscle stiffness, and improved joint flexibility in the stretched limb, with no notable changes observed in the contralateral limb.Trial RegistrationName of trial registry Effects of training and conditioning methods on mechanical properties of skeletal muscle Identifying number (URL) UMIN000051819 (https://center6.umin.ac.jp/cgi-open-bin/ctr_e/ctr_view.cgi?recptno=R000058080) Date of registration 5th Aug 2023.

Introduction

Dynamic stretching (DS), including ballistic stretching, involves actively moving a joint through a maximal or submaximal range of motion (ROM) at a frequency of approximately 1 Hz using repeated cycles of muscle lengthening and shortening. Continued DS targeting the ankle plantar flexors or hamstrings over a period of 6–12 weeks has been shown to improve joint flexibility (Mahieu et al. 2007; Konrad and Tilp 2014; Gunaydin et al. 2020; Ikeda et al. 2025; Hosseini et al. 2025). Static stretching, characterized by elongation of the muscle while holding the position for a fixed duration, also enhances joint flexibility when performed regularly over time (Freitas et al. 2018; Medeiros and Martini 2018; Konrad et al. 2024).

Long‑term static stretching programs have additionally been reported to increase flexibility in non‑stretched limbs (Behm et al. 2021; Nakamura et al. 2022). A similar cross‑limb improvement was observed following 12 weeks of DS targeting the ankle plantar flexors (Ikeda et al. 2025). These findings indicate that neurophysiological factors, such as changes in stretch tolerance, may contribute to bilateral improvements in flexibility. However, the mechanisms underlying these cross‑limb responses remain unclear, and whether similar effects occur when DS is applied to muscle groups other than the ankle plantar flexors remains unknown.

Increased joint flexibility in the stretched limb following stretching interventions is influenced by reductions in muscle stiffness and neurophysiological changes (Behm et al. 2016). In contrast, improvements in the non-stretched limb following static stretching have been associated with neural inhibition and psychophysiological factors, including reduced sympathetic nervous system activity and activation of pain modulation pathways (Behm et al. 2016, 2021). Although these mechanisms may contribute to cross-limb responses, direct evidence remains limited.

Hamstrings stretching interventions generally produce greater improvements in ROM than stretching of the ankle plantar flexors (Konrad et al. 2024), potentially attributable to differences in joint mobility. Therefore, long-term hamstrings DS is expected to improve joint ROM in the stretched limb through reductions in muscle stiffness and increases in stretch tolerance. Previous studies examining hamstrings DS have reported inconsistent findings regarding changes in muscle function, such as muscle strength and athletic performance (Turki‑Belkhiria et al., 2014; Hosseini et al. 2025; Barbosa et al. 2020). Whether long‑term hamstrings DS influences muscle strength or other neuromuscular properties remains unclear.

To address this gap, we aimed to investigate the effects of a 12‑week hamstrings DS program on joint flexibility, muscle stiffness, stretch tolerance, and muscle strength in the stretched limb, with the non‑stretched limb assessed for comparison. We hypothesized that the DS program would improve joint flexibility in the stretched limb through reductions in muscle stiffness and increases in stretch tolerance, whereas no specific adaptations were expected in the non‑stretched limb.

Methods

Participants

Eighteen healthy university students (12 males and 6 females; males: age, 20 ± 2 years; height, 1.72 ± 0.07 m; body weight, 64.9 ± 11.1 kg; females: age, 20 ± 1 years; height, 1.61 ± 0.08 m; body weight, 56.1 ± 5.5 kg; mean ± standard deviation) participated in this study. All participants had no history of orthopedic disorders of the lower limbs (such as injuries to muscles, tendons, ligaments, or peripheral nerve dysfunction). They participated in sports activities (ball games such as baseball, tennis, and badminton [n = 15], cycling [n = 2], resistance exercise [n = 1]) at the recreational level once to twice per week. Participants were instructed to refrain from initiating any new lower-limb strength or flexibility training outside the intervention during the study period.

Before participation, all participants were informed of the study’s purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and risks, and provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of Ritsumeikan University (approval number: BKC-LSMH-2021-055). This study was carried out in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki 1975.

Study design

In this study, measurements were obtained from both legs of all participants. One leg received hamstrings DS for 12 weeks, whereas the other did not undergo any DS intervention (control leg). The intervention leg was randomly assigned irrespective of limb dominance using computer-generated random numbers (Excel software). Research assistants randomized the process. All measurements were conducted in the same manner for both limbs. Pre- and post-intervention assessments, including knee extension range of motion (KEROM), peak passive torque during KEROM (as an indicator of stretch tolerance), muscle stiffness, and knee flexor strength, were conducted.

DS protocol

The DS protocol was developed based on the literature (Haff and Triplett 2015). Participants performed two types of DS exercises targeting the hamstrings. The first exercise was a seated hamstrings stretch in which participants extended their knees while seated and rhythmically flexed and extended their hips at a frequency of 1 Hz. The second was a front leg swing, performed in a unilateral standing position, involving rhythmic hip flexion and extension through a wide ROM at 0.7 Hz (Fig. 1).

Two types of dynamic stretching targeting the hamstrings. a Seated hamstrings stretch. b Front leg swing hamstrings stretch

Both DS exercises were performed using a bouncing motion to actively stretch the hamstrings. Participants were instructed to perform the stretches at an intensity corresponding to a moderate-to-painful stretch sensation, rated as 3–4 on a visual analog scale ranging from 1 to 5 (1: no pain at all, 2: mild stretch feeling, 3: moderate stretch feeling, 4: discomfort [defined as pain], and 5: intolerable discomfort) (Ikeda et al. 2025). The DS program was conducted over a 12-week period, three times per week, with each session consisting of five sets of 60 s per set for each of the two stretching types (Ikeda et al. 2025). The intervention was supervised to ensure adherence to the protocol.

Measurement of KEROM

KEROM assessment was conducted using an isokinetic dynamometer (BIODEX SYSTEM4, Biodex Medical Systems, Shirley, NY, USA) following established protocols (Muanjai et al. 2017; Brusco et al. 2019) (Fig. 2). Participants were seated, with the thigh of the measured leg placed on a custom device that was elevated approximately 45° from the horizontal plane. The seat back was reclined by approximately 20° from the vertical side, resulting in a hip flexion angle of approximately 25° on the measured side.

Illustration of knee extension range of motion measurement

The knee was passively extended from approximately 100° of flexion to full extension (0°) at a constant angular velocity of 5° per s until the participants experienced moderate stretching (visual analog scale score of 3) (Ikeda et al. 2025). KEROM was defined as the maximum knee extension angle achieved during the test. The torque measured at this angle in the direction of knee flexion was defined as the peak passive torque (Ikeda et al. 2024).

Each measurement was repeated five times. The mean of three intermediate values (excluding the highest and lowest values) was used as the representative KEROM value. The same averaging procedure was applied to determine the representative peak passive torque. Participants were instructed to remain relaxed and avoid resisting the passive extension throughout the measurement.

Torque and knee angle signals from the dynamometer were digitized at a sampling rate of 1 kHz using an analog-to-digital converter (PowerLab/16SP, ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia) and recorded on a personal computer using LabChart8 software (v8.1.2, ADInstruments). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC [1,2]) value for the KEROM measurement variable was 0.98. The coefficient of variation of KEROM was 2.7%, based on 10 repeated measurements performed on the same participant (n = 10).

Measurement of muscle stiffness

Muscle stiffness was assessed by measuring the shear modulus of the long head of the biceps femoris (BF) using shear wave elastography (Aixplorer, MSK mode; SuperSonic Imagine, France). Based on a previous study (Le Sant et al. 2015), measurements were taken at two knee flexion angles (70° and 50°). Participants were seated on an isokinetic dynamometer in the same posture as that used for the KEROM assessment.

The BF measurement site was located at approximately 50% of the thigh length from the proximal end, corresponding to the center of the muscle belly (Le Sant et al. 2015). A linear ultrasound probe (scan width: 50 mm; SL10-2, SuperSonic Imagine, France) was placed over the measurement site, and longitudinal B-mode ultrasound images with color-coded shear modulus mapping were acquired. For each knee angle, five images were captured, and participants were instructed to remain relaxed during measurements to avoid muscle activation.

Acquired ultrasound images were analyzed using dedicated software (elastography image analysis program [T.K.K. 5840], Takei Scientific Instruments, Japan) on a personal computer (Fig. 3). For each image, a rectangular region of interest (ROI) was defined, and the mean shear modulus within the ROI was extracted as the representative value. For each knee angle, BF stiffness was defined as the average of three middle shear modulus values, excluding the highest and lowest values from the five images obtained.

Measurement of knee flexion strength

Knee flexion strength was assessed in a seated position using the aforementioned isokinetic dynamometer based on two parameters—the rate of torque development (RTD) during isometric knee flexion and peak torque during isokinetic knee flexion.

For RTD assessment, participants were positioned with the hip in 20° extension and the knee flexed at 70°. Before testing, each participant performed one warm-up contraction at 30%, 50%, and 80% of their maximal voluntary effort. RTD measurements were then conducted across five trials at 60-s intervals. Participants were instructed to generate torque “as fast and forcefully as possible” (Maffiuletti et al. 2016; Ikeda et al. 2025).

Torque and knee angle signals from the dynamometer were digitized at a sampling rate of 1 kHz via an analog-to-digital converter and recorded using dedicated analysis software. Torque signals were smoothed using a fourth-order zero-lag digital Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 15 Hz (Aagaard et al. 2002; Ikeda et al. 2025). RTD was defined as the peak value of the time derivative of the torque signal for each trial. The mean RTD was calculated from the three middle trials, excluding the highest and lowest values.

Isokinetic knee flexor strength was assessed in the same posture as the RTD test, with maximal voluntary contractions performed at an angular velocity of 60° per s. The ROM was set from 0° (full extension) to 90° of knee flexion. After each contraction, the limb was returned to the starting position by an examiner. Three trials were conducted, and the highest torque value was adopted as the representative value.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29 (SPSS Japan, Tokyo, Japan). A two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) (intervention [DS vs. control] × time [pre- vs. post-intervention]) was conducted for each dependent variable. Paired t-tests were performed when a significant interaction or main effect of time was observed within each condition. In addition, paired t-tests were used to confirm that there were no significant differences in baseline measurements between the conditions prior to the intervention. Cohen’s d (post-hoc comparison) was used to calculate the effect size. Cohen’s d was obtained using the following equation: d = Mdiff/SDpooled √2[1 − r], where Mdiff is the difference between the mean value of the pre-and post-measurements, and r is the correlation between the mean values (Morris and DeShon 2002). A priori power analysis indicated that a minimum of 14 participants per condition was required for the study design. Estimation was based on a within-subject repeated measures ANOVA, assuming an effect size of 0.4, statistical power of 0.8, and an alpha level of 0.05 (Cohen 1988). The analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3. The effect size (Cohen’s d) was defined as |0.20|–|0.50| small effect, |0.50|–|0.80| medium effect, and >|0.80| large effect (Cohen 1988). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

A significant interaction between condition and time was observed for the peak passive torque during the KEROM test (p < 0.01). Discussion

This study demonstrated that a 12-week hamstrings DS program improved KEROM, enhanced stretch tolerance, and reduced hamstrings stiffness in the stretched limb. The direction of change in KEROM—specifically, a decrease in the passive knee extension angle from 45.2° to 37.5°—indicates movement toward full extension, reflecting improved flexibility in the stretched limb. No significant changes were observed in isometric RTD or isokinetic knee flexion strength in either limb, and the non-stretched limb showed no notable changes.

Comparison with previous literature

The absence of meaningful changes in the non‑stretched limb is consistent with the unilateral nature of DS‑induced adaptations. Although contralateral improvements following long‑term DS of the ankle plantar flexors have been reported (Ikeda et al. 2025), such responses are not consistently observed across muscle groups or stretching modalities. Several factors may help contextualize the present findings. The magnitude of improvement in the stretched limb, although meaningful, may not have been sufficient to elicit detectable cross‑limb responses. In addition, DS typically involves shorter tension‑holding durations and lower levels of discomfort than static stretching, which has been more consistently associated with bilateral effects (Behm et al. 2021; Konrad et al. 2024). Given these considerations, the absence of contralateral changes should be interpreted as consistent with the localized nature of DS rather than as evidence regarding broader cross‑limb mechanisms.

Stretch tolerance, stiffness, and neural considerations

Peak passive torque increased only in the stretched limb, indicating improved stretch tolerance, whereas no meaningful change occurred in the non‑stretched limb. Because stretch tolerance is a key contributor to flexibility gains (Magnusson et al. 1996; Behm et al. 2016), these findings suggest that neurophysiological adaptations were localized to the intervention limb. Although neural mechanisms have been proposed as an explanation for contralateral improvements (Behm et al. 2021), the present study did not assess neural variables, such as electromyography or reflex activity. Therefore, interpretations regarding neural contributions should remain cautious.

Hamstrings stiffness decreased following the 12‑week DS program, supporting the notion that longer‑duration DS may be necessary to elicit changes in muscle–tendon properties. No change in stiffness was observed in the non‑stretched limb, consistent with previous findings in plantar flexor DS (Ikeda et al. 2025).

Why hamstrings may differ from plantar flexors in contralateral adaptation

Differences in anatomical and functional characteristics may contribute to variability in cross‑limb responses. The hamstrings, as biarticular muscles with substantial proximal tendon contributions (Takeda et al. 2023), may experience a more localized distribution of mechanical strain during stretching. Meanwhile, the gastrocnemius–soleus complex is heavily involved in repetitive load‑bearing tasks and may be more susceptible to cross‑limb neural influences (Nakamura et al. 2022). These distinctions may help explain why contralateral adaptations have been more frequently reported in plantar flexor stretching than in hamstrings stretching, although the present study was not designed to directly test these mechanisms.

Muscle strength

Neither isometric RTD nor isokinetic knee flexion strength changed following the intervention. This finding aligns with a previous study reporting no improvements in muscle function after hamstrings DS (Barbosa et al. 2020), although other studies have reported increases (Turki‑Belkhiria et al., 2014; Hosseini et al. 2025). No changes were observed in the non‑stretched limb, consistent with previous findings in plantar flexor DS (Ikeda et al. 2025).

Study design considerations

In this unilateral intervention design, the contralateral limb served as an internal control to reduce inter‑individual variability. However, the absence of a separate no‑intervention control group limits the ability to distinguish intervention‑specific effects from natural variability in the non‑stretched limb. Therefore, interpretations regarding contralateral responses should be made with caution.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, neural variables were not measured, preventing the evaluation of potential neural mechanisms underlying contralateral responses. Second, although the contralateral limb served as an internal control, this approach assumes minimal cross‑limb interaction and may not fully capture subtle bilateral influences. Third, the findings are limited to young, healthy adults, and generalizability to other populations remains uncertain. Fourth, the absence of an independent no‑intervention control group limits the ability to distinguish intervention‑specific effects from normal day‑to‑day variability in muscle–tendon properties or joint flexibility. This design also makes it difficult to determine whether any observed contralateral changes reflect true cross‑limb adaptations or simply measurement‑related fluctuations. Future studies incorporating a true no‑intervention control group and direct neural assessments would allow for a more rigorous evaluation of contralateral adaptations.

Conclusion

The 12‑week DS program improved KEROM, stretch tolerance, and hamstrings stiffness in the stretched limb. Measurements in the non-stretched limb showed no meaningful changes, consistent with the localized nature of DS-induced adaptations. These findings suggest that, although DS is effective in enhancing flexibility in the stretched limb, it may not provide sufficient stimulus to elicit cross-limb responses. Further research, including neural assessments and a no‑intervention control group, is needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying cross‑limb responses.
