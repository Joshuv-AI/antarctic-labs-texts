Influence of Corneal Diameter on Corneal Biomechanics and Biometry in Chinese Patients: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Huanmin Cheng, Qiwei Li, Fan Yang, Emmanuel Eric Pazo, Jonathan E. Moore, Zhanglin Liu, Ming Sun, Shaozhen Zhao, Yue Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to investigate the influence of corneal diameter (CD) on corneal biomechanics and biometry parameters to allow more accurate evaluation of refractive surgery patients.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 203 Chinese participants were categorized into four groups based on CD (group A = CD ≤11.1 mm, group B = CD = 11.2–11.5 mm, group C = CD = 11.6–12 mm, and group D = CD >12.0 mm). Data collected included age, gender, intraocular pressure, spherical equivalent, and parameters from the Pentacam and Corvis ST. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software.ResultsThe study included 203 eyes. For the Pentacam examination, corneal curvature, thinnest pachymetry (TP), back elevations (BEs), corneal elevation difference (front and back), pachymetry progression indices (PPIs), index of surface variance (ISV), keratoconus percentage index (KISA%), and most of the Belin/Ambrósio Enhanced Ectasia Display (BAD) parameters were negatively correlated with CD (linear regression analysis, P < 0.05). The abnormal rate of Pentacam parameters in the CD ≤11.1 mm group was higher than other groups. For Corvis ST, CD was positively correlated with peak distance (PD) and Ambrósio relational thickness to the horizontal profile (Arth), but negatively correlated with tomographic and biomechanical index (TBI) and Corvis biomechanical index (CBI; linear regression analysis, P < 0.05). The CD ≤11.1 mm group demonstrated the highest CBI and TBI values, although these parameters remained within the normal range.ConclusionsCorneal diameter significantly influences Pentacam and Corvis ST parameters, particularly on BE, BAD-D, PPIs, CBI, and TBI.Translational RelevanceIncorporating corneal diameter into preoperative screening may help improve the specificity of keratoconus detection and reduce unnecessary exclusion of eligible refractive surgery candidates with small corneas.

Introduction

Corneal refractive surgery has become a widely accepted and effective method for correcting myopia, with a strong safety profile.1 However, postoperative corneal ectasia remains a serious complication. The risk of ectasia can be significantly minimized through comprehensive preoperative screening.2 Despite this, the lack of a clear definition for subclinical keratoconus presents a challenge, as no standardized criteria currently exist to distinguish it from normal corneas. Previous studies3–5 defined subclinical keratoconus as manifest keratoconus in one eye, whereas the fellow eye exhibited a slight deviation from normality in elevation and / or pachymetric map without an evident sign of keratoconus. Scheimpflug tomography has emerged as a widely used and effective tool for preoperative screening and the early diagnosis of keratoconus.6 Pentacam is a device that uses a rotating Scheimpflug camera system to capture high-resolution images of the anterior segment. Additionally, the Pentacam provides built-in normal ranges for various parameters7 and visually flags abnormal values with yellow or red highlights to alert users. However, the reference data used by the Pentacam's built-in system primarily derive from North and South American populations, which may not accurately reflect the characteristics of other ethnic groups.8 It is well established that corneal diameter varies across different ethnic groups.9 Spanish researchers reported that the corneal diameter of myopic individuals, measured by Orbscan Ⅱ corneal topography system, ranged from 10.40 to 13.60 mm.10 Additionally, Qin et al.11 found that the corneal diameter of Asians was 0.5 mm narrower than Caucasians, on average. Corneal horizontal diameter plays a significant role in influencing the outcomes of corneal tomography for detecting suspected keratoconus.12 Ding et al.13 reported that the rate of abnormal cases was significantly higher in patients with CD ≤11.1 mm compared with those with CD >12.0 mm. Similarly, Lin et al.14 observed that smaller corneal diameters were associated with greater thinning of the central cornea. Furthermore, other studies9,15,16 have demonstrated that the corneal diameter also impacted the BAD parameters. As a result, the mismatch between Pentacam’s reference database and the CD of the Chinese population often lead to abnormalities in Pentacam parameters, particularly in eyes with smaller diameters. This discrepancy increases the risk of misdiagnosing small-diameter corneas as suspected keratoconus. To mitigate this, additional corneal biomechanical assessments should be utilized as auxiliary diagnostic tools to improve diagnostic accuracy. Corneal Visualization Scheimpflug Technology (Corvis ST) is a non-contact air-puff tonometer designed to evaluate corneal biomechanical properties in vivo.17 Studies have demonstrated that corneal biomechanics exhibit high accuracy in distinguishing healthy eyes from those with keratoconus.18 However, limited research exists on the relationship among corneal topography, biomechanics, and corneal diameter, particularly in cases of small corneas. This study aims to analyze the influence of corneal diameter on Pentacam and corneal biomechanical parameters, providing a reference for preoperative screening of refractive surgery for patients with a small cornea.

Methods

Patients

This cross-sectional study included 203 participants (203 eyes) selected from the Refractive Center database of Tianjin Medical University Eye Hospital from January 2023 to March 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Eye Hospital of Tianjin Medical University (2024 KY-01) and complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: age ≥18 years; stable refractive status over the past 2 years; normal slit lamp examination; and normal topography. Normal corneal morphology was defined as the absence of steep areas in the central, inferior, or superior regions, or the presence of such steep areas without any of the following: steep keratometry >47 diopters, oblique cylinder >1.5 diopters, or CCT <500 µm.19 Exclusion criteria included the presence of pathological ocular conditions, autoimmune diseases, and a history of soft contact lens, hard contact lens, or orthokeratology wearing lenses (Fig. 1).

Corneal Tomography Assessments

All patients underwent a comprehensive ocular examination, including objective and subjective refraction, slit-lamp examination. Pentacam HR (Oculus Optikgeräte GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany; software version 6.10r53) was used for imaging. The scans marked as “OK” by the “Quality Specification” were included into analysis. All the imaging was performed by a same operator, and three measurements were averaged for each individual. The analytical scale of these indices was set to 8-mm diameter.

The following corneal indices were obtained: corneal diameter; flat and steep central power (Kf and Ks); mean and maximum central power (Km and Kmax); the Best Fitted Sphere for the front cornea (BFSf); the Best Fitted Sphere for the back cornea (BFSb); Q value; thinnest pachymetry (TP); front and back corneal elevations (FE and BE) at thinnest pachymetry; anterior radius of curvature (ARC) for a 3-mm zone centered on the thinnest point; posterior radius of curvature (PRC) for a 3-mm zone centered on the thinnest point; pachymetric progression index (PPI; minimum, average, and maximum); Ambrósio's maximum relational thickness index (ARTmax); front elevation difference (FE-difference) between the enhanced BFS and the standard BFS; back elevation difference (BE-difference) between the enhanced BFS and the standard BFS (Fig. 2).

Biomechanical Assessments

All patients underwent Corvis ST examination. Corvis ST (Oculus Optikgerate GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany; software version 6.08r30) is a dynamic, non-contact Scheimpflug analyzer designed to assess corneal biomechanics in real time. It uses a high-speed camera (4330 frames/second) to capture a series of horizontal Scheimpflug images, analyzing corneal deformation through precise measurements and timing during an air-puff test.20 The following parameters were obtained: the first applanation length (AL1), the second applanation length (AL2), first applanation velocity (A1V), second applanation velocity (A2V), peak distance (PD), highest concavity radius (HCR), deformation amplitude (DA), stiffness parameter at first applanation (SP-A1), Ambrósio relational thickness to the horizontal profile (ARTh), deformation amplitude ratio at 2 mm (DA ratio = 2 mm), integrated radius (IR), Corvis biomechanical index (CBI), tomographic and biomechanical index (TBI), biomechanic intraocular pressure (bIOP), and stress-stain index (SSI). Each eye was measured at least three times. Only images with “OK” quality ratings were accepted, and the highest quality measurement was used for analysis. Examinations were conducted by the same operator to ensure consistency, and the device was calibrated at the start of the study for accurate measurements.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size of our study was determined using G*power 3.1.9.6 (Heinrich Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). The parameters were set as follows: significance level (α) was 0.05, statistical power (1-β) was 0.80, and effect size was 0.25. The minimum sample size was calculated to be 180, and we enrolled 203 participants. SPSS statistical software (version 22.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. To compare the four corneal diameter groups, 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Kruskal-Wallis H test were applied, depending on the data distribution. Pairwise comparisons between groups were conducted using the least significant difference (LSD) test. The Pearson correlation test was used to analyze the relationships between normally distributed variables, whereas the Spearman correlation test was used for non-normally distributed data. Additionally, linear regression analyses were performed to assess correlations between corneal indices and corneal diameter. The percentages of abnormality between groups were compared by chi-square test. A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 (P < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic Data of the Study Population

This study included 203 eyes of 92 men (45.3%) and 111 women (54.7%). The mean age of the participants was 24.21 ± 5.25 years (range = 18–45 years). The number of each group was 11.1 mm or less: n = 52; 11.1 to 11.5 mm: n = 48; 11.5 to 12.0 mm: n = 48; and 12.0 mm or greater: n = 55. Only the right eyes of the subjects were included. No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in terms of intraocular pressure (IOP; P = 0.803) or spherical equivalent (P = 0.070; Table 2).

Mean Values and Linear Regression of Pentacan Parameters

Among the 4 groups, 25 of the 31 Pentacam parameters analyzed exhibited significant differences. In contrast, parameters such as FE, IHA, IVA, IHD, I-S value, and KI showed no significant differences. The mean values and regression analyses of Pentacam parameters for the four groups are summarized in Table 2. In addition, the results of pairwise comparison between groups with different Pentacam parameters showed that the difference between group A and group D was statistically significant (Supplementary Table S1). Corneal diameter was negatively correlated with Kf, Ks, Km, Kmax, Q value, TP, BE, FE-difference, BE-difference, Df, Db, Dp, Da, D, PPImin, PPImax, and PPIavg, whereas positively correlated with BFSf, BFSb, Dt, ARTmax, ARC, and PRC (linear regression analysis, P < 0.05). Linear regression analysis revealed that CD had the greatest influence on the deviation of back elevation (Db, R² = 0.414, P < 0.01), followed by BE-difference (R² = 0.398, P < 0.01). Notably, corneal elevation differences were more strongly correlated with CD compared to corneal elevation, particularly on the posterior surface.

There was no correlation between the other biomechanical parameters and corneal diameter (P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study examined the impact of corneal diameter on Pentacam and Corvis ST parameters in a Chinese population. The results revealed that corneal diameter was significantly correlated with corneal morphological and corneal biomechanical parameters.

The results indicated that smaller corneas exhibited steeper corneal curvature, consistent with the findings of Li et al.21 This can be attributed to the cornea’s asphericity, as the transition from central to peripheral curvature flattens more rapidly in smaller diameter corneas, resulting in a higher Q value in these eyes.22,23 Corneal elevation is a relative value obtained by Pentacam based on the best-fit sphere (BFS). The BFS serves as a standard reference surface (within 8 mm diameter range centered at the corneal apex), calculated to minimize deviations from the corneal morphology based on original corneal elevation data. This study demonstrated that both BFSf and BFSb values were smaller in corneas with smaller corneal diameters, aligning with corresponding changes in ARC and PRC. Furthermore, we observed that FE showed no correlation with corneal diameter, whereas BE decreased with increasing corneal diameter. This relationship may be influenced by the BFSb. These findings are consistent with the earlier results reported by Almorin et al.24 It is noteworthy that BFS is susceptible to outliers and may potentially mask corneal abnormalities. In contrast, the enhanced best-fit sphere (EBFS) is constructed by excluding a central 3.5 mm zone around the thinnest point and fitting a reference surface from the remaining “normal” corneal data. This method reduces the impact of high curvature and localized ectasia near the thinnest region, thereby enhancing the detection of keratoconus. The elevation difference is calculated as the difference between the enhanced elevation map and the elevation map. In the CD ≤11.1 mm group, the abnormal rates were 4.1% for FE, 13.5% for BE, 37.6% for FE-difference, and 39.4% for BE-difference. This result suggested that CD has a more pronounced effect on corneal elevation difference than on corneal elevation, particularly on back elevation difference. An abnormal elevation of the posterior corneal surface is a well-established indicator of early keratoconus.25 Therefore, when abnormal corneal elevation is observed, in addition to suspecting keratoconus, the potential influence of corneal diameter should also be considered.

We observed that TP decreased as the corneal diameter increased, likely due to the greater distance from the center to the peripheral cornea in eyes with larger corneal diameters. Compared with corneal thickness, PPI parameters have higher accuracy and specificity in distinguishing a normal cornea from keratoconus.26 In our study, the highest PPI parameters were observed in the CD ≤11.1 mm group, suggesting a more rapid corneal thickness change from the central to the peripheral zone, which is consistent with the results of Ding et al.13 This phenomenon was attributed to the shorter central-peripheral distance in small corneas, which accentuates their unique thickness profile. ARTmax also was a critical parameter for distinguishing healthy corneas from keratoconus.27 Liu et al. reported14 significantly lower ARTmax values in patients with small corneas compared with those with large corneas, which is consistent with our research results. Boyd et al. demonstrated9 that CD had a more substantial effect on ARTmax and D values in the Chinese population compared with the North American population. Among Chinese individuals, small corneal diameters were frequently associated with decreased ARTmax and increased D value. Consequently, increased attention should be given to patients with small corneas during preoperative keratoconus screening.

BAD is a composite index that incorporates nine different indices derived from elevation and pachymetric data. Previous studies28,29 have demonstrated that BAD has high accuracy in detecting both subclinical and clinical keratoconus. In our study, we found that Df, Db, Dp, Da, and D indices were negatively correlated with corneal diameter, consistent with the findings of Cao et al.30 Furthermore, we observed that the abnormal rate of D index was 52.9% in the CD ≤11.1 mm group, 33.3% in the 11.1 to 11.5 mm group, 19.8% in the 11.5 to 12.0 mm group, and 10.9% in the CD >12.0 mm group. Niu et al.15 followed patients who had undergone refractive surgery for 3 years and found that the abnormal rate of BAD reached 60% in patients with CD ≤11.1 mm, even though no signs of postoperative keratoconus were observed. The results suggest that D index may overestimate the risk of ectasia of small-diameter corneas. Similarly, Ding et al.16 reported that patients with CD >11.9 mm exhibited fewer abnormal indicators in the subclinical keratoconus population, suggesting that BAD indices may underestimate ectasia risk in large corneas. Notably, the current BAD diagnostic system does not incorporate the corneal diameter, which may contribute to inaccuracies when applied to the Chinese population. Thus, BAD parameters should be combined with corneal diameter evaluation to improve the accuracy and reliability of refractive surgery screening.

Topometric indices also play a crucial role in the diagnosis of keratoconus.31 Huseynli et al.32 found increased ISV, IVA, and KI values in subclinical keratoconus compared with healthy eyes. In our study, the ISV was significantly higher in the CD ≤11.1 mm group than in the other 3 groups; however, most values remained within the normal range. Zhang et al. observed no correlation between corneal asymmetry parameters and corneal diameter,33 further supporting the notion that corneal diameter has minimal influence on surface asymmetry.

However, abnormal topographic findings do not necessarily indicate subclinical keratoconus. Combining corneal morphology with biomechanical assessments can further optimize the diagnosis of subclinical keratoconus.5,34,35 Previous studies36,37 have established that corneal thickness influences corneal biomechanics, with thicker corneas exhibiting less deformation under applied force. Additionally, high IOP may mask abnormalities in corneal biomechanical behavior.38 Therefore, all biomechanical comparisons in this study were adjusted for TP and IOP. Our study demonstrated that CD was positively correlated with PD and ARTh, whereas it was negatively correlated with CBI and TBI. This is consistent with the findings of Elizabeth et al.39 Although patients with CD ≤11.1 mm exhibited relatively higher CBI and TBI values, both indices remained within normal limits, suggesting that the effect of CD on corneal biomechanics is insufficient to confound the diagnosis of keratoconus. Notably, corneal diameter showed a stronger correlation with TBI than with CBI. This may be due to the composite nature of TBI, which incorporates both CBI and D values, rendering it more sensitive to variations in corneal morphology. Therefore, in patients with small corneas and abnormal morphology, greater attention should be given to CBI values. It is important to note that the reference values for corneal deformation response parameters in Corvis ST were derived from a mixed South American and Caucasian population.40 Given that Chinese individuals generally have smaller corneal diameters than European populations, this discrepancy may contribute to abnormal Corvis ST parameters—particularly the CBI and TBI. Previous study41 has reported a high false-positive rate for CBI among healthy Chinese subjects. In response, Vinciguerra et al. introduced42 a China-specific Corvis Biomechanical Index (cCBI), which exhibited improved specificity over the original CBI and TBI in Chinese populations.

In our study, patients with CD ≤11.1 mm exhibited the highest rate of Pentacam abnormalities, followed by those CD ≤11.5 mm. However, these parameter deviations may not directly indicate abnormal corneal morphology in small corneas. In most cases, there may be a mismatch between the corneal diameter of the Chinese population and the built-in standard database of Pentacam. This discrepancy increases the risk of misdiagnosing patients with a small cornea as suspected keratoconus, potentially excluding some individuals who are otherwise suitable for refractive surgery from vision correction. To mitigate this risk, preoperative screening should incorporate corneal biomechanical evaluation and interpret topographic results in the context of corneal diameter. This integrated approach is particularly crucial for patients with small corneas and abnormal morphology to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary exclusion from vision correction surgery.

The main limitation of our study is that the sample size was small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, our study lacked long-term follow-up of patients, which is essential for evaluating the predictive value of these parameters over time. Future research will aim to address these limitations, including performing prospective multicenter validation combined with long-term follow-up to provide more comprehensive insights.

Conclusions

Our investigation demonstrated that the corneal diameter significantly influences Pentacam parameters (e.g. corneal elevations, BAD-D, and PPIs) and Corvis ST parameters (e.g. ARTh, TBI, and CBI). Therefore, considering corneal diameter into an individualized assessment protocol may enhance the sensitivity and specificity of keratoconus screening in candidates for refractive surgery.
