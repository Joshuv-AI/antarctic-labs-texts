Risk Factors for Neurological Complications in Severe Spinal Deformity Surgery
Authors: Xinwei Wang, Haoran Zhang, Chenkai Li, Xiaohan Ye, Yiwei Zhao, You Du, Shengru Wang, Jianguo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveSevere spinal deformities, including scoliosis and kyphosis, present significant challenges in corrective surgery due to the elevated risk of neurological complications. The identification of preoperative risk factors is of paramount importance for the optimization of outcomes and the prevention of complications.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 130 patients with severe spinal deformities who underwent surgical treatments from January 2002 to May 2022. A comprehensive collection and analysis of preoperative clinical, imaging, and surgical data were conducted with the objective of identifying risk factors for neurological complications. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to ascertain the independent predictors.ResultsA total of 130 patients were included in the study, with a female ratio of 50% and a mean age of 21.4 ± 15.3 years, and 18 (13.8%) of them experienced postoperative neurological complications. Significant factors included preoperative spinal cord anomalies (38.9% in the complication group vs. 8.9% in the non‐complication group, p = 0.001) and a higher kyphosis angle (112.4° in the complication group vs. 98.2° in the non‐complication group, p = 0.018). The incidence of intraoperative neuromonitoring alarms was significantly higher in the complication group (38.9% vs. 15.9%, p = 0.022). No significant differences were observed in operative time (p = 0.095) or blood loss (p = 0.179). A higher angle of kyphosis (OR = 1.027, 95% CI: 1.001–1.055, p = 0.045) and the occurrence of spinal cord anomalies (OR = 6.715, 95% CI: 1.694–26.615, p = 0.007) were independent predictors of surgical neurological complications.ConclusionsPreoperative spinal cord anomalies and a higher kyphosis angle are independent risk factors for postoperative neurological complications. A comprehensive preoperative evaluation is essential for optimizing surgical strategies in these high‐risk patients.

Introduction

Severe spinal deformities are defined by significant curvature of the spine, including severe scoliosis, kyphosis, and kyphoscoliosis. These conditions can be further classified according to their typical clinical presentations. The severity of spinal deformities is typically assessed based on the spinal curvature angle. Adolescent and adult spinal deformities with a Cobb angle greater than 50° [1], or children's spinal deformities greater than 40° are typically regarded as severe scoliosis [2]. In contrast, spinal deformities with a kyphosis angle exceeding 80° can be classified as severe spinal deformities [3, 4, 5]. Severe kyphosis may be congenital or acquired (e.g., tuberculosis or trauma). Congenital cases are frequently accompanied by an abnormality of one or more vertebrae and may worsen over time. The causes of severe spinal deformities can be classified into the following categories: idiopathic scoliosis, neuromuscular disorders, congenital spinal deformities, syndromic conditions, traumatic injuries, and metabolic bone diseases. Additionally, the severity of spinal deformities is influenced by factors such as the age of onset and the rate of progression during growth and development.

At present, surgical intervention remains the primary treatment for severe spinal deformities. The occurrence of complications is a significant determinant of the overall outcome and patient satisfaction in the context of surgical intervention. In general, the overall complication rate for surgical treatment of spinal deformity ranges from 17.0% to 71.5% [6]. Neurological complications (NC) represent the most significant prognostic component of surgery‐related complications. These include postoperative spinal cord dysfunction, spinal cord ischemia, postoperative neurologic deficits, and so forth. The incidence of these complications ranges from 2.4% to as high as 16.7%, depending on the specific patient population, surgical techniques, and deformity types [7, 8, 9, 10]. To minimize the risk of these NC, it is essential to employ meticulous surgical techniques, intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring, and careful preoperative planning. The timely detection and management of neurological issues are crucial to optimizing the prognosis of patients who have undergone surgical treatment for severe spinal deformities.

The extent of NC resulting from surgical treatment of severe spinal deformities can be evaluated across multiple dimensions. First, a neurological examination and grading should be conducted in order to obtain a comprehensive understanding of the patient's neurological function before and after the surgical procedure. Second, magnetic resonance imaging and electrophysiological studies, such as somatosensory evoked potentials (SEP) and motor evoked potentials (MEP), can be conducted for assessment purposes [11]. In addition, some functional outcome measures and grading systems specific to NC are also frequently employed. A growing body of research has demonstrated that postoperative impairment of neurologic function has a significant impact on the prognosis of patients with spinal deformities [7, 8, 9]. The existing research on this topic is limited. Nevertheless, only a limited number of studies have investigated the potential risk factors for NC following surgical treatment of severe spinal deformities. In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of data on clinical, imaging, intraoperative, and postoperative conditions from a cohort of 130 patients with severe spinal deformities. Our aim was to identify potential risk factors for the occurrence of NC in order to provide evidence to support the development and improvement of related orthopedic spinal surgery.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This study mainly included patients with severe spinal deformities from our center who underwent posterior spinal fusion from January 2002 to May 2022. By analyzing the information collected (including demographic parameters, preoperative conditions, imaging parameters, surgical conditions, postoperative complications, and follow‐up information of these patients), we searched for potential risk factors associated with NC in these patients.

In order to assess the neurological alterations and the occurrence of NC in patients more objectively, we collected data at three different stages:

Preoperative period: Spinal cord anomalies were assessed using preoperative 3.0 T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including T1‐weighted, T2‐weighted, and constructive interference in steady state (CISS) or heavily T2‐weighted sequences. Typical MRI findings included: syringomyelia (appearing as hyperintense lesions on T2‐weighted images); tethered cord (with a low‐lying conus medullaris and thickened filum terminale); and other malformations such as myelocele. All spinal cord anomalies were confirmed by the first two authors, based on both imaging appearance and clinical context.

Intraoperative period: We monitored intraoperative neuromonitoring alarms (IONM alarms), which were defined as a ≥ 50% decrease in MEP amplitude or a ≥ 50% decrease in SSEP amplitude, with or without prolonged latency (> 10%). We also recorded the occurrence of cerebrospinal fluid leaks, nerve root and spinal cord injuries, and other special circumstances. When an IONM alarm was detected, the surgical team initiated a standardized protocol including: immediate pause of the procedure, reassessment of intraoperative positioning, irrigation and decompression of the spinal canal if necessary, intraoperative fluoroscopy to exclude mechanical compression, and administration of high‐dose methylprednisolone.

Postoperative follow‐up, we recorded the occurrence and regression details of NC in the patients, which represent the most critical prognostic component of surgery‐related complications. NC included postoperative spinal cord dysfunction, spinal cord ischemia, postoperative neurologic deficits, device‐related neurologic complications, and postoperative spinal cord edema or hematoma.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

In order to ensure the appropriate sample inclusion and the feasibility of the data analysis, we screened the patients who visited our center according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) patients who were diagnosed with scoliosis, kyphosis, or kyphoscoliosis based on imaging criteria and clinical evidence; (2) patients undergoing osteotomy at the apex followed by posterior instrumented fusion; (3) patients who met the criteria of scoliosis or kyphosis greater than 80°; (4) patients with a follow‐up duration of at least 24 months.

Cases that met the following exclusion criteria were screened and excluded from the study: (1) presence of an active spine tumor or infection; (2) patients with previous treatment or any previous history of spinal surgery; (3) absence of intraoperative neuromonitoring; (4) incomplete or inaccurate documentation of neurological function changes before and after surgery.

This research was performed in accordance with the guidelines set out by the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Peking Union Medical College Hospital Institutional Review Board (Approval No. K2326). Informed consent was obtained from all participating patients.

Interventions

Generally, to ensure consistency and reduce variability, we included only patients who underwent posterior spinal osteotomy with instrumentation or fusion. Various spinal osteotomy techniques were performed to treat patients with severe kyphoscoliosis. As for osteotomies, vertebral column resection (VCR) was used for severe, rigid kyphoscoliosis, especially in cases with significant spinal cord compression or deformities exceeding 100°; hemivertebra resection was considered for localized severe deformities with good overall spinal flexibility, particularly in asymmetrical deformities caused by hemivertebrae; pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) and Ponte osteotomy were considered when encountering relatively moderate kyphoscoliosis. Cases with significant spinal cord compression or neurological deficits prioritized VCR or hemivertebra resection for decompression. Internal fixation was selected for strong stabilization and supporting hemivertebra resections, while adolescents with ongoing bone growth were treated with growing rod fixation to minimize growth restriction. The number and location of resected vertebrae and fusion levels were determined on the basis of the patient's scoliosis or kyphosis and the stage of injury of the spine, and were finalized by the surgeons. In addition, complete IONM was performed during all surgeries, and the patient's pre‐ and post‐operative neurologic function changes were recorded in detail.

Data Collection

In regard to the data, we collated and integrated patients' clinical information with imaging parameters and surgical information across a range of modules. In terms of demographic parameters, data pertaining to gender, age, and follow‐up time were collected. In terms of surgical conditions, the type of deformity, spinal cord anomalies, ventilation dysfunction, traction history, and preoperative neurologic functional disabilities were recorded. The relevant neurological postoperative complications were meticulously documented, and neurological recovery was evaluated using the ASIA impairment scale during outpatient visits or structured telephone follow‐up for patients with NC. With regard to imaging parameters, the patient's imaging data were reviewed, and the relevant parameters were obtained with the assistance of computer programs. These included the main curve, secondary curve, coronal/sagittal/total deformity angular ratio (C/S/T‐DAR), apical vertebral translation (AVT), coronal balance (CB), thoracic kyphosis (TK), lumbar lordosis (LL), sagittal vertical axis (SVA), T1‐S1 height, and the corresponding changes from preoperative to postoperative and final follow‐up assessments.

Statistical Analysis

Independent t‐tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests were used for continuous variables, and chi‐square or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables. The severity of NC following the surgical treatment of severe spinal deformities can be assessed in several dimensions. After that, indicators with significant differences or clinically meaningful differences between the two groups were screened and subjected to separate univariate logistic analyses. These variables were subjected to variance inflation factor analysis to monitor covariance between the variables and were deleted or adjusted as appropriate. Finally, multivariate logistic regression models were constructed using the screened variables, and necessary diagnostic tests such as residual analysis were refined. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Characterization of Cohort

A total of 130 cases diagnosed with severe scoliosis, kyphosis, or kyphoscoliosis were included in this study. The basic preoperative information and demographic parameters of these cases were collected in groups and organized (Table 1). The mean age of the total cohort is 21.4 ± 15.3 years, and the female‐to‐male ratio of the entire cohort is 50%. With regard to the diagnosis of spinal deformity, 66.9% (n = 87) were diagnosed with congenital deformity. Furthermore, a greater proportion of cases were diagnosed with tuberculous deformity (16 cases, 12.3%), neuromuscular deformity (4 cases, 3.0%), and idiopathic deformity (4 cases, 3.0%). With regard to curve type, the percentage of kyphoscoliosis was 53.1% (n = 69), while kyphosis and scoliosis accounted for 23.8% (n = 31) and 23.1% (n = 30), respectively. Following a statistical analysis, it was determined that 12 cases (9.2%) exhibited preoperative ventilatory dysfunction. Additionally, 29.8% (n = 38) of the cohort demonstrated preoperative neurological dysfunction, while 13.1% (n = 17) exhibited spinal cord anomalies, as identified through imaging and clinical symptoms.

Differences in Demographic Characteristics

The patients were divided into two groups: the NC Group (n = 18) and the non‐NC Group (n = 112). This division was based on the presence or absence of postoperative NC. The differences between the two groups were then analyzed, and relevant information for the 18 patients in the NC Group was collected and presented (Table 2). Among these 18 patients, follow‐up ASIA grades were available for all cases. Thirteen patients (72.2%) achieved full neurological recovery (ASIA grade E), while five patients (27.8%) retained mild deficits but reached ASIA Grade D. None of the patients remained at Grade C or below at the time of the latest follow‐up. The statistics for the main curve of case 18 were not available due to the unavailability of the imaging data. No statistically significant differences were observed in preoperative and demographic information, including age (p = 0.323), sex (p = 0.448), curve type (p = 0.290), C/S/T‐DAR (p = 0.243/0.128/0.366), preoperative ventilation dysfunction ratio (p = 0.232), and preoperative neurological dysfunction ratio (p = 0.171). Conversely, a statistically significant discrepancy was observed in the prevalence of spinal cord anomalies between the NC and non‐NC groups (38.9% vs. 8.9%, p = 0.001).

Changes in Spinal Alignment

The changes in spinal alignment of patients were monitored and recorded at three time points: preoperative baseline, postoperative review, and last follow‐up. Patients were grouped according to the presence or absence of postoperative NC, and the analysis of variance was performed (Table 3). In general, the two groups demonstrated statistically significant discrepancies in the secondary curve, TK, LL, and SVA, irrespective of the baseline, postoperative, or last follow‐up. In the non‐NC group, the mean angle of scoliosis at baseline was 98.1° ± 20.9°, while in the NC group, it was 107° ± 8.0°, which was a statistically significant difference (p = 0.037). Similarly, the angle of kyphosis exhibited notable disparities at the baseline measurement (98.2° ± 19.5° vs. 112.4° ± 24.6°, p = 0.018). However, there was no statistically significant difference in either the postoperative angle or the correction rate of scoliosis and kyphosis, or the angle and correction rate at the last follow‐up. It is noteworthy that the baseline AVT (44.7 ± 28.7 mm vs. 59.9 ± 40.5 mm, p = 0.074), CB (15.8 ± 14.6 mm vs. 23.6 ± 22.4 mm, p = 0.078), and T1–S1 height (323.0 ± 68.3 mm vs. 290.9 ± 72.5 mm, p = 0.085) of the two groups exhibited some degree of difference, though not to a statistically significant extent. Differences in Surgical Details

The surgery‐related and intraoperative‐related information was organized and presented (Table 4). Similarly, the cohort was divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of postoperative NC, and the differences between the groups were analyzed. The mean operative time for the total cohort was 5.8 ± 1.4 h, the mean blood loss was 1240 ± 920 mL, and the mean number of resected vertebrae involved in the surgery was 1.70 ± 0.92, accompanied by a fusion level of 10.7 ± 3.0. During the surgical procedure, among the 25 patients who exhibited IONM alarms, all had MEP‐related signal attenuation, either unilateral or bilateral. No isolated SSEP alarms were recorded in this cohort. More importantly, a significantly higher IONM alarm ratio of the spinal cord in the NC group than in the non‐NC group (38.9% vs. 15.9%, p = 0.022) is revealed. In comparison, no notable discrepancies were observed between the two groups with respect to the adoption of anterior column reconstruction (ACR), the employment of intraoperative temporary rods, and the incidence of pleural rupture. No cases of cerebrospinal fluid leak were observed in these patients. Furthermore, a notable discrepancy was observed between the two groups in terms of operative time (6.3 ± 1.4 h vs. 5.7 ± 1.4 h, p = 0.095).

Surgical and Clinical Predictors of NC

To identify potential predictors of NC occurrence, we conducted a screening of available metrics based on their statistical significance and potential variability. For alignment‐related metrics, we reassessed with pre and postoperative change values. Unadjusted univariate regression revealed that a higher baseline angle of kyphosis (OR = 1.033, 95% CI: 1.007–1.058, p = 0.012), the occurrence of spinal cord anomalies (OR = 5.982, 95% CI: 1.894–18.896, p = 0.002), and the occurrence of monitoring alarm (OR = 3.369, 95% CI: 1.144–9.922, p = 0.028) were all associated with increased odds of occurrence of surgical NC. Furthermore, a higher baseline AVT, CB, the magnitude of correction of T1‐S1 height, longer operating time, and more preoperative neurological dysfunction also demonstrated some degree of association with the occurrence of surgical NC. Ultimately, the multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that a higher angle of kyphosis (OR = 1.027, 95% CI: 1.001–1.055, p = 0.045) and the occurrence of spinal cord anomalies (OR = 6.715, 95% CI: 1.694–26.615, p = 0.007) were independent predictors of surgical NC (Table 5).

Discussion

Summary of Key Findings

In this retrospective cohort study of 130 patients with severe spinal deformities, we identified preoperative spinal cord anomalies and preoperative kyphosis angle as independent risk factors for postoperative neurological complications (NC). Patients with spinal cord anomalies or larger kyphosis angles had a significantly higher likelihood of postoperative neurological issues. These findings underscore the critical importance of comprehensive preoperative imaging assessment and individualized surgical planning in reducing the risk of NC during complex deformity correction surgeries.

Overview of Surgical Complications in Spinal Deformity Correction

The occurrence of surgical complications in the context of spinal deformities, such as lateral kyphosis, has been a significant concern for surgeons since the widespread adoption of orthopedic internal fixation for the spine. The overall complication rate for spinal deformity surgery has been reported to range from 10% to 80%, with the specific incidence varying depending on the patient population, surgical access, and other risk factors [12, 13, 14]. The most common complications associated with spinal deformity surgery can be broadly classified into the following categories: NC, wound‐related complications, cardiopulmonary and thromboembolic complications, implant‐related complications, and alignment‐related complications [15, 16]. Certain risk factors have been identified that can increase the likelihood of complications, such as older age, comorbidities, previous spinal surgeries, and the complexity of the surgical procedure [12, 13]. Literature indicates that the use of pedicle screw fixation in spinal deformity surgery carries a risk of NC, which is a significant concern in adult spinal deformity surgery, with reported rates ranging from 0.6% to 40% [17, 18, 19]. Careful patient selection, surgical planning, and intraoperative monitoring are important to minimize the risk of NC in these complex spinal deformity surgeries.

Rationale and Significance of the Present Study

In the surgical management of adult spinal deformities, several independent factors, such as the type of osteotomy and the extent of the deformity, have been associated with an increased risk of NC [20]. A substantial degree of deformity is frequently associated with an elevated risk of surgical complications. This underscores the necessity for a more comprehensive investigation into the risk factors for surgical complications in patients with severe deformities. Such an inquiry could facilitate a deeper understanding of the available treatment options and modalities. A variability analysis of a cohort of 177 patients with this type of severe deformity in three dimensions (preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative) was conducted by Chen et al. [21] The analysis identified potential differences in osteotomy method, spinal cord function, angle of kyphosis, whether preoperative halo gravity traction was performed, and operative time. Moreover, univariate and independent risk factor analyses related to surgery have emerged as a prominent focus within the domain of surgical complication analysis in recent years. The present study undertook a risk factor analysis of a cohort of 130 individuals with severe spinal deformities, with the objective of identifying the potential causes of NC in this group of patients from multiple dimensions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the inaugural univariate and independent risk factor analysis of neurologic complications in a severe deformity cohort within the field.

Key Findings From the Present Study

In our study, we divided the cohort of 130 individuals with severe deformities into two groups: the NC group and the non‐NC group. We initially analyzed the differences between the two groups based on demographic characteristics, imaging parameters, and surgery‐related information. The results demonstrated that the two groups exhibited notable discrepancies in the angle of scoliosis and the angle of kyphosis. Additionally, the ratio of spinal cord anomalies and the incidence of IONM alarms exhibited significant differences with regard to categorical variables. Regarding IONM, prior research has demonstrated that IONM can assist surgeons in identifying and addressing potential causes of neurophysiologic alterations, thereby enabling prompt interventions. However, the presence of false‐positive or false‐negative signals can render this prediction imperfect [22, 23]. The results of the difference‐in‐difference analysis indicate a statistically significant discrepancy in the proportion of patients exhibiting IONM‐detected abnormalities between the two groups. This suggests that patients presenting with relevant IONM alarms are more likely to experience postoperative neurological impairment. In a meta‐analysis conducted by Daniel [24], it was revealed that IONM plays a role in identifying neurological deficits. However, the introduction of IONM does not result in a reduction in the occurrence of neurological injury events. To minimize the occurrence of neurological impairment events and further reduce the impact on patients in the event of relevant events, our center conducted full MEP monitoring of both sides of the patient's MEP during the surgical treatment process. In the event of an MEP alarm, the patient was examined according to the surgical situation to determine whether there was any compression of the spinal cord, and the intraoperative imaging fluoroscopy was used. In addition, methylprednisolone shock was administered if necessary.

In the univariate risk factor analysis, the angle of kyphosis, spinal cord anomalies, and IONM alarm were identified as significant factors influencing the occurrence of NC. The final results indicated that preoperative spinal cord anomalies and preoperative angle of kyphosis were independent risk factors for NC in surgery for severe spinal deformities.

The Role of Spinal Cord Anomalies in Neurological Complications

Prior research has demonstrated that patients with preoperative spinal cord anomalies or neurological deficits are at an elevated risk of developing NC following spinal deformity surgeries [5, 25, 26]. Additionally, the rate of postoperative revision is increased in patients with preoperative spinal cord anomalies [27]. Revision surgery itself is identified as a risk factor for NC in the surgery [20]. Spinal cord anomalies can be broadly classified into three categories: spinal cord malformations, spinal cord cysts, and spinal cord dysplasia. In our study, the most commonly identified spinal cord anomalies included syringomyelia, tethered cord, and myelocele. These anomalies have been associated with functional deficits, motor and sensory conduction abnormalities, and neurologic deterioration [28, 29, 30]. We reported preoperative spinal cord anomalies as an independent risk factor for NC in surgery for severe deformities. Potential explanations for this association include: (1) Mechanical vulnerability of neural tissues: Spinal cord anomalies may render neural tissues more susceptible to injury, thereby elevating the risk of intraoperative injury. For instance, developmental malformations may lead to abnormal vascularization, such as arteriovenous malformations, which can compromise perfusion and oxygenation of the spinal cord tissue [31, 32]. This impaired microcirculation increases the risk of ischemic injury during corrective procedures. (2) Altered anatomical structure and cord mobility: Structural anomalies such as syringomyelia, tethered cord, or spinal cord kinking can distort normal cord architecture, reduce spinal cord mobility, and create abnormal tension within the neural axis [33]. In posterior orthopedic surgery, these fixed points or abnormal attachments may subject the cord to traction, compression, or rotational stress, increasing the risk of neurologic injury [34]. Additionally, such alterations may affect the exposure and visualization of the surgical field, thereby complicating the intraoperative strategy. (3) Impaired functional reserve and postoperative recovery: Spinal cord anomalies may be associated with pre‐existing subclinical deficits or impaired neurophysiological reserve. Even minor intraoperative insults could therefore translate into clinically significant neurological complications. Furthermore, the presence of congenital abnormalities may reduce the regenerative or adaptive capacity of neural tissues, negatively affecting postoperative recovery. Given that spinal cord anomalies are independent risk factors for neurologic complications in orthopedic spine surgery, it is imperative that surgeons assess the presence of spinal cord anomalies through preoperative imaging, such as magnetic resonance imaging, in order to gain a comprehensive understanding of the patient's neurologic status and perform a thorough risk assessment prior to surgery. By taking the patient's spinal cord condition into account, surgeons can create a more personalized surgical plan and enhance the monitoring and management of patients with spinal cord anomalies before and after surgery. This can be achieved through effective communication and patient education, which helps to mitigate the potential risks associated with spinal cord anomalies.

Influence of Kyphosis Angle on Neurological Complications

A growing number of clinical studies have confirmed that larger preoperative kyphosis angles are associated with an elevated risk of NC. In the study by Chen, it was found that preoperative kyphosis angles constituted a risk factor for NC in severe and rigid spinal deformity correction [21]. Another study revealed that the presence of sharp, angular kyphosis deformities is associated with an increased risk of postoperative NC in adult spinal deformity surgery [20]. Our study, after correction through logistic regression, identified the angle of kyphosis as an independent risk factor for postoperative NC in orthopedic surgery for severe deformities. This implies that even in the cohort of spinal deformity patients with kyphosis exceeding 80°, the probability of NC in the postoperative period remains directly proportional to the degree of kyphosis. We categorized the potential reasons into three groups: (1) Altered mechanical loading. An increase in the kyphosis angle results in alterations to the biomechanical loading of the spine, thereby subjecting the spine to elevated levels of intraoperative stress and strain. This, in turn, elevates the risk of intraoperative nerve injury. Furthermore, a higher angle of kyphosis may result in a force line bias of the spine, potentially increasing the incidence of proximal junctional kyphosis [35], affecting spinal stability, and leading to a higher complication rate. (2) The intricacy of the surgical approach. The potential for larger kyphosis angles to affect the surgeon's surgical field of view and to complicate correction during surgery, such as the selection of alternative osteotomy types or surgical approaches, should be considered [36, 37]. (3) Resulting in further impairment of neurological function during the surgical procedure: if the angle of posterior convexity is considerable, it may result in the traction of the nerve roots or spinal cord [28], thereby elevating the probability of postoperative neurological dysfunction. Should the blood supply to the spinal cord be affected, the recovery of postoperative neurological function may be compromised. In light of the considerable influence of the posterior convexity angle on the incidence of NC in orthopedic surgery, it is imperative that a more comprehensive preoperative assessment of this angle be undertaken to identify high‐risk patients and to develop a well‐informed surgical plan that will optimize surgical outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, we focused specifically on a cohort of patients with severe spinal deformities, all of whom met strict radiographic criteria (scoliosis or kyphosis > 80°), ensuring that the findings are directly applicable to high‐risk clinical scenarios. Second, all patients underwent posterior osteotomy and instrumentation procedures at a single high‐volume center, with standardized intraoperative neuromonitoring, which minimizes variability due to surgical technique. Third, the study incorporated a comprehensive set of clinical, radiographic, and intraoperative data, allowing for a multidimensional analysis of risk factors for NC. To our knowledge, this is one of the few studies to perform both univariate and multivariate analyses of neurologic complications specifically in the setting of severe spinal deformity surgery.

There are also several limitations of this study: First, the retrospective design may introduce recall bias or misclassification of certain variables, particularly when relying on patient records for long‐term follow‐up data. Although we employed detailed data collection protocols, the possibility of missing or incomplete data from the period between 2002 and 2022 remains. Second, the sample size of 130 patients, although sufficient for preliminary analysis, limits the generalizability of our findings to broader populations with severe spinal deformities. Given the rarity of some specific spinal conditions and the high variability in surgical approaches, a larger, multicenter cohort would strengthen the conclusions and allow for more robust subgroup analyses. Third, while we utilized advanced imaging techniques and IONM to assess preoperative and intraoperative neurological status, there are inherent limitations in the sensitivity and specificity of these methods. False positives or negatives in IONM signals could have influenced the surgical decisions and potentially impacted the occurrence of complications. Fourth, although we successfully collected long‐term ASIA grades for all patients who developed postoperative neurological complications, functional assessments, and MRI‐based spinal cord morphology grading were still missing in many patients without complications, as some were subjectively satisfied with the surgical outcome and functional recovery and did not return for regular follow‐up. This limited our ability to perform systematic comparisons across the entire cohort. Future studies should establish more standardized follow‐up schedules and integrate consistent functional assessment protocols to ensure the completeness and accuracy of outcome evaluation. Finally, our study did not fully explore the long‐term functional outcomes of patients beyond NC. Future studies should include a more comprehensive evaluation of postoperative recovery, functional mobility, and quality of life, which are critical in assessing the overall success of severe spinal deformity surgeries.

Conclusions

This study identified preoperative spinal cord anomalies and kyphosis angle as independent risk factors for NC in severe spinal deformity surgeries. Patients with spinal cord anomalies or larger kyphosis angles had a significantly higher likelihood of postoperative neurological issues. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive preoperative assessment and individualized surgical planning to mitigate the risk of NC in this high‐risk population.
