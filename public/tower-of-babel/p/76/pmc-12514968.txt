Integrating Cell Population Data With Hematology Analyzer Messages for Improved Detection of Immature White Blood Cells and Laboratory Efficiency
Authors: Lin‐Chen Hsu, Tsung‐Han Tsai, Yu‐Hua Lin, Kai‐Yun Lu, Wen‐Chun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImproving efficiency and reducing turnaround time are crucial in clinical laboratories. While automated analyzers such as the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 streamline workflow, subtle abnormalities like blasts and immature granulocytes (IGs) may be missed, especially in the absence of WBC‐related suspect messages. This study evaluated whether integrating cell population data (CPD) with instrument messages could enhance detection accuracy.MethodsA total of 587 blood samples were analyzed and classified with (Group A) or without (Group N) WBC‐related suspect messages. Manual slide review (MSR) and digital morphology (DM) served as reference standards. CPD parameters were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and Youden's index to establish optimal detection rules.ResultsAmong the 587 samples, 55.3% were in Group A and 44.7% in Group N, with Group A showing significantly lower red blood cell, hemoglobin, and platelet counts (p < 0.001). The DxH 900 flagged 37.7% and 55.4% of samples with blast‐ and IG‐related messages, respectively. However, it failed to detect blasts in 20.6% of cases confirmed by MSR and 21.3% by DM, and missed IGs in 16.2% of MSR‐confirmed and 18.7% of DM‐confirmed positive samples. CPD parameters (e.g., SD_V_NE, MN_V_NE) identified additional true positives, with area under the curve (AUC) values > 0.9. Integrating CPD rules with WBC‐related suspect messages improved sensitivity/specificity to 91.9%/99.0% for blasts and 91.5%/96.7% for IGs, using MSR as the reference standard.ConclusionsIntegrating CPD parameters with WBC‐related suspect messages provides a sensitive, automated secondary screening strategy that reduces false negatives and enhances hematologic diagnostics.Keywords: blasts, cell population data, digital morphology, immature granulocytes, manual slide review

Introduction

Improving efficiency and reducing turnaround time are critical in modern clinical laboratories, given the increasing demands and skilled personnel shortage. Automated hematology analyzers (AHAs) are integral to routine blood testing, offering high precision, accuracy, and scalability, significantly streamlining laboratory processes [1, 2, 3]. However, challenges persist, particularly in detecting subtle abnormalities, including immature white blood cells (WBCs), which often require manual slide reviews (MSR) for accurate diagnosis [4, 5]. Hence, studies have highlighted the need for more advanced automation to reduce manual intervention while maintaining diagnostic accuracy [6, 7].

Using algorithms, AHAs like the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 identify potential abnormalities by generating suspect messages that flag the presence of atypical cell populations, including blasts and immature granulocytes (IGs), which are crucial markers for various hematological conditions. Notably, IGs, known as precursors to neutrophils, are usually confined to bone marrow, and their presence in peripheral blood (PB) signals increased myeloid activity, which is often linked to infections, inflammation, hematological disorders, or bone marrow infiltration. Thus, monitoring IG levels aids in diagnosing these conditions and assessing treatment progress [8, 9]. However, these AHAs still rely on predefined thresholds and may miss subtle but clinically significant findings, causing false negatives.

Cell population data (CPD) from AHAs offer a detailed view of WBC characteristics, surpassing traditional complete blood counts (CBCs) by analyzing cell size, structure, and light scatter [10, 11, 12, 13]. Through this precision, subtle morphological changes can be detected, making CPD valuable for identifying immature or abnormal cells [12]. Parameters such as mean cell volume (MN_V) and axial light scatter (AL2) help differentiate between mature and immature cells, aiding in diagnosing conditions such as infections, inflammatory disorders, and leukemia [14, 15]. Moreover, CPD can identify early granulated cells (EGCs), blasts, and IGs, proving its high value for prompt and precise hematological evaluations [12, 14]. Integrating CPD parameters with instrument messages also reportedly enhances the detection of early indicators of hematological disorders, including myelodysplastic syndromes and acute leukemia, highlighting the need for ongoing innovation [16, 17, 18]. The Beckman Coulter DxH 900 AHA provides five standard flags and 70 CPD metrics related to WBCs [3, 19], allowing users to customize CPD rules alongside CBC parameters for tailored alarms, enhancing abnormality detection and triggering MSR for accurate diagnoses [7, 12, 14].

Although recent studies have integrated CPD parameters with traditional WBC flags to improve cell detection, they often focus narrowly on specific abnormalities, limiting broader diagnostic application [12, 14]. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate whether integrating comprehensive CPD metrics with the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 instrument's WBC‐related suspect messages can enhance the detection of immature WBCs, specifically blasts and IGs, compared to standard methods. Importantly, the study also assessed the diagnostic utility of CPD in samples without WBC‐related suspect messages, aiming to determine whether CPD parameters could serve as an effective secondary screening tool to reduce false negatives in routine hematology workflows.

Materials And Methods

Study Samples

This retrospective study, conducted from May to December 2023 at the Department of Laboratory Medicine, An Nan Hospital, China Medical University, used 600 anonymized clinical blood samples (IRB Approval No.: TMANH112‐REC011). All samples were collected as part of routine blood testing performed for standard clinical care, with no additional procedures conducted, thereby preserving patient anonymity. Blood specimens anticoagulated with K3‐EDTA were processed within 2–4 h to ensure the accuracy of CPD parameters. After excluding 13 samples due to incomplete data, a total of 587 samples were included in the final analysis (318 males, 269 females; median age: 60 years; range: 0–96) (Table 1). Study Design

Participants were enrolled according to age group classifications defined by the study design, irrespective of sex, race, ethnicity, or disease status. Patients were excluded if their samples showed signs of hemolysis, significant clotting, or insufficient volume. Samples with missing data were also excluded.

This study specifically examined “Suspect Messages” generated by the DxH 900 automated hematology analyzer, predefined diagnostic alerts indicating abnormalities in WBCs, red blood cells (RBCs), or platelets. These include 14 categories such as IG, left shift, LY blasts, MO blasts, NE blasts, variant LY, dimorphic RBCs, and giant platelets. Unlike general system flags related to instrument issues, these messages reflect potential hematologic abnormalities.

Among these, we focused exclusively on “WBC‐related suspect messages”, which include six categories: IG, left shift, LY blasts, MO blasts, NE blasts, and variant LY—collectively referred to as “IG‐related suspect messages.” Due to the clinical importance of detecting blast cells, particular emphasis was placed on LY blasts, MO blasts, NE blasts, and variant LY, designated as “blast‐related suspect messages.” When a sample is described as lacking “blast‐related suspect messages,” it means none of these four blast indicators were triggered, although IG or left shift messages may still have been present. The presence of blast cells may also trigger other suspect messages. This classification aligns with definitions provided in the DxH 900 reference manual (UniCel DxH 900 Series with System Manager Software, v1, p.234).

Based on the presence or absence of WBC‐related suspect messages, eligible participants were categorized into two groups: Group A (with WBC‐related suspect message) and Group N (without any WBC‐related suspect messages). Group N consisted of blood samples processed during the same study period that did not trigger any WBC‐related suspect messages (e.g., blasts or immature granulocytes) or system‐level alerts. These samples were randomly selected from routine outpatient and inpatient specimens and all underwent both MSR and digital morphology (DM) for consistency and valid comparison.

Importantly, CPD parameters are generated for all samples regardless of whether suspect messages are present, as part of the routine CBC analysis. Standard Hematology Reference Method

The MSR is the gold standard for differential WBC counting and was used here to assess the performance of the DxH 900 Hematology System (Beckman Coulter Inc., Brea, CA, USA). Peripheral blood (PB) smears were prepared with the DxH Slidemaker Stainer II (Beckman Coulter, Miami, Florida, USA) using the Wright–Giemsa method. MSRs were conducted by experienced staff, and the differential counts were performed by counting 100 WBCs per sample, in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines (CLSI H20‐A2). To reflect modern clinical workflows, DM using the CellaVision DM9600 (CellaVision, Lund, Sweden) was also employed as an additional reference. Samples with over 1 blast cell or other abnormal cells (e.g., promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes, or ≥ 5% band neutrophils) were classified as blast‐ or IG‐positive. Detailed information is available in Supporting Information S1.

Advanced Analysis Using the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 AHA

All blood samples were analyzed using the Beckman Coulter DxH 900, which utilizes Volume, Conductivity, and Scatter (VCS) technology to assess leukocyte by volume (via impedance), nuclear features (via conductivity), and cytoplasmic complexity (via light scatter). The analyzer automatically produces CPD parameters, including key metrics (e.g., SD_V_NE, MN_V_NE, SD_AL2_NE) relevant to detecting IGs and nucleated RBCs. In this study, CPD values were extracted via the TUNYEN Laboratory Automation Middleware system and analyzed using thresholds or combination rules derived from operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. For laboratories without middleware, alternative extraction methods are detailed in the Supporting Information S1. These CPD metrics were used to enhance the identification of abnormal cells and aid in comprehensive pathological screening. A complete list of CPD parameters and detailed information is provided in Supporting Information S1 and Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. ROC curve analysis evaluated diagnostic performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values classified as acceptable (0.7–0.8), good (0.8–0.9), or excellent (> 0.9) [20, 21]. Optimal cutoff values were determined by Youden's index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), diagnostic efficiency, and AUC (with 95% confidence intervals) were calculated for CPD rules in detecting blasts and abnormal cells. Group A and N were compared using the Mann–Whitney U‐test, with further analysis of significant CPD parameters. Significant parameters identified from ROC analysis were further examined. Levene's test assessed variance equality (p < 0.01); t‐tests were used as appropriate. Summary statistics followed rounding rules, and a two‐tailed p < 0.01 was considered statistically significant [22, 23]. Analyses were conducted using SPSS v29.0.1 and R v4.3.3. Detailed information is available in Supporting Information S1.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

Of the 587 samples analyzed, 55.3% were classified as Group A and 44.7% as Group N. The distribution of patient types (inpatients and outpatients) and departmental origins differed significantly between the two groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1). The overall study design, including participant grouping and data analysis procedures, is outlined in Figure 1.

Distinct Hematological Profiles in Groups With and Without WBC‐Related Suspect Messages

Of the 11 FDA‐approved CBC parameters analyzed, 7 showed significant differences between Groups A and N (p < 0.01) (Table S2). Notably, Group A exhibited significantly lower RBC counts (3.46 vs. 4.42 × 106/μL), hemoglobin levels (10.2 vs. 12.8 g/dL), and hematocrit volume (30.9% vs. 38.4%), but higher RDW (14.9% vs. 13.8%) (all: p < 0.001), suggesting potential microcytic anemia. Platelet counts were lower (82 vs. 232 × 103/μL), while mean platelet volume (8.0 vs. 7.6 × 103/μL) and monocyte distribution width (29.4% vs. 19.7%) were elevated (all: p < 0.001). WBC counts were slightly lower (5.3 vs. 6.5 × 103/μL, p = 0.033), with no significant differences in MCV, MCH, or MCHC (Table S2).

Among the 56 CPD parameters analyzed, 40 differed significantly between Groups A and N (p < 0.01) (Table 2, Table S3, Figure S1). Group A showed higher mean volumes for neutrophils (MN_V_NE: 153 vs. 136), lymphocytes (MN_V_LY: 94 vs. 88), monocytes (MN_V_MO: 180 vs. 166), and EGCs (MN_V_EGC: 174 vs. 146) (all: p < 0.001), but lower mean conductivity for neutrophils (MN_C_NE: 142 vs. 146) and EGCs (MN_C_EGC: 132 vs. 137) (all: p < 0.001). These findings suggest distinct cellular characteristics in Group A.

Using MSR as the reference (Table 3 upper panel), blasts were detected in 160 samples (160/587, 27.3%). Among these, 33 cases (33/160, 20.6%) did not trigger any blast‐related suspect message. Notably, 17 of these 33 showed non–blast‐related suspect messages (i.e., IGs or Left Shift), while the remaining 16 samples (10.0% of all blast‐positive cases) had no WBC‐related suspect messages at all, representing potential false negatives by the analyzer. For IG detection, MSR identified 284 IG‐positive samples (284/587, 48.4%). Among these, 46 samples (46/284, 16.2%) failed to trigger any IG‐related suspect message, indicating false negatives missed by the analyzer (Table 3).

Using DM (Table 3 lower panel), blasts were identified in 160 samples (27.3%), similar to MSR. Among them, 34 cases (34/160, 21.3%) did not trigger blast‐related suspect messages. Of these, 18 cases had non–blast‐related suspect messages, while 16 had no WBC‐related suspect messages, mirroring a 10.0% false‐negative rate. For IGs, DM identified 305 (305/587, 52.0%) IG‐positive samples, of which 57 samples (57/305, 18.7%) had no WBC‐related suspect messages, representing a subset of false negatives missed by the analyzer (Table 3).

Key CPD Parameters for the Enhanced Detection of Blasts and IGs

Logistic regression identified key CPD parameters with an odds ratio > 1 and high AUC values for both blasts and IG detection. For blasts detection, SD_V_NE, SD_AL2_NE, and MN_V_NE were most predictive; for IGs, SD_V_NE, SD_AL2_NE, and MN_V_EGC were selected (Table S4). ROC curve analysis identified 8 effective CPD combinations for blasts and 6 for IGs, all with AUC > 0.8 (Table 4). In comparison, the DxH 900 WBC‐related suspect messages alone achieved lower AUCs (0.752–0.758 for blasts; 0.751–0.759 for IGs). The best predictive performance was achieved using CPD parameter combinations, with an AUC of 0.916 for blasts (SD_V_NE, SD_AL2_NE, MN_V_NE) (Figures 2A,B, Table 4) and 0.911 for IGs (SD_V_NE, SD_V_LY, SD_V_MO) (Figure 2C,D, Table S5). These CPD‐based models outperformed individual CPD parameters and isolated WBC‐related suspect messages, providing more accurate and consistent predictions across both MSR and DM.

Improved Detection of Blasts and IGs by Combining CPD Parameters With Instrument Messages

The effectiveness of combining CPD rules with the instrument's WBC‐related suspect message for detecting blasts and IGs was further evaluated. Incorporating specific CPD parameters enhanced both sensitivity and specificity compared to using the WBC‐related suspect message alone. For blast detection, sensitivity increased to 91.9% (MSR) and 91.3% (DM), with specificity reaching 99.0% for both methods (Table 5). For IG detection, sensitivity improved to 91.5% (MSR) and 89.9% (DM), while specificity reached 96.7% and 97.5%, respectively (Table S6).

Representative Case: Enhanced Detection of IGs by CPD Integration

To further illustrate the clinical utility of CPD‐based screening, we present a representative case in which immature granulocytes were successfully detected by CPD parameters but missed by the initial instrument‐generated WBC‐related suspect messages (Figure S2). Sample ID 18854621 was collected from a 59‐year‐old female patient admitted to the Department of Hematology and Oncology for the management of right breast carcinoma. The Beckman Coulter DxH 900 analyzer did not issue any WBC‐related suspect messages for blasts or IGs. However, upon application of our CPD‐based detection rules, the sample was flagged for further review. Subsequent morphological analysis using the CellaVision DM9600 digital morphology system revealed the presence of two myelocytes, confirming the presence of IGs. This case illustrates how CPD integration enhances routine hematology diagnostics by detecting subtle abnormalities missed by conventional flag‐based workflows, effectively reducing false negatives.

Discussion

This study appears to be the first in Taiwan to integrate CPD parameters with automated instrument WBC‐related suspect messages using the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 AHA, aiming to improve the detection of immature white blood cells, including blasts and IGs. Using MSR as the reference standard, integrating CPD parameters significantly improved sensitivity and specificity, achieving 91.9% and 99.0% for blasts, and 91.5% and 96.7% for IGs, while also reducing false negatives compared to using WBC‐related suspect messages alone. Our findings demonstrate that CPD‐based models not only outperform individual CPD parameters but also surpass conventional WBC‐related suspect messages, making them a valuable adjunctive tool in routine hematology laboratory workflows.

Based on our findings, we propose a practical clinical workflow that incorporates CPD parameters as a secondary screening layer to enhance blast and IG detection. As shown in Figure 3, the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 analyzer generates CBC results and suspect messages. Laboratories may implement CPD rules either directly within the analyzer or via middleware software that processes both CPD data and WBC‐related suspect messages. Samples meeting predefined CPD thresholds trigger additional smear review (MSR or DM), while others proceed directly to reporting. Notably, this setup does not require manual retrieval or interpretation of CPD values by laboratory personnel, as the rule‐based evaluation occurs automatically within the instrument or middleware. This approach improves diagnostic sensitivity without increasing the manual workload associated with CPD analysis, supporting seamless integration into routine hematology practice.

Proposed Clinical Workflow for Integrating CPD Rules in Routine Hematology Practice. This diagram illustrates a practical workflow for integrating CPD parameters into routine hematology laboratory practice using the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 analyzer. Upon sample arrival, the AHA (DxH 900) generates test results and WBC‐related suspect messages. Two approaches are presented: (1) direct configuration of CPD rules within the hematology analyzer (AHA), or (2) configuration via middleware software capable of receiving WBC‐related suspect messages and CPD metrics. If predefined thresholds (based on 12 blast and 10 IG detection rules*) are triggered, the sample is flagged for further evaluation using manual slide review (MSR) or digital morphology (DM). Laboratories may choose to apply all or a subset of these rules depending on their diagnostic needs and workflow configuration. If not triggered, results are sent directly to the laboratory information system (LIS) and reported to the hospital information system (HIS). Importantly, this workflow does not require laboratory personnel to manually retrieve or interpret CPD values; all rule‐based evaluations are automated within the system. This workflow enables enhanced diagnostic accuracy without significantly increasing laboratory workload.

In standard laboratory workflows, MSR or DM is usually initiated when suspect messages or abnormal analyzer flags are generated. However, conventional CBC parameters and preset thresholds may overlook subtle morphological abnormalities, potentially leading to missed or incorrect diagnoses [24, 25, 26]. Although MSR remains the gold standard for morphological assessment, its time‐consuming nature limits its utility in high‐throughput settings [25]. Digital morphology systems, such as CellaVision DM9600, improve workflow efficiency but still require manual interpretation, which introduces variability [9]. Automated instrument messages provide rapid screening, yet their reliance on fixed thresholds increases the risk of false negatives [24]. Conversely, CPD parameters offer a more nuanced view by analyzing factors such as cell size, internal complexity, and light scatter, thereby providing a comprehensive assessment of WBC subtypes [12, 14, 15, 26].

Previous research have explored CPD‐based diagnostics. For instance, Urrechaga reviewed the clinical utility of leukocyte CPD in managing sepsis [12] and evaluated neutrophil CPD as biomarkers for acute bacterial infections [15], while Kim et al. assessed the efficacy of CPD combined with analyzer flags on the Beckman Coulter DxH 900 AHA for detecting blasts in hematologic diseases [14]. However, these studies often focused on specific clinical scenarios or single CPD parameters, limiting their generalizability and applicability to broader routine hematology workflows. Building upon these limitations, our study demonstrated that integrating the CPD parameters with the instrument WBC‐related suspect messages significantly enhanced the detection accuracy, particularly for IGs and blasts. Here, “WBC‐related suspect messages” refer specifically to DxH 900 alerts such as LY Blasts, MO Blasts, NE Blasts, IGs, Left Shift and Variant LY. We collectively termed all six WBC‐related suspect messages as “IG‐related suspect messages.” Due to the clinical significance of blast cell identification, special attention was given to LY blasts, MO blasts, NE blasts, and variant LY, which were specifically designated as “blast‐related suspect messages.” Notably, CPD models performed well even in WBC‐related suspect message‐negative samples, supporting their use as an effective secondary screening tool. Integrating CPD into standard protocols could improve sensitivity and reduce false negatives without added burden or resources.

Our study further demonstrated that suspect messages generated by the hematology analyzer correlated strongly with underlying hematological abnormalities, including anemia and platelet disorders. Distinct differences in RBC counts, platelet volume, and cell conductivity observed between groups with and without suspect messages emphasized the potential clinical utility of integrating analyzer‐generated flags with CPD parameters. However, when used as the sole trigger for smear review, WBC‐related suspect messages showed limited sensitivity, with false‐negative rates of 2.7% for blasts (16/587, based on both MSR and DM) and approximately 9.0% for IGs (7.8% by MSR and 9.7% by DM). To overcome this limitation, we applied 12 CPD rules for blasts and 10 for IGs. When these were integrated with WBC‐related suspect messages, detection accuracy improved markedly, achieving sensitivity and specificity of 91.9% and 99.0% for blasts, and 91.5% and 96.7% for IGs, respectively, using MSR as the reference standard. This integration substantially reduced false negatives, detecting 12 of 16 previously missed blast cases and 29 of 46 missed IG cases. Consequently, the false‐negative rates were lowered to 8.1% (MSR) and 8.8% (DM) for blasts, and 8.5% (MSR) and 11.1% (DM) for IGs (Table 5, Table S6).

Importantly, the CPD‐based approach proved particularly valuable in time‐sensitive clinical settings such as hemato‐oncology and emergency care. For example, a representative case in our cohort, flagged only by CPD despite the absence of WBC‐related suspect messages, showed confirmed IG presence via digital morphology, highlighting CPD's role as an effective secondary screening tool to support early and accurate clinical decision making.

Another key finding of this study was the clinical utility of specific CPD parameters, such as SD_V_NE and MN_V_EGC, in effectively differentiating between samples with or without suspect analyzer messages. Significant variations in these parameters between the two groups suggest their potential as sensitive markers for early identification of morphological changes indicative of IGs or blasts. Notably, SD_V_NE variations reflect shifts in neutrophil populations associated with inflammatory states or hematological abnormalities [26], while changes in MN_V_EGC are indicative of immature cell populations commonly observed in myelodysplastic syndromes or acute leukemia. These findings underscore the additional diagnostic value of CPD parameters, which may detect subtle cellular abnormalities beyond the scope of conventional CBC measurements.

The demographic analysis highlighted distinct differences in clinical characteristics between samples with WBC‐related suspect messages (Group A) and those without (Group N). For instance, Group A had a significantly larger proportion of samples from the hemato‐oncology department, where patients are more likely to present with hematological disorders such as myelodysplastic syndromes and acute leukemia. This group also had a higher percentage of inpatients (55.7% vs. 22.9%, p < 0.001) than Group N, suggesting that patients with more complex or severe conditions, often requiring hospitalization, are more prone to receive suspect messages. In contrast, samples without WBC‐related suspect messages were primarily obtained from emergency and general medical departments, often representing less severe or transient conditions such as acute infections or inflammatory responses. These observations underline the necessity of context‐specific interpretation of suspect flags and support tailored approaches in clinical diagnostics [25].

Customizing CPD algorithms according to patient demographics and clinical contexts can significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy. For instance, hematology departments benefit from CPD algorithms designed to maximize sensitivity, enabling the early detection of hematological malignancies. Conversely, settings with higher rates of acute infection or inflammation might adopt CPD rules optimized to reduce false positives by better distinguishing reactive from pathological changes [27, 28]. This tailored, context‐sensitive application of CPD parameters minimizes unnecessary microscopic smear reviews, promotes efficient resource utilization, and facilitates timely clinical decision making.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 587) and limited demographic diversity across clinical settings may restrict the generalizability of our findings; therefore, validation in larger, multi‐center studies is required. Second, the predominant inclusion of samples from specialized departments, such as hemato‐oncology, might introduce selection bias, potentially influencing the performance of CPD algorithms in broader clinical contexts such as emergencies or general medicine. Third, although CPD integration reduces dependency on manual slide review, microscopic examination remains necessary for certain complex cases. Fourth, while the study demonstrated that CPD‐based detection could identify blasts and IGs missed by the analyzer, we did not evaluate the clinical implications of these findings. In specific departments such as hemato‐oncology or pediatrics, even a small number of abnormal cells may reflect marrow recovery or treatment response. Future studies incorporating clinical follow‐up data are warranted to clarify the diagnostic and prognostic significance of these cases. Despite these limitations, CPD integration substantially enhances diagnostic efficiency and accuracy.

Conclusions

Integrating CPD parameters with automated instrument messages effectively enhances diagnostic efficiency and accuracy while reducing the need for manual smear reviews. This approach outperforms traditional methods, providing improved sensitivity and specificity for detecting immature cells. Future studies should explore optimized CPD algorithms and incorporate artificial intelligence techniques to further improve diagnostic capabilities in diverse hematological conditions. Continuous advancement and validation in varied clinical settings will ensure these automated diagnostic systems remain clinically relevant and responsive to evolving healthcare demands.
