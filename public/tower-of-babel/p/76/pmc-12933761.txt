Postpartum self-harm thoughts and subsequent risk of intentional self-harm
Authors: Xiaoqin Liu, Erik Christiansen, Veerle Bergink, Mette-Marie Zacher Kjeldsen, Merete Lund Mægbæk, Trine Munk-Olsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSuicide is a leading cause of maternal mortality, with many cases considered preventable. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) item 10 screens for self-harm thoughts in the past 7 days. This study aimed to investigate whether a positive response (‘hardly ever’, ‘sometimes’ or ‘quite often’) is associated with subsequent hospital-registered intentional self-harm, non-suicidal or suicidal, among postpartum women.MethodsWe conducted a population-based cohort study of 170 218 childbirths (38% of the source population) from 142 795 unique women in Denmark by linking nationwide registers with the HOPE cohort. Self-harm thoughts were defined as any non-zero response to EPDS item 10. The primary outcome was a hospital-recorded episode of intentional self-harm within 365 days after childbirth. To account for confounding, we applied inverse propensity-score weighting, and we used Firth’s penalised logistic regression to estimate ORs.ResultsOf the 170 218 childbirths, 2935 (1.7%) reported self-harm thoughts. During a mean (SD) follow-up of 303.0 (11.2) days, eight women (0.27%) with self-harm thoughts were hospitalised due to intentional self-harm, demonstrating a 24-fold higher crude rate compared with women without self-harm thoughts (0.01%). After applying weighting, the adjusted OR remained significantly elevated at 10.91 (95% CI 7.03 to 17.99).ConclusionThis is the largest study to date on this topic. We found that postpartum women reporting self-harm thoughts had a low absolute risk of intentional self-harm, but the relative risk was 11-fold higher. These results highlight the importance of further assessment and timely support for mothers identified as being at risk.Keywords: Psychiatry, Depressive Disorder, Mental Health, Mood Disorders

Introduction

Suicide is a leading cause of maternal mortality, and many of these deaths are preventable.1 The postpartum period represents a well-defined high-risk window for suicide. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) is widely used for postpartum mental health screening. It is a 10-item questionnaire, with a total score of 0–30; a score ≥11 usually indicates clinically significant depressive symptoms.2 Item 10, ‘the thought of harming myself has occurred to me’, screens for self-harm thoughts.

Although a positive response to item 10 can raise clinical concern, interpretation is complex. Self-harm thoughts are relatively common in the postpartum,3 occurring in approximately 3.8% of women in population-based settings,4 whereas hospital-recorded postpartum self-harm is rare, with a median prevalence of 0.17% (range 0%–2.41%).5 Such thoughts may occur as part of depression or as intrusive thoughts without suicidal intent.6 Women may also hesitate to disclose them due to fear of potential involvement of child-protective services. As EPDS item 10 is widely used to identify perinatal women who may benefit from further assessment, a key clinical question remains: Does endorsement of item 10 indicate elevated risk of self-harm or suicide attempt?

Methods

We conducted a population-based cohort study linking national registers with the HOPE cohort in Denmark, which includes prospective data from 67 of Denmark’s 98 municipalities (2015–2021).7 In total, 170 218 childbirths (38% of the source population and representative of it) from 142 795 mothers with an EPDS screening within 12 weeks postpartum were linked to Danish health registers using unique personal identifiers (online supplemental eFigure 1 and eMethods). Self-harm thoughts were defined as any non-zero response to item 10 (score ≥1). The primary outcome was a hospital-registered intentional self-harm, non-suicidal or suicidal, within 365 days postpartum. Follow-up was limited to the first year after delivery, a period associated with an increased risk of self-harm. We adopted a broad definition of self-harm, consistent with the International Classification of Disease 11th revision (ICD-11) framework, encompassing self-injurious behaviour with or without suicidal intent, based on the following criteria.

A hospital diagnosis of intentional self-harm (ICD-10 codes X60–X84) as a primary or secondary diagnosis.

Hospital treatment with a psychiatric diagnosis (ICD-10 codes F00–F99) as the primary diagnosis, with a secondary diagnosis of poisoning or cut (ICD-10 codes T36–T50, T52–T60 and S51–S69).

Covariates included maternal demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, obstetric factors, psychiatric history, psychotropic medication use, family psychiatric history, adverse birth outcomes and year of delivery (online supplemental eMethods, eTable and table 1). We used Firth’s penalised logistic regression to minimise small-sample bias due to rare outcomes. To account for confounding, we applied inverse probability of treatment weighting based on propensity scores and included all covariates in the weighting model (online supplemental eMethods).

Results

Among the 170 218 childbirths, self-harm thoughts were reported in 2935 (1.7%) (ie, endorsement of EPDS item 10). Among these 2935 childbirths, 1835 (62.5%) also had an EPDS total ≥11. Compared with women without such thoughts, those endorsing item 10 were more likely to have shorter education, a higher body mass index, be non-Danish born, live without a partner and have more obstetric complications, psychiatric history and prior suicide attempts (table 1). After propensity-score weighting (online supplemental eFigure 2 for weight distribution), the groups were well balanced (online supplemental eFigure 3).

During a mean follow-up of 303 (11.2) days, eight out of 2935 women (0.27%) with self-harm thoughts were hospitalised for intentional self-harm, corresponding to a 24-fold higher crude rate than among women without self-harm thoughts (0.01%). After weighting, the adjusted OR remained significantly elevated at 10.91 (95% CI 7.03 to 17.99) (table 2).

Discussion

We observed a 1.7% prevalence of self-harm thoughts, which is lower than the 3.8% reported in a US cohort using the EPDS.4 This difference likely reflects variations in geography, screening timing or reporting. Our estimated 0.27% risk of postpartum intentional self-harm aligns with prior reports (0%–2.41%).5 Despite the low absolute risk, the relative risk was markedly elevated (OR=10.91), identifying a subgroup of postpartum women at increased clinical self-harm risk.

Self-harm thoughts and intentional self-harm are related but distinct phenomena influenced by factors such as prior self-harm and psychiatric comorbidity.8 For some women, such thoughts reflect suicidality in the context of severe depression; for others, they are intrusive and unwanted thoughts that occur without suicidal intent. It is also important to recognise that postpartum experiences can be psychologically taxing in ways that may lead to distressing thoughts without indicating a psychiatric crisis. The challenge is to navigate this ambiguity with care: responses to item 10 should neither be dismissed nor trigger alarm but prompt calm, empathetic follow-up.

Our study has limitations. First, underreporting may arise from stigma and variation in help-seeking behaviour, and our reliance on register data captures only events severe enough to result in hospital contact. Less severe or concealed self-harm episodes are therefore not fully ascertained. These outcome misclassifications will bias the results toward the null. Second, our study did not distinguish self-harm with vs without suicidal intent, which merits future study. Third, while self-harm thoughts are influenced by many factors, we adjusted for these potential covariates without exploring their individual associations with self-harm thoughts.

In conclusion, although the absolute risk of hospital-recorded intentional self-harm was low (0.27%) among postpartum women reporting self-harm thoughts compared with 0.01% among those without such thoughts, the relative risk was markedly elevated (OR=10.91). Even though single risk factors for suicidal behaviour have low predictive value, endorsement of EPDS item 10 is still clinically meaningful, as it identifies individuals with substantially elevated relative risk. EPDS item 10 is consequently an important clinical signal. In routine screening, a positive response should prompt a rapid, careful, non-judgmental clinical follow-up psychosocial assessment to ensure that mothers at risk of self-harm receive timely support.
