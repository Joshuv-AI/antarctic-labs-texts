Effects of detraining on cardiovascular risk factors in older adults: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yaxiong Zheng, Liangliang Li, Hye Gwang Jeong, Young Jin Moon, Hwi-yeol Yun, Jung-Woo Chae, Jae-Hyun Lee, Wooyeon Jo, Soyoon Lee, Seyean Jang, Minseo Kim, Xudong Yang, Sang Ki Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effects of detraining on cardiovascular risk factors (CVRF) in older adults and to explore potential moderating factor (duration of detraining) to gain a better understanding of the factors influencing changes in CVRF.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in four databases: Web of Science, PubMed, Google Scholar, and Scopus. A total of 17 studies involving 513 participants were included. Meta-analyses, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and publication bias assessments were performed using RevMan 5.4 and Stata version 15.0.ResultsDetraining significantly increased SBP (SMD = 0.40, 95% CI [0.21, 0.58]), DBP (SMD = 0.22, 95% CI [0.11, 0.33]), BGL (SMD = 0.48, 95% CI [0.15, 0.80]), TC (SMD = 0.49, 95% CI [0.18, 0.80]), TG (SMD = 0.64, 95% CI [0.35, 0.92]), and body fat (SMD = 0.36, 95% CI [0.22, 0.51]), while significantly reducing HDL-C (SMD = −0.42, 95% CI [−0.78, −0.06]). Subgroup analyses revealed that the adverse effects of detraining were more pronounced after ≥3 months. SBP and TG increased significantly regardless of detraining duration, but the effect sizes were larger after ≥3 months. In contrast, DBP, BGL, TC, and LDL-C increased significantly only after ≥3 months of detraining. Although BMI and body weight did not change significantly, body fat, in contrast, increased significantly in both groups, with a greater increase observed after ≥3 months.ConclusionDetraining has significant and detrimental effects on cardiovascular risk factors in older adults, with risks increasing as the duration of detraining extends.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide and a significant contributor to reduced quality of life [1]. Among the key contributors to CVD are cardiovascular risk factors (CVRF), which play a crucial role in increasing the incidence of CVD and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [2]. Well-established risk factors associated with CVD include hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and obesity [3]. Additionally, aging is recognized as an independent risk factor for CVD, so the elderly more vulnerable to these diseases [4].

Regular long-term physical activity has consistently been shown to mitigate the risk of CVD [5]. For instance, 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week reduces CVD morbidity by 17% and mortality by 23% [6]. Numerous studies have highlighted the physiological benefits of exercise: endurance training has been shown to reduce systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) by −3.4 mm Hg and −2.5 mm Hg, respectively [7]. An 8-week exercise program for older women significantly lowered blood glucose levels from 112.5 to 94.5 mg/dL [8]. Aerobic exercise has also been found to improve lipid profiles, reducing total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), while increasing high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) in elderly populations [9]. Additionally, in obese individuals, exercise has been shown to significantly improve body composition [10]. These findings have fueled initiatives such as “Exercise is Medicine,” which emphasizes the preventive and therapeutic potential of physical activity in combating chronic diseases.

However, these positive physiological adaptations produced by exercise can be lost when training is stopped. “Detraining” is defined as the partial or complete loss of previously acquired physiological adaptations due to a reduction or cessation of exercise [11]. For example, resistance training has been shown to increase muscle mass in older adults, but muscle size declines significantly after training stops [12]. Similarly, left ventricular mass decreases following the cessation of training [13]. In a study of older adults who completed a 9-month exercise program, cessation of training for 3 months resulted in significantly higher SBP, DBP, and body fat percentage [14].

Interestingly, some studies suggest that certain exercise-induced benefits may be partially maintained even after training cessation [15,16]. Despite these findings, there remains substantial ambiguity regarding the effects of detraining on CVRF, with inconsistent results across studies.

Recognizing the clinical relevance of this issue, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the effects of detraining on CVRF in older adults who previously participated in multicomponent, aerobic, or resistance exercise programs. In addition, this study employs meta-analysis to investigate potential moderators, such as the duration of detraining, in order to elucidate the factors contributing to these changes.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [17] to investigate the effects of detraining on cardiovascular risk factors in the elderly. The systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 statement for conducting systematic reviews and was registered at PROSPERO (registration number CRD42025562081).

Search strategy

Electronic databases were searched in Web of science, PubMed, Google scholar and Scopus. Searches were limited to papers published in English. Boolean operators and keywords were: ["older men " OR "older women" OR "elderly" OR "older adults " OR "old"] AND ["detraining" OR "exercise detraining" OR "exercise cessation" OR "exercise" OR "training cessation" OR "retraining"] AND ["cardiovascular risk factors" OR "cardiovascular disease" OR "blood pressure" OR "systolic blood pressure" OR "diastolic blood pressure" OR " blood glucose levels" OR " blood glucose" OR "blood sugar" OR "blood lipids" OR "lipids" OR "total cholesterol" OR "triglycerides" OR "high density lipoprotein cholesterol" OR " low density lipoprotein cholesterol" OR "BGL" "TC" OR "TG" OR "HDL-C" OR "LDL-C" OR "body mass index" OR "BMI" OR "body fat" OR "obesity" OR "body weight"].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were determined following the PICOS strategy. Population (P) = older adults mean aged ≥60 years; Intervention (I) = physical exercise intervention followed by a period of detraining; Comparison (C) = post-exercise intervention and post-detraining data; Outcome (O) = SBP, DBP, BGL, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, BMI, body fat and body weight; Study design (S) = randomized controlled clinical trials and non-randomized controlled clinical trials.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) not in English; (2) documents without available full texts and duplicate publications; (3) reviews, conference paper or reports; (4) studies with incomplete data, defined as those that did not report key CVRF outcomes, those lacking either post-intervention or post-detraining values required for effect size calculation, or those presenting only descriptive findings without extractable numerical data (e.g., means, standard deviations, or standard errors).

Literature screening and data extraction

The process of selecting literature was independently conducted by 2 investigators (LLL and ZHL) using Endnote 20 software. In case of disagreement, LLL and ZHL consulted SKL, and all discrepancies were resolved through joint discussion. The extracted information encompassed details such as first author, publication year, country, age, sample size, gender distribution, duration of detraining and cardiovascular risk factors assessment.

Risk of bias evaluation

Two researchers independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4. The evaluation was based on the Cochrane Collaboration's risk of bias tool, which examines domains including random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessors, completeness of outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias.

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis of continuous variables was performed using Stata version 15.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). The evaluation indicators of CVRF in this study included SBP (mmHg), DBP (mmHg), BGL (mg/dL), TC (mg/dL), TG (mg/dL), LDL-C (mg/dL), HDL-C (mg/dL), BMI (kg/m−2), body fat (%), and body weight (kg). The standardized mean difference (SMD) was utilized to synthesize the studies included in this meta-analysis, to assess the association between detraining and outcomes. In the forest plot, the square represents the pooled SMD effect, accompanied by its corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity among the included studies was assessed using Cochran's Q test (reported with a corresponding p-value) and the I² statistic. If heterogeneity was low (I² < 50% and P > 0.05), a fixed-effects model was applied for data analysis; otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the impact of different detraining durations on CVRF. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the meta-analysis results. Additionally, funnel plots were generated to evaluate potential publication bias.

Results

Literature screening process and result

The search process spanned four databases, yielding a total of 3966 articles, which were curated in accordance with PRISMA guidelines for systematic study screening. An additional 32 articles were identified through manual searches. After removing duplicates and conducting a comprehensive screening based on titles, abstracts, and full-text reviews, 17 articles were ultimately selected for systematic evaluation and meta-analysis. The risk of bias in the 17 included studies was evaluated using RevMan software. Among these, 8 studies were classified as having a low risk of bias, 7 as having a moderate risk, and 2 as having a high risk. While most studies reported using randomization, details on the specific randomization process and allocation concealment were often lacking. In contrast, all studies were rated as having a low risk of bias concerning data completeness, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Meta-analysis results

Effects of detraining on blood pressure

A meta-analysis of 36 detraining intervention results from 9 studies revealed a significant increase in blood pressure following detraining compared to post-training levels. For SBP, the analysis showed moderate heterogeneity (I² = 61.1%, p < 0.001) with a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.40 (95% CI [0.21, 0.58]). For DBP, the heterogeneity was smaller (I² = 3.0%, p = 0.417), with an SMD of 0.22 (95% CI [0.11, 0.33]), as depicted in the Fig.3.Fig. 3Forest plot for detraining on the blood pressure.Abbreviations: SBP, systolic blood pressure; DBP, diastolic blood pressure.Fig. 3

Effects of detraining on blood biomarkers

Blood glucose levels (BGL) were evaluated in 17 detraining intervention results across 10 studies, showing a significant increase after detraining compared to post-training levels (I² = 77.9%, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.48, 95% CI [0.15, 0.80]). Similarly, total cholesterol (TC) levels were significantly elevated in 17 detraining intervention results from 8 studies (I² = 74.3%, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.49, 95% CI [0.18, 0.80]). Triglyceride (TG) levels also demonstrated a notable increase across 16 detraining intervention results from 8 studies (I² = 68.9%, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.64, 95% CI [0.36, 0.92]). The 9 detraining intervention results from 5 studies reported a statistically significant decrease in HDL-C levels (I² = 63.9%, p = 0.005; SMD = −0.42, 95% CI [−0.78, −0.06]). Twelve of the 7 studies examined the impact of detraining on BMI, revealing no statistically significant increase (I² < 0.1%, p = 0.998; SMD = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.13, 0.25]). Subgroup analysis results

Impact of detraining duration on blood pressure

A subgroup analysis was conducted to investigate the impact of different durations of detraining on SBP and DBP. The results indicated that SBP increased significantly compared to post-training levels, regardless of the duration of the detraining period. For detraining periods of <3 months (I² = 62.9%, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.30, 95% CI [0.04, 0.56]), the increase was significant, and a similar significant increase was observed for detraining periods of ≥3 months (I² = 58.5%, p = 0.002; SMD = 0.52, 95% CI [0.26, 0.78]). Conversely, DBP showed a significant increase only when the detraining period was ≥3 months (I² = 22.9%, p = 0.200; SMD = 0.34, 95% CI [0.18, 0.50]), whereas no significant effect on DBP was observed for detraining periods of <3 months (I² < 0.1%, p = 0.847; SMD = 0.10, 95% CI [−0.05, 0.26]), as shown in Fig.6.Fig. 6Subgroup analysis for duration of detraining on the blood pressure.Abbreviations: SBP, systolic blood pressure; DBP, diastolic blood pressure.Fig. 6

Impact of detraining duration on blood indexes

The analysis revealed that GBL increased significantly only when the detraining period was ≥ 3 months (I² = 46.0%, p = 0.035; SMD = 0.37, 95% CI [0.13, 0.60]), whereas no significant change was observed for detraining periods <3 months (I² = 93.5%, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.77, 95% CI [−0.45, 1.98]). A similar trend was observed for TC, with significant increases occurring only after detraining periods ≥3 months (I² = 65.0%, p = 0.006; SMD = 0.54, 95% CI [0.15, 0.92]), while no significant change was found for periods <3 months (I² = 80.5%, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.46, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.96]). For TG, significant increases were observed regardless of the detraining duration. For periods <3 months, TG levels rose significantly (I² = 67.0%, p = 0.002; SMD = 0.59, 95% CI [0.20, 0.97]), and for periods ≥3 months, the increase was similarly significant (I² = 75.0%, p = 0.001; SMD = 0.70, 95% CI [0.25, 1.15]). Impact of detraining duration on basic body indices

The findings indicate that no statistically significant increase in BMI or body weight was observed, regardless of the duration of detraining. Specifically, for detraining periods of <3 months, BMI showed no significant change (I² < 0.1%, p = 0.925; SMD = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.47, 0.56]), and similarly, for periods of ≥3 months, BMI remained stable (I² < 0.1%, p = 0.989; SMD = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.14, 0.26]). Body weight followed the same pattern, with no significant increases for detraining periods of <3 months (I² < 0.1%, p = 0.921; SMD = 0.24, 95% CI [−0.01, 0.50]) or ≥3 months (I² < 0.1%, p = 1.00; SMD = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.15, 0.28]). In contrast, body fat showed a statistically significant increase for both durations of detraining. Sensitivity analysis and publication bias analysis

Due to the significant heterogeneity observed among studies reporting SBP, BGL, TC, TG, HDL-C, and LDL-C (I² > 50%; p < 0.05), with respective I² values of 61.1%, 77.9%, 74.3%, 68.9%, 63.9%, and 53.4%, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure the robustness of the findings. The results of this analysis are illustrated in Supplementary material 2. Furthermore, the study results were assessed for publication bias, with the following p-values obtained: SBP (p = 0.511), DBP (p = 0.005), BGL (p = 0.162), TC (p = 0.881), TG (p = 0.130), HDL-C (p = 0.832), LDL-C (p = 0.102), BMI (p = 0.663), body fat (p = 0.526), and body weight (p = 0.033). A detailed results of the publication bias analysis is provided in the Supplementary material 3.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the impact of detraining on cardiovascular risk factors in older adults. The findings revealed that detraining leads to a significant increase in these risk factors, with the duration of detraining playing a critical role in amplifying the effects.

Key findings on blood pressure. The meta-analysis demonstrated that cessation of training significantly elevated both SBP and DBP in older adults, particularly when the cessation period extended to ≥3 months. Specifically, SBP increased with an SMD of 0.52 (95% CI [0.26, 0.78]), while DBP rose with an SMD of 0.34 (95% CI [0.18, 0.50]). Leitão et al. reported that the physiological adaptations gained from exercise training in elderly hypertensive patients were difficult to sustain after stopping exercise, with SBP and DBP reverting to pre-intervention levels just 3 months post-detraining [33]. Similarly, Moker et al. found that the cardiovascular benefits attained through 6 months of exercise intervention in individuals with prehypertension and dyslipidemia dissipated after only 2 weeks of detraining [34]. This effect is especially pronounced in older adults due to age-related arterial changes, such as plaque formation, calcification, and narrowing of the vascular lumen, which contribute to gradual blood pressure increases [35]. Exercise has been shown to enhance arterial elasticity and lower vascular resistance by increasing nitric oxide release, thereby reducing blood pressure [36]. These findings underscore the importance of sustained participation in physical activity to maintain the long-term physiological adaptations that exercise imparts on blood pressure regulation.

Blood biomarkers and the impact of detraining. In terms of blood biomarkers, the study showed that detraining significantly increased BGL, TG, and TC while reducing HDL-C. Although LDL-C levels showed no significant changes initially, subgroup analysis revealed that detraining periods of ≥3 months significantly increased LDL-C levels in older adults (SMD = 0.65, 95% CI [0.14, 1.16]). This highlights the critical role of detraining duration. For example, Motoyama et al. found that after 4 weeks of detraining, TG levels increased significantly, HDL-C levels declined, and LDL-C remained unchanged [29]. Older adults often pause their exercise routines due to illness, fatigue, vacations, or other interruptions. Our findings suggest that while short-term detraining may not immediately result in significant physiological changes, prolonged interruptions (≥3 months) trigger noticeable adverse effects. This emphasizes the importance of maintaining consistent physical activity to mitigate these risks.

The study also found a significant increase in body fat levels following detraining, while no substantial changes were observed in BMI and body weight. Specifically, BMI showed a SMD of 0.06 (95% CI [−0.13, 0.25]), and body weight exhibited an SMD of 0.14 (95% CI [−0.02, 0.30]). Previous research indicates a strong positive correlation between changes in basic physical indicators and blood pressure in individuals at high risk for cardiovascular disease [37]. For older adults, BMI, body fat, and body weight are closely tied to CVRF. Ample evidence highlights the beneficial effects of exercise in improving these metrics in older populations [19,33,38]. Remarkably, even brief periods of exercise—such as 10 days—can reduce weight and body fat, particularly in obese individuals [39]. Our study emphasizes the significant impact of detraining on body fat levels, reinforcing the importance of consistent exercise to sustain the benefits achieved through physical activity.

Addressing contradictory findings. While the overall meta-analysis demonstrated that detraining in older adults adversely affects cardiovascular risk factors, several individual studies reported findings that appear inconsistent with this trend. For instance, an 8-week break from training did not significantly influence muscle power, fall risk, body composition, or cognitive function in older adults with mild cognitive impairment [15], and another found that reductions in blood pressure achieved after moderate-intensity exercise were largely preserved during subsequent detraining [16]. Such discrepancies may be attributable to differences in the type of exercise performed, the initial health status of participants, the frequency and intensity of the exercise interventions, or the duration of both intervention and detraining phases. By integrating both supportive and contradictory findings, this review highlights the complex and context-dependent nature of detraining responses. Although some studies suggest that short-term breaks may not immediately abolish exercise-induced benefits, the weight of evidence indicates that prolonged detraining (≥3 months) markedly diminishes cardiovascular protection.

We added a focused methodological appraisal to better contextualize the findings. While most of the included trials demonstrated an acceptable risk of bias, several features limit the certainty and generalizability of the pooled estimates—particularly the modest sample sizes of many studies, marked heterogeneity in intervention characteristics (type, frequency, intensity, and duration), variability in detraining or follow-up periods, and inconsistent reporting of adherence and co-interventions. To address these issues, we performed sensitivity analyses and assessed publication bias (see Supplementary materials 2 and 3). These robustness checks reinforced the overall direction of the primary results, while also highlighting specific outcomes and contexts that warrant cautious interpretation.

The findings of this study provide valuable insights for sports professionals and healthcare practitioners. By encouraging older adults to adopt and sustain long-term exercise habits, these professionals can help maximize the health benefits of physical activity, maintain cardiovascular health, and mitigate the adverse effects of detraining.

Importantly, this study constitutes the first systematic review and meta-analysis to comprehensively synthesize evidence on the impact of detraining on CVFR in older adults. A notable methodological strength of the included studies lies in the overall low risk of bias, with particularly strong adherence to blinding-related criteria. This methodological rigor substantially enhances confidence in the validity of the synthesized findings and strengthens the reliability of the meta-analytic conclusions. Nonetheless, certain limitations warrant consideration. Variability in the frequency, duration, and intensity of exercise interventions across the included studies likely introduced heterogeneity into the findings, potentially influencing the overall assessment of the intervention’s impact. To develop a more robust understanding of how detraining affects CVRF in the older people, future research is imperative. Such studies should prioritize the adoption of standardized exercise intervention protocols and employ consistent methods of data collection and analysis to improve the reliability of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this meta-analysis suggest that detraining appears to have adverse effects on cardiovascular risk factors in the elderly, particularly in terms of blood pressure, blood glucose, blood lipids, and body fat. Furthermore, these risks are exacerbated as the duration of detraining increases. Future research is needed to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving these effects.
