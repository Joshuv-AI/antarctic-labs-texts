Utility of repeat MRI in assessing treatment non-response in axial spondyloarthritis: data from two large tertiary rheumatology centres
Authors: Jake Weddell, Ayaan Farah, Rahul Shah, Lara Rossi, Sophia Lekh, Sabrin Mohamud, Philip Robinson, Jane E Freeston, Andrew Barr, Vandevelde Y Claire, Denis Remedios, Dennis McGonagle, Pedro M Machado, Helena Marzo-Ortega
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe role of MRI of the spine/sacroiliac joints to aid the diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is well established. Limited data, however, exist on the use of MRI to assess disease activity, resulting in current Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS)/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) guidelines not recommending the use of MRI for this purpose. We aimed to assess the current use of MRI to assess disease activity and its impact on clinical decision making.MethodsAs part of a service evaluation, we identified patients with a prior diagnosis of axSpA, who had an MRI of the spine/sacroiliac joints requested between May 2020 and December 2023 at The Leeds Teaching Hospitals Trust (Leeds) and Northwick Park Hospital (London). Clinical and demographic data were extracted from the medical notes. Data on MRI findings were extracted from the radiologist’s report.ResultsOverall, 346 scans were performed in 335 patients. 301 patients had axSpA (170 radiographic axSpA, 131 non-radiographic axSpA) and 31 axial psoriatic arthritis. Patients were predominantly male (60.0%) and HLA-B27 positive (60.6%). 140/172 (80.1%) of patients had evidence of inflammation on a pre-treatment MRI scan. 224 scans (64.7%) were performed in patients on biologic/targeted synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs. Of the 346 MRIs performed during the audit period, 179 (54.7%) had evidence of active inflammation and those with active inflammation were more likely to have their treatment escalated (59.3% vs 23.2%).ConclusionsMRI is of utility in assessing disease activity with results of MRI scans directly influencing treatment decision making at the bedside. Further research exploring the relationship between MRI findings and clinical outcomes is warranted.Keywords: Axial Spondyloarthritis, Magnetic Resonance Imaging, Treatment

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

MRI of the spine and sacroiliac joint is widely used to aid the diagnosis of axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) due to its ability to detect acute inflammatory or structural changes suggestive of previous inflammation. However, there are limited data about the utility of MRI in assessing patients with ongoing symptoms despite treatment.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This is the largest multicentre study investigating the real-world use of MRI in patients with ongoing symptoms of axSpA while on regular therapy. This study demonstrated that nearly half of patients with symptoms while on treatment have active bone marrow oedema lesions on MRI, suggestive of ongoing inflammation, and are three times more likely to have their treatment escalated than those without inflammatory lesions. Further, MRI showed non-inflammatory findings, including structural lesions and concomitant findings of osteoarthritis, fracture, etc. in nearly half of patients.

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is characterised by inflammation within the spine and sacroiliac joints (SIJs), leading to back pain and stiffness which ultimately can lead to spinal fusion and disability. Diagnosis can be challenging, due to the often-insidious nature of the symptoms and the lack of reliable biomarkers. Historically, many patients were diagnosed following the development of structural lesions at the spine or SIJs visible on plain X-ray, which often present late into the disease course.

In recent years, the use of MRI has transformed the diagnosis of axSpA prior to the development of X-ray detectable structural lesions, with bone marrow oedema (BMO) on MRI correlating histologically with active inflammation,1 response to anti-TNF therapy2 3 and the development of future new bone formation. Nevertheless, MRI has several limitations, chiefly low sensitivity and specificity and interobserver disagreement on the presence and significance of inflammatory and structural lesions which can be found in non-inflammatory conditions and in the general population without axSpA4 5 and poor tolerability in some patients. Additionally, up to 15% of patients with axSpA may not show any evidence of active inflammation on their initial MRI scan,6 which may just be due to the timing of imaging or to a specific molecular endotype. In the UK, the widely adopted British Society for Spondyloarthritis (BRITSpA) consensus guidelines recommend performing MRI of the whole spine/sacroiliac joints with T1 and Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR)/T2-weighted Fat Suppression sequences,7 8 to assess for active and structural changes suggestive of axSpA, which can help make or refute the diagnosis.9 The international Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society (ASAS)/Spondyloarthritis Research and Treatment Network (SPARTAN) collaboration recommends an additional T1-weighted thin slice (erosion sensitive) sequence such as Volumetric Interpolated Breath-hold Examination in the SIJs, to further assess for chronic structural damage.10

Despite the widespread use of MRI to aid the diagnosis of axSpA, the relationship between MRI inflammation and patient-reported symptoms remains unclear. Previous studies have shown an association between short-lived fluctuations in MRI-BMO and subjective loss of clinical response to TNF inhibitors;11 however, other studies have shown no relation between clinical outcome measures and MRI-BMO12 or a relation only in males.13 14 Similarly, despite the known association between MRI inflammation at baseline and response to treatment,3 there is little evidence on the predictive value of MRI inflammation when switching treatments. The most recent ASAS/EULAR guidelines15 do not recommend the routine use of MRI in treatment monitoring and assessing disease activity due to the lack of evidence and cost implications. A recent survey of rheumatologists in the UK16 found nearly half of respondents use MRI routinely or frequently to assess patients experiencing treatment non-response in routine clinical practice, despite the lack of evidence supporting its use in this context. There is limited real world data about the MRI findings in patients experiencing non-response to treatment and how findings influence clinical decision making.

Here, we present real-world clinical data from two large tertiary centres in the UK on the current use of MRI to assess disease activity in people with axSpA who are experiencing ongoing symptoms despite treatment. In particular, we focus on the utility of MRI in guiding treatment decision-making at the bedside.

Methods

Data collection

This report is a retrospective service evaluation. MRIs of the whole spine and SIJs, requested under the axSpA protocol in accordance with the BRITSpA MRI consensus guidelines (Sagittal T1 and STIR/T2FS of the whole spine; semicoronal T1 and semicoronal/semiaxial STIR/T2FS of the SIJs),7 were identified between May 2020 and December 2023 at the Leeds Specialist Spondyloarthritis Service, The Leeds Teaching Hospitals NHS Trust and the Spondyloarthritis Clinic, London Northwest University Healthcare NHS Trust. Clinical health records were reviewed to identify patients with a pre-existing diagnosis of axSpA/ankylosing spondylitis (historic terminology)17 or axial psoriatic arthritis (axPsA) (clinician diagnosis), for whom MRI was requested to assess disease activity or treatment non-response to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), conventional synthetic (cs) or to biologic/targeted synthetic (b/ts) disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) therapy. Additional clinical data were extracted from routinely collected health records including demographics, extra-musculoskeletal manifestations, comorbidities including fibromyalgia and other chronic pain syndromes, pretreatment MRI findings (if available), current and previous medications, history of raised inflammatory markers secondary to axSpA and patient reported outcome measures. MRI findings were categorised as indicating inflammatory changes related to axSpA (positive scan), based on the presence of terms such as ‘active inflammation’, ‘bone marrow oedema’ or ‘osteitis’ within the local radiologist’s report, in either the spine, SIJ or both. Non-axSpA related findings, such as degenerative changes, disc disease, etc. were also extracted from the local radiologist report.

Statistical analysis

Most of the data are presented descriptively, including counts with percentages, and means with SD. Inferential analysis was performed on a subset of data to assess the relationship between the presence of inflammation and other outcome measures, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI). Binary data were analysed using the χ2 test, while continuous data were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, as normality assumptions were not met. Data analysis was performed using Python V.3.11.4, and statistical testing was performed using the SciPy package V.1.10.1. Figures were produced using Plotly (Plotly Technologies Inc. Collaborative data science. Montréal, QC, 2015. https://plot.ly).

Results

Over the study period, 346 MRI monitoring scans of the spine and SIJs were performed in 335 patients to evaluate worsening of axSpA related symptoms while on regular drug therapy. Of these, 301 scans were conducted in patients with a primary diagnosis of axSpA (131 non-radiographic axSpA (nr-axSpA), 170 radiographic axSpA (r-axSpA)) and 34 in patients with axPsA. Among these 346 scans, 224 were performed, while patients were on b/ts-DMARD therapy, 13 on cs-DMARDs, 74 on NSAIDs alone and 35 who were not taking any axSpA-specific pharmacological treatment.

Patients had a mean age of 46.1 years (range 18–79) and median disease duration from diagnosis of 6.5 years (range: 2 months – 49 years). Most were male (58.9%) and HLA-B27 positive (62.7%). Extra musculoskeletal manifestations (EMMs) were common, with 43.9% having a history of any EMM, 22.2% psoriasis, 11.6% inflammatory bowel disease and 17.3% uveitis. MRI findings on the monitoring scan

Inflammatory BMO lesions were identified in 51.7% (n=179) of all MRIs performed for disease monitoring, 44.9% (n=99) of which were in patients receiving b/ts-DMARD therapy. At the patient level, BMO was the most common in the SIJ alone (n=75, 21.6%) or in both the SIJ and spine in the same patient (n=67, 19.4%). Isolated spinal inflammation was identified in 37 (10.7%) of all MRIs. Patients with active MRI inflammation were more likely to be male (63.6% vs 53.9%), HLA-B27 positive (66.4% vs 59.2%), have previous raised CRP levels secondary to axSpA (≥5 mg/L; 53.0% vs 40.7%) and have prior MRI evidence of inflammation on the pretreatment or diagnostic MRI (81.8% vs 63.9%), compared with those without active MRI inflammation. Only 13 patients had peripheral arthritis, and there was no difference in rates of peripheral arthritis in those with and without MRI inflammation (3.3% and 4.2%) (table 2).

MRI findings in relation to the existing pretreatment MRI scan and outcome

A pretreatment or diagnostic MRI scan was available for 172/335 (51.3%) patients, of whom 140 (81.3%) had evidence of BMO (any location) reported by the local radiologists as consistent with active inflammation related to axSpA. In patients with a positive monitoring MRI, there was little changeover on the location of initial inflammation between the pre-existing or diagnostic and follow-up MRI. Of 34 patients with no inflammation on MRI at baseline, seven had inflammation on follow-up located at the SIJ (n=5), spine (n=1) and both spine and SIJ (n=1) (figure 1).

Of interest, 11 patients (n=5 HLA-B27 positive) had a total of two monitoring MRI scans during the 18-month audit period as part of disease activity assessment (range 226 days–797 days, mean/SD 578.4 [177.2]). At the time of the first monitoring scan, eight were on a b/ts-DMARD and three on NSAIDs or no therapy. MRI showed active inflammation in both the spine and SIJ in 2two cases, isolated SIJ inflammation in two with no cases of isolated spinal inflammation. At the time of the second monitoring scan, four remained on the same b/ts-DMARD, three had switched to a different b/ts-DMARD, two NSAID-treated patients had commenced a b/ts-DMARD, one switched from b/ts-DMARD to NSAID, and one remained on NSAID only. Of the second 11 scans, four had isolated SIJ inflammation, one had both SIJ and spinal inflammation and none had isolated spinal inflammation. One patient (an HLA-B27 positive female) who had no inflammatory findings at the time of the first monitoring scan (negative scan) had new findings of inflammation (positive scan) on the second monitoring scan. Disease classification in these patients was: n=7 nr-axSpA, 3 r-axSpA and 1 transitioned from nr-axSpA to r-axSpA.

Overall, patients with active inflammation in the monitoring scan were more likely to undergo b/ts-DMARD switching, b/ts-DMARD dose escalation, cs-DMARD dose escalation, cs-DMARD commencement or escalation from NSAIDs to b/ts DMARDs (59.3% vs 23.2%). In contrast, patients without inflammation were more likely to be referred to other medical or surgical specialties, such as spinal surgery, pain medicine or oncology (10.2% vs 6.1%).

In patients with active inflammation on their monitoring scan, those with a raised CRP were more likely to have their treatment escalated (67.9% vs 51.7%). Similarly, in those with no inflammatory findings on MRI, treatment escalation was more likely to occur in the presence of a raised CRP (32.5% vs 23.9%) (figure 2).

Discussion

This study represents the largest real-world assessment of MRI use in axSpA patients with persistent or worsening symptoms despite b/ts-DMARD therapy to date. It underscores MRI’s role in informing treatment adjustments or escalation and highlights its utility in distinguishing between inflammatory and non-inflammatory causes of symptoms.

Our analysis included 346 MRI scans performed in 335 patients across two large tertiary rheumatology centres in the UK. Nearly half of MRI scans showed findings of BMO indicative of active inflammation in patients reporting ongoing symptoms while on advanced therapies. As expected, based on the findings shown on the monitoring MRI scan, patients with findings of active inflammation were more likely to have their treatment changed or escalated. In addition, nearly half of the scans showed non-inflammatory pathology and notably, 1 in 12 MRIs had findings which led to referrals for further specialist care, including pain clinics, spinal surgery and, in one case, oncology. These results highlight the crucial role of MRI in evaluating treatment non-response in axSpA, supporting clinical decision-making and guiding interventions such as treatment escalation or specialist referrals.

Assessing patients with axSpA who are experiencing ongoing symptoms despite treatment is a common clinical problem, with only 49% of patients achieving ASAS 40 response to first TNF inhibitor according to real world registry data.18 Identifying the cause of ongoing symptoms or recurrence of symptoms can be challenging with multiple potential causes including undertreated inflammation, mechanical back pain, central pain sensitisation or a combination of these factors among others. MRI has the potential to identify those patients with active inflammation and other structural causes which may explain their symptoms. While patients with MRI inflammation were more likely to have treatment escalated or changed in our study, nearly a quarter of patients with a negative monitoring MRI had their biologic therapy changed or escalated too, and, interestingly, over 40% of patients with a positive monitoring MRI did not have their treatment changed or escalated. This suggests that while MRI has an important role for clinicians in guiding treatment decisions, the decision to change treatment remains predominantly driven by the overall clinical picture.

A recent national UK-based survey16 explored clinicians’ perceptions and use of MRI in patients experiencing treatment non-response. Nearly half of responding clinicians report using MRI frequently or routinely for assessing treatment non-response and just under half report an increase in use for this indication over the last few years, demonstrating the increasing importance clinicians are placing on MRI in treatment decision-making at the bedside. However, there were concerns raised about the cost of MRI, MRI waiting times and interpreting the clinical implications of findings, in addition to variations in practice around withholding NSAIDs and extent of the spine/SIJ scanned. Some of these uncertainties and lack of consensus are highlighted in the ASAS/EULAR guidelines, which do not advocate for routine use of MRI in treatment monitoring due to the evidential limitations including cost implications.15 Further research into the use of MRI for monitoring of disease activity and specifically in those patients experiencing treatment non-response is therefore vital to improve clinical care.

Currently, clinical decision making to escalate/switch therapy in axSpA is guided primarily by disease activity scores. BASDAI is the most used outcome measure in UK clinics (as currently recommended by National Institute for Health and Care Excellence19) and relies solely on patient reported symptom scores, with high BASDAI scores often leading to treatment being switched or escalated. Interestingly, the results from our study showed that those patients whose MRI scans showed active inflammation had lower BASDAI than those without, which suggests that BASDAI may be an unreliable measure of objective inflammation. These findings underscore the need for alternative composite measures of disease activity such as the ASAS/EULAR-recommended15 and OMERACT (Outcome Measures in Rheumatology)-endorsed20 axial spondyloarthritis disease activity index (ASDAS)21 22 and of exploring possible differential diagnoses including non-inflammatory causes of pain. ASDAS is now recommended by many international and national societies, beyond ASAS/EULAR,23 with less redundancy compared with BASDAI,24 the incorporation of an objective marker of inflammation in the form of CRP and a better correlation between improvement in ASDAS and improvement in MRI inflammation than BASDAI in patients treated with TNF inhibitors.25 While there is a significant number of missing data regarding ASDAS in our report, results show that despite ongoing clinical symptoms, less than 1/3 patients had an abnormal CRP with no association seen between abnormal CRP and presence of MRI inflammation. ASDAS may also therefore fail to distinguish between those with and without MRI inflammation, underscoring the need for considering repeat MRI even if guided solely by clinical symptoms.

Patients with inflammation present on their MRI scan were more likely to be younger, HLA-B27 positive, male and have previous objective evidence of inflammation in the form of raised CRP or previous positive MRI scan. This is in keeping with previous studies showing that HLA-B27 positive males are the most likely to have inflammation present on an MRI scan.26 However, the biggest factors influencing positive MRI scans in our study were a history of raised CRP or previous positive MRI for inflammation. There was no difference in MRI inflammation between those with and without active peripheral arthritis; however, only 13 patients in total had active peripheral arthritis at the time of their monitoring MRI.

A small number of patients (n=11) had two monitoring MRI scans performed during the audit period. Of these four had an initial positive monitoring MRI scan, and all 4 of these patients and one other patient had a positive second monitoring MRI scan. The only patient who had a positive second monitoring MRI after a negative first monitoring MRI was an HLA-B27 positive female, with a gap of 18 months between the two MRI scans. Previous evidence has demonstrated that repeat MRI scan for diagnosis is only of utility in HLA-B27 males at 3 months or 1 year.6 27 While our patient did have new SIJ inflammation, their treatment was not changed following the second MRI scan, casting doubt on the clinical relevance of the inflammatory changes in this case. Nevertheless, this observation may suggest a role for repeat MRI in HLA-B27 positive females.

The rate of HLA-B27 positivity was low in our study, with only 60.6% of patients positive for the HLA-B27 allele, despite over half of patients having radiographic disease. Estimates of HLA-B27 positivity in axSpA vary widely with rates between 64% and 93%28 reported in the literature. The rate of HLA-B27 positivity varies significantly with race,29 with higher rates in white Europeans compared with those of other ethnic backgrounds. Both centres serve large, ethnically diverse populations, which may explain the lower overall rates of HLA-B27 positivity.

The presence of objective signs of active inflammation in the form of raised CRP or MRI findings is often required to start biologic therapy in nr-axSpA and the burden of inflammation predicts who responds to treatment in axSpA.3 30 31 However, CRP is only ever elevated in up to 40% of patients with axSpA.32 In our study, 35.9% of patients with MRI inflammation and 29.5% without MRI inflammation had raised CRP levels >5 mg/L, highlighting the limited utility of relying on MRI or CRP alone in assessing for objective evidence of inflammation in patients with axSpA. Previous studies have highlighted a relationship between CRP levels and MRI inflammation in the spine, but not the SIJs,33 which is consistent with the findings from our study where raised CRP was more common in patients with isolated spinal MRI inflammation than those with isolated SIJ inflammation. In our cohort, patients with objective evidence of active inflammation in the form of BMO on MRI and/or raised CRP were more likely to have their treatment escalated or switched; however, there is little evidence supporting the use of objective inflammation in predicting response to changes in treatment, and further evidence is needed.

Nearly half of patients in this report had non-inflammatory findings on MRI scans, and around 17% had concomitant inflammatory and non-inflammatory findings including structural changes such as SIJ ankylosis or degenerative changes and disc pathology. The presence of disc and degenerative changes increases with age and is more common in males, which is in keeping with the overall demographics of our cohort.34 The relationship between degenerative changes and disc disease on MRI with low back pain is unclear. A large study of healthy volunteers and those with lower back pain symptoms found no relationship between degenerative changes on lumbar MRI and symptoms,35 whereas other studies have demonstrated a relationship between MRI findings and symptoms.36 Caution should therefore be taken when attributing patient symptomology to MRI findings, such as degenerative changes and disc disease.

Some rarer findings included possible malignancy, spinal fractures and spinal stenosis. These findings resulted in a significant minority of patients being referred on to other medical specialties including spinal surgery, oncology, and pain team. This highlights the importance of MRI in identifying non-inflammatory pathology, some of which required further specialist input.

In this study, we chose to use the radiologist’s report as the ‘ground truth’ for the presence of inflammation on the MRI scans. While numerous semi-quantitative scoring systems37 have been developed to improve the reliability of identifying and reporting BMO on MRI, these are only meant to be used in research settings and are unfeasible in routine clinical practice. Moreover, these scoring systems assess individual SpA-related features, more often BMO, and do not provide information about alternative non-inflammatory or non-axSpA-related findings. Therefore, we elected to use the radiology report as the information contained within is available to the clinician when deciding whether to change treatment based on the MRI findings. A 2017 study of UK radiologists38 found low understanding of awareness of positive MRI findings in suspected axSpA, leading to the development and dissemination of the 2019 BRITSpA7 guidance. Subsequently, a freedom of information act request in 2024 showed the impact of this work with significant improvements including higher awareness within the radiology community of the SIJ and spinal findings supportive of axSpA.8 Interestingly, previous work has highlighted that although significant discrepancies between expert central readers and local readers exist, they are of minor impact when it comes to classifying MRI changes in spondyloarthritis.39 Nevertheless, both centres in this study are large teaching hospitals with specialist spondyloarthritis services and all MRIs were reported by specialised consultant MSK radiologists, which may not be fully representative. Further, longitudinal studies including different clinical set-ups as well as others incorporating validated scoring systems and dedicated central readers or multiple readers are warranted.

While our study highlights the real-world use of MRI to assess disease activity and its impact on clinical decision-making at the bedside, there are several limitations when considering these results. First, there was a significant proportion of missing baseline and follow-up outcome data, particularly with regard to ASDAS with variable follow-up periods after changing treatment, and therefore we are unable to identify whether ASDAS can discriminate between patients with active inflammation on MRI and whether patients with inflammation on MRI were more likely to respond to a change in treatment than those without. Second, the retrospective nature of the study meant treatment decisions were altered by the MRI findings, limiting any comparison between those with and without MRI inflammation who switched treatment. It is also relevant to consider that the timeframe of our report includes the period of the COVID-19 pandemic and the post-pandemic effects on waiting lists and clinical services, which may have influenced the frequency of MRI being performed and lack of outcome documentation. In addition, imaging protocols around withholding NSAIDs before MRI differ between the two centres. In Leeds, patients are advised to withhold NSAIDs for at least 48 hours before their MRI of the spine and SIJs, whereas Northwick Park does not make any recommendations about holding NSAIDs. We were only able to characterise lesions as affecting either the spine or SIJ, with further localisation not possible, due to varying degrees of detail provided by the radiologist report. Furthermore, information on the precise type and number of MRI or radiographic lesions present at diagnosis, prior to treatment or throughout the disease was not available. This is a significant limitation since data on structural and inflammatory lesions present prior to the monitoring MRI would allow us to better understand lesion expectation on the monitoring scan. Similarly, we are unable to comment on the impact of structural progression on clinical outcomes due to the inability to precisely localise lesions and their progression over time. Finally, little information about the nature of the back pain or other symptoms experienced by patients was available, whether this was inflammatory back pain, mechanical or a mixed pattern. Yet, these data give real-world evidence on current practice in two large teaching hospitals with expertise in the clinical care and imaging of axSpA.

Conclusion

MRI plays a valuable role for clinicians in assessing axSpA patients with persistent or worsening symptoms in our practice, by influencing decisions to change or escalate therapy and identifying alternative pathologies. Further research exploring the predictive value of MRI findings with clinical outcomes, including economic evaluation of routine MRI to assess disease activity in axSpA, is warranted.
