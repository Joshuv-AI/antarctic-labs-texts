The Associations Between Neighborhood Safety and Address Stability With HbA1c Levels Among Adults With Diabetes
Authors: Dove-Anna Johnson, Owusua Yamoah, Yashashvi Raghuwanshi, Tyler Barnett, Julia E. Blanchette, Betul Hatipoglu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectiveNonmedical variables such as neighborhoods and economic status impact health care management for patients with diabetes. This study aimed to discover which nonmedical drivers of health (NMDOH) were associated with responses to a diabetes intervention among a sample of adults with diabetes who previously participated in a quality improvement program (QI) to improve diabetes outcomes.MethodsThis study included 666 participants from the QI program. We used retrospective HbA1c data from the program to categorize patients as responders (values reduced by ≥ 0.5% postintervention) or nonresponders (values reduced by < 0.5% postintervention). Data were collected from a public record NMDOH dataset between December 2023 and October 2024 and linked to each patient’s demographic and clinical data in the electronic medical record. Participants with complete NMDOH and clinical/demographic data were analyzed (n = 473). ANOVA and Fisher’s exact tests examined differences in NMDOH factors between responders versus nonresponders.ResultsThe mean age was 55.5 years, mean preintervention HbA1c was 9.0%, mean postintervention HbA1c was 8.3%, and mean change in HbA1c was −0.72%. Fifty-one percent (n = 243) of patients were postintervention responders. We found that variables related to crime and address stability (frequency of address change) in the “neighborhood and built environment” domain were significantly associated with responses to the intervention.ConclusionNeighborhood crime and more frequent address changes appear to impact patients’ responses to the diabetes QI intervention. Future interventions should include social support navigators to identify and address neighborhood stressors and socioeconomic barriers that may impact patient outcomes.

Clinical Relevance

Nonmedical determinants of health greatly impact diabetes care. Our findings suggest that crime and address stability were associated with response to intervention. Therefore, consideration of safety in the patient’s neighborhood and built environment and housing stability may prove beneficial in diabetes management.

Introduction

Diabetes affects 38.4 million Americans and, if not effectively managed, can lead to complications such as kidney and cardiovascular disease.1 Diabetes management can be impacted by socioeconomic, educational, community, and neighborhood factors. These factors are nonmedical drivers of health (NMDOH), also known as social determinants of health, which account for 80% of modifiable factors of population health outcomes and are responsible for disparities in a person’s risk of diabetes and diabetes-related complications.2, 3, 4 Variables such as food insecurity, low income, and renting are associated with a lower likelihood of diabetes self-care behaviors such as eating vegetables, exercising, and checking blood glucose levels.5 People with several nonmedical drivers that are impacting their health have more severe health complications because they often cannot access treatment and resources that can help them maintain good health.6,7 Additionally, differences in cancer screening rates and availability of clinical trials plus treatment for cancer are linked to areas with increased social vulnerability.8 Among people with diabetes, low educational attainments, lower income, and neighborhood deprivation have been associated with poor physical and psychosocial outcomes.1,9

Furthermore, the total annual medical expenditure is about 2.6 times higher for a person with diabetes compared to a person without diabetes.10 The high cost of diabetes treatment and management can lead to medication adherence obstacles and self-management barriers. Extensive research has examined diabetes management and outcomes and factors such as income/cost, food insecurity, physical environment, and access to health care. A study of adults with diabetes found that 1 in 4 experienced cost-related insulin underuse, which was associated with less optimal glycemic outcomes.11 Another study found that limited access to food was a challenge for glycemic outcomes among patients with diabetes.12 Additionally, several studies of the built environment have found that unhealthy food environments, low walkable neighborhoods, and limited access to green spaces were associated with higher incidence of type 2 diabetes.13 However, most of these studies were prospective or retrospective studies that relied on self-reported data and only focused on a few NMDOH domains.

Given the great influence of NMDOH variables on diabetes management and outcomes, we conducted a post-hoc analysis using a sample of adults who previously participated in a quality improvement (QI) program that implemented a collaborative novel care model. The model brought diabetes specialty care to primary care providers (PCPs) in the form of personalized, evidence-based recommendations to the PCPs for patients with at least one HbA1c level above 8.5%.14 More details can be found in the published paper.14

Materials and Methods

QI Intervention Program

This study used retrospective clinical and demographic data from the original QI intervention (June 2021 to December 2022).14 The QI intervention included a multidisciplinary team of diabetes specialists, including endocrinologists, registered dieticians, and certified diabetes care and education specialists. Prior to implementing the QI intervention, the multidisciplinary team obtained written consent from the health system Primary Care Institute chair to approach PCPs in the network and from PCPs to participate in the intervention. The multidisciplinary team reviewed the electronic medical records for more than 1000 selected adult patients and provided evidence-based recommendations to the patient's primary care team. These recommendations resulted in the placement of 666 patients into different care pathways in which they received singular or multi-department clinical care (eg medication change, endocrinology follow-up). During the QI project patient race, ethnicity, gender, age, diabetes type, body mass index, blood pressure, HbA1c, and medication were collected prior to participating in the intervention. Postintervention HbA1c levels were measured at least 3 months postintervention.

Study Participant Categorization

For the current study, we categorized patients into 2 groups using pre and postintervention HbA1c levels: patients with a ≥0.5% reduction HbA1c from pre-to postintervention HbA1c were "responders" and those with a <0.5% pre-post reduction HbA1c were "nonresponders."

Data Analysis of NMDOH

To examine any association between NMDOH factors and clinical outcomes we collected retrospective NMDOH data (December 2023 through October 2024) from the LexisNexis Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) Attributes, a dataset with over 440 variables.15 For this study, we selected 37 LexisNexis variables across the 5 NMDOH domains (Supplementary Table 1). We chose these variables because they have been discussed in the literature as having a potential impact on diabetes management and outcomes (education access and quality, economic stability, social and community context, neighborhood and built environment, and health care access and quality).16 We used patient medical record numbers to link their clinical data from the health system EMR with their LexisNexis variables. We included patients with complete data during the period of data collection for analysis.

Demographic, clinical, and LexisNexis data were compiled into a Microsoft Office Excel (2016) spreadsheet, Version 2501. Missing LexisNexis data with numerical values were replaced by the group mean of the variable, whereas categorical variables with missing data were replaced by the most frequent value. ANOVA and Fisher's exact tests were used to identify which LexisNexis variables were associated with patients’ responses to the intervention. Logistic regression was performed to test whether there was a statistically significant association between NMDOH variables and a patient being a responder or nonresponder to the intervention. A P value <0.05 determined statistical significance.

Results

Of the 666 adult patients from the QI program, 473 had complete HbA1c and LexisNexis data for analysis. The majority of participants were female (54%) and White (63%) (Table 1). Based on a ≥0.5% reduction in HbA1c, 242 (51.2%) were responders and 231 (48.8%) nonresponders. The demographic characteristics were similar for responders and nonresponders (Table 1). The average age of responders was 56.03 ± 9.21 years compared to 54.9 ± 11.65 years for nonresponders. The mean preintervention HbA1c for responders was 9.65 ± 1.78 compared to 8.31 ± 1.4 for nonresponders. Discussion

In this retrospective study, we analyzed the associations between 37 LexisNexis-derived NMDOH factors and HbA1c levels among 473 adults with diabetes. LexisNexis data have been linked to the National Cancer Institute Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results registry.17 To our knowledge, LexisNexis has not been utilized to explore the association between NMDOH variables and HbA1c response to a diabetes QI project, making the current study a unique contribution to research on nonmedical factors impacting diabetes. Our ANOVA analysis demonstrated an association between neighborhood crime and address stability and participants responses to the QI project intervention, highlighting the importance of considering the neighborhood and built environment in HbA1c management amongst patients with diabetes.

The association between crime, safety, and neighborhood aesthetics and metabolic diseases and diabetes has been explored. This association with crime is likely due to the impact it has on a resident’s perception of safety and their willingness to engage in physical activity outside the home. A study found that after adjusting for confounding variables such as race and income level, physical inactivity mediated 60% of the relationship between crime and type 2 diabetes.18 Another study found an association between violent crime (shootings) and obesity and elevated blood pressure.19 The neighborhood a person lives in can also influence their dietary habits and access to healthy food. Neighborhood aesthetics, such as noise, crime, and neighborhood attractiveness are associated with diet and diabetes knowledge.20

Within the context of our study, address stability was defined as how often the individual changed addresses. In the broader literature, address stability or instability has various definitions, including the ability to pay rent, frequent address changes, high expenditure of monthly income on housing, stress about the ability to pay rent/mortgage, and overcrowded living conditions.21, 22, 23 A narrative review concluded that housing instability is adversely associated with cardiometabolic conditions, such as diabetes.21 This review included studies in which people with diabetes who were experiencing housing instability had decreased diabetes self-efficacy and increased likelihood of uncontrolled diabetes.24, 25, 26, 27 The narrative review also noted that some researchers did not find a difference in diabetes management or glycemic control in people experiencing housing instability.21 Another study exploring diabetes self-care behaviors found a decreased likelihood of HbA1c checks, eye exams, and physician visits among people experiencing housing insecurity, defined as being stressed about being able to pay rent or mortgage.22 Given the high association between response to the QI project and crime levels and address stability in our study, it will be important to consider neighborhood crime levels and address stability when designing future interventions.

Our study has limitations. First, the study population only included individuals enrolled in a health system insurance plan. Thus, this sample may not reflect diabetes patients in the general population and may not be generalizable. Second, although the population included diverse demographics, income levels, and diabetes types, employment at a health system may have affected participant behavior or diabetes knowledge, resulting in implicit bias. Third, individuals in the sample had health care within the past year. As a result, we could have missed adults with diabetes who were at high risk of complications and had difficulty with diabetes management. Fourth, differences in pre-intervention HbA1c levels between the 2 groups, with responder levels higher than nonresponders, suggests the potential for confounding from unmeasured variables that may have influenced the intervention response and the associated NMDOH data. Fifth, due to the nature of the analysis, it is difficult to determine whether the crime and address stability variables were associated with a specific degree of increase or decrease in HbA1c levels. Additionally, the nuances of NMDOH may influence the outcomes. For example, where a person lives, how long they stay at one address, or how frequently they move can be influenced by neighborhood crime, income, education level, and occupation. Therefore, there could be several factors influencing address stability and subsequently the association with response to intervention in this study. On the other hand, individuals with address instability may not benefit from green spaces or access to grocery stores when living in a stable neighborhood. Sixth, we only included patients with complete (prepost-QI intervention) HbA1c data and LexisNexis data for analysis, which could be a source of bias because differences in patients with incomplete data were not assessed.

Future studies should explore the extent to which HbA1c levels change based on nonmedical and environmental variables. We also hope to identify and address specific neighborhood stressors that impact people with diabetes and recruit participants with decreased health care interactions.
