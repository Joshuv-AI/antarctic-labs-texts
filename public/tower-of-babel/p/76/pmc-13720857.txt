Analysis of Independent Risk Factors and Construction of a Risk Prediction Model for Non-Suicidal Self-Injurious Behavior in Adolescents with Depression
Authors: Ying He, Shengying Dai, Tian Tang, Ying Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The aim is to investigate the independent risk factors of non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) in adolescents with depression and to construct a risk prediction model to evaluate its predictive efficacy.Methods:Clinical data were collected from 150 adolescents diagnosed with depression who were admitted to West China Hospital of Sichuan University between October 2022 and October 2024. The patients were categorized into 2 groups based on the presence or absence of NSSI behavior: NSSI group (n = 65) and non-NSSI group (n = 85). Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) and Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA) were used to evaluate the degree of depression and anxiety. The Adolescent Self-rated Life Events Scale (ASLEC), Family Intimacy and Adaptability Scale II (FACES II-CV), Childhood Trauma Questionnaire Short Form (CTQ-SF), and Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) were used to evaluate the frequency and intensity of stressful life events, family functioning, childhood trauma, and perceived social support. Independent risk factors associated with NSSI behavior were screened by univariate and multivariate multivariable logistic regression analysis, and a Nomogram model for NSSI risk prediction was established based on these factors.Results:Significant variations were observed between the NSSI group and the non-NSSI group regarding factors such as gender, depression scores, interpersonal stress, learning stress, closeness to family, adaptability within the family, emotional abuse, emotional neglect, physical abuse, physical neglect, as well as perceived support from both family and friends (P < .05); univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses revealed that gender (P < .001, 95% CI:1.10~1.46), interpersonal stress (P < .001, 95% CI:1.10~1.46), academic stress (P = .004, 95% CI:1.06~1.37), and somatic abuse (P < .001, 95% CI:1.51~3.34) were independent risk factors for the presence of NSSI behaviors in adolescent depression (P < .05); patients’ perceived support from family (P = .002, 95% CI:0.57-0.88) was an independent protective factor for the presence of NSSI behaviors in adolescent depression (P < .05); good fit between calibration curve and ideal curve; and the area under curve of receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.868, and when the optimal risk cut-off value reached 29.30%, the model accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were 0.787, 0.694, and 0.908, respectively.Conclusion:Adolescents with depression are at higher risk of NSSI behavior and have complex risk factors for its occurrence. The risk prediction model constructed in the study for the occurrence of NSSI behaviors in adolescents with depression is effective in predicting the risk and may enable effective differentiation of patients at risk.

Introduction

Depression is one of the most common mental illnesses worldwide and is projected to become the leading cause of global disease burden by 2030, posing a significant public health challenge. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable to depression during this critical period of brain development, emotional growth, and cognitive maturation.1 Unlike adult depression, adolescent depression is characterized by prolonged, recurrent episodes and high rates of functional impairment, which disrupt both personal and academic lives.2-4 Both their personal and intellectual lives are disrupted by the reduced interest in life, self-guilt, and self-blame that people suffering from depression often display. People who suffer from severe depression may also injure themselves, which can seriously endanger young people’s physical and mental health.

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), defined as the deliberate and repeated infliction of harm to one’s body without suicidal intent, is a particularly concerning behavior among adolescents with depression. The patient repeatedly and purposefully causes direct, intentional, and repeated harm to their body, such as burns, cuts, and other injuries.5,6 About 25% of teenagers in China commit NSSI, which is a percentage that is far higher than the global average. Teenagers between the ages of 15 and 16 had the highest prevalence of NSSI, with a discernible decline observed after the age of 17. However, adolescents who engage in repetitive NSSI appear to be at high risk of continuing to utilize dysfunctional emotion regulation strategies promptly after NSSI cessation.7,8 People with depression accompanied by NSSI have a higher risk of suicide than the general population, posing a significant threat to their lives and health and the lives of their families.9

The pathophysiology of NSSI in adolescent depression remains poorly understood, with current research primarily focusing on neurobiological mechanisms, particularly dysregulation in neurotransmitter systems. Childhood trauma, a significant factor affecting adolescents’ mental health, is increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to NSSI behavior. Exposure to adverse childhood experiences, such as abuse, neglect, or household dysfunction, can disrupt normal psychological development and increase vulnerability to mental health problems in later life, including depression and self-injury behaviors. This association underscores the importance of considering childhood trauma when investigating risk factors for NSSI in adolescents with depression.10 Strong pain stimuli that generate a positive emotional response are experienced by adolescents with NSSI due to reduced levels of resting opioid transmitters and cerebrospinal fluid endorphins and enkephalins.11 The prefrontal cortex, insula, and amygdala circuits are among the brain regions that have been the subject of numerous studies using functional magnetic resonance to examine the neuropathic circuits of NSSI. Of these, the reward circuits’ nucleus accumbens, amygdala, and gray substance are the ones that are most closely linked to NSSI.12,13 Current approaches for adolescents with depression and NSSI include psychotherapy, pharmacotherapy, dietary interventions, and physiotherapy.14-16 However, adolescent depression patients with NSSI are influenced by multiple factors. In addition, current efforts to educate the public and raise awareness about depression and NSSI remain inadequate, resulting in delays in early screening and diagnosis. Therefore, greater attention to this issue is urgently needed.

This study hypothesizes that there are independent risk factors for NSSI behavior in adolescents with depression and that a risk prediction model based on these factors can effectively predict the risk of NSSI behavior. The aim is to explore those independent risk factors and construct a risk prediction model to assess its predictive efficacy, thereby offering a scientific basis for early screening, diagnosis, prevention, and treatment.

Material and Methods

Patient Population and Grouping

This cross-sectional study analyzed independent risk factors and developed a risk prediction model for NSSI in adolescents with depression. Data were collected from adolescents aged 11-18 years who visited the West China Hospital between October 2022 and October 2024. Among these patients, 227 were randomly selected and met the clinical diagnostic criteria for depression. After applying other inclusion and exclusion criteria, 150 adolescents were included as the final study sample. Inclusion criteria: (1) patients should have their depression confirmed with a clinical diagnosis based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria;17 (2) age: 11-18 years old; (3) no abnormalities in verbal communication, writing and reading, and listening and comprehension; (4) those who could independently cooperate in completing the questionnaire about the study; and (5) the patients or their family members were informed and gave consent. Exclusion criteria: (1) combined with other serious brain diseases; (2) patients with organic pathology; (3) patients with life safety risks; (4) patients with poor communication and co-operation; and (5) patients who had undergone other treatments or interventions within a 3-month period prior to the commencement of this study. The flow chart is shown in Figure 1. Adolescents diagnosed with depression were categorized into 2 groups based on the presence or absence of NSSI: the NSSI group (n = 65) and the non-NSSI group (n = 85). Demographic and clinical characteristics, including gender, age, disease duration, and handedness, were recorded for all participants. This study was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the West China Hospital of Sichuan University (Ethical Approval Number: 2024-225; Date: March 5, 2024). All patients voluntarily signed informed consent forms.

Data Collection

This study employed a cross-sectional design, collecting data from 150 adolescents diagnosed with depression. Participants were categorized into 2 groups based on the presence or absence of NSSI: the NSSI group (n = 65) and the non-NSSI group (n = 85). Demographic and clinical characteristics, including gender, age, disease duration, and handedness, were recorded for all participants. The severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms was assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD-17) and the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), respectively, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. The Adolescent Self-Rated Life Events Scale (ASLEC) was used to evaluate the occurrence and impact of recent life events, covering 6 dimensions: interpersonal relationships, academic stress, punishment, loss, health adaptation, and others, with higher total scores reflecting a greater impact of life events on the patient. Family functioning was assessed using the Family Adaptability and Cohesion Scale II-Chinese Version (FACES II-CV), which measures 2 dimensions: family closeness and adaptability. Based on the “Arch Pole Model,” family closeness was classified into 4 types: loose (<55.0), free (55.0-63.9), intimate (63.9-71.9), and enmeshed (>71.9), while family adaptability was categorized as rigid (<44.0), structured (44.0-50.0), flexible (50.9-57.1), and chaotic (>57.1). Childhood trauma was evaluated using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire Short Form (CTQ-SF), which includes 5 dimensions: emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, emotional neglect, and physical neglect. Perceived social support was measured using the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS), a 12-item scale that evaluates support from family, friends, and significant others, with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA) and R 3.6.2 software (glmnet, caret, pROC, and Hmisc packages) (R Core Team; Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± SD and compared using independent samples t-tests or 1-way ANOVA, as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify risk factors associated with the presence of NSSI in adolescents with depression. Variables with P < .05 in the univariate analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to construct risk prediction models. Multicollinearity among variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The calibration of the predictive models was evaluated using calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) goodness-of-fit test. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to assess the net benefit of the model across different risk thresholds. The optimal cut-off value for the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was determined using Youden’s index (Youden’s Index = Sensitivity + Specificity − 1), ensuring the best balance between true positive and true negative rates. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of General Information of Patients in 2 Groups

No notable difference was observed regarding age, disease duration, and handedness among the 2 groups (P > .05). However, the male proportion in the NSSI group exceeded that of the non-NSSI group, with this difference being statistically significant (P < .05). The analysis results are presented in Table 1.

Comparison of Patient-Related Illness Characteristics Between the 2 Groups

There were statistically significant differences between the 2 groups of patients in terms of depressive symptom scores, interpersonal stress, study stress, family closeness, family adaptability, emotional abuse, emotional neglect, somatic abuse, somatic neglect, perceived support from family, and perceived support from friends (P < .05), and there were no significant differences between the 2 groups of patients in terms of anxiety symptom scores, punished, loss, adaptability, emotional abuse, and perceived support from the rest of the society (P > .05); specific data analysis is shown in Table 1.

Univariate Logistic Regression Analysis

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed with the presence of NSSI behaviors in adolescents with depression as the dependent variable (NSSI = 1, non-NSSI = 0), and variables with statistically significant differences between the 2 groups as the independent variables, continuous variables entered at their original values, and the categorical variables were coded as follows: for gender, male was assigned a value of 0 and female was assigned a value of 1. Family cohesion was categorized into 4 levels: Loose (0), Free (1), Intimate (2), and Enmeshed (3). Family adaptability was similarly divided into 4 categories: Irregular (0), Flexible (1), Regular (2), and Rigid (3). The results showed that gender as female, family closeness as free and intimate, depressive symptoms, interpersonal stress, study stress, acculturation, somatic abuse, emotional neglect, somatic neglect, support from family and friends were statistically different (P < .05) on whether or not adolescent depression was accompanied by NSSI behaviors, and the specific data for the analysis are shown in Table 2.

Multivariable Logistic Regression Analysis

The results showed that gender (P < .001, 95%CI:1.10-1.46), interpersonal stress (P < .001, 95%CI:1.10-1.46), study stress (P = .004, 95%CI:1.06-1.37), and somatic abuse (P < .001, 95%CI:1.51-3.34) were the adolescent depression patients with independent risk factors for the presence of NSSI behaviors (P < .05). Patients’ perceived support from family (P = .002, 95%CI:0.57-0.88) was an independent protective factor for the presence of NSSI behavior in adolescent depression (P < .05). All variables in the multivariable logistic regression analysis passed the diagnosis of covariance (VIF < 5), which eliminated the interference of multiple covariance on model stability. Specific results of the data analysis are shown in Table 2.

Constructing a Column-line Graphical Model of the NSSI in Adolescents with Depression

Based on the results of logistic multifactor regression analysis, a nomogram model was developed to predict the occurrence of NSSI in adolescent patients diagnosed with depression. This predictive model incorporates 5 key variables: gender, interpersonal pressure, academic stress, experiences of physical abuse, and perceived familial support. The nomogram is illustrated in Figure 2.

Model Evaluation

The ROC curve was plotted to evaluate the predictive ability of the column-line graph model (Figure 3), and the area under curve (AUC) of this regression equation was 0.868 (95%CI:0.813-0.923), suggesting that the accuracy of the predictive model of this ROC curve was high. After the HL goodness-of-fit test and calibration curve results showed that the predicted values were not significantly different from the measured values (Figure 4), and the model was well fitted (P = .613 > 0.05). A decision curve (DCA) for the risk column-line diagram of NSSI behaviors in adolescents with depression was constructed to make the column-line diagram model clinically useful. This decision curve demonstrated comparable net benefits on a scale of 0 to 1 based on a column-line diagram model of risk for NSSI in adolescents with depression (Figure 5). At the optimal threshold, the model has an accuracy of 0.787 (0.712~0.849), a sensitivity of 0.694 (0.596~0.792), a specificity of 0.908 (0.837~0.978), a PPV (positive predictive value) of 0.908 (0.837~0.978), an NPV (negative predictive value) of 0.694 (0.596~0.792), and an optimal cut-off value of 0.293 or predicted risk: 29.3%.

Discussion

Depression is a common mental and psychological disorder, primarily characterized by persistent and long-term low mood, and it has an extremely high prevalence rate. Research has shown that low-frequency and limited-site NSSI behaviors in children and adolescents are indicative of mental health issues in adulthood.18,19 Conversely, high-frequency and multi-site NSSI behaviors are strongly associated with suicidal ideation and are considered significant predictors of suicide risk.20

According to the current study, female adolescents are more likely than male adolescents to have depression and engage in NSSI practices. This may be because female adolescents are more likely than their male counterparts to experience emotional instability due to significant hormonal changes during puberty, particularly involving estrogen and progesterone.21 The prefrontal cortex and the amygdala, 2 regions of the female brain involved in emotional processing, exhibit heightened reactivity to stress and emotional discomfort. Additionally, studies have demonstrated gender differences in the coping strategies used to manage negative emotions. Males tend to rely on cognitive control to manage their negative emotions, while females are more likely to seek emotional support. This distinction may contribute to the increased likelihood of female adolescents with depression engaging in NSSI as a coping mechanism in response to negative life events. Furthermore, gender differences in NSSI behavior are not fixed but evolve throughout adolescence, peaking during mid-puberty (16-17 years), decreasing in late adolescence, and gradually subside in early adulthood.22

In this study, interpersonal stress was identified as an independent risk factor for NSSI in adolescents with depression. Interpersonal stress refers to the psychological strain that emerges during interpersonal interactions due to various factors, such as communication barriers, trust issues, and social isolation. This stress may originate from multiple sources, such as family, friends, teachers, and other social contexts.23 Relationships play a crucial role in the lives of young people, and positive relationships are beneficial for both their physical and mental health.24 Adolescents with depression often struggle with emotional regulation and may find it difficult to process or articulate their emotions effectively. This emotional dysregulation is often exacerbated by interpersonal stress. When faced with challenging interpersonal conflicts, these individuals may resort to self-injury as a means of regaining control over their emotional pain. This behavior also serves as a form of emotional catharsis, providing temporary relief from external pressures and offering a degree of psychological comfort.25,26

Adolescents, particularly those in middle and high school, are often expected to prioritize academic achievement as their primary focus.27 In contemporary society, competition is intense, social comparison is prevalent, and both parents and teachers frequently impose high expectations Consequently, adolescents face substantial pressure from various sources, including family, educators, and the broader social environment. Negative emotions such as frustration, feelings of inadequacy, low self-esteem, and self-blame are common responses to poor academic performance and difficulties encountered in their educational journey. When adolescents are unable to cope with these pressures effectively or in a timely manner, and in the absence of adequate external support, some may internalize their emotions, leading to self-injurious thoughts and behaviors. These findings are consistent with the results of semi-structured interviews conducted by Chen X et al,28 who interviewed 24 adolescents across various studies in China. The heavy academic burden faced by Chinese adolescents, coupled with both parental and student anxiety over academic performance, is a significant factor leading to NSSI behaviors in this population.

Adolescents with depression who have experienced childhood maltreatment are significantly more likely to engage in NSSI behaviors. Several prominent theoretical models of NSSI emphasize the significant role of childhood maltreatment in the onset of NSSI behaviors among depressed adolescents.29 The findings of this study suggest that childhood trauma significantly influences the occurrence of NSSI in adolescents with depressive disorders, with physical abuse identified as an independent risk factor. The definition of physical abuse varies across countries and depends on the severity of the trauma experienced by the child as a result of physical mistreatment. The National Child Traumatic Stress Network (NCTSN) defines physical abuse broadly as any act by a caregiver that causes physical harm to a child.30,31 According to Yates et al.’s developmental psychopathology framework, individuals develop 5 key competencies—motivational, attitudinal, instrumental, emotional, and interpersonal—while adapting positively to their environment. Adolescents often rationalize childhood abuse experiences through self-blame, leading to maladaptive beliefs such as feelings of unworthiness and worthlessness. The development of maladaptive cognitions resulting from adverse experiences can lead to a range of psychological and behavioral issues that may persistently affect an individual’s physical and mental well-being.32

The family environment represents one of the most significant external influences on the mental health of children and adolescents during their developmental years.33 As the fundamental unit of human society, the family plays a pivotal role in an individual’s personal development, shaping various aspects of growth and well-being. Psychological functioning is a key responsibility of the family unit, and a supportive family environment can significantly enhance the mental health and well-being of its members.31 Extensive research has demonstrated a strong correlation between family dysfunction and the occurrence of NSSI among adolescents. Moreover, evidence suggests that poor family functioning is a significant predictor of NSSI prevalence in both childhood and adolescence, and may serve as a longitudinal predictor of NSSI. This study identified a significant discrepancy in perceived familial support between adolescents with depression and those with both depression and NSSI. This factor has been recognized as an independent risk factor for NSSI behaviors in adolescents with depression. These findings align with previous reports, which concluded that adolescents with greater family support are less likely to engage in NSSI behaviors.34,35 Therefore, it is recommended that greater attention be given to the role of family functioning, family atmosphere, and family support in preventing and treating NSSI behaviors in adolescents.

Early detection of depressed adolescents with NSSI behaviors in clinical settings is crucial for the prevention of severe outcomes, such as suicide. Non-suicidal self-injury behaviors are often manifested subtly, with the sites of physical injury typically concealed. Moreover, individuals who engage in NSSI behaviors are frequently at an elevated risk for suicidal ideation and action. This underscores the importance of implementing screening procedures for individuals at heightened risk of engaging in NSSI behaviors. Unlike traditional statistical analyses in the medical field, clinical predictive modeling aims to integrate a variety of variables to assess the likelihood of an individual developing a specific condition or experiencing a particular future outcome.36 In this study, 6 statistically significant factors were used to construct a predictive model for NSSI behaviors in adolescents with depression. The results were visualized through column-line plotsnomogram, enabling the prediction of adverse NSSI behavioral events in clinical settings. The AUC of 0.868 in this study indicates a high degree of model differentiation. Additionally, the HL test of the calibration curve yielded a P-value greater than .05, indicating that the model exhibited a good fit. Advantages of the clinical prediction model used in this study is that each indicator is derived from the baseline data of patients, which eliminates the need for costly diagnostic procedures and additional human and material resources, thus reducing the overall burden on medical resources. In comparison to individual risk factors, the column-line plots allow for the integration of multiple predictors, with the sum of scores from several predictors offering higher predictive efficacy than individual predictions. While column-line plots facilitate the visualization of multiple predictor probabilities, the effectiveness of any risk identification strategy depends on the implementation of appropriate interventions. Therefore, it is essential to prioritize attention, treatment and the adoption of relevant interventions for high-risk populations.

The predictive model of NSSI behaviors in adolescent depression, established in this study, incorporates asymptotic variations, enabling intuitive prediction of NSSI behaviors in adolescents with depression. Furthermore, it provides patients with personalized and more accurate NSSI behavioral risk assessments. Raising awareness of the behavioral predictors of NSSI in adolescents with depression is the first and crucial step in developing effective prevention and intervention strategies to prevent recurrent lethal injury. Future research should examine the etiology of NSSI behaviors in adolescents with depression within an integrated framework that considers biological, social, and psychological factors, guiding the design of targeted interventions.

This study advances the field by providing a comprehensive analysis of risk factors for NSSI in adolescents with depression, integrating psychological, environmental, and historical dimensions. Unlike previous studies, the authors developed a nomogram-based risk prediction model, which offers a practical tool for clinicians to assess individual risk levels and tailor interventions. The authors’ use of validated scales and rigorous statistical methods further enhances the reliability and applicability of the authors’ findings. Additionally, by focusing specifically on adolescents, this study addresses a critical gap in the literature and provides insights that are directly relevant to adolescent mental health care.
