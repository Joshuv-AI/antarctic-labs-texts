Eco-friendly synthesis of CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite for efficient photocatalytic degradation of rhodamine B dye
Authors: Asfaw Negash, Lemma M Derseh, Abebe Tedla, Jemal M Yassin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant-mediated synthesized materials are receiving more attention than conventional ones due to their wide availability, ease of access, simple preparation methods, environmental benign, and possess superior physicochemical properties. In this work, plant extract-mediated CuO, Bi2O3, and CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite samples were successfully synthesized using bamboo leaves extract as a capping agent. These materials were utilized for the photodegradation of Rhodamine B (RhB) dye, which served as a model organic dye pollutant. The physicochemical characterization techniques such as XRD, SEM-EDS, FTIR, and DRS-UV-vis spectrophotometry provide insight into the crystal structure, morphology, surface functional groups, and optical properties. These analyses confirm the effective formation of CuO, Bi2O3, and CuO/Bi2O3 materials. Surprisingly, upon calcination at 450 °C for 4 h, the color of the nanocomposite changed from pale green to gray greenish, providing evidence for the formation of the CuO in CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite. The photocatalytic optimization parameters such as pH (4), catalyst load (35 mg), irradiation time (180 min) and concentration of RhB (10 mg L−1) dye were investigated. By coupling CuO with Bi2O3 nanoparticles resulted in an improved photocatalytic property for the degradation of RhB dye under optimal conditions. As a result, CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite exhibited a significantly boosted photocatalytic degradation efficiency (95.6%) compared to pure CuO (40.2%) and Bi2O3 (80.5%) photocatalysts, with good reusability. For comparison purpose, the photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye using selected photocatalyst was evaluated under dark and sunlight systems. This eco-friendly approach holds great potential for synthesis new nanocomposite with modified properties, thereby enabling the practical application of high-efficiency photocatalysts. Introduction

Industrialization and urbanization have continuously compromised the quality of drinkable and clean water over time. Various industries, including textiles, cosmetics, pulp and paper, paint, pharmaceuticals, carpet and printing, leather, hair, and food, have extensively utilized dyes as colorants1,2. Unfortunately, the excessive use of chemicals and inadequate treatment of these dyes have resulted in severe pollution of the environment, especially water bodies. Consequently, these pollutants possess very toxic and carcinogenic compound, thereby posing significant health risks and the potential for disease outbreaks3,4.

Nowadays, the textile and paper industries utilize over 10,000 different synthetic dyes and pigments, which has a detrimental effect on human health, and the environment. The major discharge of dye waste from the industries includes various types of organic dyes such as methylene blue, malachite green, rhodamine B, congo red, and etc5–7. These dyes have a direct or indirect negative impact on the proper functioning of the ecosystem. Specifically, they interfere with the absorption of sunlight and consequently disrupt the life activities of aquatic organisms and the process of photosynthesis8–10. Moreover owing to anthropogenic activities, accessing pure drinkable water is one of the big global challenge, and roughly 47% of the world’s population will face the challenge of clean water scarcity by 203011. In terms of legislation, there is still no worldwide consensus on the release of wastewater containing dyes and the textile effluents associated with them. However, researchers have been developed numerous methods for the removal of pollutant dyes and achieving complete mineralization (to water and carbon dioxide) using novel methods and materials based on the characteristics of wastewater. The most commonly used approaches to treat wastewater are physical methods: adsorption, ion exchange, and membrane filtration, chemical methods: coagulation-flocculation, advanced oxidation approaches, and electrochemical, or biologically methods: enzyme, bacteria, fungal, yeast, and algae-assisted wastewater degradation12–15. Among the mentioned methodologies/technologies advanced oxidation approaches using semiconductor-mediated photocatalysis has been extensively investigated since 197216. It offers an efficient means of eliminating organic contaminants that are resistant to degradation or mineralization by alternative methods17,18. However, non-advanced oxidation techniques require additional treatments due to the generation of secondary pollutant (transfer of dyes from liquid to solid phase)19,20.

ZnO, TiO2, CuO, and so on semiconductor materials can be prepared at the nanoscale using various approaches, including chemical, physical, and biological methods21,22. These nanomaterials exhibit remarkable differences in properties compared to their bulk counterparts, owing to their extremely small size, greater surface area, simple preparation methods, more functionality, and lower cost22–25. The physicochemical properties of these nanomaterials are highly dependent on factors such as material type, preparation method, morphology, size, and crystal structure. These differences arise from the unique physical and structural properties of the atoms, molecules, and bulk materials that make up the nanomaterials26,27. While the chemical method can enable the rapid synthesis of a large quantity of nanoparticles (NPs), it often requires the use of capping or stabilizing agents to prevent particle aggregation28–30. The chemicals used for NPs synthesis and stabilization can be toxic, costly, and lead to the generation of non-eco-friendly byproducts.

To address these concerns, there has been a growing interest in developing biological approaches for various NPs synthesis. These methods are free from the use of toxic and special chemicals, are generally faster and more robust, and do not require costly equipment. As a result, biological approaches are emerging as more environmentally benign and cost-effective alternatives for the production of NPs29,31–33.

In recent years, plant-mediated synthesis of semiconductors has garnered great attention as an advantageous and popular biological method. This is due to its wide availability, easy accessibility, biodiversity, simple preparation, environmental friendliness, and superior physicochemical properties34,35. The reason for the efficacy of this approach is that plants contain phytochemicals such as phenols, flavonoids, aldehydes, and ketones. These compounds have the ability to reduce inorganic salts into metal oxide nanoparticles due to their reducing and capping power36,37. This green synthesis method is an efficient way to produce semiconductor nanoparticles with desirable properties. Among various plant species, biomolecules derived from bamboo leaves extracts can serve as both reducing and capping agents for the preparation of copper oxide (CuO), bismuth oxide (Bi2O3), and CuO/Bi2O3 composites. Therefore, the aim of this work is to synthesize highly efficient and environmentally friendly photocatalyst materials using these materials, with the goal of degrading rhodamine B (RhB) dye, a cationic pollutant, from aqueous solutions. The strong color intensity, widespread presence, chemical stability, environmental impact, and their flat band potentials of RhB dye make it an ideal candidate for evaluating photocatalytic activity38,39.

Cu, Bi, and their oxides are considered promising semiconducting materials for photocatalytic applications. This is due to their outstanding catalytic properties, favorable electronic structure and/or structural and morphological features, exceptional physicochemical characteristics, low environmental toxicity, simple preparation methods, chemical stability, and cost-effectiveness40–42. Both CuO and Bi2O3 can be utilized as photocatalysts to degrade organic contaminants in aqueous solutions. However, these photocatalysts can face certain limitations, such as: (i) rapid recombination of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, which reduces their catalytic efficiency, (ii) relatively lower solar light utilization, and (iii) relatively smaller specific surface area.

To address these challenges, various strategies can be employed, such as: (i) enhancing visible light absorption through elemental doping, (ii) modifying the properties and band gap structure of the photocatalysts, (iii) forming nanocomposite and /or heterojunction structures, and (iv) implementing dye sensitization techniques43–49. These approaches can help improve the overall performance and efficiency of Cu- and Bi-based photocatalysts for effective degradation of organic pollutants in aqueous environments. Among these techniques, the coupling of Bi2O3 with narrow band gap semiconductor materials to form nanocomposite materials is quite common. Specifically, Bi2O3 has been coupled with CuO to form a CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite. This coupling has leveraged the synergistic effects between the two materials and improved the desirable characteristics of the material. The resulting nanocomposite has exhibited several key benefits: (i) reduced drawbacks associated with flexibility in extending the light response range, (ii) improved ionic conductivity, (iii) improved reusability, and (iv) enhanced separation and transfer of photogenerated charge carriers50–53. Overall, these improvements have led to enhanced photocatalytic activity compared to the individual components.

In this work, we synthesized a bamboo leaves extract-mediated CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite under simple, low cost, highly efficient, eco-friendly, and convenient preparation approach for photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye application. Compared with some earlier reports, the as-synthesized nanocomposite materials also offered superior photocatalytic activity.

Experimental

Chemicals and reagents

All the chemicals and reagents employed in this study were of analytical grade and used as received, without additional purification steps. Copper chloride dihydrate (CuCl2.2H2O, 99%, Loba Chemie), Bismuth nitrate pentahydrate, (Bi(NO3)3.5H2O, 98%, Loba Chemie), Sodium chloride (NaCl, 99.9%, SRL Chemical), Sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 98%, Loba Chemie), Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 35.4%, Loba Chemie), RhB dye (C28H31ClN2O3, Sigma Aldrich, ≥ 99.9%) and ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.9%, Loba Chemie).

Synthesis procedures

Preparation of bamboo leaves extract

Fresh leaves of bamboo were collected from Tarmaber woreda kebele 02, North Shoa Zone, Amhara Regional State, Ethiopia. The collected fresh bamboo leaves were washed with tap water, followed by distilled water, to remove any dust or impurities. The leaves were then air dried for three weeks at room temperature in the shade. After drying and crushing, the bamboo leaves were mashed into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle, preparing them for further extraction.

20 g of dried bamboo leaf powder was weighed and refluxed in a 100 mL conical flask containing distilled water followed the procedure elsewhere54, with some modification. The mixture was heated at 70 °C for 30 min. After 30 min, the obtained extract was allowed to cool and then filtered using Whatman filter paper. This resulted in a brown-red aqueous filtrate (extract), which was then used as a reducing agent or capping agent for the synthesis of target materials, such as CuO, Bi2O3, and CuO/Bi2O3.

Synthesis of bamboo leaves extract-assisted CuO and Bi2O3 NPs

CuO NPs was synthesized using an aqueous extract of bamboo leaves following a previously reported method55, with some modification. Specifically, 40 mL of the aqueous bamboo leaf extract and 100 mL of distilled water were combined in a conical flask (solution 1). Then, 0.70 g of CuCl2·2H2O and 0.23 g of NaOH pellets were added to solution 1. The resulting mixed solution was stirred and refluxed for 2 h at 60 °C. The reaction mixture was then centrifuged and washed with deionized water (x4, four times), followed by one washing with ethanol (x1, one time). Finally, the precipitate was dried in an oven at 100 °C for 6 h and then calcined at 450 °C for 4 h. The resulting dark green sample was represented as CuO (Fig. 1a).

Biosynthesis procedure of (a) CuO nanoparticles, (b) Bi2O3 nanoparticles, and (c) CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite samples.

Bi2O3 NPs was synthesized using an aqueous extract of bamboo leaves following a previously reported method56, with some modification. In a typical synthesis, 40 mL of the aqueous bamboo leaf extract and 100 mL of distilled water were combined in a round-bottom flask (Solution 1). Then, 1.99 g of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O and 0.40 g of NaOH was added to Solution 1. The resulting mixture was stirred and refluxed for 2 h at 60 °C. Finally, the sample was centrifuged, washed, dried, and calcined using the same procedure described in the previous section. The final product was represented as Bi2O3 (Fig. 1b).

Synthesis of bamboo leaves extract-assisted CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite

The CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite was synthesized using aqueous extract of bamboo leaves by following the method elsewhere57, with modification. In a conical flask, 40 mL of the bamboo leaf extract and 100 mL of distilled water were combined (Solution 1). Then, 0.034 g of CuCl2.2H2O, 1.99 g of Bi(NO3)3.5H2O and 0.40 g of NaOH were added to Solution 1. The mixed solution was then stirred and refluxed for 2 h at 60 oC. Finally, the product was centrifuged, washed, dried and calcinated using the procedure mentioned above in “Synthesis of bamboo leaves extract-assisted CuO and Bi2O3 NPs” section and labeled as CuO/Bi2O3 (Fig. 1c).

Characterization of nanomaterials

The structural properties of the as-synthesized materials were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The measurements were carried out on an X’Pert Pro PANalytical instrument, which utilized a Cu Kα radiation source (wavelength of 0.15406 nm) at step scan rate of 0.02 (step time: 1s; 2θ range: 5.0–90). The sizes of particles within as-synthesized samples were estimated using Debye-Scherrer’s equation, which was applied to the most intense peak observed in the XRD patterns. The surface functional groups of the as synthesized samples were identified after dispersed in paraffin liquid and sandwiched within two KBr pellets using FT-IR spectroscopy, spectra were recorded over 400–4000 cm−1 in a Perkin Elmer FT-IR spectrometer. Scanning electron microscopy and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) were carried out using a Hitachi Tabletop Microscope TM1000 with a tungsten filament electron gun to detect the morphology and elemental composition of materials. From energy dispersive X-ray (EDX, an acquisition time (s) 40.0; process time 3 h; and an accelerating voltage (kV) 15.0) linked with SEM, the elemental percent weight distribution of the as-synthesized sample was determined. UV-vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS) measurements were run in a Varian Cary 5000 double-beam UV-vis Near-IR spectrophotometer. Collected spectra of the photocatalysts were converted to Kubelka-Munk function, F(R) versus wavelength.

Photocatalytic degradation study of RhB dye

RhB dye is commonly used as a representative of widespread organic dyes that are very difficult to decompose in waste streams under visible-light irradiation. The photocatalytic activities of plant-mediated CuO, Bi2O3, and CuO/Bi2O3 photocatalysts were evaluated by the photodegradation of RhB dye under sunlight irradiation in open air at room temperature. The reaction suspensions were prepared by adding the required amount of photocatalyst into 100 mL of 10 mg L−1 RhB dye solution. Before starting the illumination, the reaction mixture was stirred for 30 min in the dark to establish the adsorption-desorption equilibrium between the dye and the photocatalyst58,59. The photoreactor was maintained under magnetic stirring to promote a uniform suspension and the time range of sunlight exposure was from 10:30 am to 1:00 pm. Samples were collected at regular 20 min intervals, centrifuged, and filtered before measuring the absorbance. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy analysis

FT-IR analysis of the vibrational absorption bands in the aqueous leaf extract of bamboo and photocatalysts provides information about the chemical species present on the surface. The FT-IR spectrum of bamboo leaves extract-mediated Bi2O3, CuO, CuO/Bi2O3 and the aqueous bamboo leaves extract samples are shown in Fig. 4. The absorption bands of the CuO NPs are observed at 1007, 806, and 463 cm-1, corresponding to C-O stretching, C-H bending out of the plane (aromatic structures), and Cu-O bond vibrations, respectively65. The absorption band peaks of the Bi2O3 NPs are seen at 1049, 980, and 465 cm-1, confirming the presence of C-O, C-H bending out of the plane of aromatic structures, and Bi-O bond, respectively66. This suggests that the phytochemical constituents of the bamboo leaves acted as effective capping and stabilizing agents. The absorption peaks of the CuO/Bi2O3 composite are observed at 1072, 928, and 496 cm-1, which can be assigned to the carbonyl group of C-O, Bi-O-Cu, and metal-oxygen (Bi-O-Bi) vibrations, respectively. The remaining bands indicate the presence of various functional groups belonging to the phytocompounds. The extract is likely composed of phytochemicals that can reduce the Cu2+ and Bi3+ ions by donating electrons, and subsequently cap and stabilize the formed nanoparticles, similar mechanism behind the formation of nanoparticles mentioned elsewhere54,67.

FT-IR spectrum of plant mediated CuO (black line), Bi2O3 (blue), CuO/Bi2O3 (olive) and plant (bamboo) extract (red) samples.

The broad absorption peak observed around 3296 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of alcohol, carboxylic acid, or phenol functional groups. The medium absorption peak at 2924 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the C-H stretching vibrations. The absorption peak at 2848 cm⁻¹ is due to the CH2 absorption. Diffuse reflectance-UV-visible spectroscopy analysis

The co-friendly synthesized CuO, Bi2O3, and their composite were characterized using diffuse reflectance UV-vis spectroscopy to investigate their optical properties and band gap energy. The results showed that the CuO NPs exhibit absorption edge of 500 nm, while the Bi2O3 material displays relatively narrow absorption peak (417 nm). Interestingly, the CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite exhibited enhanced light absorption capacity toward visible light region with the absorption edge (468 nm) than bare Bi2O3 NPs, as can be seen in Fig. 4a. This suggests the nanocomposite material has intimate mixing of the component materials likely contributes to the observed improvements in optical properties due to the synergistic effect than individual oxide components. Therefore, the CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite resulted in a material with enhanced light harvesting capabilities that extend into the visible light range, offering potential advantages for various optoelectronic and photocatalytic applications69.

The optical band gap energy (Eg) of each material was estimated using standard techniques. This involved transforming the plot of F(R) versus wavelength shown in Fig. 5a into either (F(R)hν)(1/2) versus photon energy E (in eV) (Fig. 5b). The relationship used is F(R)hν = A(hν - Eg)(n/2), where F(R) is the Kubelka-Munk (K-M) function, h is Planck’s constant, ν is the light frequency, A is a constant, Eg represents the band gap, and n is determined by the type of optical transition (n = 1 for direct transition)70. The optical band gap Eg can be estimated graphically from these plots, as shown in Fig. 5b. CuO NPs have relatively narrow band gaps (2.1 eV), in good agreement with literature. Bi2O3 has a wider band gap (2.9 eV), similar to previously reported values71. In the CuO/Bi2O3 composite, the band gap was modified compared to the pristine Bi2O3, having the average band gap 2.4 eV of individual components. This allows the composite photocatalyst to exhibit extended light absorption into the visible region compared to pristine Bi2O3, likely due to a synergistic effect between the constituents.

Photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye

The photocatalytic activity of as-synthesized plant-extract-mediated CuO, Bi2O3, and CuO/Bi2O3 photocatalyst materials was tested in the degradation of RhB dye in aqueous solution under direct sunlight (solar) system. In Fig. 6, the photocatalytic efficiency of the as-synthesized samples was compared. RhB dye is a well-known pollutant that can contaminate the environment, particularly water72,73. Before the photocatalytic process the following preliminary optimization parameters were carried out using the selected CuO/Bi2O3 photocatalyst.

Photocatalytic operating parameters such as the effect of: (a) pH of solution, (b) catalyst load, (c) initial RhB dye concentration, and (d) the UV-vis absorption spectra of the RhB solution after photodegradation reaction. Inset: images of the RhB dye solution after 0 min (left) and 210 min (right) of the photocatalytic degradation.

The pH of the solution has a critical role in the photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye74. The effect of pH was investigated over a range of 2–12 (Fig. 6a), with an initial RhB dye concentration of 10 mg L-1 and a catalyst load of 25 mg. Before the experiment, the solution was kept in the dark for 1 h to reach adsorption/desorption equilibrium. Under dark conditions, the removal of RhB dye was almost negligible, while when exposed to sunlight irradiation, the photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye increases. The results show that as the pH is increased, the photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye decreases. The highest photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye, around 95.9%, was achieved at pH 4, which considered as an optimal pH. Further increasing the pH led to a decline in the photocatalytic efficiency (Fig. 6a). At low pH, the CuO/Bi2O3 photocatalyst shows higher activity due to the efficient separation of electrons and holes75. In contrast, at alkaline pH, the degradation rate is much lower. Based on these results, pH 4 was selected for further experiments to determine other important parameters.

The effect of CuO/Bi2O3 catalyst load on the degradation of RhB dye under sunlight irradiation is shown in Fig. 6b. As the catalyst load increased from 15 to 40 mg with 5 intervals, the number of absorbed photons increased, which led to a greater number of active sites available at the photocatalyst surface74,76. This in turn produced more hydroxyl radicals, resulting in more dye molecules being adsorbed on the catalyst surface. Consequently, the photodegradation of RhB dye increased over time, reaching 95.6% after 180 min with a 35 mg catalyst load. However, further increasing the catalyst load beyond 35 mg led to a decline in photocatalytic efficiency. This was due to reduced light penetration and increased solution turbidity, which caused greater light scattering from the catalyst surface and reduced the overall degradation efficiency77–79. Based on these results, a 35 mg catalyst load was determined to be the optimum for RhB dye degradation under the given conditions. As shown in Fig. 6b, the degradation efficiency of the CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite was 41.8%, 54.3%, 63.0%, 79.1%, 95.9% and 95.6% after 3 h of reaction time for loading amount of 15, 20, 25, 30, 35 and 40 mg, respectively. The lower catalyst loads resulted in a decline in photocatalytic efficiency.

The effect of the initial concentration of RhB dye on its photocatalytic degradation was investigated by varying the concentrations of RhB dye of 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 mg L-1 while the catalyst load, pH, and irradiation time were kept constant (Fig. 6c). The results showed that as the initial concentration of RhB dye increased, the rate of photocatalytic degradation decreased; that is, 98.6%, 95.6%, 84.2%, 77.9%, 69.2%, and 59.9%, respectively after 180 min of sunlight irradiation (Fig. 6c). However, the degradation efficiency declined at higher RhB dye concentrations, attributed to reduced light penetration into the solution at higher dye concentrations, limiting the photocatalytic activity and resulting in less degradation of the RhB dye80,81. In addition, the effect of contact time on the photocatalytic degradation of RhB dye was simultaneously determined by optimizing the rest parameters. As the irradiation time increased from 30 to 180 min, the photocatalytic degradation of the RhB dye (10 mg L[-1) increased up to 95.6%. However, the degradation steadily declined after extended the irradiation time beyond 180 min. The inset graph in Fig. 6d shows the spectra of the RhB dye during the photocatalytic degradation process at the different irradiation time points.

The photocatalytic activity of plant-extract-mediated as-synthesized CuO, Bi2O3, and CuO/Bi2O3 was compared, as shown in Fig. 7. The photocatalytic efficiency of as-synthesized materials 40.2%, 80.5%, and 95.6%, for CuO, Bi2O3, and CuO/Bi2O3, respectively, under direct sunlight (solar) irradiation for 180 min. The results indicate that the CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite exhibited significantly enhanced photodegradation performance compared to the pure CuO and Bi2O3 counterparts. This suggests that the CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite holds promise as an effective photocatalyst for the remediation of RhB dye.

Proposed photocatalytic degradation mechanisms

The photodegradation process involves different critical active species, comprising .OH (hydroxyl radicals), .O2− (superoxide radicals), and photogenerated e− (electrons) and h+ (holes). To evaluate the impact of these active species in the photocatalytic reaction, different quenching experiments were carried out with CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite as a photocatalyst (Fig. 9). Specific scavengers were employed to trap the different active species. Sodium sulphate (Na2SO4), sodium hydrogen carbonate (NaHCO3) and methanol/water (CH3OH/H2O) was used to capture .O2−, photogenerated h+ and .OH, respectively58,59. The photodegradation of RhB dye was scarcely affected when sodium sulfate was introduced (conversion of 94.6%), suggesting that .O2− radical have no significant influence. Meanwhile, the addition of NaHCO3 and CH3OH/H2O scavengers significantly impacted the photocatalytic activity (72.3 and 74.2%, respectively). This indicates that the photogenerated holes and the hydroxyl radicals are the most important active intermediates in RhB dye degradation than supper oxide radicals.

Impact of scavengers in the photocatalytic activity of RhB dye by CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite under sunlight irradiation. The color of the columns is a blend of the olive shade assigned to the nanocomposite and the color assigned to any scavenger. For comparison purposes, the photocatalytic reaction performed without trapping agents is also given (olive).

To understand the photogenerated charge separation, it is essential to estimate the flat band potentials and band gap energies of the photocatalysts involved. The photocatalytic process was initiated by absorption of photon energy equal to or greater than the band gap of composite (һν ≥ 2.4 eV). The photogenerated electrons and holes come from the visible active CuO and Bi2O3 materials47,80. To explain the photocatalytic activity, the valance band (VB) and conduction band (CB) edge position of the as-obtained photocatalysts were estimated by EVB = X-Eo + 0.5Eg and ECB = EVB-Eg equations, where Eg is band gap energy, EVB is VB energy and ECB is CB energy, X is the absolute electronegativity of semiconductor:84 the X value of CuO and Bi2O3 is 5.81 and 5.986 eV, respectively85 and the Eo is the energy of free electrons on the hydrogen scale; that is 4.5 eV64,65. The band gap values of CuO and Bi2O3 are 2.1 and 2.9 eV, respectively. For CuO, the values of CB and VB is + 0.26 eV and + 2.36 eV (vs. NHE) but the electrons in CB of CuO NPs cannot reduce dissolved oxygen to superoxide radicals due to the CB potential is more positive than that of the potential of O2/O2.−(-0.33 eV vs. NHE)86. For Bi2O3, CB and VB energy values are + 0.036 eV and + 2.936 eV (vs. NHE) and the photogenerated holes in VB of Bi2O3 can react with adsorbed water molecules to form hydroxyl radical due to more positive potential than the standard redox potential (.OH/H2O = + 2.8 eV vs. NHE)84,87. For RhB dye, CB and VB energy values are − 1.42 eV and + 0.96 eV (vs. NHE), respectively38.

Based on this information provided, the plausible mechanisms of the electrons and holes transfer were proposed in Fig. 10. The CuO photocatalyst has a narrow band gap, making it visible light active and the excited electrons are promoted from VB to CB. Since the ECB edge of Bi2O3 (+ 0.036 eV vs. NHE) is less positive than the ECB of CuO (+ 0.26 eV vs. NHE) and the RhB (-1.42 eV vs. NHE). This allows the exited electrons in the CB of RhB to transfer to the CB of the Bi2O3 and the electron directly moved to the CB of CuO. This separation of photoinduced charge carriers delays their recombination. Remarkably, Bi2O3 has a higher valance band edge potential (+ 2.936 eV vs. NHE) than CuO (+ 2.36 eV vs. NHE). The VB potential of Bi2O3 is aligned with the redox potentials .OH/H2O (+ 2.8 eV vs. NHE) and .OH/OH− (+ 2.38 eV vs. NHE). This allows the photogenerated holes in the VB of Bi2O3 to easily oxidize water and OH− to hydroxyl radicals (.OH), indicating that the photoinduced holes on the VB of Bi2O3 facilitate the photocatalytic reaction. Meanwhile, CuO possesses lower valance edge potential (+ 2.36 eV vs. NHE) and makes it less capable of oxidizes H2O and OH− to form hydroxyl radicals. Therefore, the photogenerated holes and hydroxyl radicals of Bi2O3 are crucial reactive intermediates for oxidizing RhB dye directly under sunlight irradiation, which is in good agreement with the results of quenching experiments and in accordance with previously reported study88.

Conclusions

In this study, CuO, Bi2O3 and CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite were synthesized from aqueous leave extract bamboo using copper oxide dihydrated and bismuth nitrate pentahydrate as precursors through a simple, as a green and eco-friendly approach to remove RhB dye from aqueous contaminated water under sunlight/ direct solar light irradiation. The synthesized CuO, Bi2O3 and CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposites were characterized by XRD, SEM-EDX, FT-IR and DRS-UV-vis techniques. The crystallinity and the presence of CuO and Bi2O3 in the as-synthesized nanocomposite confirmed from the XRD analysis revealed that the characteristics diffraction peaks. Average crystallite size of CuO, Bi2O3 and CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite samples synthesized from plant extract was calculated to be 19.5, 45.6 and 40.9 nm, respectively. As the crystallite size of the nanoparticles and nanocomposite decreases the available surface area increases. The CuO/Bi2O3 nanocomposite exhibited excellent photocatalytic performance for the degradation of RhB dye under sunlight irradiation, achieving 95.6% degradation under sunlight irradiation under optimized conditions. This confirm that an efficient semiconductor photocatalyst for the effective removal of RhB dye from aqueous solutions, which makes it a promising material for water treatment applications.
