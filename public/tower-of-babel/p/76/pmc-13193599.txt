Cytotoxic Lesions of the Corpus Callosum Patterns and Etiologies: Insights from an Indian Cohort
Authors: Sagar Badachi, Sonia Shivde, Saikanth Deepalam, Raghunandan Nadig, Mohammed Shakeebuddin Kashif, GRK Sarma, Vishal Chandra Sharma, Shagun Bhardwaj, GG Sharath Kumar, Thomas Mathew

ABSTRACT

Introduction

The corpus callosum (CC) is the largest commissural pathway in the brain. The splenium of corpus callosum (SCC) represents the thickest and most posterior portion of the CC, comprising numerous axonal fibers that predominantly connect the bilateral temporal, posterior parietal, and occipital cortices. A major portion of the CC receives its arterial supply from the carotid system, except for the splenium, which is supplied by the vertebrobasilar system.[1] The precise function of the SCC remains poorly understood.[2] Transient signal alterations in the SCC on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been observed in various neurological and non-neurological conditions.[3] Transient splenial hyperintensity (TSH) and reversible splenial lesion syndrome (RESLES) were the terms used to describe these transient signal alterations in the SCC.[4] Presently, the term cytotoxic lesions of the corpus callosum (CLOCCs) has been proposed as a more precise description of this phenomenon.[5] Numerous case reports have described children with CLOCCs occurring during febrile illnesses, a condition termed mild encephalitis/encephalopathy with a reversible splenial lesion (MERS).[6] There is a dearth of data concerning CLOCCs in adult patients. In this context, the present study intends to highlight the different distribution patterns in patients with CLOCCs and correlate the probable etiologies with clinical and radiological presentations.

Methods

This observational retrospective study was conducted at a tertiary care center in South India from January 2014 to December 2022. Participants of all ages and both sexes were included in the study. Demographic data, clinical features, and laboratory investigations were captured from medical records. MRI of the brain was performed using a 1.5 Tesla MRI machine (GE Signa Explorer). The MRI sequences performed included diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), T1-weighted, T1-weighted contrast-enhanced, and gradient-echo images. MRI images of splenial lesions were analyzed with respect to size, shape, involvement of the genu, and involvement of extracallosal sites. CLOCCs were diagnosed if the following neuroimaging criteria were met:[7]

Based on the shape and distribution of lesions, CLOCCs were further classified into dot sign, boomerang sign, double boomerang sign, and boomerang plus sign. The “dot sign” is defined as a well-circumscribed, small, oval lesion occupying the central part of the splenium. The “boomerang sign” refers to a splenial lesion that occupies the complete splenium and resembles a boomerang. The “double boomerang sign” is defined as lesions occupying both the splenium and genu of the CC, resembling a double boomerang. The “boomerang plus sign” describes lesions involving the splenium as well as extracallosal sites. A pictorial representation of these patterns is given in Figure 1.

The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee on March 18, 2025 (Ref. no. 68/2025). Approval was also received for a waiver of consent.

Statistical analyses

The data collected were entered into a Microsoft Excel sheet. Descriptive statistics, including median, interquartile range (IQR), and percentages, were used to summarize the data.

Results

During the study period of 8 years, 10,000 MRI brain scans were reviewed. A total of 127 patients with splenial involvement (1.27%) on MRI were analyzed. Cases of splenial involvement due to stroke and tumors were excluded from the study. Ultimately, 35 out of 127 patients (27.5%) who had CLOCCs were enrolled in the study. In CLOCC patients, brain MRIs were performed between 2 and 7 days from the onset of symptoms and on day 1 of presentation to our hospital.

Demographic data and patterns of CLOCCs

A total of 35 patients, comprising 29 (82.9%) adults and 6 (17.1%) children, were included. Among adults, 16 (55.2%) were males and 13 (44.8%) females, with an age range of 18–74 years (median 30 years, IQR 13.5). The pediatric cohort included 4 (66.7%) males and 2 (33.3%) females, with an age range of 7 days to 9 years. Overall, the most common CLOCC pattern was the dot sign (54.3%), followed by the boomerang plus sign (28.6%), whereas the boomerang sign and double boomerang sign each accounted for 8.6%. In adults, the dot sign predominated (65.5%), whereas in children, the boomerang plus sign (66.7%) and double boomerang sign (33.3%) were noted exclusively [Table 1].

Dot sign

All 19 patients with the dot sign were adults. Fourteen patients (73.68%) presented with seizures, four patients (21.05%) presented with encephalopathy, and one patient (5.26%) presented with acute headache. Among the seizure group, all 14 patients had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. The probable etiologies included non-lesional generalized epilepsy in 13 (92.85%) patients and acute symptomatic seizures due to head injury in 1 (7.14%) patient. Among the encephalopathy group, the probable etiologies included infection with Mycobacterium tuberculosis in 2 (50%) patients, dengue viral infection in 1 (25%) patient, and enteric fever in 1 (25%) patient. The patient who presented with an acute headache had an infection with COVID-19. The dot sign in MRI is depicted in Figure 2.

Boomerang sign

All three patients with the boomerang sign were adults. 2 (66.67%) patients presented with seizures, and 1 (33.33%) patient presented with encephalopathy. Among the seizure group, both patients had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. The probable etiologies included withdrawal of the phenytoin drug for a day in a generalized non-lesional epilepsy patient and acute symptomatic seizures due to hypoglycemia in another patient. The patient with encephalopathy had an infection with the dengue virus. The boomerang sign in MRI is depicted in Figure 3.

Double boomerang sign

The double boomerang sign was observed in three patients, which included 2 (66.67%) pediatric patients and 1 (33.3%) adult patient. Both pediatric patients presented with seizures. The type of seizures was generalized as tonic-clonic seizures. The probable etiology for seizures was hypoxic injury. The adult patient presented with encephalopathy, with infection by scrub typhus as the probable etiology. The double boomerang sign on MRI is depicted in Figure 3.

Boomerang plus sign

The boomerang plus sign was observed in 10 patients, comprising 6 (60%) adult patients and 4 (40%) pediatric patients. Among the adult patients, 4 presented with encephalopathy and 2 presented with seizures. The probable etiology for encephalopathy was hypernatremia. Among the seizure group, both patients had generalized tonic-clonic seizures. The probable etiology was an acute symptomatic seizure due to hypoglycemia. In patients with hypernatremic encephalopathy, extracallosal lesions involved the posterior limb of the internal capsule and hippocampus in all cases, with symmetrical involvement of the cerebral peduncles observed in 2 patients. Cerebellar white matter and corona radiata involvement was noted in 1 patient. In patients with hypoglycemia and acute symptomatic seizures, extracallosal lesions were located in the corona radiata and internal capsule in both patients, with additional involvement of the bilateral cerebral peduncles and globus pallidi in one patient.

Among the 4 pediatric patients, 3 patients presented with seizures. The type of seizures in these patients was generalized tonic-clonic seizures. The probable etiology was hypoxic injury, and 1 patient presented with encephalopathy. The probable etiology of encephalopathy was infection with epidemic typhus. The locations of extracallosal sites in patients with hypoxic seizures included the centrum semiovale, corona radiata, and internal capsule in all patients. The encephalopathy patient with typhus fever had extracallosal lesions in the globus pallidus interna and medial temporal lobes. The boomerang plus sign in MRI is depicted in Figure 4. In the present study, patients who presented within 2–7 days were more likely to exhibit the dot sign, boomerang sign, and double boomerang sign, whereas the boomerang plus sign was more likely to be observed in those who presented after 7 days. Patients with the dot sign and boomerang sign had a short duration of drowsiness/encephalopathy lasting a few hours to one day, whereas those with the double boomerang sign had an encephalopathy lasting 2-4 days. Patients with a boomerang plus sign had encephalopathy lasting more than 4 days.

Out of 35 patients, repeat MRI scans were done only in 7 patients due to financial constraints. Out of these 7 patients, repeat scans were conducted for 6 within 2–10 weeks. CLOCCs were completely resolved in all 6 patients. In 2 out of 6 patients, the time to resolution was 2 weeks, and in 4 out of 6 patients, the time to resolution was 6–10 weeks. In the 7th patient, who had the boomerang plus sign, a repeat scan two weeks later revealed partial resolution of the lesions in the splenium and complete resolution of lesions in the internal capsule. A few extracallosal lesions persisted. We did not repeat any other scans in this patient.

Discussion

In our study cohort, CLOCCs were observed in 35 MRI scans out of 10,000 performed for various neurological conditions. All patients exhibiting the dot sign and boomerang sign were adults. The double boomerang sign was identified in 2 pediatric patients and 1 adult patient. The boomerang plus sign was observed in 6 adult patients and 4 pediatric patients. The most common pattern observed in adults was the dot sign, while in children it was the boomerang plus sign. Non-lesional generalized seizures were the most frequent probable etiology in adults, whereas acute symptomatic seizures due to hypoxia were the most common probable etiology in children. This study had a few cases of drug or drug withdrawal seizures, as an MRI of the brain was not routinely performed in obvious cases of drug or drug withdrawal. Furthermore, there was less representation of probable infection-associated CLOCCs in pediatric patients, primarily due to small sample size and referral bias.

CLOCCs is a clinicoradiological syndrome resulting from a wide variety of causes. [Table 3][3568] The clinical presentation of CLOCCs is nonspecific and primarily relates to the event that caused this condition. Drugs or drug withdrawal and seizures are the main probable causes of CLOCCs in adults, while infections are the most common probable causes in children. The most frequent presentation is seizures, followed by encephalopathy or encephalitis. Lateralizing signs are uncommon. The precise incidence of this entity is difficult to ascertain, but it is likely rare, underrecognized, and underreported.[3]

Etiologies of CLOCCs

The most common etiology of CLOCCs in the literature is related to seizures or antiepileptic drug withdrawal, followed by infection and metabolic disorders. In the majority of these patients, neuroimaging displayed a “dot sign.”[8] Our findings are consistent with these observations. Epilepsy was the predominant probable etiology in our study cohort, and the dot sign was the most common pattern observed. In patients with epilepsy, CLOCCs have been estimated to occur in 0.7% of patients with drug-resistant epilepsy who have undergone presurgical evaluation.[9] Even in patients without a history of seizures, CLOCCs can occur due to toxicity or abrupt withdrawal of antiseizure medications. The most common antiseizure medications associated with CLOCCs are carbamazepine, phenytoin, and lamotrigine.[8] Newer antiseizure medications associated with CLOCCs include pregabalin and vigabatrin.[1011] In our pediatric cohort with CLOCCs, the majority had seizures due to hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. It has been noted that restricted diffusion of the splenium is specific for recent seizures in neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy.[12]

In our case series, the most common probable metabolic etiologies of CLOCCs were hypernatremia, followed by hypoglycemia. In cases of hypernatremia, the “boomerang plus” pattern was observed in all patients. In patients with hypernatremia, extracallosal lesions included involvement of the posterior limb of the internal capsule and the hippocampus in all patients. Reported neuroimaging data in cases of hypernatremia include cerebral shrinkage and involvement of the external capsule, cerebellum, hippocampus, lateral thalamic nuclei, crus cerebri, pons, and subcortical white matter.[131415] Symmetrical hyperintensity of the white matter of the corticospinal tracts, extending from the internal capsule to the pons and referred to as the “wine-glass” appearance, is also described in hypernatremic myelinolysis.[16] These lesions usually resolve within 2–8 weeks.[1517] In hypoglycemia, the most common pattern of CLOCCs observed was the “boomerang plus,” followed by the “boomerang sign”. The extracallosal lesions involved the corona radiata, internal capsule, cerebral peduncles, and globus pallidus. Studies describing neuroimaging findings in hypoglycemia are limited; however, data show that typical lesions involve the cerebral cortex, basal ganglia, hippocampus, cerebral white matter, and internal capsule.[18] Although the most common pattern in hypoglycemia is cortical involvement, we did not have any hypoglycemia patients with cortical involvement. This may be due to the exclusion of patients with hypoglycemic injury without splenial hyperintensity (selection bias and small sample size). Splenial involvement was seen in only 5.7% of patients with hypoglycemia.[19] Cortical sparing is also known to occur in patients with hypoglycemic brain injury.[20]

The third probable cause of CLOCCs in this study was infections. A wide variety of infections can cause CLOCCs.[821222324] In our series, infectious causes included dengue, tuberculosis, scrub typhus, enteric fever, and SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19), with the dot sign being the most common MRI finding in infection-associated CLOCCs.

The pathophysiology of CLOCCs remains incompletely understood. Glutamate-mediated excitotoxicity leading to cytotoxic edema is considered central.[8] Infections, trauma, and inflammation activate macrophages, monocytes, and endothelial cells, triggering the release of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-〈), which activate T lymphocytes, astrocytes, and microglia, resulting in endothelial dysfunction and blood-brain barrier disruption.[56] IL-1 further stimulates astrocytic glutamate release and inhibits its reuptake, resulting in excess glutamate, which activates N-Methyl-D-Aspartate (NMDA) and α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors, sodium-potassium pumps, and aquaporins, causing intracellular water influx into neurons and astrocytes, leading to cytotoxic edema. The CC in general, and the SCC in particular, is vulnerable to developing cytotoxic edema due to its high density of cytokine, glutamate, toxin, and drug receptors,[5] and hence may be considered the “black box of the brain,” reflecting its susceptibility to diverse insults.

The strengths of this study include the description of various patterns of CLOCCs and diverse probable etiologies in both adults and pediatric patients, along with a detailed elaboration of etiopathogenesis. However, the main weaknesses of this study are its descriptive nature, reliance on retrospective data, and small sample size.

Conclusions

CLOCCs is a clinicoradiological syndrome resulting from a wide variety of causes, the most common being seizures, followed by infectious and metabolic etiologies. Cytokinopathy leading to glutamate excitotoxicity is the most accepted pathophysiology. MRI brain patterns can range from splenial involvement alone to involvement of extra-callosal sites, and these patterns may help identify probable etiologies.
