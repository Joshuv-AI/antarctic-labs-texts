Efficacy of repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation in managing spasticity of the triceps surae muscle in children with cerebral palsy: a retrospective analysis of influencing factors
Authors: Xin Zhao, Yujuan Wang, Li Gao, Yao Shi, Jing Yang, Guojun Yun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral palsy (CP) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by spasticity, which significantly impairs motor function. Traditional treatments offer limited relief and carry risks. Repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS) presents a promising non-invasive alternative. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of rPMS in managing triceps surae spasticity in children with CP and to identify factors influencing its effectiveness.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted with 271 children aged 6–12 years with CP (GMFCS levels I-II). Participants were assigned to conventional treatment (n = 131) or rPMS (n = 140) groups. Outcomes were assessed using the Modified Tardieu Scale (MTS), Berg balance scale (BSS), Edinburgh Visual Gait Score (EVGS) and integrated electromyography (IEMG). Treatment efficacy was defined based on improvements in the Gross Motor Function Measure-88 (GMFM-88). Logistic regression identified factors influencing rPMS efficacy.ResultsThe rPMS group showed significantly greater improvements in spasticity (reduced MTS and EVGS scores), balance (increased BBS scores), and muscle coordination (IEMG changes) compared to the conventional group (P < 0.05). The overall effectiveness rate was 85.00% for rPMS vs. 74.81% for conventional treatment (P = 0.036). Multivariate analysis identified the type of CP (diplegia vs. hemiplegia) and patient compliance as significant factors influencing the efficacy of rPMS.ConclusionOur results indicate that rPMS significantly controls spasticity and improves lower limb motor function. Importantly, we identified that certain types of CP and patient compliance are significant predictors of treatment efficacy. These findings suggest that optimizing rPMS protocols based on patient-specific characteristics can enhance therapeutic outcomes.

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a significant neurodevelopmental disorder affecting approximately 1 in 500 live births globally, characterized by a group of permanent movement and posture development disorders attributed to non-progressive disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain [1]. This multifaceted condition often manifests as spasticity, a sensory-motor disorder marked by an increase in tonic stretch reflexes, which can severely inhibit voluntary movement and functional independence in affected individuals [2]. Among various muscle groups, the triceps surae muscle, comprising the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles, was frequently affected by spasticity, leading to equinus and impeding locomotion—a common challenge among children with CP [3].

Conventional therapeutic modalities for managing spasticity in CP primarily include physical therapy, pharmacologic agents, and surgical interventions, each with its limitations [4]. Physical therapy, while essential, often yields limited relief, demanding continuous intensive application [5]. Medications like baclofen or botulinum toxin provide only temporary symptom relief and carry the risk of adverse effects [6]. Surgical interventions, such as selective dorsal rhizotomy, though effective in controlling spasticity, involve inherent surgical risks and long recovery periods [7]. Thus, there was an ongoing quest for novel, effective, and less invasive therapeutic options.

In recent years, repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS) has emerged as a promising non-invasive technique in the realm of neurorehabilitation for various neurological conditions, including CP [8]. By delivering magnetic fields to induce electrical currents in peripheral nerves, rPMS may modulate neuronal excitability and promoting neuroplasticity [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated rPMS’s efficacy in enhancing muscle strength and controlling spasticity in adult populations, yet its application in pediatric patients with CP remains under-explored [10].

Several theoretical advantages are offered by rPMS. By targeting peripheral nerves, rPMS may modulate afferent input to the central nervous system, potentially altering central motor circuits and reducing pathological reflex activity contributing to spasticity [11]. Additionally, its ability to facilitate functional reorganization within the central motor pathways might support enhanced motor control and coordination, critical in managing CP-related motor dysfunctions [12]. Furthermore, the non-invasive nature and minimal side effects make rPMS an attractive option for pediatric populations, providing a potential adjunct to comprehensive CP management strategies [13].

Given the potential benefits and relative safety of rPMS, this study aims to assess its efficacy in managing spasticity specifically in the triceps surae muscle of pediatric CP patients and to explore factors influencing treatment outcomes. By doing so, we hope to provide insights into how best to optimize rPMS protocols for children with CP, ultimately enhancing their motor function and quality of life.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This research received approval from our institution’s Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee [Insert Specific Approval Code]. For this retrospective study, informed consent was waived since only de-identified patient data were utilized, eliminating any potential risk or effect on patient care. For children involved in this study, all data were obtained from existing medical records, which had been collected with informed consent from their legal guardians at the time of initial treatment. Therefore, additional assent forms were not required for this retrospective study. This exemption was granted by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee in adherence to established regulatory and ethical guidelines governing retrospective research.

Study design

This study employs a retrospective cohort design involving children with CP who were admitted to our hospital between September 2022 and March 2025. Participants were assigned to either a conventional treatment group (n = 131) or a rPMS group (n = 140) based on the treatments received. The study aimed to compare the effectiveness of these two therapeutic approaches. Furthermore, based on the response to rPMS, patients were further classified into an effective group (n = 119) and an ineffective group (n = 21) to examine factors influencing treatment outcomes. Additionally, data on the primary caregivers of the children with CP were collected.

Eligibility and grouping criteria

Inclusion Criteria: Participants had to meet the diagnostic criteria for CP [14], be aged between 6 and 12 years, and be classified as level I or II according to the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS). Additionally, they needed to exhibit significant spasticity in the triceps surae muscle on the affected side, indicated by a Modified Tardieu Scale (MTS) score of ≥ 1. Eligible participants also had to possess good cognitive function, enabling them to understand simple instructions, and have complete medical records.

Exclusion Criteria: Excluded were individuals with functional disorders of key organs, including the heart, liver, lungs, or kidneys. Also excluded were those who had taken medications affecting neuromuscular control in the past three months, had comorbidities like bleeding disorders or neuromuscular junction diseases, or had a history of trauma or infections impacting movement in the past six months. Additionally, patients who were unable to cooperate with treatment or complete the prescribed treatment regimen were excluded.

Treatment approach

Both groups of children underwent routine rehabilitation therapy, which consisted of individualized exercise sessions conducted by therapists. These sessions primarily employed neurodevelopmental techniques such as Bobath and were supplemented with joint range of motion exercises, muscle strength training, and gait training. Each therapy session lasted 30 min and included physical therapy and massage, conducted once daily, five days a week, for eight weeks [15].

For the rPMS group, rPMS was applied using a Magpro R100 stimulator (MagVenture, Skovlunde, Denmark). A butterfly coil type D-B80 (MagVenture, Skovlunde, Denmark) was used to achieve the most focal activation of the nerve. Participants are stimulated in an upright position to allow optimal accessibility of the coil to the nerve with the knee joint extended. The coil location was found by the systematic movement of the coil over the region of the tibial nerve in the popliteal fossa. The optimal position was found in that the lowest stimulation intensity at this position could still cause a palpable contraction of the calf muscles compared to neighboring stimulation sites. The handle of the coil points vertically downward, with the current flow from the distal to the proximal direction over the nerve. The magnetic coil was placed in the upper area of the posterior crurius region, in the immediate vicinity of the tibial nerve. During the stimulation of the left tibialis nerve, contractions of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles produced plantar flexion and relaxation of the calf muscle. The stimulator frequency was 5 Hz, with pulse stimulation lasting for 3 s, followed by a 5-second interval. One treatment session lasted for 20 min. This procedure was performed once daily, five days a week, over an eight-week period. To ensure precision and consistency, the positions of nerve stimulation points were marked on the skin for accurate placement of the magnetic coil throughout the treatment period. The experimenters who performed the stimulations were instructed in detail and experienced in the administration of the stimulation [16].

General information

Patient general information, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), CP type, and GMFCS level, was obtained from the medical records system. The GMFCS categorizes the gross motor function of children with CP into five levels, ranging from Level I, indicating the highest function, to Level V, indicating the lowest. Higher levels correspond to reduced gross motor function capabilities. The GMFCS demonstrates excellent reliability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.945 (95% CI: 0.861 to 0.979) and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.972 [17]. The 13-item Modified Caregiver Strain Index (MCSI) [18] was self-assessed by the caregiver, where caregiver responses were rated on a 0 to 2 scale and summed to produce a total score out of 26. Higher scores indicated greater levels of strain associated with caregiving. This method ensures a quantitative assessment of caregiver burden.

Berg balance scale (BSS)

The Berg Balance Scale (BSS) is used to assess patients’ balance ability and consists of 14 items. After the patient performs the specified actions, a professional tester evaluates the patient’s performance based on their specific completion of each task. Each item is scored on a 5-point scale from 0 to 4, with 0 indicating the lowest level of function and 4 the highest. The maximum total score is 56 points. A higher total BBS score indicates better overall balance ability. A score below 40 suggests an increased risk of falling. The Cronbach’s alpha for this scale is 0.943 [19]. Outcome measure was assessed at baseline and immediately following the completion of the 8-week intervention period.

Modified tardieu scale (MTS)

The MTS was used to assess the degree of spasticity in the triceps surae muscle through two parameters. First, the Muscle Response was graded on a scale from 0 to 5, where 0 indicates no spasticity and 5 denotes severe spasticity. Second, the Angle of Muscle Response was measured: the Fast Angle (R1) was recorded as the angle at which a catch occurs during rapid stretching, and the Slow Angle (R2) was the angle where the joint achieves its full range of motion during slow movement. The MTS demonstrates moderate reliability, with an ICC of 0.74 [20]. Outcome measure was assessed at baseline and immediately following the completion of the 8-week intervention period.

Electromyography (EMG)

EMG were recorded from the midbellies of the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles (TA; negative control) using preamplifier surface electrodes (DE2.1, Delsys; 10-mm interelectrode distance). The term ‘midbelly’ refers to the central portion of the muscle, typically located where the muscle fibers are thickest and most active. This location is chosen because it provides the most stable and reliable EMG signals. The earth electrodes were placed over the thighbones of both legs. The skin was cleaned with ethanol to reduce the skin– electrode impedance. The analog EMG signals were acquired continuously throughout the calf-raise exercise, filtered using a band pass-filter set to between 15 and 500 Hz, and digitized at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz using Mac Lab/16 (AD Instruments). The digital signals were then full-wave rectified and integrated over 40 s to yield integrated EMG (IEMG), which were then averaged over the five sets of exercises for each subject. The IEMG was used as a measure of the overall magnitude of muscle activation during the task, providing an index of muscle coordination rather than a direct measure of tone [21]. Outcome measure was assessed at baseline and immediately following the completion of the 8-week intervention period.

Edinburgh visual gait score (EVGS)

EVGS is used to evaluate 2D visual gait-posture in children with CP. The EVGS has 17 test items. The EVGS employs a 3-level scoring system based on the degree of deviation from normal range of motion: 0 = normal, 1 = moderately deviated from normal, 2 = significantly deviated from normal. The maximum total score for the 17 items is 34 points. A score greater than 0 indicates the presence of gait abnormalities, with higher scores reflecting more severe deviations. During the test, the subjects walk at an optimal speed on an 8-meter-long trail, while two smartphones with high-speed camera functions are placed at one end of the trail and in the middle to collect the walking data of the children with CP. The walking data is then processed and analyzed using Kinovea motion video analysis software [22]. Outcome measure was assessed at baseline and immediately following the completion of the 8-week intervention period.

Patients treatment compliance

A hospital-developed compliance scale was utilized to assess the children’s adherence to the treatment regimen. This scale has a maximum score of 100 points, with higher scores reflecting better compliance. Outcome measure was assessed immediately following the completion of the 8-week intervention period.

Treatment efficacy evaluation

The Gross Motor Function Measure (GMFM-88) was employed to assess the motor functions of children in both groups across a variety of activities. Items on the GMFM-88 are grouped into five dimensions: A: lying and rolling (17 items); B: sitting (20 items); C: crawling and kneeling (14 items); D: standing (13 items); and E: walking, running and jumping (24 items). Each item on the GMFM-88 was scored on a 4-point scale: 0 points indicate incapacity to perform the task; 1 point for completing less than 10% of the task; 2 points for partial completion; and 3 points for full completion. The GMFM-88 demonstrated high reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.989 [23]. Outcome measure was assessed at baseline and immediately following the completion of the 8-week intervention period. Outcomes were categorized based on established clinical interpretation of GMFM-88 score changes and functional improvements: Significant improvement indicated marked enhancement in clinical symptoms post-treatment, defined as an increase of more than 15% in the total GMFM-88 score or multiple items in sections D or E improving from a score of 0 or 1 to 3; Improvement referred to relative enhancement in symptoms, characterized by a 5–15% increase in the total GMFM-88 score (a threshold set based on clinical consensus at our institution) or multiple items in sections D or E improving from a score of 0 or 1 to 2; No effect was defined as no observable symptom improvement, indicated by a less than 5% increase or even a decrease in the total GMFM-88 score, or minimal to no improvement in the scores of items in sections D or E. The overall effective rate was calculated using the formula: (Significant improvement + Improvement)/Total number of cases × 100% [24].

Statistical analysis

Using G*Power 3.1.9.7, the “Means: Difference between two independent means (two groups)” option under t tests was selected for a Post hoc analysis. The settings were as follows: Two tails mode, Effect size d = 0.5, α err prob = 0.05. The sample sizes for the two groups were then entered, and the Power (1-β err prob) was calculated, resulting in a Power of 0.984.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.0 statistical software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data were represented as [n (%)], and the chi-square test (χ2) was used to assess them. Continuous variables were initially tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For data that followed a normal distribution, results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to examine the relationships between variables. Indicators that displayed significant differences in both differential analysis and correlation analysis were included as covariates in the logistic regression analysis.

Results

Demographic and basic data

In this study, a total of 271 children with CP were enrolled, with 131 in the conventional treatment group and 140 in the rPMS group (Table 1). The mean age was 8.48 ± 1.35 years in the conventional group and 8.66 ± 1.24 years in the rPMS group, with no statistically significant difference (t = 1.137, P = 0.256). The gender distribution was balanced, with males representing 60.31% in the conventional group and 52.86% in the rPMS group (χ²=1.527, P = 0.217). The BMI was similar between groups, 17.43 ± 1.45 in the conventional group and 17.15 ± 1.36 in the rPMS group (t = 1.655, P = 0.099). The distribution of CP types also did not differ significantly, with diplegia present in 19.85% and 29.29% of the conventional and rPMS groups, respectively, and hemiplegia in 80.15% and 70.71%, respectively (χ²=3.239, P = 0.072). Furthermore, the GMFCS levels showed no significant differences, with Grade I observed in 70.23% of the conventional group and 80.00% of the rPMS group, and Grade II in 29.77% and 20.00%, respectively (χ²=3.472, P = 0.062). Overall, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between the groups, allowing for a comparable analysis of the treatment outcomes.

In the study cohort, primary caregivers of the children with CP were assessed across several demographic parameters between the conventional treatment group (n = 131) and the rPMS group (n = 140) (Table 2). The mean age of caregivers was 39.65 ± 3.14 years in the conventional group and 40.28 ± 2.59 years in the rPMS group, with no statistically significant difference observed (t = 1.773, P = 0.077). The majority of caregivers were female, comprising 84.73% in the conventional group and 90.00% in the rPMS group, a difference that was not statistically significant (χ²=1.711, P = 0.191). The education level of caregivers was also similar between groups, with primary, secondary, and university education levels distributed as 25.95%, 48.09%, and 25.95% in the conventional group, and 17.14%, 50.71%, and 32.14% in the rPMS group, respectively (χ²=3.438, P = 0.179). Employment status showed that 36.64% of caregivers were employed in the conventional group compared to 47.14% in the rPMS group, while the remainder were unemployed, again without significant difference (χ²=3.063, P = 0.080). These sociodemographic characteristics indicate comparable baseline conditions between the groups’ primary caregivers. At baseline, there were no significant differences in the levels of caregiver strain between the conventional treatment group and the rPMS group (t = 0.626, P = 0.532). However, after the intervention, the rPMS group demonstrated a significantly greater reduction in caregiver strain compared to the conventional treatment group (t = 2.649, P = 0.009). This suggests that rPMS may be an effective adjunctive therapy for reducing the strain experienced by caregivers.

Lower limb motor function

At baseline, there were no significant differences between groups in the MTS scores, BBS, EVGS, or IEMG of the soleus and gastrocnemius muscles (P > 0.05) (Figs. 1 and 2). Post-treatment, the rPMS group showed a significantly greater reduction in MTS scores, from 2.63 ± 0.51 to 1.52 ± 0.21, compared to the reduction in the conventional group, from 2.57 ± 0.56 to 1.78 ± 0.23 (t = 10.019, P < 0.001). BBS improved in the rPMS group, increasing from 35.71 ± 4.83 to 42.19 ± 4.38, significantly more than in the conventional group (t = 3.030, P = 0.003). The EVGS in the rPMS group was significantly lower after treatment compared to the conventional group, with post-treatment scores of 4.43 ± 1.28 and 5.07 ± 1.84, respectively (t = 3.298, P = 0.001). Analysis of muscle activation patterns via IEMG revealed significant neuromodulatory effects of rPMS. We observed a significant increase in integrated activity of the soleus muscle in the rPMS group, from 37.48 ± 8.61 to 50.18 ± 7.28, outperforming the conventional group (t = 3.157, P = 0.002), alongside a greater reduction in integrated activity of the gastrocnemius muscle (from 36.09 ± 7.82 to 27.83 ± 6.24) compared to the conventional group (t = 4.587, P < 0.001). This shift in the activation profile suggests improved coordination and a more selective control between these synergistic plantar flexors, rather than a simplistic overall increase or decrease in activity.

Patients treatment compliance

In the conventional group, 45.80% of patients were classified as very compliant, compared to 51.43% in the rPMS group (Table 3). Compliance was observed in 38.93% of patients in the conventional group and 35.71% in the rPMS group, while non-compliance rates were 15.27% and 12.86%, respectively. The overall compliance rate was 84.73% for the conventional group and 87.14% for the rPMS group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (χ²=0.326, P = 0.568). These findings suggest similar levels of treatment adherence across both groups.

Efficacy evaluation

The rPMS group achieved a significant effective rate of 50.00%, compared to 29.01% in the conventional group (Table 4). Additionally, an effective outcome was observed in 35.00% of the rPMS group and 45.80% of the conventional group, while no effect was noted in 15.00% of the rPMS group compared to 25.19% in the conventional group. The overall effectiveness rate was significantly greater in the rPMS group at 85.00% compared to 74.81% in the conventional treatment group (χ²=4.405, P = 0.036). These results underscore the superior efficacy of rPMS in managing spasticity in the triceps surae muscle of children with CP.

General information of patients undergoing rPMS

Both groups had an average age of 8.66 years (t = 0.001, P = 0.999), and gender distribution was similar, with 51.26% males in the effective group and 61.90% in the ineffective group (χ²=0.812, P = 0.368) (Table 5). BMI showed no significant difference, averaging 17.08 ± 1.35 kg/m² in the effective group and 17.53 ± 1.42 kg/m² in the ineffective group (t = 1.393, P = 0.166). Notably, a significant difference in the type of CP was observed; 32.77% of the effective group had diplegia compared to 9.52% in the ineffective group, while hemiplegia was present in 67.23% of the effective group and 90.48% of the ineffective group (χ²=4.659, P = 0.031). There were no significant differences in GMFCS level distribution (χ²=2.553, P = 0.110), baseline spasticity level measured by MTS score (t = 0.631, P = 0.529), baseline BBS (t = 1.225, P = 0.223), baseline EVGS (t = 1.097, P = 0.274), baseline IEMG of the soleus (t = 0.377, P = 0.707), or gastrocnemius muscles (t = 0.402, P = 0.688). Significant reduction in the effective group compared to the ineffective group (t = 2.897, P = 0.004). Discussion

The present study explores the potential benefits of rPMS in controlling spasticity in the triceps surae muscle among children with CP, offering insight into the mechanisms influencing treatment outcomes.

The significant reduction in MTS and EVGS scores and improvement in lower limb motor function, as demonstrated by BBS measure, suggest that rPMS effectively diminished spasticity while enhancing muscular strength and coordination in children. This could be attributed to rPMS’s capacity to modulate neuromuscular activity by inducing nerve and muscle microstimulation through magnetic fields, which may alter synaptic activity within the spinal cord and higher neural circuits involved in spastic muscle control [25, 26]. The observed changes in muscle activation patterns, as measured by IEMG, provide insights into the neuromuscular adaptations following rPMS. The increased activity in the soleus coupled with decreased activity in the gastrocnemius suggests a refinement in the recruitment strategy of the triceps surae complex. We postulate that rPMS may have induced neuroplastic changes, leading to improved motor unit control and reduced co-contraction—a common pathological trait in spasticity [16]. This improved coordination likely contributed to the functional gains (e.g., better balance and gait) and the reduction in clinical spasticity (as measured by MTS) by allowing more efficient and selective muscle activation. We acknowledge that IEMG amplitude is an indirect measure and future studies incorporating measures of muscle fatiguability and reflex responses could provide a more direct assessment of spasticity’s neurophysiological components. The significant reduction in caregiver strain observed with rPMS therapy likely reflects improved patient outcomes and enhanced caregiver confidence, suggesting its potential value in long-term care management.

While robotic-assisted gait training (RAGT) devices like AiWalker-K focus on improving gait kinematics through repetitive task-specific movements, our findings highlight rPMS’s distinct role in directly targeting spasticity via neuromodulation. Unlike RAGT studies prioritizing safety and gross motor outcomes [27], our large cohort and electrophysiological analyses provide mechanistic evidence for rPMS-induced neuromuscular rebalancing. The use of a high-frequency (5 Hz) magnetic field is thought to induce depolarization of peripheral nerve axons, potentially leading to activation of inhibitory circuits or causing temporary hyperpolarization and a reduction in neural excitability via mechanisms such as neurotransmitter depletion or changes in ion channel conductivity [28]. This modulation of peripheral neural input likely influences spinal-level reflex excitability and facilitates functional reorganization within the motor cortex [29]. The use of a high-frequency magnetic field likely influences neuronal excitability and plasticity, potentially facilitating functional reorganization within the motor cortex [30]. This was particularly critical for CP patients, as the condition was characterized by disrupted motor pathways and abnormal muscle tone due to brain lesions [31, 32]. The repetitive nature of the peripheral stimulus might support synaptic plasticity, promoting neuroplastic changes that underpin improved motor control and functional outcomes, as observed in this study.

It was noteworthy that certain subtypes of CP, notably diplegia and hemiplegia, responded differently to rPMS treatment. The study’s multivariate logistic regression analysis identified the type of CP as a significant predictor of treatment efficacy, indicating that diplegic patients may experience more pronounced benefits. The underlying neural mechanisms contributing to this divergent response could involve differences in the extent and location of neural injury. In diplegia, where spasticity often affects the lower limbs symmetrically, the more focused motor impairment might allow rPMS to exert targeted effects more efficiently, enhancing neuromuscular coordination [33, 34]. Conversely, hemiplegic patients exhibit unilateral impairments which might complicate the uniform application of rPMS due to asymmetric neurological involvement [35], potentially accounting for the observed variance in treatment outcomes.

Compliance emerged as another vital factor influencing the success of rPMS therapy. The multivariate analysis further confirmed this observation, revealing that higher compliance rates correlate with better therapeutic outcomes. Patient adherence to therapy regimens was crucial in achieving significant clinical improvements, possibly due to the cumulative effects of sustained neuromuscular conditioning over time. Non-compliant patients likely missed the synergistic benefits of consistent stimulations that encourage enduring neuroplastic changes. The role of caregivers in ensuring adherence cannot be overstated, as consistent support and encouragement were essential in maintaining high compliance levels in pediatric populations [36, 37].

This study’s results also suggest minimal influences from socio-demographic variables of caregivers, such as age, gender, educational background, and employment status, on the treatment efficacy of rPMS. This observation might reflect the overriding influence of the direct neuromodulatory effects of rPMS on spastic muscle management, which do not substantially interact with or rely upon these background variables. However, it was plausible that the emotional and psychological support from caregivers, while not directly quantified, plays an indirect role in adherence and motivation, further underscoring the necessity of supportive caregiver engagement.

Given the significant improvements in motor function seen with rPMS compared to conventional therapy, there appears to be a strong argument for integrating rPMS into standard rehabilitative protocols for managing spasticity in children with CP. For children with severe spasticity, rPMS could serve as a precursor to RAGT by reducing hypertonia, thereby enhancing gait training tolerance and efficacy. The improvements in muscle coordination and control in spasticity not only enhance the quality of life but also align with broader rehabilitation goals aimed at increasing functional independence. However, optimal protocols regarding frequency and intensity of rPMS, suited to individual patient profiles, particularly concerning CP type, require further investigation to maximize outcomes.

While this study provides promising insights into the efficacy of rPMS for managing spasticity in children with CP, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, the sample size was relatively small and may not adequately represent the broad diversity of the CP population. Additionally, the study design did not control for potential placebo effects, which could influence outcomes. Furthermore, the short duration of the intervention period limits the ability to assess the long-term sustainability of the observed benefits. The heterogeneity in CP subtypes and individual patient characteristics also presents a challenge in generalizing results. Furthermore, the use of orthoses, which could influence functional gait outcomes, was not controlled for in this study and represents a potential confounding variable. The use of the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), while showing high internal consistency in our sample, is not the gold-standard pediatric-specific balance measure. Future studies should employ the Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS) for better age-appropriateness and validity. Future studies should aim to include larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods, and potentially integrate advanced neuroimaging techniques to better understand the mechanisms underlying rPMS efficacy and its impact over time.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides robust evidence supporting the efficacy of rPMS in the control of spasticity in young patients with CP, highlighting it as a promising adjunct to conventional therapy. The mechanistic enhancements observed, such as controlled spasticity and improved muscle coordination, were indicative of rPMS’s potential as a neurorehabilitative tool. The study’s findings emphasize the critical influence of CP subtype (with diplegic patients responding better) and patient compliance on treatment success, offering pathways for refining treatment protocols to improve individualized patient care. Further research should aim to explore long-term outcomes, the potential for integrating rPMS with other therapeutic modalities, and the examination of underlying neural changes through advanced imaging techniques to fully elucidate the broad-spectrum benefits of this innovative treatment strategy.
