Case Report: Diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of secondary spinal tuberculosis following percutaneous vertebroplasty: a case of staged surgery with bone cement retention report
Authors: Qi Shuai, Jun Tian, Wenlong Yang, Santing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe incidence of secondary infectious complications following percutaneous vertebroplasty is less than 1%. Among these complications, tuberculous spondylitis is particularly rare, with only a few cases reported in the literature since this condition is usually caused by the hematogenous spread of a pre-existing latent Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection rather than by direct intraoperative contamination. Misdiagnosis and improper treatment of such a condition may lead to catastrophic consequences including infection dissemination, nerve damage, and progressive kyphotic deformity.Case reportThis report describes a 76-year-old male patient who underwent percutaneous vertebroplasty for a T11 vertebral fracture at a local hospital one month earlier. After admission, imaging examinations and needle biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of spinal tuberculosis. Given the patient's rapidly progressive condition and risk of incomplete paraplegia, we adopted a staged surgical strategy: first, emergency posterior laminectomy with decompression and lesion debridement was performed to relieve neural compression and control infection, which preserved the previously injected vertebral bone cement. Once the infection was controlled and stabilized, a second-stage procedure with pedicle screw internal fixation was performed to restore spinal stability. This staged approach with bone cement retention has not been previously reported in the literature. Postoperatively, the patient showed significant improvement in bilateral lower limb numbness and muscle strength, and achieved functional mobility with thoracolumbar brace support. At the 12-month follow-up, the patient was able to walk independently with no recurrence of low back pain, and no complications such as tuberculosis recurrence or internal fixation loosening were detected.ConclusionThis case report documents a rare case of postoperative tuberculous spondylitis following percutaneous vertebroplasty, which was successfully managed with a unique staged surgical approach featuring bone cement retention. This strategy effectively balanced infection control and spinal stability, ultimately achieving favorable clinical outcomes and providing a novel therapeutic reference for the management of such complex complications.

Introduction

Tuberculous spondylitis is a chronic infectious spinal disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. This condition may result in vertebral bone destruction, pathological fractures, kyphotic deformity, and neurological impairment. In recent years, with the widespread clinical application of percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) for the management of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) (1), case reports of vertebral infections following PVP/PKP treatment have been rare, with an incidence rate lower than 1% (2). The most commonly reported cases involve bacterial pyogenic infections. Herein, we report a rare case of tuberculous spondylitis diagnosed after PVP. Misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment of this condition may lead to catastrophic consequences, including infection dissemination, nerve damage, and progressive kyphotic deformity. In this study, we present an elderly male patient with vertebral collapse shown on spinal imaging. Due to a history of trauma, the patient was initially misdiagnosed with a compression fracture and underwent vertebroplasty. We analyze the etiology of spinal tuberculosis infection and propose a staged bone cement-preserving treatment strategy, which is an uncommon therapeutic approach to provide a reference for clinical practice. Written informed consent for the publication of the patient's clinical data and imaging findings was obtained.

Case report

The patient was a 76-year-old male admitted with a 1-month history of low back pain accompanied by numbness and weakness in both lower limbs, rendering him unable to stand or walk. One month prior, he presented with T11 vertebral fracture to a local hospital. Preoperative imaging studies, including computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), were performed before the initial PVP. CT revealed decreased vertebral bone mineral density, as well as sparse and fractured trabeculae (Figure 1A). MRI demonstrated heterogeneous signal changes in the vertebral body (T1WI hypointensity and T2WI/STIR hyperintensity), with subtle discitis involving the adjacent intervertebral disc space and mild paravertebral soft tissue thickening (Figures 1B,C). These initial imaging features were initially misinterpreted as typical osteoporotic compression fractures. The overall imaging features were highly consistent with typical OVCFs, which directly led to the misdiagnosis. Inflammatory markers ESR/CRP were tested before the first PVP, with results of erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 5 mm/h and C-reactive protein (CRP) 6.43 mg/L, both within normal ranges and showing no obvious inflammatory elevation suggestive of infection. However, standardized preoperative anti-tuberculosis screening (including tuberculin skin test, Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid detection, T-SPOT.TB assay, etc.) was not performed before the initial PVP surgery. He subsequently underwent PVP for a T11 vertebral fracture at the local hospital, and his symptoms improved postoperatively prior to discharge. However, he sustained an accidental fall 2 weeks before readmission, resulting in the recurrence and exacerbation of his initial symptoms. Following detailed interviews with the patient and his immediate family members, no documented or confirmed history of close contact with patients with active pulmonary tuberculosis was identified in this patient.

On admission, clinical examination revealed marked local tenderness and percussion pain over the T10–T12 vertebrae, limited lumbar spine mobility, extensive numbness in both lower limbs, grade 4 muscle strength in the left lower limb, grade 3 muscle strength in the right lower limb, preserved physiological reflexes, and absent pathological reflexes. Routine blood tests showed the following results: white blood cell count, 3.8 × 109/L; neutrophils, 75.7%; lymphocytes, 14.6%; hemoglobin, 114 g/L; ESR, 27 mm/h; CRP, 19.97 mg/L. The tuberculin skin test and Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid detection were performed after the patient's readmission, triggered by clinical suspicion of secondary spinal infection; the tuberculin skin test (TB-PPD) showed an induration of approximately 16 mm in diameter, which was interpreted as positive, and Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid detection was positive (+), indicating active Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. Tumor markers (CEA, AFP, CA199) and other serological tests were within normal ranges.

Thoracic CT revealed osteoporotic compression fractures at the T10 and T11 vertebrae (postoperative changes at T11 following vertebroplasty), vertebral bone destruction, and paravertebral soft tissue swelling. MRI showed compressive changes in the T10 vertebral body, an intraspinal space-occupying lesion (encapsulated abscess) at the T10 segment compressing the dura mater and nerve roots, spinal cord injury at the T10 segment, and bone marrow edema in the T11–12 vertebral bodies, suggesting an infectious lesion (possible tuberculosis) (Figure 2). Chest CT demonstrated multiple small nodules in both lungs, mild inflammation in the lower lobes, and small bilateral pleural effusions, with no obvious tuberculous lesions.

To confirm the diagnosis, computed tomography-guided percutaneous puncture biopsy of the T10 thoracic vertebra was performed. Bone specimens and secretions were collected for histopathological examination and microbial culture. Results were as follows: no bacterial growth in routine culture at 48 h, negative acid-fast bacilli smear, but positive (+) Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid detection. Histopathological examination revealed extensive necrotic tissue (Figure 3A). Given the rapid disease progression, progressive bilateral lower limb muscle weakness, and risk of incomplete paraplegia, emergency surgery was performed following 1 day of adjunctive anti-tuberculosis therapy consisting of isoniazid (0.3 g/day intravenous infusion), rifampicin (0.6 g/day intravenous infusion), ethambutol (0.75 g/day oral administration), and pyrazinamide (1.5 g/day oral administration). The surgical procedure included total laminectomy of T10 via the posterior approach, spinal canal decompression, lesion debridement of the T10 spinal canal, and abscess drainage. Postoperatively, the patient was placed on strict bed rest and received symptomatic treatment, Ceftriaxone was administered for perioperative prophylaxis against secondary pyogenic bacterial infections, parecoxib for analgesia, mannitol and sodium aescinate for edema reduction (Figure 4).

One month postoperatively, the patient's tuberculous infection was well-controlled, but spinal stability remained poor. A second procedure was performed: percutaneous pedicle screw internal fixation at T8, T9, T12, and L1 to stabilize the unstable thoracolumbar spine secondary to T10–12 pathological fractures. Postoperative pathology further confirmed the diagnosis of tuberculous infection (Figure 3B). Postoperative CT demonstrated complete clearance of necrotic bone and abscess within the vertebral canal, satisfactory positioning of the internal fixation, and adequate spinal cord decompression (Figure 5A). Compared with the preoperative status, the patient exhibited significant improvement in bilateral lower extremity numbness and muscle strength postoperatively. Muscle strength of the left lower extremity recovered to grade 5, and that of the right lower extremity recovered to grade 4. The patient achieved functional ambulation with thoracolumbar brace support. The patient continued standardized 12-month anti-tuberculosis therapy and received calcitonin combined with calcitriol for osteoporosis management. At the 12-month follow-up, the patient was able to walk independently with no recurrence of low back pain. No complications such as tuberculosis recurrence or implant loosening were detected (Figures 5C,D). Clinical outcome scores at different assessment time points are shown in Table 1. Discussion

The core issue in this case is the misdiagnosis of spinal tuberculosis as an osteoporotic fracture. The patient was admitted with low back pain and a history of minor trauma. Imaging revealed vertebral collapse, a feature strongly suggestive of OVCF, which led to PVP. This misdiagnosis is not uncommon; multiple studies have reported that early spinal tuberculosis lacks specific clinical and imaging features and closely mimics OVCF (3). In elderly patients, clinicians often attribute vertebral collapse to osteoporosis and thus overlook systematic screening for tuberculous infection. A case series of 9 patients with post-PVP secondary tuberculous spondylitis showed that all were preoperatively misdiagnosed with OVCF or malignancy, underscoring the common diagnostic challenge (4). In this patient, symptoms improved transiently after PVP, followed by recurrence and aggravation, along with markedly elevated inflammatory markers including ESR and CRP. These are key indicators of infection rather than a simple fracture, but they were not sufficiently recognized during the initial diagnosis and treatment, eventually resulting in neurological dysfunction.

In light of a comprehensive literature review and the clinical features of the present case, the development of this condition may be attributed to multiple factors (5, 6). Notably, a substantial proportion of extrapulmonary tuberculosis cases, particularly spinal tuberculosis, occur in patients without a documented history of close contact with active tuberculosis patients. This common clinical observation is well documented in tuberculosis epidemiological studies. Most of these cases are attributed to occult and unrecognized community exposure to Mycobacterium tuberculosis in the distant past, or to the reactivation of latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) acquired years earlier. The global burden of latent tuberculosis infection is considerable, and dormant bacilli can be reactivated when host immunity is compromised by factors such as advanced age, malnutrition, diabetes, or local tissue injury caused by invasive surgical procedures. In the present case, the elderly patient had no clear history of tuberculosis exposure, and his clinical course strongly supports the pathogenic role of latent tuberculous focus reactivation triggered by PVP-related local tissue trauma. This finding further emphasizes that the absence of a definite tuberculosis contact history does not exclude the possibility of spinal tuberculosis, especially in elderly and immunocompromised patients undergoing spinal interventions. First, advanced age and impaired immunity are common features observed in both this patient and previously reported cases. As age increases, immune function gradually deteriorates, creating favorable conditions for the reactivation or hematogenous dissemination of latent Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Second, the “locus minoris resistentiae” theory offers a compelling pathophysiological rationale for its development. This theory holds that PVP, as an invasive procedure, induces direct trauma to local bone and soft tissues, resulting in a region of decreased resistance (7). This “weak area” may have activated the patient's pre-existing, undiagnosed latent tuberculous foci or served as an ideal “niche” for the hematogenous spread of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (8). Given the patient's lack of a history of active pulmonary tuberculosis, reactivation of a local latent focus following surgical trauma is more likely.

Misdiagnosis was the primary factor leading to the progression of neurological dysfunction in this case. Although MRI is the most sensitive imaging modality for the diagnosis of spinal tuberculosis, it can clearly demonstrate vertebral bone destruction, paravertebral abscesses, and dural sac compression. However, its early manifestations may still overlap with post-fracture findings (9). Both conditions can present as low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, and both can be associated with paravertebral soft tissue opacities. Therefore, we should not overly rely on imaging for differential diagnosis. The definitive diagnosis in this case was based on pathological findings from percutaneous vertebral biopsy and positive Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid testing, confirming that microbiological and histological evidence remains the “gold standard” for the diagnosis of spinal infectious lesions (10).

Upon readmission, we formulated a correct clinical decision-making pathway from recurrent pain to suspected infection and spinal tuberculosis based on comprehensive evidence: the patient's transient symptom relief after initial PVP followed by recurrent and aggravated low back pain with progressive lower limb numbness and muscle weakness was inconsistent with simple fracture recurrence; marked local tenderness, percussion pain and neurological deficits over T10–T12 were observed on physical examination; significantly elevated ESR (27 mm/h) and CRP (19.97 mg/L) indicated active infectious inflammation; positive TB-PPD and Mycobacterium tuberculosis nucleic acid detection provided direct laboratory evidence; and repeated thoracic CT and MRI showed typical imaging features of infectious spondylitis including vertebral bone destruction, paravertebral soft tissue swelling and intraspinal abscess compressing neural structures. This multi-dimensional diagnostic strategy effectively corrected the initial misdiagnosis and provided a reliable reference for clinical identification of such complex cases.

To prevent such catastrophic misdiagnoses, establishing an effective preoperative screening protocol is of utmost importance. However, a review of current clinical practice guidelines and consensus statements indicates that there are no clear guidelines or consensus mandating routine preoperative screening for spinal tuberculosis in elderly patients scheduled for percutaneous vertebroplasty (11, 12). Based on the experience gained from this case and relevant literature reports, we strongly recommend that for elderly OVCF patients with immunosuppression risks (e.g., long-term corticosteroid use, diabetes, malnutrition), atypical symptoms (e.g., accompanied by low-grade fever, night sweats, weight loss, or other systemic symptoms), or abnormally elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), tuberculosis infection should be included in the routine differential diagnosis before deciding on PVP/PKP. Screening methods may include tuberculin skin tests (TST) and serological assays such as T-SPOT.TB (13). It should be emphasized that T-SPOT.TB cannot differentiate latent tuberculosis infection from active tuberculosis disease, and it was only used as an auxiliary indicator in this case; the final diagnosis was entirely based on the gold-standard microbiological and histopathological evidence. For cases with high suspicion but inconclusive results, preoperative diagnostic needle biopsy should be considered to rule out malignancy or infection (14), thereby avoiding the injection of bone cement into active tuberculosis lesions.

This patient exhibited progressive neurological deterioration, which constituted a clear surgical indication. The decision to retain the preoperatively injected bone cement instead of performing one-stage cement removal and reconstruction was made after careful case-specific clinical evaluation. Given the patient's rapidly progressive neurological deterioration and imminent risk of incomplete paraplegia, the first-stage emergency surgery prioritized urgent neural decompression and focal infection debridement; cement removal could have prolonged operative time, increased surgical trauma, and potentially compromised the primary goal of neurological salvage. Imaging and intraoperative findings showed no obvious cement leakage at T11, and the tuberculous lesion was predominantly localized to T10, with no definitive evidence that the cement had become an infected nidus. The bone cement was not located directly within the core of the infection focus. No bone cement loosening, displacement or infection-related dead space formation was found around the cement. Retaining the cement was considered to provide temporary vertebral structural support, mitigate further spinal instability from immediate cement extraction, and reduce the risk of mycobacterial dissemination by minimizing unnecessary manipulation in the infected region. This cautious, individualized approach helped balance urgent neurological rescue, infection control, and spinal stability, serving as a practical reference for similar complex cases. This staged approach with cement preservation represents an unreported technique in the literature (Table 3), maximizing lesion debridement and reducing bacterial load during the initial surgery to establish safe conditions for secondary internal fixation.

The timing of surgery and preoperative antituberculous therapy, the patient presented with rapidly progressive neurological deterioration and impending incomplete paraplegia, which constituted an absolute indication for urgent surgical decompression. The 1-day course of preoperative antituberculous treatment prior to emergency surgery was clinically necessary and evidence-based. This short-duration preoperative chemotherapy was administered to rapidly reduce the mycobacterial load, mitigate local inflammatory activity, and minimize the risk of intraoperative tuberculosis dissemination, which is a well-established strategy in the emergency surgical management of active spinal tuberculosis. Instead of initiating medical therapy and decompression concurrently, prioritizing a brief preoperative antituberculous course can further lower perioperative infectious risks without significantly delaying life- or function-preserving decompression. This decision aligns with clinical principles reported in the literature that appropriate short-term preoperative antituberculous therapy is safe and beneficial for patients with active spinal tuberculosis requiring urgent decompression (18, 19).

Regarding the use of corticosteroid therapy in the initial management, corticosteroids were not administered for the following key reasons. First, the patient was diagnosed with tuberculous spondylodiscitis, and systemic corticosteroid administration is relatively contraindicated in active tuberculosis infection, as it may suppress cellular immunity, promote mycobacterial dissemination, and aggravate infectious destruction. Second, the patient's spinal cord compression was mainly mechanical compression caused by epidural abscess and granulomatous lesions, which cannot be relieved by corticosteroid-induced anti-edema effects alone. For tuberculous spondylodiscitis with progressive neurological deficits and spinal cord compression, emergency surgical decompression combined with standardized anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy is the first-line treatment strategy, whereas corticosteroids are not recommended for routine use and only have limited adjuvant value in specific severe tuberculosis lesions such as tuberculous meningitis. Therefore, the omission of corticosteroid therapy was in line with the standard clinical principles of spinal tuberculosis treatment.

The indications for bone cement preservation and relative contraindications were determined based on intraoperative findings, infection status, neurological urgency, and spinal stability in this case. Indications for bone cement preservation mainly include: (1) the patient presents with rapidly progressive neurological deficit or imminent paraplegia, requiring emergency decompression and debridement, and cement removal would prolong operation time and increase surgical risks; (2) the bone cement is well-positioned without obvious leakage, and is not directly involved or invaded by tuberculous lesions, with no evidence of being an infectious nidus; (3) the patient is in the acute infection stage, and aggressive cement removal may raise the risk of mycobacterial dissemination, while retained cement can provide temporary vertebral structural support; (4) the patient's general condition is poor and cannot tolerate one-stage cement extraction and complex reconstruction. Circumstances in which preservation is not appropriate include: (1) bone cement leaks into the spinal canal or paraspinal region, causing neural compression or acting as a carrier of infection; (2) the cement is directly invaded by tuberculous or pyogenic lesions and cannot be completely debrided while retained; (3) the cement induces secondary vertebral instability, loosening or displacement; (4) thorough debridement and bone graft fusion are required, and the cement obstructs subsequent reconstruction and fusion.

The safety of internal fixation implantation in active tuberculous lesions, both previous studies and the favorable outcome of the present case indicate that Mycobacterium tuberculosis exhibits relatively weak adhesion to metal materials and low biofilm formation ability, which are significantly lower than those of pyogenic bacteria. Therefore, the placement of internal fixation after thorough debridement is safe and necessary (20). It can achieve immediate spinal stability, facilitate early ambulation, and reduce complications related to prolonged bed rest (2). Postoperative standardized anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy (usually a 12-month course) combined with continuous osteoporosis management constitutes the cornerstone for guaranteeing long-term efficacy, preventing tuberculosis recurrence, and reducing the risk of refracture.

Conclusion

In summary, misdiagnosis of tuberculous spondylitis following PVP/PKP can lead to catastrophic consequences. Clinicians should raise awareness of this rare complication. For elderly patients with vertebral collapse who have nonspecific systemic symptoms or abnormal inflammatory markers, spinal tuberculosis should always be included in the differential diagnosis. Thorough history taking, targeted preoperative tuberculosis screening (e.g., T-SPOT® test), and timely preoperative biopsy for highly suspected cases are essential to preventing misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment. Once the diagnosis is confirmed, an individualized comprehensive treatment plan integrating anti-tuberculosis chemotherapy with surgical intervention should be developed based on neurological function, infection extent, and spinal stability to optimize patient prognosis.
