Evaluating inferior vena cava reflux and RV/LV ratio as predictors of acute pulmonary thromboembolism severity
Authors: Ehsan Goudarzi, Diba Akbarzadeh, Saba Aghajani, Mohammad Amin Sabbagh Alvani, Taraneh Faghihi Langroudi, Isa Khaheshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of inferior vena cava (IVC) reflux and the right ventricle to left ventricle ratio (RV/LV ratio) in risk stratification of acute pulmonary thromboembolism (APTE) patients.MethodsIn this retrospective, cross-sectional study, eighty-six patients diagnosed with APTE underwent computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA). The pulmonary arterial obstruction index (Qanadli score) was evaluated by an experienced radiologist, along with the RV/LV ratio and IVC reflux grading (1–6) based on CTPA. Correlations between the Qanadli score, IVC reflux grade, and RV/LV ratio were analyzed. Linear regression was performed after confirming regression assumptions. Additionally, IVC reflux grade and Qanadli score were compared between patients with RV/LV ratios below and above 0.9.ResultsThe mean Qanadli score was 7.20 ± 8.04. A significant correlation was found between the Qanadli score and IVC reflux grade (r = 0.601, p < 0.001), whereas no significant correlation was observed between the Qanadli score and RV/LV ratio (r = 0.131, p = 0.230). Further analysis demonstrated a significant linear association between the Qanadli score and IVC reflux grade, with a regression coefficient of 3.98 ± 0.55 (p < 0.001) and an adjusted R² of 0.38. However, patients with an RV/LV ratio > 0.9 did not show a significant difference in IVC reflux grade (p = 0.176) or Qanadli score (p = 0.439).ConclusionOur study highlights IVC reflux as a potential valuable imaging marker for assessing APTE severity, while also pointing out the limitations of relying solely on the RV/LV ratio for risk stratification.

Introduction

Pulmonary thromboembolism (PTE) ranks as the third most prevalent cardiovascular pathology globally, occurring at a rate of 100 to 200 cases annually for every 100,000 individuals. It is estimated that approximately 1–2% of hospitalizations is due to PTE [1]. Considering its significant morbidity and mortality, early detection of PTE severity is vital for effective risk stratification and optimal treatment planning [2–4].

Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction serves as a critical prognostic indicator in PTE, as elevated RV pressure resulting from pulmonary arterial obstruction can cause RV dilation and impaired function [5, 6]. The right ventricular to left ventricular (RV/LV) ratio, assessed using imaging techniques such as computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTA) or echocardiography, is a widely recognized marker of RV strain. An elevated RV/LV ratio has been linked to higher mortality and worse clinical outcomes in patients with acute PTE (APTE). As a non-invasive imaging marker, it provides valuable insights into right heart overload, aiding clinicians in early risk stratification and guiding decision-making for the treatment [7–9].

Inferior vena cava (IVC) reflux, characterized by the retrograde flow of contrast into the IVC during CTA, has been proposed as a supplementary marker of elevated right heart pressure. The presence and severity of IVC reflux may offer additional insights into right ventricular dysfunction in APTE [10–12]. However, despite its potential prognostic value, its precise role in APTE assessment remains unclear, necessitating further research to validate its utility in clinical practice.

Furthermore, the CT angiographic pulmonary artery obstructive index (Qanadli index) has been described as a valuable tool for quantifying the degree of pulmonary arterial obstruction on CTPA. The Qanadli index offers an additional, objective measurement to assess the severity of PTE and has been linked to better risk stratification in clinical practice. In the context of APTE, it may serve as a useful tool for evaluating disease severity and guiding clinical decision-making [13, 14].

Previous studies have examined the RV/LV ratio and IVC reflux as markers of right ventricular dysfunction. A comprehensive analysis incorporating both parameters could improve risk stratification models and enhance the precision of APTE severity assessment. This study aims to investigate the relationship between the RV/LV ratio, IVC reflux, and APTE severity as assessed through the Qanadli index. By comparing these parameters, we aim to determine their relative significance in risk stratification and clinical management of APTE.

Methods

This retrospective, cross-sectional analytical study was conducted between August 2022 and March 2024 in Shahid Modarres hospital in Tehran, Iran, on patients with APTE that referred to or admitted to this hospital. The patient data was obtained from medical records and CTPA images.

Patient population

Study population included adult patients older than 18 years of age with definitive diagnosis of APTE according to CTPA at our center. Inclusion criteria were as followed: (1) Definitive diagnosis of APTE according to CTPA (2) No history of chronic pulmonary diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or interstitial lung disease, that may affect the imaging findings (3) Absence of left ventricular failure (LVEF less than 50%). Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with suboptimal quality CTPA (due to motion artifacts or insufficient pulmonary vascular opacification due to incorrect injection method) (2) Patients with poor-quality CTPA imaging that made it difficult to precisely interpret the RV/LV ratio and IVC reflux. (3) Patients with congenital cardiac disease that may affect the radiologic findings.

Sample size and sampling method

The sample size was determined based on a significance level (two-sided test) of 0.05, a test power of 0.8 (Type II error β = 0.2), and an expected correlation coefficient (r) of 0.3, which was selected as a conservative estimate to reduce the risk of underpowering, considering variability across previous studies. The estimated sample size was 85 patients, which was expanded to enhance the power of the test and minimize Type II error. Participants were selected according to a non-random consecutive approach, enrolling all eligible patients during the designated period.

Imaging methods

All the CTPA imaging was conducted using Philips Brilliance Computed Tomography 64 channel scanner and in compliance with the standard protocol of CTPA for APTE. All patients were instructed to inhale deeply, or in case of dyspnea, take a shallow breath. Patients were positioned caudocranially during the scan. The scan coverage was adjusted from lung apex to the base according to patient size covering the entire thorax. 50 ml of Visipaque contrast agent (320 mg/ml) from GE Healthcare, Cork, Ireland was injected to antecubital veins of the patient. Injection rate was 5 ml per second and scan was initiated by bolus tracking method in main pulmonary artery. The device was configured with a tube voltage of 120 kV, a tube current of 200 mA, a tube rotation time of 400 ms, a collimation of 64 × 0.625 mm, and a pitch of 1. Additionally, echocardiographic evaluations were performed using the Philips Affinity 50, Philips EPIQ7, and Philips CVx systems. These evaluations helped exclude patients with significant left ventricular dysfunction (EF < 50%) to ensure that observed right ventricular dysfunction was primarily due to APTE. CTPA and echocardiographic evaluations were performed during the same hospitalization period as part of routine clinical assessment.

Implementation method

After patient selection and imaging process, the data was obtained from medical records and CTPA imaging using the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). All measurements were performed on axial CTPA images using standard PACS tools. The RV/LV ratio was calculated based on the maximum transverse diameters of the right and left ventricles. All the imaging was interpreted by a radiologist with over 20 years of experience, blinded to the clinical data.

Qanadli pulmonary arterial obstruction index

Any obstruction the ten segmental arteries was first detected by failure of contrast filling distal to the branch, helping determine whether the obstruction was partial or complete. Each segmental artery received a score: 0 for no obstruction, 1 for partial obstruction and 2 for complete obstruction. A complete obstruction in a subsegmental branch, was given a score of 1, equal to a partial obstruction in its segmental branch. Finally, Qanadly score was calculated by adding the numbers of obstructed arteries, each multiplied by the degree of obstruction [13]. This tool is open access.

Assessment of IVC reflux using groves classification on CTPA

In the assessment of IVC reflux on CTPA imaging, we utilized Groves classification, which categorizes reflux severity into six grades: Grade 1 no opacification of the IVC, Grade 2 trace contrast in the IVC only, Grade 3 IVC opacification without hepatic vein involvement, Grade 4 contrast extending into the IVC and proximal hepatic veins, Grade 5 involvement of the mid-hepatic veins, and Grade 6 contrast reaching the distal hepatic veins [15]. This tool is open access.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using STATA version 17.0. Continuous quantitative variables were reported as mean and standard deviation (mean ± SD) whereas qualitative variables were measured using percentage and classified frequency. The Shapiro-wilk test was used to examine the normal distribution of quantitative data. Given the non-normal distribution of variables, the correlation between Qanadli score and IVC reflux grading, as well as between Qanadli score and RV/LV ratio, was assessed using the Spearman correlation test. Statistical significance level was set to p-value < 0.05 in all analytics. To further investigate IVC reflux and its correlation with APTE grade as measured by Qanadli score, linear regression analysis was performed after assessing the linear regression assumptions. All three assumptions including linearity between independent and dependent variable, homoscedasticity of variances for dependent variable, and normality of residual distribution, were assessed for this linear regression. Due to failure to stablish linear regression assumptions, the correlation between Qanadli score and RV/LV ratio couldn’t be evaluated using linear regression. Also, to further explore the link between RV/LV ratio and the severity of the disease, patients were divided into two groups according to the previous studies: (1) RV/LV ratio < 0.9 and (2) RV/LV ratio ≥ 0.9 [16, 17]. The other factors such as Qanadli score and IVC reflux grade were then compared between these groups using an independent t-test. In addition, mean difference (MD) and standardized mean difference (SMD) were calculated to assess effect size. SMD values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as cut-offs for small, medium, and large effects, respectively [18].

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, and with international regulations, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee prior to the initiation of the study, with the ethics code IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1403.605, issued by the National System of Ethics in Biomedical Research.

Human ethics and consent to participate declarations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

All patient information was kept strictly confidential and anonymized. To minimize bias, the radiologist interpreting the imaging studies was blinded to patient identities and clinical data.

Data availability statement

The datasets generated and/or analyzed in the current study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

A total of 221 patients were initially included in the study. However, 63 were excluded due to a diagnosis of APTE confirmed by methods other than CTPA. 51 patients were excluded either due to the absence of echocardiography or LVEF < 50%, as these individuals had significant left ventricular dysfunction that could confound the results. Three patients had COPD, and 18 were excluded due to low-quality CTPA or inappropriate CTPA phases.

After applying the inclusion criteria, a total of 86 patients were included in the study. The mean age of the participants was 64.26 ± 15.46 years, with 37 women (43%) and 49 men (57%). For all patients, the Qanadli score, ranging from 0 to 35, was calculated. The mean Qanadli score was 7.20 ± 8.04, with a median of 4. The total RV/LV ratio was calculated to be 0.90 ± 0.1. The IVC reflux index was assessed for each patient according to the Groves classification, with a mean score of 2.22 ± 1.19 and a median of 2 (ranging from 1 to 5). The Shapiro-Wilk test demonstrated that Qanadli score (p < 0.001), IVC reflux grade (p = 0.007), and RV/LV ratio (p = 0.010) were not normally distributed. Spearman correlation analysis between RV/LV ratio and Qanadli score showed no significant association (r = 0.131, p = 0.230). In contrast, a significant correlation was observed between IVC reflux grade and Qanadli score (r = 0.601, p < 0.001).

Considering the assumptions for linear regression, further analysis was performed to explore the relationship between IVC reflux and the Qanadli score. The results of the linear regression analysis showed a significant linear relationship (p-value < 0.001), with a regression coefficient of 3.98 ± 0.55. The model’s fit was evaluated using the R-squared value of 0.39 and the adjusted R-squared of 0.38. The constant of the equation was − 1.63 ± 1.37, with a standardized beta coefficient of 0.62 (Fig. 1).

Additionally, the patients were divided into two groups based on the RV/LV ratio, according to previously established thresholds [16, 17]: one group with an RV/LV ratio less than 0.9 and another with an RV/LV ratio of 0.9 or greater. Each group consisted of 43 patients. The Qanadli scores in these two groups were compared. In the group with an RV/LV ratio of less than 0.9, the mean Qanadli score was 6.56 ± 6.97, while in the group with an RV/LV ratio of 0.9 or greater, the mean score was 7.84 ± 8.24. The effect size for the difference between the two groups was calculated as a mean difference of –1.28 (95% CI: –4.55 to 1.99) and a standardized mean difference (Cohen’s d) of –0.17 (95% CI: –0.59 to 0.26), indicating a negligible effect size and showing no statistically significant difference (p-value = 0.439).

Similarly, the difference in the IVC reflux grades between the two groups was also calculated. In the group with an RV/LV ratio less than 0.9, the mean IVC reflux grade was 2.05 ± 1,09, whereas in the group with an RV/LV ratio of 0.9 or greater, the mean grade was 2.40 ± 1.28. The effect size for the difference was a mean difference of -0.35 (95% CI: –0.86 to 0.16, p-value = 0.176) and a Cohen’s d of –0.29 (95% CI: –0.72 to 0.13), indicating a small effect size and no significant difference between the two groups.

Discussion

As PTE is considered a major cardiovascular emergency, effective risk stratification strategies for optimizing patient management seems crucial. The RV/LV ratio has conventionally been regarded as a principal imaging marker of RV dysfunction in PTE [8, 19]; however, increasing evidence suggests that IVC reflux could be another sensitive and specific parameter of hemodynamic instability [20, 21]. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the correlation between IVC reflux, RV/LV ratio, and APTE severity using CTPA. Our findings showed that IVC reflux was considerably correlated with Qanadli score, whereas RV/LV ratio did not demonstrate a remarkable correlation with APTE severity. These findings highlight the limitations of isolated RV/LV ratio and suggest the need for a more comprehensive approach to assessment.

The RV/LV ratio has long been considered a key parameter for assessing RV strain in PTE, as elevated PAP (pulmonary arterial pressure) can lead to RV dilation and dysfunction [22, 23]. Multiple studies have reported a notable correlation between RV/LV ratio > 0.9 and severity of APTE [16, 17]. A study by Foley et al. confirmed that RV/LV ratio measured with CTPA could be an independent predictor of short-term mortality [24]. Our study found that patients with an RV/LV ratio exceeding 0.9 had a higher Qanadli score (approximately 1.3 points higher) and a greater average IVC reflux grade (around 0.4 grade higher), though these associations were not statistically significant. Our results align with more recent studies questioning the predictive accuracy of the isolated RV/LV ratio. For example, Bach et al. [25] demonstrated that the RV/LV ratio did not differ significantly between survivors and non-survivors’ groups, suggesting that it may not be a consistently reliable predictor of outcomes in all APTE patients. Similarly, Kang et al. [26] found that whereas the RV/LV ratio had some correlation with adverse outcomes, it was not an isolated predictor of mortality compared to other parameters like IVC reflux and interventricular septal position.

The lack of a correlation between the RV/LV ratio and the Qanadli score in our study could be attributed to several factors. First, isolated RV dilation does not necessarily reflect hemodynamic decompensation, as some patients with mild RV enlargement can still have adequate cardiac output [5]. Second, the measurement of the RV/LV ratio has considerable interobserver variability, particularly when relying on axial imaging alone, which may further limit its reliability as a prognostic factor [27, 28].

In contrast, our study identified a significant linear relationship between the IVC reflux grade and the Qanadli score. Specifically, each one-grade increase in IVC reflux was associated with an approximate four-point rise in APTE severity, underscoring the potential relevance of IVC reflux as an imaging marker associated with APTE severity. Physiologically, IVC reflux reflects increased RA pressure and impaired right heart function, which happen in response to increased PAP following pulmonary vascular obstruction. This mechanism aligns with the investigation of Raza et al. [20], who concluded higher grades of IVC reflux were correlated with grater in-hospital mortality. Moreover, Singer et al. [29] showed that IVC reflux severity was strongly correlated with clinical deterioration, supporting its role as an early warning sign of impending hemodynamic collapse.

Considering the limitations of using isolated RV/LV ratio for APTE risk stratification, our study highlights the need for a multi-parametric risk assessment approach. Some studies have suggested integrating imaging with laboratory biomarkers to increase the accuracy of APTE severity prediction. Henzler et al. [30] showed that combining RV/LV ratio with cardiac biomarkers such as NT-proBNP and troponin remarkably enhance the predictive accuracy of RV/LV ratio, necessitating a combined approach mortality prediction. Similarly, Ayöz et al. [8] found that isolated RV/LV ratio is insufficient and should be assessed along with cardiac biomarkers and other imaging features, such as IVC reflux.

Despite the strong correlation between IVC reflux and APTE severity, several limitations should be considered. First, our study was conducted in a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Given the variation among imaging protocols and patient populations, multi-center studies are crucial for ensuring the reproducibility of our findings. Second, all CTPA evaluations were performed by a single radiologist, which limited the assessment of interobserver variability. Furthermore, the retrospective design of the study may introduce inherent limitations. Additionally, standardized IVC reflux grading still is a challenge, as previous studies have demonstrated interobserver variability [28]. Also, a relatively high proportion of patients were excluded based on predefined criteria, which may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of the findings. Another important limitation of this study is the exclusion of patients with LVEF < 50%, which was performed to reduce confounding effects. However, this may have limited the inclusion of higher-risk patients, in whom the prognostic value of RV/LV ratio could be more evident. This may partly explain the lack of significant association observed for RV/LV ratio. A further limitation of this study is the use of the Qanadli score as a measure of radiological severity, which is not routinely applied in clinical practice and may not fully reflect clinical prognosis. Future studies should consider including patients with LV dysfunction or performing subgroup analyses to better evaluate the prognostic role of RV/LV ratio across different risk groups. In addition, further research is needed to assess the association between IVC reflux and clinically relevant outcomes, such as mortality and hemodynamic instability, to better define its role in risk stratification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the potential clinical relevance of IVC reflux as an imaging marker associated with APTE severity, while also underscoring the limitations of using isolated RV/LV ratio for risk stratification. Considering the observed association between IVC reflux and Qanadli score, IVC reflux may serve as a useful parameter in the assessment of disease severity on CTPA. However, further studies are required to evaluate its role in clinical risk stratification and outcome prediction. Future research should focus on validating these findings in multi-center settings and integrating IVC reflux with additional imaging and biomarker-based parameters to improve APTE assessment and management.
