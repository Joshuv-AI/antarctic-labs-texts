Synthesizing supercoiled circular DNA molecules in vitro
Authors: Sepideh Rezaei, Monica Moncada-Restrepo, Sophia Leng, Jeremy W Chambers, Fenfei Leng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSupercoiled (Sc) circular DNA, such as plasmids, are essential in molecular biology and hold strong therapeutic potential. However, they are typically produced in Escherichia coli, resulting in bacterial methylations, unnecessary sequences, and contaminants that hinder certain applications including clinical uses. These limitations could be avoided by synthesizing plasmids entirely in vitro, but synthesizing high-purity Sc circular DNA biochemically remains a significant technical challenge. To overcome this challenge, we have developed two novel biochemical methods for in vitro synthesis of Sc circular DNA. Linear DNA with two loxP sites in the same orientation is generated by polymerase chain reaction or rolling circle amplification. Cre recombinase efficiently converts the linear DNA into relaxed circular DNA. T5 exonuclease is then used to digest unwanted linear DNA, and topoisomerases are employed to generate Sc circular DNA. Using this approach, we synthesized EGFP-FL, a 2 kb mini-circular DNA encoding essential EGFP expression elements. EGFP-FL transfected HeLa and C2C12 cells with significantly higher efficiency than its E. coli-derived counterpart. These methods enable the efficient production of Sc circular DNA from 196 bp to several kb, and in quantities from micrograms to milligrams, providing a versatile, scalable, and bacteria-free platform for basic research and therapeutic applications.

Introduction

Supercoiled (Sc) circular DNA molecules, such as plasmids, play a crucial role in molecular biology, often used as vectors for molecular cloning, recombinant protein expression and production, genome editing, synthetic biology, and various other biotechnologies due to their versatility and ease of manipulation [1]. Traditionally, plasmids are constructed, propagated, and purified from Escherichia coli [1–4]. Because they carry a DNA replication origin and an antibiotic resistance gene for selection, plasmids can replicate independently of the host chromosome, enabling efficient propagation of foreign DNA in E. coli [5].

Conventional plasmid construction relies on DNA ligation-cyclization of linear DNA fragments using T4 DNA ligase, followed by transformation into E. coli [1]. Although the cyclization efficiency is low, it is typically sufficient to obtain plasmids after selection in transformed bacterial cells [1]. T4 DNA ligase has also been used for cyclizing short DNA fragments to study DNA bending [6–8]. However, due to its low efficiency, the primary ligation products are often linear multimers rather than circular DNA [9–11]. Consequently, T4 DNA ligase-mediated ligation is not suitable for applications requiring large quantities of Sc circular DNA. Currently, there is no in vitro biochemical method available to synthesize high-purity Sc circular DNA. Developing such methods remains technically challenging.

Plasmids are also being actively developed as therapeutics [2, 12–14]. For example, ZyCov-D, a plasmid-based COVID-19 vaccine, has been approved for emergency use in India [12, 15, 16]. Several plasmid-based vaccines are approved for veterinary use in animals [17–20]. Additionally, plasmids are widely used in gene therapy, with 12.6% of all gene therapy clinical trials (483 clinical trials) by 2023 utilizing plasmids [21]. There are several advantages to use plasmids as therapeutics. Their purification is straightforward and relatively inexpensive [1]. They are also heat stable and easily stored and transported [2]. Additionally, plasmids have a very low integration rate into host genome [22] and can be administered repeatedly [23].

Despite these benefits, plasmids produced in E. coli pose several drawbacks for clinical use. Plasmids typically carry modified bases, such as 6-methyladenine (6mA) in the 5′-GATC-3′ sequences (Dam sites) and 5-methylcytosine (5mC) in the 5′-CCWGG-3′ sequences (Dcm sites), that do not exist in human cells [24–27]. Humans also lack enzymes, such as methylases and demethylases, to remove or modify these methylated bases. The potential health risks associated with these base modifications are not yet fully assessed and understood [2, 28, 29]. Furthermore, due to the essential role of Dam methylation in E. coli [24, 30–32], producing unmodified or unmethylated plasmids in E. coli for therapeutic use is currently not feasible [33]. Although small amounts of unmethylated plasmids can be produced using E. coli dam−/dcm− strains such as GM48 and GM2163, a substantial proportion of the plasmids exist as multimers because these strains do not carry a recA mutation [34]. The multimerization of high-copy-number plasmids leads to plasmid instability in E. coli [35].

Another issue is that plasmids isolated from E. coli typically contain bacterial DNA sequences, such as a DNA replication origin and an antibiotic resistance-encoding gene, which are necessary for propagation and selection in E. coli [36]. Plasmids can form dimers or higher multimers due to homologous recombination between sister copies [37–39]. Natural plasmids may contain a multimer resolution site, cer in the case of ColE1 [35], which directs XerCD site-specific recombination for plasmid multimer resolution to monomers. These DNA sequences increase the size of the plasmid and may trigger immune response and gene silencing [40]. Additionally, antibiotic resistance genes pose the risk of horizontal transfer to bacteria in the human microbiome [41, 42]. To address this issue, DNA minicircles, produced in vivo using site-specific recombination and consisting mainly of the target gene unit without bacterial DNA sequences, have been explored for clinical applications [14, 43–48]. However, parent plasmid contamination and the high cost of producing minicircles remain a challenge [49–51].

Moreover, it is difficult to completely remove unwanted contaminants, such as E. coli genomic DNA, RNA, protein, and residual endotoxins and antibiotics, from the final plasmid product for different applications including therapeutic use [14, 52–55]. These contaminants may trigger immune reactions in patients, further complicating their clinical application [14, 52–55]. So far, there are no feasible methods to overcome these limitations. This highlights the urgent need for an alternative approach to generate plasmids or Sc circular DNA molecules that are free from bacterial sequences, modifications, and contaminants.

To address this urgent need, we have developed two novel in vitro biochemical methods for the high-yield synthesis of high-purity Sc circular DNA molecules, completely bypassing the use of E. coli cells. These methods involve either polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using Taq DNA polymerase or rolling circle amplification (RCA) using Φ29 DNA polymerase to generate linear DNA molecules containing two loxP sites oriented in the same direction. Subsequently, Cre DNA recombinase efficiently converts these linear DNA molecules into relaxed (Rx) circular DNA molecules. T5 exonuclease is used to remove any unwanted linear DNA. Finally, E. coli DNA gyrase or variola DNA topoisomerase I is employed to transform the Rx circular DNA molecules into Sc circular DNA molecules.

Using these two biochemical methods, we synthesized several Sc circular DNA molecules including EGFP-FL, a 2002-bp mini-circular DNA molecule that carries only the essential elements for expressing enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) in mammalian cells and does not contain any bacterial modifications. Our results showed that the in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL efficiently transfected human HeLa cells and mouse C2C12 myoblast cells. Its transfection efficiency is much higher than that of E. coli-derived circular DNA with bacterial modifications. Furthermore, we synthesized several small Sc minicircles with high purity, which are excellent tools to study supercoiling-induced DNA bendability, looping, and other physical properties. These methods represent a significant advance toward enabling scalable, cell-free production of high-purity Sc circular DNA for both basic research and therapeutic applications.

Materials and methods

Plasmids and oligonucleotides

Plasmid pLoxFL was constructed by inserting a 29-bp oligomer carrying BamHI and Nt.BbvCI sites into the PstI and AatII sites of pLOX2+ [56]. Plasmid pLoxFL2 was constructed by inserting a 690-bp synthetic DNA fragment carrying two loxP sites in the same direction into EcoRI and HindIII sites of pUC18. Plasmid pLoxFL3 was constructed in two steps. First, plasmid pEGFP-C1 [57] was digested by using BamHI and BglII to remove the multiple cloning site and purified using Qiagen gel purification kit. The linearized DNA fragment was then religated by T4 DNA ligase to generate pEGFP-C1-FL. Plasmid pLoxFL3 was then constructed by inserting a 1,903-bp PCR fragment carrying an EGFP gene and all other necessary components to express EGFP in mammalian cells amplified from pEGFP-C1-FL into the KpnI and XhoI sites of pLoxFL2. Plasmids pET28a(+)_His_Tev_Cre_recombinase, which expresses recombinant Cre DNA recombinase, and pET28a(+)_His_Tev_Phi29_DNAP, which expresses recombinant Φ29 DNA polymerase, were synthesized and purchased from Gene Universal (https://www.geneuniversal.com/). All synthetic oligonucleotides including random hexamer primers and random pentamer primers were purchased from Eurofins Genomics, Inc. (+) Sc, (−) Sc, Nk, and Rx pLoxFL DNA samples were prepared as described previously [58, 59].

Proteins and enzymes

T5 exonuclease, E. coli DNA gyrase, and variola virus DNA topoisomerase I were purified as described previously [59]. A His-tagged Cre DNA recombinase was purified from E. coli strain BLR(DE3) carrying plasmid pET28a(+)_His_Tev_Cre_recombinase by Ni-NTA column. The His-tag was then removed by TEV protease. A His-tagged Φ29 DNA polymerase was purified from E. coli strain BLR(DE3) carrying plasmid pET28a(+)_His_Tev_Phi29_DNAP by Ni-NTA column, followed by an SP-Sepharose Fast Flow column. The His-tag was removed by TEV protease as well. Thermal stable Taq DNA polymerase and SYBR Green were purchased from Fisher Scientific, Inc. Restriction enzymes Nt.BbvCI, BamHI, EcoRI, DpnI, and DpnII were purchased from New England Biolabs, Inc.

Polymerase chain reaction

PCR reactions were performed in an MJ Research PTC-200 thermal cycler using Taq DNA polymerase (1.5 units per 50 μl; Fisher Scientific, Inc), 0.2 mM of dNTPs, 0.5 μM of forward and reverse primers, and a DNA template in 1× PCR buffer (10 mM Tri–HCl, pH 8.8, 50 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.08% NP-40). PCR thermal cycling conditions are as follows: an initial denaturation step for 3 min at 95°C, 27 cycles of denaturation for 30 s at 95°C, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for required amount of time (∼1 min per kb), and a final extension step at 72°C for 10 min. PCR products were purified using GeneJET PCR Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and analyzed in a 1% agarose gel by using 1× TAE buffer [40 mM Tris–acetate (pH 7.8) and 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)].

RCA reactions by Φ29 DNA polymerase

RCA reactions were performed using Φ29 DNA polymerase, 0.5 mM dNTPs, 30 ng per 100 μl nicked (Nk) circular DNA template or other circular DNA templates, and 0.2 μM of a pair of specific primers or 5 μM of random hexamer or pentamer primers in 1× RCA reaction buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM (NH4)2SO4, 4 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and 0.5 mg/ml bovine serum albumin (BSA)] at 30°C overnight or for 14–16 h. The high molecular weight DNA products of the RCA reactions may be digested by a restriction enzyme, such as BamHI or EcoRI, and then analyzed in a 1% agarose gel by using 1× TAE buffer.

Generating Rx and Sc circular DNA molecules

Recombination reactions by Cre DNA recombinase (75 nM) were performed in 1× recombination buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 33 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM DTT) at 37°C for 30 min using a linear DNA template carrying two loxP sites in the same direction, which generates Rx circular DNA molecules and certain unwanted linear DNA molecules. The reaction mixtures were incubated at 65°C for 10 min to inactivate Cre DNA recombinase. Subsequently, 0.2 μM of T5 exonuclease was added to the reaction mixtures and incubated at 37°C for additional 2 h to digest unwanted linear DNA molecules. The Rx circular DNA molecules were purified by using GeneJET PCR Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) or phenol extraction, followed by ethanol or isopropanol precipitation and analyzed in a 1% or 2% agarose gel by using 1× TAE buffer (pH 7.8). Sc circular DNA was generated by incubating Rx circular DNA with E. coli DNA gyrase, typically 30 nM, in 1× gyrase buffer (35 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 24 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 2 mM DTT, 2 mM ATP, 0.1 mg/ml BSA, and 5% glycerol) or variola virus DNA topoisomerase I in the presence of 25 μM of ethidium bromide in 20 mM Tris–acetate (pH 7.9), 50 mM KAc, 10 mM Mg(Ac)2, and 1 mM DTT at 37°C for 30–60 min. The Sc circular DNA molecules were also purified by phenol extraction, followed by ethanol or isopropanol precipitation and analyzed in a 1% or 2% agarose gel by using 1× TAE buffer.

Determining supercoiling density of Sc EGFP-FL

Supercoiling density of Sc EGFP-FL was determined according to previously published procedure [60]. Briefly, 2.5 μg of EGFP-FL was incubated with variola DNA topoisomerase I in the presence of various concentrations of ethidium bromide in 150 μl of 20 mM Tris–acetate (pH 7.9), 50 mM KAc, 10 mM Mg(Ac)2, and 1 mM DTT at 37°C for 1 h. Subsequently, the reactions were stopped and purified by addition of an equal volume of phenol. The topological status of each DNA sample was analyzed by electrophoresis in a 1% agarose gel in 1× TAE buffer ontaining different concentrations of chloroquine. After electrophoresis, agarose gels were stained with ethidium bromide, destained, and photographed under UV light. The DNA linking number change (ΔLk) was determined by analyzing the distributions of the topoisomers in the gel images. Real-time PCR assays

Real-time PCR assays were carried out using MiniOpticon Real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). A 20 μl reaction contains 400 ng of total DNA, 0.3 μM of each primer, and 10 μl of Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (2×; Applied Biosystems, catalog number A25741, lot number 3196677). The reaction started at 95°C for 10 min and continued for 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 1 min. The Cq values (quantification cycle values) were calculated from exponential phase of each PCR amplification reaction as recommended by the manufacturer. Primers used in the RT-PCR reactions are FL586F and FL587R to amplify a 99-bp DNA fragment of 16S rRNA (ribosomal RNA) genes of E. coli chromosome [61].

Endotoxin determination

Endotoxin concentrations were determined using Pierce™ Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Catalog number A39553, Lot number 0A23Q50600). First, a standard curve for low standard (0.001–0.1 EU/ml) was generated according to a procedure recommended by the manufacturer. Samples containing 2 μg of DNA were tested for the endotoxin concentrations.

Cell culture

HeLa cells and mouse C2C12 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) high glucose supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin (growth medium) in a humidified incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Transfection of circular DNA containing EGFP into HeLa and C2C12 cells

HeLa cells and mouse C2C12 cells were grown in black, optical-bottom polystyrene 96-well plates (Thermo Scientific™, Nunc). HeLa and C2C12 cells were seeded at a density of 15,000 and 4,000 cells per well, respectively, in growth medium and incubated for 24 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. Subsequently, the cells were transfected with 0.4 μg of pEGFP-C1 isolated from E. coli, E. coli-derived EGFP-FL, or in vitro-synthesized unmodified EGFP-FL, using 1 μl of PolyFect transfection reagent (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

At 24- and 48-h post-transfection, cells were incubated with 2 μg/ml Hoechst 33342 at room temperature for 30 min, prior to image acquisition. Fluorescent images were captured using identical acquisition parameters on either a Nikon Eclipse Ti-U Inverted Fluorescence Microscope or a Keyence BZX800 Fluorescence Microscope. Image analysis was performed using Fiji (National Institutes of Health) or BZ-X800LE Analyzer (Keyence) software. Total fluorescence intensity was quantified by measuring the integrated density (Fiji) or brightness (BZ-X800LE Analyzer) of the fluorescent cells, with consistent threshold settings applied across all images.

Results

Figure 1 shows our strategy to synthesize Sc circular DNA molecules through a combination of in vitro DNA synthesis using PCR or RCA, formation of the Rx circular DNA molecules through site-specific recombination by Cre DNA recombinase [62, 63], and selective digestion of unwanted linear DNA by T5 exonuclease [59, 64, 65]. The final Sc circular DNA molecules will be generated by two methods. Method 1 is using E. coli DNA gyrase to convert the Rx circular DNA to Sc circular DNA in the presence of ATP [59]. Method 2 is using a eukaryotic DNA topoisomerase I, such as variola virus DNA topoisomerase I, in the presence of ethidium bromide to convert Rx circular DNA into (−) Sc circular DNA (Fig. 1). Rx circular DNA can be temporarily (+) supercoiled by a DNA intercalator, such as ethidium bromide [66]. Variola DNA topoisomerase I can relax the temporarily (+) Sc circular DNA. After phenol extraction removing ethidium bromide, (−) Sc circular DNA molecules are produced. Please note that the in vitro-synthesized Sc circular DNA does not contain any bacterial modifications. DNA templates, if containing Dam methylation sites, can be removed by DpnI [67].

A PCR-based biochemical method to synthesize supercoiled double-stranded circular DNA molecules in vitro

We established a PCR-based biochemical method to synthesize Sc circular DNA molecules in vitro (Fig. 2A). DNA templates with two loxP sites were used in the PCR reactions to produce double-stranded linear DNA molecules containing two loxP sites oriented in the same direction. Cre DNA recombinase was employed to convert the linear DNA molecules into Rx circular DNA molecules [62, 63]. T5 exonuclease was then used to digest unwanted linear DNA molecules [59]. Subsequently, E. coli DNA gyrase or variola virus DNA topoisomerase I was utilized to convert Rx circular DNA molecules to Sc circular DNA molecules. A 2,870-bp linear PCR product carrying these two loxP sites (lane 1, Fig. 2B) was generated through PCR using two specific primers FL1064F and FL1065R targeting the smaller region of pLoxFL (Supplementary Table S1). Using a similar approach, a 427-bp minicircular DNA molecule (minicircle 2) was synthesized (Fig. 2C and D). PCR reactions generated a 779-bp linear DNA molecule containing two loxP sites oriented in the same direction (lane 9, Fig. 2D; primers: FL1084F and FL1085R; Supplementary Table S1). A recombination reaction with Cre DNA recombinase, followed by T5 exonuclease digestion, produced Rx minicircle 2 (lanes 8, 10, and 14, Fig. 2D). The identity of minicircle 2 was confirmed through restriction digestion assays (Fig. 2D, lanes 2–6) and DNA sequencing.

Interestingly, under our experimental conditions, E. coli DNA gyrase in the presence of ATP could not completely supercoil minicircle 2 (Supplementary Fig. S1). In contrast, variola virus DNA topoisomerase I, when used in the presence of 25 μM ethidium bromide, followed by phenol extraction, successfully and completely supercoiled minicircle 2 (Fig. 2D). A likely explanation is that wrapping the 427-bp minicircle 2 around the large E. coli DNA gyrase imposes significant structural constraints on the small DNA minicircle (Supplementary Fig. S2). As a result, E. coli DNA gyrase cannot efficiently supercoil minicircle 2. On the other hand, variola virus DNA topoisomerase I, a smaller type IB topoisomerase, employs a controlled rotation mechanism to relax DNA molecules, which does not require wrapping the DNA around the enzyme [68, 69]. The supercoiling efficiency is expected to be much higher (Fig. 2D). Our findings and proposed explanation are consistent with previously reported biochemical and biophysical studies of type IB and type IIA topoisomerases interacting with highly constrained circular DNA molecules [70, 71].

We also explored the possibility of including loxP sequences in both forward and reverse primers to amplify DNA sequences with two loxP sites. We reasoned that the 34-bp AT-rich loxP sequence, having a low melting temperature, would not significantly affect PCR reactions if incorporated into the 5′ ends of both forward and reverse primers (Supplementary Fig. S3). If this hypothesis was correct, it would allow the production of linear DNA fragments carrying two loxP sites in the same orientation for any DNA sequence through PCR amplification. Rx and Sc circular DNA molecules could then be generated using Cre DNA recombinase, T5 exonuclease, and E. coli DNA gyrase or a DNA topoisomerase (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Our results, shown in Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. S4, confirmed that this PCR-based biochemical method can be used to produce Rx and Sc circular DNA molecules. Two primers with loxP sequences at their 5′ ends (FL1102F and FL1104R; Supplementary Table S1) were used to amplify a large fragment of plasmid pGFPuv by PCR, yielding a 3,352-bp linear DNA fragment containing two loxP sites (lane 1, Fig. 3B). Recombination using Cre recombinase, followed by T5 exonuclease digestion, produced a smaller Rx circular DNA, plasmid pGFPuv-loxP (3,287 bp; lane 4, Fig. 3B). After incubating with E. coli DNA gyrase, Sc circular DNA was obtained (lane 5, Fig. 3B). DNA sequencing confirmed the identity of pGFPuv-loxP. The Rx and Sc pGFPuv efficiently transformed E. coli Top10 cells, which were fluorescent under UV (Fig. 3C). Again, DNA sequencing confirmed the identity of pGFPuv-loxP isolated from E. coli cells containing this plasmid.

Next, we synthesized a 1,933-bp unmodified minicircular DNA molecule, EGFP-FL1, using this PCR-based biochemical method. EGFP-FL1 contains only the essential DNA elements required for expressing EGFP in mammalian cells, including a CMV promoter and enhancer, an EGFP open reading frame, and an SV40 poly(A) signal (Supplementary Fig. S4). It does not include any additional sequences, such as an E. coli origin of DNA replication. A PCR reaction using forward and reverse primers, each with a loxP site at the 5′ end, and a DNA template plasmid pEGFP-C1-FL generated a 2,023-bp linear DNA fragment (lane 3, Supplementary Fig. S4B). Recombination using Cre DNA recombinase, followed by T5 exonuclease digestion, produced the Rx form of EGFP-FL1 (lane 4, Supplementary Fig. S4B). Escherichia coli DNA gyrase converted Rx EGFP-FL1 to Sc EGFP-FL1 (lane 5, Supplementary Fig. S4B). DNA sequencing confirmed the identity of EGFP-FL1. Due to the limited amount of Sc EGFP-FL1 produced, it was not used for transfection into mammalian cells. However, a similar minicircular DNA molecule, EGFP-FL, generated using an RCA-based biochemical method, was efficiently transfected into human HeLa cells and mouse C1C12 muscle cells, as detailed in the next section.

An RCA-based biochemical method to synthesize unmodified, supercoiled double-stranded circular DNA molecules in vitro

We also established an RCA-based biochemical method to synthesize Sc circular DNA by utilizing Φ29 DNA polymerase (Fig. 1). Φ29 DNA polymerase [72] from the Bacillus subtilis phage Φ29 is a DNA polymerase known for its high fidelity due to its inherent 3′→5′ proofreading exonuclease activity [73], extreme processivity [74], and exceptional strand displacement [74]. Because Φ29 DNA polymerase has these unique properties, it has been used for synthesizing large amount of single-stranded and double-stranded DNA [75], and also for whole genome amplification [76, 77]. Here, we used this DNA polymerase to synthesize Sc circular DNA molecules.

Figure 4A shows our procedure for synthesizing Sc pLoxFLA from plasmid pLoxFL using the RCA-based biochemical method. Our first step was optimizing experimental conditions for RCA reactions. We found that all circular DNA molecules—Sc, Rx, and Nk DNA—could serve as templates for RCA reactions (Supplementary Fig. S5). However, Nk pLoxFL (lane 3, Fig. 4B; pLoxFL nicked by Nt.BbvCI) produced significantly more RCA products (Supplementary Fig. S5 and Supplementary Table S2). For example, 30 ng of Nk pLoxFL used as the DNA template in a 100 μl RCA mixture containing 1× Φ29 DNA polymerase buffer, Φ29 DNA polymerase, and primers FL1038 and FL1041 typically yielded ∼30 μg of high-molecular-weight double-stranded DNA after overnight amplification at 30°C (∼15 h). We determined that the optimal concentration of Φ29 DNA polymerase for RCA reactions was 200–300 nM. Pentamer and hexamer random primers performed effectively in RCA-based reactions and were selected for subsequent experiments. Additionally, 0.5 mM dNTPs, an incubation temperature of 30°C, and a duration of 15 h were found to be optimal conditions for RCA reactions.

After synthesizing sufficient amounts of high-molecular-weight double-stranded DNA using Nk pLoxFL as the template, BamHI digestion was performed to generate a linear 2.9 kb DNA fragment (Fig. 4B, lane 5). The resulting linear DNA fragment, carrying two loxP sites (pLoxFL BamHI digest, lane 6, Fig. 4C), was then converted into the Rx circular DNA molecule, pLoxFLA (lanes 8 and 9, Fig. 4C), using Cre DNA recombinase purified in-house. The recombination efficiency was estimated to be ∼74%, consistent with previous results [62, 63]. T5 exonuclease completely removed unwanted linear and Nk DNA molecules (lane 9, Fig. 4C). Subsequently, E. coli DNA gyrase efficiently converted Rx pLoxFLA into Sc pLoxFLA (compare lanes 9 and 10, Fig. 4C). One milligram of Sc pLoxFLA could be produced with an overall yield of 70% from amplification of ∼1.5 μg of Nk pLoxFL as the RCA DNA template. After RCA reactions, the DNA template pLoxFL can be digested by DpnI.

Next, we synthesized Sc EGFP-FL, a 2,002-bp Sc circular DNA molecule, using the RCA-based biochemical method to study its transfection efficiency in mammalian cells (Fig. 5). EGFP-FL contains only the essential DNA elements required for expressing EGFP in mammalian cells, including a CMV promoter and enhancer, an EGFP open reading frame, and an SV40 poly(A) signal (Fig. 5A). It does not include a bacterial origin of replication or any antibiotic resistance-encoding genes for selection and, therefore, cannot be replicated or produced in E. coli. The only bacterial DNA sequence present is the 34-bp loxP site (Fig. 5A). Since the RCA-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL lacks bacterial modifications, such as Dam methylation, it was completely digested by DpnII and resistant to digestion by DpnI (Supplementary Fig. S6C). In contrast, Sc EGFP-FL generated from plasmid pLoxFL3, isolated from E. coli Top10 cells, contained Dam methylation sites. As a result, it was completely digested by DpnI but not by DpnII. This difference arises because both DpnI and DpnII recognize the same 5′-GATC-3′ Dam sequence; DpnI digests methylated Dam sequences, while DpnII digests unmethylated Dam sequences [78].

We generated a series of circular EGFP-FL DNA samples with varying supercoiling densities using variola virus DNA topoisomerase I in the presence of different concentrations of ethidium bromide. The topological status of these DNA samples was analyzed by electrophoresis in a 1% agarose gel prepared with 1× TAE buffer (40 mM Tris–acetate, pH 7.8, and 1 mM EDTA) containing 0, 0.5, 1, 2.5, or 5 μg/ml of chloroquine. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. S7. This method effectively produces circular DNA molecules with a range of superhelical densities suitable for various applications. Notably, at ethidium bromide concentrations of 10 μM and higher, hypernegatively supercoiled DNA was produced, and the resulting topoisomers could not be resolved in agarose gels containing 5 μg/ml of chloroquine (lanes 9 and 10, Supplementary Fig. S7). In this experiment, we also determined the supercoiling densities of Rx and Sc EGFP-FL synthesized using the RCA-based biochemical method. As expected, Rx EGFP-FL was fully relaxed (lane 11, Supplementary Fig. S7). Sc EGFP-FL, generated by E. coli DNA gyrase, was negatively supercoiled, with a supercoiling density of −0.047, comparable to that of plasmids isolated from E. coli cells.

Using the in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL, we transfected human HeLa cells and mouse C2C12 myoblast cells. To our surprise, not only did the in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL efficiently transfect both HeLa cells (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig.S8) and mouse C2C12 myoblast cells (Supplementary Fig. S9), but its transfection efficiency was substantially higher than that of Sc DNA molecules derived from E. coli Top10 cells. For example, we compared the transfection efficiency of the in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL with the same Sc EGFP-FL generated using plasmid pLoxFL3 isolated from E. coli. The in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL demonstrated significantly greater transfection efficiency in HeLa cells than the E. coli-derived Sc EGFP-FL (Fig. 5C and D). Under our experimental conditions, the number of EGFP-positive cells and the fluorescence intensity per EGFP-positive cell were both markedly higher for the in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL compared to the E. coli-derived counterpart (Fig. 5C and D).

Similar results were observed when comparing the transfection efficiency of the in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL with that of plasmid pEGFP-C1 isolated from E. coli Top10 cells in both human HeLa cells and mouse C2C12 cells (Supplementary Figs S8 and S9). It is worth noting that increasing the excitation light intensity, exposure time, or gain allowed for the detection of more plasmid pEGFP-C1-transfected HeLa and C2C12 cells.

Furthermore, we synthesized a 196-bp minicircle, i.e. minicircle 4, using an RCA-based biochemical assay with Nk pLoxFL as the DNA template (Supplementary Fig. S10A). RCA reactions, followed by PvuII digestion, produced a 2,977-bp linear DNA molecule containing two loxP sites oriented in the same direction (lane 2, Supplementary Fig. S10B). Subsequent recombination with Cre DNA recombinase and digestion with T5 exonuclease generated Rx minicircle 4 (lane 4, Supplementary Fig. S10B). Variola virus DNA topoisomerase I in the presence of 25 μM ethidium bromide, followed by phenol extraction, produced Sc minicircle 4 (lane 5, Supplementary Fig. S10B). Interestingly, the majority of minicircle 4 molecules were dimers (lanes 4 and 5, Supplementary Fig. S10B), with some existing as monomers and tetramers. This is consistent with the small size of minicircle 4 and the DNA persistence length, which ranges between 100 and 150 bp in the presence of Mg2+ [79, 80]. The high bending force required to form a 196-bp DNA minicircle likely contributed to the low yield of monomeric minicircle 4. Restriction enzyme digestion with BamHI, HindIII, and a combination of BamHI and HindIII produced DNA fragments consistent with the predicted lengths based on the minicircle 4 DNA map/sequence (Supplementary Fig. S10C). The minicircles generated in this study serve as excellent tools and materials for investigating supercoiling-induced DNA bendability, looping, and other physical properties using single-molecule techniques, such as atomic force microscopy, and DNA sequencing (data not shown), and also for RCA-based biosensing studies [81].

Discussion

We have established and developed two novel biochemical methods—one PCR-based and one RCA-based biochemical methods—for synthesizing Sc circular DNA molecules in vitro. These methods mimic the natural mechanism by which E. coli utilizes the site-specific recombinase XerCD to convert plasmid dimers into monomers via the ∼280-bp cer sequence in ColE1 plasmids, as plasmids commonly form dimers through homologous recombination between sister plasmids [37–39]. These two approaches enable scalable, efficient production of Sc circular DNA in quantities ranging from micrograms to milligrams and beyond. They are the only in vitro synthesis methods capable of producing sufficient quantities of high purity of Sc circular DNA for diverse biomedical and therapeutic applications.

The PCR-based and RCA-based biochemical methods are closely related and complementary, each with distinct advantages and limitations. A major advantage of the PCR-based method is its versatility; PCR templates do not require loxP sites or other stringent DNA elements (see Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs S3 and S4). This flexibility makes it suitable for constructing Sc circular DNA from virtually any DNA sequence. Furthermore, because loxP sites are placed at the ends of PCR products, the majority of the PCR product can be converted into Sc circular DNA. A potential drawback is the relatively low fidelity and processivity of Taq DNA polymerase [82]. However, when PCR reactions are limited to fewer than 25 cycles, the error rate becomes undetectable for the four different Sc circular DNA products synthesized using the PCR-based method in this study: pLoxFLA (Fig. 2A), minicircle 2 (Fig. 2C), pGFPuv-loxP (Fig. 3), and EGFP-FL1 (Supplementary Fig. S4) according to our sequencing results.

The RCA-based biochemical method, which uses Φ29 DNA polymerase, offers high fidelity and processivity [72–74], enabling accurate production of large quantities of Sc circular DNA. For example, we successfully synthesized 1 mg of pLoxFLA and EGFP-FL, and 150 μg of minicircle 2 using this approach within a few days. However, this method requires a circular DNA template containing two loxP sites in the same orientation (Fig. 4), and thus cannot be used with linear DNA or circular DNA lacking loxP sites. Another limitation is that circular plasmid templates typically contain unnecessary bacterial sequences such as replication origins and antibiotic resistance genes, which are also replicated during RCA. Although these unnecessary bacterial DNA sequences can be digested with T5 exonuclease and excluded from the final product, they reduce overall yield. For example, pLoxFL3, the RCA template, is a 4,900-bp plasmid (Fig. 5), while the final product, EGFP-FL, is only 2,002 bp. Thus, only ∼28% of the synthesized DNA is converted into the final product, even with ∼70% Cre DNA recombinase recombination efficiency. Whenever possible, smaller circular templates without unnecessary DNA sequences should be used for RCA reactions.

As mentioned in the “Introduction” section, T4 DNA ligase has historically been used to construct circular DNA molecules for molecular cloning of new plasmids [1], for studying DNA bending [6–8], and for constructing DNA minicircles [83]. However, ligation-cyclization reactions using T4 DNA ligase are generally inefficient, typically yielding only nanogram to sub-microgram quantities of circular DNA. While these amounts are sufficient for transforming E. coli and isolating plasmids after screening and selection [1], they are limited in scale.

In DNA bending studies, self-ligation of short, restriction enzyme-digested DNA fragments by T4 DNA ligase predominantly results in linear multimers rather than circular products [9–11]. The low efficiency of cyclization is attributed to the short cohesive ends generated by restriction enzymes (typically 4 bp) and the intrinsic properties and limitations of T4 DNA ligase [84, 85]. The probability of ring closure is described by the j-factor, which represents the ratio of the equilibrium constants for intramolecular cyclization versus intermolecular ligation [86]. For these reasons, T4 DNA ligation reactions are typically carried out at 16°C or room temperature and at low DNA concentrations to favor intramolecular ligation [1].

Nevertheless, this method is impractical for generating large quantities of high-purity Sc circular DNA molecules in vitro. Other strategies have also been employed to generate circular DNA, particularly DNA minicircles [87]. For example, small DNA minicircles have been produced from plasmids carrying two loxP sites via loxP-Cre recombination [87]. However, the yield from this method is low, making it suitable only for small-scale research applications.

Su’etsugu and colleagues have previously developed an in vitro biochemical method of synthesizing Sc circular DNA [88, 89], commercialized as the OriCiro Amp Kit. This kit is currently unavailable, as the technology was acquired by Moderna in 2023. Nevertheless, this method requires 26 purified enzymes and produced up to 1 μg of the DNA product [88, 89], which makes it expensive and non-scalable. It also requires the circular DNA template carrying an E. coli DNA replication origin, OriC, for DNA replication [88, 89]. The 26 purified enzymes include multiple subunit E. coli DNA polymerase III, which is sensitive to temperature and other experimental conditions [90, 91]. This introduces variability and stability issues for certain applications.

The in vitro-synthesized Sc circular DNA molecules developed here offer several advantages over plasmid DNA derived from E. coli, making them highly suitable for a range of applications in biomedical research, synthetic biology, and therapeutics—including DNA vaccines and gene therapy.

First, these molecules lack modified bases, reducing the likelihood of being recognized as alien DNA by host cells, and making them more compatible with human and other mammalian systems. In other words, they offer a more favorable DNA construct for therapeutic use than bacterial plasmid DNA. Consistent with this, the in vitro-synthesized Sc EGFP-FL efficiently transfected both human HeLa cells and mouse C2C12 muscle cells (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs S8 and S9). Its transfection efficiency was significantly higher than that of Sc DNA derived from bacteria. One likely explanation is that bacterial plasmids contain methylated bases, including 6mA in 5′-GATC-3′ (Dam sites) and 5mC in 5′-CCWGG-3′ (Dcm sites). Since human cells lack the enzymes to modify or remove these methylations [92], they may interfere with transfection efficiency and gene expression. Further studies are needed to understand the impact and mechanism of methylated bases on transfection efficiency and gene expression in human cells.

Second, the in vitro-synthesized Sc circular DNA molecules, such as Sc EGFP-FL, do not contain unnecessary DNA sequences, such as bacterial DNA replication origins and antibiotic resistance genes, and cer or psi sequence for XerCD site-specific recombinase, with the exception of a 34-bp loxP sequence. Consequently, these molecules are significantly smaller than their bacterial plasmid counterparts, which require a DNA replication origin and an antibiotic resistance gene for propagation and selection in E. coli. These extra DNA elements in bacterial plasmid DNA can also trigger immune responses and gene silencing, complicating therapeutic applications [40].

Third, the in vitro-synthesized DNA should be free of bacterial genomic DNA, RNA, endotoxins, and antibiotics. Since the synthesis methods do not inherently generate genomic DNA or RNA, the risk of contamination is minimal. Trace amounts of contaminants introduced through bacterial plasmid templates or protein reagents can be effectively removed using DpnI digestion, followed by T5 exonuclease treatment [64, 78]. Indeed, our real-time PCR experiments demonstrated that the in vitro-synthesized circular DNA contains significantly less genomic DNA than plasmid DNA purified using the Qiagen plasmid isolation kit or obtained from commercial sources (Supplementary Table S3). These in vitro-synthesized DNA samples also have lower endotoxin levels (Supplementary Fig. S11 and Supplementary Table S4). This may also contribute to the higher transfection efficiency of human HeLa cells and mouse C2C12 muscle cells for the invitro-synthesized Sc circular DNA.

Fourth, both methods are highly scalable, capable of producing Sc circular DNA in microgram to milligram quantities and beyond. The required enzymes, Φ29 DNA polymerase, Taq DNA polymerase, Cre DNA recombinase, T5 exonuclease, E. coli DNA gyrase, and restriction enzymes such as BamHI and EcoRI, can be overexpressed and purified from E. coli at low cost. As a result, the cost of production is expected to be comparable to plasmid isolation. Additionally, the RCA-based biochemical method can be adapted for high-yield linear DNA synthesis, which is valuable for RNA production (see Supplementary Fig. S12). Using a 10 ml of RCA reaction, 3–4 mg of linear DNA can be produced in 2–3 days (data not shown). These methods are also compatible with full automation, offering advantages over traditional plasmid production via bacterial fermentation.

Finally, Taq DNA polymerase and Φ29 DNA polymerase can use modified nucleotides for PCR [93] or RCA [94–96] reactions, enabling the production of Sc circular DNA molecules with modified nucleotides specific for tailored applications.

In summary, we introduce two novel in vitro biochemical methods, PCR-based and RCA-based biochemical methods, for the synthesis of Sc circular DNA molecules. Using these unique approaches, we have synthesized six different Sc circular DNA molecules ranging in size from 196 bp to several kilobases: pLoxFLA, minicircle 2, pGFPuv-loxP, EGFP-FL, EGFP-FL1, and minicircle 4. The 2,002-bp Sc EGFP-FL, which contains a CMV promoter and enhancer, EGFP coding sequence, and SV40 poly(A) signal, efficiently transfected HeLa and C2C12 cells. Its transfection efficiency of mammalian cells is significantly higher than that of bacteria-derived Sc DNA.

Unlike plasmid DNA from bacteria, our in vitro-synthesized circular DNA is free from modified bases, unwanted sequences (e.g. replication origins, resistance genes), and other biological contaminants. These characteristics provide a significant benefit for a broad spectrum of applications. Moreover, the scalability of our methods allows for production from microgram to milligram scales and beyond, supporting use in therapeutics, basic biomedical research, and synthetic biology.
