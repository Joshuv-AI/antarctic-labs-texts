Putrescine reprogram phytochemical, oxidative and osmolyte status of Satureja rechingeri Jamzad exposed to salinity stress
Authors: Ghasem Eghlima, Fateme Aghamir, Hanifeh Seyed Hajizadeh, Azam Seyedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSatureja rechingeri Jamzad, which belongs to the Lamiaceae family, is a rich source of essential oil particularly carvacrol, and rosmarinic acid. Salinity stress significantly impacts plant growth and biochemical traits. The present study aimed to examine the impact of exogenously applied putrescine (PUT) polyamine (0, 1, 2, 3, and 4 mM) on the agro-morphological, physio-biochemical, and phytochemical traits of S. rechingeri plants cultivated under different levels of salt stress (0, 50, and 100 mM NaCl). Conducted as a factorial experiment with a completely randomized design. Results showed that PUT application ameliorated the negative effects of salt stress on agro-morphological traits, including plant height, leaf dimensions, biomass, and essential oil yield, alongside increased cell membrane integrity and leaf chlorophyll. Under severe salinity, biomass reduction by 55–60% was improved by up to 80% with 4 mM PUT. PUT also increased cell membrane integrity and chlorophyll content, with the highest chlorophyll enhancement observed at 4 mM PUT. In addition, higher PUT concentrations under salinity stress significantly promoted phytochemical traits. TPC and TFC increased by up to 34% and 51%, respectively, while rosmarinic acid rose by about 19% compared with untreated stressed plants. Essential oil content increased under salinity by 20–44%, and PUT application further elevated essential oil yield by more than 60%. Also, observed reducing in cell membrane damage, osmolytes like proline, antioxidant enzymes activity including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase, and catalase (CAT), with application of PUT under salinity stress that highlighting PUT potential in increasing in antioxidant activity in order to decreasing ROS and reprograming osmolytes like proline, and antioxidant enzymes activities in order to enhancing plants resilience to salinity stress. Overall, putrescine application can be considered an effective approach to enhance the salinity tolerance of S. rechingeri, while improving agro-morphological parameters, secondary metabolite production, and supporting agricultural sustainability and essential oil production.Keywords: Abiotic stress, Antioxidant activity, Antioxidants, Medicinal plant, ProlineSubject terms: Ecology, Evolution

Introduction

Salinity, a critical abiotic factor, adversely impacts plant growth, development, and yield1. Saline soils affect crop reproductively globally, with over 800 million hectares of irrigated land at risk due to unsustainable irrigation and climate change2,3. Increased salinity alters soil texture and reduces porosity, hampering water uptake of plant4. This disruption affects various plant processes, including morphology, physiology, and biochemistry, leading to lower crop yields. Salt stress negatively influences photosynthesis, biomass accumulation, and essential organ senescence, ultimately resulting in economic losses5,6.

Satureja rechingeri Jamzad, a perennial Lamiaceae endemic to Iran, possesses essential oils predominantly composed of carvacrol (> 90%) and a phenolic-rich extract, notably containing rosmarinic acid. They are notable for their essential oil content, which ranges from 2–6% and is concentrated in vegetative organs like stems and leaves7. This species exhibits a diverse array of bioactive properties, such as antibacterial, antifungal, and antinociceptive effects8. Additionally, it demonstrates antibiofilm activity9, antioxidant capabilities10, and potential benefits for antidiabetic, antihyperlipidemic, anti-inflammatory, and triglyceride-lowering applications11–13. Pharmaceuticals like Dentol®, Saturex®, and Aortodntol® have been derived from the mentioned plant, alongside modern veterinary chemical structures, such as Zagrol®. Additionally, post-extraction plant waste serves as animal fodder.

Putrescine (PUT), a polyamine, have a crucial role in plant stress responses, especially encountering with environmental stressors like salt stress14,15, drought16, and low temperatures17. PUT helps regulate osmotic balance, which is crucial for combating osmotic stress resulting from high soil salt stress. It also enhances the plant’s antioxidant defense system and effectively eliminates harmful ROS produced during salt stress, thereby minimizing oxidative damage to plant cells. PUT also plays a role in stomatal regulation and helps control water loss from plant leaves under salt-stress conditions. This dual role of PUT helps plants adapt to salt stress conditions15. Its effectiveness as a stress mitigated can be linked to various mechanisms, including cellular structure stabilization, reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, and metabolic pathway modulation. Research indicates that PUT serves as a direct radical scavenger, alleviating oxidative harm. For example, Mohammadi et al.,15 found that PUT reduces oxidative stress in salinity conditions by boosting antioxidant enzyme activity, facilitating better oxidative management. Tanou et al.,17 illustrated that polyamines can modify the oxidative and nitrosative conditions, as well as the proteomic profile, of citrus fruits subjected to salinity. Specifically, they found that PUT reduced the activity of SOD and POD, while simultaneously increasing the activity of CAT in response to cold stress. Zaid et al.18 and Wani et al.19 demonstrated that plant growth regulators and stress-mitigating compounds can improve growth, photosynthesis, and antioxidant systems under heavy metal and salinity stress. Similarly, research by Zaid et al.20 highlighted that salicylic acid priming enhances stomatal conductance and antioxidant defenses, contributing to stress tolerance in medicinal plants. Furthermore, studies on the application of polyamines under salt stress have reported improvements in essential oil composition, phenolic accumulation, and membrane stability21,22. These studies collectively underscore the importance of PUT in enhancing plant resilience to environmental stressors.

In this study, the aim was to investigate the effects of PUT application on the growth and phytochemical characteristics of S. rechingeri, which has not been evaluated in previous studies. This study hypothesized that different concentrations of PUT can induce different physiological and biochemical responses leading to increased biosynthesis of secondary metabolites under salinity without negatively affecting plant growth in S. rechingeri. To confirm these hypotheses, this study investigated the effects of different concentrations of PUT on growth, essential oil content and chemical composition, and phenolic compounds accumulation in S. rechingeri to enhance cultivation in saline, low-yield areas for pharmaceutical and food applications.

Materials and methods

Plant material, experimental design, and treatments

Seeds of S. rechingeri obtained from Pakanbazr Company in Isfahan, Iran, were cultivated in trays measuring 61 × 40 mm with a depth of 76 mm, composed of coco peat and perlite in a 1:1 ratio. After 35 days, the seedlings were transferred to plastic pots with a diameter of 15 cm and a height of 20 cm, containing sandy loam soil (as detailed in Table 1). The experimental study was conducted over a 65-day period in a greenhouse at the Medicinal Plants and Drug Research Institute of Shahid Beheshti University in Tehran, Iran (coordinates: 32° 43′ 13.7″ N, 51° 31′ 42.2″ E). The greenhouse conditions included an 11-h light and 13-h dark photoperiod, relative humidity levels ranging 60 ± 5%, nighttime temperatures between 18 ± 1 °C, and daytime temperatures maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. The factorial experiment was structured based on a completely randomized design with two independent variables (PUT and salinity). Each replication consisted of five pots per treatment, resulting in a total of 180 pots for the study. The seedlings at four-true-leaf phase, treated with two complete foliar solutions containing 0, 1, 2, and 4 mM of PUT from Sigma-Aldrich, USA, with a four-day gap between each application. To improve the effectiveness of the treatments, a surfactant (Tween 10) was added at a concentration of 0.5%. The entire surface of the plants was manually sprayed, while control plants were treated with distilled water (0 mM PUT). Seven days following the administration of PUT treatments, salinity was introduced at levels of 0, 50, and 100 mM, accompanied by 250 mL of irrigation water. NaCl (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was utilized for inducing salinity. Following each saline treatment, regular water irrigation was applied once to avoid the buildup of salt. Throughout the growth phase, the greenhouse temperature ranged from 29 ± 1 °C during the day and 19 ± 1 °C at night. At the start, the light cycle comprised 10 h of light and 14 h of darkness, but during the flowering phase, as the season changed, the light and dark periods became roughly equal at 12 h each.

Assessment of agro-morphological parameters

Morphological parameters such as plant height and leaf length/width were recorded at the full flowering stage using a ruler and digital caliper. The fresh weight of the above-ground portions was measured right after harvesting with a digital scale. The plant material was then allowed to dry at the ambient temperature. After drying, the digital scale was used again to determine the dry weight of the shoots, while the leaves and flowers were included to calculate the total drug weight for each plant.

Essential oil content and yield

To obtain the essential oil, the dried plant samples were meticulously cleaned to eliminate any residual material. The dried plants were then homogenized through grinding into a fine powder. Subsequently, 30 g of the powdered plant material was combined with water and subjected to distillation using a Clevenger apparatus for a duration of three hours23. The essential oil content and yield were determined according to Eqs. 1 and 2, respectively.

Assessment of rosmarinic acid by HPLC

The assessment and quantification of rosmarinic acid (RA) content were performed utilizing the Waters 2695 Alliance HPLC system (Wellchron-K1001) equipped with a photodiode array (PDA) detector. Separation of the analyte was achieved by means of reverse-phase C18 chromatography column with an internal diameter of 4.6 mm and a length of 250 mm (Eurospher). The mobile phase consisted of a binary mixture of acetonitrile (designated as mobile phase A) and phosphoric acid (designated as mobile phase B). A sample volume of 20 μL was injected, and the analysis was conducted at a controlled temperature of 25 °C. A standard solution of rosmarinic acid was sourced from Sigma Aldrich. To quantify the rosmarinic acid concentration, calibration curves were generated by injecting varying concentrations of the standard compound (5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, and 380 ppm). The concentration of rosmarinic acid was reported as mg g-1DW24.

Analysis of essential oil by GC and GC–MS

The analysis of essential oil was performed by means of a Shimadzu 15A Gas Chromatography (GC) system. Nitrogen served as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/minute, utilizing a DB-5 column characterized by a length of 30 m, an inner diameter of 0.25 mm, and a film thickness of 0.25 μm. The temperature of the column was maintained at 60 °C for an initial duration of 3 min, subsequently increased to 220 °C at a rate of 5 °C/minute, and held constant at 220 °C for an additional 5 min.

For the GC–MS analysis, a Hewlett-Packard 5973 instrument was utilized, which was fitted with an HP-5MS column of identical dimensions as mentioned above. The temperature protocol mirrored that of the GC analysis, starting at 60 °C for 3 min, ramping up to 220 °C at 5 °C/minute, and maintaining this temperature for 5 min. Helium was employed as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.1 mL/minute, with 1 μL of essential oil samples injected at a 1:1 ratio. The ionization energy was established at 70 eV, with a scan time of 0.4 s and a mass range set between 40 and 460 amu. Identification of the essential oil components was carried out by retention index values, mass spectral data, relevant literature, and the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) computer library. The relative percentages of the essential oil compounds were calculated via normalizing the surface area while disregarding the response coefficients24.

Extraction and quantification of total phenolic content (TPC)

To determine the TPC, 0.5 g of the S. rechingeri shoots were finely ground and subsequently combined with 20 ml of 80% methanol. The extraction was performed using a sonicator (SingenHtw Elmasonic-D 78,224; Elma Germany) at 40 °C for a duration of 20 min. Following extraction, the mixture was centrifuged in a refrigerated centrifuge (R5702; Eppendorf) at 3000 rpm for 10 min, after which the supernatants were stored in a refrigerator for further analysis. The quantification of TPC in the extracts was evaluated by means of the Folin–Ciocalteu protocol. Specifically, 300 μl of the extract (at a concentration of 1 mg/mL) was combined with 450 μl of 1 M Folin’s reagent and 270 μl of sodium carbonate. dH2O was added to the mixture, which was then incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 50 min. The absorption of the extracts was measured at 765 nm using a spectrophotometer (SCINCO, S-3100). A calibration curve was established utilizing Gallic acid (GAE) as the standard, allowing for the expression of TPC in terms of mg of GAE equivalent per gram of dry weight25.

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

To measure TFC, a methanol extract (0.5 mL) was combined with 1.5 mL of methanol, 0.1 mL of a 10% aluminum chloride solution in ethanol (prepared by dissolving 10 g of aluminum chloride in 100 mL of ethanol), 2.8 mL of dH2O, and 0.1 mL of 1 M potassium acetate. A control mixture was prepared by means of pure methanol in place of the methanolic extract. The absorption of the resulting solutions was measured after a 30-min incubation in the dark at a wavelength of 415 nm26. The TFC was quantified using a standard curve for Quercetin (QE), with the linear equation derived from the standard curve (R2 = 0.985, y = 0.0112x + 0.0004) applied for calculations.

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant potential of S. rechingeri extract was assessed by means of the Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) method. This evaluation involved measuring the extracts’ capacity to reduce ferric ions (Fe3+) in the presence of antioxidant compounds. The reduction of Fe3+ to Fe2+ occurred under acidic conditions and in the presence of TPTZ, leading to the formation of the Fe-TPTZ complex. A standard curve was established using FeSO4. To conduct the assay, 3 mL of the FRAP reagent was incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 100 μL of the extract was added and maintained at the same temperature for an additional 10 min, after which its absorption was recorded at 593 nm. The result was expressed in terms of antioxidant activity and recorded as mM Fe g-1DW27.

Assessment of physio-biochemical properties

Cell membrane damage

The procedure established by Lutts et al.28 was followed to assess electrolyte leakage. Initially, the leaves were rinsed completely with dH2O to remove any surface ions. Following this, 0.3 g of fresh leaf tissues were cut into the small pieces and placed in 10 mL of dH2O. This extract was then left to incubate at the ambient temperature for 24 h. The initial electrical conductivity (EC 1) was measured with an EC meter. To guarantee the complete release of all electrolytes, the samples were autoclaved at 12 °C for 15 min. Chlorophyll content measurement

To assess the amount of chlorophyll, 0.5 g of S. rechingeri fresh leaves were ground using 80% acetone. The resulting extract was then placed in a Falcon tube and diluted to a total volume of 10 ml with 80% acetone. Following this, the extract was centrifuged, and the absorption was measured at 645 and 663 nm using a spectrophotometer (SCINCO, S-3100). Proline content

Measuring the amount of proline in plant leaves was conducted utilizing the protocol established by Bates et al.30. In this process, powdered leaf samples were combined with a sulfosalicylic acid solution, followed by treatment of the supernatant with a mixture of ninhydrin and glacial acetic acid. The resulting reaction was quantified by means of a spectrophotometer (SCINCO, S-3100) and measured at 520 nm, and the absorption readings were subsequently plotted against a standard concentration curve.

Activity of SOD, POD, and CAT

SOD activity was quantified through the inhibition of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) light reduction, by means of the extract absorption at 560 nm. To prepare the sample, a reaction mixture comprising phosphate buffer, NBT, sodium EDTA, riboflavin, methionine, and the enzyme extract was subjected to sunlight exposure at 25 °C for 8 min before the reaction was halted. The difference in optical density between the experimental samples and the control at 560 nm was indicative of the degree of NBT light reduction inhibition attributed to the presence of SOD in the samples. This difference in absorption was utilized to express the enzyme’s activity in units of U g−1 fresh weight (FW)31. The POD enzyme breaks down hydrogen peroxide by oxidizing substrates such as phenolic components or antioxidants. Guaiacol serves as an electron donor for assessing enzyme activity. The present enzyme, often referred to as guaiacol peroxidase, was analyzed at ambient temperature by means of a spectrophotometer (SCINCO, S-3100), and the findings were expressed as Unit g-1 FW min-131. Conversely, the activity of CAT was assessed by measuring the decrease in the absorption at 240 nm during the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide. The reaction solution contains potassium phosphate buffer, hydrogen peroxide, and the enzyme extract, with enzyme activity calculated via an extinction coefficient of 39.4 mM−1 cm−132.

Data analysis

An analysis of data variance was performed utilizing a completely randomized design with ten replicants. The comparison of average values was executed based on the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test at a significance level of p < 0.05, employing SAS software (Version 9.4). Graphical representations were created using Excel 2016. Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA), and correlation plots were generated using Origin software (Version 2024) and R software.

Results

Agro-morphological parameters

The effects of salinity stress and putrescine application on the growth traits of S. rechingeri were evident across all measured parameters. The highest plant height (68.25 cm), leaf length (19.05 mm), and leaf width (13.70 mm) were observed in plants grown under no salinity with the highest concentration of putrescine. Conversely, the lowest values for plant height (43.51 cm), leaf length (9.71 mm), and leaf width (7.69 mm) were recorded in plants exposed to severe salinity without putrescine application. Fresh weight, dry weight, and drug yield followed a similar trend, reaching their maximum values of 191.81 g/plant, 91.17 g/plant, and 36.62 g/plant, respectively, under no salinity with high putrescine, while the minimum values of 88.45 g/plant, 40.72 g/plant, and 17.59 g/plant were found under severe salinity without putrescine (Table 2). These results indicate that putrescine application effectively mitigates the negative impacts of salinity on plant growth, promoting greater biomass accumulation and leaf development.

Phytochemical responses

Essential oil content and essential oil yield

Our findings (Fig. 1) indicate that the effect of both salinity and PUT on essential oil content and yield was significantly different. As salinity concentration increased, essential oil content rose significantly (Fig. 1a), whereas essential oil yield decreased (Fig. 1b). In terms of essential oil content, the highest values were observed at 4 mM putrescine under severe salinity (3.4%), while the lowest content occurred in plants without putrescine under control conditions (2.0%). Across all putrescine treatments, moderate and severe salinity generally increased essential oil content compared with non-saline conditions. Similarly, essential oil yield per plant was maximized under 4 mM putrescine with no salinity (0.93 g/plant), whereas the minimum yield was recorded in plants without putrescine under severe salinity (0.51 g/plant). These results indicate that putrescine application can enhance both the accumulation and total yield of essential oils, and that mild to moderate salinity may further stimulate essential oil content, highlighting a synergistic effect of exogenous putrescine and salinity stress on secondary metabolite production.

TPC and TFC increased proportionally with the increase in salinity level. Also, treatment with different levels of PUT had an increasing influence on TPC and TFC compared to non-application of PUT. As in the mild and severe salinity salt conditions increased caused to an increase in TPC by 41 and 92% and TFC by 95 and 215% in comparison with the normal conditions (no salinity). Application of 1, 2, and 4 Mm PUT under increased. Under intense salinity stress, application of 1, 2, and 4 mM PUT concentrations increased TPC by 8, 20, and 34% and TFC by 13, 51, and 31% in comparison with distillated water, respectively. In general, the greatest TPC content was recorded under severe salinity in conjunction 4 mM PUT treatment, while the maximum levels of TFC were recorded under the same severe salinity in combination with 2 mM PUT treatment.

Physio-biochemical responses

Cell membrane damage and total chlorophyll

This study revealed a significant positive relationship between cell membrane damage and salinity levels, while an inverse relationship was observed between chlorophyll content and salinity. Elevated salinity was associated with heightened cell membrane damage (Fig. 7a) and a reduction in chlorophyll levels (Fig. 7b). In contrast, there was an inverse correlation between cell membrane damage and PUT concentration, with chlorophyll content exhibiting an increase in response to higher levels of PUT. The most pronounced cell membrane damage and the lowest chlorophyll content were noted under conditions of high salinity in the absence of PUT, whereas the least damage and the highest chlorophyll levels were recorded at a concentration of 4 mM PUT in non-saline environments. Mild and severe salinity stress resulted in increases in cell membrane damage of 42 and 74%, respectively. Treatment with PUT at levels of 1, 2, and 4 mM in mild salinity stress mitigated cell membrane damage by 23, 53, and 78%, in comparison with the application of distillated water, respectively, and by 28, 49, and 75% under conditions of severe stress. Salt stress reduced chlorophyll content by 66 and 131% compared to non-saline conditions. Conversely, PUT treatment at levels of 1, 2, and 4 mM enhanced chlorophyll content by 14, 43, and 89% under mild stress, and by 41, 60, and 84% under severe stress, in comparison with the application of distillated water, respectively.

Activity of antioxidant enzymes

The findings presented in Fig. 8 indicate a drastic increment in antioxidative enzymes activities such as SOD, POD, and CAT in response to elevated salinity levels, with notable decreases in activity of SOD, POD, and CAT observed along with increase in PUT concentration, under both mild and severe salinity. Specifically, mild salinity resulted in a 27, 36, and 20% increase in the activity of SOD, POD, and CAT accumulation, while severe salinity stress led to a 61, 73, and 44% increase. Furthermore, treatment with PUT at levels of 1, 2, and 4 mM under mild salinity stress conditions resulted in reductions of activity of SOD, by 13, 32, and 64%, POD by 19, 27, and 39%, and CAT by 20, 34, and 55% respectively. Similarly, under severe salinity stress, these concentrations of PUT corresponded to decreases in SOD, by 23, 41, and 72%, POD by 17, 31 and 53%, and CAT by 29, 44, and 63% compared to distillated water, respectively.

Discussion

Salinity stress exerts adverse effects on plant growth by disrupting photoassimilation, protein and lipid metabolism, and impairing cellular homeostasis14. Excessive salt accumulation leads to osmotic imbalance and ion toxicity, which together reduce biomass production and limit reproductive success in crops. To counter these constraints, plants rely on biochemical and molecular defense mechanisms that adjust osmotic potential, regulate ion transport, and enhance antioxidant systems. Polyamines such as PUT have been widely recognized as modulators of stress responses. Mechanistically, PUT contributes to osmotic adjustment by interacting with negatively charged molecules, stabilizing membranes, and maintaining ion balance under saline conditions16. Moreover, PUT has been shown to regulate the expression of genes associated with stress tolerance, including those involved in polyamine metabolism, ion transporters, and antioxidant defense pathways15. Through these actions, PUT not only protects the structural integrity of cells but also sustains photosynthetic efficiency and metabolic activity under salt stress. The improvements in growth parameters observed under PUT application can therefore be attributed to its dual role in mitigating osmotic stress and enhancing cellular signaling mechanisms that maintain physiological resilience. Such biochemical modulation aligns with previous reports highlighting the role of polyamines as biostimulants that fortify plants against environmental constraints33,34. Collectively, these findings support the view that PUT acts as a critical regulator of stress adaptation, offering a promising strategy to improve crop performance and productivity in saline environments.

Our findings demonstrate that increased salinity significantly enhances essential oil content while diminishing essential oil yield, consistent with previous studies on drought stress effects on plant metabolism24. The application of PUT mitigates these adverse effects, with 2 and 4 mM concentrations yielding notable increments in essential oil content and yield encountering with severe salinity stress. Specifically, the increases in essential oil content of 11 and 7% with PUT highlight its role as an osmoprotectant35. This suggests that PUT application can be a strategic approach to optimize essential oil production in saline environments, supporting sustainable agricultural practices.

Salinity stress is well known to trigger oxidative stress through excessive ROS generation, which in turn activates defense mechanisms, including the biosynthesis of phenolic and flavonoid compounds. Polyamines such as PUT play a central role in this adaptive response by modulating redox homeostasis, stabilizing cellular membranes, and regulating gene expression involved in secondary metabolism15. The observed enhancement of TPC, TFC, and RA in S. rechingeri under combined salinity and PUT treatment can therefore be explained by the stimulatory effect of PUT on key enzymatic pathways, including phenylpropanoid metabolism, which is crucial for phenolic accumulation23,36. Mechanistically, PUT may act as a signaling molecule that upregulates phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) and other enzymes linked to the phenylpropanoid pathway, thereby facilitating the synthesis of rosmarinic acid and other antioxidant compounds37. This biochemical modulation aligns with previous findings that exogenous polyamines enhance antioxidant defenses by activating both enzymatic (SOD, CAT, POD) and non-enzymatic antioxidant systems. The strong increase in RA under severe salinity combined with 4 mM PUT suggests a synergistic effect where stress-induced signaling and polyamine-mediated regulation converge to maximize phenolic accumulation. Overall, these findings highlight the mechanistic role of PUT as a biostimulant that enhances plant tolerance to salinity not only by mitigating oxidative stress but also by stimulating molecular signaling pathways that drive the biosynthesis of phenolic antioxidants. Such responses reinforce the adaptive strategies of S. rechingeri and support the application of polyamines for improving secondary metabolite production under abiotic stress conditions.

The use of PUT effectively reduces stress impacts on plants by minimizing harmful electrolyte leakage during salt stress. It enhances cell membrane stability by regulating ion absorption (like potassium) and water movement, fostering a better growth environment. This treatment not only curtails electrolyte loss but also improves plant morphology14,38. PUT prevents harmful chloride and sodium from entering cells, safeguarding key physiological functions and boosting salinity tolerance. Sodium leakage can harm cell membranes, disrupting cellular balance due to ion pump and signaling issues39. The study reveals that high salinity damages cell membranes and lowers chlorophyll content, while PUT application counteracts these effects, it increases chlorophyll and reduces damage. These results indicate the potential of PUT as a protective agent under saline conditions and suggest that it can enhance plant resilience under abiotic stresses14,15.

Proline is a vital compatible solute in plants that significantly contributes to their adaptation to environmental stress40. As a low molecular weight compound, proline accumulates in plant tissues as an amino acid following protein breakdown under abiotic stresses. In this experiment, a reduced level of proline was noted in plants treated with putrescine, likely because of the increments of the antioxidative compounds and activity. In untreated plants, it seems that increased protein breakdown and competition for glutamate, a substrate for proline biosynthesis, were observed, resulting to elevated proline levels and decreased chlorophyll. Elevated proline levels can raise osmotic pressure within cells, potentially hindering water absorption necessary for chlorophyll synthesis39,41. PUT treatment resulted in reduced proline levels and improved tolerance to salinity, enhancing chlorophyll levels in treated samples. PUT showed to have a protective role in preserving membrane stability index and function of essential proteins. Additionally, PUT may induce plant defense mechanisms against salt stress, including the synthesis of antioxidant compounds, which were observed to be higher in PUT-treated plants15. The findings presented in Fig. 6 highlight the adaptive response of proline accumulation under salinity stress, which aligns with the hypothesis that proline serves as an osmoprotectant in plants14. The significant increase in proline levels by 29 and 49% under mild and severe salt stress, respectively, underscores the stress-induced metabolic adjustments plants undergo14. Furthermore, the observed reduction in proline levels with increasing concentrations of PUT suggests a potential interaction where PUT mitigates osmotic stress effects, thereby minimizing cellular damage14,15. Such insights into the role of polyamines in stress resistance mechanisms are crucial for developing salinity-resistant plant varieties.

Antioxidative enzymes including SOD, POD, and CAT, are the main enzymes the are able to scavenge ROS and inhibition of oxidative damage to macro molecules including DNA, protein, and lipid. In the present work, it was demonstrated that under mild and severe salinity, SOD, POD, and CAT activities increased, which can be perceived as a defense reply of the plant to oxidative damage. The observed increase in antioxidant enzyme activity, specifically under elevated salinity aligns with existing literature emphasizing the role of these enzymes in mitigating oxidative damage15,42. However, proportion with application of PUT concentrations in all salinity levels the activity of these enzymes decreased. However, it exhibited an increasing trend in response to elevated levels of salinity. The potential function of PUT in saline conditions might be to protect the superoxide radicals. H2O2 could be detoxified through the coordinated action of CAT and POD enzyme systems. Previous research by Chen and Zhang43 indicated that PUT can inhibit the generation of hydroxyl radicals, which may account for the observed reduction in the activity of CAT and PO. These results are in line with the results of Xiong et al.44. Also decreasing in activity of antioxidant enzyme with PUT exposed to salinity stress can be because of the exceeding the plant defense potential45, because we observed in this conditions antioxidant compounds (TPC, TFC, and Chl) and antioxidant activity significantly increased and cell membrane damaged decreased. The subsequent inhibition of enzyme activity with escalating concentrations of PUT suggests a complex interaction where high PUT levels may disrupt enzymatic function or alter regulatory pathways. This dual response underlines the necessity for further exploration into the biochemical mechanisms governing antioxidant activity in saline environments, particularly the threshold effects of polyamines like PUT. Tanou et al.,17 reported that polyamines treatments remarkably reprogram oxidative status in salt-stressed plants. Understanding these dynamics is important for developing methods to improve plant tolerance to salinity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that exogenous application of putrescine significantly enhances the salinity tolerance of S. rechingeri by modulating its physiological, biochemical, and phytochemical responses. Putrescine alleviates the detrimental effects of salinity on plant growth, maintaining leaf development, biomass accumulation, and photosynthetic pigment content. It also stabilizes cell membranes and reduces osmotic stress, thereby supporting the plant’s structural and metabolic integrity under saline conditions. Furthermore, putrescine promotes the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, including essential oils, rosmarinic acid, total phenolic, and flavonoid compounds, which collectively strengthen the plant’s antioxidant capacity. These findings highlight the dual role of putrescine as a protective agent and a metabolic stimulator, offering a practical strategy to improve the cultivation of S. rechingeri in saline environments. Overall, the study provides novel insights into using polyamines to enhance both the productivity and medicinal value of plants under abiotic stress, contributing to sustainable agriculture and high-quality essential oil production.
