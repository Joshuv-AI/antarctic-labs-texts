Study of conditions to evaluate light hypersensitivity in a light–dark box using a mouse model and a mydriatic
Authors: Shogo Hirota, Leo Gotoh, Reo Hayashi, Yoshikazu Kitai, Hideki Matsuoka, Masato Masuda, Hiroaki Kawasaki, Hikaru Hori
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe pathophysiology and treatment of sensory hypersensitivity remain unclear. This study aimed to identify a simple behavioral analysis device and animal model evaluating glare sensitivity and to test the effects of psychotropic drugs using this device.MethodsWe recorded the response to light stimulation using a light–dark box (LDB) in male mice in mydriatic‐induced glare sensitivity (MiG) and normal control (NC) groups. We also investigated the effects of chronic drug administration (aripiprazole, risperidone, and diazepam). For biological analysis, we measured monoamine levels in the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, amygdala, hypothalamus, and visual cortex using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).Results“Time spent in the light box” under the condition “dark‐reared and a light box with 1000 lux illuminance” showed significantly shorter durations in the MiG compared to the NC. Aripiprazole administration in MiG and diazepam administration in NC and MiG caused a significant decrease in amygdalar serotonin (5‐HT). The administered drug in this study did not restore the MiG's time spent in the light box to the same level as the NC.Conclusion“Time spent in the light box” under the condition “dark‐reared and a light box with 1000 lux illuminance” was considered useful as an item for evaluating glare sensitivity. The trends in brain monoamine changes and behavioral analysis suggest that the reduction of 5‐HT in the amygdala by aripiprazole and diazepam may be involved in alleviating glare sensitivity and anxiety. However, no significant behavioral change was observed, so aripiprazole/risperidone/diazepam could not be said to function clearly as a treatment for glare sensitivity.

INTRODUCTION

Sensory hypersensitivity significantly affects the quality of life and development of young children. 1 , 2 Persistent overstimulation can lead to social withdrawal, negative affect, and poor academic performance. 2 Sensory hypersensitivity is associated with multiple disorders. For example, it is reported to occur in over 90% with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). 3 In addition, sensory hypersensitivity has been observed in attention‑deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), schizophrenia, and post‑traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and abnormalities in sensory processing patterns have also been reported in obsessive–compulsive disorder, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, and anxiety disorder. 4 , 5 Fibromyalgia, 6 characterized by pain hypersensitivity, and Tourette syndrome, 7 characterized by tics, are known to frequently occur with hypersensitivity to light, sound, and touch. Photophobia is commonly observed in migraine, traumatic brain injury, meningitis, and eye diseases. In migraine, it is often accompanied by phonophobia, osmophobia, and cutaneous allodynia. 8

The mechanisms of sensory hypersensitivity remain unclear, and neither established pharmacological therapies nor treatment targets have been identified. 9 The neuropeptide calcitonin gene‐related peptide (CGRP)‐targeted monoclonal antibody therapy for migraine has been reported to improve migraine symptoms and reduce light sensitivity during attacks, 10 but this does not necessarily apply to other types of sensory hypersensitivity. Clinically, antipsychotics such as aripiprazole (APZ) 11 and risperidone (RIS), 12 for irritability in ASD, have been used. The mechanisms underlying sensory hypersensitivity may vary depending on the sensory organ. Animal models with sensory hypersensitivity are important for clarifying these mechanisms. For example, previous studies include migraine‐like photophobia model induced by CGRP injection, 13 tactile abnormalities in mice with mutations in ASD‐related genes (Mecp2, Gabrb3, Shank3, and Fmr1), 14 and auditory hypersensitivity in Fmr1 knockout mice. 15 However, animal model research is limited.

If sensory hypersensitivity can be evaluated using a simple behavioral analysis device, it would be easier to identify model animals with sensory hypersensitivity. This would facilitate exploration of disease‐specific and general mechanisms, as well as identifying effective treatment drugs. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the conditions for evaluating light sensitivity in mice induced by mydriatics using a simple behavioral analysis apparatus, a light–dark box (LDB). Furthermore, we administered psychotropic drugs under these conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We focused on visual and light stimuli to investigate sensory hypersensitivity and enhance light stimulation with mydriatic. We divided the mice into a mydriatic‐induced glare sensitivity (MiG) group and a normal control (NC) group.

For the light stimulation experiment, we used the LDB. The LDB has been widely used to measure anxiety‐like behavior in mice. Longer first light entry time, fewer light entries, and shorter time spent in the light indicate higher anxiety. 16 Because blind mice show no differences in time spent in the light, 17 the mice may have disliked the light. Therefore, we assumed that the LDB functions as an apparatus for testing light‐sensitive behavior. In fact, the LDB is sometimes used as an experimental apparatus for evaluating migraine‐related photophobia. 13 As a supplementary experiment, we used the elevated plus maze (EPM), assessed anxiety unrelated to light.

The drugs administered included APZ, RIS, and diazepam (DZP), an antianxiety drug.

For biological analysis, monoamines were measured by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) in the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, 18 amygdala, 19 hypothalamus, 20 and visual cortex, 21 which are important for multisensory processing and related to anxiety and characteristics of ASD.

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Kanagawa, Japan). They were at least 7 weeks old at the time of the experiment. Before preexperimental treatment, mice were housed in a room with a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 a.m.). All cages were transferred to the behavioral test room at least 24 h before the behavioral test. The rearing method was performed according to the regulations of Fukuoka University. Procedures followed the US National Institutes of Health guidelines and the Animal Experiment Ethics Review Committee of Fukuoka University (Approval No. 2205008).

Drugs

Eye drops

We instilled 0.5% tropicamide/0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride (Santen Pharmaceutical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) each a drop in both eyes of the MiG group. In the NC group, saline was instilled into the eyes following the same procedure. All behavioral tests were performed at least 1 h after instillation of the eye drops for eliminating the effects of eye irritation 22 and allowing the full effects of the eye drops. 23

Chronic intraperitoneal administration

Saline served as an injection control. APZ, RIS, and DZP were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). APZ 100 µg was dissolved in 50 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and then diluted with 100 µL 45% cyclodextrin (CD) and 850 µL saline. It was prepared to be administered at a dose that did not cause sedation and exceeded the general dose (3 mg/kg) used for irritability in humans with ASD based on previous research. 24 The final concentration was 0.1 mg/mL. RIS 20 µg was dissolved in 850 µL of saline and diluted with 100 µL of 45% CD and 50 µL of DMSO. Based on previous research, 25 it was prepared at a dose that did not cause sedation and has been used in animal studies of sensory hypersensitivity (thermal and pain hypersensitivity). The final concentration was 0.02 mg/mL. DZP 100 µg was dissolved in 45% CD 100 µL and then diluted with 50 µL DMSO and 850 µL saline. Based on previous research, 21 the dose was prepared to demonstrate an antianxiety effect in the LDB experiment without sedation. The final concentration was 0.1 mg/mL. Intraperitoneal chronic injections were continued for at least 2 weeks.

Experimental apparatus

LDB

The LDB was composed of opaque acrylic two boxes (light box: light gray, dark box: black, 20 × 20 × 20 cm each box), joined by a 4 ×5 cm openable door. The light box was covered with transparent acrylic and lit by LED lights (500–5000 lux), whereas the dark box was darkened by the dark gray smoked acrylic cover during the experiment (less than 10 lux).

EPM

The apparatus was placed 62 cm above the floor and composed of four gray acrylic arms (30 cm long × 6 cm wide). Two closed arms were surrounded by a translucent black wall (25 cm high), whereas the other two open arms had no enclosure. The lighting at the end of the closed arms was adjusted to be 10 lux darker than that at the end of the open arms. The apparatus was placed 62 cm above the floor.

Recording

Behavior recorded using LifeCam Studio Q2F‐00021 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA).

In Experiment 2, before behavioral testing, mice were transferred every 12 h from a light/dark cycle room to the behavioral testing room and divided into two housing environments. One group (cycle‐reared group; c‐NC/MiG) was kept in an environment where the light/dark cycle was switched every 12 h, the same as their previous housing environment. Chronic drug administration before behavioral experiments

No drugs were administered in Experiments 1–3.

In Experiment 4, the control group (NC/MiG‐sal) received 0.3 mL of saline by intraperitoneal injection daily for more than 2 weeks. The other three groups (NC/MiG‐APZ/RIS/DZP) received 0.3 mL of the APZ/RIS/DZP solutions daily by intraperitoneal injection for more than 2 weeks.

Behavioral experiments

In Experiment 1, the EPM test was used as an anxiety test.

In Experiments 2–4, the LDB test was performed as a glare sensitivity experiment. In Experiments 2 and 4, the illumination in the light box was 1000 lux. In Experiment 3, the illumination was varied between 500/1000/2000/5000 lux.

Analysis of brain samples

In Experiments 1 and 4, brain samples collected after the experiments were analyzed by HPLC.

During the 10 min of exploration, the time to first entry into the light box (first entry time [s]), the number of times the mouse moved between the boxes (number of movements [/10 min]), and the time spent in the light box (time spent in the light box [s]) were measured. We visually inspected the videos. The base of the mice's tails in the light box was used as an indicator of movement between the light and dark boxes and time spent in the light box.

EPM

Before the start of the experiment, all arms were cleaned with 70% ethanol to avoid olfactory bias.

The camera was turned on, and mice were placed in the central area facing the open arms and allowed to freely explore the maze for 5 min. The camera was then switched off, and the experiment terminated.

During the 5‐min exploration period, the time spent in the open arms and the number of entries into the open arms were scored. The videos were mechanically analyzed using Smart v3.0.06 (Panlab, Holliston, Massachusetts, USA).

Biological samples

Brain samples

After the behavioral experiments, mice were decapitated under inhalation anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, which was the induction concentration, to minimize the degradation of monoamines in the brain after death. Samples were frozen at −80°C until biological analysis.

Extraction solution

We prepared 200 mM perchloric acid HClO4 and 100 µM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as the sample preparation solution. We also added 5 µL of sample preparation solution and 1 ng isoproterenol (ISO) per 1 mg of brain sample, and the homogenization buffer was disrupted using an ultrasonic homogenizer. The homogenate was kept on ice for 30 min to allow the reaction to proceed. After centrifugation at 20,000 G for 15 min at 0°C, the supernatant was collected. Sodium acetate CH3COONa (1 M) was added to the supernatant at a rate of approximately 1 µL per 1 mg of sample to adjust the pH to 3.0.

HPLC

Four types of brain monoamines (norepinephrine [NE], epinephrine [Epi], dopamine [DA], and serotonin [5‐HT]) were quantified. These monoamines were measured from the extraction solution using a trace biological sample analysis system (HTEC‐510, EICOM) equipped with a separation column (SC5‐ODS, EICOM) according to the manual provided by EICOM. In addition, monoamines were separated and quantified using an electrochemical detector.

Statistical analyses

A t‐test was used to compare the difference between the two groups. For a test with two independent variables, two‐way analysis of variance (anova) was used followed by t‐test. anova followed by Dunnett's test was also used to compare the three groups. JMP (version 12.2.0, SAS Institute) was used for all analyses. Results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Experiment 1: Verification of the applicability of mydriatic drugs in this study: Evaluation of the effects of mydriatic drug administration on brain monoamines and anxiety‐like behavior

We examined whether mice treated with a mydriatic were suitable for this experiment.

First, in the NC group and MiG group without behavioral testing, we examined whether the mydriatic agent directly induces central changes by analyzing brain monoamines using HPLC. As shown in Figure 2, there were no significant differences in HPLC measurements of brain monoamines in the brain regions measured in this study between NC and MiG groups.

Experiment 2: Verification of the availability of LDB and appropriate rearing environment in this study

Next, we searched for LDB conditions suitable for measuring glare sensitivity. By refraining from exposing participants to light, the stimulus used in this study, until immediately before the behavioral experiment, we assumed that the influence of hypersensitivity to light stimulation on LDB results would be maximized, relative to other related factors.

The LDB behavioral experiments were first performed in the dark‐reared group (d‐NC/MiG, N = 6/6). The center of the light box was 1000 lux. Mice were exposed to this light for up to 10 min, with no reports of retinal damage at this duration. This illumination is commonly used to assess anxiety‐like behavior in light–dark boxes and was also applied in a study 26 comparing normal and light‐sensitive albino mice. As shown in Figure 4a, a significant difference was observed only for the time spent in the light box (d‐NC: 319.8 ± 30.4SEM s, d‐MiG: 206.5 ± 30.0SEM s, p = 0.0168). It was shorter in the MiG group than in the NC group. No significant differences were observed in the first entry time or in the number of movements.

We performed the same experiment for the cycled‐reared group (c‐NC/MiG, N = 6/6). As shown in Figure 4b, no significant differences were observed between the c‐NC and c‐MiG groups in terms of first entry time, number of movements, or time spent in the light box.

In dark‐reared LDB mice, the time spent in the light box showed a significant difference between NC and MiG groups. We simultaneously verified whether this difference remained consistent throughout the entire 10‐min period. As shown in Figure 4a for Time spent in the light box, no significant difference was found between 0 and 5 min (d‐NC: 148.6 ± 13.5SEM s, d‐MiG: 123.0 ± 15.0SEM s, p = 0.2226), but a significant difference was observed between 5 and 10 min (d‐NC: 171.2 ± 18.8SEM s, d‐MiG: 83.5 ± 18.9SEM s, p = 0.0044).

Experiment 3: Verification of appropriate light intensity for LDB behavior analysis

To confirm the differences due to the change in illuminance of the central light field between the experiments, the illuminance was changed to 500/2000/5000 lux. The LDB behavior experiments were performed with the NC and MiG groups (NC‐500/2000/5000, N = 9/6/6, MiG‐500/2000/5000 lux, N = 8/5/6). The 1000 lux version was referred to in this experiment only as NC/MiG‐1000 lux (N = 9/10). Figure 5 shows the results. In comparison of first entry time and number of movements, two‐way anova indicated no significance. In the time spent in the light box, two‐way anova revealed a significance (F(7,52) = 3.9980, p = 0.001). Subsequent tests of effects showed that the interaction term and effect of illuminance showed no significant difference, whereas the effect of NC/MiG showed a significant difference (F(1,58) = 19.7524, p < 0.0001). Post hoc t‐tests between NC and MiG for each illuminance showed that the time spent in the light box was significantly shorter in the 5000 lux MiG group than in the NC group (NC‐5000lux: 278.7 ± 27.18SEM s, MiG‐5000lux: 107.8 ± 18.14SEM s, p = 0.0006). At 500/2000 lux there were no significant differences.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that an increase in the “time spent in the light box” in an LDB behavioral experiment conducted under the conditions of “dark rearing, 1000 lux light box” after administering a mydriatic can be an indicator of glare sensitivity.

This is the first study to use the LDB as a behavioral experimental tool for hypersensitivity in mouse models, with glare sensitivity induced by 0.5% tropicamide/0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride eye drops. We also investigated changes in behavior when APZ, RIS, and DZP were used, and simultaneously measured the dynamics of monoamines in the brain using HPLC.

As mentioned above, sensory hypersensitivity is complicated and co‐occurs with many diseases. Therefore, to simplify the issue, we first focused on single sensory hypersensitivity. Visual and auditory hypersensitivity have been reported to significantly reduce quality of life. 27 In addition, the visual hypersensitivity may be used to make an early diagnosis and treatment of ASD in humans. 2 We therefore targeted visual hypersensitivity using the glare sensitivity mice model with mydriatic. Under certain conditions, behavioral changes would occur as the MiG group had a higher light intensity than the NC groups.

Because the LDB is commonly used to measure anxiety, we confirmed that MiG administration did not alter brain monoamines and anxiety‐like behavior in Experiment 1. Comparing the HPLC results of NC and MiG, no significant differences were observed in the brain regions and brain monoamines measured in this study (Figure 2). This suggests that simply administering a mydriatic agent does not induce changes in the central nervous system. In addition, we could not identify any previous reports suggesting that the use of 0.5% tropicamide/0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride directly affected the central nervous system and the behavioral change in LDB without light stimulation. Next, as a behavioral analysis of anxiety not mediated by light stimulation, we conducted the EPM. It is generally accepted that a shorter time spent in the open arms indicates higher anxiety. However, in this experiment, no significant differences were observed between NC and MiG in terms of time spent in the open arms (Figure 3). This confirms that MiG is not a group in which anxiety is induced solely by the mydriatic. Therefore, differences in the behavioral analysis of NC and MiG could be due to increased light stimulation.

In Experiment 2, a significant difference in time spent in the light box was observed in the dark‐reared group (d‐NC/MiG) (Figure 4a). Conversely, we observed no significant differences in any of the LDB test items in the cycle‐reared group (c‐NC/MiG) (Figure 4b). In addition, a significant difference in time spent in the light box was also observed in the chronic saline group (NC/MiG‐sal) (Figure 6). In other words, this also indicated that not all conventional indicators of LDB anxiety (first entry time, number of movements, time spent in the light box) changed. In Experiment 3 (Figure 5) that varied the illuminance of the light box, no significant difference was found at 500/2000 lux. At 1000/5000 lux, significant differences were observed between NC and MiG in the time spent in the light box.

While higher illuminance levels are often assumed to produce comparable effects on time spent in the light compartment, we found significant group differences at 1000 and 5000 lux, but not at 2000 lux. Consistently, a two‐way anova revealed no significant main effect of illuminance. Because lower illuminance is generally regarded as safer for the retina and other tissues and is less likely to produce ceiling effects when assessing drug efficacy, we selected 1000 lux for subsequent experiments to maximize dynamic range and facilitate comparability. In summary, these results suggest that the increase in “time spent in the light box” when LDB was used under conditions of “dark rearing and a light box with an illuminance of 1000 lux” may be used as an indicator of glare sensitivity in mice induced by dropping 0.5% tropicamide/0.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride. As previously reported, when evaluating light sensitivity using LDB, a significant difference was observed in the time spent in the light box. 13 Since our study used a mydriatic agent that does not exert a clear effect from the retina to the central nervous system, it suggests that the inability to control light intensity due to the mydriatic agent may be involved in the behavioral changes related to light hypersensitivity. Furthermore, in this study, the difference in time spent in the light box between NC and MiG groups was larger in the latter half of the LDB experiment compared to the first half (Figures 4, 6). This suggests that not only the previously known retinal‐to‐central pathway response 28 but also light intensity control at the pupil level may contribute to adaptation to light stimulation. Recent studies 29 have reported that pupil movements may activate retinal neural responses and directly influence visual processing; such mechanisms may also be involved. Mice that significantly reduce only time spent in the light box under the proposed conditions are considered to exhibit light sensitivity at least comparable to that induced by mydriatics, and can be regarded as a mouse model of glare sensitivity. Comparing brain monoamines and other factors during behavioral analysis between these mice and control mice may be useful for elucidating the pathophysiology of hypersensitivity.

In Experiment 4, the experiment of drug administration, the first entry time of the MiG‐sal group was significantly longer than that of the MiG‐RIS and MiG‐DZP groups (Figure 7b). However, based on the results of this study, it is difficult to use the first entry time as an indicator of glare sensitivity. Therefore, based solely on the results showing significant differences in the LDB of this study, it is difficult to interpret the effects of the drugs used here from the perspective of glare sensitivity. In the chronic drug administration group to MiG, all three drugs showed a tendency toward prolonged first entry time, increased number of movements, and extended time spent in the light box (Figure 7b). This tendency was not observed in the NC group. Increased stimulation due to sensory hypersensitivity may have induced anxiety, which the drugs may have alleviated. We will discuss the results, incorporating the HPLC findings.

In the DZP‐treated groups, a significant decrease in 5‐HT was observed in the amygdala of both the NC and MiG groups (Figure 8a,f). In addition, the time to first administration was significantly shorter in the MiG‐DZP group than in the MiG‐sal group (Figure 7b). APZ administration in MiG caused a decrease in 5‐HT in the amygdala (Figure 8f). A previous report 30 stated that an increase in 5‐HT in the amygdala is associated with anxiety. A study also reported increased 5‐HT release in the amygdala in rats with neuropathic pain. 31 Although this area has not yet reached definitive conclusions, the results of this study may reflect changes that occur when APZ and DZP reduce anxiety and hyperactivity.

In the NC, NE and DA in the hypothalamus were significantly lower in all groups administered APZ/RIS/DZP compared to the saline group (Figure 8d). No consistent behavioral changes were observed in the NC group following administration of the three drugs (Figure 7a). One study 32 reported that DZP reduced NE that had been elevated in the hypothalamus due to a fear response, and our results also support this notion. The effects of APZ and RIS on the hypothalamic NE system and data on the hypothalamic DA system in relation to APZ/RIS/DZP remain unclear. Furthermore, no studies have reported changes in brain monoamines in the hypothalamus related to the pathophysiology of hypersensitivity. Our results only suggest that these drugs may also affect the DA system.

In the MiG, Epi increased and 5‐HT decreased in the frontal cortex after RIS and APZ administration, respectively (Figure 8g). Although a previous study 33 suggested that a decrease in Epi in the prefrontal cortex may be related to motor and cognitive functions, changes in other monoamines were also observed. In this study, the MiG‐RIS group had a shorter first entry time than the MiG‐sal group, but no significant reduction in the number of movements was observed (Figure 7b), indicating no change in motor function. The relationship between cognitive function and first entry time remains unclear. Studies have suggested that APZ may have an antidepressant effect by increasing 5‐HT in the prefrontal cortex. 34 However, such studies are limited, and our results are conflicting, making it difficult to discuss the relationship between APZ administration and 5‐HT.

These results suggest that APZ and DZP may reduce either sensory hypersensitivity itself or the anxiety induced by it. Even if a drug alters both first entry time and number of movements, a drug that significantly prolongs time spent in the light box to a greater extent than the rate of change in those parameters might be a potential treatment for glare sensitivity. Alternatively, significantly improving the state of anxiety induced by hypersensitivity might also contribute to reducing hypersensitivity itself. However, since none of the drugs used in this study produced significant differences in LDB results, these drugs cannot be considered treatments for glare sensitivity.

Limitations

This study could not verify the level of light sensitivity that can be detected. Furthermore, an improved experimental apparatus is needed that enables real‐time measurement of brain monoamines, such as microdialysis, without interfering with behavioral analysis.

Only male mice were used. One study 35 reported that female mice spent significantly less time in the light box than males. Using female mice could have resulted in an unnecessarily short duration of time spent in the light box. Additionally, another 36 reported no sex difference in retinal light responsiveness. However, because no work has tested females under identical conditions, our results should be considered male‐specific and confirmed by replication including females.

CONCLUSION

This study showed the usefulness of mice treated with mydriatic eye drops and LDB in evaluating light‐induced visual hypersensitivity. It proposed new insights into the mechanism of visual hypersensitivity through drug administration.

Future studies could use this experimental setup to verify whether genetic and nongenetic mouse models exhibit glare sensitivity above a certain threshold, and to identify drugs that reduce visual hypersensitivity. Ultimately, we would like to identify animals with visual hypersensitivity, such as those with ASD‐related gene mutations, and effective drugs.
