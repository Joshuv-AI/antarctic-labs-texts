Rubella immunization policy in Japan: Immunity gaps arising from program differences and inconsistent cutoff standards
Authors: Masamichi Yoshika
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRubella elimination remains a global health priority due to the risk of congenital rubella syndrome (CRS) following maternal infection during early pregnancy. Japan’s immunization policy began in 1977 with female-only vaccination, reflecting a narrow focus on CRS prevention, and gradually expanded to universal childhood immunization. However, men born between 1962 and 1978–the so-called “lost generation” – were left with few vaccination opportunities, leading to major outbreaks in 2012–2013 and 2018–2019. In response, the government introduced the Phase 5 catch-up program in 2019, while women planning pregnancy and their partners were addressed through the CRS prevention program, which applies stricter serological cutoffs. Our single-institution data illustrate how program design and inconsistent cutoff standards generate apparent immunity gaps: the Phase 5 cohort showed seropositivity rates consistent with national surveys, whereas the CRS cohort revealed unexpectedly low immunity, particularly among young women. Applying a unified cutoff reduced these differences, underscoring the influence of threshold setting and participant selection. Japan’s experience highlights the need to harmonize standards, strengthen interventions among young women, and sustain adult surveillance. More broadly, it offers international lessons on how policy design and inconsistent criteria can shape the assessment of immunity and the success of elimination strategies.KEYWORDS: Rubella, congenital rubella syndrome, immunity gap, vaccination policy, Japan

Introduction

Rubella infection during early pregnancy can cause congenital rubella syndrome (CRS), making its elimination a top global public health priority.1 Maternal infection may result in congenital anomalies such as hearing impairment, cataracts, and heart disease, and past outbreaks have had profound social impacts.

Japan’s rubella prevention policy began in 1977 with vaccination of junior high school girls only, reflecting an initial focus on CRS prevention; boys were not included. In 1989, the introduction of the combined measles-rubella (MR) vaccine expanded opportunities for both sexes, but in 1995 vaccination shifted to a voluntary basis, leading to fluctuations in coverage. A two-dose routine childhood immunization schedule was finally established in 2006 and continues today.2

National surveillance data from the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare and the National Institute of Infectious Diseases have shown that, using a cutoff equivalent to HI 1:8 (approximately 20 IU/mL), rubella antibody seropositivity exceeds 90% among children and adults up to their early 30 s. In contrast, seropositivity among men in their 40 s and 50 s remains around 80%, whereas women of the same age maintain rates near 90%, indicating a residual immunity gap among adult men.3 These findings demonstrate how program transitions have had long-term effects on population immunity in Japan.

Men born between 1962 and 1978, often referred to as the “lost generation,” had virtually no access to vaccination under these evolving systems and consequently retained immunity gaps. Large outbreaks occurred in 2012–2013 and 2018–2019 within this cohort,4–6 creating serious social challenges. The 2012–2013 epidemic included multiple CRS cases, while the 2018–2019 epidemic was associated with declining birth rates and disruption of prenatal care services.5–7

In response, the government launched the Phase 5 catch-up program in 2019, distributing antibody testing vouchers to men in the affected cohort and providing free vaccination to seronegative individuals.8 However, participation remained limited due to insufficient public awareness and the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic.2,9,10

Meanwhile, the CRS prevention program targeted women planning pregnancy and their household contacts. To prioritize fetal protection, this program adopted a stricter threshold, defining seronegativity as <45 IU/mL by CLEIA (equivalent to HI 1:16).11 This standard complicates direct comparison with national surveillance, which uses the HI 1:8 equivalent, and is further influenced by self-selection bias, as individuals concerned about their immunity are more likely to undergo testing.12

Globally, the WHO Western Pacific Regional Strategy emphasizes rubella and CRS elimination.13 The United States has already achieved elimination,14 whereas Europe continues to experience localized outbreaks due to regional disparities in vaccination coverage.15,16 In Italy and other regions, insufficient immunity has been reported among women of childbearing age.16 In Australia, residual susceptibility among young adults has also been highlighted.17

Against this background, this study compares Japan’s two approaches–catch-up vaccination for middle-aged men and CRS screening for women and their partners–focusing on how program differences and inconsistent cutoff standards affect the assessment of immunity. To this end, we conducted a single-center retrospective analysis of antibody test results from individuals who underwent testing at our clinic after 2019.

Materials and methods, results

Adults who underwent rubella antibody testing at our clinic between 2019 and 2025 were included. The Phase 5 cohort comprised 156 men born between April 2, 1962, and April 1, 1979. The CRS prevention cohort included 65 individuals: 39 women planning pregnancy and 26 male partners or cohabitants. Median age was 48 years (range 40–57) in the Phase 5 group and 29 years (range 22–42) in the CRS group; all resided in Osaka City.

In the Phase 5 cohort, questionnaire data included information on prior rubella infection and vaccination history, although the number of doses was not specified.

A history of prior infection was reported by four seronegative and nine seropositive individuals, while the remainder indicated “none” or “unknown.”

Regarding vaccination history, one seronegative and 14 seropositive individuals reported previous vaccination, and the rest responded “none” or “unknown.”

In the CRS prevention cohort, no medical history items were included in the application form; therefore, infection and vaccination histories were unavailable for that group.

Antibody titers were measured using CLEIA (Access Rubella IgG, Beckman Coulter)18 and expressed in international units (IU/mL). Based on government guidelines, seronegativity was defined as <20 IU/mL (equivalent to HI 1:8) in the Phase 5 group and <45 IU/mL (equivalent to HI 1:16) in the CRS group.8,11

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.11.9) with pandas, scipy, and statsmodels libraries. Group comparisons of proportions were performed using the chi-square test, while continuous variables were assessed with the Mann–Whitney U test. For multiple comparisons, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was applied. A p-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Overall seropositivity in the Phase 5 cohort was 77.6%, consistent with national survey data3 (Figure 1). No significant differences were observed among age groups (χ2 = 5.02, df = 4, p = .285).

In the CRS cohort, seropositivity was 40.0%, with significant variation across age groups (χ2 = 11.36, df = 5, p = .045). Particularly low rates were observed in women aged 25–29 years, while no sex-based differences were detected (χ2 = 0.00, df = 1, p = 1.000) (Figure 1).

When a unified cutoff of <20 IU/mL (HI 1:8) was applied to both groups, seropositivity was 77.6% in the Phase 5 cohort and 66.2% in the CRS cohort, with no significant between-group difference (χ2 = 2.63, df = 1, p = .105) (Figure 2). Under this standard, neither age group differences (χ2 = 4.47, df = 5, p = .484) nor sex differences (χ2 = 0.77, df = 1, p = .381) were significant within the CRS group.

This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval no. 0004) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given its retrospective design, informed consent was waived in favor of an opt-out process. All data were anonymized to protect participant confidentiality.

Discussion

This study demonstrated how differences in program design and cutoff standards influence the evaluation of immunity.

In the Phase 5 catch-up program, seropositivity was consistent with national surveillance,3 and no age-related differences were observed. Although this suggests a stable baseline level of immunity, the program’s overall impact was limited. Reports from the Ministry of Health indicate that uptake was constrained by low voucher use, insufficient public communication, limited workplace vaccination opportunities, and reduced healthcare attendance during the COVID-19 pandemic.2,9,10 Men are generally less likely than women to participate in health checkups or preventive measures, which may have contributed to the persistence of immunity gaps.

Although the overall seropositivity of 77.6% among men in the Phase 5 cohort was comparable to national averages, this still indicates that approximately one in four men lacked sufficient immunity. This residual susceptibility represents a persistent coverage issue rather than a marked decline, consistent with national surveillance findings.3

In contrast, the CRS prevention cohort showed a seropositivity rate of only 40.0%, with the lowest levels observed in women aged 25–29 years. The absence of sex-based differences suggests that the low rate reflected both the strict cutoff standard ( <45 IU/mL) and self-selection bias. Women with clear vaccination records may have foregone testing, while those with uncertainties were more likely to participate.9,12 Thus, direct comparison with nationwide data likely underestimates immunity, but it also highlights the presence of a vulnerable subgroup with insufficient protection.

The cutoff level used in the CRS prevention program was set according to the protective threshold of HI 1:16 defined by the Japanese reference method. When converted to international units, this level corresponds to approximately 40–45 IU/mL depending on the commercial reagent; in our institution, the assay used (Access Rubella IgG, Beckman Coulter) defines seronegativity as <45 IU/mL. Clarifying this conversion helps explain the apparent discrepancies across programs that use different assays.

Both the Phase 5 and CRS prevention programs adopt a single additional dose policy for adults identified as seronegative.

The Phase 5 program specifically targets men born between 1962 and 1978, who had little or no opportunity for rubella vaccination during childhood, serving as a catch-up initiative for previously unvaccinated adults.

In contrast, the CRS prevention program focuses on supplementing immunity among women of childbearing age and their partners.

International evidence, including a meta-analysis and a recent longitudinal study from Japan, has demonstrated that while a single dose induces high immunogenicity, two doses can further enhance antibody titers, improve long-term persistence, and reduce the risk of congenital rubella syndrome (CRS).19,20

These findings underscore the immunologic rationale for maintaining robust population immunity; however, Japan’s single-dose approach remains a pragmatic policy tailored to its historical and logistical context.

Continued surveillance of antibody persistence will be essential to ensure sustained protection and to assess whether additional booster strategies may be warranted in the future.

When both groups were analyzed using a unified cutoff of 20 IU/mL, differences largely disappeared. This indicates that seropositivity is highly dependent on threshold settings and that program-specific cutoffs can generate apparent disparities.

Japan’s threshold (40–45 IU/mL, equivalent to HI 1:16) diverges considerably from the WHO standard of 10 IU/mL (HI 1:4).21,22 Japan’s stricter approach reflects a policy emphasis on preventing CRS at all costs, whereas WHO and many Western countries prioritize herd immunity and cross-national comparability, adopting 10 IU/mL as the minimum protective level.21 This fundamental difference in rationale complicates international comparisons and assessments of elimination progress.

Our findings reveal dual immunity gaps–among middle-aged men and young women–while demonstrating how policy design, cutoff definitions, and program participation influence interpretation. These issues are not unique to Japan; similar concerns have been reported regarding residual adult susceptibility in Australia17 and regional disparities across Europe.15,16

From the author’s perspective, these findings should prompt policymakers to reconsider not only the technical aspects of serological testing but also the broader alignment of program objectives. Japan’s experience suggests that fragmented approaches risk undermining public trust and international comparability. Therefore, a coherent national framework that reconciles the protection of vulnerable individuals with global elimination targets is urgently required.

Conclusion

This study highlights how inconsistencies in program design and cutoff standards have substantial consequences for immunity assessment and policy decision-making in rubella prevention. Specifically, it identifies two critical risks: persistent gaps among middle-aged men and unexpected vulnerabilities among young women. While rooted in Japan’s unique policy history, such inconsistencies represent a universal challenge in vaccine program implementation.

Moving forward, rubella elimination strategies must focus on harmonizing cutoff standards, addressing immunity gaps through targeted interventions, and sustaining adult surveillance systems. Japan’s experience underscores the importance of consistency and international alignment in policy design, serving as a vital lesson for the achievement and maintenance of rubella elimination worldwide.

In the author’s view, rubella elimination will not be achieved by technical thresholds alone but by policies that are transparent, consistent, and internationally harmonized. Japan’s lessons underscore that immunization strategies must be continuously adapted, not only to epidemiological data but also to societal behavior and program participation. These reflections may inform future global initiatives aimed at achieving sustainable elimination.

Biography

Masamichi Yoshika, MD, PhD, is the director of Yoshika Clinic in Japan and a board-certified internist with extensive clinical and research experience in hypertension, infectious diseases, and vaccine-preventable diseases. During his tenure at a university hospital, his primary research focused on hypertension and cardiovascular risk management, including the effects of patient characteristics and seasonal variation on blood pressure control. After establishing his own clinic, he broadened his research interests to areas uniquely suited to community-based practice, such as real-world investigations of hypertension management, the clinical epidemiology of infectious diseases in outpatients, and vaccination policy including rubella and COVID-19. He has presented research at academic conferences on discrepancies between home and office blood pressure, the relationship between SARS-CoV-2 PCR cycle threshold values and clinical course, and rubella immunization policy and immunity gaps in Japan, and he is currently preparing several manuscripts for peer-reviewed journals. His current work emphasizes bridging academic medicine and primary care, aiming to generate evidence that improves both individual patient care and public health policy. In addition, he serves as a regional officer of the Japan Medical Association and is a member of several professional societies, including the Japanese Society of Internal Medicine, the Japanese Society of Hypertension, the Japanese Society of Laboratory Medicine, and the Japanese Association for Infectious Diseases.
