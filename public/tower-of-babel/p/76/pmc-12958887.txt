The spleen–brain axis in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias: Integrating immune and metabolic regulation
Authors: Jingsi Yan, Jiayu Zhang, Xinyi Ye, Huihong Wang, Hang Li, Weirong Li, Huifei Wu, Wei Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmerging evidence highlights the central role of peripheral immune–metabolic regulation in the pathogenesis of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). Among peripheral organs, the spleen has gained increasing attention as a critical immune–metabolic hub linking systemic homeostasis to central neurodegeneration. This review systematically elucidates the regulatory functions of the spleen–brain axis from three complementary perspectives: (1) structural remodeling and immunopathological alterations of the spleen in ADRD; (2) spleen‐driven immunometabolic crosstalk, which integrates immune function with iron homeostasis, glucolipid metabolism, and amino acid turnover, contributing to the progression of neurodegenerative pathology; and (3) key molecular pathways mediating immune–metabolic crosstalk, such as Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4)–nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB), NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, high‐mobility group box 1 (HMGB1)–receptor for advanced glycation end‐products (RAGE), and Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription protein (JAK/STAT) signaling. We systematically elucidate how splenic dysfunction drives a positive feedback loop involving systemic inflammation, metabolic disorder, and neuroinflammation. This novel perspective, which views the spleen as an integrated immune–metabolic regulator, highlights spleen‐targeted interventions as a potential therapeutic strategy for ADRD.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) are a group of progressive neurodegenerative disorders that includes Alzheimer's disease (AD), vascular dementia (VaD), Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD), and Lewy body dementia (LBD). According to a Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, the number of people living with dementia worldwide increased from 57.4 million in 2019 to > 60 million in 2023, with a projected incidence of 152 million by 2050. 1 , 2 AD accounts for ≈ 60% to 70% of all dementia cases, with VaD following closely at ≈ 20%. 3 , 4 While age‐standardized prevalence rates appear to be stabilizing or even declining in some high‐income countries, 5 , 6 low‐ and middle‐income countries are experiencing accelerating rates, 7 with the most pronounced increases observed in East Asia. 8 China currently bears the largest burden of ADRD cases globally, with the number of patients increasing by > 300% since 1990. 9

For decades, research on AD followed a brain‐centric paradigm, focusing on the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein within the central nervous system (CNS). 10 , 11 , 12 This approach laid a crucial foundation for elucidating the pathogenesis of AD. Current therapeutics targeting these characteristic pathologies yield suboptimal clinical outcomes, 13 , 14 , 15 leading to a paradigm shift in our understanding of the brain: the peripheral immune system, once thought to be isolated from the brain by the blood–brain barrier (BBB), is now recognized as a pivotal regulator in the pathogenesis of ADRD. 16 , 17 Key evidence supporting this shift includes the observed dysregulation of peripheral immune cell populations in ADRD. Recent studies indicate that, compared to cognitively normal controls, various immune cell types in the blood of ADRD patients—including monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and multiple T lymphocyte subsets—have altered proportions and functional states. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Some immune cells acquire a pro‐inflammatory phenotype, releasing cytokines that activate glial cells and induce neuroinflammation, 22 , 23 while the function of neuroprotective regulatory cell subsets is impaired, further exacerbating neural damage. 24 In addition to enhanced peripheral inflammatory signaling, the clearance of Aβ in the periphery is also compromised. Together, these mechanisms contribute to the progression of ADRD pathology. 25 , 26

The spleen harbors a substantial reservoir of specialized immune cells. 27 , 28 Previous studies have demonstrated that this organ modulates peripheral inflammation, facilitates the migration of immune cells to the brain, and contributes to the clearance of cerebral amyloid deposits, thereby significantly influencing ADRD pathophysiology. 27 , 29 , 30 , 31 Recent studies have further proposed the concept of a brain–spleen axis, highlighting the potential contribution of splenic immune regulation to neurodegenerative processes. 32 However, the molecular and immunometabolic mechanisms underlying this interaction remain incompletely understood.

Therefore, this review (1) focuses on the regulatory role of the spleen–brain axis in ADRD, (2) provides a comprehensive synthesis of splenic pathological alterations and their immunological implications, (3) delineates key molecular mechanisms through which the spleen modulates neuroinflammation and metabolic dysfunction, and (4) integrates current evidence within a neuroimmune–metabolic framework. By bridging immune signaling and systemic metabolic regulation, this review offers a novel perspective on how peripheral homeostatic imbalance contributes to ADRD pathogenesis and highlights the spleen as a potential therapeutic target for modifying disease progression.

THE SPLEEN AS A DYNAMIC IMMUNE RESERVOIR

The spleen, as the largest peripheral immune organ in the human body, serves as a crucial interface between the circulatory and immune systems. 33 Anatomically, it is enclosed by a fibrous capsule that extends trabeculae into the parenchyma, dividing it into two distinct functional compartments: the white pulp and the red pulp. 34 The white pulp consists primarily of periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths (PALS) containing T lymphocytes, neighboring B‐cell follicles, and the surrounding marginal zone, which is essential for antigen trapping and immune cell activation. 28 , 35 The red pulp, comprising splenic cords and sinusoids, functions primarily in filtering blood, removing senescent erythrocytes, and recycling iron. 28 , 36 A unique microanatomical feature is the marginal zone, strategically positioned at the interface between the red and white pulp, 28 which facilitates the initial capture of blood‐borne antigens and serves as a critical site for the initiation of immune responses. 37 The sophisticated immunological functions of the spleen are closely aligned with its complex anatomical structure. As the primary site for systemic immune surveillance, it continuously monitors the blood for pathogens, cellular debris, and abnormal cells, thereby maintaining a dynamic balance among various lymphocyte subsets. 38

Beyond these roles, the spleen's highly vascularized architecture creates a unique microenvironment that fosters immune cell retention, functional maturation, and surveillance, enabling it to function as a dynamic reservoir for multiple key immune cell subsets. 38 , 39 , 40 This reservoir function is particularly critical for innate immune cells such as monocytes and macrophages, as well as for adaptive immune cells like T lymphocytes, which can be rapidly mobilized in response to peripheral challenges such inflammation, infection, or tissue injury. 41 Monocytes are predominantly maintained in a quiescent state within the splenic red pulp and marginal zone. 42 Upon exposure to inflammatory triggers such as pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) 43 these cells are mobilized into the systemic circulation through cytokine‐ and neuro‐mediated signaling pathways, such as C‐C motif chemokine receptor 2 (CCR2), granulocyte‐macrophage colony‐stimulating factor (GM‐CSF), and β‐adrenergic receptor activation. 44 , 45 , 46 Subsequently, monocytes migrate to inflammatory sites, including the brain, 47 , 48 where they differentiate into macrophages or dendritic cells and modulate local immune responses. 49 Similarly, the spleen serves as a critical residential niche for multiple macrophage subsets, which exhibit functional specialization according to their anatomical localization. 50 Metallophilic macrophages and marginal zone macrophages (MZMs) are primarily responsible for capturing blood‐borne antigens and initiating immune responses, 51 while red pulp macrophages contribute to iron recycling and the clearance of apoptotic cells. 36 , 52 Beyond innate immune cells, the spleen also supports the expansion and maintenance of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and memory T cells (TMEMs). 53 , 54 These populations are sustained through their interactions with antigen‐presenting cells and via survival signals provided by the splenic stromal niche. 54 , 55

It is important to emphasize that the spleen's role as a reservoir for immune cells is not one of passive storage; rather, it is dynamically regulated through neuroimmune interactions. 56 Neural signals, particularly those mediated by the sympathetic nervous system, modulate the retention and release of immune cells. 46 , 56 For instance, norepinephrine signaling occurs through β2‐adrenergic receptors on splenic stromal and immune cells, downregulating the expression of adhesion molecules. 57 , 58 This reduction in adhesion decreases the retention of immune cells within the spleen and facilitates their egress into the bloodstream. 40 , 57 , 58 Furthermore, the reservoir function of the spleen is highly adaptive—under chronic inflammatory conditions, it can undergo expansion (splenomegaly) to accommodate more immune cells, thereby enhancing its capacity to modulate systemic immunity. 59 The spleen's ability to modulate systemic immune responses, influence inflammatory mediator production, and serve as a reservoir for immune cells positions it as a potentially significant contributor to peripheral immune processes that may impact neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration (Figure 1).

STRUCTURAL REMODELING OF THE SPLEEN IN ADRD

Pathological morphological alterations in the spleen have been observed across various animal models. In aged amyloid precursor protein/presenilin 1/tau (APP/PS1/Tau) mice, researchers observed pronounced splenomegaly with the destruction of white pulp follicles and reduced B lymphocyte numbers, 60 suggesting impairments in the spleen‐mediated adaptive immune response. Furthermore, significant amyloid‐like deposits have been observed in the splenic tissue of triple‐transgenic (3xTg) and 5xFAD‐transgenic mouse models (expressing five familial AD mutations in APP and PSEN1), with notable accumulation in the red pulp and marginal zone; 61 these may be closely associated with splenomegaly and the structural degeneration of the spleen. 61 , 62 It should be noted, however, that not all AD models or even all individuals within a model exhibit these phenotypes to the same extent. 61 , 63 Such heterogeneity suggests that genetic drivers and disease stage collectively shape the peripheral immune phenotype.

Aging is not only a critical risk factor for ADRD but also a major contributor to pathological alterations in the splenic vascular system and stromal microenvironment. Single‐cell RNA sequencing has revealed that the spleens of aged mouse models exhibit a loss of vascular developmental functions, heightened inflammatory signaling, and distinct endothelial–immune cell interactions. 64 These findings are further corroborated by high‐resolution imaging observations showing substantial vascular wear and remodeling in the spleen with advancing age. 65 Concurrently, the splenic stromal niche undergoes structural and functional decline with advancing age, characterized by an increased production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and impaired support for immune cell homeostasis. 64 , 66 , 67 Recent studies have documented transcriptomic and immunophenotypic alterations in the 3xTg‐AD mouse model, characterized by an upregulation of genes associated with cell migration, adhesion, and inflammation—changes that may contribute to sinusoidal congestion and microvascular rarefaction in the spleen. 68 These age‐related structural changes are likely to compromise critical functions such as antigen transport, cellular recirculation, and the maintenance of an immunoregulatory microenvironment. 69 , 70

Functional assessments via splenectomy have provided important experimental evidence relevant to potential causal links between splenic pathology and ADRD, although such evidence remains largely derived from animal models. In APP/PS1 mouse models, splenectomy significantly reduced the number of circulating Ly6Chi monocytes compared to sham‐operated controls, thereby diminishing the abundance of plaque‐associated macrophages of definitive hematopoietic origin. 71 This reduction, in turn, exacerbated cerebral amyloid plaque burden and elevated circulating Aβ levels, ultimately accelerating both Aβ deposition and cognitive decline. 72 , 73 Concurrently, studies have demonstrated that splenectomy in aging Sprague Dawley rats leads to transient cognitive decline and triggers the activation of astrocytes, resulting in the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines. 74 However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given species‐specific differences in immune regulation, the timing of splenectomy relative to disease stage, and the potential for compensatory immune mechanisms after splenic removal.

Taken together, these studies support a contributory role for the spleen in peripheral Aβ handling and systemic immune homeostasis, which may indirectly influence CNS pathology. However, the available evidence does not yet establish definitive causality, particularly with respect to human ADRD.

THE SPLEEN–BRAIN IMMUNOMETABOLIC AXIS: A DRIVER OF ADRD

The spleen functions as a critical immunometabolic organ, integrating systemic immune surveillance with metabolic regulation. 39 , 40 In ADRD, splenic dysfunction is characterized by alterations in immune cell composition, chronic inflammatory activation, and systemic metabolic disturbances. 75 , 76 These dysregulated processes contribute not only to systemic immune imbalance but also to metabolic stress, oxidative injury, and energy failure within the brain, thereby accelerating cognitive decline and neurodegeneration. 77 It is widely recognized that the immunometabolic activities of the spleen do not occur in isolation but rather represent two tightly interconnected axes of the spleen–brain interaction: immune activation drives metabolic reprogramming, and metabolic disturbances in turn shape immune phenotypes. 78 , 79 This section aims to elucidate how the spleen contributes to the advancement of ADRD by fostering a cycle of immune activation and metabolic reprogramming within the spleen–brain axis (Figure 2).

Myeloid cell alterations and peripheral inflammatory amplification

In ADRD, significant alterations occur within the splenic myeloid compartment. In the APP/PS1 mouse model, pro‐inflammatory Ly6Chi monocyte levels are increased in both the brain and the spleen. 71 , 73 These cells migrate to the CNS through the CCL2–CCR2 signaling axis, 80 where they accumulate around vascular amyloid deposits. 81 After Aβ immunotherapy, their clustering becomes even more prominent. 82 As a key reservoir for Ly6Chi monocytes, the spleen plays a crucial role in their maintenance, 83 indicating dual roles in inflammatory amplification and potential clearance. Splenectomy reduces the availability of these cells and increases the risk of Aβ deposition, 31 underscoring their importance in peripheral Aβ clearance and recruitment to the brain.

Moreover, elevated plasma Aβ levels modulate splenic monocyte and macrophage populations in a stage‐dependent manner. In the early stage of AD, high levels of Aβ1‐42 suppress the frequency and activity of peripheral pro‐inflammatory macrophages, splenic myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSCs); granulocyte–monocyte progenitors (GMPs) in the bone marrow; and inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)‐6, indicative of an immunosuppressive state. 84 , 85 , 86 As the disease progresses, sustained high Aβ1‐42 levels promote the expansion of these cellular populations and inflammatory mediators—including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α)—along with enhanced infiltration of bone marrow‐derived macrophages (BMDMs) into the brain, shifting the systemic and central immune milieu toward a pro‐inflammatory phenotype. 84 , 85 Consequently, splenic macrophages in AD models adopt a more inflammatory profile, characterized by increased CD86 and decreased CD206 expression, a transformation that parallels changes observed in brain microglia. 84 , 87

These alterations extend beyond AD. In cerebral small‐vessel disease (CSVD), a key contributor to VaD, elevated proportions of monocytes and neutrophils in peripheral blood are significantly correlated with increased white‐matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume. 88 This suggests that the expansion of pro‐inflammatory monocyte subsets may contribute to the pathogenesis of VaD. Monocyte trafficking via adhesion molecules such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM‐1) further facilitates leukocyte infiltration into perivascular spaces. 89 Conversely, the loss of reparative non‐classical monocytes aggravates microinfarcts and cognitive impairment. 90 Similar pathogenic dynamics occur in LBD and PDD, in which α‐synuclein‐induced monocyte activation drives chronic neuroinflammation. 91 , 92 Clinical studies demonstrate that α‐synuclein–induced microglial activation and infiltrating monocytes drive chronic neuroinflammation. 93 This process of cerebral inflammation in PDD closely correlates with the activation state of peripheral monocytes, 94 thereby facilitating α‐synuclein aggregation and region‐specific neuronal damage. Animal models further show that peripheral administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exacerbates α‐synuclein–dependent central inflammation and neural injury. 95 These findings delineate a feed‐forward loop linking splenic myeloid activation, peripheral inflammation, and neurodegenerative progression.

Lymphocyte dysfunction and autoantibody responses

Lymphocyte dysregulation in the spleen represents another critical dimension of ADRD pathophysiology. Single‐cell RNA sequencing analysis revealed a significant reduction in circulating and splenic B‐cell populations in AD patients, correlated with cognitive severity. 96 , 97 Parallel findings in transgenic mice confirm that B‐cell deficiency accelerates amyloid plaque accumulation and cognitive decline. 96 , 98 Contradictorily, B‐cell depletion in some contexts mitigates neuroinflammation and Aβ pathology, 99 suggesting the functional heterogeneity of B‐cell subsets. Anti‐inflammatory B cells may exert neuroprotective effects through IL‐35 and IL‐10 secretion, 98 , 100 whereas pro‐inflammatory B‐cell subsets promote CNS inflammation via cytokine release and antibody‐mediated mechanisms. 101 In α‐synucleinopathies such as LBD, altered humoral immunity is evident. Patients exhibit disease‐specific autoantibody profiles against α‐synuclein, characterized by elevated immunoglobulin (Ig)G and decreased IgM levels. 102 This shift likely reflects a loss of immune tolerance and a chronic autoimmune state. 103 In both AD and LBD, impaired B‐cell–mediated immune surveillance may facilitate the accumulation of misfolded proteins, thereby bridging systemic immune dysregulation and neurodegenerative pathology.

In the spleen, T cells undergo activation and polarization before migrating into the CNS through the BBB or blood–cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB). 104 , 105 , 106 Different T cell subsets exhibit differential migratory properties. Under non‐inflammatory conditions, Th1* and Th1 cells exhibit preferential transmigration across the BBB, whereas under inflammatory conditions, all Th subsets demonstrate comparable migration rates. 107 , 108 The BCSFB is more restrictive, with reduced trafficking of all Th subsets compared to the BBB. 107 Under inflammatory conditions, the upregulation of adhesion molecules (ICAM‐1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1) and chemokine axes facilitates their transmigration. 109 , 110 Sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P) and its receptor S1PR1 regulate their egress from the spleen to the bloodstream, 111 providing an essential prerequisite for their subsequent recruitment to the brain.

A polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis has revealed a significant association between AD risk and regulatory genes involved in T cell signaling and activation. 112 CXCR6 is reportedly expressed on brain‐infiltrating CD8+ T cells, where it facilitates their retention within the CNS and enables interactions with plaque‐associated microglia. 113 , 114 Conversely, deficiency in CXCR6 impairs T cell residency, exacerbates pro‐inflammatory responses in glial cells, and aggravates AD pathology. 113 , 114 Immune dysregulation within T cell subsets, such as impaired Treg function and heightened effector T cell activity (Teff), compromises immunosuppressive capacity, sustains inflammatory responses, and exacerbates neural damage. 115 Consistent with this, numerical and functional abnormalities in CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and Tregs have been observed in both the brain tissue and peripheral blood of AD patients. 116 , 117 , 118 Animal studies further demonstrate that enhancing Treg quantity or function attenuates AD pathological progression, whereas the depletion of Tregs exacerbates cognitive impairment and neuroinflammatory responses. 119

Natural killer (NK) cell dysfunction adds another layer of immune impairment. Animal models of AD exhibit a decrease in the proportion of peripheral NK cells as early as the initial disease stages—a decline that is further exacerbated with aging. 120 NK cells are primarily divided into two major subsets: immunoregulatory CD56bright populations and cytotoxic CD56dim populations. 121 Although the total number of NK cells and the prevalence of the CD56bright subset in the peripheral blood of AD patients are comparable to those in healthy controls, some studies have reported impaired migratory capacity in these cells, suggesting functional compromise. 122 , 123 This functional decline may lead to dysregulated inflammation, the accumulation of abnormal proteins, and weakened immune surveillance, thereby collectively accelerating the pathological progression of AD. 124 The mechanistic contributions of NK cells to ADRD pathogenesis await further investigation.

Metabolic reprogramming in spleen–brain crosstalk

Iron homeostasis and redox imbalance

The spleen's red‐pulp macrophages (RPMs) serve as essential mediators of systemic iron recycling through erythrophagocytosis. 36 After the degradation of senescent erythrocytes, iron released from heme is exported via ferroportin (Fpn1) under the regulation of the hepcidin–ferritin axis. 125 , 126 Fpn1 expression results in iron sequestration within macrophages, systemic iron overload, and oxidative stress. 127 , 128

In ADRD, disrupted splenic iron metabolism has dual consequences. On the one hand, systemic iron overload increases redox‐active iron pools, catalyzing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production through Fenton chemistry. 129 These ROS trigger lipid peroxidation and DNA damage, contributing to ferroptosis—an iron‐dependent form of neuronal death implicated in AD. 130 , 131 On the other hand, iron output deficiency reduces systemic iron availability, leading to decreased transferrin saturation and impaired iron transport. This systemic dyshomeostasis, in turn, disrupts cerebral iron metabolism and promotes pathological iron deposition in brain regions such as the hippocampus, cortex, and basal ganglia. 132 , 133 , 134 Both iron excess and iron deficiency disrupt mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation, inducing AMP‐activated protein kinase activation and autophagy. 135 , 136 Studies using APP/PS1 transgenic mice have demonstrated that systemic iron overload accelerates Aβ plaque formation, whereas iron chelation therapy reduces plaque burden and improves cognitive deficits. 137 Similarly, in tauopathy models, excess iron exacerbates tau hyperphosphorylation and aggregation through oxidative stress pathways. 138 Thus, splenic iron dysregulation constitutes a peripheral driver of oxidative and metabolic stress in ADRD.

Glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity

The spleen primarily modulates systemic glucose metabolism via immunometabolic pathways. Pro‐inflammatory cytokine release from splenic immune cells interferes with insulin receptor signaling in peripheral tissues, inducing insulin resistance. 139 , 140 Conversely, insulin signaling directly regulates immune cell metabolism, differentiation, and effector functions, highlighting the bidirectional interaction between the metabolic and immune systems. 140 Clinical and experimental studies demonstrate that splenic immune cells directly take up glucose, and that their metabolic reprogramming induced by interventions like Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccination significantly reduces systemic glucose levels, even without insulin secretion. 141 , 142

Impaired cerebral glucose use is one of the early pathological hallmarks of ADRD. In AD, fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG PET) consistently demonstrates hypometabolism in the hippocampus, a change that closely correlates with memory decline. 143 , 144 Similar metabolic deficits are observed in VaD, particularly in regions affected by small‐vessel pathology and white‐matter damage. 145 Patients with LBD and PDD likewise exhibit pronounced cerebral hypometabolism, most prominently in the posterior cingulate and parietal regions. 146 Importantly, when AD pathology coexists with LBD or PDD, these metabolic abnormalities are exacerbated, underscoring shared and potentially synergistic mechanisms linking impaired glucose metabolism to cognitive decline in ADRD. 147 , 148 Peripheral insulin resistance, modeled in APP/PS1 mice, accelerates both cognitive decline and Aβ accumulation. 149 , 150 These findings highlight the spleen as a critical interface between immune regulation and metabolic stability, thereby linking splenic immunometabolic activity to the exacerbation of neurodegeneration via peripheral insulin resistance.

Lipid and cholesterol metabolism

Splenic macrophages and dendritic cells are actively involved in lipoprotein clearance and cholesterol transport, processes vital for systemic plasma lipid homeostasis. 151 Research has identified two primary pathways through which splenic macrophages modulate lipid and cholesterol metabolism. First, these cells uptake and degrade low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) and its oxidized derivatives, thereby regulating cholesterol acquisition and efflux to maintain cellular cholesterol homeostasis. 151 Second, via receptors such as CD36 and scavenger receptor class B type I (SR‐BI), macrophages facilitate the uptake and clearance of lipoproteins and promote cholesterol transfer to high‐density lipoprotein (HDL), a key step in reverse cholesterol transport. 152 , 153 Concurrently, liver X receptor (LXR) signaling coordinates fatty acid and cholesterol metabolism in dendritic cells, linking lipid flux to immune activation. 154 , 155

Dyslipidemia, a known risk factor for ADRD, exacerbates Aβ accumulation, tau pathology, and BBB damage. 156 , 157 Multimodality imaging and single‐plaque‐level analyses have revealed the co‐localization of specific lipid metabolites with Aβ plaques, a phenomenon observed in both post mortem AD brain tissues and animal models. 158 , 159 Represented by gangliosides, this substance, on the one hand, can form a complex with Aβ monomers (GAβ) that acts as a “seed” for Aβ fibrillization, promoting the harmful aggregation and fibrillation of Aβ in environments such as neuronal membranes or lipid rafts; 160 on the other hand, it can co‐assemble with Aβ fibrils, influencing their structure and growth rate, a process that exhibits subtype specificity. 160 , 161 Thus, disturbances in splenic lipid metabolism via impaired macrophage clearance or altered cholesterol transport may indirectly promote amyloidogenic cascades and vascular dysfunction in ADRD.

Amino acid and tryptophan pathways

The metabolism of amino acids, such as tryptophan, plays a significant role in the pathophysiology of ADRD. Tryptophan metabolism, governed by the serotonin and kynurenine pathways, is critically influenced by splenic immune activation. 162 , 163 Under chronic inflammation, indoleamine 2,3‐dioxygenase (IDO) expressed in splenic macrophages and dendritic cells diverts tryptophan toward kynurenine production. 162 , 164 Alterations in this metabolic axis are consistently observed in ADRD patients, including reduced serotonin availability and an elevated kynurenine‐to‐tryptophan ratio—a symbol of chronic inflammation and sustained activation of IDO. 162 , 164 Elevated levels of quinolinic acid, a neurotoxic kynurenine pathway metabolite, have been documented in the brains and cerebrospinal fluid of AD patients. 163 , 165 Quinolinic acid acts as an agonist of the NMDA receptor, promoting excitotoxic neuronal damage and facilitating the aggregation of Aβ. 166 IDO‐driven metabolic reprogramming also biases T cell differentiation toward pro‐inflammatory phenotypes. 167 , 168 Pharmacological inhibition of IDO reduces neuroinflammation and rescues cognition in AD models, implicating splenic tryptophan catabolism as a peripheral contributor to central neurotoxicity. 144

Other amino acid pathways, including glutamate, arginine, and methionine metabolism, are also perturbed in ADRD. Dysfunctional glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) disrupts the glutamate–glutamine–GABA cycle in PDD and LBD, 169 , 170 while impaired arginine and methionine metabolism exacerbates oxidative stress and nitric oxide signaling deficits. 171 , 172

The role of spleen‐derived extracellular vesicles in ADRD

In addition to immune cell imbalance, spleen‐derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes and microvesicles, have emerged as key mediators of peripheral–central communication in ADRD. Splenic macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes actively release EVs loaded with bioactive cargo such as inflammatory cytokines, microRNAs (miRNAs), and pathological proteins. 173 , 174

EVs are lipid bilayer–enclosed particles capable of carrying proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids. In ADRD, EVs have been shown to encapsulate and transport pathological proteins such as Aβ, tau, and α‐synuclein. Studies demonstrate that Aβ can specifically bind to EVs, facilitating interneuronal transfer and inducing protein misfolding and aggregation in recipient cells, thereby driving neurodegenerative processes. 175 , 176 Experimental evidence further indicates that viral infections, including herpes simplex virus 1, can stimulate the release of phosphorylated tau‐loaded EVs, which are subsequently internalized by recipient neurons, promoting tau aggregation and neurotoxicity. 177 EVs derived from PD patients exhibit elevated levels of α‐synuclein that correlate with disease severity. 178 Peripherally derived EVs, such as those of erythrocyte origin, can cross the BBB and induce dopaminergic neurodegeneration, suggesting that EVs of systemic origin also contribute to CNS pathology. 179 Furthermore, miRNAs associated with EVs have been shown to reprogram microglia, polarizing them toward an M1 phenotype, and to dysregulate their autophagy and inflammatory responses, thereby exacerbating pathological changes in ADRD. 180 , 181

KEY IMMUNOMETABOLIC SIGNALING PATHWAYS LINKING THE SPLEEN AND BRAIN IN ADRD

Through its role in systemic immunity, the spleen is thought to participate in a dynamic bidirectional communication network with the brain, known as the spleen–brain axis. CNS‐derived danger signals—such as Aβ, tau oligomers, and other DAMPs—can enter the circulation and activate peripheral immune cells, potentially including those within the spleen. 182 , 183 In response, splenic‐derived immune cells have been shown to migrate to the brain and produce pro‐inflammatory cytokines, thereby contributing to neuroinflammatory responses. 39 , 184 Beyond its role in immune activation, splenic metabolic reprogramming represents an essential component of this crosstalk. Neurodegeneration has been associated with associated with inflammation reshapes splenic metabolism, leading to alterations in glucose use, lipid turnover, and amino acid catabolism. These metabolic shifts may modulate immune cell polarization and cytokine secretion, thereby potentially linking peripheral energy metabolism with central neuroinflammatory tone; however, the molecular mechanisms that link these changes to CNS pathology remain incompletely defined. Innate immune dysregulation and core signaling pathways

The Toll‐like receptor 4–nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells axis and persistent inflammatory priming

Monocytes and macrophages within peripheral immune compartments, including the spleen, constitute an important component of the innate immune response. In ADRD, these cells exhibit chronic activation, shifting toward a pro‐inflammatory phenotype. 84 , 185 Aβ oligomers act as Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4) agonists, initiating MyD88‐dependent Myddosome assembly on macrophages, 186 , 187 thereby triggering downstream nuclear factor kappa‐light‐chain‐enhancer of activated B cells (NF‐κB)‐mediated inflammatory cascades. Compared to canonical ligands such as LPS, Aβ oligomers trigger slower but more sustained Myddosome formation and signal propagation, leading to chronic low‐grade inflammation 188 —a mechanism that may contribute to the persistent neuroinflammation observed in neurodegenerative disorders.

High‐mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a key DAMP associated with neuronal injury, also binds TLR4 and activates the MyD88–NF‐κB axis, leading to upregulated production of TNF‐α and IL‐6. 189 These cytokines propagate systemic inflammation and increase BBB permeability, thereby exacerbating CNS damage. 190 Pharmacological inhibition of TLR4 or NF‐κB has been shown to reduce peripheral cytokine levels, including those derived from splenic immune cells, and to attenuate microglial activation in APP/PS1 and ischemic models. 191 , 192 However, such interventions are not organ restricted and may also exert direct central effects.

Crosstalk between NF‐κB and mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways further amplifies this inflammatory cascade: p38–MAPK‐dependent histone H3 phosphorylation and phosphoacetylation selectively occur at inflammatory gene promoters. 193 This epigenetic remodeling enhances the accessibility of NF‐κB binding sites within these promoters, thereby facilitating the recruitment of NF‐κB and driving high‐level expression of inflammatory genes. 194

NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 inflammasome activation and peripheral–central crosstalk

The NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome is a cytosolic multiprotein complex that can be activated by aggregated Aβ, tau protein aggregates, α‐synuclein aggregation, or mitochondrial ROS. 195 Within peripheral immune compartments, including splenic macrophages, NLRP3 activation promotes caspase‐1 cleavage, leading to the secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β and IL‐18. 195 Once systemically released, these cytokines act on the CNS, amplifying the pre‐activated state of microglia and exacerbating synaptic dysfunction. 196 They also promote Aβ deposition and tau pathology, forming a vicious cycle that aggravates ADRD pathology. 197

Notably, the TLR4–NF‐κB signaling pathway provides a “priming signal” for NLRP3 activation, while DAMPs serve as a “trigger signal.” Specifically, TLR4–NF‐κB activation first promotes the transcriptional expression of NLRP3 and precursor IL‐1β, among other inflammation‐related genes, thereby enabling NLRP3 inflammasome activation. 198 Subsequently, DAMPs released during cellular damage or stress act as a “second signal” that directly induces NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and activation. 198 This two‐step model illustrates how peripheral innate immune cells, including those within the spleen, may contribute to systemic inflammatory signaling capable of influencing CNS pathology.

In 5xFAD mice, increased NLRP3 activation has been observed in peripheral immune compartments and has been reported to parallel the progression of brain pathology, with IL‐1β implicated as a potential mediator of peripheral–central immune crosstalk. 199 However, given the widespread expression of NLRP3 across multiple peripheral organs as well as within the CNS, splenic involvement in these studies is largely inferred rather than directly demonstrated. Moreover, pharmacological inhibitors such as MCC950 are not spleen restricted and may modulate inflammasome activity in other peripheral tissues or exert direct central effects. Accordingly, while inhibition of NLRP3 with MCC950 has been shown to restore peripheral cytokine balance and reduce white matter injury in vascular dementia models, 200 definitive attribution of these effects to splenic NLRP3 signaling remains a limitation of the current evidence.

The HMGB1–receptor for advanced glycation end‐products axis as a peripheral amplifier

HMGB1 released from damaged neurons can enter the circulation and engage receptor for advanced glycation end‐products (RAGE) receptors on peripheral immune cells, including macrophages and dendritic cells within the spleen and other secondary lymphoid organs, 201 , 202 activating ERK1/2 and NF‐κB pathways that promote robust cytokine secretion. 203 Elevated HMGB1 levels in both AD patients and mouse models have been associated with systemic inflammatory responses and alterations in peripheral immune organs, including splenic enlargement. 204 , 205 RAGE blockade reduces cytokine release, neuroinflammation, and cognitive impairment in APP/PS1 mice. 206 However, these effects are not restricted to the spleen and may involve multiple peripheral compartments as well as direct central actions. Together, HMGB1–RAGE and TLR4–NF‐κB signaling operating across peripheral immune compartments, including the spleen, may form a feed‐forward loop that sustains NLRP3 activation and chronic systemic inflammation, establishing a self‐reinforcing inflammatory circuit linking peripheral immunity and central neurodegeneration.

Immunometabolic signaling pathways and inflammatory amplification

Emerging evidence suggests that immunometabolic reprogramming of peripheral immune cells, including those residing in the spleen, modulates inflammatory output and systemic metabolic homeostasis. Dysregulation of several immunometabolic signaling pathways has been linked to ADRD pathogenesis, thereby positioning the spleen as a potential regulatory node within peripheral–central immune crosstalk.

Iron overload, ROS, and inflammasome activation

Systemic iron overload leads to iron accumulation within splenic red pulp macrophages, generating excessive ROS. 129 ROS act as key signaling molecules that trigger inflammasome activation through three primary mechanisms. (1) Mitochondrial damage releases mtROS and mtDNA, which serve as DAMPs to activate NLRP3 and absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2). 207 (2) Lysosomal iron accumulation increases membrane permeability, promoting cathepsin B release and subsequent NLRP3 assembly. 208 (3) Iron load facilitates ATP release, which activates the P2 × 7 receptor, induces K+ efflux, and activates NLRP3. 209 Upon activation, NLRP3/AIM2 associates with the adaptor protein apoptosis‐associated speck‐like protein containing a CARD to recruit and activate caspase‐1, forming the canonical inflammasome complex. 210 Activated caspase‐1 then cleaves pro‐IL‐1β and pro‐IL‐18 into their mature forms. 210 These potent cytokines can either cross the BBB or activate cerebral endothelial/glial cells, exacerbating microglial hyperactivation, neuroinflammation, and, ultimately, neuronal damage. 211 , 212

Insulin resistance and NF‐κB crosstalk in splenic inflammation

The insulin‐mediated insulin receptor substrate (IRS)–phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)–AKT pathway is crucial for regulating glucose uptake and maintaining cellular metabolic homeostasis. Macrophages and T cells within peripheral immune compartments, including the spleen, release a plethora of pro‐inflammatory cytokines that promote the serine/threonine phosphorylation of IRS proteins, 213 which impedes their binding to the insulin receptor, suppresses downstream signaling transduction, and exacerbates systemic insulin resistance. 214 , 215 Under these conditions, inflammatory signaling pathways, such as TLR4, are activated in peripheral myeloid cells, leading to the upregulation of both the NF‐κB and MAPK pathways and a consequent enhancement of inflammatory cytokine expression, thereby establishing a vicious cycle between metabolic dysfunction and chronic inflammation. 215 , 216 , 217 NF‐κB activation upregulates the expression of beta‐secretase 1 and other Aβ‐producing enzymes, facilitating Aβ aggregation and deposition. 218 , 219 Concurrently, impaired insulin signaling reduces PI3K–Akt pathway activation, resulting in reduced inhibitory phosphorylation of glycogen synthase kinase‐3β (GSK‐3β) and subsequent induction of tau hyperphosphorylation. 220 The co‐assembled delivery of insulin using guanidinium‐modified calixarene and cyclodextrin co‐assembly (GCD) has been demonstrated to significantly mitigate aberrant neuronal apoptosis and synaptic damage in 5xFAD mice, leading to an amelioration of features associated with metabolic dysregulation. 221

Lipid and cholesterol dysregulation in the spleen–brain axis

The accumulation of excess cholesterol and lipid metabolites within peripheral myeloid cells, including those residing in the spleen, can lead to the formation and deposition of cholesterol crystals in the tissue, 222 which are subsequently phagocytosed by macrophages and other immune cells, where they are recognized as DAMPs, initiating the innate immune response. 223

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4, the strongest genetic risk factor for late‐onset AD, disrupts lipid trafficking and Aβ clearance by reducing cholesterol transport and lipidation efficiency. 224 , 225 , 226 A key regulator of apoE lipidation is the ATP‐binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1). Impaired ABCA1 function exacerbates APOE ε4 pathology by further reducing its lipidation, increasing its propensity to aggregate, and intensifying both cholesterol metabolic dysfunction and defective Aβ clearance. 227 , 228 Pharmacological activation of ABCA1 has been shown to restore systemic lipid homeostasis and attenuate neurodegenerative pathology, 229 although the relative contributions of specific peripheral immune compartments, including the spleen, remain to be fully delineated.

The tryptophan–kynurenine pathway and neuroimmune excitotoxicity

The immunometabolic regulation of tryptophan catabolism provides another mechanistic link. The inflammatory activation of IDO in peripheral immune cells, including macrophages residing in the spleen, diverts tryptophan toward the kynurenine pathway. 230 , 231 Kynurenine metabolites, including quinolinic acid, activate aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling, promoting Th17 polarization and neurotoxic responses. 232 , 233 Large‐scale metabolomics studies revealed significantly elevated kynurenine‐to‐tryptophan (Kyn/Trp) ratios in patients with AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) compared to healthy controls, and these progressively increased with disease severity. 234 An elevated Kyn/Trp ratio correlates with declines in cognitive domains such as memory and executive function and is linked to hallmark AD pathological changes such as cerebral gray‐matter atrophy, as well as Aβ and tau deposition. 165 , 235

Adaptive immune remodeling and regulatory checkpoints

Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription protein–driven T‐cell polarization and effector amplification

Evidence from human and animal studies indicates that ADRD is associated with a shift toward pro‐inflammatory Th1 and Th17 cell populations, accompanied by reduced Foxp3+ regulatory T cells. 236 , 237 This imbalance sustains systemic inflammation and facilitates the migration of leukocytes into the CNS. Central to this process is the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription protein (JAK/STAT) pathway. STAT3 activation in peripheral CD4+ T cells, including those residing in the spleen, and drives Th17 differentiation and the secretion of IL‐17A, which disrupts the BBB and exacerbates microglial activation. 238 , 239 Conversely, STAT5 signaling promotes the homeostasis of Tregs. 240 However, the contribution of this pathway to ADRD pathogenesis remains incompletely defined. Pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 has been shown to restore Treg function and ameliorate memory deficits in AD models, highlighting the therapeutic potential of modulating JAK/STAT signaling within peripheral immune compartments, including the spleen. 241

Programmed cell death 1/programmed death ligand 1 checkpoint imbalance

The programmed cell death 1 (PD‐1)/programmed death ligand 1 (PD‐L1) immune checkpoint axis plays a critical role in regulating T cell activation and tolerance. Studies have revealed upregulated PD‐L1 expression in both peripheral T cells and brain tissues of AD patients. 242 However, the therapeutic blockade of PD‐1 has yielded paradoxical effects: while it enhances T‐cell infiltration into the CNS and facilitates amyloid clearance, excessive immune activation may conversely foster an immunosuppressive microenvironment. 243 This environment could ultimately impair inflammatory responses and hinder the clearance of Aβ. 243 Given that the spleen represents a major site of peripheral T cell priming and immune checkpoint regulation, these findings highlight the complexity of PD‐1/PD‐L1 modulation within the spleen–brain axis, rather than establishing a spleen‐restricted mechanism. The adaptive immune dysregulation observed in ADRD is not independent but interconnected. STAT3‐driven Th17 polarization promotes IL‐17 release, which synergizes with PD‐1 resistance to sustain effector activity. Such dysregulated T cell responses within peripheral immune compartments, including the spleen, sustain peripheral immune activation, lower the threshold for CNS infiltration, and exacerbate neurodegeneration.

Cytokine and chemokine networks in peripheral–central communication

Cytokines and chemokines mediate peripheral–central immune communication, with the spleen representing an important reservoir of responsive immune cells. The CCR2–CCL2 axis mobilizes CCR2+ monocytes from peripheral compartments, including the spleen, into the circulation, promoting their recruitment into the brain and differentiation into pro‐inflammatory macrophages, thus amplifying microglial activation. 244 A similar monocyte outflow is observed in models of VaD induced by chronic cerebral hypoperfusion. 245 Genetic knockout or pharmacological inhibition of CCR2 attenuates neuroinflammation and preserves cognitive function in both AD and VaD mouse models. 244 However, these interventions do not selectively target splenic monocytes, and contributions from other peripheral sources cannot be excluded. CX3CL1–CX3CR1 signaling also regulates peripheral monocyte responses. In APP/PS1 models, impaired CX3CL1–CX3CR1 signaling leads to microglial dysfunction, exacerbates neuroinflammatory responses, and may disrupt the migration and brain infiltration of peripheral monocytes. 246 , 247 Furthermore, in models of α‐synucleinopathies, altered CX3CR1 expression on macrophages, including those residing in the spleen, promotes their pathological recruitment into the CNS. 248 These findings suggest that chemokine‐driven migratory pathways may provide a mechanistic basis through which spleen‐associated immune cells contribute to neuroinflammatory processes.

Cytokines produced by peripheral immune organs, with the spleen representing an important source, constitute a key effector component of spleen–brain immune communication. Evidence indicates that systemic levels of IL‐6 and TNF‐α are elevated in both AD patients and animal models. 249 IL‐6 drives Th17 polarization via activation of the JAK/STAT3 pathway, 250 while TNF‐α stimulates the NF‐κB pathway in both peripheral and central immune cells. 251 In models of VaD, persistently elevated IL‐6 and TNF‐α levels sustain systemic inflammation and contribute to progressive white‐matter injury. 252 Similarly, interferon gamma produced by peripheral T cells, including splenic T‐cell populations, has been shown to enhance microglial activation in the context of LBD and PDD. 253

CONCLUSIONS

ADRD is increasingly recognized as a group of systemic disorders driven by the interplay between peripheral immune and metabolic dysregulation. The spleen, as the largest secondary lymphoid and metabolic regulatory organ, emerges as a central hub integrating these systemic signals. Structural remodeling, chronic activation of myeloid and lymphoid populations, and the consequent cytokine imbalance contribute to sustained neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier disruption, and neuronal injury. Parallel to its immunological functions, the spleen also orchestrates systemic metabolic homeostasis through the regulation of iron storage, glucose use, and lipid and amino acid metabolism. Aberrations in these processes promote oxidative stress, inflammasome activation, and insulin resistance, thereby reinforcing immune activation and accelerating neurodegenerative cascades.

Molecular evidence indicates that key signaling pathways, including TLR4–NF‐κB, NLRP3, HMGB1–RAGE, and JAK/STAT, form a convergent network linking immune and metabolic dysfunction in the spleen to central pathology. This integrated immune–metabolic network provides a mechanistic basis for understanding how peripheral disturbances propagate to the CNS and amplify ADRD progression. Current evidence suggests that the spleen may interact with peripheral lymphatic pathways to influence the clearance of CNS waste in ADRD, though research in this area remains limited and is therefore not elaborated on in this review. Potential interactions between the spleen and other peripheral organs in ADRD remain poorly characterized, with only indirect evidence implicating the gut or other tissues in spleen–central immune communication. Accordingly, the possibility of coordinated inter‐organ interactions and pathogenic relevance of a coordinated axis should be considered speculative. Future studies using lymphatic imaging, immunometabolic profiling, and targeted modulation of splenic function are warranted to clarify the spleen's role within the broader peripheral–central communication network.
