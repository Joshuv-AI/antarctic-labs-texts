Predicting FOX gene candidates for oxic nitrogen fixation using multi-omic machine learning and comparative bioinformatics
Authors: James Young, Liping Gu, Ruanbao Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nitrogen fixation in oxygenic cyanobacteria depends on a system of genes that protect oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase, many of which likely remain uncharacterized. Here we predict FOX (fixation in the presence of oxygen) gene candidates in Anabaena sp. PCC 7120 by integrating nitrogen step-down RNA-seq (0/6/12/21 hours), quantitative proteomics, promoter architecture, genomic context, and reciprocal-best-hit conservation across diazotrophic and non-diazotrophic cyanobacteria. Using 68 literature-validated FOX genes and 835 conserved non-essential genes as a proxy negative class, we trained logistic regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost models and evaluated them using 20 repeated stratified 80/20 train–test splits. The best models achieved ROC–AUC up to 0.80 and average precision up to 0.55 and precision among the top 20 ranked genes reached 0.39 versus a 0.075 prevalence baseline. Model interpretation highlights late step-down induction, diazotroph-biased conservation, and genomic neighborhood signals as leading predictors. We generated genome-wide FOX probability scores used primarily for candidate ranking, nominating conserved genes spanning heterocyst envelope processes as well as broader redox, metabolism, and electron-pool regulation. We release these predictions and a public web-based optimizer that applies comparative-bioinformatics filters and size constraints to propose candidate accessory-gene complements for experimental testing and heterologous reconstitution efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41873-w.

Introduction

Biological nitrogen fixation (BNF) is central to the global nitrogen cycle and remains a long-standing target for sustainable agriculture1, with renewed interest in self-fertilizing cereal crops through synthetic biology2,3. Modern agricultural productivity is tightly coupled to industrial ammonia production, and the dominant Haber–Bosch method is estimated to consume roughly 5% of global natural gas and ~ 2% of global energy4. Limited recovery of applied nitrogen in agriculture contributes to eutrophication and other water-quality impacts5–9. These coupled energy and environmental costs motivate “BioNitrogen” approaches10 that can supplement or partially replace fossil-derived ammonia using photosynthetic nitrogen fixation (PNF).

The enzyme complex that catalyzes nitrogen fixation, nitrogenase, is inactivated by oxygen, yet many cyanobacteria coordinate both oxygenic photosynthesis and oxygen-sensitive nitrogen fixation with unique strategies1011. Anabaena sp. PCC 7120 (hereafter Anabaena 7120) is one example of this balancing act that develops differentiated cells called heterocysts that provide a micro-oxic environment for nitrogenase11. In Anabaena 7120, aerobic diazotrophic growth requires not only the core nif genes (nitrogenase components) but also a broader set of accessory genes that enable heterocyst development12,13 and its subsequent redox balancing, metalloenzyme production and protection, and metabolite exchange14. Genes whose loss prevents growth on ﻿N₂ under oxic conditions have historically been referred to as FOX genes (fixation in the presence of oxygen)12. In this work, we operationally define strict FOX genes as those whose inactivation prevents growth on N2 under oxic conditions in Anabaena 7120 in published genetic knockout and complementation studies12. We refer to diazotrophy-promoting genes as those that enhance nitrogenase activity, heterocyst physiology, or aerobic diazotrophic robustness but are not strictly required for growth on N2 in the native host. While our models are trained on strict FOX labels (lab validation with knockout and complement), the high pFOX genes are intended as ranked candidates for experimental follow up and may include both strict FOX and diazotrophy-promoting genes.

The differentiation of heterocysts and the onset of aerobic N₂ fixation involves extensive gene expression changes during nitrogen step-down in model strains1315. Some published estimates postulate there are 100–140 total FOX genes specifically required for the fixation of N₂ in the presence of oxygen16,17. Although at least 68 FOX genes have been validated by classical genetic knockouts and complements12, the remaining FOX gene repertoire is incompletely characterized, particularly outside of well-studied heterocyst envelope18 and respiration modules19. The value of discovering more FOX genes from the genetically tractable and heavily investigated Anabaena 7120 is its support of a portable blueprint of oxic N₂ fixation. This blueprint can also provide comparative understanding through bioinformatic approaches like reciprocal-best-hit with less genetically-tractable unicellular diazotrophs like Cyanothece sp. ATCC 51142 (hereafter Cyanothece 51142) which exhibits oxic nitrogen fixation20.

Towards this motivation of a portable blueprint for better nitrogenase function in non-native hosts, it is worth noting that some genes could drive the magnitude of nitrogen fixation but potentially be non-essential for aerobic or oxygenic diazotrophic function in native hosts. Multiple groups have now transplanted 30–34 nif-associated genes from Cyanothece 51142 into the model unicellular photoautotroph Synechocystis PCC 6803 yet achieved activities ≤ 2% of the donor diazotroph in aerobic conditions2122. These efforts, with valuable but mixed outcomes, suggest that essential accessory genes beyond the core nif cluster were not included in the transferred cassettes, underscoring the need to systematically identify the remaining FOX genes that could bridge the performance gap. Identifying those missing pieces is essential for the long-term goal of endowing crop plants or other industrial organisms with self-sufficient nitrogen fixation23.

With the value of this endeavor articulated above, the challenges should also be weighed. Systematically prioritizing new FOX gene candidates is challenging because essentiality may be dependent on growth conditions, which can vary between labs and organisms, and other interacting or redundant genes. These factors are more relevant when the goal is to project gene essentiality for diazotrophic function in an otherwise non-diazotrophic host, like this work aims to be translated to, because the non-diazotrophic target organism may be missing the interacting genes. Another potential factor contributing to FOX gene incompleteness, disregarding diazotrophy-promoting genes that are not essential, is insertion biases in the genetic screens used for their discovery24, a limitation we address further in the Discussion section.

Precisely capturing a negative class for FOX genes is also challenging due to publication bias against negative results. This is a common challenge in supervised learning for gene function prediction where positive examples are available while the negative class must be inferred25. We specifically chose to use 835 conserved non-essential cyanobacterial genes18 as a negative proxy class. The logic supporting these being an appropriate negative class is that these genes are conserved across both diazotrophic and non-diazotrophic cyanobacteria making it less likely they are specialized for nitrogen fixation. Additionally, while the non-essentiality of these genes across organisms under varying conditions fails to absolutely exclude their FOX gene potential, it is still reasonable to interpret these as less likely to be FOX genes based on this evidence relative to other genes. This imperfect but principled labeling scheme is well-suited to supervised machine learning methods that can tolerate label noise when the signal-to-noise ratio remains favorable25,26.

Given these constraints, we evaluated machine learning approaches for FOX gene prediction and ranking. Machine learning approaches, like those used in our paper, have proven effective for predicting gene essentiality in bacteria, with prior studies achieving receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC) values of 0.75–0.85 by integrating network topology, sequence characteristics, expression data, and phylogenetic conservation27,28. For tabular biological datasets with limited sample sizes, tree-based ensemble methods such as Random Forest29 and XGBoost30 historically have outperformed deep neural networks in both prediction accuracy31,32, computational efficiency, and interpretability making them well-suited for our dataset of 68 positive and 835 negative labeled genes. We interpret our tree-based predictions using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values33, which decompose each prediction into additive feature contributions grounded in cooperative game theory, enabling identification of the biological patterns that distinguish FOX genes. These insights extend beyond classification accuracy to reveal which biological signals, including late transcriptional induction, diazotroph-specific conservation, or genomic context, most strongly distinguish FOX genes.

In this study, we integrate nitrogen step-down transcriptomics, quantitative proteomics, transcription-start-site-derived promoter features, genomic context, and reciprocal-best-hit conservation metrics into a unified gene-level feature matrix for Anabaena 7120. We achieve three objectives in this work. First, we train and evaluate supervised classifiers to rank unlabeled genes by their similarity to literature-validated strict FOX genes relative to a proxy negative class. Second, we interpret the learned predictors using feature importance and SHAP analyses to identify biological patterns that distinguish FOX-associated genes. Third, we release genome-wide ranked predictions and a public web application that supports exploration and size-constrained design of candidate accessory-gene complements for experimental testing and heterologous reconstitution efforts. Results

Model performance

All machine learning models meaningfully differentiated known FOX genes from conserved non-essential genes based on ROC-AUC averages. In our 20 repeated stratified 80/20 (train/test) splits, the average ROC-AUC for test sets ranged from ~ 0.75 for the logistic regression up to ~ 0.80 for Random Forest (Table 1). ROC-AUC measures how well a classifier ranks positive examples above negative examples across all decision thresholds, with 0.5 representing random performance and 1.0 representing perfect separation. The tree-based models (Random Forest, XGBoost) showed slightly higher averages in ROC-AUC and average precision relative to logistic regression, likely owing to their ability to capture interactions and non-linear effects by default. The tree-based models also had greater performance in precision in the top 20 and 50 predicted FOX genes on labeled holdout, indicating they have stronger true-positive enrichment among top-ranked predictions. Given the class imbalance (68 FOX genes among 903 labeled genes, or 7.5% prevalence), these precision values demonstrate that all classifiers substantially outperform a random baseline. Throughout this paper, results refer to the “Principled” feature set (RPKM expression, proteomics, promoter architecture, conservation, gene ontology, and genomic position) unless otherwise noted; full comparisons across feature sets appear in Supplementary Table S5. Probability scores (pFOX) represent the arithmetic mean (ensemble model) of predictions from all three individual classifiers in this configuration.

In this work, pFOX denotes the predicted class probability output by a classifier and is intended primarily as a ranking statistic rather than a calibrated estimate of true biological probability. We did not perform post hoc probability calibration, so pFOX values should not be interpreted as literal probabilities that a gene is FOX essential. Several top-ranked candidates have independent experimental support in published genetic studies that were not used to define the training labels, providing an external check on the biological plausibility and validity of the ranked list. Among the predicted FOX candidates, we highlight a small set of hits that possess reciprocal best hits in the unicellular diazotroph Cyanothece 51142 and support the performance of the models through recovery of core nif genes that are not explicitly FOX labeled. One leading candidate from our unknown genes, all1457 (nifS, pFOX = 0.66), which has not been experimentally confirmed as a FOX gene in Anabaena 7120 at this time in our labeled data12 or our searches. Research outside of Anabaena 7120 has mixed results, with nifS being determined to be non-essential for diazotrophy in Anabaena variabilis34 but an amplifier of diazotrophy in Azotobacter vinelandii35 through its role as a cysteine desulfurase. Another conserved diazotroph-associated gene, alr1407 (nifV, pFOX = 0.87) encodes a predicted homocitrate synthase that supports nitrogenase activity42. Prior work found nifV to be non-essential for diazotrophic growth in Anabaena 7120, but its knockout reduced nitrogenase activity by 30–40%36. These rankings illustrate that some high pFOX genes may be strong diazotrophy-promoting candidates that amplify nitrogenase activity or robustness without meeting the strict FOX essentiality definition in the native host.

Additional high-confidence genes merit discussion because they underpin the heterocyst-specific oxygen barrier, respiratory burn-off, and electron transport activities that are important to diazotrophy. The genes all5342 and all5344 (pFOX 0.90 and 0.87) form an uncharacterized gene block embedded in the hgl (heterocyst glycolipid) island37. In Anabaena 7120, the adjacent hglB/C genes are known to generate the head-group essential for the glycolipid envelope that excludes O₂37 and the evidence from that same study strongly suggests these genes are FOX genes but they stop short of specifically labeling them as such. The gene alr0267 (hesF, pFOX = 0.73) has also previously been found necessary for normal growth and is believed to be heterocyst capsule related in Anabaena 712038. Closer to the goal of transferring nitrogen fixation, asr2513 (fdxB, pFOX = 0.84) was seen to be very important for transfer of nitrogenase activity to E. coli39.

The hup element and adjacent genes (all0688, asr0689, asr0690, alr0691, and alr0692) illustrate an important caveat in model interpretation. These genes received moderate pFOX scores (0.36–0.61) in the main model but substantially higher scores (0.77–0.88) when genomic location features were ablated. This example was the most apparent effect of location ablation on genes with diazotrophy-related literature and is consistent with their location in a region whose genomic location context is disrupted during differentiation. These genes reside within or adjacent to the hup gene element, one of three programmed DNA rearrangements that occur late during heterocyst differentiation40. Excision of this element by the site-specific recombinase XisC (alr0677) is required to reconstitute the hupSL operon encoding uptake hydrogenase, which recovers reductant from H₂ produced as a byproduct of nitrogenase activity40. The reaction catalyzed by this enzyme supports ATP synthesis via oxidative phosphorylation in the microoxic heterocyst interior41. Location ablated pFOX is available in both the web-app and code repositories referenced in Data Availability. This example illustrates that coordinate-based predictors can under-rank biologically relevant loci when genomic architecture is rearranged during differentiation, so we recommend consulting both the main and location-ablated ranks when interpreting such regions (both are available in the web application).

Conversely, the model assigns low pFOX to genes that have been experimentally confirmed as non-essential for diazotrophic growth, providing validation of its specificity. The secondary nitrogen regulators NsrM (all0345, pFOX = 0.25) and NsrX (alr1976, pFOX = 0.18) are transcriptionally responsive to nitrogen limitation and modulate heterocyst differentiation genes downstream of FurC and NtcA, yet their deletion mutants grow diazotrophically and display only subtle phenotypes under standard laboratory conditions43. Similarly, the PP2C-type phosphatase PrpJ2 (alr4641, pFOX = 0.02) is dispensable for cell growth under all nitrogen regimens tested, despite being closely related to the heterocyst maturation factor PrpJ144. The basic amino acid transporter Bgt (alr3187, pFOX = 0.01) is likewise dispensable for diazotrophic growth45. Perhaps most interesting, alr4741 knockout, one of our negative class proxy genes, actually grew to higher density relative to the wild-type strain under diazotrophic conditions46. These examples illustrate that nitrogen-responsive expression alone does not guarantee a FOX phenotype, and that the model can meaningfully pick out predictive patterns from the other variables to differentiate and rank FOX gene candidates. Collectively, these low-scoring genes that were independently confirmed as dispensable provide orthogonal validation of model specificity.

Constructing FOX gene complements with rank-order and greedy selection

Having obtained a set of candidate FOX genes from our unlabeled cohort of genes, we constructed a tool to select size-constrained candidate gene sets for downstream engineering. We use a 50 kb coding-sequence payload as a design constraint starting point because it represents nitrogen fixation systems that have been transferred to Synechocystis PCC 6803 and maintained stably22. The 50 kb payload is based on coding-sequence (CDS) nucleotides only and does not include regulatory elements, linkers, or vector backbone. This size also accommodates accessory genes for oxygen protection while remaining within efficient single-construct DNA assembly limits. Larger payloads (100–300 kb and even up to > 1Mbp47) are achievable using BAC vectors and yeast-mediated TAR cloning47,48 but require hierarchical assembly and could face reduced delivery efficiency and stability in cyanobacterial systems.

For a given size limit, we provide two baseline selection strategies consisting of (i) rank-order selection, which adds genes by descending pFOX until the size budget is exhausted, and (ii) greedy selection, which adds genes by descending pFOX-per-length ratio. Because these strategies optimize only for ranking scores and length, they should be interpreted as prioritization heuristics rather than proofs of sufficiency or completeness. To improve interpretability for users, we provide the full definition of each filter and its default value in a new Supplementary Table S4.

We found that these two approaches produced substantially different gene lists. The rank-order selection yielded a smaller set of genes because it tended to include some large genes with higher individual probabilities. The relatively small overlapping set of genes underscores that there may be multiple solutions to assembling a FOX or diazotrophy-promoting complement. It is also reasonable that different multi-gene solutions could achieve similar diazotrophic robustness levels for different reasons. Users might focus on the most certain crucial players with the rank method or try to maximize breadth by including many small candidates in the greedy optimization method. Alternatively, users may take a hybrid approach using context and intuition with predicted probabilities as a guide.

The app visualizes the functional makeup of each complement by creating word clouds of gene annotation terms as seen in Fig. 3. In the example shown, the rank-order set’s word cloud is dominated by terms such as ferredoxin, glycosyltransferase, heterocyst, nitrogenase-associated annotations, and uncharacterized proteins, consistent with enrichment for redox/electron-transfer and envelope-associated functions alongside diazotrophy machinery. The greedy complement, in contrast, highlights additional photosynthetic electron transport and regulatory terms, most prominently cytochrome and b6 complex components, reflecting its broader inclusion of shorter candidates prioritized by pFOX-per-length.

The greedy complement achieved a higher cumulative pFOX score (sum of pFOX values across included genes) compared to the rank-order complement, by design. However, it may also include more genes of uncertain necessity, which could be redundant or non-essential. The rank-order complement, though smaller and more focused on the highest-ranked candidates, may be a more practical starting point for experimental testing. It is important to note that these probabilities are not explicitly calibrated, and the sum of pFOX scores do not guarantee a specific number of FOX hits. We use this sum only as a heuristic ranking summary. Our complements are meant to supplement the known nif regulon used in previous work22, so we assume the biological engineer is already including the nif cluster in any transfer.

Interpreting biological significance

Having established tools for candidate selection, we next examined which biological features most strongly distinguish FOX genes from the negative control set. Across models, the most informative features reflected nitrogen step-down response at the transcript level, genomic context like islands of known FOX genes, promoter architecture, and conservation clues. In our main models, the genomic-coordinate predictor (chromosome_region_end) ranked highly, consistent with the known clustering of Anabaena 7120 diazotrophy-related loci. To address concerns that coordinate features might act as positional proxies if labeled FOX genes are concentrated in well-studied genomic islands, we performed an ablation analysis in which we trained a model with the same configuration as our main models but excluded coordinate predictors and repeated evaluation (Supplementary Table S5). For location-blocked cross-validation, genes were ordered by chromosome_region_start coordinate and partitioned into n_groups = 5 contiguous blocks. Block boundaries were shifted n_partitions = 4 times using evenly-spaced offsets across the genome, yielding 20 total holdout configurations. This design tests whether predictive signal generalizes across genomic regions rather than relying on local clustering of labeled genes. (Supplementary Table S6).

Our model-driven identification of genomic location as a feature that contributes, but does not dominate model importance (Table 3), suggests that FOX genes are not randomly scattered. Many appear in genomic clusters or islands, some corresponding to known gene clusters like the het and hep gene clusters for heterocyst envelope polysaccharide and glycolipid synthesis, and perhaps others not previously recognized as clusters. There is also the hupL cluster of interest, which was given higher pFOX in the model that excluded genomic location, providing some motivation for exploring the predictions of both modeling approaches when searching for novel FOX genes.

The strong predictive power of transcriptomic features confirms that FOX genes are tightly regulated at the mRNA level in response to nitrogen availability. Many FOX candidates remained undetected at the protein level in short time frames, which may explain why the model picked up on transcription signal more than the proteomics signal. This agrees with prior research, highlighting the importance of regulatory networks controlled by NtcA and HetR49 in orchestrating FOX gene expression, and suggests the value in future work to map the promoters and transcription factors controlling the top new candidates.

Sequence homology features further distinguished FOX genes from housekeeping genes. The presence of reciprocal-best-hits (RBH) to non-diazotrophic organisms (has_nondiazo_hit, nondiazo_mean_pident) negatively predicted FOX gene status, while genes showing greater sequence identity to diazotrophs than to non-diazotrophs (diazo_minus_nondiazo_mean_pident) were more likely to be FOX genes based on logistic regression coefficients. This pattern supports the hypothesis that nitrogen fixation machinery has been selectively retained in diazotrophic lineages while being lost or diverging in non-diazotrophs. GO features in the model are GO-Slim (generic) one-hot indicators derived from UniProt annotations (more details in Methods). GO variables contributed modestly relative to multi-omic dynamics and comparative-genomics features. Transporter activity (GO:0005215) was negatively associated with FOX classification, possibly reflecting that many characterized transporters serve housekeeping roles rather than nitrogen fixation-specific functions. Conversely, catalytic activity (GO:0003824) showed positive association, consistent with the enzymatic nature of nitrogenase and the metalloenzyme maturation, redox, and biosynthetic activities required for heterocyst function.

Our most unexpected finding was that a divergent promoter arrangement reduces the likelihood of a gene being a FOX gene. Divergent promoters indicate a shared regulatory region controlling two opposing-strand genes, which in some cases are constitutively expressed house-keeping pairs50. This work suggests that many FOX genes are more often in operonic arrangements that do not involve divergent promoters. This raises the question for future research of whether diazotrophic genes, and perhaps other rare and sensitive functional genetic systems, avoid divergent arrangements across organisms to prevent interference by neighboring gene regulation. Our results integrate in silico predictions with biological context to explore and explain predicted new FOX gene candidates. The consistent model performance across resampling schemes, together with interpretable feature-importance patterns, suggests that the top-ranked candidates are enriched for true FOX genes. However, functional genetic verification through targeted gene knockout, complementation, CRISPRi knockdown, or comparable approaches is required to confirm the role of any candidate gene under oxic diazotrophic growth conditions. Notably, independent experimental evidence supports the importance for several high-scoring candidates that were not part of the training label set, further supporting the ability of our models to meaningfully rank-order FOX genes and diazotrophy-promoting candidates. The convergence of high pFOX scores with independent literature support for genes like fdxB, hesF, and the hgl island genes suggests that systematic exploration of other top-ranked but uncharacterized candidates is warranted.

Discussion

Our integrative analysis reveals that FOX genes are distinguished from housekeeping genes primarily by late nitrogen step-down induction, diazotroph-biased sequence conservation, and avoidance of divergent promoter architectures. These patterns, learned independently by multiple classifiers, provide both a biological signature of oxic nitrogen fixation machinery and a practical basis for candidate prioritization. By integrating heterogeneous datasets including transcriptomics, proteomics, and comparative bioinformatics and leveraging multiple algorithms, we achieved strong predictive performance in rank-order differentiation of our labeled FOX genes against our negative proxy set. We balanced interpretability and predictive accuracy by using simpler models like logistic regression as well as more sophisticated models to avoid missing nonlinear patterns that could improve predictive performance. Our ROC-AUC of up to 0.80 is comparable to published bacterial gene essentiality classifiers27,28, despite the additional challenge of predicting condition-specific (diazotrophic) rather than universal essentiality.

The most important variables in our model align well with current research that genes needed for heterocyst function see strong up-regulation after nitrogen step-down and can be tightly repressed otherwise to avoid detrimental activity under normal growth conditions. The negative coefficient for 0_hour_rpkm but positive coefficient for 12_hour_rpkm and 21_hour_rpkm in the logistic model (Table 3), as well as the SHAP values from XGBoost support this interpretation (Fig. 5). This quantifies the intuitive notion that housekeeping genes with higher RPKM expressed during normal growth are less likely to be FOX genes. Conversely, genes that have high RPKM expression longer after nitrogen step-down are more likely to be FOX genes.

One novel insight from our models is the relationship between divergent promoters and FOX genes and raises questions about gene regulatory architecture in cyanobacteria. Divergent promoters are common in bacterial genomes50 but in this work, it appears that genes necessary for nitrogen-fixation are less likely to share regulatory regions. One reason could be to prevent leaky expression or ensure that each FOX gene can be more precisely modulated.

The results of this work also provide updated and practical guidance to build on past genetic engineering attempts that transferred the nif gene cluster plus a more limited number of accessory genes22. Those attempts struggled to produce detectable nitrogenase activity under normal air conditions, underscoring the complexity of the trait. Our findings support that there are likely many more FOX genes. Our top 50 ranked candidates alone span an additional ~ 45 kb of coding sequence, and many encode proteins of unknown function that may fill critical gaps in oxygen protection, electron flow, or metabolite exchange.

Applying model predictions in the FOX genome optimization process requires trade-off decisions. Greedy selection maximizes the sum of probabilities, which makes the simplifying assumption that each gene’s contribution to overall nitrogen fixation is proportional to its predicted score and that contributions are additive. While these models have meaningful rank-order performance, they are not explicitly calibrated probabilities, and their sums do not guarantee an exact number of FOX gene hits. Also, biology has epistatic interactions, and two genes might be redundant, or they might be synergistic where the combined effect (in terms of nitrogen fixation magnitude) is greater than the sum of the individual contributions. Our approach does not capture those potential interactions. A possible extension of this work would be to integrate known gene network information to ensure certain groups of genes are selected together if known to function as a complex.

Several practical limitations should be considered when using pFOX ranks. Our training labels and multi-omic predictor variables are derived from a single strain, Anabaena 7120, and from a specific nitrogen step-down experiment with defined time points, so genes that are conditionally essential under alternative diazotrophic regimes may be under-ranked. The proxy negative class is imperfect and may contain unrecognized diazotrophy-promoting genes, which introduces label noise that can attenuate performance and blur the boundary between strict FOX essentiality and broader diazotrophy support. Transposon-based screens used historically to define FOX labels can miss loci due to insertion biases and chromosomal accessibility, which further limits label completeness. For these reasons, high pFOX candidates should be viewed strictly as hypotheses that require functional genetic verification, including targeted gene knockout and complementation or CRISPRi-based knockdown under oxic diazotrophic conditions, before being interpreted as FOX or diazotrophy-promoting genes.

Transposon mutagenesis screens used to define FOX labels can be subject to systematic biases. Transposon insertion sequencing studies have documented sequence-specific51 and higher-order insertion biases beyond local nucleotide preferences, including preferences for DNA methylation patterns, replication fork targeting, and accessibility constraints imposed by nucleoid-associated proteins and local DNA supercoiling states24,52. These biases could systematically exclude certain genomic regions from mutagenesis, potentially causing us to miss FOX genes in AT-rich regions (for GC-preferring transposons like Tn5) or genes in regions with constrained DNA accessibility51. While the use of transcriptomic regulation and comparative conservation provides some independence from positional biases, future FOX gene discovery efforts would benefit from integrating screens using multiple transposon systems with complementary insertion preferences or from complementary approaches such as CRISPRi-based essentiality screens. Our ranked candidate list provides a starting point for targeted validation of genes in genomic regions potentially under-sampled by existing Tn-seq screens.

As synthetic biology efforts scale toward engineering complex traits into non-native hosts, computational prioritization will become increasingly essential for managing the combinatorial complexity of multigene systems. The tools and predictions presented here, which include ranked candidate lists, interpretable feature analyses, and an interactive complement designer, provide a foundation for the nitrogen fixation community to coordinate validation efforts and systematically close the gap between native diazotroph performance and engineered systems. We anticipate that iterative cycles of prediction, validation, and model refinement will progressively sharpen these tools, ultimately contributing to the long-term goal of sustainable photosynthetic nitrogen fixation in agriculture. The gap between 2% and 100% of native diazotrophic activity represents both an engineering challenge and an opportunity for gene discovery and the candidates prioritized here offer a systematic path toward closing that gap.

Methods

Data collection and labeling

We compiled a gene-level feature table for Anabaena 7120 by integrating multiple publicly available datasets plus derived genomic-context features. Transcriptomic features were obtained from a strand-specific RNA-seq time course following combined-nitrogen step-down, with samples at 0 h (nitrogen replete) and 6, 12, and 21 h after deprivation (NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus: GSE26633)53. Proteomic features were obtained from a quantitative proteomics study measuring protein abundance changes under nitrogen deficiency at 3, 12, and 24 h, which we mapped to the corresponding Anabaena 7120 locus identifiers54. Divergent transcription start sites were derived from published transcription start site maps under nitrogen stress-induced differentiation55. Supplementary Table S7 documents the exact time-point mapping and feature definitions used in the released feature matrix. Full code for replication is available at https://github.com/jamesyoung93/FoxGenes_ML.

In addition to multi-omic features, we assembled training labels from prior genetics. A set of 68 experimentally validated FOX genes in Anabaena 7120 was compiled from the literature (including complementation-validated mutants)12. For the negative class, we used 835 conserved non-essential genes as defined by a published cyanobacterial essentiality and conservation analysis18. This negative set is intended as a proxy negative-control group enriched for housekeeping functions that are unlikely to be specifically required for aerobic N₂ fixation (further rationale and background in Introduction). All remaining genes not in the known FOX set and not flagged as broadly conserved non-essential genes were treated as unknowns. This unknown group constitutes the target of our predictions which we rank by the predicted FOX gene probability. After merging all data sources, the master table contained 5,055 genes composed of 68 experimentally validated FOX genes, 835 proxy negatives, and 4,152 unlabeled genes.

After merging the transcriptomic, proteomic, transcriptional start site, and label data, we had a master table of features for each gene. Data integration was performed by gene identifiers, matching expression measurements and other attributes to the corresponding gene name or locus tag in the Anabaena 7120 genome. After the join operations, when data was missing, we implemented imputations rather than discarding the gene. Specifically, missing numeric values were filled with zeros for expression levels below detection and additional binary flags were introduced to indicate missingness in those features. This approach preserves genes with partial data, avoiding unnecessary exclusion that could bias the model. Overall, no genes were removed due to missing data but instead we augmented the feature set with indicators so that models could learn from the pattern of missing vs. present values.

GO annotations were taken from the UniProtKB proteome annotation table for Anabaena 7120 (downloaded export including Gene ontology IDs/GO fields) and mapped to Anabaena 7120 locus identifiers via gene/locus tag fields. Detailed GO terms were one-hot encoded and then collapsed to GO-Slim (generic) categories and the raw go_id field was excluded from model fitting.

Model training and evaluation

The initial feature matrix was refined down to 93 candidate predictors after removing redundant RNA-seq read-count proxies (retaining RPKM as the normalized expression measure at each timepoint) and excluding non-numeric columns. A zero-variance filter within the modeling pipeline removed features with no variation among labeled genes (primarily sparse Gene Ontology features), yielding 72 active predictors spanning transcriptomics, proteomics, promoter architecture, reciprocal-best-hit conservation, and genomic context, consistent with the feature-importance matrix reported in Supplementary Table S2.

We discarded variables with near-zero variance and collapsed pairs showing Pearson correlation ∣r∣>0.95 in the logistic regression model. Tree-based algorithms are inherently tolerant of residual collinearity, so they received the full filtered matrix. To keep the logistic baseline interpretable, a bidirectional stepwise search (Wald entry < 0.01, exit > 0.05) was applied only to the logistic model; no further manual pruning was performed. Model development followed a two-stage workflow. First, we evaluated generalization performance using repeated stratified holdout splits (described below), which provides estimates of how well models distinguish FOX genes from the negative control set. Second, after confirming acceptable performance, we retrained each classifier on all 903 labeled genes to produce genome-wide FOX probability scores (pFOX) for the 4,152 unlabeled genes.

For evaluation, three classifiers were assessed using 20 repeated stratified random train–test splits at train fractions of 0.7, 0.8, and 0.9, with metrics from the 80/20 splits reported in the main text (Table 1). Within each split, hyperparameters for Random Forest and XGBoost were tuned on the training portion using 3-fold inner cross-validation with RandomizedSearchCV (30 iterations, scoring by average precision). Logistic Regression used fixed regularization with bidirectional stepwise feature selection (Wald entry p < 0.01, exit p > 0.05). This nested tuning ensures that reported test-set metrics are unbiased by hyperparameter selection.

We evaluated multiple feature configurations: a baseline set including all available features, a principled set retaining only RPKM expression values (excluding redundant read-count proxies), proteomics fold-changes, promoter architecture, and conservation metrics, and variants with genomic position features ablated. The principled set with position features is used for main-text results unless otherwise noted. To assess whether genomic position acted as a proxy for label clustering, we performed two sensitivity analyses: (i) location-feature ablation, excluding chromosome_region_start and chromosome_region_end from the feature matrix, and (ii) location-blocked cross-validation, partitioning the genome into 5 contiguous regions with 4 partition offsets (20 total splits) and holding out entire regions together. Performance under both regimes is reported in Supplementary Tables S5 and S6.

The three classifiers were: (i) Logistic Regression (scikit-learn, penalty tuned over L1/L2 via randomized search); (ii) Random Forest (scikit-learn RandomForestClassifier, balanced class weights, with n_estimators, max_depth, max_features, min_samples_leaf, and min_samples_split tuned); and (iii) XGBoost 2.1.3 (binary: logistic objective, scale_pos_weight set to the negative/positive class ratio of ~ 12.3, with n_estimators, learning_rate, max_depth, subsample, colsample_bytree, gamma, min_child_weight, and reg_lambda tuned).

After confirming acceptable generalization via the repeated holdout evaluation, we retrained each classifier on all 903 labeled genes to produce final models for genome-wide prediction. Hyperparameters for the final models were tuned using 5-fold stratified cross-validation with RandomizedSearchCV (60 iterations, scoring by average precision). The final Random Forest used n_estimators = 771, max_depth = 5, max_features = 0.3, min_samples_leaf = 7, and min_samples_split = 8. The final XGBoost used n_estimators = 693, learning_rate = 0.023, max_depth = 3, subsample = 0.77, colsample_bytree = 0.67, gamma = 4.5, min_child_weight = 6, and reg_lambda = 26.1. These final models were applied to generate pFOX scores for all unlabeled genes.

Each trained model generated class probabilities for the unlabeled genes. The ensemble probability for each gene was computed as the arithmetic mean of the Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost predicted class probabilities. The ensemble mean probability with genomic position information serves as the preferred ranking metric (Supplementary Table S1) but the genomic position ablated pFOX is available as well. Full code for replication is available at https://github.com/jamesyoung93/FoxGenes_ML.

All analyses were implemented in Python using NumPy (1.26.4), pandas (2.2.3), scikit-learn (1.4.2)56, and XGBoost (2.1.3). SHAP values were computed using XGBoost’s native TreeSHAP implementation via the pred_contribs parameter33. All SHAP values illustrated here represent contributions in the model output space (log-odds). For divergent_promoter_distance, genes lacking a divergent promoter within 2,000 basepairs (bp) upstream were encoded with a ceiling value of 10,000 bp to represent absence. Code is available at https://github.com/jamesyoung93/FoxGenes_ML (machine learning pipeline) and https://github.com/jamesyoung93/FoxGenesApp (web application).

Comparative bioinformatics

We retrieved reference proteomes for a panel of cyanobacteria spanning heterocyst-forming diazotrophs (4 unique organisms, including Anabaena 7120), unicellular diazotrophs (three unique organisms; where available, multiple database proteome identifiers representing taxonomic synonyms were included, as detailed in Supplementary Table S3) and non-diazotrophs (two unique organisms, one filamentous and one unicellular). Supplementary Table S3 lists species/strain designations, diazotrophy status, database sources, and accession identifiers for each proteome, including cases where multiple database proteome identifiers were available for the same organism. To move beyond identity-only matching and to reduce ortholog/paralog ambiguity, we quantified conservation using reciprocal best hits (RBH) derived from bidirectional BLASTp (ncbi-blast/2.14.157) searches with additional filters on alignment coverage (70% coverage of both sequences, scov and qcov, in a pair), greater than or equal to 30% amino acid identity, and bidirectional BLASTp searches with e-value ≤ 1e − 5. For each query–subject species pair, the best hit was defined as the alignment with the lowest e-value, with ties broken by highest percent identity and then longest alignment length. For each Anabaena 7120 gene, we computed (i) RBH counts across diazotroph proteomes, (ii) RBH counts across non-diazotroph proteomes, and (iii) mean percent identity across RBH matches; these comparative features were used both as post hoc filters in the web tool and as additional numerical predictors in the machine-learning feature set. Full code is available at https://github.com/jamesyoung93/FoxGenes_ML.

Identifying high-probability candidates and minimal complement design

The final models yielded a ranked list of candidate FOX genes from the unknown cohort, with associated probability scores which we integrated with comparative bioinformatic filters and an optimization strategy. The rationale is that true FOX genes should be conserved among more known aerobic diazotrophs yet absent from non-diazotrophs. Thus, the user can choose to filter the high-probability candidates by requiring presence across a cohort of diazotrophic cyanobacteria and requiring absence in non-N₂-fixing cyanobacteria. This yielded a subset of candidates that are both high scoring and unique to the diazotrophic lineage.

We formulated an optimization solver in our web app where we maximize the total FOX functionality of a gene set under a given nucleotide size constraint. Each candidate gene has an associated predicted probability and gene length. We used a greedy optimization algorithm to select genes based on the highest ratio of probability benefit to length cost. Starting from the top predicted probability/length ratio, we kept adding genes until a chosen total length limit was reached. This greedy approach tends to include many small genes if they still carry decent probability scores, thereby carrying more cumulative FOX gene probability into the limited space. We also provide simpler rank-order selection strategy, where the user can take the top probability genes irrespective of length until the same length limit is met. The two approaches can yield different complements where rank-order selection favors a fewer high-probability genes, whereas greedy optimization selection might include a larger number of smaller nucleotide length genes that collectively give more total probability. The resulting gene sets are then analyzed for overlaps and differences in functional composition in the app. Full code is available at https://github.com/jamesyoung93/FoxGenesApp.
