Introduction

Diverticulosis may affect any segment of the gastrointestinal tract. Appendiceal diverticulitis (AD), however, is rare, with a reported prevalence of 0.014% to 3.7% in appendectomy specimens[1]. Its clinical presentation commonly mimics acute appendicitis, rendering preoperative diagnosis difficult. Radiologic detection can also be challenging, particularly when severe inflammation obscures the diverticular neck[2].

AD is not only a rare mimic of acute appendicitis but also a clinically significant entity due to its higher risk of perforation and its well-documented association with appendiceal neoplasms[3]. This dual implication is particularly relevant for surgeons, as it reinforces the need for careful intraoperative assessment and systematic histopathological examination, even when the appendix appears macroscopically inflamed without obvious atypical features.

We report a case of acute AD diagnosed intraoperatively and subsequently confirmed through histopathological examination. This case report has been documented in accordance with the SCARE checklist[4].

Case presentation

A 59-year-old man with a medical history of renal lithiasis presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of right iliac fossa pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting. He reported no urinary symptoms, changes in bowel habits, or weight loss.

On examination, he was hemodynamically stable, with a blood pressure of 131/76 mmHg, a pulse rate of 70 bpm, and a temperature of 37.4°C. Abdominal examination revealed localized tenderness in the right lower quadrant without guarding or rigidity. Specific peritoneal signs, including Rovsing’s sign, psoas sign, and obturator sign, were negative. No palpable abdominal mass or rebound tenderness was detected, and there were no clinical features suggestive of generalized peritonitis.

Laboratory investigations showed leukocytosis (13 × 10^9/L) and elevated C-reactive protein (55 mg/L). Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a 12-mm thick-walled, non-compressible appendix with hyperenhancement, consistent with acute appendicitis.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) imaging was not performed preoperatively due to the patient’s classical presentation of acute appendicitis on ultrasonography and the immediate indication for surgical intervention based on clinical and laboratory findings. This represents a diagnostic limitation, as CT may have provided additional preoperative characterization of the appendiceal pathology.

The patient underwent a laparoscopic appendectomy on the same day. Laparoscopic exploration confirmed a latero-cecal appendix with marked inflammatory changes. Careful inspection revealed multiple inflamed pseudodiverticula distributed along the anti-mesenteric border of the appendix, without evidence of perforation or surrounding abscess formation. No macroscopic signs of appendiceal neoplasm were identified intraoperatively (Figs 1 and 2). Histopathological examination confirmed acute AD with inflamed pseudodiverticula and peridiverticulitis. No dysplasia or malignancy was identified (Fig. 3). Discussion

Appendiceal diverticulitis was first described by Kelynack[1]. Diverticula are generally small (<5 mm) and typically arise from the distal, anti-mesenteric aspect of the appendix. Diverticula may be congenital (true), incorporating all layers of the appendiceal wall, though these are exceedingly rare (0.014%). Acquired (false) diverticula, lacking the muscular layer, are more common[5].

Predisposing factors include advancing age, male sex, and conditions such as Hirschsprung disease[6]. The prevalence of AD has been reported to be up to 3.7% in surgical series[7]. Clinical presentation closely resembles acute appendicitis, making distinction based solely on symptoms nearly impossible[8].

The present case is noteworthy due to the presence of multiple appendiceal diverticula identified intraoperatively in a 59-year-old male patient, an age group known to have a higher association with appendiceal diverticular disease and neoplastic conditions. The multiplicity of diverticula may reflect an advanced form of acquired diverticular disease and underscores the importance of meticulous intraoperative inspection, even in routine-appearing appendicitis cases.

CT plays an important role in the preoperative evaluation of appendiceal diverticulitis and may demonstrate a focal outpouching arising from the appendix surrounded by inflammatory changes. However, its diagnostic sensitivity remains limited, particularly in cases of advanced inflammation where diverticular anatomy may be obscured, leading to misdiagnosis as uncomplicated acute appendicitis[7,8]. The most specific sign is a focal diverticulum arising from the appendix at the epicenter of inflammation[1,5,6]. Nevertheless, many cases remain unrecognized until surgical exploration or pathological examination.

Four types of appendiceal diverticular disease have been described[9]: Type 1 – normal appendix with an inflamed diverticulumType 2 – inflamed diverticulum with concurrent appendicitisType 3 – acute appendicitis with incidental, non-inflamed diverticulumType 4 – incidental diverticulum without inflammation

Clinically, appendiceal diverticulitis and acute appendicitis share many overlapping features; however, appendiceal diverticulitis is more frequently observed in older patients, may present with a more insidious course, and carries a significantly higher risk of perforation. These differences, although subtle, may influence the surgeon’s level of suspicion and reinforce the importance of histopathological confirmation[9].

Appendectomy remains the treatment of choice and should not be delayed, as AD carries a significantly higher risk of perforation and complications compared with typical appendicitis[9]. Laparoscopic management is safe and effective.

A review of the literature reveals that, although appendiceal diverticulitis has been reported in multiple case series, most cases involve a single diverticulum or are diagnosed incidentally on histopathology. The present case adds value by demonstrating multiple intraoperatively visible diverticula in a symptomatic patient, reinforcing the spectrum of disease severity and the importance of careful surgical and pathological assessment.

Given the strong association between AD and appendiceal neoplasms, thorough macroscopic and microscopic examination of all appendectomy specimens is crucial[3,8]. AD may also play a role in the development of mucinous appendiceal tumors[5].

Conclusion

Appendiceal diverticulitis is a rare yet important differential diagnosis of acute appendicitis. It carries a higher risk of perforation and is associated with a range of appendiceal neoplasms. Although preoperative diagnosis remains difficult, appendectomy offers definitive treatment. Routine and meticulous pathological examination of appendectomy specimens is essential to exclude underlying malignancy. In addition, recognition of this entity is important not because it alters immediate surgical management, which remains appendectomy, but because of its association with perforation risk and underlying neoplasia, which have direct implications for pathological evaluation and postoperative surveillance.
