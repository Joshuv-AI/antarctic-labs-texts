Sensor-derived multidimensional gait domains mediate the association between intrinsic capacity impairment and fall risk in community-dwelling older adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Siyu Chen, Ji Shen, Yuting Kang, Yushan Zhang, Hong Shi, Chi Zhang, Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalls are a leading cause of injury and functional decline in older adults. Intrinsic capacity (IC) impairment has emerged as an important predictor of fall risk, yet the biomechanical pathways linking IC decline to falls remain uncharacterized. Whether sensor-derived multidimensional gait domains serve as domain-specific indirect pathways in the IC-fall association has not been examined.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study of 813 community-dwelling adults (aged ≥60 years), IC impairment and a 12-month self-reported fall history were assessed. Gait was quantified using a wearable multi-sensor system, with nine parameters summarized into three domains: pace, gait phase, and kinetics. Logistic regression-based mediation analysis was used to test the indirect effects of gait domains on the association between IC and falls.ResultsHigher IC impairment was independently associated with greater odds of falling after full adjustment (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.11–1.66). Among gait domains, pace (OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.56−0.99) and kinetics (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.59−0.99) were independently associated with lower fall odds, whereas gait phase was not. Mediation analyses indicated that pace and kinetics partially mediated the IC-fall association, accounting for 18.76% and 10.64% of the total effect respectively, with gait phase showing no significant indirect effect.ConclusionsThese exploratory findings suggest that multidimensional gait assessment may complement IC screening to improve fall risk stratification and identify domain-specific intervention targets in older adults.Keywords: Intrinsic capacity, Falls, Multidimensional gait assessment, Wearable sensors, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Falls are a leading cause of injury, disability, and mortality among older adults, with global incidence reaching 32–42% in those aged 70 years and older [1,2]. Beyond immediate physical harm, falls accelerate frailty progression through fear and inactivity [3]. Intrinsic capacity (IC), a composite of physical and mental abilities proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO), has emerged as an important predictor of fall risk in community-dwelling older adults [[4], [5], [6]]. However, the functional pathways linking IC decline to falls remain uncharacterized at the biomechanical level.

Gait, as an integrator of neuromuscular output, postural control, and cognitive-motor coordination, holds a unique position among the functional expressions of IC decline [7]. While gait speed is widely used as a fall risk marker, it captures only one dimension of a biomechanically complex behavior. Sensor-based technologies now enable precise quantification of multidimensional gait characteristics, including temporal-spatial, gait phase, and kinetic parameters, offering richer information on fall-related functional decline than speed alone [[8], [9], [10]].

Despite growing evidence linking IC impairment to falls, the specific biomechanical pathways through which IC decline translates into fall risk have not been systematically decomposed in large community samples. This study aimed to quantify the proportion of the IC–fall association explained by each gait domain as an indirect pathway, using logistic regression-based mediation analysis with 1000 bootstrap resamples, in a cross-sectional study with retrospective fall ascertainment of 813 community-dwelling older adults in China.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study with retrospective fall ascertainment was conducted in Beijing, China, between January and August 2023, with ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital (No. 2024BJYYEC-KY239-02). Participants aged ≥60 years were recruited from four urban communities. Eligible individuals had lived in the community for at least 6 months and were able to complete questionnaires and physical assessments independently. Exclusion criteria included major medical events within the preceding 6 months, recent surgery, terminal illness, or concurrent participation in interventional trials. Of 936 screened participants, 123 were excluded due to missing data (n = 50) or gait measurement outliers (n = 73), a final sample of 813 community-dwelling older adults.

Falls

Fall history during the preceding 12 months was collected through structured self-report interviews. A fall was defined per WHO criteria as an unexpected event in which the individual comes to rest on the ground or lower level [1].

Intrinsic capacity

IC was assessed across six domains per WHO recommendations [11]: locomotion (SPPB, impaired if score ≤9 [12]), cognition (Mini-Cog, score <4 [13]), vitality (MNA-SF, score ≤11 [14]), psychological function (PHQ-9, score ≥5 [15]), vision (WHO eye chart, acuity <6/7.5 [11]), and hearing (Whisper test [11]). Each domain was scored as impaired (1) or unimpaired (0) and summed to generate a total IC impairment score (range 0–6, higher scores indicating greater impairment).

Gait assessment

Gait was assessed using the Intelligent Device for Energy Expenditure and Activity (IDEEA v3.0; MiniSun, CA, USA), a validated five-sensor wearable system affixed to the sternum, thighs, and ankles [16]. Participants walked a 12-metre straight path at usual speed. Nine parameters were selected based on established protocols [[17], [18], [19]] and grouped into three domains: pace (velocity, step length, stride length), gait phase (support time, swing time, support/swing ratio), and kinetics (ground impact, footfall control, thigh swing power). Each parameter was standardized to Z-score, and a composite Z-score for each domain was calculated by averaging the constituent parameters.

Covariates

Covariates included age, sex, education level, marital status, living alone, COPD, hypertension, diabetes, PVD, and number of medications taken [20].

Statistical analyses

Group differences between fallers and non-fallers were examined using t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, and chi-square tests as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression was performed with fall status as the dependent variable and IC impairment score and three gait domain Z-scores as independent variables, adjusted for covariates in two sequential models. Three separate logistic regression-based mediation analyses were conducted using the PROCESS macro for SPSS, with IC impairment score as the independent variable (X) and each gait domain as the mediator (M), adjusted for all covariates. Indirect effects were assessed using 1000 bootstrap resamples; mediation was established if the 95% CI excluded zero. To verify the linearity assumption, a likelihood ratio test was conducted comparing logistic regression models with linear and quadratic IC impairment score terms.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Of the 813 older adults included, 100 (12.3%) reported at least one fall in the preceding 12 months. Compared with non-fallers, fallers had higher IC impairment scores (p < 0.001). Among gait domains, pace and kinetics composite Z-scores were significantly lower in fallers than non-fallers (p < 0.01), whereas gait phase did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.685). Detailed participant characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Multivariable analysis of factors associated with falls

Results of the multivariable logistic regression are presented in Table 1. After full covariate adjustment, IC impairment remained independently associated with falls (OR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.11–1.66), as did pace (OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.56−0.99) and kinetics (OR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.59−0.99). Gait phase was not associated with falls in any model. The likelihood ratio test confirmed a linear association between IC impairment score and fall risk, with the quadratic term not reaching statistical significance (LRT χ² = 1.484, df = 1, p = 0.223) see Supplementary Fig. S1.

Mediating effect analyses

Mediation analyses revealed a significant total effect of IC impairment on falls (β = 0.051, 95% CI: 0.030−0.072). Pace partially mediated this association (indirect effect β = 0.010, 95% CI: 0.005−0.059), accounting for 18.76% of the total effect. Kinetics also served as a partial mediator (indirect effect β = 0.006, 95% CI: 0.002−0.035), explaining 10.64% of the total effect. The indirect effect via gait phase was not significant (β = −0.001, 95% CI: −0.020−0.009). In all models with significant indirect effects, the direct effect of IC impairment on falls remained significant, indicating partial mediation. Results are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 1.

Discussion

In this study, IC impairment was independently associated with fall risk in 813 community-dwelling older adults. Pace and kinetics partially mediated this association as exploratory indirect pathways, accounting for 18.76% and 10.64% of the total effect respectively, while gait phase was not a significant mediator. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory indirect associations rather than evidence of causal mediation pathways.

The finding that pace partially mediated the IC-fall association is consistent with evidence linking slower gait speed and shorter stride length to elevated fall risk [21,22]. The pace domain integrates velocity, step length, and stride length, providing a more comprehensive representation of locomotor propulsion than speed alone. The independent mediating role of kinetics extends these findings beyond the temporal-spatial domain, capturing neuromuscular forces underlying forward propulsion and shock absorption. Prior work has demonstrated that kinetic parameters carry independent predictive value for fall risk [10], but their role as an indirect pathway within the IC-fall association had not previously been quantified. Declining locomotor function, cognitive-motor interference, muscle mass loss, and nutritional decline converge on pace and kinetic deficits through partially distinct biological pathways [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]].

In contrast, gait phase was neither associated with falls nor a significant mediator, and the absence of a significant association between gait phase and falls undermines one of the foundational assumptions of mediation for this domain. This negative finding can be understood in terms of task demands and sample characteristics, our 12-metre straight-path walk at usual speed limits stance and swing timing variability, and gait phase parameters are more sensitive under dual-task or challenging conditions [30]. Our functionally independent participants with early-to-moderate IC decline may also retain sufficient reserve to compensate through stable gait phase timing.

These findings carry practical implications. Integrating sensor-derived multidimensional gait assessment into IC screening may enhance fall risk stratification by identifying which biomechanical pathways are most implicated in individual fall vulnerability. At the parameter level, velocity, step length, and footfall control were the most discriminative individual parameters after full adjustment. Older adults with pace deficits may benefit from cognitive-motor dual-task training, whereas those with kinetic deficits may respond better to progressive resistance training and nutritional support. This domain-specific approach may advance beyond generic exercise prescriptions that do not account for the heterogeneity of gait impairment in older adults with declining IC.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The conceptual overlap between the SPPB-based locomotion domain of IC and the biomechanical gait parameters may mean that part of the observed indirect associations reflects shared variance rather than independent mediating pathways. The cross-sectional design with retrospective fall ascertainment precludes causal inference and limits directional interpretation. Fall history was ascertained by self-report, subject to recall bias, partially mitigated by caregiver clarification. The observed fall prevalence of 12.3% is consistent with urban community-based studies in China but lower than Western estimate, recruitment from four-communities limits generalizability. Longitudinal studies with prospective fall ascertainment are needed to confirm these pathways.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that IC impairment is independently associated with fall risk in community-dwelling older adults, partially explained through sensor-derived pace and kinetics as exploratory indirect pathways. Multidimensional gait assessment may complement IC screening to improve fall risk stratification and inform targeted prevention strategies. Longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm the temporal directionality of these pathways.
