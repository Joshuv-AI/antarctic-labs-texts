Faculty consensus on competitiveness for the new competency‐based emergency medicine standardized letter of evaluation
Authors: Benjamin Schnapp, Morgan Sehdev, Caitlin Schrepel, Sharon Bord, Alexis Pelletier‐Bui, Alai Alvarez, Nicole M Dubosh, Yoon Soo Park, Eric Shappell
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency medicine (EM) has introduced a new, competency‐based standardized letter of evaluation (SLOE) template. While a previous version of the SLOE has been shown to promote a high degree of faculty consensus regarding competitiveness, this has not been shown for the new SLOE template.ObjectiveThe objective was to evaluate faculty consensus on competitiveness for the new EM SLOE 2.0.MethodsFifty mock SLOE 2.0 letters using the new template were drafted and sent to a group of experienced EM educators. The 50 letters were ranked by the experienced faculty as well as a point‐based prediction model and a regression model and the results were compared.ResultsFaculty consensus on competitiveness remained strong when using the new SLOE 2.0 format. The points‐based prediction model and regression model both demonstrated a high level of agreement with faculty consensus rankings for the SLOE 2.0.ConclusionsIntroduction of the new, competency‐based SLOE 2.0 format did not have a deleterious effect on faculty consensus rankings of competitiveness.

INTRODUCTION

Standardized letters of evaluation (SLOEs) continue to gain popularity as a method for differentiating applicants during residency selection, with specialties such as dermatology, orthopedics, internal medicine, emergency medicine (EM), obstetrics and gynecology, and radiology all utilizing this model. 1 The SLOE offers multiple advantages to programs over the standard narrative letter of recommendation, including decreased time for review, increased depth of evaluation, and greater predictive validity for success during residency. 2 The SLOE has also proved to be easier for reviewing faculty to interpret than narrative letters, even for faculty inexperienced with reading them. 3

For applicants and faculty alike, a common understanding of SLOE competitiveness is critical for advising students about where they may be likely or unlikely to match. Previous studies have shown some disagreement in how program directors interpret information from narrative letters of recommendation, 4 which can lead to ambiguity among students and advisors. EM, as the first specialty to adopt the SLOE, recently made a major update to its SLOE since its introduction nearly 30 years ago, moving to a more competency‐based model. 5 This was in part due to issues around inflated evaluations, 6 an issue that has also been seen in other specialties. 7

The previous version of the EM SLOE showed a high degree of faculty consensus regarding applicant competitiveness when evaluating mock SLOEs. 8 With the recent major change to the EM SLOE, it is unknown whether all of the SLOE's previous desirable characteristics, including its high degree of faculty consensus, have been maintained. The goal of this study was to determine whether experienced EM educators similarly rank the competitiveness of mock EM SLOE 2.0 evaluations. We also measured whether two prediction models could assess applicant competitiveness using the new EM SLOE 2.0.

METHODS

Study participants

Seven faculty authors/participants represented experienced education‐focused EM faculty from across the country, with a mean (±SD) of 7.8 (±2.4) years of experience reading SLOEs and 5.2 (±4.1) years of experience writing SLOEs. 8 Authors were identified based on known interest in the SLOE's role in the application process. Four different Association of American Medical Colleges regions were represented (northeast, western, central, and southern).

Data acquisition

Fifty mock SLOE 2.0 letters using the new template, including all applicant ratings and narratives but no institutional data, were drafted and scored by faculty participants using a previously described method. 8 The distribution of ratings of the mock SLOEs were created to match the distribution of the national data sample. The only variation from the previous methodology was that this study used the EM SLOE 2.0 template and blueprint from national ratings in 2022–2023, while the previous study used the 2021–2022 EM SLOE template and rating data. As in the previous study, faculty were asked to rank each of the mock SLOEs from 1 to 50 in order of competitiveness, as they would when creating a rank list for their residency program.

Outcomes

The average faculty rating of each SLOE was used as the “consensus ranking.” Levels of agreement with the average faculty rating were defined for individual faculty SLOE rankings as outlined in the previous study—“exact” agreement was defined as the faculty rating identical to the consensus rank, with “tight,” “close,” and “loose” agreement being defined as within two positions (4%), four positions (8%), and six positions (12%), respectively.

Prediction models

As in the previous study, we devised a points‐based system (Table S1) for each rating in the EM SLOE 2.0 based on senior author experience with evaluating applicants for residency as well as the previous point system for the 2021–2022 EM SLOE. A linear regression model, with rankings as ordinal variables, was also used to predict competitiveness. The regression model was trained on the 25 even‐ranked mock SLOEs and validated on the 25 odd‐ranked mock SLOEs. Narrative data were not considered by the prediction models. This study was deemed exempt by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board.

RESULTS

Faculty consensus on competitiveness for the new EM SLOE 2.0 was strong, with over 90% of individual rankings close (within four positions) to the consensus rank (Table 1). Both prediction models performed well, with 88% close rankings and 84% close rankings for the point system and the regression model, respectively. Individual faculty, point system, and regression comparative rankings are shown in Figures 1A–1C, respectively; each demonstrate a high level of correlation with the consensus rankings.

DISCUSSION

Faculty consensus ratings of the new competency‐based EM SLOEs 2.0 was strong. Compared to the previous 2021–2022 EM SLOE, the new EM SLOE 2.0 shows even higher levels of faculty consensus at each level of agreement (exact, tight, close, and loose). 8 The predictive models perform similarly on the new SLOE compared to the previous version, with a strong correlation with faculty consensus rankings in both models. Both the point system and the regression model perform best in the extremes of high‐ and low‐competitiveness SLOE 2.0s; this finding tracks with faculty rankings as well (Figure 1). These outlier candidates may be easiest to identify due to marked differences in the overall ranking patterns of their SLOEs compared to most applicants. Taken together, our findings may be reassuring to faculty and applicants by providing validity evidence that applicant competitiveness based on SLOE 2.0s is being interpreted similarly after transitioning to more competency‐based ratings. This validity evidence may be considered in other specialties looking to support a decision to move to competency‐based ratings for their SLOEs.

The new SLOE 2.0 has come under criticism for some of its revisions, with concerns that valuable rating questions had been removed while retaining lower yield rating questions, potentially disadvantaging some students. 9 There was also concern that insufficient research had been done showing that the new questions would be perceived and utilized similarly by faculty. This study provides preliminary evidence suggesting that letters written in the new SLOE format are likely utilized similarly to previous versions of the SLOE. While the new SLOE has moved to primarily competency‐based ratings, it does retain several questions that ask for a comparative ranking of applicants. These questions may account for the high levels of faculty agreement seen in our study, although more research is needed to determine how faculty are reaching their decision on applicant competitiveness.

LIMITATIONS

Limitations for this study include the small number of faculty raters from a limited number of institutions and the use of mock SLOE 2.0s rather than real SLOEs.

CONCLUSIONS

Similar to the previous iteration of the EM SLOE, faculty consensus regarding competitiveness remains high for the competency‐based EM SLOE 2.0.
