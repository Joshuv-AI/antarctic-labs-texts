Adolescents’ intention to reduce alcohol use: a health belief model approach to cancer prevention
Authors: Steffi De Jans, Liselot Hudders, Dieneke Van de Sompel, Emma Beuckels, Hayley Pearce, Kleio Koutra, Lia Lombardi, Chiara Pilotti, Laura Del Campo, Severin Haug, Nick Dietrich, Nikolai Kiselev, Nikolaos Boumparis, Teresa de Pablo-Pardo, Maria Krini, Polonca Serrano, Eunate Arana-Arri, Delia Nicoara, Vassilis Kilintzis, Andreas Triantafyllidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAlcohol consumption is a well-established risk factor for health problems such as cancer; yet, public health campaigns have shown mixed results in their effectiveness to reduce alcohol use. Drawing on the health belief model, this study examines adolescents’ self-reported alcohol consumption and explores drivers and barriers to abstaining from or reducing their alcohol consumption, with the ultimate goal of reducing alcohol-attributable cancers.MethodA large-scale, cross-sectional and cross-national online survey was conducted in 2024 among N = 4054 adolescents between 12 and 18 years, chiefly recruited through schools, in eight European countries (Belgium, Cyprus, Greece, Italy, Romania, Slovenia, Spain, and Switzerland).ResultsResults indicate that 47.5% of adolescents reported never consuming alcohol. Alcohol use increased with age and country differences were observed, while we found no differences in gender. Among the 52.5% participants who reported drinking alcohol, the average intention to reduce consumption was moderate. Adolescents who drank more frequently were less inclined to reduce their alcohol use. Intention to reduce drinking decreased with age. Perceived benefits of alcohol reduction followed by self-efficacy were the strongest predictors of this intention.ConclusionsThe study’s results provide valuable insights into adolescent alcohol consumption across Europe, leading to the formulation of concrete guidelines for future prevention campaigns regarding target groups (e.g. age, country), messaging, and framing. Specifically, future alcohol prevention campaigns could emphasize long-term health benefits and aim to increase adolescents’ perceptions of self-efficacy to refrain from initiating or to reduce alcohol consumption.

Introduction

Adolescence is a critical developmental period for health due to neurological and hormonal changes that occur and significant psychosocial development that takes place (Pechmann et al. 2005). It is a pivotal stage for the development of habits and lifestyle choices that can have lifelong consequences (Kreniske et al. 2020). As such, adolescence is marked by the initiation of many harmful health behaviors such as alcohol use, smoking, and drug use (Viner et al. 2015). Across Europe, Central Asia, and Canada, alcohol is by far the most common substance used by adolescents, with 57% of 15-year-olds having tried it at least once and 37% stating they have consumed alcohol within the past 30 days (WHO 2024). Early onset of drinking is consistently linked to an increased likelihood of alcohol dependence and related harms in adulthood (Hingson et al. 2006, Liang et al. 2012). This is specifically problematic as the 2022 HBSC survey showed that in Europe >15% of 11-year-olds, >30% of 13-year-olds, and >55% of 15-year-old adolescents have usually already drunk alcohol (Charrier et al. 2024).

Alcohol consumption contributes substantially to the global burden of disease. Strong evidence shows that alcohol is a causal factor for several types of cancer (Connor 2017), with a dose–response relationship between consumption and cancer risk (Rehm and Shield 2021). In 2020, an estimated 741.300 new cancer cases worldwide, accounting for 4.1% of all newly diagnosed cancers, were attributable to alcohol consumption (Gapstur et al. 2023). Within the European Union, nearly 8.000 people died from alcohol-attributable cancers in 2016 (Rehm and Shield 2021). Based on epidemiologic evidence, Gapstur et al. (2023) concluded that reducing or stopping alcohol consumption lowers the risk of some types of cancers, emphasizing the importance of prevention initiatives targeting young populations.

Accordingly, numerous health campaigns in European countries encourage people to consume less alcohol. In Belgium and Switzerland, for example, there are several campaigns, such as ‘Dry January’, which encourages people to abstain from alcohol during January, and ‘Tournée Minérale’ which promotes alcohol-free living throughout February. In Italy, the campaign ‘Alcol e Giovani’ aims to raise awareness about the dangers of underage drinking and encourages responsible alcohol consumption among young people. However, research shows mixed results regarding the effectiveness of such interventions. Systematic literature reviews indicate that mass media campaigns have little to no impact on reducing alcohol consumption (Young et al. 2018), and social media campaigns demonstrate inconsistent effects on drinking behaviors (Yeh et al. 2023). Studies also specifically highlight the ineffectiveness of campaigns aimed at reducing alcohol consumption among adolescents (Flynn et al. 2015). Yet, review articles also show that not all prevention efforts are ineffective. School-based interventions, for example, have been associated with reduced drinking frequency and alcohol consumption among adolescents (Hennessy et al. 2014, Das et al. 2016). Given these mixed findings, it is important to gain a better understanding of the psychological mechanisms that influence adolescents’ willingness to reduce alcohol use in order to design more effective interventions.

This study is part of a broader European research project aimed at improving adolescents’ health and preventing cancer by promoting healthy behaviors. As we intend to develop a school-based intervention within the project, it is essential to obtain a detailed understanding of adolescents’ drinking behavior and the determinants for reducing or abstaining from alcohol so that the intervention can be appropriately tailored to their needs. To investigate the determinants of adolescents’ intention to reduce alcohol consumption, the current study applies the health belief model (HBM) (Hochbaum 1958, Becker 1974, Champion and Skinner 2008). The HBM posits that individuals’ motivation to engage in health-promoting behaviors depends on their perceptions of susceptibility and severity of a health threat, the perceived benefits and barriers of taking action, and their perceived efficacy to carry out the behavior. The model provides a useful framework for understanding adolescents’ beliefs about alcohol use and their willingness to change drinking behaviors or continue to abstain from drinking.

Although the HBM has been widely applied to various adolescent health behaviors, including diet, smoking prevention, and safer sex practices (e.g. Petosa and Jackson 1991, Boone and Lefkowitz 2004, Mohammadi et al. 2017, Razaq 2022), few studies have examined it in relation to adolescent alcohol use. For instance, relying on the ideas of the HBM, De Leon et al. (2023) showed that the perceived effectiveness of protective behavioral strategies (e.g. related to manner of drinking, stopping/limiting drinking and serious harm reduction strategies) increased the use of such strategies, further decreasing alcohol consumption and alcohol-related problems among college students. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study to date has specifically examined how the different factors of the HBM contribute to adolescents’ intention to stop or reduce alcohol consumption. The current study therefore focuses on the HBM to examine adolescents’ intentions to reduce alcohol consumption and the key factors determining this. The aim is to gain insight into effective strategies for health campaigns, with the ultimate goal of preventing alcohol-related cancers.

With this study, we aim to examine adolescents’ intention to reduce alcohol consumption and identify which constructs of the HBM are the strongest predictors of this behavioral intent. Based on the assumption of the HBM, it is expected that perceived severity (H1), perceived susceptibility (H2), perceived efficacy (H3), and perceived benefits (H4) of reducing alcohol use are positively related to adolescents’ behavioral intent to reduce alcohol use, while perceived barriers of reducing alcohol use (H5) are negatively related to behavioral intent to reduce alcohol use.

In addition, while individual countries or regions have been studied extensively in the context of adolescent alcohol consumption, few studies focus on a multi-country adolescent sample. Significant variations in young people’s alcohol consumption are reported based on country (Ahlström and Österberg 2004), for instance due to differences in alcohol prices and control policies (Gilligan et al. 2012). By collecting data from eight European countries (i.e. Belgium, Cyprus, Greece, Italy, Romania, Slovenia, Spain, and Switzerland), this study fills a gap in multi-country studies on adolescents’ alcohol use and its predicting factors. In almost all of the eight countries studied, the legal age for buying and consuming alcohol is 18, with the exceptions of Belgium and Switzerland, where beer and wine can be purchased from the age of 16. The countries included in this study represent different adolescent drinking cultures, as classified by Bräker and Soellner (2016), ranging from countries with mainly non-using adolescents (Cyprus, Spain), over countries with mild but frequent users (Eastern European countries, such as Romania), to countries with the highest proportions of episodic drinking adolescents [Central European countries, such as Switzerland, Belgium, Italy and Slovenia, and Greece (ESPAD 2025)]. Including countries with different adolescent alcohol drinking cultures allows for a more comprehensive examination of factors influencing adolescents’ alcohol use and their intention to cut down.

Finally, we aim to identify which adolescents should be targeted by health campaigns to reduce alcohol consumption. It is therefore crucial to examine which socio-demographic characteristics may predict alcohol consumption among adolescents. To answer the hypotheses and research questions, a large-scale, cross-sectional and cross-national survey is conducted among adolescents in eight European countries.

Method

Study design

This research employed an observational design, gathering self-report data from respondents through a large-scale, cross-sectional, and cross-national online survey administered via the online platform Qualtrics. The survey targeted adolescents from eight European countries representing diverse regions: Southern Europe (Greece, Italy, Spain, Cyprus), Western Europe (Belgium, Switzerland), Central Europe (Slovenia), and Eastern Europe (Romania). Participants completed the survey in the language of their choice, and it lasted ~10 to 15 minutes. The study was pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (available at https://osf.io/c52yv/?view_only=6304d318fabe45bf92ed6ed30d0cfaf3), and data collection occurred between 18 April and 18 June 2024.

Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ethical Committee of the Faculty of Political and Social Sciences at Ghent University (approval number 2024-14), the Committee of Research and Ethics HMU (approval number 12213), and the Directorate of Secondary Education of Heraklion Prefecture in Greece (approval number F23/7306), as well as the Ethics Committee of Research of Foundation FISABIO in Spain.

The survey consisted of two main parts. In the initial section, active parental consent and informed consent from all participants was obtained before they could proceed. The second part of the survey focused on the constructs and questions central to the research (detailed in the measures section). Upon completing the survey, participants were provided with a debriefing, which explained the study’s purpose and included links to resources about national health care and cancer prevention (tailored to the participant’s country and language).

This research forms part of a broader European research project aimed at exploring various aspects of adolescents’ psychological and physical well-being, health perceptions, and behaviors. Key topics addressed in the survey include self-rated health, acceptance of health conditions, perceived control over health, stress, and social support. Additionally, the study investigates behaviors associated with cancer risk, such as smoking, alcohol use, and diet, as well as participants’ awareness of how these behaviors relate to cancer. Other sections examine unhealthy food consumption, intentions to adopt healthier behaviors, advertising literacy concerning unhealthy food advertising, and the impact of social media and social media influencers on food choices. For the current study, we specifically focused on the questions related to alcohol consumption.

Sample

Data in each country were collected by a designated local contact person, with clear instructions provided to the entire consortium to promote as much uniformity as possible in recruitment. The instructions emphasized striving for diversity in gender, age, rural versus urban schools, migration background, and educational track. While recruitment primarily focused on schools, flexibility was allowed to use convenience and snowball sampling if necessary. The provision of monetary incentives varied across countries due to differences in common practices and respondent expectations, as well as school and local regulations. See the Appendix for the data collection procedure per country.

In total, 7857 questionnaires were collected, of which 4054 were deemed valid after a thorough quality control process. The sampling error was determined to be 1.54%. Questionnaires were validated based on three inclusion criteria: respondents had to be between 12 and 18 years old, at least 70% of the survey questions needed to be completed, and both parental consent and informed consent from the adolescent were required.

Regarding respondent demographics, 51.7% identified as female, 42.2% as male, 3.3% preferred not to disclose their gender, 2.2% identified as another gender, and 0.7% identified as non-binary. For gender group comparisons, only participants identifying as male or female were included, as the other groups were too small to provide meaningful analysis. The participants were between 12 and 18 years (Mage = 15.38, SD = 1.72). Of the participants, 22.8% had a migration background (i.e. their mother and/or father were not born in the country where the adolescent currently lives). Measurement instrument.

Alcohol use was measured with the item ‘At present, how often do you drink any alcoholic drinks like beer, ciders, wine, liquors or others?’

The following constructs were measured on a five-point Likert-scale from 1 = ‘Not at all’ to 5 = ‘Definitely’ and were only presented to the adolescents that reported drinking alcohol on the behavior measure, thus, who answered ‘less than once a week’, ‘once a week’, ‘several times a week’, or ‘daily’ on the alcohol use item above.

Behavioral intent to reduce alcohol use was measured with three items derived from Zemore and Ajzen (2014) (α = .95).

Furthermore, the six factors from the HBM were measured. ‘Perceived susceptibility’, referring to one’s belief about their likelihood of getting a disease or health condition (i.e. belief about likelihood of getting cancer), was measured with one item derived from Popova (2014). ‘Perceived severity’ represents an assessment of the seriousness of the health condition and its potential consequences, and was also measured with one item from Popova (2014). Third, ‘perceived benefits’ were measured with five items (α = .74). This construct emphasizes the importance of demonstrating the positive outcomes of health behaviors. ‘Perceived barriers’ refer to the potential obstacles that may deter individuals from taking action. This was gauged with four items (α = .80). ‘Perceived efficacy’ pertains to an individual’s confidence in their ability to successfully perform a health behavior (i.e. self-efficacy) and their belief that the action will effectively reduce the risk of the health problem (i.e. response efficacy). Self-efficacy was measured with one item, and response efficacy with two items (r = .45, P < .001) following Popova (2014).

Finally, the socio-demographic factors age, gender, migration background, and country were measured. Migration background was measured by asking the participants whether their mother and/or father were born in the country they live in now (‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘I don’t know’). These socio-demographic variables were included because previous research has demonstrated that they are important explanatory factors for adolescent alcohol use (Donath et al. 2011, Marić 2011, Ramsoomar et al. 2013, Barsties et al. 2017, Koschollek et al. 2019, Nazir and Thabassum 2022).

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 29 software. To assess potential cross-country heterogeneity in the dependent variables, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted with country as the fixed factor and the eight dependent variables representing alcohol use, behavioral intent to reduce alcohol use, and the HBM constructs. The multivariate test was significant, Wilks’ Λ = .80, F(8, 40) = 11.77, P < .001, η2p = .04, indicating significant heterogeneity across countries. Univariate ANOVAs were subsequently examined to identify the differences between the countries.

To examine the predictors of adolescents’ alcohol use, we conducted a multiple regression analysis. We investigated whether the socio-demographics age, gender, country, and migration background predict adolescents’ alcohol use. Dummy variables were created for the categorical predictors gender, country, and migration background, with male, Greece (as the coordinating country of the research project) and migration background serving as the reference categories, respectively. A one-way ANOVA, with post hoc comparisons using the Scheffé test, was further conducted to examine differences in alcohol use between the different countries.

To investigate the predictors of adolescents’ intention to reduce alcohol use among those participants who indicated drinking alcohol, we conducted a hierarchical multiple regression. In the first block, we entered the socio-demographic factors age, gender, country, and migration background, again with dummy variables for gender, country, and migration background. Then, the HBM factors, namely perceived severity, perceived susceptibility, self-efficacy, response efficacy, perceived benefits, and perceived barriers were entered in the second block. A one-way ANOVA and Scheffé post-hoc test were again conducted to examine differences based on country. Finally, repeated-measures ANOVA and post-hoc comparisons using Bonferroni correction were conducted to examine which perceived benefits are most important for adolescents to reduce their alcohol use.

Results

Adolescents’ alcohol use and its predictors

When looking at adolescents’ alcohol use, 47.5% indicated never drinking any alcoholic beverages. Further, 35.7% indicated drinking alcohol less than once a week, 11.7% once a week, 4.3% several times a week and .9% daily.

The regression model explained 12.8% of the variance in alcohol use [R2 = .13, Adjusted R2 = .13; F(10, 3742) = 55.75, P < .001]. Age was a significant positive predictor of alcohol use (β = .30, P < .001): older adolescents reported higher alcohol consumption. Gender showed no significant effect (β = .03, P = .080). Migration background was negatively associated with alcohol use (β = −.03, P = .029), with adolescents with a migration background reporting lower use. Some country effects were also significant (βs between −.08 and .04). A One-Way ANOVA further revealed a significant effect of country on alcohol use [F(7, 4052) = 32.25, P < .001, η2p = .06]. Alcohol use was highest in Slovenia, followed by Italy, Switzerland, Cyprus, Belgium, Greece, and Romania. It was lowest in Spain. Alcohol use was significantly higher in Slovenia and Italy compared to Belgium, Greece, Romania, and Spain. It was also significantly higher in Switzerland compared to Spain and Romania. Adolescents’ intention to reduce alcohol use and the role of health belief model factors

As 47.5% of the participants indicated never drinking alcoholic beverages, the following analyses were conducted among the 52.5% participants (N = 2130) who indicated drinking alcohol (from ‘less than once a week’ to ‘daily’). Among those participants, the average intention to reduce their alcohol use was 2.85 (SD = 1.37) on a five-point scale, indicating a moderate level of intention to reduce alcohol consumption among the participants who consumed alcohol. In addition, there was a negative correlation between alcohol use and intention to reduce alcohol use (r = −.15, P < .001), indicating that the adolescents’ intention to reduce their alcohol consumption was lower among those who drank more often.

With regard to the socio-demographic factors, these only explained a negligible 3.8% of the variance in intention to reduce alcohol use [R2 = .04, Adjusted R2 = .04; F(10, 1975) = 8.88, P < .001]. Only age showed to be a significant predictor of intention to reduce alcohol use (β = −.10, P < .001): the intention to reduce alcohol consumption decreased with age. Gender (β = −.01, P = .751) and migration background (β = −.01, P = .665) were not significant predictors of intention to reduce alcohol use. Regarding country (βs between −.05 and .07) only the dummy-coded variable Romania showed a significant effect compared to the reference group (β = .14, P = .031). A One-Way ANOVA [F(7, 2128) = 1.49, P < .001, η2p = .03] further showed that the intention to reduce alcohol use was highest in Romania, followed by Greece, Italy, Switzerland, Spain, Cyprus, and Belgium. The intention to reduce alcohol use was lowest in Slovenia. The intention to reduce alcohol use was significantly higher in Romania compared to Slovenia, Spain, Italy, and Belgium. When the HBM factors were included, this significantly improved the model [ΔR2 = .17, F(6, 1958) = 67.86, P < .001]. The final model explained 20.1% of the variance in intention to reduce alcohol consumption [R2 = .21, Adjusted R2 = .20, F(16, 1975) = 32.13, P < .001]. Perceived susceptibility (β = .082, P < .001), self-efficacy (β = .15, P < .001), response efficacy (β = .079, P = .003), and perceived benefits (β = .28, P < .001) were positive predictors of intention to reduce alcohol use. Perceived severity (β = .04, P = .152) and perceived barriers (β = −.03, P = .245) were not significant predictors. These results confirm H2, H3, and H4, but cannot confirm H1 and H5. Perceived benefits were the strongest predictor of intention to reduce alcohol use followed by self-efficacy, perceived susceptibility, and response efficacy. All perceived benefits differed significantly from each other [F(4, 8408) = 983.63, P < .001, η2p = .32; all P < .001, except for one P = .006]. The benefit that it will lower the chances of developing health problems (M = 3.99, SE = .02) was rated highest. This was followed by the benefits that it will save them money (M = 3.89, SE = .03), make them proud (M = 3.45, SE = .03), and result in getting the approval of others (M = 2.75, SE = .03). The benefit that it will make them popular among friends received the lowest rating (M = 2.45, SE = .03).

Discussion

This study provides cross-national insights into adolescents’ alcohol use and their intention to reduce alcohol consumption. More than half of the adolescents in our sample reported having used alcohol. Age, migration background, and country significantly predicted alcohol use: older adolescents and those without a migration background reported higher consumption. Country-level differences also emerged, with alcohol use being highest in Slovenia and lowest in Spain. Regarding the intention to reduce alcohol use, adolescents showed a moderate willingness to cut down. Those who drank more frequently were less inclined to reduce their alcohol use, whereas younger adolescents reported higher intentions to do so. In addition, the psychological factors of the HBM, particularly perceived benefits and self-efficacy, were the strongest predictors of adolescents’ intention to reduce their alcohol use, underscoring the importance of health beliefs in shaping behavioral motivation.

Our findings confirm prior research showing that age, migration background, and country are important determinants of adolescent alcohol use. Consistent with earlier research (Marić 2011, Nazir and Thabassum 2022), alcohol consumption increased with age. In addition, adolescents with a migration background consumed alcohol less often, also aligning with earlier findings (Donath et al. 2011, Barsties et al. 2017, Koschollek et al. 2019). Moreover, country-specific differences were observed, suggesting that national alcohol policies, cultural norms, and alcohol availability shape adolescents’ drinking behaviors. Alcohol use was highest in countries such as Slovenia, Italy, and Switzerland, while it was lowest in Spain. This is in line with the classification of adolescent drinking behavior of Bräker and Soellner (2016), categorizing Central European countries as having the highest proportions of episodic drinking, while Spain is categorized as a country with mainly non-using adolescents. While earlier research often reports a higher alcohol use among males compared to females (Ramsoomar et al. 2013), our study did not confirm this. Recent studies show that while gender differences in alcohol use were more common in the past, girls’ drinking has increased in recent years, leading to smaller or no differences between boys and girls (Charrier et al. 2024, Raninen et al. 2024).

Adolescents showed a moderate intention to reduce their alcohol consumption, which highlights the relevance of health campaigns promoting this behavior. The inverse relationship between alcohol use and the intention to reduce consumption indicates that those who drink more often are less motivated to cut down. Moreover, while adolescent alcohol use increased with age and was lower among those with a migration background, the intention to reduce alcohol consumption decreased with age. Notably, countries with the highest alcohol use (e.g. Slovenia) show the lowest intention to reduce drinking, whereas countries with the lowest use (e.g. Romania) exhibit the highest intention to reduce. We found no gender differences in intention to reduce alcohol consumption, which is in line with the broader trend of girls’ drinking becoming more common (Raninen et al. 2024). Our findings suggest that health campaigns should particularly target older adolescents and those in high-consumption countries. It may also be valuable to engage younger adolescents, as they might be more receptive to reducing alcohol use, thereby supporting preventive efforts.

Our findings support some of the assumptions of the HBM. Perceived susceptibility, perceived efficacy, and perceived benefits were positively associated with adolescents’ behavioral intent to reduce alcohol consumption. Perceived severity and perceived barriers did not predict behavioral intent in this study. Perceived benefits emerged as the strongest predictor, suggesting that interventions should mainly emphasize the advantages of reducing alcohol use, rather than removing barriers. Adolescents’ belief that it lowers their chances of developing health problems was identified as the most important benefit. As such, campaigns should prioritize raising awareness about the short- and long-term health benefits associated with abstaining from or limiting alcohol consumption, such as the prevention of cancer. Increasing awareness about the health risks of alcohol, including its link to cancer, may enhance adolescents’ willingness to adopt healthier behaviors. It is already argued that public awareness on the link between alcohol and cancer needs to be increased (Rehm and Shield 2021), for instance through counter-marketing campaigns (Strebel and Terry 2021). In 2025, both academics and the WHO called for alcohol beverages to carry warning labels that warn for the risk of cancer (Ferreira-Borges et al. 2025), as public awareness of the link between alcohol and cancer among the general population remains limited (Kokole et al. 2023). Nonetheless, research has already shown that such labels can effectively increase awareness on the association between alcohol and cancer risks (Babor 2020). However, the alcohol industry has strongly opposed these measures, even leading to the suspension of research evaluating their impact (Babor 2020).

Additionally, highlighting financial savings may be an effective strategy, as saving money was rated as the second most important benefit for reducing alcohol consumption. This also shows that increasing taxes on alcohol can be an effective strategy to reduce adolescent alcohol consumption. Prior research has already shown that higher alcohol taxes are linked with reduced risk of escalating alcohol consumption patterns among adolescents (Fairman et al. 2019).

Social approval from friends was identified as the least important benefit. It is, however, important to note that this does not indicate that peer influence is not important in adolescents’ drinking behavior, as prior research has consistently shown that peer influence is an important predictor of young people’s alcohol consumption (Chawla et al. 2015, Steers et al. 2019). Because peer influence plays such a pivotal role in adolescent drinking, young people may not describe it as a viable motivator for ‘reducing’ alcohol consumption. More specifically, they might assume that their peers would neither support nor socially reinforce a behavior (i.e. abstaining from alcohol) that often serves as a means of constructing a positive social identity.

Following the perceived benefits, both aspects of perceived efficacy, namely self-efficacy and response efficacy, appear to be related to adolescents’ intention to reduce their alcohol consumption. Health campaigns can strengthen self-efficacy by providing adolescents with practical strategies to reduce their alcohol consumption, such as limiting and tracking the number of alcoholic drinks per week. In addition, campaigns could include relatable role models who share success stories of quitting their alcohol use, which can be inspiring and motivating, and can increase confidence in one’s own abilities. Simultaneously, response efficacy can be reinforced by highlighting the tangible health benefits of cutting back on drinking, which is in line with the findings on perceived benefits. Campaigns could present clear evidence that reducing alcohol consumption leads to both immediate (e.g. mental clarity, better sleep) and long-term health benefits (e.g. preventing cancer).

Despite the strengths of this study, including its large sample size and cross-national design, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to social desirability bias or recall inaccuracies. Second, while this study examined a range of socio-demographic predictors for alcohol use, it did not account for external environmental influences, such as parental drinking behaviors or media exposure. Third, a key limitation of our study is the variability in recruitment procedures across countries, which may have introduced sampling biases. Future research could use stratified sampling with predefined quotas for each stratum to avoid over- or underrepresentation of certain groups. Lastly, the cross-sectional nature of this study limits causal inferences. Longitudinal research is needed to determine the directionality of these relationships and explore how these factors evolve over time.

This study enhances our understanding of socio-demographic and psychological predictors of adolescent alcohol use and their intention to cut down across eight European countries. The findings underscore the importance of targeting older adolescents and youth in high-consumption contexts through tailored prevention campaigns. Emphasizing the health and financial benefits of reducing alcohol consumption and strengthening adolescents’ confidence to do so are key strategies for effective intervention. Future research should empirically test whether these strategies effectively change adolescents’ attitudes and behaviors regarding alcohol use. Longitudinal and experimental studies will be particularly valuable in determining which approaches most successfully promote lasting reductions in alcohol consumption.
