3D‐Printed Platforms for Enzyme Immobilisation Screening and Direct Translation into Continuous‐Flow Biocatalysis
Authors: Simone Marchetti, Gianluca Palmara, Cristopher Tinajero, Luis David Lisintuña, Paulo Roberto de Oliveira, Marcileia Zanatta, Victor Sans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of immobilised biocatalysts for continuous‐flow processes is still largely based on laborious trial‐and‐error screening, and conditions optimised on conventional carriers are often not directly transferable to structured reactors. Here, we introduce a 3D‐printed methodology that integrates high‐throughput screening of enzyme immobilisation with implementation in a continuous‐flow reactor using the same photopolymeric formulation. The surface of the printed objects was modified through imidazolium‐based supported ionic liquid phases. Immobilisation conditions for an enzymatic solution with alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH‐200) were rapidly evaluated in a 96‐well format using a colorimetric assay that enables quantitative comparison of activity and loading across multiples of conditions in a parallel fashion. Methyl‐imidazolium‐modified supports showed markedly higher enzymatic activity and immobilisation efficiency than other imidazolium‐based ionic liquids, and the best‐performing formulation was directly applied to functionalise the 3D‐printed honeycomb‐structured reactor. The resulting heterogeneous biocatalyst catalysed the oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol to acetophenone under continuous flow with high conversion at moderate residence times and sustained performance over several hundred hours on stream. This integrated 3D‐printed platform facilitates the identification of effective immobilisation chemistries and their translation to robust flow reactors, providing a general strategy for the rapid development of immobilised biocatalysts.Keywords: additive manufacturing, advanced materials, biocatalysis, continuous‐flow reactors, enzyme immobilisation, high‐throughput screening

Introduction

Drug discovery and development in the pharmaceutical industry have undergone a profound transformation in recent decades, strongly driven by the increasing implementation of biocatalysis in industrial biotechnology [1, 2, 3]. By using enzymes as (bio)catalysts, chemists can build complex molecules with high chemo‐, regio‐ and stereoselectivity, often under mild and environmentally friendly conditions [3, 4]. One key advantage of enzymatic routes is that they can avoid many protection and deprotection steps, which makes synthetic sequences shorter, faster and less wasteful [4, 5]. For these reasons, biocatalysis is now widely recognised as a key technology for the sustainable production of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and fine chemicals [1, 2, 4, 6].

Within this context, aromatic secondary alcohols and their corresponding ketones are relevant substrate classes in biocatalysis and fine chemical synthesis [7, 8, 9]. Among them, the 1‐phenylethanol/acetophenone redox pair was selected here as a benchmark model system because it is structurally simple, easy to analyse and widely used in ADH‐catalysed method development and continuous‐flow studies [10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

Alcohol dehydrogenases (ADHs) and related ketoreductases (KREDs) are widely employed to catalyse these transformations [3, 15]. They can perform enantioselective reductions of acetophenone to chiral 1‐phenylethanol or the reverse oxidation under appropriate conditions and cofactor management [15, 16, 17]. The growing availability of engineered ADHs and other enzymes has strongly expanded the synthetic toolbox of the pharmaceutical industry [3, 18]. However, several practical challenges still limit the broad adoption of biocatalytic processes on scale [2, 19]. These include the cost and regeneration of cofactors, limited substrate scope in some cases, constraints on space–time yields and the need to ensure robust operation under process‐relevant conditions [20, 21, 22].

A key strategy to address some of these issues is the enzyme immobilisation on solid supports, because immobilised enzymes are typically easier to separate from the reaction medium, can be reused and often show improved stability towards temperature, organic solvents and mechanical stress caused by pumping and mixing in flow reactors [23, 24]. This makes immobilisation particularly attractive for continuous‐flow operation, most commonly in packed‐bed reactors [25, 26]. In such systems, however, the performance of the biocatalyst depends strongly on the support material, the immobilisation chemistry, mass transfer and reactor hydrodynamics. Optimising these parameters is usually done by empirical trial‐and‐error, often with a limited set of commercial carriers, which can be slow and case‐specific [23, 24, 27].

Other relevant limitations of classic catalytic processes are limited scalability and relatively poor control over key reaction parameters. Continuous‐flow chemistry offers a well‐established route to improve both aspects by providing enhanced heat and mass transfer and precise control of reaction conditions [28, 29]. Within this framework, additive manufacturing (3D printing) has emerged as a complementary technology that enables the fabrication of custom flow reactors and structured supports tailored to a specific process [30, 31]. In particular, 3D printing allows the rapid and low‐cost production of devices with complex channel geometries, defined surface areas and integrated mixing features that are difficult to achieve with conventional manufacturing techniques [30, 31, 32]. In biocatalysis, 3D printing has been used both to produce reactors that host immobilised enzymes and to create enzyme‐containing materials such as hydrogels and porous scaffolds [33, 34, 35].

Despite these advances, there are still few examples where 3D‐printed materials act simultaneously as the reactor body and as a chemically tuneable support for enzyme immobilisation and where immobilisation conditions are systematically optimised in a format directly transferable to the final reactor [33, 36, 37, 38]. Among all these materials, polymeric supported ionic liquid phases have also attracted attention as versatile supports that can stabilise enzymes and improve their performance in flow [39, 40]. High‐throughput screening methods for immobilisation, for example in microplates or miniaturised packed beds, have been proposed to accelerate the discovery of suitable carriers and chemistries [41, 42, 43]. However, a methodology to directly translating the immobilisation conditions to scalable reactors has not been fully developed [30, 36, 41].

In this work, we developed a 3D‐printed platform that links immobilisation screening and continuous‐flow biocatalysis using the oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol to acetophenone catalysed by a commercial alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH‐200) as a benchmark reaction. This transformation was selected as a benchmark reaction because the 1‐phenylethanol/acetophenone redox pair is industrially relevant, structurally simple and widely employed in biocatalytic method development and flow studies [13, 14]. The same photopolymeric resin [44], processed by masked stereolithography (MSLA) 3D printing, is used to fabricate both microplate‐compatible screening devices and a structured reactor. The surface of the printed objects is modified using a variety of covalently immobilised imidazolium‐based polyionic liquids (PILs), which are evaluated in a high‐throughput microplate format to identify optimal immobilisation conditions and easily transferred to continuous‐flow oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol. The main idea of this work was to develop a simple 3D‐printed platform for enzyme immobilisation and continuous‐flow biocatalysis reactors. This was achieved by using the same process, consisting of the same printing technology (MSLA), the same photopolymeric formulation and the same post‐printing functionalisation, to avoid using different materials, which often make it difficult to facilitate the transfer of immobilisation conditions. In this way, the optimal results identified in the screening can be transferred to continuous‐flow reactors. This can simplify the path towards scaling‐up processes and their transfer to industry.

Results and Discussion

The employment of 3D printing techniques facilitates the rapid design and fabrication of multiple devices with the same formulations. Here, a MSLA 3D printer was used to fabricate a screening device compatible with a standard 96‐well plate, allowing many immobilisation conditions to be tested in parallel by a simple colorimetric assay, employing a previously optimised formulation (Figure 1) [44]. The printed plate is compatible with commercial plates and microplate readers, allowing for a rapid determination of optimal immobilisation and reaction conditions employing a colorimetric assay (central panel) [45]. The optimised protocol was then directly applied to print a structured reactor, in which the same material acts as the reactor body and as the support for the immobilised enzyme, enabling continuous‐flow operation under conditions consistent with the screening step.

MSLA 3D printer was used to fabricate a screening device and a structured reactor, enabling rapid optimisation of immobilisation conditions and their direct translation to continuous‐flow bioreactors.

Screening of Ionic Liquid Functionalisation for ADH‐200 Immobilisation

A high‐throughput screening platform was developed to test enzyme activity on 3D‐printed supports in a standard 96‐well microplate format (Figures 2A and S1). The device was based on a polymeric cap holding an array of hollow cylinders that match the geometry of a microplate, so each cylinder can be inserted into a well and act as an individual solid support without interfering with optical measurements. The cylinders were printed by MSLA using a glycidyl methacrylate formulation, and a separate 3D‐printed functionalisation rack (Figure S2), made from a chemically resistant resin, allowed us to carry out the ionic liquid modification under acidic conditions without damaging the polystyrene plates.

High‐throughput screening of surface functionalisation and enzymatic immobilisation. (A) 3D‐printed screening device mounted on a 3D‐printed 96‐well microplate for parallel functionalisation. (B) Schematic functionalisation via immobilised ILs. (C) Apparent activity of ADH‐200 immobilised on different functionalised supports, measured by the colorimetric assay. Reaction conditions: 10 mM (R)‐1‐phenylethanol, 5 mM NAD+, 2 mg mL−1 ADH‐200 immobilised solution, 10 min at room temperature. (D) Effect of ADH‐200 concentration on immobilised protein loading and immobilisation efficiency for the IL derived from 1‐methylimidazole. Incubation conditions: 5 min at room temperature.

The epoxy‐rich surface of the cylinders was then functionalised with a series of imidazolium‐based ionic liquids (I–VI) in ethanol/HCl, generating supported IL phases with different substituents on the imidazolium ring (Figure 2B). ATR–FTIR characterisation of all functionalised materials (Figure S3) showed disappearance or marked attenuation of the epoxy band of the parent polymer together with the appearance of new bands associated with the imidazolium functionalities, supporting successful surface modification across the whole series. Although these modifications are chemically simple, they are expected to influence the interfacial microenvironment experience by the enzyme. In particular, changing the substituents can alter surface polarity, local charge distribution, hydrophobicity, steric accessibility and hydrogen‐bonding ability, all of which may affect enzyme adsorption and the preservation of a catalytically competent hydration shell. In addition, the relatively hydrophobic character of the underlying photopolymeric matrix may not be equally favourable for all enzymes, and the improved performance observed here likely reflects a support–enzyme match specific to ADH‐200 rather than a universally optimal immobilisation environment. Since no direct structural characterisation of the immobilised protein was performed in this study, these effects should be interpreted as plausible contributors rather than as a direct demonstration of enzyme orientation or binding mode. After functionalisation and washing, the cylinders were incubated with ADH‐200 in phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), allowing immobilisation under identical conditions. This pH was selected as a fixed screening condition based on preliminary tests performed at different pH values using an alternative functionalisation route with polyethyleneimine (PEI) and glutaraldehyde (GA), in which pH 7.0 gave the highest activity (Figure S4), and was then maintained across the imidazolium series to allow direct comparison of the effect of surface chemistry under identical immobilisation conditions.

Enzyme activity was measured through the ADH‐200‐catalysed oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol to acetophenone, coupled to WST‐8 reduction (Figure 2C). The results clearly show that the choice of ionic liquid matters (Figure 2C, bar chart). A non‐functionalised polymer support (CONT, used as control) was included for comparison and showed only low activity. Supports modified with derivatives I and II gave the highest apparent activities, while derivatives III–VI resulted in much lower activity. A plausible explanation is that the best‐performing surfaces provide a better balance between ionic interactions with the enzyme and a sufficiently accessible, hydrated interface, whereas bulkier or more hydrophobic substituents may create a less favourable microenvironment by increasing steric crowding at the surface and/or perturbing substrate and cofactor access. Differences in local charge density and ion pairing may also influence the behaviour of NAD+ and the local pH experienced by the enzyme, further contributing to the observed activity differences. These considerations are consistent with previous reports on enzyme immobilisation using supported ionic liquid phases [46].

Using the best‐performing ionic liquid, the effect of enzyme concentration during immobilisation was subsequently investigated (Figure 2D). The plot reports the amount of protein immobilised per surface area as a function of the ADH‐200 concentration in the incubation solution. Increasing the enzyme concentration from 1 to 11 mg mL−1 led to higher surface loadings, which tended towards a plateau at the highest values. At the same time, the immobilisation efficiency (defined as the fraction of the offered protein that became immobilised) decreased with increasing concentration, indicating diminishing returns when using more enzyme in the incubation step. In this context, an incubation concentration of 8 mg mL−1 provided a good compromise between the amount of enzyme immobilised per unit area and the immobilisation efficiency, avoiding unnecessary consumption of biocatalyst while still achieving high surface loadings.

Overall, the screening protocol enabled rapid identification of a best‐performing ionic liquid–based functionalisation and suitable enzyme loading conditions for ADH‐200 immobilisation on the glycidyl acrylate devices, providing conditions that could be directly transferred to the corresponding flow reactor. In this context, the data in Figure 2C should be interpreted as a comparative screening of the apparent activity obtained after immobilisation on the different functionalised supports. Since protein loading and retained activity were not determined for the full set of imidazolium derivatives, the present dataset does not allow a full distinction between the contribution of enzyme binding and retained catalytic performance for each surface chemistry. Accordingly, the ionic liquid functionalisation with 1‐methylimidazole, which afforded the highest apparent activity in Figure 2C, combined with an immobilisation step using an ADH‐200 concentration of 8 mg mL−1, was selected as the standard protocol to prepare the 3D‐printed monolithic reactor described in the following section.

To further assess the catalytic relevance of the selected immobilisation condition, the activity of the immobilised ADH‐200 was compared with that of the free enzyme under identical reaction conditions (Figure S5). The free ADH‐200 showed a normalised activity of 1.02 µmol min−1 mg−1, whereas the immobilised ADH‐200 exhibited a normalised activity of 0.105 µmol min−1 mg−1. This corresponds to an activity retention of approximately 10% with respect to the homogeneous enzyme system.

The decrease in apparent normalised activity after immobilisation is consistent with the behaviour typically observed for heterogeneous enzymatic systems [23, 47]. This reduction may arise from a combination of enzyme–support interactions, reduced conformational mobility, partial limitations in substrate accessibility and possible diffusional effects associated with the 3D‐printed polymeric structure. Nevertheless, the selected immobilisation condition provided a suitable compromise between immobilised protein amount, immobilisation efficiency and retained catalytic performance, while enabling the operational advantages of a heterogeneous biocatalyst, including easier handling, catalyst recovery, potential reuse and direct integration into continuous‐flow reactor architectures.

Continuous‐Flow ADH‐200 Catalysis in the 3D‐Printed Reactor

The immobilisation conditions identified in the microplate screening were next transferred to a 3D‐printed reactor for the continuous‐flow oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol. The reactor was fabricated using the same glycidyl methacrylate–based formulation and ionic liquid functionalisation. The reactor is based on extruded hexagonal cells with 0.5 mm characteristic length, 0.13 mm wall thickness and 0.5 mm extruded distance (Figure 3A). The cells are fused into layers that form periodic open‐cell (POC) structure fabricated by stacking laterally shifted hexagonal strut‐extruded layers (AB stacking) along the axial direction and cropping the resulting lattice to a cylindrical envelope (Figure 3B–D). The architecture forms an interconnected open‐cell network with hexagonal windows in cross‐section and intrinsic radial and tangential flow components, promoting enhanced interfacial renewal. The structure exhibits a porosity of approximately 0.76 (76%) and a high solid–fluid interfacial area density, making it particularly suitable for enzyme immobilisation and intensified continuous‐flow biocatalysis. The architecture was cut into a cylindrical shaped structure from 1 cm diameter and 11 cm length (Figure 3E) and inserted into a glass Omnifit column.

CAD design of the 3D‐printed wall‐based POC reactor. (A) Hexagonal channel unit cell showing characteristic dimensions. (B) Planar slice obtained by periodic repetition of the unit cell. (C) Cross‐section of the cylindrical wall‐based POC reactor (1 cm diameter). (D) Perspective view of a short reactor segment illustrating the open wall‐based architecture. (E) Full 11‐cm‐long reactor used for continuous‐flow experiments.

A solution containing 1‐phenylethanol, NAD+ and acetone was pumped through the column, where ADH‐200 immobilised on the structure catalysed the oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol to acetophenone (Figure 4A). In this system, acetone is used for cofactor recycling. ADH‐200 first oxidises 1‐phenylethanol to acetophenone, converting NAD+ into NADH. The same enzyme can then use the NADH formed to reduce acetone to isopropanol, and in this second step, NADH is converted back to NAD+. In other words, the cofactor is continuously regenerated inside the reactor and only small amounts of NAD+ are required. The ordered honeycomb geometry maximises the surface area available for enzyme immobilisation while maintaining open channels for convective transport, thus providing high catalytic capacity with low pressure drop and a uniform flow distribution. The hydrodynamic behaviour of the 3D‐printed reactor was evaluated by residence time distribution (RTD) experiments using a non‐reactive tracer (Figure 4B). The RTD curves showed a symmetric shape with maximum closed to a mean residence time of 1. This is indicative of a low axial dispersion. Increasing the flow rate from 0.128 to 0.50 mL min−1 decreases the mean residence time and sharpens the RTD peaks, reflecting more confined residence times at higher flow. The absence of pronounced shoulders or long tails suggests that stagnant regions, dead volumes and preferential channels are minimal within the reactor. The observed tail was mainly associated to the tubing and connectors within the RTD set‐up. These features confirm that the internal geometry of the reactor ensures efficient use of the available volume, which is essential for predictable kinetic performance and scale‐up.

Continuous‐flow oxidations of 1‐phenylethanol were then carried out at the same flow rates in order to relate reactor hydrodynamics to catalytic performance. The conversion of 1‐phenylethanol increases with residence time (Figure 4C), reaching high values at the longest residence time investigated. Notably, the increase in conversion when extending the residence time from 60 to 90 min was negligible (ca. 2% increase). On this basis, a residence time of 60 min was selected as a good compromise between high conversion and reasonable processing time and was used for long‐term stability experiments. The overall trend suggests that the reactor has limited axial mixing, leading to efficient mixing, thus resulting in increased contact time between the substrate/cofactor mixture and the immobilised enzyme directly translates into higher conversion.

A photograph of the experimental set‐up is shown in Figure 4D. The system includes an HPLC pump delivering the reaction solution, the Omnifit column containing the 3D‐printed reactor and a 3D‐printed auto‐sampler used to collect samples at defined time intervals. The extensive use of additive manufactured components highlights the versatility of the different 3D printing techniques for assembling modular continuous‐flow biocatalytic platforms tailored to specific transformations.

Finally, the long‐term operational stability of the immobilised ADH‐200 reactor was assessed at a fixed residence time of 60 min (Figure 4E). Under these conditions, the reaction was first run continuously for 72 h (black symbols), during which effluent samples were periodically collected and analysed by gas chromatography to determine the conversion of 1‐phenylethanol to acetophenone (see Section 3). After this initial period, the Omnifit column containing the 3D‐printed immobilised reactor was stored immersed in phosphate buffer at 4°C (grey breaks) and then brought back to operation under continuous flow with the same reaction conditions at 480 and 720 h from the beginning of the first run, still remarkably providing conversions above 80% and 75%, respectively. These results show that the combination of ionic liquid functionalisation and 3D‐printed polymeric matrix provides a robust microenvironment for ADH‐200, enabling sustained cofactor‐recycled oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol in flow with favourable hydrodynamics, high conversions and excellent operational and storage stability. To quantitatively assess the material efficiency and sustainability of the developed system, green metrics commonly used in fine chemical manufacturing were calculated, namely the reaction‐stream E‐factor and the process mass intensity (PMI). Considering the first 72 h of continuous‐flow experimentation, the calculated reaction‐stream E‐factor of 13.52 and the PMI 14.52 (water excluded) fall within the typical range reported for fine‐chemical processes (E‐factor = 5–50) [48]. The calculations along with the boundaries selected can be found in the Supporting Information. This suggests that the immobilised enzyme continuous‐flow reactor achieves material efficiencies consistent with state‐of‐the‐art catalytic processes.

Experimental Section

Enzyme Immobilisation Protocols

After functionalisation, our 3D‐printed plate device was placed into a 96‐well microplate and immobilised with ADH‐200 solution in phosphate buffer, pH 7.0, using 200 µL per well to fully immerse the cylinders. The enzyme concentration in the incubation solution was varied in the range 1–11 mg mL−1 to evaluate the protein loading on immobilisation efficiency. After immobilisation at room temperature for 20 h, each well was washed three times with fresh buffer to remove unbound or loosely bound enzyme.

The continuous‐flow reactor was put in a glass test tube and incubated by immersing it in 12 mL of ADH‐200 solution in phosphate buffer pH 7.0 at a concentration of 8 mg mL−1; it was then agitated on a magnetic stirrer (200–250 rpm) at room temperature for 20 h to allow enzyme binding. After incubation, the enzyme solution was removed, and the reactor was washed three times with buffer to remove unbound enzyme.

Enzymatic Activity Assay in Microplate Reader

The enzymatic activity of immobilised ADH‐200 was quantified using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8), a commercial colorimetric assay based on the water‐soluble tetrazolium salt WST‐8. In this system, WST‐8 [2‐(2‐methoxy‐4‐nitrophenyl)‐3‐(4‐nitrophenyl)‐5‐(2,4‐disulfophenyl)‐2H‐tetrazolium, monosodium salt] is reduced in the presence of an electron carrier to a water‐soluble brownish formazan dye, whose formation can be monitored at 450 nm (Figures S6 and S7).

A 200 µL reaction mixture containing 10 mM (R)‐1‐phenylethanol, 5 mM NAD+ and the CCK‐8 reagent in phosphate buffer was added to the wells of our 3D‐printed screening device, immobilised with a 2 mg mL−1 ADH‐200 solution. In this assay, (R)‐1‐phenylethanol is oxidised to acetophenone in the presence of NAD+, which is concomitantly reduced to NADH. The reduced cofactor then acts as electron donor for the WST‐8 contained in the CCK‐8 kit, leading to the formation of the formazan dye. The increase in absorbance (Figure S8) at 450 nm, corresponding to formazan production, was used as an indirect measure of the enzymatic conversion of substrate to product in solution.

Protein Quantification

Protein quantification of the incubation solutions was performed using the Pierce 660 nm Protein Assay in cuvette format. Aliquots of the enzyme solution were taken before and after incubation of the wall‐based POC reactor (surface area 2387.95 mm2) with ADH‐200 solutions in phosphate buffer at different enzyme concentrations in the range from 1 to 11 mg mL−1. For the incubations made at 5, 8 and 11 mg mL−1, aliquots were diluted to 1 mg mL−1 prior to analysis to ensure that the measurements fell in the linear range of the assay. For each measurement, 100 µL of sample or protein standard was mixed with 1.5 mL of Pierce 660 nm reagent in a 1 cm pathlength cuvette, vortexed to ensure homogenisation and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The absorbance at 660 nm was then recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer, and protein concentrations were determined from a previously established calibration curve (Figure S9). The amount of immobilised protein was calculated from the difference between the initial and final protein content in solution, and the immobilisation efficiency was expressed as the ratio between the immobilised protein and the initial protein amount before incubation.

RTD study

The experimental setup (Figure S10) consisted of two syringe pumps: one used to control the main flow rate and the other to load a 50 μL loop connected to an automated six‐way Rheodyne valve. This valve enabled the injection of a tracer pulse into the flow stream. The valve outlet was connected to a commercial flow cell, through which a light beam was passed and detected by a UV–visible spectrometer operating at a fixed wavelength. All components were connected using 1/16‐inch PEEK capillary tubing. The experiments were performed using isopropanol as the solvent and a Methyl Red tracer solution (1 mg L−1) in the same solvent, at a flow rate of 0.128–0.5 mL min−1. The change in tracer concentration over time was monitored by measuring the absorbance peak at 525 nm in the UV–Vis spectra.

Continuous‐Flow Reaction

Continuous‐flow experiments were carried out using the 3D‐printed reactor containing immobilised ADH‐200 (8 mg mL−1 in phosphate buffer, pH 7.0) packed inside an Omnifit glass column.

For the oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol, the immobilised bioreactor was fed with a reaction mixture containing 10 mM 1‐phenylethanol, 2.5 mM NAD+ and 50 mM acetone in buffer. For residence time experiments, a total volume corresponding to five times the reactor free volume was pumped through the column at each tested flow rate. The system was allowed to reach steady state by passing at least three reactor bed volumes before sampling. Effluent samples were then collected in triplicate over the subsequent two bed volumes, corresponding in total to five void volumes for each condition. Samples were analysed by using gas chromatography to determine the conversion of 1‐phenylethanol to acetophenone, whose calibration curves are reported in Figures S11 and S12.

For long‐term stability tests, the same reaction mixture was continuously passed through the immobilised reactor at a fixed residence time, using a total pumped volume sized according to the targeted number of hours on stream. Effluent samples were periodically collected with a 3D‐printed and automated auto‐sampler (Figure S13) over the course of the experiment and analysed to monitor the evolution of catalytic performance with time.

Conclusions

In this study, we have established an integrated 3D‐printed platform that uses the same photopolymeric material and surface chemistry for both high‐throughput screening and continuous‐flow biocatalysis. A custom MSLA‐printed screening device enabled rapid evaluation of imidazolium‐based ionic liquid functionalisation and enzyme loading for ADH‐200, identifying selected IL‐based surface chemistries and an incubation concentration of 8 mg mL−1 as the best‐performing combination within the screened conditions. While enzyme‐specific optimisation may still be required, this platform reduces trial‐and‐error associated with the transition from screening to flow by enabling the direct transfer of immobilisation conditions using a unified material.

These conditions were directly transferred to a highly intricated 3D‐printed POC reactor with hexagonal structures. The efficient mixing together with the elevated surface area resulted in the efficient NAD+‐dependent oxidation of 1‐phenylethanol with in situ cofactor recycling. High conversions were achieved at a 60 min residence time, and the system displayed excellent stability over prolonged continuous operation and after storage. Overall, this approach provides a simple and modular blueprint for designing 3D‐printed immobilisation supports and flow reactors that can be consistently used from screening to continuous processing. Future work will focus on expanding the platform to other enzymes and reactions, as well as exploring new immobilisation strategies to further enhance performance and versatility. This approach has the potential to open a wide scope in the field, enabling more versatile and efficient biocatalytic processes.
