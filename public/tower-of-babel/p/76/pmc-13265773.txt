activeDISCO enables large-scale exploration of human brain anatomy by light sheet microscopy
Authors: James Oakes-Klein, Christoph Fuchssteiner, Saiedeh Saghafi, Peter C Brugger, Klaus Becker, Sebastian Böhm, Karim Abd El-Ghany, Gregor Kasprian, Hans-Ulrich Dodt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTissue clearing and light microscopy of large human brain specimens remains challenging due to factors including accumulation of pigments and lengthy protocols required to achieve a high level of optical clarity. In this study we present a novel solvent-based clearing protocol capable of clearing intact human brain regions such as the hippocampus and cerebellar hemispheres in around 12 days. We term this protocol “activeDISCO”, as it utilises active dehydration by the ketal 2,2-dimethoxypropane which rapidly reacts with water, therefore expediting the clearing process. We subsequently performed imaging of autofluorescence using meso-aspheric based light sheet microscopy and were able to reconstruct intact brain regions in three dimensions, including high-resolution visualisation of structures such as the dentate gyrus and Horn of Ammon in the hippocampus. This work represents a tool with diverse applications in the fields of neuroscience and medicine; and sets a baseline for further improvements to active dehydration based clearing protocols and imaging possibilities.Subject terms: Light-sheet microscopy, Brain, Fluorescence imaging, Optical imaging

Introduction

In the last decades, the tissue clearing field has made a great deal of progress in the clearing and imaging of intact mouse brains1–3. In contrast, the human brain has received relatively little attention; the reasons for this are several-fold. The small size of the mouse brain allows straightforward clearing and imaging of intact specimens, whereas comparable processing of larger brain specimens presents significant challenges such as incomplete penetration of clearing reagents. Tissue is generally collected from mice or other model organisms when they are still relatively young, in contrast, human brain specimens are usually obtained from aged body donors. Aged tissue is considerably more difficult to clear due to the accumulation of pigments such as lipofuscin and melanin, and the associated increased light scattering hinders light sheet imaging4.

Despite the difficulties faced by those wishing to clear large human brain specimens, several studies have made notable contributions to this objective including the solvent-based clearing protocols MASH and SHANEL5,6 (Supplementary Table 1). Solvent-based tissue clearing protocols rely on dehydration and subsequent refractive index matching to render tissues optically transparent; usually these methods also employ some method of tissue decolourisation and delipidation. MASH utilises an iDISCO-based approach for tissue bleaching by hydrogen peroxide7 whereas SHANEL uses depigmentation by long incubations in a solution of CHAPS detergent and N-methyldiethanolamine. iDISCO, MASH and SHANEL achieve tissue dehydration by application of a solvent gradient of tetrahydrofuran, methanol/dichloromethane or ethanol respectively. After tissue dehydration, iDISCO and SHANEL include an additional dichloromethane (DCM) incubation to facilitate delipidation. The final stage of these solvent-based tissue clearing protocols is refractive index matching by dibenzyl ether (iDISCO), 2,2’-thiodiethanol/trans-cinnamaldehyde or methyl salicylate/trans-cinnamaldehyde (MASH), or benzyl alcohol:benzyl benzoate (SHANEL). MASH was able to clear human brain samples up to a thickness of 5 mm whereas SHANEL could clear a 15 mm human brain slice. iDISCO was optimised for use on intact adult mouse brains, but has also been used for clearing of human brain specimens up to 5 mm in thickness4,8. Another solvent-based clearing protocol, 3DISCO, was utilised for clearing 4 mm thick human brain specimens9,10. The Visikol and ECi tissue clearing methods do not perform well for clearing of human brain specimens4.

A plethora of hydrophilic clearing methods have also been applied to human brain tissue, including CLARITY, SHIELD, SWITCH, SHORT, ScaleS, Opticlear, CUBIC, HxD and hFRUIT11–19 (Supplementary Table 1). A limitation of aqueous approaches is that they are usually restricted to samples thinner than 1 mm, or else require months of clearing to achieve sufficient transparency. Some aqueous protocols are able to clear thicker specimens; notably, a high level of transparency was achieved in a 5 mm thick human brain specimen using CLARITY processing, however this required more than 4 months of processing4. A further study was able to clear an 8 mm specimen with 10 months of CLARITY processing20. The hFRUIT protocol achieved reasonable transparency in a 7 mm human brain specimen with a processing time of 11 days13. So far, aqueous clearing methods have been outperformed by solvent-based methods when it comes to clearing large human brain specimens, however they have some advantages such as being generally more amenable to immunolabelling techniques.

Following clearing, tissues are commonly imaged using a 3D light microscopy technique such as confocal or light sheet microscopy. Despite advances in clearing technology, with samples up to 15 mm thickness rendered transparent in the visible light spectrum6, the maximum volumes imaged by light microscopy have so far been much smaller. The largest human brain specimen recorded by light sheet microscopy (ct-dSPIM) is a 50 x 35 x 3 mm (~5 cm3) occipital lobe specimen21 whereas a 7.5 x 5 x 0.4 cm (15 cm3) specimen was recorded using confocal microscopy (MAVIG RSG4 upright confocal microscope)6. Despite the utility of confocal microscopes to scan many adjacent fields of view, the maximum specimen thickness to which the technique can be applied is limited. In contrast, light sheet microscopy is ideally suited for imaging thick specimens at a considerable depth, with the limiting factors being tissue transparency, light sheet quality, focal length, and imaging objective working distance.

A major challenge in light sheet microscopy, addressed in our previous research, is the generation of a high-quality light sheet22–26. An ideal light sheet would have a small beam waist, long Rayleigh range and minimal power loss through beam truncation or side shoulders. In the simplest optical design for illuminating specimens with a planar sheet of light, a combination of a Keplerian or Galilean beam magnifier, with a slit aperture and a cylindrical lens is utilised. However, a common problem arises wherein the resulting light sheet exhibits either a small focus that expands rapidly with numerous side shoulders around the focal point or a thick light sheet with an extended Rayleigh range. An additional problem is that truncation of the beam using a slit aperture leads to substantial power loss.

In previous work we effectively tackled these challenges by first incorporating a beam expander in conjunction with a straightforward three-cylindrical lens design, a configuration commonly employed in mode-converters for achieving control over both phase and amplitude26. Although there was a noticeable enhancement in the uniformity of the intensity distribution across the light sheet at the focus and its surrounding area, our goal shifted to extending this uniformity while generating a thinner light sheet. We subsequently enhanced the design of our optical light sheet generator by incorporating an axial-meso-aspheric element, specifically a Powell lens, at the focal point of the initial lens within our Keplerian magnifier unit. Guiding the output beam toward another optical unit housing two aspheric-cylindrical lenses of specific characteristics allowed us to achieve a thin light sheet with optimized optical parameters24. The resulting system was subsequently used for imaging a variety of cleared specimens including mouse brains and embryos, human breast cancer samples, Drosophila melanogaster and a variety of other model organisms25,27–30.

With light sheet microscopy imaging in mind, we aimed to develop an improved protocol for clearing of large human brain specimens. This involved combination of powerful hydrogen peroxide bleaching with a method for rapid and efficient dehydration. The solvent-based clearing protocols described above rely on passive dehydration of tissues by exposing them to increasing concentrations of solvents. Whilst effective for the clearing of relatively small tissue samples, these techniques require lengthy dehydration stages which rapidly increase in duration with greater tissue size – for example the SHANEL protocol required more than one month to clear a 15 mm thick human brain specimen. In order to speed up the dehydration process our group developed the first active dehydration protocol for tissue clearing: pathoDISCO30. This protocol utilises the ketal 2,2-dimethoxypropane (DMP) for sample dehydration. In the presence of an acid catalyst, DMP undergoes rapid hydrolysis upon contact with water to yield methanol and acetone31,32. This means that specimens can be dehydrated in a rapid single step rather than by a solvent series of increasing concentration.

By combining depigmentation methods from iDISCO with our method of active dehydration using DMP, we have developed a novel protocol that we term “activeDISCO”. ActiveDISCO is able to clear human brain specimens up to at least 20 mm in thickness, including intact brain regions such as the hippocampus and medulla oblongata. Following clearing we applied light sheet microscopy to the specimens. Autofluorescence imaging in the far-red range allowed 3D visualisation of large specimens with resolution in the cellular range. We have demonstrated for the first time the clearing of intact human brain regions such as the hippocampus and the application of multiresolution light sheet microscopy allowing the identification of major sub-regions such as the Horn of Ammon and dentate gyrus in the entire specimen, as well as microanatomical features such as the CA1-4 fields and cellular layers in selected regions of interest.

Overall, this work represents a significant addition to the palette of tissue clearing methods available to those wishing to perform optical imaging on large human brain specimens and introduces the possibility of imaging large intact brain regions with cellular resolution.

Results

Optimisation and characterisation of the activeDISCO protocol

pathoDISCO is the only previous clearing protocol to utilise chemical dehydration by 2,2-dimethoxypropane, allowing clearing of 5 mm thick human breast cancer specimens30. In order to clear even larger specimens, we made several modifications to the protocol including addition of detergent based permeabilisation with CHAPS, bleaching with hydrogen peroxide, prolongation of the active dehydration phase, and addition of a dichloromethane delipidation treatment. We found the presence of dark-yellow to brown-red pigmentation to be the greatest barrier to optical clarity. In smaller specimens, this level of pigmentation does not significantly impair optical clarity; however, the cumulative effect throughout a thicker specimen can be severe. Although a variety of chemical treatments can be utilised for decolourisation, the best results are observed when using hydrogen peroxide; with this in mind, we attempted to optimise H2O2 bleaching conditions. After exposing six groups of pig brain hemispheres (n = 3) to a range of H2O2 concentrations between 0% and 30% for approximately 16 hours (overnight) at 4 °C we found that the maximum bleaching effect was achieved with 10% H2O2, prepared in methanol (e.g., 20 ml 50% aqueous H2O2 + 80 ml methanol). A sequential increase in specimen clarity was observed up to 10% H2O2, however further increases in concentration did not yield further improvements in decolourisation (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 1). We also examined the effect of activeDISCO on the morphometric parameters across all pig brain hemispheres. Pole-pole distance and telencephalic thickness were reduced 1.28- and 1.25-fold, whilst weight and volume were reduced 2.04- and 2.12-fold respectively (Fig. 1B). These findings demonstrate isotropic shrinkage as the change in linear measurements was similar in all axes and the cube of linear measurements approximately equalled the change in volume – i.e., 1.283 = 2.1 ≈ 2.12. Isotropic shrinkage is an important factor for high-quality microscopic imaging as it ensures accurate spatial representation of tissue architecture.

Further experimentation revealed that in 10 mm thick human telencephalon specimens, decolourisation continued to improve beyond 10% H2O2 with the maximum effect seen at 30% (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Fig. 2). Light sheet imaging of autofluorescence with 638 nm excitation revealed that resolution was comparable at 1 mm, 3 mm, 5 mm and 7 mm depths, even in the absence of H2O2 bleaching (Fig. 1C). However, H2O2 bleaching was essential to avoid reduced intensity with increased z-depth (Fig. 1E). Additionally, higher H2O2 treatment concentration caused a reduction in autofluorescence signal, with a drastic reduction seen with 20% and 30% treatment (Fig. 1C, E). When using 488 nm illumination, a reduction in intensity with increasing z-depth was observed with all H2O2 treatments, however higher concentrations yielded less severe intensity reductions (Fig. 1D, F). Specimens treated with 0-5% H2O2 exhibited a reduction of pixel intensities towards the centre of optical slices, suggesting significant scattering of the illumination light sheet. In contrast, more uniform illumination was observed in samples treated with >10% H2O2 (Fig. 1D). Finally, we measured the transmittance of a 638 nm laser through the specimen, observing that increased H2O2 was significantly associated with increased laser transmittance (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1G). No chemical induced distortion of tissue was observed, even with the highest H2O2 concentration.

As we were principally interested in red-range autofluorescence imaging, we chose to use a 10% H2O2 treatment for preparation of subsequent samples. This concentration allows uniform illumination with both 638 nm and 488 nm lasers, whilst also retaining sufficient autofluorescence signal in the far-red emission range.

activeDISCO performs favourably in comparison to other protocols for clearing thick human brain specimens

Previously published protocols for solvent-based clearing of larger brain specimens include MASH, SHANEL and iDISCO (Fig. 2A)5–7. In order to characterise the effectiveness of activeDISCO in comparison to these protocols we cleared human telencephalon samples of 5, 10 and 20 mm thickness (n = 3) with each protocol. Qualitatively, specimens cleared by activeDISCO became more transparent than tissues cleared by other protocols (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 3). In particular, orange-brown pigmentation was reduced, indicating improved decolourisation of pigments due to the optimised bleaching conditions. 5 mm thick human telencephalon specimens were cleared to a reasonable degree by all protocols, however 20 mm samples processed by iDISCO, MASH or SHANEL exhibited considerably greater opacity in comparison to the corresponding activeDISCO samples. This result is likely due to improved dehydration by the activeDISCO reagent 2,2-dimethoxypropane. 2,2-dimethoxypropane was also used in the previously published pathoDISCO protocol30. ActiveDISCO cleared specimens exhibited greater transparency than those cleared by pathoDISCO, due to additional bleaching and delipidation stages, as well as prolongation of the dehydration stage (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 4).

To investigate how the improved transparency of activeDISCO cleared samples affected microscopic imaging quality, we performed light sheet imaging of autofluorescence in specimens cleared by each of the protocols to a maximum depth of approximately 9 mm (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 5). At 1 mm imaging depth fine details such as lipofuscin and vasculature autofluorescence could be observed in samples cleared by all four protocols. At 4 mm imaging depth image quality was considerably reduced in iDISCO, MASH and SHANEL cleared specimens, and at 9 mm almost no details could be distinguished in samples cleared by these protocols. In contrast, activeDISCO cleared samples exhibited almost no loss in resolution.

To quantify the transparency of the tissue we measured the transmission of a 638 nm laser through the specimens. Specimens cleared by activeDISCO exhibited significantly higher transmittance than MASH specimens at 5 mm thickness (p = 0.0005) and both iDISCO and MASH specimens at 10 mm and 20 mm thickness (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D).

Clearing and light sheet microscopy imaging of human brain specimens reveals minute structural details

Telencephalon

activeDISCO allowed clearing of 20 mm thick human telencephalon specimens to a transparency sufficient for high-resolution light sheet microscopy imaging to a depth of at least 10 mm – the working distance of our lowest power objective (Olympus 2x XLFluor-340) (Fig. 3, Supplementary Video 1). Using a custom static light sheet system we illuminated the cleared specimens with a 638 nm laser and recorded autofluorescence using a 690 nm long pass emission filter. Utilisation of various objectives allowed lower resolution imaging of the full 20 mm thickness, as well as higher resolution imaging of specific regions of interest. Autofluorescence imaging with 2x and 4x magnification allowed differentiation of grey and white matter due to accumulation of fluorescent molecules in neuronal cell bodies; as well as visualisation of distal branches of the anterior cerebral artery (Fig. 3A–G). Use of a 16x objective allowed resolution of individual cortical neuron cell bodies due to accumulation of fluorescent lipofuscin particles (Fig. 3H–K).

Hippocampus

activeDISCO is capable of rendering an intact human hippocampus transparent, making it possible to identify the complexity of the hippocampal microanatomy in its entirety (Fig. 4, Supplementary Video 2). Autofluorescence imaging with 2x and 4x magnification allowed identification of the main anatomical features of the hippocampal formation, such as the dentate gyrus, Ammon’s horn and the subiculum (Fig. 4C–G). Imaging with a 4x objective allowed differentiation of cytoarchitectural features such as the CA1-4 fields embedded within Ammon’s horn. Finally, imaging with 16x magnification allows visualisation of the individual cellular layers of dentate gyrus and Ammon’s horn. These are well defined due to accumulated fluorescent molecules in neuronal cell bodies (Fig. 4H-J).

Medulla oblongata

After clearing by activeDISCO an intact medulla oblongata was subjected to autofluorescence imaging. Using 2x magnification allowed identification of major neuroanatomical aspects of the human medulla oblongata, such as the inferior olivary nucleus, the medial accessory olivary nucleus and the arcuate nucleus (Fig. 5A–C). With 4x magnification it was possible to image the whole inferior olivary nucleus (Fig. 5E).

Cerebellar hemisphere

An intact cerebellar hemisphere was cleared by activeDISCO and imaged by light sheet microscopy to record autofluorescence with 638 nm excitation and 690 nm emission wavelengths (Fig. 5F–I, Supplementary Video 3). The volume of the uncleared specimen was ~55 cm3. Post-clearing, the specimen had dimensions of approximately 41 x 38 x 44 mm and a volume of ~26 cm3 making it one of the larger, if not the largest, specimen so far recorded by light sheet microscopy. The specimen was recorded with 2x magnification from 2 angles with a total of 82 tiles and approximately 120 K images. A reduction of imaging resolution and loss of contrast was evident towards the centre of the specimen, especially at imaging depths greater than ~14 mm (Fig. 5I, J, Supplementary Fig. 6). Autofluorescence imaging of a 10 mm cerebellum slice using a 4x objective allowed differentiation of microanatomical features of a cerebellar folium, such as the granular layer, the Purkinje cell layer and molecular layer (Fig. 5J-K, Supplementary Video 4). Imaging with a 16x objective allowed identification of single neuronal cell bodies and vasculature within the granular layer (Fig. 5L, M).

activeDISCO allows light sheet imaging across the range of commonly used excitation wavelengths

Longer wavelengths are preferable for imaging of very large samples as they are less susceptible to scattering by biological tissue33. However, research applications of light sheet microscopy commonly require imaging in several channels in the range of 480-700 nm. In order to assess the performance of activeDISCO clearing in this range, we imaged 5 mm cerebellum (Fig. 6) and 10 mm telencephalon (Supplementary Fig. 7) slices using 488 nm, 532 nm and 638 nm laser lines. Imaging of cerebellum and telencephalon specimens with 638 nm and 532 nm excitation allowed imaging to more than 4 mm and 9 mm depth, respectively, with minimal reduction in intensity. Imaging with 488 nm excitation resulted in severe attenuation of intensity at depths of > 3 mm. Interestingly, the autofluorescence pattern differed considerably depending on the wavelength used, with lower wavelengths producing images with brighter vasculature, and higher wavelengths images with brighter lipofuscin. After correcting for the intensity drop in the 488 nm channel, the vasculature could be segmented and combined with the 638 nm channel to provide informative reconstructions of the lipofuscin and vasculature distribution (Fig. 6B–D).

Discussion

We have developed a novel tissue clearing method which we term activeDISCO. Using this method, we successfully cleared intact pig cerebral hemispheres and, notably, 5–20 mm thick human telencephalon specimens, and an intact hippocampus, medulla oblongata and cerebellar hemisphere. The clearing efficiency of activeDISCO was compared to that of other common protocols: iDISCO, MASH and SHANEL. The transparency of the specimens was assessed qualitatively by brightfield illumination and quantitatively by measuring transmittance of a laser beam (Fig. 2). Optical transparency in hydrophobically cleared samples is influenced by depigmentation, dehydration, and delipidation efficiency. Pigments like haem, melanin, or lipofuscin can scatter or absorb light, reducing light sheet penetration and imaging quality. Additionally, water or lipids cause refractive index mismatches, further diminishing image resolution.

Of the four protocols tested SHANEL is the only protocol which does not use hydrogen peroxide, widely considered the strongest decolourisation method; instead SHANEL uses the aminoalcohol N-methyldiethanolamine6. The superior decolourisation of pigments by hydrogen peroxide can be clearly observed in activeDISCO when compared with SHANEL cleared specimens (Fig. 2). MASH cleared specimens exhibited a distinct orange hue after clearing, probably due the colour of the refractive index matching solution. In contrast to the other three protocols tested, activeDISCO utilises active rather than passive dehydration30. This technique exploits the reaction of 2,2-dimethoxypropane with water to produce acetone and methanol, eliminating water from the sample. The larger specimens cleared by other protocols were somewhat or wholly opaque, suggesting incomplete dehydration (Fig. 2). Finally, activeDISCO may provide an improvement in delipidation due to probable delipidation effects of 2,2-dimethoxypropane34. activeDISCO allowed imaging of fine structures such as neuronal cell bodies and vasculature at depths of > 9 mm, an improvement compared to other methods (Fig. 2). This is perhaps the most important measure of clearing protocol effectiveness as the desire to produce high-quality volumetric imaging is the most common reason for performing a tissue clearing protocol.

Following development of the activeDISCO protocol we processed a range of human brain specimens; specifically, 5–20 mm thick cerebrum and cerebellum slices, and an intact hippocampus, cerebellar hemisphere and medulla oblongata (Figs. 3–6). This was followed by multiresolution light sheet imaging of autofluorescence in the far-red range (excitation 638 nm, emission 690 nm long pass). This approach allows recording of an intact specimen with lower resolution (e.g., 2x NA0.14 or 4x NA0.28 objectives) followed by higher resolution imaging (e.g., 16x NA0.6 objective) of specific regions of interest. The combination of this imaging paradigm with activeDISCO clearing allowed us to perform light sheet microscopy and 3D reconstruction on samples as large as a human cerebellar hemisphere (imaged volume 25.48 cm3), significantly larger than previously reported6,21.

The most frequently targeted human brain region in clearing and light sheet experiments is the cerebral cortex. activeDISCO has the ability to clear telencephalon slices in excess of 20 mm and low magnification light sheet imaging of autofluorescence allows characterisation of the arterial vasculature and grey and white matter regions. Medium and high magnification imaging allows visualisation of single cells and some discrimination of cellular layers may also be observed (Fig. 3, Supplementary Video 1). Tissue clearing and light sheet imaging have rarely been applied to human brain specimens outside of the cerebrum, here we demonstrate clearing and light sheet imaging of an intact hippocampus, cerebellar hemisphere and medulla oblongata. In these specimens we were able to combine lower magnification image of the intact specimen, allowing visualisation of macro-anatomical elements such as the hippocampal body, subiculum and primary and secondary sulci in the cerebellum, with high magnification imaging of specific regions allowing characterisation of cellular layers in the Ammon’s Horn of the hippocampus as well as the folia cortex, Purkinje cell layer and dentate nucleus of the cerebellum (Fig. 4 + 5, Supplementary Videos 2-4). This ability to image macro-anatomical architecture in combination with cellular resolution imaging of specific regions represents a major advantage of activeDISCO. The combination of activeDISCO with advanced image analysis pipelines may allow characterisation of a variety of anatomical parameters such as vessel size, tortuosity, and regression, as well as cell density or number. An advantage of activeDISCO would be the ability to perform such analysis in macro-scale specimens, giving a clearer representation of tissue architecture rather than relying on snapshots from small specific brain regions. However, the activeDISCO protocol would need to be adapted with robust labelling protocols to ensure accurate and complete labelling of vasculature and/or cells. Cellular labelling by cresyl violet is compatible with the very similar pathoDISCO-HE protocol, but this has not yet been tested with large slabs of human brain tissue and the activeDISCO protocol35. Although many larger vessels exhibited a bright autofluorescent signal, this was absent in small vessels, especially in the molecular layer of the cerebellum (Fig. 6E). Fluorescent labelling would again be required to comprehensively label the vasculature.

The largest specimen cleared and imaged in this study was an intact cerebellar hemisphere, with an apparent volume of 54 cm3. Further increases to the maximum specimen size which can be cleared and imaged will require several limiting factors to be addressed (sample volumes and data sizes are provided in Supplementary Table 2). Light sheet images of an intact cerebellar hemisphere exhibited reduction of resolution and contrast towards the centre of the specimen (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 6). This is likely due to multiple factors including attenuation of both the illumination light sheet and fluorescence emission as they traverse the tissue – this is more severe in larger specimens due to the cumulative effects of light scattering and refractive index inconsistencies. Overall, further optimisation of clearing protocols to allow even more efficient removal of scattering molecules and more homogenous refractive index matching will be essential for imaging larger specimens.

A further significant limitation of the technique is the time taken for recordings and post-processing. For example, the largest specimen described in this manuscript (cerebellar hemisphere) required days of continuous imaging to record the whole specimen. The subsequent post-processing including deconvolution, removal of stripe artefacts, intensity normalisation and alignment and stitching of tiles and angles is highly computationally intensive and only partially automated. This leads to lengthy delays before a 3D reconstruction is produced. The duration increases further with the use of multiresolution and multichannel recording techniques. The lengthy recording times could be reduced by implementation of new hardware; for example, objectives with a greater field of view would reduce the number of tiles it is necessary to record and an XY stage with a larger range of motion would allow further automation. Deconvolution of light microscopy recordings has been radically expedited by the use of GPU acceleration with state-of-the-art graphics boards36, however subsequent image processing steps remain rather long-winded, even on a reasonably powerful workstation. Implementation of efficient and automated image processing pipelines would greatly speed up this stage of the workflow and utilisation of cloud computing may also be beneficial.

The excitation and emission conditions used for autofluorescence imaging in this study were selected based on several criteria. Firstly, light of higher wavelengths is transmitted through biological tissue more readily than light of lower wavelengths, allowing imaging of thicker samples at greater depths33. Secondly, this illumination and emission paradigm is highly suited to the detection of lipofuscin, a fluorescent age-related pigment that accumulates in the central nervous system, particularly in neuronal cell bodies37–39. Characterisation of lipofuscin autofluorescence is a strong option for combination of our protocol with advanced image analysis pipelines, as a recent study quantified changes in lipofuscin parameters with age39. Lipofuscin may play an important role in neurodegenerative disease and methods with the ability to characterise lipofuscin distribution in macroscopic samples will be pivotal in understanding its role40. Lipofuscin is not the only source of autofluorescence in our specimens, for example, autofluorescence of molecules such as haem provides information on vasculature (Figs. 3 + 6). Overall, autofluorescence imaging allowed us to visualise a range of structures, and when combined with imaging of large-scale specimens, opens the door to label-free brain mapping and multimodal comparative imaging.

Multimodal imaging pipelines combining modern advanced MRI techniques and light sheet fluorescence microscopy providing three-dimensional high-resolution imaging of large-scale specimens will be a major step forward in mapping the human brain across multiple scales. Similar approaches conducted on mouse brains have already shown promising results41,42, and combination of MRI, OCT, and LSFM (500 µm slices) allowed construction of a single cell resolved reference atlas for Broca’s area in the human brain43. activeDISCO, with its unprecedented clearing capability is a powerful tool to fulfil the challenging task of rendering large human brain specimens transparent and thereby making large-scale comparative multimodal three-dimensional high-resolution imaging possible. Multimodal comparative imaging of the human brain would yield immense insights in neuronal connectivity and cytoarchitectonic characteristics of the healthy and diseased human brain, overcoming the scalar problems in modern neuroscience by connecting microscopic and macroscopic imaging data.

The human brain is affected by a wide variety of diseases, including psychiatric illnesses, cancers, neurodegenerative diseases, and neurodevelopmental disorders. Tissue clearing and light microscopy has already been used to investigate Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases, enabling characterisation of pathological hallmarks of the respective diseases, as well as cerebral cortical vasculature8,10,11,44. The technique has also been applied to various malignancies including breast, prostate, bladder, and ovarian cancers45–47. Three-dimensional characterisation of tumour biopsies and resectates may afford improved histological analysis and improve patient outcomes. Importantly, a combination of traditional histology with tissue clearing and volumetric imaging was more sensitive at detecting metastatic colorectal adenocarcinoma nodules in lymph nodes than traditional sectioning-based techniques alone, demonstrating the medical applicability of such techniques48. Most of these techniques employ some form of fluorescent histological labelling, however autofluorescence alone has also been demonstrated to be useful for characterisation of breast tumour specimens30. The above-described protocols are all limited to rather small specimens, activeDISCO provides the opportunity to investigate larger specimens, further increasing the applicability of light sheet imaging techniques to human disease research.

This study characterised cleared samples by imaging of autofluorescence. Whilst this approach can reveal detailed structural information, it does not provide labelling of specific structures, proteins or nucleic acids – a feature which would be essential for molecular characterisation of specimens. Histological labels such as TO-PRO for nuclei and acridine orange for neuronal cell bodies as well as antibody labelling of a variety of proteins has been shown to be compatible with solvent-based clearing protocols5–7. In situ hybridisation chain reaction (HCR) allows mRNA labelling and is compatible with several clearing protocols, however this has been limited to samples up to 1 mm in thickness in the human brain49–51. The compatibility of these labelling methods with activeDISCO remains to be investigated and represents an exciting research topic that could further expand the application of tissue clearing protocols.

The results presented here are the next step towards the ultimate goal of clearing and light sheet imaging of an intact human brain at considerably higher resolution than current MRI methods permit. The clearing power of activeDISCO leads us to believe that when combined with a perfusion-based clearing system, the clearing of an intact human brain might become possible.

Methods

Description of activeDISCO protocol

Human brain samples were retrieved from the body donor programme of the Centre for Anatomy and Cell Biology, Medical University of Vienna. Body donors with a post-mortem time less than 24 hours were exclusively included in this study. After perfusion with heparinized phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) via bilateral cannulation of the carotid arteries and vertebral arteries, the brain was removed from the skull and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 5 days.

After thorough washing in Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline pH 7.4 (PBS) (2.68 mM KCl, 1.47 mM KH2PO4, 136.89 mM NaCl, 8.10 mM Na2HPO4), specimens were transferred to 5% CHAPS / PBS and incubated for 2 days at 37 °C. This was followed by washing in PBS and dehydration in a MeOH / PBS series (20, 40, 60, 80, 100%, 1 hr each at room temperature). Samples were bleached overnight at 4 °C in 10% H2O2 / MeOH (1 part 50% H2O2, 4 parts MeOH). In initial experiments 20% DMSO was included in the bleaching solution however this was later found to be unnecessary. Following rehydration (reverse of above), samples were washed twice in PBS for 1 h at room temperature and incubated in HCl pH1 / dH2O for 2 days at 37 °C. Samples were dehydrated by two treatments of DMP at 37 °C for 2 days each. A 1% volume of 2 N HCl (e.g., 1 ml 2 N HCl in 100 ml DMP) was added to the DMP immediately after beginning the incubation to ensure sufficiently acidic conditions for the reaction to proceed. Following dehydration, samples were treated with DCM overnight at room temperature for delipidation (DCM should not be used at higher temperatures due to its low boiling point). DCM was pre-treated with molecular sieves with a 3 Å pore size (#8487, Carl Roth, Austria) to ensure a completely anhydrous solvent. DCM was removed and vessels were briefly left open under a fume hood to allow residual DCM to evaporate, this was followed by refractive index matching in DBE at 37 °C. DBE was refreshed after a few days if the sample did not appear adequately clear. Specimens were returned to room temperature when maximum clearing was achieved. All steps were performed with moderate shaking.

Morphometric changes following activeDISCO were characterised in pig brain specimens. Telencephalic thickness was measured as the distance from the surface of the brain to the corpus callosum across the midsagittal plane at the level of the central sulcus. Volumes were determined by placing the hemispheres in a measuring cylinder containing a known volume of liquid and reading the change in volume.

Comparison with other protocols

MASH, SHANEL, iDISCO, and pathoDISCO were performed according to the published protocols5–7,30. Specimen transparency was assessed using qualitative and quantitative methods. Firstly, specimens from each protocol were placed upon a Siemens star and photographed with brightfield illumination. Secondly, samples were imaged by light sheet microscopy and resolution at 1 mm depth intervals was assessed. Finally, sample transparency was quantitively assessed using transmittance of a laser beam as readout. Briefly, a 638 nm laser source (Omicron, Germany) was placed opposite a light power meter with a glass cuvette in the light path. The cuvette was filled with the appropriate refractive index matching medium and laser power set to approximately 20 mW. Specimens were placed in the light path in turn and transmittance was measured in three different positions. Transmittance for each sample was averaged and calculated as a percentage of the starting power.

Light sheet microscopy

This study used a previously described custom light sheet microscope. For light sheet generation we utilised an optical system developed as a continuation of our previous work with a modified optical design to produce a light sheet with optimised characteristics22–26. In the most recent version of this system the mirror system has been replaced with fibre optics (Omicron, Germany).

The illumination beam of the light sheet microscope was initially characterized in air, using a horizontal converted microscope setup25. Using a 40x objective (LUCPlanFLN NA 0.6, Olympus, Japan), the beam’s full width at half maximum (FWHM) was measured to be approximately 4 µm at the focal plane. In order to characterise the system’s resolution, we used sub-resolution fluorescent beads (200 nm diameter) embedded in gelatin as point sources. The fluorescent beads were imaged with 488 nm illumination and 525/50 nm emission filter using 20x (Olympus, NA 0.45, Japan) and 40x (Olympus, NA 0.8, Japan) water immersion objectives. Fluorescent bead recordings were reconstructed using Amira (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the intensity distribution of multiple isolated, single beads was measured. An average was taken over several beads to ensure statistical reliability. The lateral (xy) and axial (z) resolutions were determined by measuring the FWHM of the intensity distribution profiles, as detailed in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Objectives used in this study for imaging cleared tissue comprised XLFluor-340 NA 0.28 4x and XLFluor-340 NA 0.14 2x objectives (Olympus, Japan) corrected for the refractive index mismatch and a Fluotar NA 0.6 16x immersion objective (Leica, Germany). The lateral resolution of a light sheet microscope is primarily determined by the numerical aperture (NA) of the detection objective. This relationship can be predicted by the Rayleigh criterion, giving lateral resolution predictions of 3 µm, 1.5 µm and 0.7 µm for 2x, 4x and 16x objectives mentioned above, respectively. In contrast, the axial resolution is a function of both the detection objective’s NA and the thickness of the illumination light sheet. When using low-NA objectives, the detection objective’s point spread function (PSF) is relatively broad. As a result, the light sheet’s thickness of approximately 4 µm becomes the dominant factor for the overall axial resolution.

The corrected XL-Fluor objectives had a working distance of approximately 10 mm, for imaging of the intact cerebellar hemisphere the dipping cone containing corrective optics was replaced with a low-profile plastic dipping cone with a glass cover, this results in an increased working distance of 20 mm at the cost of a slight decrease in maximum achievable resolution. After imaging from one angle the cerebellar hemisphere was rotated 180° around the y-axis and imaged from a second angle to allow recording of the whole specimen.

Image processing, statistics and reproducibility

Image post-processing including deconvolution, contrast-limited adaptive histogram equilibration, destriping and unsharp masking was performed in NeuroDeblur (MBF Bioscience, USA) and Fiji36,52,53 on a Dell graphics processing workstation equipped with a 3.5 GHz 8 core Intel CPU, an NVIDIA Quadro M6000 GPU and 168 GB RAM. A tiling approach was used for recording of large specimens, and alignment was performed in BigStitcher54. Post-processed images were imported into Amira (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 3D reconstruction. Volumes of light sheet microscopy recordings were calculated using the 3D objects counter plugin for FIJI55. Statistics were performed in GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2, Dotmatics, Boston, USA). Sample sizes were carefully considered to enable efficient usage of human brain tissue. When statistical analysis was required, a minimum of three replicates were analysed. This sample size was deemed sufficient due to the generally low variance within test and control groups. Clearing and imaging results were found to be reproducible across multiple brain regions and in tissue derived from many donors.
