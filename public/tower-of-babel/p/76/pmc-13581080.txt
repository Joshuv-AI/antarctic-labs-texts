Association Between Psychiatrists' Subjective Clinical Behavior Scores and Objective Quality Indicators Based on Discharge Prescriptions: A Nationwide Multicenter Study Based on the EGUIDE Project
Authors: Kazuhiko Yamamuro, Naomi Hasegawa, Yusuke Arai, Hikaru Hori, Hiroyuki Muraoka, Toru Horinouchi, Naoki Hashimoto, Shinichiro Ochi, Takashi Tsuboi, Hirotaka Yamagata, Junya Matsumoto, Satsuki Ito, Toshiaki Onitsuka, Kazutaka Ohi, Hisashi Yamada, Jun‐ichi Iga, Ken Inada, Koichiro Watanabe, Norio Yasui‐Furukori, Ryota Hashimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimPsychiatrists often perceive their clinical practices as guideline‐concordant; however, discrepancies frequently exist between perceived and actual prescribing behaviors. To explore this gap, we examined the association between self‐assessed clinical behavior scores (CBs) and prescribing practices objectively evaluated using quality indicators (QIs) within the EGUIDE educational program framework for schizophrenia and major depressive disorder. Identifying such associations is essential for improving care quality and developing interventions fostering evidence‐based practice.MethodsA prospective observational multicenter study was conducted across Japan involving 820 psychiatrists from university, public, and private hospitals participating in EGUIDE, along with patients diagnosed with schizophrenia (n = 6714) or major depressive disorder (n = 3692) treated at these institutions. The outcome measures were associations between psychiatrists' CB scores and quality indicators (QIs) based on discharge prescriptions; these were analyzed using logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, and institution type.ResultsFor schizophrenia, CBs were significantly associated with 8 of 11 QIs, including treatment‐resistant assessment, antipsychotic monotherapy (with/without other psychotropics), absence of anxiolytics/hypnotics, mood stabilizers, anticholinergics, PRN psychotropics, and clozapine use. For major depressive disorder, 3 of 7 QIs showed significant associations: severity assessment, no anxiolytic/hypnotic prescriptions, and use of modified electroconvulsive therapy.ConclusionSelf‐assessed clinical behavior scores partially reflect actual prescribing practices. These findings highlight the value of incorporating subjective evaluations into clinical performance assessment tools to promote guideline adherence. Moreover, our results suggest that collecting self‐assessments may offer a feasible and scalable strategy for identifying areas where additional education or system‐level support is needed to enhance evidence‐based prescribing in diverse clinical settings.Trial RegistrationUniversity Hospital Medical Information Network registry identifier: UMIN000022645

Introduction

In clinical practice, the discrepancy between evidence‐based treatment guidelines and actual prescribing practices remains a persistent challenge within the healthcare sector [1]. For example, passive dissemination and the promotion of guidelines negligibly alter clinicians' behaviors [2], and even interventions targeting specialties, disorders, or clinical behaviors have yielded inconsistent and limited outcomes [3]. The barriers to guideline implementation are multifaceted, including a lack of knowledge or awareness among physicians, skepticism regarding the validity of guidelines, and psychological or institutional resistance to deviating from established practice patterns [4]. Thus, adherence to treatment guidelines transcends mere knowledge acquisition and necessitates the overcoming of complex barriers related to perception, attitude, behavior, and systemic factors.

Recent investigations have identified discrepancies between clinicians' self‐assessments and their actual clinical practices. Notably, even when physicians believe their practice aligns with treatment guidelines, their evaluations often differ from third‐party assessments or objective behavioral data [5, 6], suggesting that self‐assessments may not always accurately reflect actual performance. This underscores the importance of integrating self‐evaluations with external assessments or objective indicators. While research on the gap between subjective and objective evaluations has predominantly focused on educational assessment and specific clinical specialties, the issue is understudied within psychiatry, particularly concerning the consistency between psychiatrists' perceptions of guideline adherence and their actual prescribing behaviors. This misalignment between perceived and actual clinical practices is prevalent among physicians and is associated with clinical inertia, a phenomenon where prompt and necessary treatment adjustments are not made despite clinical indications [7]. For instance, an American study revealed that only approximately 55% of recommended care is delivered to patients, and the disconnect between subjective perception and objective behavior constitutes a significant barrier to the implementation of evidence‐based medicine [8]. Numerous reviews have documented inconsistencies between physicians' self‐assessments of clinical practice guidelines and their actual behavior [9, 10, 11]. However, in settings where care is standardized or structured feedback is provided, self‐assessments may more accurately reflect actual performance [10].

Psychiatry is not immune to the abovementioned challenges. Despite the establishment of treatment guidelines, a significant gap remains between recommended practices and actual prescribing behaviors. For example, although international guidelines advocate the use of second‐generation antipsychotic monotherapy in the treatment of schizophrenia, polypharmacy—defined as the concurrent use of multiple antipsychotics—has been reported in 20%–60% of cases [12]. Such deviations from guidelines, including excessive dosing and combination therapy, are associated with inadequate symptom control, an increased risk of adverse effects, and poorer health outcomes for patients [13, 14]. Similar patterns have been reported in Japan, where approximately 30%–50% of patients with schizophrenia are reportedly being prescribed multiple antipsychotics [15]. In response, some initiatives have introduced treatment algorithms to support clinical decision‐making and promote adherence to the treatment guidelines for schizophrenia [16]. A comparable approach has been employed to treat major depressive disorder, with algorithmic implementation to encourage guideline‐concordant practices [17]. Nonetheless, in routine practice, combination therapy involving antidepressants, as well as the concomitant use of anxiolytics and hypnotics, is widespread despite recommendations against such use [18]. This trend is evident in Japan, where antidepressant polypharmacy and frequent co‐prescription of benzodiazepine‐class agents have been reported, indicating that the principle of monotherapy recommended in guidelines is not consistently followed [19].

The findings underscore a significant challenge in psychiatric practice, where treatment guidelines are not consistently mirrored in prescribing behaviors, highlighting a critical issue in implementing evidence‐based medicine. The Effectiveness of Guidelines for Dissemination and Education in Psychiatric Treatment (EGUIDE) project was initiated in 2016 to promote adherence to treatment guidelines in psychiatric care. This initiative has implemented nationwide educational programs based on treatment guidelines for schizophrenia and major depressive disorder, while concurrently collecting data on psychiatrists' comprehension of the guidelines and their actual clinical practices. Participating institutions have established systems to continuously evaluate the quality of care using objective indicators derived from discharge prescription data [20]. Following participation in the EGUIDE educational sessions, the use of antipsychotic monotherapy for patients with schizophrenia has reportedly increased, while prescriptions of benzodiazepines, anticholinergics, and hypnotics have decreased, reflecting positive changes in prescribing behaviors [20, 21, 22, 23]. Beyond prescription data, the EGUIDE project has assessed the self‐assessed CB scores of psychiatrists, which reflect their adherence to treatment guidelines. Additionally, self‐assessed clinical behavior (CB) scores have improved following educational intervention [24]. These findings suggest the potential utility of evaluating the alignment between self‐assessment CB scores and actual prescribing behaviors. The observed association between these two dimensions in previous EGUIDE findings provides a crucial foundation for exploring the consistency between subjective perceptions and objective clinical behaviors.

By investigating the relationship between self‐assessed CB scores and prescribing practices objectively evaluated using quality indicators (QIs) from discharge prescriptions, the present study aimed to evaluate the extent to which psychiatrists adhere to treatment guidelines in clinical practice, thereby assessing the consistency between subjective self‐evaluations and actual prescribing behaviors in psychiatric care by focusing on psychiatrists participating in the EGUIDE program. The primary objective of this project was to enhance the dissemination and education of treatment guidelines for schizophrenia and major depressive disorder [21].

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This multicenter observational study was conducted under the EGUIDE project. The study cohort comprised psychiatrists affiliated with medical institutions across Japan participating in the EGUIDE project, and patients diagnosed with schizophrenia or major depressive disorder who were admitted to these institutions. The EGUIDE project assessed the impact of guideline‐based education from two distinct perspectives: (1) subjective evaluations through self‐administered questionnaires completed by psychiatrists to assess their CB scores, and (2) objective evaluations derived from QIs calculated from patients' discharge prescription data. Both subjective and objective assessments were performed to examine the alignment between psychiatrists' perceptions of their clinical practices and their actual prescribing behaviors. The QI and CB surveys were conducted every fiscal year. The QI survey analyzed prescription data covering the period from April to September of each year. The CB survey to self‐evaluate psychiatrists' clinical practices during the same April–September period was asked between October and the following February–March (typically completed by January in recent years) in the same fiscal year. Thus, both surveys referred to the same six‐month clinical period, although the CB data were collected later in the fiscal year. In total, CB and QI data were both available for 820 psychiatrists out of 1497 who participated in the EGUIDE program during the observation period. Specifically, psychiatrists who participated in the EGUIDE educational program were requested to complete self‐administered questionnaires on their CBs. Simultaneously, QIs were calculated based on the treatment during hospitalization and discharge prescriptions for patients with schizophrenia or major depressive disorder treated at the same institutions. The ethics committees of the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry and all participating institutions approved the study design. Individual informed consent was not obtained; instead, an opt‐out approach was utilized, with information about the study made available publicly on institutional websites. The approval number issued by the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry is: B2022‐004, and each of the participating universities, hospitals and clinics. The procedures were conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Patient and Public Involvement

Assessment of CB

The EGUIDE project [20, 21, 25] commenced with psychiatrists participating in a one‐day intensive educational program based on the following treatment guidelines: (1) Guideline for Pharmacological Therapy of Schizophrenia (2017–2021), (2) Guideline for Pharmacological Treatment of Schizophrenia 2022 (2022–2023), (3) Treatment Guideline II: Major Depressive Disorder (2017–2023), and (4) Clinical Guidelines for Pregnant and Postpartum Women with or at Risk of Psychiatric Disorders (General Edition: 2022; Specific Edition: 2021). Following these educational sessions, the CB of psychiatrists was evaluated using a self‐administered questionnaire designed to subjectively assess their perceived adherence to the treatment guidelines. Each item was rated on a five‐point Likert scale and assigned a representative value to compute CB scores as follows: “Not implemented” (0%–20%), “Minimally implemented” (21%–40%), “Partially implemented” (41%–60%), “Moderately implemented” (61%–80%), and “Almost fully implemented” (81%–100%). Psychiatrists could also choose a “Not applicable” option, wherein the items were not scored. For the purpose of score calculation, each response category was assigned a representative percentage: 10% for “Not implemented,” 30% for “Minimally implemented,” 50% for “Partially implemented,” 70% for “Moderately implemented,” and 90% for “Almost fully implemented.” The CB score for each domain was calculated as the average of the remaining item scores, yielding an implementation rate ranging from 10% to 90%, thereby facilitating the quantitative evaluation of psychiatrists' self‐reported adherence to guideline‐based CBs.

CB scores were categorized into three primary domains. First, CB‐G represented CBs associated with the general application of treatment guidelines. Although CB‐G initially encompassed six items addressing clinical care, education, and dissemination, we focused on two items pertinent to clinical practice: (1) employing treatment guidelines to determine treatment policy in consultation with patients and family (CB‐G1) and (2) endeavoring to treat patients following the guidelines if their previous treatments did not align with the guidelines (CB‐G2). Second, CB‐S was used to evaluate CBs related to the guidelines for schizophrenia. Depending on the version of the guideline utilized, psychiatrists participating from 2017 to 2021 were assessed using items CB‐S1 to CB‐S9 and CB‐S17 to CB‐S21 (Guideline for Pharmacological Therapy of Schizophrenia), whereas those who participated between 2022 and 2023 were assessed using items CB‐S1 to CB‐S16 (Guideline for Pharmacological Treatment of Schizophrenia 2022). Third, CB‐D was used to assess CBs related to the Treatment Guideline II: Major Depressive Disorder, comprising 14 items. Finally, the CB‐C score was calculated as the average of the three domain scores—CB‐G, CB‐S, and CB‐D—and served as a comprehensive measure of guideline‐based CB. The distributions of CB‐G, CB‐S, and CB‐D scores were visualized to illustrate the variability in the implementation of the guidelines (see Figure S1).

QIs

QIs, utilizing discharge prescription data, serve as objective metrics to assess the alignment of clinical practices for patients with schizophrenia or major depressive disorder with established treatment guidelines, as previously described in the EGUIDE studies [15, 19, 20, 21, 26]. Each QI is quantified as a proportion, representing the frequency with which the recommended treatment is administered within the patient cohort, thereby functioning as an outcome‐based measure in practical clinical settings.

According to the treatment guidelines for schizophrenia, antipsychotic monotherapy is primarily advocated, whereas the concurrent use of anxiolytics, hypnotics, antidepressants, mood stabilizers, or antiepileptic drugs is discouraged. Administration of long‐acting injectable antipsychotics (LAIs) is advised for patients who experience relapse due to inadequate medication adherence or based on patient preference. Furthermore, for treatment‐resistant schizophrenia, the combination of clozapine and modified electroconvulsive therapy (mECT) with antipsychotics is recommended. QIs for schizophrenia (QI‐S) employed in this study were formulated based on these guidelines and comprised 11 items (see Table S2). Each QI‐S was computed as a proportion reflecting the extent of guideline adherence. Elevated QI‐S values denote a higher degree of concordance with the recommended treatment protocols. Conversely, the treatment guideline for major depressive disorder advocates antidepressant monotherapy for moderate to severe depression without psychotic features, while combination therapy, particularly the prolonged use of benzodiazepine‐class drugs, is not recommended. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is endorsed as an effective therapeutic option, and mECT is recommended under specific conditions, such as severe depression without psychotic features, psychotic depression, or depression with catatonia. The QIs for depression (QI‐D) utilized in this study were developed based on these recommendations and comprised 7 items (see Table S2). Each QI‐D was similarly calculated as a proportion reflecting adherence to the guideline, with higher QI‐D values indicating greater treatment alignment with the recommended guidelines.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of both psychiatrists and patients. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous and categorical variables are represented as means and standard deviations (SDs) and frequencies and percentages, respectively. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to investigate the associations between CB scores and the attainment of each QI. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed for both unadjusted models and models adjusted for age, sex, and type of institution of patients. These covariates were selected based on prior findings suggesting their potential impact on QI performance. The distributions of CB scores across domains (CB‐G, CB‐S, CB‐D) were visualized to examine variations in guideline implementation. Based on the analysis of multiple CB scores (CB‐C, CB‐G, CB‐S, or CB‐D) for several QIs for each disorder, multiple comparisons were performed. A Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust the significance threshold to mitigate Type I error due to multiple testing. Logistic regression assumptions, including absence of multicollinearity among independent variables and linearity of the logit, were confirmed prior to analysis. All models demonstrated adequate goodness‐of‐fit. The number of comparisons was determined based on the number of CB score types and QI items, and analyses were conducted to ensure appropriate statistical power.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 820 psychiatrists from various regions of Japan participated in this study. Of these, 778 psychiatrists treated patients diagnosed with schizophrenia, while 694 psychiatrists treated individuals with major depressive disorder (Table 1). All participating psychiatrists had completed the EGUIDE educational program. In both diagnostic categories, the majority of psychiatrists were males, comprising 74.6% and 75.8% in the schizophrenia and major depressive disorder groups, respectively. The average age of the psychiatrists was approximately 33 years in both groups, with a mean duration of 3.5 years of clinical experience in psychiatry. In terms of institutional affiliation, university hospitals were the most prevalent, followed by private and public hospitals.

Association Between CB‐S and QI‐S

The associations between CB‐S and QI‐S were analyzed through logistic regression (Table 3). Higher CB‐S scores were significantly correlated with greater adherence to several QIs: assessment of treatment‐resistant schizophrenia (QI‐S1), antipsychotic monotherapy without other psychotropics (QI‐S2), antipsychotic monotherapy (QI‐S3), absence of anxiolytics or hypnotics prescriptions (QI‐S5), absence of mood stabilizers or antiepileptics prescriptions (QI‐S6), absence of anticholinergics prescriptions (QI‐S7), clozapine treatment (QI‐S9), and absence of psychotropic PRN medications prescriptions (QI‐S11). All associations were statistically significant (p < 2.27 × 10−3). Conversely, CB‐S scores and the following QIs were not associated: absence of antidepressant prescriptions (QI‐S4), use of long‐acting injectable antipsychotics (QI‐S8), and use of mECT (QI‐S10). Similar results were observed in models adjusted for age, sex, and type of institution.

Associations between QIs and both the comprehensive CB score (CB‐C) and the CBs related to the general use of guidelines (CB‐G) were examined (see Table S3). For both CB‐C and CB‐G in the context of schizophrenia, higher scores were significantly correlated with the following quality indicators: assessment of treatment‐resistant schizophrenia (QI‐S1), antipsychotic monotherapy without other psychotropics (QI‐S2), antipsychotic monotherapy (QI‐S3), absence of anxiolytics or hypnotics prescriptions (QI‐S5), absence of mood stabilizers or antiepileptics prescriptions (QI‐S6), absence of anticholinergics prescriptions (QI‐S7), clozapine treatment (QI‐S9), and absence of psychotropic PRN medications prescriptions (QI‐S11). All associations were statistically significant (p < 2.27 × 10−3). Conversely, CB‐C or CB‐G scores and the following indicators were not associated: absence of antidepressant prescriptions (QI‐S4), use of long‐acting injectable antipsychotics (QI‐S8), and use of mECT (QI‐S10). Similar results were observed in models adjusted for age, sex, and type of institution.

Thus, in addition to CB‐S scores, both CB‐C and CB‐G scores were consistently correlated with multiple QIs associated with schizophrenia, suggesting that psychiatrists' self‐assessed CBs may generally reflect their actual prescribing practices.

Higher CB‐D scores demonstrated significant associations with the following indicators: assessment of severity diagnosis (QI‐D1), absence of anxiolytic or hypnotic prescriptions (QI‐D4), and the application of mECT (QI‐D6). All associations were statistically significant (p < 2.27 × 10−3). Conversely, CB‐D scores and the following indicators were not associated: antidepressant monotherapy without other psychotropics (QI‐D2), antidepressant monotherapy (QI‐D3), CBT (QI‐D5), and absence of psychotropic PRN medication prescriptions (QI‐D7). Similar results were observed in models adjusted for age, sex, and type of institution.

Associations between QIs and both the comprehensive CB score (CB‐C) and CBs related to the general use of guidelines (CB‐G) were analyzed for major depressive disorder (Table S4). The CB‐C of major depressive disorder, the CB‐G of major depressive disorder, and CB‐D were all significantly associated with the assessment of severity diagnosis (QI‐D1) (all p < 2.27 × 10−3). The CB‐C of major depressive disorder was significantly associated with the absence of anxiolytic or hypnotic prescriptions (QI‐D4) and mECT application (QI‐D6) (both p < 2.27 × 10−3). Conversely, no significant associations were identified between either CB scores and the following indicators: antidepressant monotherapy without other psychotropics (QI‐D2), antidepressant monotherapy (QI‐D3), CBT (QI‐D5), and the absence of psychotropic PRN medication prescriptions (QI‐D7). Similar results were observed in models adjusted for age, sex, and type of institution.

Thus, we inferred that, in addition to CB‐D scores, both CB‐C and CB‐G scores exhibited significant associations with several QIs pertinent to major depressive disorder (QI‐D1, QI‐D4, and QI‐D6).

Discussion

This study constitutes a pioneering quantitative examination of the correlation between psychiatrists' self‐assessed CB scores and prescribing practices objectively evaluated using QIs derived from discharge prescription data, utilizing nationwide multicenter data from the EGUIDE project, which seeks to facilitate the dissemination of psychiatric treatment guidelines. The findings revealed a significant concordance between CB scores and prescribing behaviors across multiple QI items for both schizophrenia and major depressive disorder. These results suggest that psychiatrists' self‐assessed clinical biases may be partially reflected in their actual prescribing practices, indicating that subjective self‐evaluations could partially represent real‐world clinical behaviors. Although the scores do not encompass all facets of CB, they aligned with standard practices explicitly recommended or discouraged in the treatment guidelines. Moreover, in addition to CB‐S and CB‐D scores, the CB‐G scores, which reflected attitudes toward the general use of guidelines, exhibited significant associations with certain QIs, suggesting that general attitudes toward guideline adherence may influence actual prescribing behavior. This study provides evidence supporting the empirical relationship between subjective self‐assessment and actual clinical practices in the field of psychiatry.

Association Between CB‐S and QI‐S

QI items that demonstrated significant associations with CB‐S scores included antipsychotic monotherapy (QI‐S2 and QI‐S3), clozapine treatment (QI‐S9), and the absence of prescriptions for adjunctive agents such as anxiolytics or hypnotics (QI‐S5), mood stabilizers or antiepileptics (QI‐S6), anticholinergics (QI‐S7), and psychotropic PRN medications (QI‐S11). These items are explicitly recommended or discouraged in the Guideline for Pharmacological Treatment of Schizophrenia 2022, and are widely recognized as standard prescribing practices. Consequently, variability in clinical judgment among psychiatrists should be negligible, and their understanding of the guidelines may be more directly reflected in their prescribing behaviors. In contrast, no significant associations were observed between CB‐S scores and the following QI items: absence of antidepressant prescriptions (QI‐S4), use of LAIs (QI‐S8), and use of modified electroconvulsive therapy (mECT; QI‐S10). QI‐S8 is described in the guidelines as one of several options for ensuring medication adherence and preventing relapse but the strength of recommendation is relatively limited. Previous EGUIDE reports have shown low nationwide use of LAI [22], and various practical barriers have been highlighted, such as patient reluctance toward injections, difficulties in obtaining informed consent, limited physician experience, and insufficient institutional infrastructure [19, 24, 27]. Given these contextual factors, understanding the guidelines may not readily translate into prescribing behaviors, resulting in weaker alignment with CB‐S scores. The overall use of QI‐S10 (implementation of mECT) treatment remains low [20, 21, 28] as it requires facilities to administer general anesthesia, as well as the involvement of anesthesiologists and other specialized personnel. Iida et al. [19] found that mECT is predominantly offered at university or tertiary care hospitals, indicating that physical and institutional constraints may limit the consistency between CB‐S scores and the actual implementation of mECT.

Association Between CB‐D and QI‐D

In the context of major depressive disorder, CB‐D scores demonstrated significant correlations with three QI items: assessment of severity diagnosis (QI‐D1), absence of anxiolytic or hypnotic prescriptions (QI‐D4), and the application of mECT (QI‐D6). These indicators reflect clinical decisions made during the initial stages of treatment or in specific clinical scenarios where adherence to guidelines is more straightforward. These practices are relatively prevalent in real‐world settings. Specifically, QI‐D1 serves as a fundamental assessment to determine treatment strategies, while QI‐D4 reflects a widely held cautious approach regarding dependence, often resulting in these agents not being prescribed. Although QI‐D6 is indicated only in limited circumstances, it tends to be consistently applied at facilities where its use is feasible, potentially explaining its alignment with CB‐D scores. Conversely, CB‐D scores did not exhibit significant associations with QIs related to antidepressant monotherapy (QI‐D2 and QI‐D3), CBT (QI‐D5), and the absence of psychotropic PRN medication prescriptions (QI‐D7). The guidelines permit flexible prescribing of QI‐D2 and QI‐D3 based on severity; for instance, pharmacotherapy is not recommended for mild cases, whereas in psychotic depression, combination therapy with antipsychotics is considered the standard. However, the QIs employed in this study only evaluated the presence or absence of monotherapy, without considering symptomatic severity, which may have contributed to the discrepancy with CB‐D scores. Although the efficacy of CBT is endorsed in the guidelines (QI‐D5), both domestically and internationally [29, 30, 31], implementation is limited due to shortages in trained personnel and institutional capacity, resulting in few applicable cases. These contextual limitations may account for the weak association between CB‐D scores and specific QIs.

Association Between CB‐G and Prescribing Behaviors

CB‐G is a domain that evaluates psychiatrists' comprehension and attitudes toward the general application of treatment guidelines, rather than behaviors specific to a particular disorder. Consequently, direct correlation with specific prescribing behaviors is deemed unlikely [5, 32]. However, in the present study, CB‐G scores demonstrated significant associations with multiple QIs (Tables S3 and S4). CB‐G includes “using treatment guidelines when deciding on the treatment policy in discussion with patients and family (CB‐G1)” and “trying to treat patients in accordance with the guidelines if their previous treatments were not in accordance with guidelines (CB‐G2),” reflecting general clinical attitudes rather than specific prescribing choices. However, CB‐G scores were significantly associated with QI‐S2 (antipsychotic monotherapy) and QI‐S5 (no prescription of anxiolytics or hypnotics)—both recommended in the guidelines—suggesting that the physicians' general awareness of and attitudes toward guidelines may influence their actual prescribing behavior. These findings underscore the relevance of CB‐G as a meaningful indicator for assessing guideline comprehension, despite being a broad and non‐specific measure. Furthermore, consistent associations between CB‐C scores—calculated as the average of CB‐G, CB‐S, and CB‐D—and multiple QIs reinforce the observation that even comprehensive behavior scores can partially reflect real‐world prescribing practices. Thus, both CB‐G and CB‐C may serve as valid indices for evaluating clinicians' engagement with guideline‐based practice.

Focusing on the Consistency Between Subjective and Objective Assessments: A New Framework for Evaluation in Clinical Practice

Physicians' self‐assessments are generally based on subjective perceptions, rendering them susceptible to discrepancies when compared with actual behavior or external evaluations. This limitation has been consistently highlighted in theoretical studies [6, 32]. Although research on the agreement between subjective and objective evaluations has predominantly focused on internal medicine and primary care, examining this relationship directly in psychiatry is challenging due to variations in evaluation targets and clinical contexts. For instance, Peabody et al. [33] showed a disconnect between physicians' self‐assessments and their actual clinical behaviors, with the quality of care differing significantly based on the assessment method. In the present study, we focused on specific prescribing behaviors delineated in psychiatric treatment guidelines and aligned psychiatrists' self‐assessed CB scores with prescribing practices objectively evaluated using QIs based on discharge prescriptions, which are highly standardized and accurately recorded in clinical settings. A measurable alignment was observed between subjective and objective indicators. This consistency may be attributed to the clearly defined, simple, and standardized nature of the evaluated behaviors—such as the use of monotherapy or avoidance of specific medications. The structured and routine format of discharge data likely contributed to the close correspondence with self‐assessed behaviors. Thus, the observed alignment may reflect both the simplicity and clarity of guideline‐based behavioral criteria and reliability of clinical prescribing records. These findings underscore the importance of clearly and simply defining CBs and standardizing assessment methods when designing evaluations of guideline adherence. The findings of this study indicate that a straightforward framework integrating subjective indicators—CB scores—with objective data—QIs—can effectively elucidate aspects of clinical practice. When behavioral criteria are clearly defined and standardized, physicians' self‐assessments tend to more accurately reflect the actual behavior. This is evident in areas such as the use of structured feedback in chronic disease management, including diabetes [34]; standardized procedures in cancer screening [35]; and prescribing behaviors in dermatology [36]. Notably, the study focused on the “process quality” component of Donabedian's structure–process–outcome model [37], employing both subjective evaluations (CB scores) and objective evaluations (QIs) to represent the reality of CB. Although prior studies have highlighted potential limitations of medical record reviews due to incomplete documentation [38], QIs utilized in this study were derived from discharge prescriptions—standardized clinical actions that are documented consistently—ensuring a high degree of accuracy. Consequently, the consistency observed with CB scores may be attributed to the use of highly reliable objective indicators.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that warrant further consideration. First, it was an observational study involving psychiatrists who participated in the EGUIDE educational program, rather than a randomized controlled trial. Nonetheless, considering that CB reflects physicians' prescribing attitudes and precedes actual prescribing actions—QIs—the use of nationwide real‐world data to investigate this association is valuable. Although statistical adjustments were made for age, sex, and type of institution, we could not fully control other potential confounders, such as years of clinical experience, institutional policies, and patient characteristics. Consequently, future interventional studies are necessary to establish causality. Second, the study population was confined to psychiatrists participating in the EGUIDE program, raising the possibility of selection bias. Indeed, participating psychiatrists tended to be relatively young and may have exhibited a higher level of interest in treatment guidelines, which should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings. Third, QIs were derived from discharge prescription data and did not encompass non‐pharmacological interventions, such as psychosocial treatments or patient education. Although CB scores included items related to these domains, the present study did not examine their consistency with objective indicators, thereby limiting the ability to evaluate the full scope of clinical practice. Fourth, the outcome of this study focused on the alignment between CB scores and QIs as indicators of adherence to treatment guidelines, rather than on clinical effectiveness or patient satisfaction. Therefore, higher CB or QI scores do not necessarily imply better treatment outcomes, and this should be considered when interpreting the findings. Fifth, because of the cross‐sectional design of this study, we cannot determine the causal direction of the observed associations. While higher CB scores may have influenced prescribing behaviors (QIs), it is also possible that psychiatrists' preexisting prescribing or assessment styles affected their self‐assessed CB scores. Therefore, the causal relationship between CB scores and QIs remains unclear. Sixth, because psychiatrists who voluntarily participated in the EGUIDE guideline training program may have been more interested in guideline‐based practice than the general population of psychiatrists, the results may be subject to selection bias. Furthermore, as the CB score was based on self‐assessment, responses might have been influenced by social desirability bias. Seventh, although the CB score was originally developed and reported by Yamada et al. [25], the present study reconstructed it into four domains and expressed it as percentages to accommodate revisions in the guideline items. Therefore, the modified scoring approach used here has not yet been independently validated, which should be regarded as a methodological limitation. Finally, because detailed clinical information on individual patients, such as symptom severity, duration of illness, treatment resistance, or comorbid medical and psychiatric conditions, was not available, these patient‐level factors could not be fully accounted for in the analyses. Therefore, the influence of unmeasured patient characteristics on prescribing behaviors cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

This study analyzed data from psychiatrists involved in the nationwide implementation program for psychiatric treatment guidelines (EGUIDE) and revealed a potential correlation between self‐assessed CB scores and prescribing practices objectively evaluated using QIs. This observed alignment could be attributed to the study design, which matched CB scores and QIs based on identical clinical behaviors at the level of an individual physician. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that subjective assessments, such as CB scores, may, under certain conditions, reflect actual prescribing tendencies to some extent. The results of this study provide a valuable perspective for the development of clinical assessment methods and monitoring indicators in psychiatric practice in the future.
