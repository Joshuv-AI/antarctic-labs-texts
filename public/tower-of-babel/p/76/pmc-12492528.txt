Expression profiling and functional characterization of LoNCED gene in the pollen abortion of Lilium spp.
Authors: Haiyan Zhou, Xiang Li, Lan Ma, Qing Duan, Wenwen Du, Guangfen Cui, Xuewei Wu, Wenjie Jia, Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLilium spp. are widely cultivated for their ornamental value, but excessive pollen production poses significant commercial challenges. Male sterility, especially in pollenless cultivars, offers a promising breeding strategy to improve market appeal. This study demonstrates that abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis, mediated by 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED)‌—a rate-limiting enzyme in ABA production—constitutes the pivotal mechanism underlying complete male sterility in Lilium.ResultsThrough comparative transcriptomic analysis between fertile and sterile lily progenies, we identified significantly elevated ABA levels concomitant with upregulated LoNCED expression in the developing anthers of sterile plants. To investigate its role in male sterility, we isolated the LoNCED gene from both fertile and sterile lily progenies. Following cloning and tissue-specific expression analysis, we explored its function through homologous transient overexpression and heterologous expression. The LoNCED gene, with a 1812 bp open reading frame encoding 603 amino acids, encodes a hydrophilic protein (66.69 kDa) localized in chloroplasts and mitochondria. Sequence analysis revealed high similarity to NCED proteins from other species. Transient overexpression of LoNCED in Lilium anthers transformation downregulated key anther developmental genes (DYT1, TDF1, CYP703A2, CALS5 and DEX1), leading to tapetum degradation, microspore abortion, and complete pollen sterility. Our multispecies validation demonstrated conserved NCED-mediated sterility mechanisms across monocots and eudicots.ConclusionsThe LoNCED gene shows specific high expression during lily anther development. Its overexpression disrupts anther structure, arrests meiosis, and downregulates pollen development-related genes, revealing a conserved inhibitory role in male reproductive organ development.

Background

Lilium spp.(lilies), perennial bulbous flowers in the Liliaceae family, are prized for their vibrant colors, fragrance, and cultural symbolism of harmony and prosperity [1]. Despite their economic importance as cut flowers, lilies present significant challenges due to abundant, lightweight pollen grains that adhere to petals and clothing, causing contamination. Manual emasculation, which is commonly used to address this issue, increases labor costs and risks mechanical damage, reducing ornamental value [2]. Therefore, the cultivation of male sterile, male sterility lilies represents a pivotal direction for future breeding efforts.

Unlike in fertile hybrids, the tapetum of male-sterile lilies behaves differently during microspore mother cell development, preventing microspores from progressing to the tetrad stage. The tapetum, located in the innermost layer of the anther, plays a critical role in supplying nutrients and hormones for microspore development [3]. Disruptions in tapetum degradation can lead to male sterility [4, 5]. In tomato, mutations in tapetum cells early in meiosis lead to stamen degeneration and male sterility [6]. High-throughput RNA sequencing revealed significant differences in gene expression between male-sterile (HS) and fertile (HF) lines, with the differentially expressed genes involved primarily in hormone signal transduction, including auxin, cytokinin, gibberellin, and abscisic acid (ABA) metabolism. Notably, the abscisic acid (ABA) content was 3.3-fold greater in lily anthers than in fertile anthers during the tetrad stage, which coincided with significant upregulation of ABA biosynthesis genes (NCED: 2.8-fold; SDR: 2.5-fold; AAO: 2.6-fold). This ABA accumulation is directly correlated with disrupted tapetum degradation and oxidative stress markers (e.g., 3.7-fold higher MDA content in sterile lines) [7]. Intriguingly, auxin exhibited a divergent regulatory pattern: indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) levels in sterile Lines consistently exceeded those in their fertile counterparts by 1.5–1.8-fold across developmental stages. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that auxin signalling genes (ARF and YUCCA/TAA) are persistently overexpressed in sterile lines, potentially driving the premature tapetum vacuolization observed in Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) [8] and Chinese cabbage [9]. Such antagonism between ABA-driven oxidative responses and auxin-mediated developmental signals underscores a conserved hormonal “seesaw” mechanism in plant male sterility systems [3, 10].

9-cis-Epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED), a rate-limiting enzyme in ABA biosynthesis via the carotenoid-derived pathway, plays a pivotal role in regulating abscisic acid homeostasis. NCED is part of a multigene family, and studies have shown that it can induce increased ABA levels in response to environmental stress, thereby increasing plant stress tolerance [11]. In tobacco, the NCED gene has been linked to ABA synthesis under drought conditions, with findings indicating that NCED can promote ABA production both under normal and drought conditions [12]. Similarly, research on soybean has revealed a correlation between NCED gene expression, protein levels, and ABA contents in leaves and roots under water stress, further confirming the role of NCED in ABA regulation under drought stress [13]. In Brassica napus, the NCED gene family led to the isolation of BnNCED3, which shares significant amino acid sequence similarity with Arabidopsis NCED3 [14]. The overexpression of BnNCED3 in transgenic Arabidopsis resulted in increased ABA accumulation and increased production of nitric oxide (NO) and reactive oxygen species (ROS), contributing to improved tolerance to abiotic stresses. Furthermore, BnNCED3 was shown to regulate plant growth, including by inhibiting seed germination, affecting early-stage development, and promoting ABA-related leaf senescence. These findings suggest that BnNCED3, through the modulation of ABA biosynthesis, plays a role in both stress adaptation and plant development [15]. These studies highlight the importance of continuous activation of NCED and the coordination between ABA biosynthesis and catabolism in regulating ABA signalling and its various functions [16].

Pollen development is a crucial stage in plant sexual reproduction and is regulated by a complex molecular network involving multiple coordinated processes, such as tapetum function, sporopollenin deposition, callose dynamics, and exine formation [17]. The tapetum, often referred to as the “nutrient factory” for pollen development, plays a vital role, and its functional abnormalities directly lead to pollen abortion [18]. The transcription factor DYT1 (Defective in Tapetal Development And Function 1) regulates the timing of tapetal cell programmed cell death (PCD) through transcriptional activation of downstream targets such as TDF1 (Tapetum Development Delay 1) [19]. TDF1 further controls the expression of genes involved in sporopollenin precursor biosynthesis, and its loss of function results in tapetum retention and defective pollen wall formation. Sporopollenin, the primary component of the pollen exine, is synthesized through the action of cytochrome P450 family genes, particularly CYP703A2, which catalyzes the hydroxylation of fatty acids to generate sporopollenin precursors [20]. Mutations in CYP703A2 lead to pollen exine rupture and male sterility, highlighting its indispensable role in pollen wall formation. Callose, synthesized by CALS5 (Callose Synthase 5), forms a protective layer surrounding tetrads, preventing the leakage of microspore contents [21]. Abnormal callose degradation can result in microspore adhesion or rupture. Additionally, DEX1 (Defective in Exine Formation 1) mediates the formation of a scaffold for the initial exine layer, guiding the orderly deposition of sporopollenin [22]. The loss of DEX1 function leads to complete disintegration of the exine structure [23]. The functional defects of these genes ultimately result in the loss of pollen viability, manifesting as abnormal tapetal PCD (DYT1/TDF1), sporopollenin deficiency (CYP703A2), disruption of the callose barrier (CALS5), or exine structural collapse (DEX1).

To elucidate LoNCED’s role in anther and pollen development, we utilized a male-sterile Oriental lily (Lilium spp.) hybrid population developed by the Institute of Floriculture, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China. Parental individuals, including the maternal (F) and paternal (M) lines, as well as hybrid fertile offspring (HF) and hybrid sterile offspring (HS), were analysed. Transcriptomic studies of fertile (HF) and sterile (HS) lines revealed the LoNCED gene, which was significantly upregulated in the ABA signalling pathway during key stages of pollen development [7]. The LoNCED gene was subsequently cloned, and its encoded protein was further analysed and functionally predicted via bioinformatics tools. LoNCED was overexpressed in fertile progeny during the pollen mother cell stage and tetrad stages to observe its impact on anther development, and the gene was introduced into the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana to explore its expression characteristics and functions. We hypothesized that the LoNCED gene contributes to ABA accumulation, which disrupts pollen development and leads to male sterility in lilies. This study provides insights into the regulatory network of ABA-mediated male sterility by linking LoNCED expression to changes in pollen development and ABA content. These findings establish a theoretical foundation for understanding the role of LoNCED in male sterility in lilies and offer insights for developing male sterility lily germplasm resources.

Results

Cytological differences in anther development between fertile and sterile lily progeny

Comparative analysis of paraffin-embedded anther sections from fertile (HF) and sterile (HS) lily progenies revealed distinct developmental trajectories (Fig. 1), we found clear differences in anther development between the two groups. At the pollen mother cell stage, sterile progenies exhibited microsporangia with normal architecture: regularly arranged epidermal cells and sporogenous cells containing large nuclei and dense cytoplasm, surrounded by tapetal cells. However, tapetum and pollen mother cell development was significantly delayed in sterile progenies compared to fertile controls. At the tetrad stage, tapetum cells in sterile progenies ceased development and underwent premature degeneration. The pollen mother cells presented signs of vacuolation. After the tapetum cells broke down, their contents fused to form a periplasmodium, which entered the anther chamber and surrounded the pollen mother cells, blocking normal progression to the tetrad stage. In contrast, the fertile progenies presented microspore mother cells in the center of the microsporangia. These cells had large volumes, prominent nuclei, and dense cytoplasm and gradually grew to the tetrad stage. The tapetum cells were larger, quadrilateral-elongated, and arranged in regular concentric circles. Some tapetum cells had two or more nuclei.

ABA hormone content changes in sterile and fertile hybrids during anther development

ABA levels in sterile hybrids (HS) were significantly elevated compared to fertile hybrids (HF), particularly during the tetrad stage, where concentrations reached up to 2.1-fold higher than in HF. The ABA content in HF pollen is notably reduced during the pollen mother cell stage and tetrad stage, whereas no significant change is detected in HS pollen. These findings suggest a more active ABA biosynthesis process in sterile hybrids, potentially linked to changes in hormone regulation during anther development (Fig. 2).

Lily NCED gene cloning

The open reading frame (ORF) of the LoNCED gene was successfully amplified from Lily Anther cDNA. Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed a single 1812 bp product, matching the predicted CDS length (Fig. 3). Sanger sequencing validated the full-length ORF (1812 bp) encoding a 603-amino acid protein. Analysis of the conserved domain in the lily NCED amino acid sequence revealed that the LoNCED amino acid sequence contains one conserved NCED domain between positions 4 And 603. Additionally, a comparison of this protein sequence with homologous proteins in Arabidopsis revealed that the LoNCED protein is closer to NCED5 and NCED2 on the basis of the clustering results (Fig. 4). Expression of the LoNCED gene in different parts of lilies

During the development of lily pollen, at the microspore mother cell stage and the tetrad stage, there are significant differences in gene expression levels in different parts of the plant. At the microspore mother cell stage, in the sterile offspring of lilies, the expression level of the Lily LoNCED gene is relatively high in the floral tissues, with the highest expression in the Anther, reaching An average relative value of 1.35, whereas the expression in the style is extremely low, with An average relative value of 0.22 (Fig. 5). Compared with that in sterile offspring, the expression level of the LoNCED gene in fertile offspring is relatively lower, and its expression in the leaves is also very low. At the tetrad stage, the relative expression trend of the LoNCED gene is roughly the same as that at the microspore mother cell stage, with the expression in sterile offspring of lilies being significantly higher than that in fertile offspring, and the anther also has the highest expression among all floral tissues (Fig. 6).

Bioinformatics prediction analysis of the protein encoded by the LoNCED gene

The physicochemical properties and structural characteristics of the amino acid sequence encoded by the LoNCED gene were analysed via the Prot Param online software in the ExPASy system (http://web.ExPASy.org/protparam/). Prot Param predicted that the protein Molecular Mass was approximately 66.69 kDa, the theoretical isoelectric point (pI) was 5.87, the molecular formula was C2987H4594N814O884S20, And the total number of atoms was 9299. Its amino acid sequence is composed of 20 kinds of amino acids, among which serine (Ser) is the Most abundant, accounting for 8.6% of the total amino acids; cysteine (Cys) has the lowest content, accounting for 1.0% of the total amino acids; the total number of negatively charged residues (Asp + Glu) is 69; the total number of positively charged residues (Arg + Lys) is 56; the instability index of the protein is 47.01; and it is an unstable protein. The number of Lipophilic residues was 78.96, and the grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) was 0.381.

The hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity of the proteins encoded by the LoNCED gene were analysed via Prot Scale online software in the ExPASy system (http://web.ExPASy.org/protscale/), with positive values representing hydrophobicity and negative values representing hydrophilicity. The largest site of the amino acids encoded by the LoNCED gene was site 431, with a hydrophobic value of 2.178; the smallest site was site 202, with a hydrophilic value of −2.822. This indicated that the number of hydrophilic amino acids was greater than that of hydrophobic amino acids, so it was speculated that the protein was a stable hydrophilic protein (Fig. 7A).

The online software programs TMHMM (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM/) and SignalP-6.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/SignalP-6.0/) were used to predict the transmembrane region and signal peptide of the protein encoded by the LoNCED gene. The results revealed that the protein did not have transmembrane regions or signal peptides (Fig. 8BC). It was predicted that the protein was located in the cytoplasmic matrix or organelle matrix and was not a membrane protein or secretory protein. The subcellular localization of the LoNCED protein was predicted via WoLF PSORT online software (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/), and the results indicate that the LoNCED protein located in subcellular compartments is more likely to be distributed in chloroplasts and mitochondria. To further validate the subcellular localization of LoNCED, we transiently expressed a LoNCED-GFP fusion construct in tobacco epidermal cells. Confocal microscopy analysis confirmed that the fluorescence signal was predominantly localized in the nucleus, with minor fractions detected in the mitochondria and the cytoplasm, and notably, minimal colocalization with chloroplast autofluorescence was detected (Fig. 8). Intriguingly, the tripartite subcellular distribution of LoNCED observed here, particularly its nuclear enrichment and mitochondrial association, reveals a divergent localization pattern compared with that of its homologues, suggesting non-canonical regulatory functions. In contrast, the dual localization of LoNCED observed in this study may indicate functional diversification or species-specific regulatory mechanisms.

The secondary structure of the LoNCED protein was analysed via PSIPRED V4.0 (http://bioinf.cs.ucl.ac.uk/psipred), And the results revealed that the protein had three types of secondary structures, including 72 amino acids forming alpha Helices, accounting for 11.94%; 187 amino acids forming extended strands, accounting for 31.01%; And 344 amino acids forming random coils, accounting for 57.05%. The dominant component of the protein was a random coil (Fig. 7D).

By inputting the amino acid sequence into the SWISS-MODEL automated protein structure homology-modeling server (https://www.swissmodel.ExPASy.org/), the tertiary structure of the protein encoded by the Lily LoNCED gene was predicted (Fig. 7E). The protein was mainly composed of random coils, and the predictions of the two models were consistent with the results of the secondary structure prediction.

To elucidate the functional mechanisms of LoNCED, we conducted a multilayered bioinformatics analysis (Fig. 7F-G). First, multiple alignment revealed high amino acid sequence homology between LoNCED and NCED3, validating its classification within the NCED protein family and meeting the STRING database thresholds for interaction prediction. Second, via STRING-derived protein‒protein interaction (PPI) predictions, we identified 10 key interacting partners forming a regulatory network: ABA biosynthesis enzymes (ABA2, ABA3, AAO3), stress‒response regulators (ABI1, ABI2, ABI5, RD29A), carotenoid metabolism components (PSY1, ZEP), and the stress‒responsive membrane protein LTI65. This network suggests that LoNCED integrates ABA signalling with carotenoid metabolism And stress responses. Third, geneMANIA-based coexpression Analysis expanded this network to 20 genes, revealing NCED as the central hub with CCD gene family members (CCD1/4/7/8) positioned at downstream branches. Structural domain analysis further supported functional coordination: while NCED proteins contain the conserved catalytic domain for xanthoxin production (rate-limiting step in ABA biosynthesis), CCD isoforms possess distinct cleavage sites. This domain architecture enables metabolic channeling-NCED to direct substrate flow toward stress-related ABA synthesis, whereas CCDs generate specialized metabolites.

Homology comparison analysis of LoNCED

We performed multiple sequence alignment of LoNCED with NCED proteins from various species and conducted a BLAST search of the lily ATPase3 sequence against the NCBI database. The LoNCED sequences from other species demonstrating the highest alignment similarity were selected for phylogenetic tree construction (Fig. 9). Evolutionary analysis revealed that LoNCED forms a well-supported cluster with homologs from Iris pallida, Gladiolus hybrid cultivars, and Narcissus tazetta. Notably, the amino acid sequence of LoNCED exhibits high conservation across these species, particularly within the catalytic domain essential for ABA biosynthesis.

The impact of transient overexpression of LoNCED on lily pollen development

The relative expression levels of LoNCED in the control and overexpression groups were quantified using real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) (Fig. 10A). In fertile offspring, LoNCED expression in anthers overexpressing LoNCED was significantly higher than in the control group, confirming successful overexpression.

At the microspore mother cell stage and tetrad period, the expression of the key tapetum development regulators DYT1 and TDF1 was‌ significantly downregulated in LoNCED-overexpressing anthers (Fig. 10B-F). At the microspore mother cell and tetrad stages, the key tapetum regulators DYT1 and TDF1 were significantly downregulated in LoNCED-overexpressing anthers compared to controls. At the microspore mother cell stage, DYT1 expression in LoNCED-overexpressing Anthers was approximately 4-fold lower than in controls (p < 0.05), while TDF1 expression decreased by approximately 30% (p < 0.05). Genes involved in sporopollenin synthesis (CYP703A2), callose deposition (CALS5), and exine formation (DEX1) were also significantly downregulated at both stages in LoNCED-overexpressing anthers.

Morphological analysis revealed stage-specific anther defects in LoNCED-overexpressing lines compared to empty vector controls (Fig. 11). At the microspore mother cell stage, the tapetum cells in the anthers overexpressing LoNCED disintegrated in large numbers, and the microspore mother cells were located in the center of the microspore sac, with a large cell volume and large nucleus. At this time, in the empty vector control group, the tapetum developed relatively normally, with large square and slender cells, which were regularly arranged in a palisade state, and the microspore mother cells developed relatively normally. At the tetrad stage, owing to the abnormal development of tapetum cells at the early stage and large-scale disintegration in the anthers overexpressing LoNCED, they could not provide the nutrients and energy needed for microspore development, and the microspores began to develop abnormally and gradually disintegrated. The whole anther developed abnormally towards pollen abortion. In the control group, the tapetum cells began to disintegrate at the tetrad stage, and some microspores developed normally. However, since they are grown in an in vitro tissue culture environment, compared with normally growing tetrad anthers, many microspore development stages are abnormal, and many microspores disintegrate and cannot normally develop into the mononuclear pollen stage.

The impact of the LoNCED gene on Arabidopsis pollen development

To investigate the regulatory role of the LoNCED gene in anther development, the gene was introduced into the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. Notably, the transgenic Arabidopsis plants exhibited a smaller and weaker phenotype than did the wild-type (WT) plants. These plants displayed early bolting and reduced leaf growth. However, the stem growth rate exceeded that of leaf growth (Fig. 12A). Furthermore, the transgenic Arabidopsis plants bolted significantly earlier than the wild-type plants did (Fig. 12B). After four weeks of growth post-transplantation, when the stem heights of the transgenic and wild-type Arabidopsis plants were measured, the stem height of the wild-type plants was more than twice that of the transgenic plants (Fig. 12C). A detailed stereomicroscopic examination of the Arabidopsis flower buds revealed that the anthers of the transgenic plants were underdeveloped and that the number of pollen grains was significantly reduced (Fig. 12D). Additionally, the ABA content in transgenic plants was 8-fold higher than in WT plants, indicating a substantial increase in ABA synthesis. These observations suggested that ectopic overexpression of ABA might disrupt the balance between vegetative and reproductive growth (Fig. 12E). The LoNCED gene was highly expressed in the transgenic plants, and its expression level significantly surpassed that in the wild-type Arabidopsis (Fig. 12F). Moreover, paraffin sections were prepared from the flower buds for histological analysis (Fig. 12G). Cytological observations revealed that in wild-type Arabidopsis, the pollen sac and its contents developed fully, enabling the formation of normal anthers. In contrast, in the transgenic Arabidopsis plants, during anther development, the microspore mother cells and their contents gradually degraded. The tapetum began to disintegrate prematurely during microspore development, leading to gradual deformation of the pollen sac. The sac wall became thinner, and the contents inside the sac gradually disintegrated, resulting in the formation of a large cavity or cracking of the sac wall.

Discussion

Lily pollen contamination remains a key factor affecting the commercial value and market expansion of cut lilies. Pollen not only shortens the ornamental period of flowers but also allows them to adhere easily to the skin and clothing, making it difficult to remove and reducing consumer satisfaction. To address this issue, breeding techniques such as hybridization have been successfully used to develop lily varieties with degraded anthers, significantly mitigating the problem of pollen contamination [2]. The formation of male-sterile lilies is attributed mainly to pollen abortion and degradation or the inability to produce normal pollen grains [24]. In maize research, abnormalities in the development and degradation of tapetum cells have been identified as key factors leading to pollen abortion. The tapetum is a crucial tissue that provides nutrients and enzymes during pollen development, and its proper differentiation and timely degradation are essential for pollen maturation [25]. Therefore, utilizing male sterility traits in lilies for targeted breeding of pollenless varieties is a reasonable research direction. Research indicates that inadequate energy supply and hormonal metabolic disturbances during anther development can result in abnormal fertility, with the abnormal accumulation of abscisic acid (ABA) playing a pivotal regulatory role in these processes. Nevertheless, pollen abortion may entail the synergistic regulation of multiple hormonal networks, including auxin (IAA), jasmonic acid (JA), and ethylene (ETH) [26, 27]. For example, in the tomato male-sterile sl-2 mutant, endogenous ABA levels in stamens are six times higher than those in fertile plants, indicating that ABA accumulation may directly impact normal stamen development [28]. Similarly, in K and T cytoplasmic male-sterile materials, ABA levels significantly increase during the critical period of pollen abortion, suggesting that ABA accumulation may be closely related to the activation of male sterility gene expression [29]. Notably, antagonistic interactions between IAA and ABA play a role in regulating pollen wall formation, while the JA signalling pathway potentially impacts pollen viability by modulating programmed cell death in the tapetum [30]. Research on male sterility in lilies is urgently needed to delve deeper into the cascading regulatory mechanisms of ABA in conjunction with other hormones during pollen development.

In the regulation of ABA biosynthesis, 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED) serves as a key rate-limiting enzyme, and its expression levels are positively correlated with ABA accumulation [31]. NCED catalyzes the cleavage of carotenoid substrates to generate xanthoxin, which subsequently undergoes a two-step oxidation reaction to convert it into ABA. This entire process occurs in both chloroplasts and the cytoplasm [32]. Consequently, the spatiotemporally specific expression of the LoNCED gene may directly influence the efficiency of ABA synthesis by regulating the metabolic flux of the substrate. The LeNCED1 gene, which is isolated from tomatoes, has significantly increased transcription levels under drought stress, and its overexpression results in a marked accumulation of ABA, leading to prolonged seed dormancy [33]. In Arabidopsis, the AtNCED3 gene was cloned, and transgenic plants with sense expression presented significantly increased ABA levels and increased drought tolerance, whereas antisense transgenic plants were more sensitive to drought stress [34]. These findings suggest that NCED genes not only regulate ABA accumulation under stress conditions but also may influence plant growth, development, and reproductive processes. The transcriptional regulation of NCED may involve upstream modulators such as AREB/ABF family transcription factors and epigenetic modifications, whose species-specific regulatory mechanisms in Lilium warrant further investigation [35]. Studies in crops such as avocado [36], cowpea [37], grape [38], peanut [39], and common bean [13] have also revealed that NCED genes are significantly induced under stress conditions, promoting ABA accumulation and conferring stress resistance. Research on sweet cherry has shown that the PavNCED gene is highly expressed during floral bud dormancy, regulating ABA metabolism and controlling the transition between floral bud growth and dormancy [40]. More recent studies further indicate that NCED subfamily genes play important regulatory roles in plant pollen development [41, 42].

This study involved the cloning and functional expression analysis of the LoNCED gene in lilies, clarifying its regulatory role in anther abortion and laying a theoretical foundation for further exploration of its specific regulatory functions in male sterility in Lilium. This study revealed a positive correlation between LoNCED expression and the accumulation of abscisic acid (ABA), a key phytohormone involved in stress responses and reproductive development [43]. For example, does LoNCED respond to developmental signals through epigenetic modifications (e.g., DNA methylation or histone acetylation)? Does its promoter region harbor pollen development-associated cis-regulatory elements? Furthermore, systematic investigations are needed to elucidate how the dynamic crosstalk between ABA and other phytohormones orchestrates the spatiotemporal expression patterns of LoNCED. This regulatory cascade initiates with the overexpression of the LoNCED gene, which activates the biosynthesis pathway of the phytohormone abscisic acid (ABA), leading to significant ABA accumulation in anther tissues. Elevated ABA levels suppress the expression of key transcription factors regulating tapetum development, DYT1 and TDF1. Consequently, the expression of downstream genes critical for pollen wall formation, including the sporopollenin synthase CYP703A2, the callose synthase CALS5, and the primexine formation DEX1, is markedly downregulated. These molecular alterations ultimately result in developmental defects in pollen wall structure, triggering the disruption of nutrient supply and programmed cell death of microspores, thereby manifesting the male sterility phenotype.

The protein encoded by this gene is derived from an open reading frame (ORF) of 1,812 bp, which encodes 603 amino acids. Bioinformatics analysis of the encoded protein revealed a relative molecular Weight of approximately 66.69 kDa And a theoretical isoelectric point of 5.87. It is a hydrophilic protein with no transmembrane domains or signal peptides. The protein is primarily localized to mitochondria, which is consistent with its involvement in ABA biosynthesis [44]. Furthermore, secondary structure predictions revealed a high proportion of random coils, indicating potential structural flexibility and functional stability, which aligns with tertiary structure predictions.

The LoNCED gene in sterile lily progeny was significantly upregulated in anthers during the pollen mother cell stage and tetrad stage, suggesting that its increased expression leads to ABA accumulation, thereby affecting normal pollen development.

Notably, aberrant ABA accumulation may trigger the activation of the SnRK2 kinase signalling pathway, which in turn downregulates the expression of pollen wall-associated genes (CALS5 and PME48), ultimately culminating in microspore developmental arrest [45, 46]. This study further quantified ABA levels in lily anthers and analysed the relative expression of LoNCED during key stages of anther development. Gene cloning and bioinformatics analyses revealed that the LoNCED protein lacks transmembrane domains and signal peptides, is hydrophilic, and exhibits significant sequence homology to NCED proteins from other species. The functional characterization of LoNCED as an NCED-family enzyme—known for catalyzing the rate-limiting step in ABA biosynthesis—supports the hypothesis that its misregulation contributes to male sterility in lilies [47]. Phylogenetic analysis revealed high sequence similarity between LoNCED and NCED homologues from other species, reinforcing its evolutionary conservation and functional importance in reproductive development [48].

Transient expression experiments in Lilium anthers revealed that LoNCED overexpression caused abnormal microspore development, accompanied by the significant downregulation of genes critical for microspore formation and maturation [49, 50]. Similar effects have been observed in other plant species, where ABA accumulation has been linked to tapetum degeneration, pollen abortion, and defective anther dehiscence, as observed in rice [51] and wheat [52]. These findings further reinforce the role of LoNCED as a key regulator of ABA biosynthesis, which directly influences lily anther development and ultimately contributes to male sterility. For functional validation, the LoNCED gene was overexpressed in Arabidopsis, leading to phenotypic changes such as inhibited growth, early bolting, and accelerated flowering. These findings suggest that LoNCED may play a role in plant developmental timing and hormonal regulation. Given that ABA is a key phytohormone that regulates both stress responses and floral development, its misregulation due to LoNCED overexpression could severely disrupt anther development and pollen formation, ultimately contributing to male sterility. These findings further support the hypothesis that LoNCED-mediated ABA accumulation plays a crucial role in reproductive abnormalities.

Although this study provides an initial understanding of LoNCED expression in lily anthers and its effect on ABA accumulation, its precise regulatory mechanisms require further investigation. Future research should focus on LoNCED signalling pathways, their interactions with ABA biosynthesis and metabolism, and their specific role in lily fertility regulation to provide stronger theoretical support. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated the importance of bioinformatics in NCED functional research, with cloning and bioinformatics analysis of the AcNCED gene in kiwifruit (Actinidia chinensis) and NCED genes in Dendrobium officinale revealing their high expression under abiotic stress [53, 54]. Drawing on these research experiences and integrating genetic transformation with functional validation will further enhance our understanding of LoNCED, providing new technological pathways and theoretical foundations for the molecular breeding of male-sterile lilies.

Conclusion

The LoNCED gene is differentially expressed in various floral organs and at different developmental stages in lilies, with predominant expression in anthers during the pollen mother cell stage. The overexpression of LoNCED resulted in abnormal anther development in lilies, preventing normal microspore formation, accompanied by the significant downregulation of microspore development-related genes. In transgenic Arabidopsis plants overexpressing LoNCED, phenotypic abnormalities such as defective anther development, weak growth, and early bolting were observed. Additionally, the anther contents gradually degrade, the tapetum disintegrates prematurely, the pollen sacs deform, and the contents of the sacs degrade, leading to the formation of a large cavity or breakdown of the sac walls, ultimately preventing the formation of functional anthers.

Methods

Plant materials

This study examines fertile (HF) and sterile (HS) offspring from the hybridization of pollen-bearing ‘Siberia’ lily (maternal, F) and ‘Marco polo’ lily (paternal, M) grown at the Lily Breeding Base of the Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences (25°7’33’’ N, 102°45’48’’ E). Three biological replicates (n = 30 plants per replicate) were analysed for each offspring type (HF/HS). Anthers and floral organs at various developmental stages were collected based on bud size [55]. Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) wild-type (WT) plants were used for transgenic experiments. All the plants were cultivated in a growth chamber at 22 ± 2°C with 16 h light/8 h dark and a vermiculite-peat substrate (1:1). Fresh anthers were collected from five randomly selected plants per replicate and immediately weighed‌. The samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis.

Parameter determination

Cytological observation

Pollen samples (n = 30 anthers per group)‌ were fixed in FAA solution (100 mL containing 5 mL of 38% formaldehyde, 5 mL of acetic acid, And 90 mL of 70% ethanol) for 24 h, followed by dehydration through ethanol gradients (75%, 85%, 90%, And 95%) for 4, 2, 2, And 1 h, respectively. The samples were soaked in Anhydrous ethanol for 60 min and then immersed in a benzene‒ethanol mixture for 5–10 min, followed by two xylene treatments for 5–10 min each. The samples were infiltrated with Molten paraffin at 65°C for three 1-hour cycles and then embedded in a paraffin embedding Machine. For sectioning, 4 μm thick paraffin sections were cut with a rotary microtome And dried at 60°C. Then, the slices were classified by safranin O-fast green staining, And the sections were stained with 1% safranin O for 1 h, washed with distilled water, discoloured And then counterstained with 0.5% fast green for 1 min (G1031, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The sections were finally observed and photographed under an optical microscope (YS100; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Examination of the ABA content

The ABA content was determined via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). An appropriate amount of plant sample was extracted with 80% methanol via ultrasonic treatment for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and then injected for analysis. HPLC was performed using a C18 reversed-phase column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) with a mobile phase of methanol/water (60:40, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, a column temperature of 30°C, And a detection wavelength of 265 nm. The ABA content in the samples was calculated based on peak area via a standard curve generated from ABA standard solutions.

Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Freshly harvested Lily flowers were collected, And different floral tissues were separated. Approximately 50 mg of each sample was Weighed and rapidly ground into a fine powder in Liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, 1 mL of TRIzol reagent was added (ensuring the sample volume did not exceed 10% of the TRIzol volume to optimize lysis efficiency). The supernatant was discarded, And the RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, followed by the addition of 0.2 mL of chloroform per 1 mL of TRIzol. The tubes were tightly capped And vigorously shaken for 15 s to ensure thorough mixing. After a complete reaction, the samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, And the upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new centrifuge tube. RNA precipitation was performed by adding 0.5 mL of isopropanol per 1 mL of TRIzol, incubating at room temperature for 10 min, And centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for Another 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, And the RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol by vortexing briefly, followed by centrifugation at 7500 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The ethanol was carefully removed, And the RNA pellet was dried at room temperature or under vacuum for 5–10 min before being dissolved in RNase-free water. If necessary, the RNA was further solubilized via incubation at 55–60°C for 10 min. The extracted RNA samples were stored at −80°C. cDNA synthesis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A 10 µL reaction mixture was prepared for gDNA removal And incubated on ice, followed by the addition of a 10 µL reverse transcription reaction mixture. After thorough mixing, the mixture was incubated at 42°C for 15 min, followed by inactivation at 95°C for 1 min and then cooling on ice. The synthesized cDNA was stored at −20°C until further use.

qRT‒PCR expression

Total RNA of the LoNCED gene was extracted from anthers at different developmental stages of fertile and sterile lily progeny and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Specific primers for the LoNCED gene were designed via the NCBI database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi). qRT‒PCR was performed using the TaKaRa TB Green Premix Ex Taq Ⅱ kit in a 20 µL reaction system containing 10 µL TB Green Premix Ex Taq Ⅱ (Tli RNaseH Plus), 0.8 µL of each forward And reverse primer, 0.4 µL ROX Reference Dye (50X), 2 µL cDNA template, And 6 µL ddH₂O. The qRT‒PCR program was as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, Annealing at 60°C for 30 s, And extension 72°C for 30 s. A melting curve Analysis was performed with the following steps: 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 60 s, And 95°C for 15 s. Each sample was analysed in triplicate. The β-actin gene was used as an internal reference, and the relative expression of LoNCED in fertile and sterile Lily Anthers at key developmental stages was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. All control group measurements were normalized to 1 as reference, with only relative changes displayed graphically (Table 1).

Cloning of the LoNCED gene

Based on transcriptome sequencing data from the pollen mother cell stages of fertile and sterile lily progeny, a differentially expressed gene annotated as NCED3 was selected for cloning and validation in fertile Lily Anthers. Specific primers were designed, And PCR was performed using cDNA as a template. The 25 µL PCR reaction mixture contained 12.5 µL of Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix, 1.25 µL of each forward And reverse primer, 1 µL of cDNA template, And 9 µL of ddH₂O. The PCR program was as follows: initial denaturation at 98°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 15 s, Annealing at 56°C for 30 s, And extension at 72°C for 30 s. PCR products were Analysed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target band was excised and purified. The purified product was ligated into the pRI101-GFP vector and transformed into competent Escherichia coli cells. Positive clones were screened by colony PCR, and selected clones were submitted for sequencing to confirm the insert.

Bioinformatics analysis of the LoNCED gene

Various bioinformatics tools have been employed to analyze the properties of LoNCED proteins. The amino acid sequence of the LoNCED gene was subjected to BLASTP analysis using the NCBI database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) to determine sequence homology and evolutionary relationships.

Subcellular protein localization analysis

The ORFs of LoNCED were inserted into the pNC-Cam23FC-eGFP vector to generate the LoNCED-GFP; the empty pNC-Cam23FC-eGFP was the negative control, and mstp was the mitochondrial localization signalling protein. A. tumefaciens bacterial suspensions containing vectors were infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves for subcellular localization Analysis. After 48 h, the fluorescence signals of GFP were observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, C2-ER, Japan).

Construction of the overexpression vector

The correctly sequenced bacterial culture was incubated in LB medium containing kanamycin at 37°C for 12–16 h. Plasmid extraction was performed following the steps outlined in the TanGen Plasmid Mini Kit, And the concentration And purity of the extracted plasmid were assessed. Using the extracted plasmid as a template, primers were designed based on the In-Fusion cloning principle for vector construction. The target fragment was amplified via high-fidelity 2X F8 FastLong PCR MasterMix following the recommended amplification protocol. The amplified fragment was recovered using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and purified before being recombined with the vector. For linearization of the plant expression vector pNC-Cam23FC-eGFP, single restriction enzyme digestion was performed via SfiI, following the manufacturer’s protocol. The digestion reaction was carried out at 37°C for 1 h, and the digested product was purified and recovered. The purified vector fragment was then treated using a PCR purification kit, and the Linearized plant expression vector was Ligated with the digested target fragment at 37°C for 30 h. The ligation product was subsequently transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α. Single colonies were selected, cultured, and validated by sequencing.

Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation of lilium for overexpression of LoNCED

Competent Agrobacterium tumefaciens EHA105 cells stored at −80°C were thawed on ice. Under sterile conditions, the constructed plasmid was added to a freshly thawed competent cell suspension for transformation. The transformed Agrobacterium culture was plated onto YEP solid media supplemented with 50 µg·mL−1 kanamycin And 50 µg·mL−1 rifampicin. After the Liquid was fully absorbed, the plates were inverted And incubated at 28°C for 48–72 h. Once bacterial colonies had grown, a single colony was picked And inoculated into 500 µL of YEP liquid medium (containing 50 µL kanamycin) in a centrifuge tube And cultured at 28°C with shaking at 220 rpm overnight. The incubation time was adjusted according to colony size, which was typically adjusted to 12–16 h depending on colony growth. The overnight bacterial culture was subjected to colony PCR identification. Positive bacterial cultures displaying the correct PCR band were diluted at ratios of 1:50 or 1:25 into fresh YEP medium containing 50 µL kanamycin And 50 µL rifampicin And incubated at 28°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 8–16 h. This step was repeated in 50 mL centrifuge tubes, followed by further incubation in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL YEP medium under the same conditions for 12 h. After incubation, the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 4000×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The bacterial pellet was washed twice with sterile water and then resuspended in MS Liquid medium supplemented with 0.1 mM acetosyringone. The final bacterial suspension was adjusted to an OD600 = 0.6. Freshly excised Lilium anthers at the pollen Mother cell stage And tetrad stage were punctured with small holes And immersed in the Agrobacterium suspension. The anthers were incubated in a shaker at 100 rpm for 15 min And then transferred onto sterile filter paper to remove excess bacterial mixture. The Anthers were subsequently placed on MS solid media and cocultivated in darkness at 28°C ± 2°C for 3 days. Following cocultivation, the anthers were washed with cefotaxime, transferred to selection medium (MS medium containing 50 mg/L kanamycin) And incubated at 28°C ± 2 °C. After 1 day of MS medium culture, transgene expression was analysed via marker gene detection.

Agrobacterium-mediated transformation of Arabidopsis and selection of transgenic plants

Floral dip transformation was used for Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation of A. thaliana. The recombinant plasmid was first transformed into Agrobacterium strain EHA105, and the bacterial suspension was prepared at OD600 = 0.8–1.2. Silwet-L77 was added at a final concentration of 0.02% to enhance infection efficiency, And the entire inflorescence of each Arabidopsis plant was dipped into the bacterial suspension for 2–3 s. After being dipped, the plants were covered with plastic wrap to Maintain humidity And incubated in the dark at 25°C for 24 h. This transformation process was repeated three times at 7-day intervals. Following infection, the plants were cultivated under 16-hour light/8-hour dark conditions at 23°C until seed Maturation. Mature seeds were collected by gently rubbing siliques onto clean white paper, then wrapped in paper And dried at 37°C for 24 h. Once dried, the seeds were sieved through a 60-mesh sieve. A portion of the harvested T0 transgenic seeds was vernalized in darkness at 4°C for 2‒3 days, surface sterilized with 10% (v/v) NaClO solution for 10 min And then rinsed 4‒5 times with sterile water. The sterilized seeds were evenly spread onto MS selection medium supplemented with 50 mg·L−1 kanamycin And incubated in a growth chamber for 10‒14 days. Positive plants emerged approximately 10 days later, each having approximately four leaves. After emergence, the plants were transplanted into a 1:1 mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite for further cultivation. Cytological observations of Arabidopsis anthers at the flowering stage (stages 6 And 7) of development have been performed [56].

Statistical analysis

The data were Analysed via descriptive statistics in Excel 2024 And plotted via GraphPad. SPSS Statistics 20 statistical analysis software was used for the difference significance test, and Duncan’s test was used to determine the difference in the mean values of different treatments at the level of P < 0.05. All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates.
