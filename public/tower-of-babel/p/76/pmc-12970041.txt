Under the lens: utilizing trauma video review for performance improvement, quality improvement and resident and fellow education
Authors: Caitlin Anne Fitzgerald, Alexander C Schwed, Daniel Holena, Amelia W Maiga, Cristy Meyer, Ryan Peter Dumas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Trauma video review (TVR) was first described in the 1980s by Dr Hoyt at the University of California, San Diego as a tool for quality improvement (QI). Since then, TVR has been utilized in numerous QI, performance improvement (PI) and education initiatives including resuscitation efficiency, procedural times and leadership skills of both the surgical and emergency medicine teams. To this effect, an American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (AAST) Annual Meeting lunch session was designed to serve as a roadmap for attendees to use to navigate both the implementation and utilization of TVR at their institution. This manuscript presents a summary of this session at the 2025 AAST Annual Meeting and reviews the basics of starting a TVR program, potential medicolegal issues that could arise and how to navigate them, and the various applications of TVR including QI, PI and resident and fellow education.

Implementing a new trauma video review program

Before starting a trauma video review (TVR) program at your institution, it is critical to assess your institutional environment. Engage with hospital leadership such as the Chief Quality Officer and Chief Medical Officer to ascertain key stakeholder needs, expectations and concerns about TVR.1 Discuss the program among your department and your division and receive buy-in from faculty who will directly participate in the TVR program. Without divisional or departmental support, it is extremely challenging to implement and sustain a formal TVR program. Remember to focus on other stakeholders that make up the trauma team including nursing, emergency medicine, respiratory therapy, pharmacy, social work, etc. While your TVR program is being developed, attend stakeholders’ meetings, present your vision for why TVR is important to improving the care of trauma patients, and stress that it will never be used in a punitive fashion. Listen to their apprehension and proactively address the concerns that people may be otherwise afraid to voice in larger meetings by offering anonymous surveys or comment boxes.2 Providing peer points of contact to share their experience with TVR is also helpful when engaging with stakeholders. Additionally, lean on the ever-growing body of literature that supports the use of TVR as an invaluable tool for continuous performance improvement (PI) in trauma.3 4

In addition to securing buy-in from colleagues, stakeholders and divisional and hospital leadership, it is also necessary to review and understand specific institutional policies and local regulations surrounding any requirements needed for audiovisual recordings. Review the consent to treat form that is provided by the institution. Oftentimes, the consent to audiovisual recordings is included in the language of the hospital consent, but if it is not, you will have to incorporate language as such or obtain separate consent from the patient or the patient’s family to videotape them.

Financial support is a crucial element to any TVR program as system costs vary by equipment, vendor and subscription fees. Equipment packages differ and can be tailored to your institution in terms of the number of cameras and microphones that are installed, the information technology (IT) infrastructure including the cost of data storage, whether the cameras will be continuously recording or used in an on-demand fashion, the length of the retention of the data files and the cost and amount of IT support needed to maintain the system.5 To offset some of the start-up and maintenance costs, funding can be obtained through grants such as from the National Institutes of Health or the Department of Defense, charitable foundations, hospital quality improvement (QI) initiative funds, departmental/divisional discretionary funds or even as part of a recruitment package. Foundations such as the William P. and Lorraine Manos Foundation have funded TVR in the past to focus on PI initiatives while the Trauma Research and Education Fund has provided capital for TVR to be utilized to reduce the number of trauma-related deaths.

Once you have a TVR system installed, shift focus to building your program. Program goals are often dictated by specific environmental needs and constraints with a lot of variability between institutions. Some important factors to consider when building your program include the people who will be participating (training levels, specialty), the case selection, the logistics of when to review videos, how to review them and what data will be collected, and the role of providing feedback and whether this will occur in a group or individualized setting. When designing your program, it is helpful to draft a plan for approval to keep expectations in line with the processes that are being developed and to manage non-patient care-related data usage requests.

Navigating medicolegal issues

When navigating potential legal issues surrounding TVR, one of the most cited concerns is around patient confidentiality. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) of 1996 that was created to establish national standards to protect sensitive patient health information from unauthorized disclosures does comment on video recordings and notes that these can be utilized for internal QI without patient deidentification. For broader, external use, all 18 HIPAA identifiers such as face, voice, medical record number, dates, etc must be removed from the video.6

The Joint Commission also comments on video recording and states that ‘video may be used for care, safety or QI, but never as a replacement for required human observation’.7 For a TVR program to be compliant with The Joint Commission, centers must demonstrate clear policies and procedures to regulate any videographic data collection, storage and use, they must have established informed consent procedures if a video will be used in a way that is beyond the direct care of the patient, and they must have audit trails to prevent inappropriate dissemination or use of the videos being recorded.7

When evaluating TVR from a medicolegal perspective and case law, Succar et al identified four main high-risk litigation factors to consider and noted ways to mitigate these risks. First, the risk of spoliation (destruction of evidence) can be reduced with autodeletion policies. Next, the process of informed consent can be streamlined by including language surrounding audiovisual consent in the general hospital consent. As noted before, patient privacy can be managed by deidentifying the videos and restricting access. Finally, the risk surrounding protected health information can be lessened if a hospital is part of a peer review program as video review would fall under QI and assurance practices.8

Non-technical skills assessment

TVR has emerged as a vital tool to assess non-technical skills demonstrated during a trauma resuscitation. The framework by which non-technical skills are assessed is most often based on the modified trauma non-technical (T-NOTECHS) skills scale (figure 1). This scale evaluates five domains including leadership, cooperation and resource management, communication and interaction, assessment and decision-making and situational awareness/coping with stress. Each category is evaluated on a scale of 1–5 points with 5 points being the top score.9

The development of non-technical skills is essential as these skills have been linked to more efficient trauma resuscitations. Maiga et al evaluated video recordings of trauma resuscitations from 19 trauma centers, specifically evaluating team dynamics and communication and how that impacted patients in hemorrhagic shock as they were moving to the next phase of care. They found that centers that scored higher on the T-NOTECHS scale were able to move patients faster to the next phase of care, whereas centers with larger trauma teams and less effective leadership and communication tended to be slower.10 Higher T-NOTECHS scale scores, specifically assessment and decision-making scores, have also been associated with higher rates of return of spontaneous circulation after an emergency department thoracotomy.11

TVR can also be used to evaluate trainee behavior, specifically resilience and how it relates to leadership in a high-acuity trauma resuscitation. Using TVR, resilience behaviors such as speaking up, knowledge sharing and cooperation have been studied and noted to be highest in instances where the trauma team leader took a transformational (charismatic and motivational) or transactional (goal-oriented) leadership approach. Resiliency declined in the setting of a passive (intervenes only when necessary) leader.12 Utilizing TVR in this way provides an objective view of how trainees perform and cope during high-acuity situations and not only gives faculty a way to evaluate trainees but also gives trainees the opportunity to develop their own leadership style.

Resident and fellow education

Providing surgical trainees with direct, objective feedback can be challenging. Currently, surgical residents and fellows are evaluated in a multitude of different ways that include nationally recognized milestones, rotation and service-specific goals or one-on-one directed feedback. When determining which type of feedback will be the most effective, it ultimately depends on the resident, situation and specific feedback that is being given. However, challenges with retroactive feedback arise when you and your trainee have different interpretations of how a case went and when details of the case become less clear as the time passes. In trauma and acute care surgery in particular, it can be difficult to provide real-time feedback as call shifts are often busy with inadequate time to debrief.

In addition to the applications previously mentioned, TVR is also an educational resource. Unlike real-time feedback, video recordings are time-stamped, can be paused, or rewound and watched again. Videos can also be archived for retrospective review and for longitudinal comparison of both technical and non-technical skills allowing the faculty to tailor their feedback into an individualized approach for each trainee. The key to incorporating TVR into surgical education is to adapt the model to your institution and recognize that the goals of the program are going to be different for residents and fellows. Fellows have likely already gained some degree of leadership skills during their residency training and are now making the transition to being an attending and managing the trauma resuscitation from an all-encompassing perspective. Residents, on the other hand, likely need both technical skill refinement and development of leadership skills. Regardless of the approach that is undertaken, utilizing TVR over a period of time allows for the tracking of longitudinal progress and can lead to further individualized goals and education plans.

Taking TVR to the next level: improving performance targets

To develop a fully integrated TVR program takes time. Starting with organizational safety culture is important as it is the system that rewards performance and sets what is both accepted and expected at an institution. TVR as a tool to promote a culture of safety allows for buy-in from key stakeholders such as the Chief Quality Officer at your institution and allows for longevity of the program and for rollover of video review into other aspects of patient care.

In order to build buy-in, start by setting the expectations as to where the video recording will take place, under what circumstances participants will be recorded, and how the data collected will be shared and in what forum. Create structured educational sessions such as weekly video review or incorporate TVR into simulation. Develop a staff request process by which same-day ad-hoc reviews are possible and encouraged. Communicate with staff to close the loop after a video is reviewed and to provide corrective action if needed. After your program is up and running, share progress and data at routine intervals with the stakeholders. This can take place at monthly meetings, staff meetings, educational sessions or at institution-wide quality meetings.

Building a structured education program sets formal expectations surrounding TVR. Start by holding weekly video review sessions and build these sessions into the orientation process for new faculty or staff, so they are familiar with the process. Use topics identified in these review sessions to enhance simulation to make it more applicable to your institutional needs. While labor-intensive, establishing a continuing medical education program offers participants value and encourages them to continue to attend the sessions. A formal debrief process is also important as it allows for post-case review and staff feedback.

Ultimately, TVR can be used to measure trauma quality and to understand team performance during critical patient events. By combining technical skills such as time to performance of Advanced Trauma Life Support steps, time to intubation or time to blood product administration with non-technical skills outlined in the T-NOTECHS scale you can begin to measure quality performance such as time critical targets, patient outcomes and objective versus subjective perception of the event that took place. Once you have a quality target, you can implement a process improvement plan using a Plan-Do-Check-Act model (figure 3).14 By setting standards, establishing key performance indicators that are both technical and non-technical, engaging key stakeholders and adding value with education and simulation high-quality trauma care can be delivered.

Conclusion

TVR is the contemporary solution to modern trauma PI. Traditional trauma PI relies on an electronic medical record that is fraught with missingness.15 When starting a program, engage key stakeholders early and socialize the idea that video review will be incorporated into trauma service to the entire trauma team. While legal concerns surrounding patient privacy and hospital liability are valid, both HIPAA and The Joint Commission allow for video recording to be utilized for educational and quality purposes. Collaborate with other institutions that have formal TVR programs to review and adapt their TVR policy and consent forms to your institution. Once you have a program, the opportunities are endless for education, research, QI and PI.
