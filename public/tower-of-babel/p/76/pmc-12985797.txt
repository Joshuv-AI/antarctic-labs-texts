A Synergistic Genetic Engineering Strategy Induced Nervonic Acid in Brassica juncea and Brassica napus
Authors: Pandi Wang, Xiaoyue Liu, Xiaojuan Xiong, Gang Wu, Fang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nervonic acid (NA), a very-long-chain monounsaturated fatty acid, is known for its benefits in treating neurological diseases and promoting brain health. In this study, we utilized two different receptors, Brassica juncea (B. juncea, rich in erucic acid, C22:1) and Brassica napus (B. napus, high in oleic acid, C18:1), to overproduce NA through systematic metabolic engineering. Two multi-gene vector constructs, Napin-3 and Napin-5 (CgKCS::SLC1-1::DGAT1; CgKCS::SLC1-1::BnFAE1::LdLPAAT::DGAT1), are driven by seed-specific napin promoters. In B. juncea, Napin-3 and Napin-5 expression elevated NA levels to 48.7% and 46.3% in seed oil, respectively, compared to 2.5% in wild types. In B. napus, Napin-3 and Napin-5 expression achieved NA levels of 45% and 39.6%, respectively, while NA is absent in wild types. To our knowledge, this represents the highest NA production in plants to date, with stable oil content and yield, enabling cost-effective NA production. In B. juncea, a significant increase in NA is observed alongside a decrease in C18:1, C20:1, and C22:1 levels; in B. napus, the rise in NA is accompanied by a decrease in C18:1, and an increase in C20:1 and C22:1. These patterns reflect the dynamic equilibrium of fatty acids following NA conversion, influenced by the Dynamic Substrate Tugging (DST) Mechanism, in the form of either an EA-tugging mode or C18:1-tugging mode mechanism, depending on the cellular context. NA is an elongation product derived from C18:1, catalyzed by CgKCS with broad substrate specificity, indicating that plants with high levels of C18:1, similarly to those rich in C22:1, serve as excellent candidates for NA production. This “green factory” for NA production provides strong support for its pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and industrial applications. The exogenous and the endogenous enzymes coordinate function remodeling of the intra-seed fatty acid elongation flux through the DST strategy, thereby systematically enhancing the synthesis and accumulation efficiency of the target fatty acid.

1. Introduction

Nervonic acid (NA; 24:1 ω-9; cis-tetracos-15-enoic acid) is a very-long-chain monounsaturated fatty acid (VLCMFA, 22–26 carbons) found abundantly in the human brain, breast milk, liver, and kidney [1,2]. As a key component of sphingolipids, reduced NA levels in the brain are strongly linked to brain and neurological diseases, such as demyelinating disorders [2,3,4]. Evidence suggests that NA supplementation effectively supports the development, maintenance, and repair of brain nerve tissue [4,5,6,7,8]. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommends adding moderate amounts of NA to all infant formulas. The National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China (PRC) has approved Acer truncatum (A. truncatum) seed oil (5.52% NA, 2011 Announcement No. 9) and cis-15-eicosatetraenoic acid (a synthetic NA source, 2024 Announcement No. 8) as new food materials (http://www.nhc.gov.cn/). NA is expected to soon be recognized as a health-beneficial food ingredient.

NA is difficult for the human body to synthesize and is primarily obtained through dietary sources. Initially, NA was isolated from shark brain [9], but this way is now prohibited due to conservation concerns. The chemical synthesis of NA using cis-13-docosenyl methylester as a precursor has been attempted, but it yields low amounts and produces significant by-products [10]. NA is also present in seed oils from select plants, including species from the Brassicaceae family and others, such as Lunaria annua, Malania oleifera (M. oleifera), Cannabis sativa, A. truncatum, Tropaeolum speciosum, Cardamine graeca (C. graeca), and Ximenia caffra [11,12,13], However, limited distribution, long growth cycles, and inconsistent seed yields restrict these plants from being viable sources for large-scale NA extraction [13]. Developing novel sources for NA production has become a key focus for NA-related research and applications. Due to its cost-effectiveness, the genetic engineering of microorganisms, microalgae, and plants has attracted significant attention for NA production [10,11,12,14,15,16,17,18]. Genetic engineering has proven effective in increasing seed oil levels in crops, offering a promising and cost-efficient method for NA production [11,12].

NA is synthesized in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) by a membrane-bound fatty acid elongation complex (FAE), which elongates C16 and C18 fatty acids (FAs). The elongase complex catalyzes the cyclic addition of two carbon units to the acyl chain, which involves four enzymes: 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase (KCS, sometimes designated FA elongase, FAE), 3-ketoacyl-CoA reductase (KCR), 3-hydroxacyl-CoA dehydratase (HCD), and trans-2,3-enoylCoA reductase (ECR) (Figure 1A). Then, C18:1 is elongated to C24:1 after three cycles of FA elongation [15,19,20,21] (Figure 1A). KCS, the rate-limiting enzyme, determines substrate and tissue specificity in FA elongation and regulates the final content and kind of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) [22,23,24,25,26]. Subsequently, NA is assembled and stored as triacylglycerols (TAGs) via the Kennedy pathway [27], in addition to being converted from phospholipids to TAGs at the ER [28] (Figure 1A). The pathway involves three key enzymes: glycerol-3-phosphate acyltransferase (GPAT), lysophosphatidic acid acyltransferase (LPAAT), and diacylglycerol acyltransferase (DGAT) (Figure 1A). These enzymes play critical regulatory roles in lipid and phospholipid biosynthesis [28,29]. GPAT catalyzes the attachment of FAs on acyl-CoA to the sn-1 position of glycerol-3-phosphate (G3P), which is also the first reaction step in the Kennedy pathway; LPAAT adds acyl groups to the sn-2 position of lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), while DGAT transfers FAs from acyl-CoA to diacylglycerols (DAG) at the sn-3 position, forming TAGs (Figure 1A).

Plant seed oils are economical and sustainable industrial feedstocks [30]. Rapeseed, a Brassicaceae oilseed crop, offers favorable agronomic traits, including high oil content, adaptability to temperate climates, high yields, efficient Agrobacterium-mediated transformation [31], and VLCFA content. These attributes make rapeseed an increasingly popular platform for engineering NA production.

Previous studies primarily targeted single-gene expression, such as KCS or other genes in the NA biosynthesis pathway, to increase NA content in seed oil [24,25,26,32,33,34]. While these approaches successfully elevated NA levels, the increases were modest and often accompanied by the undesirable accumulation of erucic acid (EA, C22:1) [12,33,35]. Since high dietary EA intake is associated with adverse cardiovascular effects, engineering seed oil with reduced EA content would better meet the demands for large-scale NA applications in nutraceutical and pharmaceutical industries [36]. The production of NA in engineered crops depends not only on NA biosynthesis and accumulation but also on crop yield and total oil content. We hypothesized that the coordinated overexpression of multiple genes governing NA production and storage in oilseeds will synergistically increase overall NA levels by enhancing its biosynthesis, directing its accumulation to every sn position of TAGs, and elevating total seed oil content.

In this study, five candidate genes were selected to construct two multi-gene vectors under the control of the seed-specific napin promoter to boost NA accumulation in crop seeds and systematically evaluate the synergistic roles of these genes in NA biosynthesis (Figure 1B). The first vector, Napin-3, comprised a core gene set of CgKCS (KCS gene of C. graeca), SLC1-1 (a variant LPAAT gene of yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and DGAT1 (DGAT gene of A. thaliana) (Figure 1B). This CgKCS::SLC1-1::DGAT1 combination represents the minimal genetic module for simultaneous NA and oil content improvement (Figure 1B). CgKCS is central to the promotion of NA biosynthesis, whose overexpression resulted in the biosynthesis of NA in transgenic yeast, and increased NA levels in transgenic Arabidopsis thaliana (A. thaliana), Brassica carinata (B. carinata) and Brassica napus (B. napus) by 35-, 55-, 15-, and 28-fold, respectively [12]. In most plants, the sn-2 position of TAG is typically esterified with 18C FAs. This specificity arises from the stringent substrate selectivity of plant LPAATs, which restricts VLCFAs such as NA to the sn-1 and sn-3 positions [37]. As a result, the accumulation of NA is biologically constrained, preventing its content from exceeding 66% in TAGs. SLC1-1 prefers to incorporate C18:1-CoA and VLCFA-CoA at the sn-2 position of seed TAGs, and the overexpression of SLC1-1 in A. thaliana and B. napus cv Hero successfully enhanced C22:1 at the sn-2 position of TAGs and improved oil content [38,39,40]. SLC1-1 has been selected for inclusion in the Napin-3 vector in an attempt to promote NA to combine in the sn-2 position of TAG to improve NA accumulation. DGAT1 is the major target to improve the oil content of engineered crops, and its function has been extensively reported in engineered plant seeds overexpressing DGAT1, such as Brassica juncea [41], Brassica napus [42], and Jatropha curcas [43].

Based on Napin-3, the Napin-5 vector was further developed by incorporating the BnFAE1 (FAE gene of Brassica napus, B. napus) and LdLPAAT (LPAAT gene of Limnanthes douglasii, L. douglasii) (Figure 1B). The resulting construct, CgKCS::SLC1-1::BnFAE1::LdLPAAT::DGAT1, was designed to enhance the substrate production of C22:1 and promote the assembly of NA in the sn-2 position, thereby increasing the overall NA content (Figure 1B). Currently, extensive research has demonstrated that CgKCS can convert C22:1 to NA in plants. Therefore, potential receptor plants for NA production are those with high levels of C22:1 [11,12,15,44]. BnFAE1 is responsible for the elongation of C18:1 to C22:1 [26,34,45,46,47,48]. Co-expressing BnFAE1 and CgKCS may effectively promote NA synthesis. It was reported that LdLPAAT could incorporate VLCFA-CoA into sn-2 to raise the upper limit of EA content. The introduction of BnFAE1 and LdLPAAT into B. napus with a high EA content led to a significant increase in EA levels, from 54% to a maximum of 72%. Additionally, the incorporation of C22:1 at the sn-2 position of TAGs rose dramatically, from less than 2% to 20–25% [49]. The overexpression of LdLPAAT and BnFAE1, combined with the down-regulation of CaFAD2 (Crambe abyssinica Fatty Acid Desaturase 2), in Crambe abyssinica enhanced EA accumulation from 60% to a maximum of 76.9% [50]. Given the EA engineering results, it is inferred that NA content is limited by similar factors.

To evaluate the potential of our vector for NA production using different fatty acid backgrounds, Napin-3 and Napin-5 were respectively introduced into two recipient varieties: the low-EA-content B. napus line Zhongshuang 6 (ZS6) and the medium-EA-content B. juncea sp. yellow seed line Huangzijie (HZJ).

In this study, we achieved significant NA production in rapeseed seeds through a multi-gene strategy. By employing two multi-gene vector systems, we systematically evaluated the combinatorial effects of CgKCS, SLC1-1, BnFAE1, LdLPAAT, and DGAT1 on NA production. The results showed that expressing additional genes in the FAE complex did not further increase NA content in mature seeds. By using two distinct recipient lines, we found that both C22:1 and C18:1 can serve as substrates for NA synthesis. Notably, a dramatic increase in NA was observed in a double-low (low-EA and low-thioglycoside) receptor. This study successfully developed high-NA rapeseed lines as a “green factory” for NA production and provided valuable insights into the multi-gene regulation of NA biosynthesis.

2. Results

2.1. Overexpression of Candidate Genes in HZJ and ZS6 Seeds Increases NA Content

Two constructs were generated: (i) Napin-3: CgKCS, SLC1-1, and DGAT1; (ii) Napin-5: CgKCS, SLC1-1, BnFAE1, LdLPAAT, and DGAT1. These constructs were transformed into two recipient varieties with distinct seed FA profiles: HZJ (rich in EA) and ZS6 (rich in C18:1). The resulting transgenic plants were designated Napin-3-HZJ, Napin-5-HZJ, Napin-3-ZS6, and Napin-5-ZS6. Transgenic T0 seedlings and wild-type (WT) were confirmed by PCR for Bar transgenes and Bar protein test strips (Supplementary Figure S1). The proportions of positive T0 plants for Napin-3-HZJ, Napin-5-HZJ, Napin-3-ZS6, and Napin-5-ZS6 were 52%, 62%, 50%, and 45%, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1).

A total of 37 Napin-3-HZJ, 15 Napin-5-HZJ, 23 Napin-3-ZS6, and 16 Napin-5-ZS6 independent and positive T0 lines were obtained, the T1 seeds were collected, and the FA composition was determined. Gas chromatography (GC) analyses revealed that NA eluted at approximately 12 min (Figure 2). Compared to WT, NA levels increased in transgenic lines (Figure 2). In Napin-3-HZJ and Napin-5-HZJ lines, NA levels can reach and even exceed 40%, compared to 2.5% in WT HZJ controls (Figure 3A,B). Among these, 23 Napin-3-HZJ and 13 Napin-5-HZJ lines exhibited a seed oil NA content above 30% (Figure 3A,B). Similarly, Napin-3-ZS6 and Napin-5-ZS6 lines showed that NA levels can reach and even exceed 40%, compared to the undetectable levels observed in the WT ZS6 line, with 8 Napin-3-ZS6 and 7 Napin-5-ZS6 lines surpassing 30% NA content (Figure 3C,D).

T1 transgenic lines (Napin-3-HZJ-31/34, Napin-5-HZJ-1/23, Napin-3-ZS6-32/35, Napin-5-ZS6-42/51) were propagated through continuous selection and genetic screening to ultimately isolate homozygous T2 lines for further analysis. T3 seeds from high NA-accumulating T2 lines were harvested and analyzed for transgene expression using real-time PCR. Both transgenic HZJ and ZS6 showed consistent gene expression patterns. In Napin-3 transgenic lines, CgKCS, SLC1-1, and DGAT1 were overexpressed, as expected, while in Napin-5 lines, CgKCS, DGAT1, and LdLPAAT were overexpressed, but BnFAE1 and SLC1-1 were not (Figure 4). Further PCR analysis indicated that SLC1-1 and BnFAE1 failed to integrate into the chromosomes of the Napin-5 transgenic lines (Supplementary Figure S2). The results of Western blotting demonstrated the successful overexpression of CgKCS, SLC1-1, and DGAT1 proteins in the matured seeds of the two transgenic lines, Napin-3-HZJ-31 and Napin-3-HZJ-34, compared to the wild-type HZJ (Supplementary Figure S3). The incomplete overexpression of some genes may be attributed to the repeated use of identical promoters in the construct, which may lead to transgene silencing. No growth abnormalities or adverse effects on plant vigor or seed yield were observed in greenhouses and fields.

2.2. NA Content Significantly Increases Along with Significant Shifts in Other Fatty Acids in Transgenic Seed Oil

The FA composition was determined from T3 seeds and analyzed to examine the range of FA proportional redistribution in Napin-3-HZJ and Napin-5-HZJ (Figure 5A,B) and in Napin-3-ZS6 and Napin-5-ZS6 (Figure 6A,B). In the WT HZJ, which naturally contains rich EA levels, the transgene increased NA while reducing EA. Specifically, NA levels rose from 2.5% to 48.7% in Napin-3-HZJ and to 46.3% in Napin-5-HZJ. Concurrently, palmitic acid (C16:0), stearic acid (C18:0), oleic acid (C18:1), linoleic acid (C18:2), eicosenoic acid (C20:1) and C22:1 decreased, especially C18:1, C20:1 and C22:1 (Figure 5). C22:1, the major FA in WT HZJ, shows the most pronounced reduction, dropping from 36.7% to 7.1% in Napin-3-HZJ and to 9.6% in Napin-5-HZJ. Similarly, C20:1 level declined from 7.1% to 0.7% in Napin-3-HZJ and to 1.5% in Napin-5-HZJ. Additionally, the Napin-3-HZJ and Napin-5-HZJ lines exhibited a substantial decrease in C18:1 level, from 12.9% to 5.1% and to 4.7%, respectively. In contrast, the lignoceric acid (C24:0) levels increased in all transgenic lines (Figure 5). The NA content of HZJ transgenic seeds remained stable from T0 to T3 generation, with the best-performing line, Napin-3-HZJ-34-1, reaching 48.9% NA and as low as 6.1% EA.

In WT ZS6, which naturally has high C18:1 levels, the transgene increased NA while reducing C18:1. NA rose from 0.0% to 43.8% in Napin-3-ZS6 (Figure 6A) and to 39.6% in Napin-5-ZS6 (Figure 6B). Concurrently, C16:0, C18:0, C18:1, C18:2, and C18:3 decreased, with C18:1 showing the most pronounced drop, from 57.2% to 7.5% in Napin-3-ZS6 and to 7.4% in Napin-5-ZS6 (Figure 6A,B). In contrast, C20:1, C22:1, and C24:0 levels increased but remained much lower than C24:1 levels (Figure 6). C20:1 rose from 0.9% to 2.9% in Napin-3-ZS6 and to 3.0% in Napin-5-ZS6, while C22:1 increased from 0.0% to 10.9% in Napin-3-ZS6 and to 15.3% in Napin-5-ZS6. NA content in ZS6 transgenic seeds remained stable from T0 to T3 generation, with the best-performing line, Napin-3-ZS6-35-1, reaching 44.9% NA and 10.9% EA.

2.3. Content of VLCFAs at the sn-2 Position of TAGs in Transgenic Seeds Changed

VLCFAs typically do not accumulate at the sn-2 position of TAGs in most plants. Enhancing VLCFA incorporation at sn-2 is a promising strategy to increase their natural proportion, with LPAAT playing a key role. This is evidenced by the rise in C22:1 at the sn-2 position of TAGs, elevating from 0.5% to 22.5% in engineered Crambe abyssinica through LdLPAAT overexpression [50]. To investigate NA incorporation at the sn-2 position, we overexpressed LdLPAAT in Napin-5 transgenic lines. Supplementary Figure S4 illustrates the separation of lipid classes via thin-layer chromatography (TLC), showing distinct bands corresponding to triglycerides (TG), free fatty acids (FFA), diglycerides (DG), and monoglycerides (MG). The monoglyceride (MG) bands were scraped from the TLC plate for processing and subsequent analysis by GC. C22:1 was detected at the sn-2 position in all selected Napin-5 lines but absent in WT (Figure 7A,B). C22:1 level increased from 0% to 11.1% in Napin-5-HZJ and 2.4% in Napin-5-ZS6 plants (Figure 7A,B). C18:1, C18:2, and C18:3 were present in all lines. In HZJ transgenic lines, C18:1 and C18:2 levels decreased significantly, while C18:3 increased in Napin-3 lines but decreased in Napin-5 lines (Figure 7A). In ZS6 transgenic lines, C18:1 level decreased, whereas C18:2 and C18:3 levels increased significantly (Figure 7B).

2.4. Stabilized Oil Content in All Transgenic Lines

NA yield depends on both NA content and seed oil content, with higher oil content leading to greater NA yield. The analysis of oil content revealed no significant differences between the seeds of transgenic plants and WT plants under both greenhouse and field conditions. Stability in oil content was observed from the T3 to T4 generations. Although seed quality was adversely affected by freezing rain in late 2023, leading to a decline in oil content in the following year’s samples, no statistically significant difference in oil content was detected between the transgenic lines and the WT lines (Table 1).

2.5. Stabilized Yield in All Transgenic Lines

Stable crop yield is also a key factor in ensuring high NA production. Preliminary assessments of key agronomic traits in T3 and T4 lines, such as plant height, branch number, silique count, yield per plant, seed count per plant, seeds per silique, and thousand-grain weight, showed no significant differences between transgenic lines and WT lines (Supplementary Table S2). During the T4 planting period, the crops experienced natural freezing rain damage. No significant difference in frost damage was observed between the transgenic lines and their respective WT controls against either the HZJ or ZS6 genetic backgrounds. Plants from both the transgenic and WT lines with the HZJ background uniformly sustained Grade 4 frost damage. This grade is characterized by damage to most leaves (exhibiting localized or extensive shrinkage and scorching), breakage of some main branches, and thinning of lateral branches. Although the heart leaves remained normal or only slightly damaged and most plants retained the capacity for recovery, the severity of the damage led to the termination of subsequent surveys. In contrast, all plants (both transgenic and WT) with the ZS6 background exhibited milder Grade 2 frost damage and successfully recovered. Grade 2 damage is defined as approximately half of the leaves being affected, with similar symptoms of shrinkage and scorching, while the heart leaves remain normal.

3. Discussion

3.1. An Efficient and Cost-Effective System for the Biosynthesis of NA Was Successfully Established in Rapeseeds

The healthcare benefits of NA are well-established and recognized, and have led to the development and affirmation of its applications. However, the scarcity of natural resources remains the primary constraint to its expansion. To address this, we employed a multi-gene stacking strategy to engineer B. juncea and B. napus, enabling the efficient production of NA. This approach achieved the highest levels of NA recorded to date to our knowledge, at 49% in transgenic HZJ seeds and 45% in transgenic ZS6 seeds. These results surpass the previous benchmark in B. napus (T-BN), which reported an NA content of 31.3 ± 5.0% through the overexpression of CgKCS in high-EA rapeseed B. napus cv. Hero [12] (Supplementary Table S3). Moreover, when compared to other engineered species, our results are also superior, surpassing the highest NA level of 42.2 ± 1.3% observed in transgenic B. carinata (T-BC), which was also achieved through the overexpression of CgKCS [12] (Supplementary Table S3).

Rapeseed is a principal oil crop in China, characterized by its broad distribution and significant market acceptance. In our research, WT of B. juncea showed a high yield up to 2624 kg/ha with an oil content of 47% and WT of B. napus showed a high yield up to 2609 kg/ha with an oil content of 39% (data sourced from the provincial-level approval documentation by the Oil Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences) (Supplementary Table S3). These varieties are also highly susceptible to genetic transformation, with a green callus induction rate exceeding 85% and a regeneration rate above 25% [51]. In our study, 350 explants were used for the genetic transformation of each vector against an HZJ background, with a callus induction rate of 90.96% and a regeneration rate of 31.41%. Each vector was genetically transformed into 250 explants against a ZS6 background, with a callus induction rate of 72.13% and a regeneration rate of 22.31%. These attributes make them ideal platform crops for genetic modification aimed at NA production.

In this study, we obtained an NA level of 49% in transgenic HZJ and combined a high oil content of 47% and high yield of 2624 kg/ha (data for HZJ in Gansu province of China), The total yield of NA can reach 230 g/kg of seeds and 604 kg/ha. An NA level of 45% was observed in transgenic ZS6, with a combined high oil content of 39% and high yield of 2609 kg/ha (data for ZS6 in Hubei province of China); the total yield of NA can reach 176 g/kg of seeds and 458 kg/ha (Supplementary Table S3). To our knowledge, these are the highest reported values to date.

Currently, plant-derived NA is a predominant source for commercial healthcare products. The market price for raw materials with a 95% NA content is approximately $1000 per kilogram, indicative of burgeoning demand alongside constrained NA resources [52,53]. In stark contrast, the cost of NA biosynthesized using rapeseed as the base has dropped sharply. The cost of applying for a safety certificate in the early stage of transformation is relatively high, approximately $820,000 to $960,000. However, once a mature system is established and a safety certificate is obtained, a large number of target products can be produced through standard agricultural planting techniques, and the production cost is only the planting cost. The purchase cost of rapeseed hovers around $0.86 per kilogram. Taking this figure as the benchmark for production costs—the actual cost would be lower—through our research, the production cost per ton of NA for the two varieties is between $3.5 and $4.9. Our genetically modified rapeseeds, constituting a sustainable and innovative germplasm resource, are well-positioned to enable cost-effective, large-scale NA production. The oils derived from these engineered seeds are expected to be of considerable value, particularly for their potential in pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and industrial applications.

3.2. Evaluation and Analysis of Gene Expression in the Vectors

To enhance NA synthesis in rapeseed oil, this study engineered two multi-gene vectors. The Napin-3 vector contains CgKCS, SLC1-1, and DGAT1, aiming to increase NA production and its incorporation into triacylglycerols (TAGs). The Napin-5 vector further incorporates BnFAE1 and LdLPAAT to boost elongase substrate supply and promote NA integration at the sn-2 position of TAGs. All transgenes were driven by seed-specific napin promoters.

Expression analysis showed that Napin-3 lines overexpressed the three intended transgenes. In Napin-5 lines, however, CgKCS, DGAT1, and LdLPAAT were overexpressed, while SLC1-1 and BnFAE1 did not reach expected levels (Figure 4, Supplementary Figure S2). BnFAE1, an endogenous rapeseed gene involved in elongating C18:1 to C22:1, showed higher baseline expression with the high-erucic-acid (HZJ) background compared to the high-oleic (ZS6) background.

The incomplete overexpression of some genes may be attributed to the repeated use of identical promoters, which can lead to homologous recombination, DNA instability, and transgene silencing [54,55]. For multi-gene engineering, it is advisable to avoid employing the same promoter more than four times [54]. A range of stronger seed-specific promoters, such as those from broadbean β-conglycinin, phaseolin, or Arabidopsis 2S-3 and FAE1, could improve expression outcomes [55,56,57,58,59,60].

Vector design can also be optimized. The 2A self-cleaving peptide system allows for multiple genes to be linked under a single promoter and co-translated as a polyprotein that is subsequently cleaved into individual functional proteins, improving co-expression efficiency and reducing vector size [61]. Additionally, advanced cloning systems like Cre-loxP facilitate the assembly of long multi-gene constructs and support stable expression [54,62].

3.3. Efficient Conversion of EA Substrates to NA with an HZJ Background Through CgKCS

KCS genes are recognized as a rate-limiting enzyme in the synthesis of VLCFAs, with some demonstrating high efficiency in converting EA to NA. When erucoyl-CoA (14C 22:1-CoA) was used as the substrate, yeast cells transformed with LaKCS produced 86% radiolabeled 14C-24:1 in the total labeled very-long-chain monounsaturated FA [11], and those transformed with CgKCS produced 96% 14C-24:1 [12]. Guo et al. reported that overexpressing LaKCS in B. carinata elevated NA levels from 2.8% to 19.5% and reduced EA levels from 35.8% to 29.0% relative to the WT [11]. Additionally, the overexpression of CgKCS in B. napus boosted NA levels from 1.1% to 31.3% and lowered EA levels from 56.1% to 21.6%. Similarly, in B. carinata, CgKCS overexpression increased NA levels from 2.9% to 42.2% and decreased EA levels from 38.5% to 5.6% [12].

Natural oils abundant in NA are frequently associated with elevated EA levels, which studies have linked to detrimental effects on cardiac health, thus reducing the appeal of NA-enriched oils [11,12,15,44]. In our study, we overexpressed CgKCS, along with other genes, in HZJ, which is rich in EA. This resulted in transgenic lines with high NA levels exceeding 40% and low EA levels of below 10% (Figure 5). The more efficiently EA is converted to NA, the higher the NA content and the lower the residual EA in the oil, thereby facilitating the production of oil with high NA and low EA levels.

The significant increase in C24:0 content can be attributed to several factors. First, CgKCS show substrate flexibility, and in the process of elongating C22:1 to C24:1, it may simultaneously extend part of C22:0 to C24:0. Second, the increase in C24:0 may also be related to the synergistic effect of other enzymes within the cell, which play an auxiliary role in the synthesis and metabolism of C24:0. Third, increased C24:1 production could trigger the compensatory synthesis of its saturated counterpart C24:0 to maintain lipid homeostasis, as cells may upregulate this more stable form for optimized fatty acid storage dynamics.

3.4. C20:1 Can Serve as a Substrate for NA Conversion by CgKCS

In this study, the Napin-3-HZJ and Napin-5-HZJ line exhibited a significant decrease in C20:1 levels, from 7.1% to 0.7%~1.5% (Figure 5). Reports indicated that KCS is capable of catalyzing C20:1 to produce NA as well. Overexpression of MoKCS11 in A. thaliana a and Camelina sativa (C. sativa) increased NA levels from 0.2% to 5.0% and 0.5% to 2.5%, respectively, but failed to enhance NA accumulation in high-EA B. napus cultivars, indicating a substrate preference for C20:1 [44]. Additionally, in LaKCS transgenic A. thaliana and B. carinata, a higher proportion of C20:1 was converted to NA compared to EA, indicating LaKCS prefers elongating C20:1 over EA, as supported by yeast cells expressing LaKCS producing ~96% radiolabeled 14C-22:1 from 14C-20:1-CoA [11]. In transgenic Arabidopsis overexpressing CgKCS, although the overall NA content remained low (8–10%), it is notable that CgKCS can utilize C20:1 as a substrate, reducing its proportion from 18.5% in wild-type plants to 5–8%. This result is similar to that observed in yeast cells transformed with CgKCS, which produced approximately 92% radiolabeled 14C-22:1 using 14C 20:1-CoA as a substrate, indicating the relative promiscuity of CgKCS [12].

3.5. Efficient Conversion of C18:1 Substrate to NA with ZS6 Background Through CgKCS

KCS enzymes are considered typically substrate-specific, yet researchers have not identified distinct characteristics in LaKCS or CgKCS that explain their strong preference for elongating a single FA and accumulating NA in yeast or plants. This contrasts with Tropaeolum majus KCS, which favors C20:1 elongation, and Teesdalia nudicaulis KCS, which favors C18:1 elongation [25,26]. The products of KCS depend on both the enzyme type and the cellular context [36,63].

While NA synthesis at the expense of C18:1 has rarely been reported in plants, transgenic B. carinata-expressing CgKCS demonstrated that over half of C18:1 was redirected toward NA production [12]. In this study, the Napin-3-HZJ and Napin-5-HZJ lines also showed a significant reduction in C18:1 level, from 12.7%~13.1% to 4.7%~5.1% (Figure 5), further confirming the efficient conversion of C18:1 to NA by CgKCS.

The overexpression of CgKCS in ZS6, which has abundant C18:1, resulted in transgenic lines with markedly increased NA levels and a reduced C18:1 content (Figure 6). These findings demonstrate that CgKCS preferentially and directly converts C18:1 to NA when using the ZS6 background, consistent with observations in Yarrowia lipolytica-overexpressing CgKCS [63].

Our studies demonstrate that NA biosynthesis initiates from C18:1 via three iterative and sequential periods of two-carbon elongation, adhering to a Dynamic Substrate Tugging (DST) mechanism that coordinates elongation flux with cellular acyl-CoA pool dynamics. Two distinct metabolic modes govern this process. In high-EA genotypes, EA elongates to NA, simultaneously depleting EA reservoirs while activating the compensatory elongation of C18:1 → C20:1 and C20:1 → C22:1 to sustain lipid homeostasis (EA-tugging mode). This is a feedforward/compensatory activation mechanism that ensures the efficient synthesis of NA while maintaining the homeostasis of intermediate products and preventing a shortage of metabolic raw materials. Against C18:1-enriched backgrounds, three sequential elongation cycles (C18:1 → C20:1 → C22:1 → C24:1) were driven, with the transient accumulation of C20:1 and EA intermediates reflecting the kinetic partitioning between elongation steps (C18:1-tugging mode). The accumulation of products in consecutive extension steps is a natural “ripple” in the dynamic flow process, reflecting the kinetic characteristics of enzymatic reactions and providing clues for understanding the rate-limiting steps of metabolic pathways (Supplementary Figure S5).

The DST mechanism distinguishes the elongation modes initiated by different substrates (C18:1 vs. EA), indicating that the intracellular elongase system can switch its primary metabolic mode based on the predominant available substrate. This reflects the flexibility and economy of biological systems in resource utilization. Species with a high EA content, such as B. carinata, are often preferred for genetically engineered NA production [11,12]. However, our findings reveal the high potential of C18:1 conversion to NA, expanding the range of high-oleic crop species for NA genetic engineering. High-oleic rapeseed varieties with excellent agronomic traits may emerge as a promising platform for NA synthesis.

3.6. The sn-2 Position of TAGs Combined with NA Will Further Increase NA Level

The current research showed that NA was only combined into the sn-1 and sn-3 positions of TAGs [14,64,65,66]. Under natural conditions, the sn-2 position of TAGs generally does not combine NA or other VLCFAs. The main reason for this is that the LPAAT enzymes from plants are some of the most stringent acyltransferases regarding substrate discrimination [37]. SLC1-1 and LdLPAAT exhibit specificity toward VLCFA-CoA [38,49,50,67], prompting their selection for this study. To assess NA incorporation at the sn-2 position of TAG, we introduced SLC1-1 into all transgenic plants and additionally expressed LdLPAAT in Napin-5 transgenic lines.

In all Napin-5 transgenic lines, the overexpression of LdLPAAT significantly increased the proportion of C22:1 at the sn-2 position of TAGs, but no NA was detected at this position, indicating that LdLPAAT specifically incorporates C22:1 into the sn-2 position of TAGs and cannot facilitate C24:1 incorporation. The accumulation of EA at the sn-2 position is significantly higher when using the HZJ background than when using ZS6, likely due to genetic differences in EA content. However, EA levels at the sn-2 position with both HZJ and ZS6 remained substantially lower than those reported in earlier studies [49,50], possibly because of the metabolic conversion of EA into NA.

Identifying or engineering LPAAT genes that specifically incorporate NA into the sn-2 position of TAGs is a promising strategy to enhance NA content and reduce EA levels. M. oleifera (Olacaceae), an oilseed tree with seed oil containing over 60% NA, harbors eight putative LPAAT genes [68], providing valuable resources for genetic and metabolic engineering aimed at enhancing NA deposition at the sn-2 position of TAGs. Further exploration to discover novel sn-2-specific LPAATs, from M. oleifera or other species, could significantly advance NA biosynthesis. Success in this endeavor would mark a major milestone, potentially enabling oils to be engineered with an NA content exceeding 60%.

The NA content was observed at nearly similar levels in the Napin-3 and Napin-5 transgenic lines of HZJ and ZS6. This phenomenon may be attributed to the incomplete expression of the Napin-5 gene, as its transgenic lines did not exhibit overexpression of SLC1-1 compared to the Napin-3 transgenic lines. Additionally, LdLPAAT in Napin-5 may have failed to efficiently incorporate NA into the sn-2 position, thereby preventing the expected increase in NA content relative to Napin-3. In addition, the incorporation of C22:1 at the sn-2 position of TAG by LdLPAAT reduces the pool of free C22:1. This may limit the available substrate for conversion to NA mediated by CgKCS, potentially explaining the comparable NA levels observed in Napin-3 and Napin-5 transgenic plants.

We infer that even if all five genes are successfully overexpressed, the NA levels in Napin-5 may remain comparable to those in Napin-3 transgenic lines, as NA synthesis can utilize multiple substrates beyond EA, consistent with the DST mechanism described above. The total pool of monounsaturated FAs is likely the limiting factor in NA production, as previously observed for EA [49,50]. To further enhance NA biosynthesis, the CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of competing pathways, such as FAD2 and FAD3 desaturases, could redirect more monounsaturated FAs toward elongation rather than desaturation. Follow-up studies should employ the transient expression of single or multiple genes to preemptively assess combinatorial and positional effects.

3.7. Increased Oil Content Will Further Improve NA Content

Oil content is a key factor in NA production, as total NA yield in rapeseed oil depends on both NA percentage and stable oil content. In this study, the overexpression of SLC1-1 and AtDGAT1 in two rapeseed varieties did not significantly increase oil content compared to WT, unlike previous studies. Similarly, transgenic C. sativa lines overexpressing Camelina DGAT1 also showed no increase in oil content [69]. SLC1-1 (LPAAT) and AtDGAT1 (DGAT) bind free FAs to the sn-2 and sn-3 positions of G3P, respectively. The overexpression of these genes in plants like Jatropha, A. thaliana, and B. napus cv Hero typically increases oil content and elevates C18:1 level in TAG. Specifically, SLC1-1 overexpression increases oil content and significantly raises sn-2 C18:1 levels; AtDGAT1 overexpression increases oil content and significantly raises sn-3 C18:1 levels [43,70]. Our study focused on C18:1 as one of the main substrate for NA synthesis; its significant reduction (Figure 5 and Figure 6) may limit the substrate supply of SLC1-1 and DGAT1, thereby inhibiting its potential to increase oil content. Similarly, transgenic C. sativa lines overexpressing C. sativa DGAT1 also showed no increase in oil content [69], which reflected the complexity of the species’ metabolic network and the necessity of multi-step collaborative regulation. In our study, the silencing of some genes’ (such as SLC1-1) expression (Figure 4) may also affect the synergistic effect of DGAT1. However, the overexpression of DGAT1 may enhance the efficiency of NA’s integration into TAG, indirectly supporting the accumulation of NA (Figure 1A). Although the oil content did not significantly increase, DGAT1 may optimize the TAG assembly process to ensure the efficient storage of NA in seed oil. In addition, DGAT1 may reduce the accumulation of intermediate products (such as DAG or free fatty acids) by accelerating TAG synthesis, thereby avoiding feedback inhibition or toxic effects. This effect is particularly important in the context of increased metabolic flux, especially when C18:1 or C22:1 is heavily converted into NA. In our study, the oil content remained stable in the T0–T3 transgenic lines, thereby ensuring the stable production of NA.

To further boost oil content and NA production, the co-expression of regulatory genes such as WRI1 (which regulates carbon allocation to FA synthesis) and AtDGAT1 could enhance both FA synthesis and assembly [71]. Additionally, “package” genes like OLEOSIN, which stabilize oil bodies and reduce turnover, could further increase oil content. For example, the co-expression of Sesamum indicum OLEOSIN, AtWRI1, and AtDGAT1 significantly increased TAG content in Solanum tuberosum leaves and tubers [72,73,74], demonstrating the potential of combining genes that regulate synthesis and inhibit turnover to maximize oil content in crops.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Zhongshuang 6 (ZS6, B. napus), characterized by low EA content (<0.4%), low thioglycoside levels, and undetectable NA content, and Huazijie (HZJ, B. juncea, yellow-seeded), with rich EA content (~36.7%) and low NA content (~2.5%), were used in this study. Both ZS6 and HZJ are semi-winter varieties provided by the Oil Crops Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Wuhan, China. Transgenic plants and non-transformed controls were grown in pots containing a mixture of moss peat (PINDSTRUP MOSEBRUG A/S, Ryomgård, Denmark), field soil and vermiculite, with the proportion of 3:1:1. Plants were maintained at 20 ± 2 °C for a 16/8 h photoperiod at a light intensity of 44 μmol m−2 s−1 and 60–90% relative humidity in a greenhouse. Transgenic plants and non-transgenic controls were also grown under field conditions at the Hanchuan experimental base of the Oil Crops Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. All plants were managed using standard agronomic practices.

4.2. Construction of Expression Vectors

The Napin-3 vector is a three-gene co-expression plant vector derived from the pBWA(V)BII backbone, containing three exogenous gene expression cassettes (napin:CgKCS, napin:SLC1-1, and napin:DGAT1) in its T-DNA region. Each cassette includes a napin promoter, the target gene, and a napin terminator. Primers for gene cloning are listed in Supplementary Table S1. The CgKCS, SLC1-1, and DGAT1 expression cassettes were synthesized (Biorun BioSciences Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) and were amplified via PCR using primer pairs 3300-1-L/3300-1-R, LAC-L/LBC-R, and LBC-L/LBC-R, respectively. Amplified DNA was gel-purified (FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit, DC301-01, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) and recombined with vectors pBWA(V)BII, pBWD(LA)C, and pBWD(LB)C, resulting in constructs pBWA(V)BII-CgKCS, pBWD(LA)C-SLC1-1, and pBWD(LB)C-DGAT1. Finally, the SLC1-1 and DGAT1 cassettes from pBWD(LA)C and pBWD(LB)C were transferred into pBWA(V)BII-CgKCS through homologous recombination to complete the final vector.

The Napin-5 vector is a five-gene co-expression plant vector based on the pBWA(V)BII backbone, similar to the Napin-3 vector. The CgKCS, SLC1-1, and DGAT1 gene expression cassettes were obtained as described for the Napin-3 vector. The BnFAE1 and LdLPAAT cassettes were synthesized (Biorun BioSciences Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) and amplified via PCR using primers LBC-L and LAC-R, respectively (primers listed in Supplementary Table S1). Amplified DNA was gel-purified (FastPure Gel DNA Extraction Mini Kit, DC301-01). Subsequently, CgKCS was recombined with pBWA(V)BII, SLC1-1 and LdLPAAT with pBWD(LA)C, and BnFAE1 and DGAT1 with pBWD(LB)C, resulting in the constructs pBWA(V)BII-CgKCS, pBWD(LA)C-SLC1-1, pBWD(LA)C-LdLPAAT, pBWD(LB)C-BnFAE1, and pBWD(LB)C-DGAT1. Finally, the SLC1-1, LdLPAAT, BnFAE1, and DGAT1 cassettes were transferred into pBWA(V)BII-CgKCS through homologous recombination to complete the final vector.

The structures of the final vector constructs are summarized in Figure 1B. The Napin-3 and Napin-5 vectors both contain the Bar gene as a selectable marker, driven by the CaMV 35S promoter.

4.3. Genetic Transformation and Genetic Analysis

The two expression vectors were transferred into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (Weidi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). HZJ and ZS6 were then transformed using GV3101 harboring the respective vectors. The transformation followed the method described by Liu [75], with embryonic callus production and shoot regeneration screened on selection media containing 20 mg/L glufosinate ammonium (Basta® brand, BASF SE, Ludwigshafen, Germany).

Transgenic plants were transferred to soil and grown until the seeds were collected. The T0 seedlings tested positive for transgenic identification. Subsequently, T1 seeds harvested from self-pollinated plants were subjected to biochemical analysis to screen for high-NA phenotypes. Fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) analysis by GC was performed as described by Mietkiewska et al. [26]. Selected T1 transgenic lines underwent further genetic analysis and propagation, ultimately yielding homozygous T3 lines. These were then characterized for transgene expression levels, fatty acid composition (via GC analysis), Sn-2 fatty acid distribution, oil content, and yield-related agronomic traits across T3–T4 generations.

4.4. Identification of Transgenic Plant and Real-Time PCR Gene Expression

Genomic DNA was extracted from T0 transgenic plant leaves using a Plant Genomic DNA Kit (CW0531M, Jiangsu CoWin Biosciences Co., Ltd., Taizhou, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The presence of the Bar gene in transgenic plants was confirmed by PCR amplification of a 440 bp fragment using the primer pair 5′-GAAGTCCAGCTGCCAGAAAC-3′ and 5′-GCACCATCGTCAACCACTAC-3′. Primers were synthesized using Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China), and PCR was performed on a C1000TM thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). Reactions used 2× Rapid Taq Master Mix (P222, Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 55 °C~58 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 15 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Transgenic plants were also verified using Bar protein test strips, developed by the Oil Crops Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Total RNA was extracted from seeds at the mid-development stage using an RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (DP432, TIANGEN Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1–2 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScriptTM II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (6210A, Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). Primers were designed using the Primer3 Input tool (https://primer3.ut.ee/, accessed on 8 March 2021) and are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Real-time PCRs were performed on a Bio-Rad CFX384 Real-Time system using iTaqTM Universal SYBR Green Supermix (1725124, Bio-Rad, Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

4.5. Gas Chromatographic Analysis of Fatty Acid Compositions

Fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) were prepared from a single mature rapeseed using the method of ultrasonic extraction. Each seed was ground into powder in a 2 mL centrifuge tube, mixed with 600 µL petroleum ether, and subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 20 min. Then, 250 µL of 0.4 N KOH–methanol solution was added, followed by another 20 min of ultrasonic extraction. After cooling to room temperature, 500 µL of distilled water was added, and the mixture was vigorously shaken. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation, transferred to a gas chromatography vial, and placed in the autosampler for analysis.

FAMEs were quantified using an Agilent 7890A gas chromatography system (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) equipped with a hydrogen flame ionization detector and an Agilent Technologies DB-23 capillary column (30 m; 0.320 mm; 0.25 μm). Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 20 mL/min. The oven temperature was initially held at 180 °C for 2 min, then increased to 220 °C at a rate of 5 °C per minute, and maintained at 220 °C for 5 min. Chromatograms were recorded, and peaks were identified by comparing retention times to those of an authentic FAME standard (37 Components FAME Mix, C4-C24, CDAA-252795, AMP).

4.6. sn-2 Fatty Acid Distribution

Seed samples of 65 mg were ground into powder in a 2 mL centrifuge tube, mixed with 600 µL petroleum ether, and subjected to ultrasonic extraction for 20 min. After centrifugation at 5000 rpm, the supernatant was collected, dried under nitrogen to ~100 µL, and transferred to a 10 mL plastic tube. The samples were mixed with 2 mL of 1 M Tris-HCl, 20 mg porcine pancreatic lipase (PPL), 0.5 mL of 0.05% sodium cholate, and 0.2 mL of 2.2% CaCl2, vortexed for 1 min, and shaken at 40 °C for 3 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 1 mL of 6 mol/L HCl. Next, 2 mL of ether was added, vortexed, and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 2 min. The supernatant was collected, dried under nitrogen to ~200 µL, and separated on a thin-layer chromatography (TLC) plate using a hexane:ether:acetic acid (50:50:1) solvent system. The TLC plate was stained with iodine vapor, and monoacylglycerol spots were marked. After iodine evaporation, the marked areas were scraped, transferred to a 2 mL glass tube, and mixed with 500 µL ether. The mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was dried under nitrogen, resuspended in 500 µL hexane and 0.2 mL of 1% NaOH–methanol, vortexed, and centrifuged. The final supernatant was filtered, transferred to a gas chromatography vial, and analyzed by gas chromatography.

4.7. Oil Content Determination

The dried seeds, after drying at 37 °C for 24 h, were ground into powder in a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Methyl heptadecanoate (C17:0, CAS: 1731-92-6) was added as an internal standard, and the subsequent protocol for FAMEs preparation and quantification was followed, as described earlier.

4.8. Yield Measurement

Following the field-planting survey guidelines for rapeseed varieties, the agronomic traits of transgenic and wild-type plants were evaluated. These included plant height, branch number, total silique count, yield per plant, seed count per plant, seeds per silique, and thousand-seed weight.

4.9. Western Blot

Total cellular protein was extracted using an RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF, and then protein concentration was measured and quantified using the BCA method. After boiling and denaturing, the equivalent protein samples were separated by electrophoresis on the self-made 10% SDS-PAGE gel. After electrophoresis, the protein was wet-transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After sealing the membrane in 5% skim milk powder at room temperature for 1 h, it was incubated overnight with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C. The next day, the membrane was washed with TBST and incubated with HRP-labeled goat anti-mouse secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, the membrane was developed using an ECL chemiluminescence reagent and analyzed using the Bio-rad ChemiDocTM XRS + gel imaging system to collect images.

4.10. Statistical Analysis

For pairwise comparisons, a t-test was used. For multiple comparisons, statistical significance was assessed using ANOVA, followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test in SPSS Statistics software (version 26.0). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and different probability levels were calculated. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0).
