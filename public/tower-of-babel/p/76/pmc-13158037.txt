Beyond Familial Hypercholesterolemia: Your Feet can Help Predict Your Coronary Atherosclerosis
Authors: Masa-aki Kawashiri, Hayato Tada
Published: 2026

See article vol. 33: 578-588

Coronary atherosclerosis is characterized by cholesterol accumulation within the arterial wall. The serum cholesterol levels, particularly low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, are closely associated with the presence and severity of coronary artery disease (CAD). Familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is characterized by markedly elevated LDL cholesterol levels and it is therefore strongly associated with premature CAD. Tendon xanthomas (TXs) are characteristic and specific physical findings of FH 1) . The progression of atherosclerosis and TX share similar mechanisms such as inflammation, oxidation, and cholesterol deposition. Circulating LDL particles can accumulate within tendons, particularly in tissues exposed to repeated mechanical stress where they undergo oxidative modification and contribute to TX formation.

The presence of TX is incorporated into several diagnostic criteria for FH 1) . The diagnostic criteria proposed by the Japan Atherosclerosis Society (JAS) uniquely include a quantitative assessment of the Achilles tendon (AT). In the 2022 JAS criteria, the cutoff values at the site of maximal thickening of AT, as measured by radiography, were lowered to ≥ 8.0 mm for men and ≥ 7.5 mm for women from ≥ 9.0 mm for both sex in the previous version to improve diagnostic sensitivity 2) . In addition, ultrasonographic measurements of AT thickness were adopted. This change was based on the higher spatial resolution of ultrasonography, which allows for a clearer discrimination between the AT and surrounding soft tissues and thus enables a more accurate measurement of tendon thickness 3) . Radiographic assessments may overestimate AT thickness owing to the influence of tendon twisting, whereas ultrasonography largely avoids this limitation 4) . Consequently, the ultrasonographic diagnostic thresholds in the 2022 JAS criteria were set at ≥ 6.0 mm for men and ≥ 5.5 mm for women 2) .

Despite these advances, the mechanisms underlying AT thickening in FH patients remain unclear. Notably, AT thickening is observed in individuals with FH, but not in non-FH individuals, even when adult LDL-cholesterol levels are comparable. As most dyslipidemias other than FH manifest in adulthood, one plausible explanation is that individuals with FH are exposed to markedly elevated LDL-cholesterol levels from childhood, thus resulting in a greater cumulative LDL-cholesterol burden over their lifetime. If this is the case, then interactions between LDL cholesterol and connective tissues, including AT, during early life may be critical determinants of xanthoma formation.

Consistent with this observation, approximately 20% of patients with genetically confirmed FH do not exhibit increased AT thickness 5) . Moreover, considerable heterogeneity exists among the patients with FH, with some developing AT thickening, while others do not, despite similar LDL cholesterol levels 6) . Furthermore, the presence of AT xanthomas in FH is associated with a higher incidence of cardiovascular events than in FH without xanthomas 5) .

Fujioka et al. retrospectively examined AT thickness as assessed by both radiography and ultrasonography in non-FH Japanese patients with acute coronary syndrome and evaluated its association with the severity of CAD, defined as either single- or multi-vessel disease 7) . AT thickness was significantly greater in patients with multi-vessel disease and it showed a significant positive correlation with the SYNTAX score.

As discussed above, the observation that AT thickness in FH correlates with the severity and prognosis of CAD is intriguing; however, it is also, to some extent, biologically plausible and clinically anticipated. In this context, the study by Fujioka et al. is of particular interest in that it suggests a potential association between AT thickness, especially when assessed by ultrasonography, and the severity of CAD, even in non-FH individuals. Notably, the lipoprotein(a) levels and a higher body mass index, which may reflect increased mechanical stress on the AT, were correlated with AT thickness in non-FH patients and were identified as its determinants. Nevertheless, if one accepts the hypothesis that AT xanthoma formation is determined by elevated LDL-cholesterol levels during childhood and their cumulative burden, then these findings may alternatively reflect, at least in part, exposure to higher LDL-cholesterol levels earlier in life among individuals without FH, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation.

This study is associated with several limitations. Most importantly, it was conducted in patients with acute coronary syndrome, a condition in which LDL cholesterol levels decrease during the acute phase. Indeed, LDL cholesterol was not identified as a determinant of AT thickness in non-FH patients. Therefore, it is premature to consider AT thickness in individuals without FH as a surrogate marker of CAD risk. However, if similar findings are reproduced in larger prospective studies involving the general population, then AT thickness may eventually be considered as a potential surrogate marker for predicting CAD.
