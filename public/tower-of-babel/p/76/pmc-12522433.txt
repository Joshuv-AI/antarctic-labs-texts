Comparison between vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) and conventional dressing for fournier’s gangrene: a single-centre retrospective study
Authors: Xinjian Li, Yuru Zhang, Fang Xiong, Jiayan Li, Shunli Qi, Qingbo Wang, Shijia Mu, Di Cao, Shuo Niu, Wei Liu, Liancheng Liu, Zhongtao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFournier’s gangrene (FG) is a life-threatening infection associated with high mortality and often necessitates urgent surgical intervention. Vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) has emerged as a promising adjunct to surgical debridement; however, its impact on antibiotic stewardship remains inadequately studied. This study compares the efficacy of VSD and conventional dressings in the management of FG.MethodsIn this single-center retrospective study, 104 patients with Fournier’s gangrene (FG) treated between January 2022 and December 2023 were analyzed. Patients were categorized into VSD (n = 42) and conventional dressing (n = 62) groups following surgical debridement. Data included demographics, comorbidities, infection sites, Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) scores, antibiotic duration, and clinical outcomes. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to mitigate confounding biases.ResultsBefore IPTW adjustment, the VSD group exhibited longer durations in key clinical outcomes compared to the control group, including time to leukocyte normalization (+ 1.6 days), antibiotic use (+ 2.0 days), and hospital stay (+ 2.0 days). After IPTW adjustment, all three indicators showed improvement in favor of the VSD group. Generalized weighted regression further confirmed that VSD was associated with significant clinical benefits: shortened time to leukocyte normalization (mean difference: -1.8 days; 90% CI: -3.5 to -0.1), reduced duration of antibiotic therapy (-2.0 days; 90% CI: -3.7 to -0.4), and decreased length of hospitalization (-3.0 days; 90% CI:-5.7 to -0.4).Post-IPTW, VSD emerged as a protective factor, with all standardized mean differences (SMDs) remaining below 0.2, indicating acceptable covariate balance.ConclusionsVSD may improve clinical outcomes in patients with Fournier’s gangrene by accelerating recovery and reducing antibiotic use and length of hospitalization. These benefits appear more pronounced in patients with high LRINEC scores or extensive infections. Nevertheless, individualized treatment strategies should consider systemic disease severity, such as the Fournier’s Gangrene Severity Index (FGSI).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03242-8.Keywords: Fournier's gangrene (FG), Vacuum sealing drainage (VSD), Laboratory risk indicator for necrotizing fasciitis (LRINEC) scoring system; inverse probability of treatment weight (IPTW)

Introduction

Fournier’s gangrene (FG) is a rare but potentially fatal soft tissue infection characterized by rapidly progressive necrotizing fasciitis of the perineal, genital, and perianal regions. If not promptly managed, it can lead to severe sepsis, multi-organ failure, and substantial morbidity. Despite often presenting with a subtle onset, FG progresses rapidly and is associated with high mortality rates, ranging from 20% to 40% [1, 2].

Early and aggressive surgical debridement remains the cornerstone of treatment for FG, aiming to eliminate necrotic tissue and achieve source control.Given the rapid disease progression and frequent need for repeated debridements, patients often present with large and complex wounds, which pose substantial challenges for postoperative wound management and reconstruction [3].VSD as gained increasing popularity in this context. Several studies have shown that, compared to conventional dressing methods, VSD reduces the frequency of dressing changes, alleviates patient discomfort, and decreases nursing workload [4–6]. Although the initial cost of VSD is higher, its use has been associated with shorter hospital stays and improved wound healing, supporting its potential long-term cost-effectiveness [7–10].However, despite its clinical advantages, no comparative studies have evaluated the effects of VSD versus conventional dressings when combined with systemic antibiotic therapy after surgical debridement in patients with FG.

This retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of VSD compared with conventional dressings in the management of FG.The primary outcomes included time to leukocyte normalization, duration of antibiotic therapy, and length of hospital stay. We hypothesized that VSD, when applied following thorough surgical debridement, would enhance infection control and reduce the need for prolonged antibiotic use. To minimize confounding in this observational setting, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed as a robust statistical adjustment method.

Methods

This was a retrospective cohort study in which inverse probability of treatment weighting was used. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). This study was approved by Beijing Anorectal Hospital (Beijing Erlong Lu Hospital) (approval number: 2024ELLHA-011-01). The requirement for informed patient consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. However, each patient’s privacy was strictly protected.

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 109 adult patients who underwent surgical debridement and drainage for Fournier’s gangrene at our institution between January 2022 and December 2023. Pediatric patients were excluded, as our hospital does not provide care for this population. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to surgical intervention.Inclusion criteria were a confirmed diagnosis of Fournier’s gangrene, established through a combination of medical history, physical examination, clinical manifestations, and imaging findings.Patients with necrotizing fasciitis originating from other anatomical regions—such as the oral and maxillofacial areas or extremities—were excluded.

All patients underwent imaging to support disease evaluation and guide surgical decision-making.Endorectal ultrasonography and pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were routinely employed to delineate the extent of infection and to detect the presence of gas in soft tissues. For hemodynamically unstable patients, computed tomography (CT) was preferred owing to its rapid acquisition and diagnostic efficiency [11].Clinical manifestations typically included erythema and swelling of the perianal, perineal, or genital regions. In advanced cases, additional features such as blistering, crepitus, skin ulceration, gangrenous changes, and foul-smelling purulent discharge were observed.

Treatment

Upon hospital admission, all patients received fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy for infection control, and preoperative preparation. Emergency surgical debridement and drainage were performed on the same day, typically within 6 h of admission. Intrathecal anaesthesia was the preferred method; however, general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation was administered to patients with contraindications to spinal anaesthesia or those requiring colostomy.

The timing of vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) varied according to the individual patient’s wound condition and systemic status. In some cases, VSD was applied during the initial emergency debridement, typically performed within 6 h of admission, while in others, it was implemented after additional debridement and infection control. The surgical team determined the timing of VSD placement based on each patient’s clinical presentation and response to initial treatment [12].Daily wound dressing changes were performed until VSD was initiated or discontinued (Fig. 1).

Control group (N0 = 62)

The extent of debridement was determined according to the lesion’s severity and anatomical location. Necrotic tissue was excised progressively, starting from the most severely affected area and extending outward until viable soft tissue with active bleeding was encountered. The margins and depth of the wound were carefully evaluated to ensure complete removal of all devitalized tissue. When the overlying skin remained intact and free from infection or necrosis, incision decompression and drainage were performed instead. Subcutaneous necrotic tissue was removed, drainage channels were established between incisions, and any deep cavities involved in the infection were thoroughly drained.

Intraoperative debridement continued until only healthy tissue remained. The wound was irrigated alternately with hydrogen peroxide and saline, followed by coverage with iodophor-soaked gauze.Rubber drainage catheters were placed as necessary, based on intraoperative findings.Postoperative wound care included daily dressing changes using hydrogen peroxide and iodophor-impregnated gauze, with dressing type and frequency adjusted according to the volume of wound exudate.Repeat debridement was performed in the operating room if necrotic tissue reappeared or if the lesion showed progression.

In the surgical unit, standard wound care included cleansing the affected area with iodine solution and applying sterile cotton dressings, which were changed at least once every 24 h. Intraoperatively, pus and tissue samples were collected for microbiological culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Treatment group (N1 = 42)

Following debridement, the wound was alternately irrigated with hydrogen peroxide and saline to reduce active bleeding. VSD sponges were trimmed to match the dimensions and depth of each incision and were placed individually to ensure optimal drainage and coverage. The wound was then sealed with a semipermeable film dressing. Postoperatively, continuous negative pressure therapy was applied at −200 to −400 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa). Saline was continuously infused through the side ports of the drainage catheter to prevent blockage, and hydrogen peroxide was intermittently administered based on clinical assessment.

Achieving an effective seal in the perineal region, particularly near the anus, is a recognized technical challenge in VSD application. To enhance sealing and maintain continuous drainage, a modified approach was utilized, embedding a size F30 Foley catheter within the VSD foam and routing it through the anal canal. This setup facilitated internal decompression, improved dressing stability, and minimized perianal leakage.In cases of catheter obstruction or displacement compromising the seal, the VSD dressing was promptly replaced. VSD therapy was discontinued if leakage occurred due to fecal contamination. Minor air leaks were generally well tolerated and did not significantly compromise VSD efficacy. Dressings were routinely changed every three to five days according to standard protocol.

Once the diagnosis of FG was confirmed, empirical antibiotic therapy was initiated without delay.Clinically stable patients received a combination of ceftazidime and ornidazole, while unstable patients were administered broad-spectrum coverage with imipenem/cilastatin. Intraoperative specimens were routinely collected during index surgery for microbiological culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.Antibiotic regimens were subsequently de-escalated or adjusted based on culture results, clinical response, and inflammatory markers such as white blood cell count and C-reactive protein levels.The duration of antibiotic therapy was individualized according to institutional antimicrobial stewardship protocols and was guided by each patient’s clinical course, infection resolution, and laboratory indicators of systemic inflammation.

Supportive therapies-including blood transfusion, glycemic control, nutritional supplementation, and fluid-electrolyte homeostasis-were administered when clinically indicated. Intravenous crystalloids and colloids were used for hemodynamic stabilization as needed.

Data collection and outcomes

Clinical and operative data were retrospectively collected from institutional medical records. Postoperative histopathological examinations were conducted at our center. Collected variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex), lifestyle factors (smoking and alcohol history), comorbidities, predisposing conditions, body mass index (BMI), infection site, and clinical outcomes such as the time to white blood cell (WBC) normalization in the absence of clinical recurrence, duration of antibiotic use, postoperative complications, length of hospital stay (LOS), total medical cost, transfusion requirements, and in-hospital mortality.Laboratory parameters at presentation-including WBC count, C-reactive protein (CRP), hemoglobin, serum sodium, creatinine, and blood glucose-were documented. The Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) score was calculated based on these six variables [13]. Operative data, including VSD use, number of operations involving dressing changes or VSD, number of debridement procedures, time to first surgical debridement, and number of patients who underwent colostomy, were manually extracted from operative reports as distinct variables.The primary outcomes were the time to white blood cell (WBC) normalization in the absence of clinical recurrence and the total duration of antibiotic therapy.Antibiotic therapy was discontinued once the following criteria were met: WBC ≤ 10.0*10⁹/L, normothermia maintained within 36.2–37.2 ℃, and no evidence of wound erythema, swelling, or progressive infection.

The Fournier’s Gangrene Severity Index (FGSI) was used as a prognostic tool to stratify patients based on clinical outcomes. Survival analysis and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis were performed to determine the optimal FGSI cutoff value, which was identified as 4.5. Based on this threshold, patients were classified into low-risk (FGSI ≤ 4.5) and high-risk (FGSI > 4.5) groups.To minimize potential confounding, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied to adjust for baseline differences between the groups. FGSI demonstrated good discriminatory capacity for outcome prediction, supporting its clinical utility in risk stratification and guiding treatment decisions. The corresponding survival curves and ROC analysis are shown in Fig. 2.

The secondary outcomes included surgical parameters such as the number of debridements, length of hospital stay (LOS), total medical costs, postoperative complications, and mortality. Mortality was defined as either in-hospital death or death within 30 or 90 days of admission.All patients were followed in the outpatient clinic for at least three months after discharge to monitor for delayed complications.

Statistical analysis

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed to reduce potential confounding between the treatment groups, a method that has been widely validated in retrospective clinical research [14–18]. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression, with the treatment group as the dependent variable and multiple baseline covariates as independent variables.IPTW and balance assessments were performed using R software. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated for each covariate to evaluate the adequacy of covariate balance between groups. Covariates were considered well balanced when the post-weighting SMD was ≤ 0.2.When all covariates met this criterion, the treatment groups were regarded as comparable for outcome analysis.

While an SMD < 0.1 is generally considered indicative of excellent covariate balance [19], a threshold of < 0.2 is commonly accepted in applied clinical research, particularly in studies with limited sample sizes [20, 21].Furthermore, Stuart (2010) suggested that standardized mean differences exceeding 0.25 typically reflect substantial imbalance, thereby supporting < 0.25 as a practical upper limit for acceptable covariate balance. Similarly, Rubin (2001) emphasized that for regression adjustment to yield valid inferences, SMDs should ideally remain below 0.25.

Furthermore, IPTW was applied to adjust for residual differences in baseline characteristics and mitigate potential selection bias between the treatment groups. Propensity scores were calculated based on 18 covariates, including sex, age, smoking and alcohol use history, cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, predisposing conditions such as hypertension, body mass index (BMI), infection site, number of debridements, time to white blood cell (WBC) normalization without recurrence, duration of antibiotic therapy, length of hospital stay (LOS), use of blood products, receipt of colostomy, LRINEC score, mortality, FGSI score, and unusual events(e.g., paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, toxic shock, intestinal fistula).Patients were weighted according to their propensity scores to achieve covariate balance between groups. Additionally, multivariable logistic regression was performed to assess factors associated with the LRINEC score and infection site. A two-tailed P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software.

Results

In this study, 5 patients were excluded due to treatment discontinuation, loss to follow-up, incomplete clinical records, or lack of postoperative pathological confirmation of FG. A total of 104 patients who underwent surgical debridement and drainage for confirmed FG at our institution between January 2022 and December 2023 were included in the final analysis.

Baseline measures

A total of 104 patients were included in this study, with 62 assigned to the control group (N0) and 42 to the treatment group (N1). The mean age was 51 years, and 87 patients (84%) were male, reflecting the higher prevalence of FG in men.The most frequently observed comorbidities were diabetes mellitus in 41 patients (39%), obesity in 37 (36%), hypertension in 37 (36%), smoking in 34 (33%), alcohol abuse in 15 (14%), heart disease in 14 (13%), stroke in 12 (11%), and a history of or ongoing treatment for malignancy in 16 (15%). Diabetes mellitus was the most common comorbidity in this cohort.Overall, the demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population were generally consistent with previously reported epidemiological patterns of FG.

Interpretation of the main results after inverse probability weighting

Before IPTW adjustment, patients who underwent VSD had a 2-day longer LOS, approximately 2-day longer TT-WBCN-NSR, and approximately 3-day longer DAB than those who did not receive VSD. After IPTW adjustment, VSD-treated patients showed shorter LOS, reduced TT-WBCN-NSR, and shorter DAB compared to non-VSD patients.

After inverse probability weighting, the effects between variables were analysed via multivariate analysis

To further evaluate the effect of VSD on key outcome variables, we compared multivariate regression results before and after IPTW.

To identify factors associated with the time to white blood cell count normalization without signs of recurrence (TT-WBCN-NSR), we included VSD, LRINEC classification, number of debridements, infection location (LOCATION), FGSI score, and the interaction term between LRINEC and LOCATION as covariates.Both unweighted and IPTW-adjusted regression analyses were conducted using the svyglm function in R.

Before weighting, VSD showed no significant association with TT-WBCN-NSR. However, after IPTW adjustment, VSD was significantly associated with a reduction of approximately 1.8 days (p < 0.05), indicating a potential benefit following the balancing of baseline characteristics.

Although LRINEC classification alone was not statistically significant, its interaction with infection extending beyond the pelvis (LOCATION1) was associated with a significant prolongation of TT-WBCN-NSR in both moderate and high-risk groups (LRINEC1×LOCATION1: +7.6 days; LRINEC2×LOCATION1: +4.7 days; both p < 0.001), indicating a synergistic effect between systemic disease severity and infection extent. Additionally, higher FGSI scores were independently associated with delayed recovery (p < 0.05). In contrast, among low-risk patients, LOCATION1 was linked to a modest reduction in recovery time (− 0.91 days, p < 0.1); however, this trend reversed in higher-risk groups due to interaction effects.

To avoid redundancy and conserve space, detailed regression tables for LOS and DAB before and after IPTW adjustment are not presented, as both analyses employed the same covariates as those used for TT-WBCN-NSR. Full tables are available from the authors upon request.

Regarding factors influencing LOS, VSD was not significantly associated with hospital stay duration before IPTW adjustment. However, after weighting, VSD emerged as a significant favorable factor: patients receiving VSD had a 3.0-day shorter LOS compared to those who did not, after adjusting for covariates.This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Similarly, before IPTW adjustment, VSD showed a non-significant trend toward reducing DAB. After weighting, VSD was significantly associated with a 2.0-day reduction in DAB, suggesting a potential role in accelerating infection resolution.This association was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

In both models, a positive interaction between LRINEC classification and infection location (LOCATION1,indicating infection extended beyond the pelvic cavity) was observed. This suggests that deeper anatomical involvement in patients with moderate-to-high LRINEC scores substantially prolongs treatment duration, whether measured by LOS or DAB. These findings underscore the increased complexity and slower response to treatment in severe, deep-seated infections.

Regarding factors influencing VSD use, logistic regression analysis was performed. Before IPTW adjustment, moderate LRINEC risk (LRINEC1) was negatively associated with VSD use (β = −0.174, OR = 0.84, p = 0.098), while the number of debridements was a significant positive predictor (β = 0.318, OR = 1.37, p < 0.001).

After IPTW adjustment, LRINEC was no longer significant. Instead, infection extending beyond the pelvic cavity (LOCATION1; β = 0.306, OR = 1.36, p < 0.001) and the number of debridements (β = 0.345, OR = 1.41, p < 0.001) remained independently associated with VSD use. A negative interaction was observed between high LRINEC risk and LOCATION1 (β = −0.461, OR ≈ 0.63, p = 0.087), indicating that patients with both high systemic severity and deep-seated infections were less likely to receive VSD.This may reflect clinicians’ cautious treatment decisions when weighing surgical risks and postoperative recovery potential in these more severe cases.

Discussion

After adjusting for baseline imbalances using IPTW, patients treated with VSD experienced significantly improved clinical outcomes, including shorter length of hospital stay (LOS), faster the time to white blood cell count normalization in the absence of clinical of recurrence(TT-WBCN-NSR), and fewer duration of antibiotic use(DAB). These findings suggest that VSD contributes to more effective infection control and accelerated recovery. Notably, such therapeutic benefits may be underestimated in unadjusted models due to confounding by baseline infection severity.

A significant interaction was observed between systemic infection severity, as indicated by the LRINEC score, and the anatomical extent of infection (LOCATION). Among patients with low LRINEC scores, infection extending beyond the pelvic cavity (LOCATION1) was unexpectedly associated with a shorter TT-WBCN-NSR. In contrast, in patients with moderate (LRINEC1) or high (LRINEC2) risk, LOCATION1 significantly prolonged recovery time, suggesting a synergistic delaying effect of systemic severity and anatomical depth on treatment response.

Additionally, the Fournier’s Gangrene Severity Index (FGSI), an indicator of systemic disease burden, was independently associated with prolonged TT-WBCN-NSR. However, it did not significantly influence the decision to use VSD, which appeared to be more closely associated with the extent of local infection and the frequency of debridement.

With respect to factors influencing the use of VSD, both the number of debridements and infection extending beyond the pelvic cavity (LOCATION1) emerged as strong positive predictors after IPTW adjustment.Conversely, patients with both high LRINEC risk and extensive infection (LRINEC2*LOCATION1) were less likely to receive VSD, possibly reflecting clinicians’ concerns regarding surgical risk or limited perceived benefit in systemically unstable patients.

In conclusion, VSD offers clear clinical benefits in selected patients with severe infections. Its implementation should be guided not only by objective indicators-such as systemic infection severity (e.g., LRINEC, FGSI) and anatomical extent (LOCATION)-but also by individualized clinical risk assessments to support more personalized and effective treatment strategies.

Although microbiological and resistance data would have enriched the analysis, we chose not to present them due to the limited reliability of the collected specimens. Many patients had received empirical antibiotics prior to hospital admission, significantly reducing culture positivity. Several samples yielded mixed flora or contaminants, while others were compromised by necrotic tissue or preoperative wound irrigation. Including such data could have misrepresented the true causative pathogens.

While detailed microbiological data were not included due to specimen quality concerns, a pragmatic antibiotic strategy was implemented.Given these limitations, we adopted a standardized empirical antibiotic protocol in accordance with the 2021 WSES-AAST guidelines and adjusted therapy based on clinical indicators and inflammatory markers [11]. This pragmatic approach facilitated timely and effective antimicrobial management in a real-world emergency setting.

Furthermore, we emphasize the importance of early and adequate surgical source control in combination with VSD.This approach not only ensures continuous drainage and promotes wound healing, but also helps reduce systemic infection burden and may shorten the duration of antibiotic therapy.

We acknowledge that normalization of white blood cell (WBC) count can be influenced by several confounding factors, including corticosteroid use, timing of surgical intervention, and individual variability in immune response.Therefore, in both clinical practice and data interpretation, we did not rely solely on WBC count.Instead, we evaluated patients’ overall condition by integrating WBC trends with other inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), along with clinical signs of infection control. This multimodal approach provided a more reliable basis for assessing treatment response and guiding antibiotic de-escalation.

Fournier’s gangrene (FG) is a rapidly progressing and life-threatening infection involving the perineal, genital, or perianal regions. It is typically polymicrobial in origin, involving both aerobic and anaerobic organisms, and arises from urogenital, colorectal, or cutaneous sources. Characterized by vascular thrombosis, tissue ischemia, and soft tissue necrosis, FG frequently results in sepsis, multi-organ failure, and has a reported mortality rate of up to 40% [2, 22].

Previous studies have demonstrated that patient age plays a major role in the outcomes of FG, which is consistent with the findings of the present study [23]. The aging population is associated with an increased incidence and heightened susceptibility to FG. Similar to our results (87:17), males are more frequently affected than females, with a reported male-to-female ratio of approximately 5.3:1 [24]. Factors that impair microcirculation or immunosuppression, such as diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic alcohol use, smoking, obesity, HIV, renal or liver failure, and malignancy, have been identified as risk factors for FG [25]. Among these, DM and obesity are the most prevalent, with 36% to 56% of individuals with FG having DM and 51.5% of affected individuals classified as overweight [26, 27]. In our study, DM was identified as a significant predisposing factor. Moreover, poor glycemic control was associated with more rapid disease progression and increased severity. These findings suggest that prognosis may be worse in patients with poorly controlled DM [28].

Effective management of FG requires prompt diagnosis, timely and thorough surgical intervention-including multiple debridement procedures when necessary-and early, aggressive broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy combined with adequate fluid resuscitation [3, 4, 29].

Nawijn et al. recommended performing surgical intervention for FG within 12 h of diagnosis, with surgery within 6 h considered optimal [30]. The primary objective of the initial procedure is to achieve early source control and prevent further spread of infection-both transfascial and haematogenous-through complete debridement of all necrotic and infected tissue [31, 32]. Sarani et al. reported that for every hour of surgical delay, the infection may spread locally at a rate of approximately one inch per hour, significantly increasing the risk of systemic dissemination [33]. Early surgical intervention not only reduces mortality but also lowers the incidence of septic shock, decreases the need for multiple debridements, and shortens hospital stay [34, 35].Timely removal of devitalized tissue diminishes microbial load, thereby enhancing host immune response and improving the efficacy of broad-spectrum antibiotics [31, 36].

Notably, we found that few patients sought anorectal consultation before the diagnosis of FG, and some patients and even some doctors mistakenly treated perianal swelling and pain as haemorrhoids, highlighting the importance of reducing the incidence of delayed diagnosis. Therefore, we hypothesized that an anorectal specialist could quickly diagnose FG because of their familiarity with the perianal anatomy and extensive experience in comprehensive examination of the perianal region, resulting in better outcomes.

Our center is a national referral center for critically ill patients with anorectal diseases, so patients with suspected FG receive fast green channel treatment and can generally undergo surgery within 6 h after admission. The mortality rate among patients admitted to our centre is approximately 8%, which is lower than previously reported rates. This may be attributed to our hospital’s status as a national anorectal emergency treatment centre, allowing referred patients to undergo pelvic MRI or endorectal ultrasound examinations promptly. Rapid diagnosis enables timely treatment. In this context, Wong et al. introduced the Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC) as a diagnostic tool to assist clinicians in identifying FG [22].

However, due to the rapid progression and extensive tissue involvement characteristic of the disease, multiple surgical debridements are often required. This can result in large and complex wounds, which subsequently pose significant challenges for wound closure and reconstruction.

In terms of wound management, VSD is one of the most commonly applied techniques, although its use in the treatment of FG remains controversial [37]. VSD creates a closed negative-pressure environment, facilitating continuous drainage of wound exudates and necrotic material. Compared to traditional debridement and open drainage, which often require painful dressing changes two or more times daily, VSD significantly reduces the frequency of dressing changes and associated patient discomfort. In addition, VSD has been shown to decrease oedema, improve local perfusion, reduce wound size, and lower bacterial colonization. By maintaining a clean wound environment and promoting granulation tissue formation, VSD contributes to enhanced wound healing and facilitates subsequent reconstructive procedures [3, 38–41].

Numerous studies have highlighted the clinical benefits of VSD. Its efficacy depends on maintaining a stable, airtight environment to deliver continuous negative pressure. However, in the perianal and perineal regions, anatomical complexity, deep and irregular wound morphology, and persistent exudation or contamination often compromise seal integrity. Film dressings may loosen or detach prematurely, making it difficult to sustain an effective vacuum. These challenges underscore the need for experienced surgical teams and careful technical execution when applying VSD in this setting.Our clinical experience has led to the following key insights: [1]All visible necrotic tissue should be meticulously excised during surgery to minimize the risk of postoperative bleeding. This step is critical, as residual necrotic tissue and blood clots can obstruct drainage devices, potentially leading to drainage failure [2]. In cases of skin necrosis near the anal verge, rectal perforation, or the presence of an intestinal fistula, a diverting colostomy is warranted. Postoperatively, patients are maintained on bowel rest and receive short-term parenteral nutrition to reduce the risk of fecal contamination. Additionally, disposable silicone drainage tubes are placed in the anal canal to facilitate fecal diversion and minimize wound contamination and the need for VSD replacement [3]. If the skin near the anal verge is intact, incisions are made away from this area and kept as small as possible. For deep, extensive infections, multiple incisions are created within the affected region, through which appropriately sized VSD sponges are inserted to achieve multilayer, multidirectional drainage. This technique significantly reduces the total wound surface area [4]. In cases of extensive skin necrosis, all devitalized tissue should be thoroughly debrided. During each VSD dressing change, the sponge should be centrally positioned within the wound and secured to the surrounding healthy tissue. This technique facilitates gradual wound contraction through negative pressure, thereby promoting subsequent wound closure and repair [5]. During VSD application, negative pressure is typically initiated at −50 mmHg and gradually increased to −200 mmHg depending on patient tolerance and wound characteristics. In the absence of adverse events such as wound bleeding, the pressure may be further elevated up to −400 mmHg to optimize drainage efficiency [6]. Sealing the perineal region, particularly around the anus, presents a significant technical challenge in VSD application. After evaluating multiple methods, the insertion of a Foley catheter (size F30) into the foam dressing and routing it transanally was found to be the most effective approach. This technique not only enhanced sealing but also enabled controlled gas release and partial fecal diversion during drainage.In our experience, this method effectively addressed leakage-related issues, ensured stable negative pressure delivery, and may have contributed to improved wound healing. Although minor air leakage was occasionally observed, it was generally well tolerated and did not compromise treatment efficacy. In cases of fecal contamination, prompt dressing replacement remained essential to prevent secondary infection.

Currently, the optimal timing for initiating VSD therapy is after complete removal of necrotic tissue, when the infection has entered a controlled, post-acute phase, and anaerobic pathogens are under control—evidenced by negative pus and blood cultures and the absence of malodour at the local infection site. At this stage, granulation tissue formation has typically begun. In our study, 18 patients received VSD during their initial operation and demonstrated favourable outcomes.

The necessity of faecal diversion remains a subject of ongoing debate. Colostomy is generally indicated in cases of anal sphincter injury, faecal incontinence, or persistent wound contamination by faecal matter [11]. Faecal diversion via stoma may facilitate wound healing but is associated with potential complications, including the frequent need for subsequent stoma reversal surgery. We advocate that the decision to perform a stoma should be based on a comprehensive assessment of the patient’s condition and disease progression, rather than implemented routinely for the convenience of VSD application [11].

A more rigorous investigation is warranted to elucidate the potential association between reduced antibiotic duration and the use of VSD. Our study contributes meaningfully to the literature, as few studies have directly compared antibiotic outcomes with and without VSD in the management of FG.Given the rapid progression of FG, a tailored treatment strategy is essential. While our findings suggest a potential role for VSD in promoting wound healing, further research is necessary to clarify its benefits and limitations.The complexity of FG highlights the need for high-quality studies to inform evidence-based clinical practice and ultimately improve patient outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, due to the inherent nature of a retrospective design and its single-centre setting, the findings are less comparable and standardized than those from prospective, multicentre studies, and are subject to potential selection bias.Although we accounted for various demographic and clinical variables and applied IPTW to balance baseline characteristics, potential biases may persist due to the absence of blinding and unmeasured confounding factors that could not be fully adjusted.Moreover, the VSD group exhibited clinical heterogeneity in terms of debridement frequency and the timing of VSD initiation (initial versus subsequent surgeries), which may have introduced residual confounding not fully addressed by IPTW.

Additionally, the small sample size (n = 100) limits the reliability and generalizability of our findings and precluded the use of more advanced statistical approaches, such as stratified models or marginal structural models, which may reduce statistical power and increase the risk of overfitting. Although adjusted analyses yielded consistent results across primary and secondary outcomes, the observational design and limited external validity must be acknowledged. Furthermore, the relatively short follow-up period (3 months) may not adequately capture long-term outcomes, such as wound recurrence or functional recovery, which are essential for a comprehensive assessment of VSD efficacy.Future prospective, multicentre studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up durations are needed to validate these findings and better evaluate long-term outcomes.

Third, patients with unclear outcomes were excluded to ensure accurate comparisons of treatment effectiveness, which may have introduced selection bias. While retrospective studies offer valuable clinical insights, high-quality evidence is best obtained from pragmatic randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with broader inclusion criteria. Therefore, prospective studies are planned to validate our findings. Finally, not all procedures were performed by the same surgeon, and the choice of intervention was influenced by individual surgeon preference. The decision to use VSD was ultimately made by the attending surgeon.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that vacuum sealing drainage (VSD) improves clinical outcomes in patients with Fournier’s gangrene (FG) by accelerating leukocyte recovery and reducing both antibiotic use and hospital stay. The LRINEC score and infection site exerted a synergistic effect on recovery, whereas higher FGSI was associated with delayed normalization. VSD use was more closely associated with the extent of local infection and the requirement for repeated debridement, rather than with systemic infection severity alone. These findings support the implementation of individualized treatment strategies based on infection characteristics and patient-specific risk profiles in clinical practice.
