Neural network topology in children’s deceptive behaviors: The role of cognitive control and reward processing
Authors: Liwen Yu, Meng Dai, Pei Huang, Birit FP Broekman, Michael Meaney, Yap Seng Chong, Marielle Valerie Fortier, Peipei Setoh, Henning Tiemeier, Ai Peng Tan, Xiao Pan Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe neural mechanisms related to children’s deceptive behaviors remain relatively unexplored. This study aims to address this gap by using measures of functional brain network topology, focusing on the cognitive control and reward processing networks that are closely related to children’s deceptive behaviors. The study included 113 6-year-old children from the Growing Up in Singapore Towards Healthy Outcomes (GUSTO) project, a birth cohort study. Children participated in the Dart Game designed to assess their tendencies to cheat and lie. During the game, children were required to throw the ball at a long-distance dartboard without supervision, which provided opportunities to cheat by breaking the rules. After the game, children were questioned about whether they had followed the rule, which provided them with opportunities to lie. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) data were collected from all children at the same age during a different visit. We compared three network topology measures (cognitive control network recruitment, reward processing network recruitment and reward-control network integration) between non-cheaters and cheaters, as well as between non-liars and liars. The results showed that a higher degree of cognitive control network recruitment was associated with a greater likelihood of lying. Moreover, a higher degree of reward-cognitive control network integration was associated with a lower likelihood of cheating and lying. The degree of reward processing network recruitment was not associated with deceptive behaviors. These findings help to elucidate how neural mechanisms of cognitive control and reward processing contribute to deceptive behaviors in young children.Keywords: Deceptive behavior, Lying, Reward processing, Cognitive control, Network recruitment, Network integration

Introduction

Deceptive behavior is a natural part of human development, emerging in early childhood. Children begin to exhibit deceptive behaviors, such as cheating to win a prize or lying to hide their misdeeds (Evans and Lee, 2013). On one hand, deceptive behaviors can be seen as a significant cognitive milestone (Talwar and Crossman, 2011); on the other hand, excessive deceptive behaviors are discouraged by society due to their potential negative impact on social trust and relationships. There is significant interest in understanding the neural correlates of deception, particularly the roles of cognitive control and reward systems (Abe and Greene, 2014, Ding et al., 2013, Greene and Paxton, 2009, Liang et al., 2021, Pornpattananangkul et al., 2018, Tang et al., 2018; for meta-analyses, see Christ et al., 2009; Lisofsky et al., 2014; Sai et al., 2021a). However, most of these studies are centered on adults, leaving the neural mechanisms underlying young children’s deceptive behaviors relatively understudied. The current study addresses this gap by examining the neural correlates of deception in young children using a network-based approach. Specifically, we investigated the network topology within and between two key functional networks: cognitive control network and reward processing network, aiming to better understand the task-independent neural traits linked to children’s deceptive behaviors.

Deception requires inhibitions of truthful responses and generation of false statements (Evans and Lee, 2013), thus it is likely that the cognitive control network plays an important role in deception. Behavioral studies have provided rich evidence that children’s deceptive behavior is positively related to their cognitive control abilities (Ding et al., 2014; Evans and Lee, 2013; Fu et al., 2018; Sai et al., 2021b): children with better cognitive control abilities are more likely to be equipped with the ability to deceive.

Building on these behavioral findings, neuroimaging research has begun to explore the role of the cognitive control network in deception, although the results remain inconsistent. The cognitive control network encompasses brain regions engaged in tasks requiring working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility, such as the prefrontal cortex and the posterior parietal cortex (Niendam et al., 2012). Only a few neuroimaging studies, all using task-related designs, have explored the role of cognitive control in children’s deceptive behavior (Dai et al., 2024, Ding et al., 2017, Thijssen et al., 2017, Yokota et al., 2013), which have yielded mixed results. Dai et al. (2024) found that deception elicited greater activation than truth-telling in the frontopolar cortex and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex among preschool children. On the other hand, some other studies failed to observe significant differences in these brain regions between deception and truth-telling behavior (Yokota et al., 2013) or reported contradictory results (Thijssen et al., 2017). Notably, Thijssen et al. (2017) found that only high-risk deceivers—those who consistently deceive even when there is a risk of deception being detected—showed decreased activation in the right frontal pole during low-risk conditions, compared to both honest children and low-risk deceivers. These studies focused on specific brain regions rather than examining brain networks. Only one study by Ding et al. (2017) used a task-related network analysis and revealed that the cognitive control network was involved in children’s deceptive behaviors. Specifically, they found that children’s spontaneous deception in a guessing game led to a reduction in fronto-parietal network efficiency compared to truth-telling, indicating that deceptive behaviors place greater demands on children’s cognitive control.

The inconsistency of the findings probably stems from the fact that all studies used task-related designs, with brain activity being sensitive to variations in experimental settings. For instance, Thijssen et al. (2017) employed a guessing game which was modified from Greene and Paxton (2009), where children did not need to show their initial prediction before reporting its accuracy, potentially reducing the cognitive control load which was needed to suppress the previous prediction. In the deception task used by Yokota et al. (2013), children were instructed to deceive an “evil” character and tell the truth to a “nice” character through interactive social games, whereas other studies (Dai et al., 2024, Ding et al., 2017) focus on self-benefiting deception in individual decision-making tasks. Thus, the demands for cognitive control and the motivation for lying vary across these different tasks.

As children grow older, their deceptive behavior depends not only on their ability to deceive others but also on their motivation to do so (Talwar and Crossman, 2011). Thus, an additional critical brain network of interest may be the reward processing network, which includes brain regions that are involved in processing reward stimuli, such as the anterior cingulate cortex (Adrián-Ventura et al., 2019), the orbitofrontal cortex (Angelides et al., 2017), and the nucleus accumbens (Jia et al., 2016). Potential rewards, such as tangible prizes (Kotaman, 2017, Markiewicz and Gawryluk, 2020) and social affirmation (Ding et al., 2023, Zhao et al., 2018), can serve as motivational factors underlying children’s deceptive behavior. However, the evidence linking deception to the reward brain network comes primarily from studies on adults (Abe and Greene, 2014, Ding et al., 2013, Liang et al., 2021, Pornpattananangkul et al., 2018, Tang et al., 2018), which have shown that sensitivity of the reward network in response to reward stimuli can positively predict deceptive behaviors. For instance, Abe and Greene (2014) used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and revealed that adult participants who exhibited stronger activation in the nucleus accumbens in response to anticipated rewards were more likely to engage in deceptive behaviors in a coin-flip prediction task. Similarly, Liang et al. (2021) used the functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to identify individual reward-related brain areas through a localizer task and discovered that adult participants with higher sensitivity in their reward network during decision-making were more inclined to engage in deception. Additionally, activation of the reward network was observed when participants chose to be deceptive in a guessing game.

The cognitive control and reward networks are not isolated; rather, they frequently share and integrate information within the reward-related circuitry (Liu et al., 2011). While more activation of the cognitive control network alone may facilitate the complex mental operations required for deception, better integration of the cognitive control and reward networks might facilitate the processing of reward-related information (Luna et al., 2015). For instance, Abe and Greene (2014) found that adult participants with stronger nucleus accumbens responses to anticipated rewards exhibited increased bilateral activity in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex when refraining from deceptive behaviors. This finding suggests that the cognitive control network may play a key role in regulating the reward processing network, particularly when individuals need to inhibit impulses for the immediate benefits of deception. Further insights into this regulatory mechanism come from studies on the suppression of reward drives (Delgado et al., 2008, Diekhof and Gruber, 2010, Staudinger et al., 2011). For example, Diekhof and Gruber (2010) demonstrated that higher neural activity in the anteroventral prefrontal cortex was linked to lower activity in the nucleus accumbens when pursuing long-term goals at the expense of immediate rewards, indicating how top-down modulation from the cognitive control network can influence reward encoding. Moreover, theoretical models of neural network development suggest that reward-driven socioemotional systems mature more rapidly than cognitive control systems in young children (Shulman et al., 2016). This developmental imbalance often makes younger children more susceptible to immediate rewards (Steinbeis et al., 2016). However, as children grow, the maturation of the cognitive control network allows for more effective regulation of the reward system, thus facilitating honest behavior (Kryza-Lacombe et al., 2021). Given the critical role of the cognitive control network in regulating the reward network (Abe and Greene, 2014, Delgado et al., 2008, Diekhof and Gruber, 2010, Garavan and Weierstall, 2012, Staudinger et al., 2011), the integration of cognitive control and reward networks may be associated with children’s choices to refrain from deceptive behaviors.

To address the research gap in neural network correlates of children’s deceptive behaviors, this study examined two indices of network topology: network recruitment and network integration. Network recruitment refers to communication within a single brain network, indicating the degree of activation and coordination among different regions within the network. Networks with high recruitment suggest strong within-network connectivity and are better able to function independently of other networks (Baum et al., 2017, Cao et al., 2017, Fair et al., 2007). In contrast, network integration measures communication between different brain networks, reflecting the strength of their interactions in sharing and processing information. Networks with high integration suggest strong between-network connectivity and are more interdependent on other networks for communication (Fair et al., 2007, Marek et al., 2015).

To examine the network topology related to children’s deceptive behaviors, this study utilized resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI). Previous research on deception has primarily relied on task-based fMRI, which captures brain responses to specific stimuli or tasks. However, the results from such studies are often inconsistent, likely due to variations in task design and external stimuli. In contrast, rs-fMRI captures intrinsic brain activity that is not induced by an external stimulus (Muetzel et al., 2016). It is a reliable tool for capturing unique aspects of brain functional organization (Tang et al., 2018) and has been proposed as a neural marker to identify individuals within large populations (Finn et al., 2015). Thus, rs-fMRI is advantageous for examining and predicting individual differences in socio-emotional development (Huang et al., 2024). By examining stable patterns of brain network topology, rs-fMRI can help explain inter-individual variability in neural traits underlying deceptive behaviors among children.

The current study

This study aims to examine the associations between measures of brain network topology and children’s deceptive behaviors, including cheating and lying in 6-year-old children. While both behaviors involve deceptive intent and actions, cheating is about gaining an unfair advantage through covert rule-breaking, whereas lying involves intentionally providing false information (Pascual-Ezama et al., 2020). We focused on 6-year-old children, as this developmental stage is marked by rapid advancements in cognitive abilities (Lewis et al., 2017) and the emergence of more nuanced moral judgment and moral emotions (Gautam et al., 2023, Nunner-Winkler and Sodian, 1988). This period is particularly critical because some children’s deceptive behaviors begin to shift from typical developmental patterns to potentially problematic ones (Talwar and Crossman, 2011). Studying this age group could offer a valuable opportunity to investigate how neural mechanisms associated with cognitive and motivational factors shape children’s deceptive behaviors.

In the current study, children engaged in the Dart Game where they had opportunities to cheat and subsequently lied about their actions. Additionally, rs-fMRI data were collected from all included participants at age 6, from which measures of network recruitment and integration were derived: reward network recruitment, cognitive control network recruitment, and reward-control network integration. We examined the relation between these network measures and cheating tendency, as well as lying tendency, separately. The hypotheses were formulated as follows:

Firstly, considering the critical role of cognitive control in children’s deception based on behavioral studies (Sai et al., 2021b), cognitive control network recruitment may be positively associated with both cheating tendency and lying tendency in children. Secondly, given the involvement of reward processing in children’s deceptive behaviors (Kotaman, 2017, Markiewicz and Gawryluk, 2020), reward network recruitment may be positively associated with both cheating tendency and lying tendency in children. Thirdly, based on the assumption that the cognitive control network would regulate and suppress excessive or inappropriate reward-seeking behaviors, thereby promoting more honest choices (Abe and Greene, 2014), we hypothesized that the reward-control network integration may be negatively associated with both cheating tendency and lying tendency in children. It should be noted that, according to our prediction, we cautiously propose that the cognitive control network may serve different functions depending on whether it operates independently (as indexed by cognitive control network recruitment) or interdependently with the reward network (as indexed by reward-control network integration). Without integration, the functional connectivity within cognitive control network can provide the cognitive resources necessary for deception. If integrated with the reward-control network, the cognitive control network may modulate the reward processing associated with deception, which could reduce the occurrence of children’s deceptive behavior.

Method

Participants

Data was derived from the birth cohort of the Growing Up in Singapore Towards Healthy Outcomes (GUSTO) project (Soh et al., 2014). The original project recruited pregnant women aged 18 years or older from the National University Hospital (NUH) and KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH) in their first trimester of pregnancy. Offspring underwent periodic testing from 11 to 12 weeks of gestation to the age of 14.5 years, and this testing is still ongoing. The GUSTO study was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board (NHG DSRB), the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (CIRB) and the Institutional Review Board of the National University of Singapore (NUS-IRB-2021–881, NUS-IRB-2022–432). The current study (data analysis) was approved by NUS-IRB-2023–29. Prior to participation, parents or legal guardians gave informed consent.

Within two months of their sixth birthday, 557 children participated in the Dart Game at age six. Seventy-eight children were excluded due to unwillingness to complete the task or procedural and technical errors during the game. As a result, valid data on deceptive behaviors were available for 479 children. Rs-fMRI data were collected for 113 of these children on separate days, either before or after their participation in the Dart Game (age in months at Dart Game: M = 71.28, SD = 0.60, Range = 70.08–72.00; time interval in years between rs-fMRI and Dart Game: M = 1.20, SD = 1.44, Range = −1.44–8.52). Among the 479 children, those with and without rs-fMRI data did not significantly differ in deceptive behaviors, age at the Dart Game or gender. Additionally, logistic regression analyses revealed that neither age at the time of Dart Game nor age at the time of fMRI scan was associated with children’s cheating or lying behaviors (For details, see Supplemental Materials). An additional 64 children were excluded due to excessive head motion during rs-fMRI acquisition. The final sample consisted of 113 children, including 52 boys and 61 girls. All participants resided in Singapore and came from diverse ethnic backgrounds (43 % Chinese, 38 % Malay, 19 % Indian). Parents presented with diverse education levels (father: 22 % secondary and below, 50 % post-secondary, 28 % Bachelor and above; mother: 35 % secondary and below, 38 % post-secondary, 27 % Bachelor and above), and families represented various levels of monthly household incomes (16 % below $2000, 64 % between $2000 and $5999, 21 % above $6000).

Measurements

Deceptive behaviors

Children’s deceptive behaviors, including cheating and lying, were assessed using the Dart Game (Kochanska et al., 1996). In this game, each child threw three balls from a designated line towards a dartboard positioned on the floor across the room.

Children were first informed that they could win a prize by successfully landing one or more balls on the dartboard. The experimenter then explained two specific rules: throwing each ball only once and refraining from crossing the line. To ensure the child understood how to throw the ball, the experimenter allowed them to practice until achieving one successful trial. After the practice rounds, the experimenter confirmed the child’s understanding by asking them to repeat the rules and clarified any uncertainties.

For the test trials, the experimenter positioned three balls behind the child, while placing the dartboard as far away as possible from the child and the starting line. After reminding the child of the opportunity to win a prize, the experimenter left the room, allowing the child three minutes to play the game independently. The entire session was recorded for subsequent analysis. Upon completion of the game, the experimenter returned and asked the child whether they had followed the rules. For children who had cheated, this question provided an opportunity for them to potentially engage in lying to conceal their previous transgression.

Breaking either of the two rules during the game was considered cheating, and the child’s cheating tendency was coded as 1. Conversely, if the child adhered to both rules throughout the game, their cheating tendency was coded as 0. If a child cheated and then falsely claimed to have followed the rules when questioned afterward, their lying tendency was coded as 1. On the other hand, if the child confessed to cheating when questioned, their lying tendency was coded as 0.

To prevent children’s frustration from failure, regardless of the game’s outcome, the experimenter moved the dartboard closer to the line and provided the children with additional opportunities to throw the ball until they successfully struck the dartboard and subsequently awarded the children their prize.

Three coders coded the task, with videos randomly divided into four blocks (Blocks 1–4). Each block included one reliability sub-block (27–28 videos, coded by all three coders) and three final sub-blocks (40–41 videos each, except Block 4, which had 27–28 videos). Two coders coded each final sub-block. In total, 20 % of the videos were used for reliability coding, and 80 % for final coding. Conflicts were resolved by re-watching the videos and adjusting the coding. Fleiss’ kappa was used to assess inter-rater reliability, with average scores of 0.99 for cheating tendency and 0.89 for lying tendency.

MRI data acquisition

Children underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain utilizing a 3-Tesla MRI scanner (Magnetom Skyra, Siemens, Germany). 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE images and 5 mins of rs-fMRI scans were acquired. For 3D T1-weighted MPRAGE, the following parameter was used: Field of view = 192 mm x 192 mm, Voxel size = 1 mm isotropic, TR = 2000 ms, TE = 2.08 ms, TI = 877 ms, GRAPPA parallel imaging acceleration factor = 2. The rs-fMRI data was acquired using a 2D gradient-echo planar imaging sequence (GE-EPI) with the following parameters: 5.32 mins (120 timepoints), TR = 2660 ms, TE = 27 ms, Flip angle = 90 degrees, Voxel size = 3 mm isotropic, matrix = 64 × 64, 48 axial slices acquired in an interleaved sequence with no gap. In addition, the quality control criterion was defined as a maximum motion greater than 6 mm, a mean motion greater than 0.6 mm, or more than 20 % outlier scans.

Data analysis

Rs-fMRI preprocessing and functional network construction

Preliminary pre-processing of the data was performed using the default MNI pipeline in the CONN toolbox (release 20b) and SPM12 in Matlab R2020a. All scans in each session were co-registered and resampled to the first scan via b-spline interpolation using the SPM12 functional realignment and unwarp procedure. Slice time correction with sinc interpolation using SPM12 was applied to correct for temporal misalignment. Scan volumes with framewise displacement above 0.9 mm or global BOLD signal changes above 5 standard deviations were flagged as potential outliers for subsequent confound regression. Functional and anatomical data were normalized into standard MNI space and segmented into gray matter, white matter and CSF tissue using SPM 12 unified segmentation and normalization procedure with 4th order spline interpolation. Gaussian smoothing of the functional data was carried out with a kernel of 6 mm full width half maximum (FWHM). Denoising was performed on the data in a two-step process. First, ordinary least squares regression was used to remove potential noise confounds – cerebral blood flow, subject motion parameters, identified outliers and constant and first-order linear session effects. Finally, temporal band-pass filtering was performed to remove signals below 0.008 Hz and above 0.09 Hz. Seed masks were defined using regions of interest (ROIs) which were determined a priori through a literature review. Core brain regions involved in reward processing (nucleus accumbens, orbital frontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex) (Jia et al., 2016) and cognitive control (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, lateral posterior parietal cortex) (Niendam et al., 2012) were selected (see Fig. 1). The Mindboggle 101 atlas was used to delineate this set of predetermined ROIs (Klein and Tourville, 2012). Pairwise functional connectivity was obtained by calculating the Pearson’s correlation between the rs-fMRI time courses of the two ROIs. This generated a 10 × 10 functional connectivity matrix per participant that is used for the subsequent network analysis.

Computing network metrics

A tuned Louvain community detection algorithm was used to segment ROIs into community clusters (Blondel et al., 2008). The algorithm was initialized by assigning each ROI to its own community, followed by iterative reassignment of nodes to optimize global modularity (see Supplemental Materials for details). Iterations continued until no further improvement in modularity was observed between consecutive steps, indicating convergence to a local minimum. To account for the nondeterministic nature of the Louvain algorithm and the potential for near-degenerate partitions, the community detection procedure was repeated 100 times (Rubinov and Sporns, 2011). Code for the Louvain algorithm is available at: https://github.com/huangpei90/mri_analysis/tree/main/modularity. Our methodology was adapted from a previous study that implemented a similar approach (Daws et al., 2022). The recruitment coefficient is defined as the probability of a region being assigned to the same community with other regions from the same network, while the integration coefficient is defined as the probability of a region being assigned to the same community as regions from the other network. The study normalized these network measures using a null distribution generated from 10,000 iterations with randomly permuted functional connectivity matrices to account for differences in the number of regions in each network. Three network measures were calculated for each participant and used in subsequent analyses: (1) reward network recruitment, (2) cognitive control network recruitment, and (3) reward-control network integration. The network recruitment and integration measures generated using the Louvain community detection algorithm are established graph measures (Mattar et al., 2015, Rubinov and Sporns, 2011) and have been widely applied to fMRI data across various contexts (Daws et al., 2022, Finc et al., 2020, Huang et al., 2024). High recruitment of a network indicates that regions within that network function cohesively as a unified system, reflecting strong intra-network communication and specialization. In contrast, high integration between networks reflects cross-network interactions, supporting complex cognitive processes that require coordination across distinct functional systems (Fair et al., 2007).

Statistical analysis

The preliminary analysis comprised descriptive statistics and correlation analysis. Subsequently, a multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the relation between three network variables and children’s cheating behavior. The analyses were performed using R 4.2.1 (R Core Team, 2021). Specifically, reward network recruitment, cognitive control network recruitment, and reward-control network integration were simultaneously entered as predictors of a binary cheating outcome. Demographic factors, including child gender and maternal education (used as an indicator of socioeconomic status), were included as covariates to account for their potential influence on children’s deceptive behavior (Keen and Tiemeier, 2022, VanderWeele, 2019). A parallel multiple logistic regression analysis was also conducted with lying tendency as the outcome variable in place of cheating. Finally, given that there are two subgroups—liars and non-liars—among the cheaters, we conducted additional analyses of covariance (ANCOVAs) to compare differences in network measures across three deception groups: non-cheaters, cheaters who confessed, and cheaters who lied. The results were consistent with the logistic regression analyses and are included in the Appendix.

To ensure that the effects of head motion during rs-fMRI were sufficiently minimized after de-noising, we assessed the relation between motion-related quality control metrics and our neuroimaging network measures (Ciric et al., 2017, Ciric et al., 2018). These metrics were not correlated with any network measures (|rs| < 0.10). Additionally, we found that these metrics were not associated with behavioral measures (i.e., cheating or lying; |rs| < 0.18). Furthermore, we included motion metrics as covariates of no interest in our main analyses, and the results remained consistent with those from analyses without motion metrics. We also conducted analyses using a stricter scrubbing criterion (framewise displacement < 0.2 mm) and our results remained mostly unchanged. These findings are presented in the Supplemental Materials.

Cheating

Reward network recruitment did not significantly predict children’s cheating tendency (p = .728). Similarly, cognitive control network recruitment did not significantly predict children’s cheating tendency (p = .301). However, reward-control network integration significantly and negatively predicted children’s cheating tendency, b = -0.04, SE = 0.02, p = .024, OR = 0.96 (95 %CI [0.93, 1.00]). Children whose mothers had post-secondary education were more likely to cheat compared to those whose mothers had secondary education or below, b = -1.19, SE = 0.52, p = .022, OR = 0.30 (95 % CI [0.11, 0.84]). There was no significant difference in cheating between children whose mothers had a bachelor’s degree or higher and those whose mothers had secondary education or below (p = .475). Child gender was not significantly associated with children’s cheating (p = .406).

Lying

Reward network recruitment did not significantly predict children’s lying tendency (p = .410). However, cognitive control network recruitment significantly and positively predicted children’s lying tendency, b = 0.03, SE = 0.01, p = .013, OR = 1.04 (95 %CI [1.01, 1.06]). Similarly, reward-control network integration significantly and negatively predicted children’s lying tendency, b = -0.06, SE = 0.03, p = .022, OR = 0.94 (95 %CI [0.90, 0.99]). Maternal education and child gender were not significantly associated with children’s lying (ps >.266).

Discussion

This study employed rs-fMRI to investigate the functional network patterns associated with children’s deceptive behavior. The findings revealed a positive association between cognitive control network recruitment and children’s lying tendency, as well as a negative association between reward-control network integration and both cheating tendency and lying tendency in children. We did not find an association between reward network recruitment and children’s deceptive behaviors.

Building on previous behavioral and neuroscience evidence on children’s deception (e.g., Ding et al., 2017; Evans and Lee, 2013), the current findings demonstrate the importance of cognitive control in children’s lying from a neural network perspective. Previous research has shown that functional brain networks become more segregated during childhood, shifting from a star-like toward a more line-like whole-brain configuration (He et al., 2019). This developmental shift implies that a higher level of network recruitment may be indicative of network maturity. Based on this interpretation, our current findings suggest that when children have a more mature cognitive control network, capable of executing cognitive intensive tasks, they are more likely to engage in successful lying. In contrast, the recruitment of the cognitive control network was not found to be significant in predicting children’s cheating tendency. Unlike lying, cheating does not necessarily require the verbal concealment of the truth or the construction of false statements. As a result, cognitive control skills may be less essential for cheating compared to lying. This distinction highlights the different levels of cognitive demands involved in two types of deception, suggesting that while lying relies heavily on cognitive control for the suppression and manipulation of information, cheating may often bypass these complex processes. This aligns with previous behavioral studies that found no significant link between children’s cheating and executive function (O’Connor et al., 2020, Talwar and Lee, 2008), and in some cases, even a negative association (Callender et al., 2010, Ding et al., 2014, Kochanska et al., 1996).

Consistent with our third hypothesis, another network measure, reward-control network integration, was related to a lower likelihood of deceptive behaviors in children, including both cheating and lying. As we mentioned before, it is possible that better reward-control network integration might enable top-down modulation of the reward network by the cognitive control network, which may have inhibited deceptive behavior. However, we did not find a direct relation between reward network recruitment and deceptive behaviors, which is not consistent with the second hypothesis. This may reflect the limited sensitivity of resting-state measures to detect context-specific activations of the reward system, rather than implying that the reward processing is not involved in deceptive choices.

To further clarify our interpretation that cognitive control network recruitment (i.e., intra-network connectivity) and reward-control network integration (i.e., inter-network connectivity) might serve distinct functions in children’s deceptive behaviors, we draw on the framework of cold and hot executive functions (Salehinejad et al., 2021). Specifically, when functioning independently, the cognitive control network recruitment might reflect the cold executive functions (Ward, 2020), which support emotionally neutral cognitive processing and enable the complex mental operations required for deception, such as inhibiting the prepotent response of telling the truth. In contrast, the integration between the cognitive control network and reward networks may reflect hot executive functions, which involve the regulation of emotionally and motivationally salient impulses, such as the temptation to obtain immediate rewards. This interpretation is further supported by prior research indicating that hot executive function engages both the prefrontal cortex and the nucleus accumbens, which are associated with cold executive function and the reward system, respectively (see Salehinejad et al., 2021). Moreover, increased fronto-striatal connectivity has been linked to greater delay of gratification skills, a hallmark of hot executive function (Achterberg et al., 2016). These findings suggest that children with higher levels of cognitive control network recruitment may be more capable of inhibiting the prepotent response of telling the truth, thereby increasing their lying tendency. In contrast, children with stronger reward-control network integration may be better able to resist impulses for immediate rewards or social approval, and therefore more likely to behave honestly.

Overall, this study revealed intrinsic network organizations associated with children’s honesty and deception. Specifically, the findings suggest that the cognitive control network and its integration with the reward network play an important role in children’s decisions when facing moral dilemmas about honesty. However, there are some limitations that can be addressed in future research. First, the current study only measured one type of lying behavior—self-protective lying, which is aimed at concealing transgressions. Future studies could explore whether the current findings apply to other types of lying behaviors in young children, such as instrumental lying to win a prize (Ding et al., 2015). Second, the current study focused on 6-year-old children, an age group characterized by ongoing cognitive development and an emerging motivation to suppress impulses to deceive as they internalize social norms. As such, it is unclear whether the findings can be generalized to other age groups. For instance, the association between cognitive control network recruitment and children’s lying might weaken as children grow older, as their lying may require fewer cognitive resources (Sai et al., 2022). Additionally, the relation between reward-control network integration and deception may not apply to younger children, as they have not yet internalized social norms related to honesty and may lack the motivation to suppress impulses for immediate rewards. Future studies could explore these possibilities using a longitudinal design or by including children from a broader range of age groups.

Conclusion

This study examines the association between resting-state network topology and children’s deceptive behaviors. The findings indicate that higher recruitment of the cognitive control network contributes to a greater tendency for lying, whereas greater integration of the reward-control network is related to a reduced tendency for both cheating and lying. Overall, this study contributes to our understanding of how the variations in brain network topology are associated with inter-individual differences in deceptive behaviors in children.

Neural Differences Across Different Deception Groups

Given that there are two subgroups (i.e., liars and non-liars) among the cheaters, we further compared network measures between different deception groups (non-cheaters, cheaters who confessed, and cheaters who lied). Three ANCOVAs with the deception group type as a between-subjects independent variable were performed on three network measures (reward network recruitment, cognitive control network recruitment and reward-control network integration) respectively. Child gender and maternal education were included as covariates, with maternal education dummy-coded into two variables: post-secondary (1 = post-secondary, 0 = otherwise) and bachelor and above (1 = bachelor and above, 0 = otherwise), using secondary and below as the reference category. In all ANCOVAs, neither maternal education nor child gender had significant effects on network measures.

The results showed that there were significant differences in cognitive control network recruitment across deception group types, F(2, 107) = 4.22, p = .017, ηp2 = 0.07. Post-hoc comparisons with Tukey correction revealed that children who lied about cheating had higher levels of cognitive control network recruitment compared to those who confessed to cheating, p = .028. There was no significant difference in cognitive control network recruitment between children who lied about cheating and those who did not cheat, nor between children who confessed to cheating and those who did not cheat (ps >.087). This finding aligns with the results of logistic regression analyses, which indicated that greater cognitive control network recruitment was associated with a higher tendency to lie. There was no significant main effect of deception group type on reward network recruitment (p = .980).

In addition, there were also significant differences in reward-control network integration across deception group types, F(2, 107) = 5.92, p = .004, ηp2 = 0.10. Post-hoc comparisons with Tukey correction revealed that children who lied about cheating had lower levels of reward-control network integration compared to children who confessed to cheating (p = .047) and children who did not cheat (p = .006). There was no significant difference in reward-control network integration between children who confessed to cheating and those who did not cheat (p = .937). This result indicated that the lower levels of reward-control network integration in the liar subgroup contributed to the observed differences between cheaters and non-cheaters in logistic regression analyses.

Overall, the group of children who lied about cheating stood out, showing higher levels of cognitive control network recruitment than those who confessed to cheating and lower levels of reward-control network integration than both children who confessed to cheating and those who did not cheat.
