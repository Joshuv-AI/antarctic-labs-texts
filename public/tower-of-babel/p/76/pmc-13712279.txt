Advances and perspectives of next-generation antitumor antibody-based modalities: Structural innovation and target-driven strategies
Authors: Liangjie Sun, Baixin Xu, Shuhang Wang, Ning Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibody-based therapies have gained significant traction in oncology, as evidenced by their prominent presence at international conferences such as American Association for Cancer Research (AACR) and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO). Modalities including antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), and T-cell engagers (TCEs) are increasingly recognized as pillars of precision medicine. Driven by advances in molecular engineering, antibody formats and mechanisms of action have evolved beyond conventional antigen blockade to encompass multimodal cytotoxicity and immune modulation. ADCs now leverage optimized linkers and payloads for targeted delivery and potent tumor cell killing. BsAbs simultaneously engage tumor and immune cell targets to enhance immune activation, while TCEs enable T cells to recognize intracellular antigens presented via MHC, overcoming the limitations of extracellular targeting. Emerging design strategies are increasingly tailored to target-specific features such as expression levels, internalization capacity, and cellular subtype. Innovations include non-internalizing ADCs for low-abundance targets and biased antibody architectures that preferentially activate CD8+ T cells. These developments are expanding the therapeutic landscape toward greater efficacy, safety, and personalization. This review outlines the global landscape of antibody drug development, highlighting a paradigm shift toward target-driven design principles that integrate structural innovation with functional precision.Key words: Antibody–drug conjugate (ADC), Bispecific antibody (BsAb), T-cell engager (TCE), Target-driven design, Antibody engineering, Undruggable targets, Precision oncology, Cancer immunotherapy

Introduction

As cancer continues to rise as a leading cause of mortality worldwide, conventional treatments such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy remain clinically relevant but are limited by suboptimal efficacy and substantial toxicity1. These modalities lack target specificity, making it difficult to administer therapeutically effective doses without inducing severe adverse effects. The 2025 American Association for Cancer Research (AACR) annual meeting underscored a paradigm shift toward precision medicine as a central theme in future oncology research. In this context, monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) offer highly selective recognition of tumor-associated antigens, with some capable of distinguishing single amino acid substitutions or post-translational modifications2,3. Owing to their specificity, antibody-based therapies can achieve therapeutic efficacy within a safer dose range.

Against this backdrop, the global momentum behind antibody therapeutics continues to accelerate, with over 200 candidates currently in clinical development or regulatory review, nearly half of which target oncological indications4. Antibody-based anti-cancer therapeutics have rapidly emerged as a structurally versatile and immunologically adaptive platform, distinguished by their high target specificity and potential for molecular innovation5. Recent disclosures from leading biopharmaceutical companies and presentations at major scientific conferences have showcased promising data from novel antibody programs, reinforcing their potential to enable personalized and highly effective cancer treatment.

The rise of antibody therapeutics began with hybridoma technology in the mid-1970s, though early murine-derived antibodies faced clinical limitations due to immunogenicity. By the mid-1970s, the first murine monoclonal antibodies were developed using hybridoma technology, but their clinical use was limited by immune reactions. Subsequent breakthroughs in antibody engineering, including chimerization in the early 1980s6 and humanization in the late 1980s7, alongside the development of fully human antibody discovery platforms, markedly improved safety and clinical applicability, accelerating their therapeutic deployment.

Engineering strategies have further refined Fc-mediated functions, enhancing both immune effector activity and pharmacokinetic profiles8. With these limitations addressed, structural innovation became a central focus, marking the transition toward next-generation antibody therapies. From the 2000s onward, emerging designs such as bispecific9,10, multi-specific11, and single-domain antibodies12 leveraged the modular architecture of antibodies to expand target coverage, improve tissue penetration, and enable complex functional formats. Antibodies can also be conjugated with small molecules, radioisotopes, or proteins, broadening their indication spectrum13,14. Functionally, design efforts are advancing toward complex immune modulation and tumor microenvironment responsiveness. For instance, CD8+ T cell-biased antibodies enhance activation and cytotoxicity of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, showing superior clearance in solid tumor models15. Meanwhile, non-internalizing antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) exploit microenvironment-specific payload release, bypassing the need for target internalization and enabling access to stromal or low-internalizing antigens16.

Looking ahead, antibody innovation is entering a new era of high-dimensional complexity. Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted discovery and multi-parametric optimization, emerging prominently in the 2020s17, now streamline structure prediction, affinity tuning, and developability assessment. Emerging strategies such as conditional antigen recognition (e.g., pH-dependent binding) and receptor agonist antibodies are enhancing adaptability to the tumor microenvironment and expanding immune modulation capabilities18. In parallel, functional diversification of immunoglobulin isotypes and engineering approaches targeting undruggable antigens are broadening both the indication spectrum and therapeutic depth19. Collectively, these frontiers suggest that antibody therapies will play an increasingly pivotal role across a wider range of disease contexts5.

This review delineates three key technological trajectories shaping the future of antibody therapeutics. First, structural optimization of ADCs is discussed, with emphasis on payload design, target internalization profiling, and delivery strategies for non-internalizing antigens. Second, the role of BsAbs in dual-target synergy is examined, particularly in co-targeting tumor antigens and immune checkpoint pathways. Third, the potential and limitations of T-cell engagers (TCEs) are analyzed in the context of undruggable target recognition and intracellular antigen presentation. Integrating recent preclinical and clinical insights, the review further explores how target characteristics such as CD8+ T cell bias and internalization capacity inform drug modality selection, and how multi-target synergy and precision delivery technologies are being integrated to guide rational antibody design and therapeutic strategy.

Method

Recent data on anti-cancer antibody therapeutics were sourced from abstracts released by AACR and ASCO in 2025. Additional unpublished information was extracted from Pharmcube Database (https://www.pharmcube.com), which aggregates global pharmaceutical data from publicly available sources, including but not limited to peer-reviewed publications, press releases, biotech company websites, and regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), European Medicines Agency (EMA), National Medical Products Administration of China (NMPA), and Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency of Japan (PMDA).

Global landscape of antibody-based modalities

Pivotal role and current innovation focus

Biologics are high-molecular-weight compounds derived from living organisms or produced via recombinant DNA technology, encompassing proteins, antibodies, nucleic acids, and vaccines20 (Fig. 1A). Due to their large size and limited stability, they are typically administered by injection or infusion21. Among these diverse modalities, antibody therapeutics have emerged as a cornerstone in oncology, owing to their high target specificity, structural plasticity enabled by genetic engineering, and intrinsic compatibility with the immune system22.

Antibody-based anti-cancer drugs, produced primarily through cellular and genetic engineering, are widely applied in the treatment of malignant tumors23. Key formats include mAbs, BsAbs, ADCs, TCEs, and emerging trispecific antibodies (TsAbs)23 (Table 1). Among these, mAbs remain the most established, with broad clinical use targeting canonical antigens such as HER2, PD-1/PD-L1, and EGFR24,25. BsAbs have advanced rapidly, particularly in T cell redirection (e.g., CD3 × tumor antigen) and immune modulation (e.g., PD-1 × VEGF), with several candidates entering late-stage trials or receiving regulatory approval26. ADCs have demonstrated strong efficacy against high-interest targets including TROP2, HER2, and Nectin-4, offering a promising alternative to conventional chemotherapy27. TCEs are especially suited for undruggable tumor antigens, enabling direct cytotoxicity via effector cell engagement27. TsAbs, though still in early development, were first disclosed at the 2025 AACR meeting and show potential for enhanced immune activation within complex tumor microenvironments.

Global overview and contributions from China

At the 2025 ASCO and AACR annual meetings, clinical data from numerous innovative antibody therapeutics were presented for the first time, spanning diverse tumor types and target mechanisms. We systematically compiled the disclosed pipeline updates (Supporting Information Table S1). Notably, the 2025 AACR meeting revealed a surge in ADC development, with 143 investigational programs, including 37 BsAb‒ADC hybrids, which represent a 142% increase over 2024. The number of BsAbs reached 40, with targets concentrated on EGFR, CDH17, B7-H3, and PD-L1 (Fig. 1A). According to the Pharmcube database, ADCs and BsAbs demonstrated faster progression in preclinical and early clinical stages compared to other biologic modalities, highlighting robust technological iteration and translational potential (Fig. 1B).

Among the top ten domestic research institutions, Chinese biopharmaceutical companies are actively contributing in the field, though gaps remain in platform maturity and pipeline volume relative to global leaders (Fig. 1C). Nevertheless, a comparative analysis of antibody-based therapeutics disclosed by multinational and Chinese pharmaceutical companies at AACR 2025 indicates that China maintains a considerable advantage in the quantity of ADC research and development (R&D) programs. Notably, nearly 100 investigational ADCs were disclosed by Chinese firms at AACR, showcasing differentiated innovation in dual-payload and dual-target designs (Supporting Information Table S2). Current biologics development is focused on immune checkpoints (e.g., PD-L1, B7-H4) and membrane receptors (e.g., EGFR, CDH17, HER2), aiming to enhance immune activation or disrupt oncogenic signaling for precise tumor targeting. This pattern reflects both biological rationale and practical design considerations that are particularly relevant in early-stage dual-payload ADC research. Focusing early efforts on these well-characterized targets provides a strategic advantage in mitigating risk in dual-payload designs, where complexity in conjugation chemistry and novel payload combinations already present substantial development challenges. By anchoring these advanced payload strategies to targets with established biology and clinical relevance, researchers can better assess whether dual mechanisms truly confer therapeutic benefit before extending into less validated or more heterogeneous antigen spaces (Fig. 1D).

While Chinese ADCs, particularly dual-payload/target constructs, hold considerable promise and have already demonstrated global competitiveness at the cutting edge of biologics innovation, the field is not without potential bottlenecks. Although conducting early feasibility studies on well-validated, mature targets is a prudent strategy, future progress will hinge on a shift toward truly differentiated targets and thoughtfully designed payload combinations with clear clinical rationale, rather than the mere juxtaposition or assembly of existing elements. Achieving this will require deep mechanistic insight into payload synergy and strategic foresight in target prioritization to avoid homogeneous, zero-sum competition.

Bispecific antibodies for dual pathway suppression

Since the first BsAb approval in 2014, the field has expanded rapidly, with notable advances in solid tumor applications and mechanistic diversity. The design of BsAbs has evolved to address the complexity of tumor progression by simultaneously targeting two parallel signaling pathways, creating a synergistic therapeutic effect that surpasses the limitations of monotherapy. This strategy is particularly impactful in oncology, where tumors often rely on multiple interdependent mechanisms for survival, such as immune evasion and angiogenesis. By integrating dual-targeting capabilities into a single molecule, BsAbs achieve “precision synergy”, overcoming challenges like drug resistance and toxicity associated with combinatorial therapies. Nevertheless, the increased molecular complexity of bispecific formats introduces substantial challenges in manufacturing, including low assembly efficiency and difficulties in maintaining product homogeneity, which may compromise batch-to-batch consistency and scalability10,35. In addition, their enlarged molecular size may limit deep penetration into dense solid tumor tissues, potentially attenuating therapeutic efficacy in poorly vascularized lesions10.

Dual targeting of EGFR and MET with immune activation

The EGFR × MET BsAb Amivantamab (Johnson & Johnson) exemplifies dual-pathway targeting with integrated immune activation. By simultaneously blocking EGFR and MET signaling and engaging antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP) via its Fc domain, Amivantamab suppresses tumor proliferation and recruits immune effector cells. At the 2025 ASCO meeting, multiple studies highlighted its clinical potential. Mechanistically, Amivantamab inhibits ligand binding to EGFR/MET and activates ADCC/ADCP.

In colorectal cancer, the OrigAMI-1 trial reported a third-line monotherapy objective response rate (ORR) of 22% and disease control rate (DCR) of 78%; second-line chemo-combination improved ORR to 43% and DCR to 86%. Transcriptome profiling revealed downregulation of EGFR and cell cycle pathways, alongside enhanced cytotoxic immune signatures, with baseline AREG/EREG expression correlating with response28. Ongoing phase III trials include OrigAMI-2 (first-line vs. standard anti-EGFR therapy)29 and OrigAMI-3 (second-line vs. cetuximab/bevacizumab)30, both evaluating progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) as primary endpoints.

In lung cancer, the MARIPOSA-2 study demonstrated that Amivantamab plus chemotherapy significantly outperformed chemotherapy alone in EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) resistant to osimertinib, confirming its efficacy in overcoming resistance31. Following successive approvals, Amivantamab has expanded from second-line treatment of EGFR Exon20Ins (2021) to first-line use in Exon20Ins and pan-EGFR mutations including 19del/L858R (2024)32, becoming the first BsAb to comprehensively target the EGFR mutation spectrum and validate the “oncogenic signaling + immune activation” strategy in solid tumors.

Despite its encouraging clinical activity, long-term dual blockade of EGFR and MET may impose selective pressure that drives bypass pathway activation or clonal evolution, potentially resulting in secondary resistance38. Moreover, Fc-mediated immune activation, while therapeutically beneficial, may increase the risk of infusion-related toxicities in susceptible patient populations32. Integrating adaptive biomarker monitoring and rational combination strategies targeting resistance pathways may help prolong response durability while maintaining acceptable safety profiles.

Coordinated modulation of PD-1 and VEGF pathways

A key axis in tumor progression involves the interplay between PD-1-mediated immune suppression and VEGF-driven angiogenesis. While PD-1 signaling inactivates T cells, VEGF promotes aberrant vasculature that both fuels tumor growth and impedes immune infiltration33.

Ivonescimab (Kangfang Biotech), the first approved tetravalent BsAb targeting PD-1 and VEGF, exemplifies this dual-target strategy. Its unique 2 × PD-1 and 2 × VEGF binding configuration induces a cooperative binding effect: VEGF engagement enhances PD-1 affinity 18-fold, intensifying T cell reactivation; conversely, PD-1 binding facilitates VEGF clearance and anti-angiogenic activity. In a head-to-head phase III trial against pembrolizumab, Ivonescimab achieved a median PFS of 11.14 months in advanced NSCLC, nearly double that of the control arm (5.82 months)34. A silent Fc design mitigated bleeding risks associated with conventional PD-1/VEGF combinations, and national reimbursement inclusion in 2024 further improved accessibility.

In parallel, Pfizer and 3SBio are co-developing SSG J-707, a BsAb built on the Common Light Chain Linear Fab × 2 (CLF2) platform. Its symmetric “2 + 2” architecture enhances dual-target synergy35. Phase II data presented at ASCO 2025 showed monotherapy ORR of 67.6%, comparable to Ivonescimab (50%). In the squamous NSCLC subgroup, SSG J-707 achieved an ORR of 75% and DCR of 100%, outperforming Ivonescimab (ORR 75%, DCR 89.9%)36. SSG J-707 also demonstrated high efficacy in both NSCLC and colorectal cancer, as monotherapy and in combination with chemotherapy, consistently achieving elevated ORR and DCR across indications. In April 2025, it received Breakthrough Therapy Designation from China’s NMPA, with plans to submit for additional designation in late 2025. A phase III trial evaluating SSG J-707 as first-line monotherapy in NSCLC is currently underway.

Bispecific antibodies that synchronously block dual pathways represent a paradigm shift in cancer therapy37. By integrating oncogenic pathway inhibition with immune activation or angiogenesis control, these agents achieve “1 + 1 > 2” efficacy through “single-molecule”. It not only minimizes systemic toxicity and avoids pharmacokinetic mismatches and additive toxicity seen in combination therapies, but also breaks through single-target resistance bottlenecks. The future trend will see their progression from mechanistic validation to first-line therapeutic standards across diverse cancers, propelled by biomarker-guided personalization.

However, sustained inhibition of VEGF signaling in combination with immune checkpoint blockade may still predispose patients to vascular-related adverse events, including hypertension and impaired wound healing, particularly during prolonged treatment33. Furthermore, the optimal patient selection criteria and predictive biomarkers for maximizing dual-pathway synergy remain insufficiently defined. Future studies should prioritize biomarker-driven patient stratification and dose-schedule optimization to balance immune activation with vascular safety.

Advances in ADC payload design and targeting strategies

ADCs consist of three components: a mAb, a linker, and a cytotoxic payload. The mAb selectively binds to tumor-specific surface antigens, facilitating internalization of the ADC and subsequent lysosomal cleavage of the linker to release the payload and induce tumor cell death. While clinical data have shown meaningful improvements in progression-free survival (PFS) for certain cancers, therapeutic efficacy remains constrained by two major challenges: drug resistance and a narrow therapeutic window. Mechanistically, tumor cells often evade single-payload cytotoxicity by activating compensatory signaling pathways (e.g., c-MET activation following EGFR resistance38) or upregulating drug efflux pumps such as P-gp39. Additionally, cumulative payload toxicity (e.g., hematologic adverse effects40) and tumor heterogeneity contribute to inconsistent responses, limiting the applicability of single-payload ADCs. Synergistic payloads and conjugation technologies

Dual-payload ADCs offer a novel strategy for achieving synergistic therapy within a single molecular construct. Mechanistically, they impose a higher barrier to tumor resistance, as cancer cells must simultaneously acquire escape mutations across two inhibited pathways, a scenario considered unlikely and one that helps mitigate tumor heterogeneity and resistance development27. From a manufacturing perspective, dual-payload ADCs benefit from orthogonal conjugation technologies, enabling precise control over drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) and enhancing product homogeneity and process stability. Platforms such as the glycan-specific conjugation system developed by JW Therapeutics have demonstrated improved consistency in payload distribution and scalable production41.

However, the incorporation of multiple cytotoxic agents complicates pharmacokinetic modeling and dose optimization, as mismatched payload release kinetics may exacerbate systemic toxicity or undermine the intended synergistic effect27,41. Advanced linker engineering and controllable sequential-release platforms may enable more predictable exposure profiles and improve the therapeutic index of dual-payload constructs.

Representative dual-payload constructs in clinical development

Chinese companies are at the forefront of dual-toxin ADC development, with several candidates advancing into clinical trials ahead of global peers. At the 2025 AACR meeting, multiple dual-payload and dual-target dual-toxin ADCs were disclosed (Table 3), whereas most international pipelines remain in preclinical stages with limited public data. Kanghong Pharmaceutical developed KH815, the first dual-toxin ADC to enter clinical evaluation. KH815 comprises a humanized IgG1 antibody (hRS7) conjugated with both a topoisomerase I (TOP1) inhibitor (irinotecan) and an RNA polymerase II (RNA POL II) inhibitor (triptolide). An average DAR of ∼7.5 (4 irinotecan + 3.5 triptolide) enables KH815 to exhibit synergistic anti-tumor activity through simultaneous transcriptional suppression and DNA double-strand break induction. Compared to conventional TROP2-targeting ADCs carrying only TOP1 inhibitors (e.g., Trodelvy, Datroway), KH815 exerts dual mechanistic effects and has shown favorable safety and potential efficacy in camptothecin-resistant models. Additionally, non-GLP toxicity studies in cynomolgus monkeys indicated a highest non-severely toxic dose (HNSTD) of 40 mg/kg, supporting its acceptable safety profile. It is currently undergoing a global phase I trial in advanced solid tumors (NCT06885645)42. Separately, JW Therapeutics has attracted attention with JSKN021, the first-in-class dual-target dual-payload ADC. JSKN021 employs a BsAb targeting both EGFR and HER3, conjugated with a TOP1 inhibitor (T01, DAR = 4) and a microtubule inhibitor (MMAE, DAR = 2). By fine-tuning target-binding affinities, JSKN021 enhances tumor selectivity while minimizing off-target toxicity. This design addresses common limitations of single-target ADCs, including resistance and non-response. In multiple cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) models, JSKN021 demonstrated superior tumor suppression compared to single-payload ADCs. Preclinical studies confirmed the synergistic cytotoxicity of the T01-MMAE combination43. Although these early clinical candidates demonstrate promising activity, dual-toxin exposure may raise concerns regarding cumulative organ toxicity under repeated dosing schedules. Long-term follow-up studies and translational safety biomarkers will be critical to define tolerable dosing paradigms and identify early signals of chronic toxicity.

Linker engineering and payload balancing

Moving forward, optimization of dual-payload ADCs will require balancing payload synergy, linker control, and safety profiles. Combining payloads with synthetic lethality, such as TOP1 and ATR inhibitors, can enhance cytotoxicity. Technologies like branched linkers and orthogonal conjugation enable differential payload release and precise DAR control. Staggered-release linker designs, which trigger payload liberation intracellularly or within the tumor microenvironment, may broaden cytotoxic coverage and amplify bystander effects. Beyond KH815, Araris Biotech has developed ARC-201, a dual-payload ADC targeting NaPi2b, conjugated with irinotecan and its low-permeability derivative, both of which are TOP1 inhibitors. Irinotecan mediates the bystander effect, while its derivative enhances direct killing of target cells. This dual mechanism improves tumor selectivity and potency44. However, cumulative toxicity remains a concern, necessitating pharmacokinetic modeling to optimize payload ratios and mitigate adverse effects. Rational pairing of mechanistically complementary but non-overlapping payload classes may reduce normal tissue liability while preserving synergistic antitumor activity.

Expanding toward multi-payload and immune-integrated designs

Beyond dual-payload formats, the development of tri-payload ADCs and BsAbs is expanding multi-target coverage and mechanistic synergy, driving integration toward immune-combinatorial modalities. A tri-payload ADC targeting Nectin-4 incorporates two distinct TOP1 inhibitors and the microtubule inhibitor MMAE, both of which have been validated in Nectin-4-directed applications. Leveraging the immunogenic cell death properties of MMAE, such constructs may synergize with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), forming a chemo-immunotherapy loop. The tri-payload ADC exhibits high homogeneity and purity, with a DAR of 6, meeting design expectations and demonstrating a favorable safety profile without exceeding toxicity observed in single-payload counterparts45.

Despite their conceptual appeal, tri-payload and immune-integrated ADCs may face diminishing therapeutic returns as molecular complexity increases, potentially resulting in unpredictable toxicity profiles and reduced manufacturability. Future development should emphasize stepwise clinical validation and modular design strategies rather than maximal payload stacking in a single molecular entity.

T cell engagers for intracellular and undruggable targets

In oncology, conventional antibody therapeutics predominantly target cell surface receptors or secreted factors. However, growing evidence underscores the pivotal role of undruggable targets, including transcription factors and protein-protein interaction interfaces, in tumor initiation, progression, and resistance. For instance, the tumor suppressor TP53, one of the most frequently mutated cancer drivers46, lacks a defined small-molecule binding pocket, and no approved therapies currently target its protein product47. Other structurally intractable yet functionally essential targets include oncogenic fusion proteins and non-enzymatic scaffold proteins, which are critical for tumor cell survival and proliferation48,49. Recent advances in structural biology50, targeted protein degradation51, and nanoparticle delivery systems52 are beginning to unlock these previously inaccessible targets, catalyzing innovation in cancer immunotherapy, precision medicine, and multimodal therapeutic strategies.

Among emerging TCEs, the most notable advances in targeting undruggable antigens center on p53 and KRAS (Table 4). Intracellular and nuclear proteins are inaccessible to conventional antibody-based therapies, yet they undergo proteasomal degradation into peptide fragments, some of which are presented on the cell surface by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules53,54. This mechanism enables selective recognition of intracellular proteins via surface-displayed peptide–HLA (pHLA) complexes. In tumor cells, mutated proteins generate aberrant peptides that are processed and presented by HLA, forming mutation-associated neoantigens (MANAs)55. These MANAs can be exploited to develop TCEs that specifically engage T cell receptors, offering a strategy to therapeutically target intracellular oncogenic drivers.

Targeting TP53 R175H through pHLA presentation

Among known TP53 hotspot mutations, R175H is the most prevalent, with reported frequencies of 3.69% and 4.21% in cancer patients according to TCGA and IARC databases56. Meanwhile, HLA-A02:01∗ is the most common HLA-A allele in individuals of European descent, with a population frequency of ∼26.7%, making TCEs targeting this allele broadly applicable57.

Built upon the structural and functional foundation of H2-scDb developed at Johns Hopkins University, CLSP-1025 is the first TCE to enter clinical evaluation for TP53-mutant cancers58. Its anti-pHLA domain selectively binds the R175H-derived peptide presented by HLA-A02:01, with no cross-reactivity to wild-type TP53 peptides or unrelated HLA-presented epitopes, thereby ensuring high specificity and minimizing off-target toxicity. Its anti-CD3∗ domain activates T cells to mediate tumor killing. Preclinical studies showed potent T cell activation (EC50 = 0.8 nmol/L) and tumor cell lysis (EC90 = 10 nmol/L), along with significant tumor regression in CDX models. Safety assessments revealed no nonspecific tissue binding or aberrant immune activation. The phase I GUARDIAN-101 trial, initiated in early 2025, is evaluating CLSP-1025 in patients with advanced solid tumors harboring TP53 R175H mutations and HLA-A∗02:01 positivity, focusing on safety, pharmacokinetics, and preliminary efficacy59.

Other TCEs targeting TP53 R175H remain in preclinical development (Table 4). However, reliance on specific HLA subtypes such as HLA-A∗02:01 limits patient eligibility. To address this, Immunocore is advancing a γδ TCR platform (in collaboration with Gadeta) to enable broader tumor recognition60. Beyond TCEs, additional therapeutic modalities under investigation include PROTACs61 and TCR-T cell therapies62, offering complementary strategies to expand access to treatments targeting undruggable oncogenic drivers. Overall, expanding to multi-allele or HLA-agnostic recognition platforms could significantly enlarge the treatable patient population for p53-directed TCE therapies.

Expanding TCE strategies for KRAS-driven cancers

KRAS is the most frequently mutated member of the RAS family63, with alterations found in 17% of solid tumors according to MSKCC IMPACT and AACR Project GENIE cohorts64. Mutation rates reach 90% in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), 50% in olorectal cancer (CRC), and 25% in NSCLC65. These mutations lock RAS proteins in an active state, aberrantly activating downstream MAPK and PI3K pathways to promote proliferation, survival, and immune evasion. The most common variants G12C, G12V, and G12D account for 40%, 19%, and 15% of KRAS mutations in NSCLC, respectively64. In 2021, the FDA approved sotorasib, the first KRAS G12C inhibitor, for NSCLC. However, resistance mechanisms have emerged, diminishing long-term efficacy66. Moreover, the diversity of KRAS mutation subtypes and the limited coverage of current therapies, which do not address variants such as G12V and G12D, underscore the urgent need for innovative drug development to overcome subtype-specific vulnerabilities and resistance.

Recent studies demonstrate that combining covalent inhibitors of oncogenic proteins with engineered antibodies can selectively eliminate resistant tumor cells67,68. Upon modification by KRAS G12C inhibitors, the protein is degraded into hapten-peptides, which are presented by MHC-I molecules as drug-induced pMHC neoantigens. Using immunopeptidomics, researchers identified distinct pMHCs modified by sotorasib, adagrasib, and divarasib69. Based on these findings, the TCE molecule AETX-R114 was developed to target HLA-A3 supertype alleles (A03:01, A11:01, A68:01), selectively recognizing drug-modified pMHCs without interference from free drug. Despite low pMHC abundance (<1 copy/cell) and short half-life (42 min), AETX-R114 achieves picomolar affinity, extending surface half-life to >24 h and increasing antigen density 15-fold. It induces potent T cell-mediated cytotoxicity in resistant KRAS G12C cell lines and suppresses tumor growth by 84% in CDX models when combined with sotorasib70.

MA022-2037 is another TCE with extended half-life, targeting the KRAS G12V/HLA-A∗11:01 complex. It exhibits high specificity, sparing wild-type KRAS and unrelated peptides. In vitro, MA022-2037 effectively directs T cells to kill KRAS G12V-mutant cells without affecting HLA-mismatched or wild-type cells. With favorable pharmacokinetics, it is currently in IND-enabling studies71.

Emerging programs are advancing TCE therapies for non-G12C KRAS subtypes. MDG3010 targets KRAS G12V, common in pancreatic, colorectal, and lung cancers. WO2021224261A1 describes a CD3 bispecific format recognizing KRAS G12D, enabling T cell engagement in G12D-mutant tumors. These approaches address subtypes still largely beyond the reach of current covalent KRAS inhibitors. Despite promising preclinical efficacy, KRAS-targeted TCEs face several challenges. The low abundance and short half-life of KRAS-derived pMHCs limit antigen presentation, necessitating ultra-high affinity binders and precise HLA matching. HLA restriction further narrows patient eligibility, while cytokine release remains a dose-limiting concern. Future strategies may include tumor-activated prodrug formats, pan-HLA targeting, and combination approaches with degraders or checkpoint inhibitors to enhance antigen density and safety.

Future direction of anti-tumor antibody-based modalities

Multi-targeting approaches to overcome resistance

It is worth noting that the concept of multi-targeting in this context can be realized at different levels of therapeutic design. On the other hand, multi-target effects can also be achieved through rational combination regimens, for example by integrating antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), where distinct modalities collectively address complementary resistance mechanisms. Over recent decades, cancer therapy has evolved from monotherapy to combination regimens and, more recently, to multi-targeted therapeutics72. Early antitumor antibodies, such as rituximab and trastuzumab, were initially developed primarily as adjuncts to conventional treatments including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, aiming to enhance overall response rather than serve as standalone modalities. With the continuous identification of novel therapeutic targets and deeper insights into tumor immune evasion mechanisms, immunotherapy-based combinations, including ICIs73, cytokines74, and therapeutic vaccines75, enable synergistic modulation of immune escape mechanisms, restoring T cell function and improving response rates and survival, particularly in patients refractory to single-agent immunotherapy76. These strategies also reshape the tumor microenvironment, enhancing immune infiltration and overall efficacy77.

On the one hand, advances in antibody engineering have enabled the incorporation of multiple binding specificities into a single molecular construct, such as bispecific or trispecific antibodies, thereby allowing coordinated modulation of several disease-relevant pathways within one agent. Multi-targeted agents simultaneously modulate multiple disease-relevant pathways, addressing tumor heterogeneity, reducing resistance risk, and amplifying anti-tumor activity through pathway synergy72,78. This approach also facilitates dose optimization, minimizing toxicity while maintaining efficacy79. Since 2010, the number of multi-target therapies approved by the FDA has risen markedly, reflecting growing interest in this paradigm72. T cell-redirecting bispecific or multispecific antibodies represent a promising structural class that integrates targeted delivery, immune activation, and oncogene blockade within a single molecule26,80. TsAbs, in particular, offer enhanced precision over BsAbs. Nonetheless, whether such “single-molecule combination” strategies can consistently outperform optimized drug combinations across heterogeneous tumor types remains to be validated in long-term clinical settings37. Large-scale comparative trials and real-world evidence analyses will be essential to clarify the true therapeutic margin of bispecific antibodies over conventional combination regimens.

Trispecific antibodies for enhanced tumor selectivity

A notable example is JNJ-79635322 (Johnson & Johnson), a trispecific antibody targeting BCMA, GPRC5D, and CD3, designed for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (RRMM). JNJ-79635322 incorporates a low-affinity CD3-binding domain to redirect T cells toward dual myeloma-associated antigens, aiming to maximize tumor specificity while minimizing off-tumor toxicity.

In vitro, it induces potent cytotoxicity and T cell activation across multiple myeloma cell lines. In a first-in-human phase I trial, the agent demonstrated strong efficacy and safety at the recommended phase II dose. Among 27 treatment-naïve patients, ORR reached 100%, with mostly grade 1 cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and a convenient dosing schedule. In the RP2D cohort (n = 36), ORR was 86%, with 75% achieving very good partial response (VGPR). Across the full study population (n = 124), ORR was 73%, with 66% reaching VGPR81.

CD8-biased designs to minimize cytokine release syndrome

Despite their therapeutic promise, conventional TsAbs often exhibit off-tumor toxicity in cancer immunotherapy. A key contributor is the CD3-mediated recruitment of broad CD3+ T cell subsets, which disproportionately activates CD4+ T cells, which lead to excessive cytokine release, Treg-mediated immunosuppression, and systemic toxicity such as CRS and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome, posing significant clinical challenges. For example, traditional GPC3 × CD3 TCEs are associated with high CRS risk82,83.

To address this, CD8-biased design adds TCEs with CD8-directed specificity that preferentially recruit and activate CD8+ T cells over CD4+ T cell. AstraZeneca developed AZD9793, a next-generation GPC3/TCR/CD8 TCE that preferentially engages CD8+ T cells via CD8/TCR domains, enhancing tumor cell killing while minimizing CD4+ T cell activation and reducing CRS incidence. AZD9793 is currently the first and only clinical-stage CD8-biased TsAb with bivalent GPC3-binding domains. Toxicology studies confirmed favorable tolerability, with a defined no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 6 mg/kg/week84. In addition, AstraZeneca’s second CD8-biased molecule, AZD5492, targets CD20/TCR/CD8 and incorporates a low-affinity TCR-binding domain. It has entered phase I/II clinical trials, though efficacy data remain undisclosed. While conceptually attractive, CD8-biased design may face clinical translational hurdles. Unlike CD3 engagement, engineering the binding affinities to preferentially engage CD8+ cells without compromising efficient immune synapse formation is non-trivial. Too high affinity can cause prolonged binding that may cause T cell exhaustion, whereas suboptimal affinity may fail to activate T cells effectively. Optimizing these parameters is critical but complex.

Combination of ADCs with ICIs

ADCs and ICIs produce synergistic antitumor effects through interconnected mechanisms. ADCs bind to tumor surface antigens, undergo internalization to release payloads that induce apoptosis, and crucially trigger immunogenic cell death, releasing tumor-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns, such as calreticulin, heat shock protein 70/90, ATP, high mobility group box-1. Dendritic cells recognize these molecules, process antigens, and present them via MHC II to activate CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells. ICIs (including PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors) block checkpoint-ligand binding, alleviate T-cell immunosuppression, and maintain antitumor activity of T cells85. ADCs also promote memory T cells for long-term immunity, with preclinical evidence86.

Building on the groundwork laid for this synergy, numerous clinical trials are currently underway to investigate the combination of ADCs and ICIs in the treatment of diverse cancer types. Recent clinical trials highlight the therapeutic synergy between ADCs and ICIs across multiple solid tumors. In lung cancer, the TROPION-Lung04 trial demonstrated that Dato-DXd (TROP2 ADC) combined with rilvegostomig (PD-1/TIGIT bispecific antibody) achieved an ORR of 57.5% and disease control rate (DCR) of 95.0% in treatment-naïve, driver-gene-negative advanced NSCLC patients87. In urothelial cancer, the phase II TROPION-PanTumor03 study showed that the same combination yielded an ORR of 68.2% and 12-week DCR of 95.5% in first-line locally advanced/metastatic patients, with median PFS not reached88. Another novel combination, 9MW2821 (Nectin-4 ADC) plus toripalimab (PD-1 inhibitor), achieved an impressive ORR of 87.5% in treatment-naïve urothelial cancer, even in subgroups with liver metastasis or Nectin-4 negative expression89. In breast cancer, the phase III ASCENT-04 trial confirmed that sacituzumab govitecan (TROP2 ADC) combined with pembrolizumab prolonged median PFS to 11.2 months (vs. 7.8 months with chemotherapy + pembrolizumab) in PD-L1-positive triple-negative breast cancer, with a 35% reduction in disease progression risk. For muscle-invasive bladder cancer, the KEYNOTE-905 trial revealed that enfortumab vedotin (Nectin-4 ADC) plus pembrolizumab significantly improved event-free survival and pathologic complete response rate in cisplatin-ineligible patients90. These data collectively validate the clinical potential of TROP2/Nectin-4-targeted ADCs in combination with dual ICIs or PD-1 inhibitors, with synergistic efficacy observed across different PD-L1 expression levels and tumor subtypes.

Like other joint strategies, the widespread adoption of ADC-ICI combinations may be constrained by overlapping toxicities and cumulative immune-related adverse events, which necessitate careful regimen optimization and patient selection. Biomarker-guided combination strategies and adaptive dosing algorithms may help maximize synergy while minimizing immune-mediated toxicity.

Click chemistry platforms for extracellular payload release

Since its recognition as a Nobel Prize-winning technology in 2022, click chemistry has gained traction in ADC development92. Leveraging click-to-release mechanisms, Tagworks has pioneered a platform enabling extracellular drug release via in vivo click reactions, extending ADC applicability to non-internalizing targets93. The approach involves two steps: tumor-localized ADC binding followed by systemic administration of a click trigger, inducing linker cleavage and payload release at the tumor surface. The liberated drug diffuses within the tumor microenvironment, achieving broad cytotoxicity even in antigen-heterogeneous tissues. The first clinical-stage Click-cleavable ADC, TGW101, targets TAG72, a non-internalizing antigen widely expressed in adenocarcinomas. TAG72 is a pan-carcinoma marker composed of STn and Tn glycans presented by multiple mucins, and has been validated as a clean and stable surface target in clinical radioimmunotherapy. Its expression spans a broad range of solid tumors, including gastric, breast, lung, ovarian, and colorectal cancers, highlighting TGW101’s potential applicability across diverse indications. It comprises a tetrazine trigger (TRG001) and a click-cleavable ADC (TGW101-ADC), built from a 60 kDa bispecific antibody site-specifically conjugated to MMAE via a trans-cyclooctene (TCO) linker, with a DAR of 3.8. TGW101 demonstrated potent and selective anti-tumor activity in vitro and in vivo, with improved tolerability over conventional MMAE ADCs, and has entered phase I trials93. Tagworks is also developing click-cleavable radiotherapeutics, using triggers to eliminate circulating agents while preserving internalized radioactivity, thereby enhancing tumor-to-blood ratios (Fig. 3).

Schematic illustration of the two-step dosing strategy for click-cleavable antibody–drug conjugates. TGW101-ADC binds to non-internalizing TAG72 antigens on tumor cells following the first injection. Upon second injection of the tetrazine trigger (TRG001), a click reaction induces linker cleavage and extracellular payload release. The liberated MMAE exerts direct cytotoxicity on TAG72-positive cells and diffuses within the tumor microenvironment, enabling bystander killing of neighboring TAG72-negative cells.

In contrast, Shasqi’s Click Activated Protodrugs Against Cancer platform (CAPAC) separates targeting and therapeutic components, also built on click chemistry. In 2023, Shasqi partnered with Johnson & Johnson to co-develop therapies based on the CAPAC. At the 2024 AACR meeting, Shasqi presented preclinical data on HER2-and Nectin-4-targeting candidates, showing superior efficacy compared to internalizing ADCs94,95. Additionally, non-internalizing B cell antigens such as CD20, CD21, and CD72 have shown therapeutic potential, suggesting that poorly internalized targets may be tractable with next-generation ADC formats96. Despite the high spatial precision offered by click-to-release systems, the requirement for sequential administration of an antibody conjugate and a chemical trigger introduces additional pharmacokinetic uncertainty and increases operational complexity in clinical settings, potentially affecting reproducibility and patient compliance. Next-generation platforms may benefit from self-immolative or tumor-autonomous activation mechanisms that eliminate the need for external triggers while preserving spatial selectivity of payload liberation.

ADCs responsive to tumor microenvironment cues

Beyond click chemistry-based platforms, a parallel frontier in non-internalizing ADCs targets the extracellular matrix (ECM) of tumors. These agents exploit tumor-specific chemical or enzymatic microenvironments to activate cleavable linkers, enabling localized payload release and reducing off-target toxicity97. Among early clinical efforts in this space, Pfizer’s PYX-201 targets EDB-FN, an ECM-associated antigen, and releases its payload extracellularly. In a phase I trial, among six evaluable patients, one achieved complete response (CR) and two achieved partial response (PR)98. Extending this concept to linker engineering, Yilink Biotech has developed the Tumor Microenvironment Activable LINker (TMALIN) platform, which features enzyme-sensitive linkers cleaved specifically within the tumor microenvironment. Based on TMALIN, Yilink has partnered with multiple companies to develop ADCs against targets including B7-H3, HER3, PD-L1, NaPi-2b, DLL3, EGFR, c-MET, CDH17, VEGF, and LRRC15. Among these candidates, the B7-H3-targeting ADC YL201 demonstrated promising results in a phase I trial involving 312 patients with solid tumors. In a subset of 72 patients with extensive-stage small cell lung cancer, YL201 achieved an ORR of 63.9%, DCR of 91.7%, and a median PFS of 6.3 months. Notably, in patients with brain metastases, ORR was 52.2% and median PFS was 5.3 months. Based on these findings, Yilink is preparing phase III trials for YL201 in small cell lung cancer and nasopharyngeal carcinoma99. These tumor-microenvironment-responsive linkers rely on the presence and activity of specific enzymes or chemical conditions, which may exhibit substantial inter-patient and intra-tumoral variability, potentially leading to inconsistent payload activation and heterogeneous therapeutic responses. Future optimization should integrate multi-stimuli-responsive linker designs and companion diagnostic assays to ensure robust activation across diverse tumor microenvironments while minimizing off-tumor cleavage.

Target-driven design principles for anti-tumor antibody-based modalities

For the R&D of innovative antibody-based therapeutics, structural innovation and target selection have become inseparable and mutually reinforcing design dimensions. Advances in antibody architecture not only enable new mechanisms of action, but also redefine which classes of tumor antigens are pharmacologically accessible. Conversely, the biological properties of therapeutic targets, including expression pattern, cellular localization, internalization capacity, and immunological accessibility, directly constrain feasible molecular formats and engineering strategies. Due to fundamental differences in molecular architecture and mechanism of action, different classes of antibody therapeutics exhibit varying degrees of compatibility with specific target characteristics100. Rational modality selection should therefore be guided by a target-driven design logic integrated with structure-based optimization, rather than by format preference alone101. Optimization of chemical structure and physicochemical properties is essential not only to maximize therapeutic efficacy. But also tomitigate development risk and translational failure. Establishing robust platforms and scalable workflows to overcome technical bottlenecks further enables rapid iteration between target validation and structural refinement across pipelines102. Monoclonal antibodies for selective and durable targeting

Even in the era of multi-specific antibodies, mAbs retain irreplaceable value in tumors driven by single signaling pathways. Their mechanistic simplicity and well-characterized safety profile make them foundational components of combination therapy103. As the most established antibody format, mAbs exhibit clear target compatibility, typically requiring membrane-bound molecules with high tumor specificity and well-defined biological functions. These targets are often overexpressed in tumor cells but minimally present in normal tissues, and their activity is directly linked to tumor progression. Through selective binding, mAbs can block oncogenic signaling or modulate immune responses, offering a precise and durable therapeutic effect. Taken together, the clinical success of mAbs highlights how structural simplicity can be optimally matched to biologically dominant and well-validated targets, underscoring that increasing architectural complexity is not universally required when target biology is sufficiently clear and homogeneous.

Multi-specific antibodies and their design requirements

While both BsAbs and TsAbs are designed for multi-target engagement, their requirements for target compatibility differ substantially in complexity104. BsAbs rely on two co-expressed and functionally related targets within the tumor microenvironment, often involved in parallel or intersecting oncogenic pathways. Their expression levels must be balanced to enable simultaneous binding and generate synergistic effects, which depend on the degree of functional coupling between the two targets105. TsAbs introduce a third target, typically an immune cell-engaging domain, which adds further design constraints. In addition to co-expression and functional relevance of the tumor-associated targets, the third must align with the antibody’s immune activation mechanism, ensuring precise T cell recruitment and activation at the tumor site while minimizing systemic immune toxicity106. These design requirements illustrate that the escalating structural complexity of multi-specific antibodies is fundamentally driven by the increasing biological interdependence of therapeutic targets within the tumor microenvironment, rather than by structural innovation alone.

Antibody–drug conjugates and internalization-dependent delivery

ADC target compatibility is primarily defined by antigen expression level, internalization capacity, and tumor specificity107. Conventional ADCs rely on antigen-mediated endocytosis to deliver cytotoxic payloads intracellularly, necessitating high surface expression in tumor cells for sufficient drug accumulation, low expression in normal tissues to minimize off-target toxicity, and efficient internalization to facilitate lysosomal release108. In this context, ADC development exemplifies how target trafficking biology directly dictates molecular architecture, including linker chemistry, payload selection, and conjugation strategy.

Dual-payload ADCs further require targets capable of supporting synergistic payload activity, while non-internalizing ADCs, despite bypassing endocytosis through technological innovation, still depend on high tumor-specific antigen expression to ensure therapeutic selectivity109. Thus, recent structural innovations expand the target space but do not eliminate the central role of target biology in defining therapeutic feasibility.

T and NK cell engagers for immune-selective targeting

T/NK-cell engagers (T/NK-CEs) require highly selective target compatibility, balancing tumor specificity with immune recognition feasibility110,111. Their targets may include membrane-bound tumor-associated antigens or intracellular mutant peptides presented as pHLA complexes via HLA molecules.

Regardless of target class, expression should be largely restricted to tumor cells to minimize off-target immune activation and collateral damage to healthy tissues. In parallel, candidate antigens must display structural features that are amenable to productive recognition by T cells or NK cells, thereby enabling stable immune synapse formation and potent cytotoxic signaling upon engagement. This dual requirement, comprising tumor exclusivity and immunologic accessibility, is central to the design of safe and effective T/NK-CEs. Collectively, these constraints highlight that for immune cell engagers, structural engineering is largely dictated by immunological target properties, positioning target selection as the primary determinant of both efficacy and safety.

Conclusions

This review systematically summarizes the paradigm shift of next-gen antitumor antibodies from conventional antigen blockade to target-driven, multi-modal precision therapy. Importantly, this shift is enabled by the co-evolution of antibody structural innovation and increasingly sophisticated target selection strategies. Unlike previous focused reviews on single modalities, this work integrates structural optimization, target compatibility, and clinical translation to provide a holistic framework for antibody drug design. Dual-payload ADCs still face challenges in payload ratio balancing and cumulative toxicity; TCEs are limited by HLA restriction and low pMHC abundance. Future efforts should focus on AI-assisted multi-parametric optimization of antibody structures, development of pan-HLA TCEs to expand patient eligibility, and clinical validation of non-internalizing ADCs in low-abundance target tumors. Together, these advances will further consolidate structural innovation as a tool to unlock challenging targets, and target-driven design as the guiding principle of next-generation antibody engineering.
