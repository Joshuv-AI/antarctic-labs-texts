Geographic and genetic factors shape acoustic divergence and dialect-like variation in communication calls in the greater horseshoe bats
Authors: Kang-Kang Zhang, Hong-Wei Wang, Hong-Jun Lin, Guan-Jun Lu, Xiao-Bin Huang, Tong Liu, Walter Metzner, Jiang Feng, Ying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcoustic divergence is a fundamental component of signal-based communication in animals, including humans, and has major implications for individual recognition, mate choice, and speciation. Bats have recently become an important model system for investigating acoustic communication due to their structurally complex vocal repertoires. This study explored communication calls of greater horseshoe bats (Rhinolophus nippon) to determine whether geographically structured dialects occur and to identify factors associated with dialect formation. Communication calls from nine populations distributed across three regions were analyzed, and 12 syllable types shared among populations were identified. The associations of dialect differentiation with acoustic, climatic, morphological, and genetic distances, together with the influence of geographic barriers, were investigated. Results demonstrated significant acoustic variation among populations and regions for each syllable type, and this divergence was driven by diverse factors. As bats typically emit syllables in combination, all shared syllable types were subsequently analyzed as an integrated dataset. At this broader level, acoustic distance showed significant correlations with both geographic and genetic distance. Causal modeling further revealed that geographic distance and geographic barriers exerted direct effects on dialect differentiation. The sampled populations were classified into three dialect regions: Northeast, Central-East, and Southwest. The Qinling Mountains formed the boundary between the Central-East and Southwest regions. These findings provide clear evidence for the existence of dialect structure in communication calls of greater horseshoe bats and identify geographic isolation as a major force in the formation and preservation of vocal divergence. This study advances current understanding of animal acoustic evolution and offers insight into mechanisms linking vocal diversification with the generation of biological diversity.Keywords: Acoustic divergence, Dialects, Geographic variation, Driving forces, Communication calls, Bats

INTRODUCTION

Acoustic signals mediate a wide range of essential behaviors across humans and many other animal taxa, including mate choice, resource defense, and species and individual recognition (Wilkins et al., 2013). Geographic variation in vocal signals represents one of the most common forms of acoustic divergence and indicates that intraspecific vocal differentiation often covaries with spatial separation (Barker et al., 2021; Henry et al., 2015). Differences in acoustic repertoires observed over long distances and among populations that do not normally interbreed are generally described as geographic variation (Conner, 1982). In contrast, vocal differences between neighboring populations with potential for interbreeding are typically termed dialects (Filatova et al., 2012). Previous studies have demonstrated that genetic mutation, sexual selection, ecological selection, and drift can rapidly alter signal structure within and among populations, thereby promoting acoustic divergence and contributing to speciation (Fan et al., 2022; Irwin et al., 2008; Lachlan & Servedio, 2004; Puechmaille et al., 2011). Although vocal divergence has been examined in birds, frogs, and mammals, the mechanisms shaping variation in acoustic traits remain poorly understood (Wilkins et al., 2013; Wright et al., 2008; Zürcher & Burkart, 2017).

Microchiropteran bats are a group of highly social mammals that persist in environments where light is limited and visual cues are constrained (Barclay & Jacobs, 2023; Guo et al., 2023; Neuweiler, 2000; Wang et al., 2025). In addition to emitting echolocation calls for orientation and prey detection, bats produce structurally complex communication calls that mediate social information transfer (Fenton et al., 2016; Kanwal, 2009). Many bat species also occupy broad geographic ranges and are structured into multiple distinct populations (Gillam & Fenton, 2016). This combination of sociality, acoustic complexity, broad distribution, and population differentiation makes bats a powerful system for investigating acoustic divergence and the evolutionary forces underlying vocal diversification.

A substantial body of research has examined acoustic divergence in bats, with a predominant focus on echolocation calls in sympatric and allopatric populations. For example, variation in peak frequency among allopatric populations of Rhinolophus pusillus and Myotis davidii suggested that divergence in echolocation calls may have been shaped by ecological selection and local adaptation (Jiang et al., 2010; 2013). In Rhinolophus cornutus pumilus, divergence in resting frequency was significantly associated with both genetic and geographic distances, indicating that acoustic differences may have arisen through random cultural drift, consistent with the maternal transmission hypothesis (Yoshino et al., 2008). Nevertheless, divergence in bat communication calls has received far less attention.

Geographic differentiation in communication calls may be governed by processes distinct from those influencing echolocation, given the strong dependence of bats on acoustic signaling and the marked functional differences between social and echolocation calls (Kanwal, 2009; Wilkinson, 2003). In many bat species, the repertoire of social calls is also more diverse than the echolocation repertoire (Gadziola et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2019; 2024). Existing work has largely been limited to group-specific calls in Phyllostomus hastatus (Boughman, 1997), geographic and individual variation in Saccopteryx bilineata (Davidson & Wilkinson, 2002), and geographic variation in a single syllable type of Phyllostomus discolor (Esser & Schubert, 1998). Across bats as a whole, studies addressing either diversity of communication-call syllable types or drivers of acoustic divergence across broad geographic scales remain scarce, especially in relation to dialect structure. Progress in this area is important for clarifying the pivotal role of acoustic signals in social and evolutionary divergence and for advancing understanding of mechanisms that contribute to speciation (Henry et al., 2015; Trudgill, 2012).

The greater horseshoe bat (Rhinolophus nippon) produces structurally complex communication calls composed of diverse syllable types (Zhang et al., 2019; 2024). In China, this species is widely distributed and is partitioned into Northeast, Central-East, and Southwest lineages, with the Qinling Mountains acting as a major barrier that restricts gene flow among regions (Flanders et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2013). Previous work on geographic variation in echolocation calls suggested that both cultural drift and ecological selection have played important roles in shaping regional divergence in acoustic traits. However, whether communication calls in this species exhibit geographic acoustic divergence across populations remains unknown, and the extent to which genetic distance, geographic distance, and geographic barriers contribute to the emergence and maintenance of such divergence also remains poorly resolved.

In the present study, acoustic variation in communication calls of greater horseshoe bats was examined across nine populations from three regions of China to determine whether vocal traits exhibit large-scale geographic structuring. Following characterization of acoustic divergence, potential evolutionary drivers were further evaluated. It was hypothesized that communication calls would exhibit dialect-like divergence among populations and broader geographic variation among regions, and further that the driving forces associated with divergence in communication calls would differ from those implicated in echolocation-call variation. To test these predictions, relationships between acoustic distance and morphological, genetic, and ecological factors were analyzed to identify forces associated with divergence in communication calls of greater horseshoe bats. Clarifying acoustic divergence in this system will advance current understanding of animal communication, conspecific recognition, and mechanisms contributing to speciation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sampling and populations

Sampling and acoustic recording were conducted in nine populations across three regions (Northeast, Central-East, and Southwest) in mainland China between June and August of 2012 and 2013 (Figure 1; Supplementary Table S1). A total of 127 greater horseshoe bats (39 males and 88 females) were captured at cave entrances using mist nets after sunset. Forearm length was measured using a digital vernier caliper (accuracy±0.01 mm). Geographic coordinates for each site were recorded using a GPS device (eTrex Vista, China), and pairwise geographic distances among populations were subsequently calculated.

Sample locations of nine populations across three regions in China

Populations were grouped based on molecular phylogeny of greater horseshoe bats. Localities correspond to codes given in Supplementary Table S1.

Recording and analysis of communication calls

A temporary field laboratory was established near each sampling site for recording communication calls. Vocalizations and associated behaviors were recorded using an ultrasonic acquisition system (UltraSoundGate 116, Avisoft Bioacoustics, Germany; sampling rate 250 kHz, 16-bit resolution) in combination with an infrared closed-circuit camera system (HDR-CX760E, Sony Corporation, Japan). Audio and video recordings were obtained simultaneously and stored for subsequent analysis. During each recording session, 3–5 individuals were randomly selected from the captured population and transferred to an observation cage (80 cm long×20 cm wide×20 cm high) with free access to mealworms and water. Communication calls were recorded daily following acclimation to the environment and feeding conditions. Recording sessions extended from 2 h before sunset to 0600h the following morning. Each trial lasted 6–8 days, allowing collection of a sufficient number of communication calls from each group. Recording periods included peak activity phases when bats exited and returned to roosts, facilitating acquisition of communication calls. All experimental individuals were adults that had completed lactation. Colored bird bands (Porzana, UK) were used for individual identification, with males banded on the left forearm and females on the right, enabling sex determination based on band position. Each group required approximately one week for acclimatization, followed by one week of recording. Most individuals were maintained under experimental conditions for approximately two weeks and were subsequently released at the site of capture.

Recorded calls were analyzed using the Avisoft-SASLab Pro 5.1 sound analysis system (Avisoft Bioacoustics, Germany). Syllable classification followed criteria outlined by Kanwal et al. (1994) and Ma et al. (2006). For each location, an average of 30 syllables per type (30±18) was selected randomly for analysis. Eight spectral-temporal parameters were measured for each syllable: duration, peak frequency, minimum frequency, maximum frequency, lower quartile, mean frequency, upper quartile, and bandwidth. Principal component analysis (PCA) and multidimensional scaling (MDS) were applied to characterize acoustic structure. PCA was used to reduce dimensionality and extract principal components (PCs) from correlated acoustic variables. MDS was used as an independent approach to quantify acoustic distances among syllable types. Model fit was evaluated using the stress value, and results were visualized as contour plots generated with Systat (Point Richmond, USA). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v.21 (IBM, USA).

Syllable types shared across the nine populations were further analyzed using nested analysis of variance (ANOVA) and multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) to assess geographic variation among regions and populations (Boughman, 1997; Davidson & Wilkinson, 2002; Jiang et al., 2013). Variation in syllables was partitioned across three hierarchical levels: region, population, and syllable. Individual-level variation could not be resolved because signal sources could not be unambiguously assigned during recording. Variability within a given syllable type, arising from differences among individuals or repeated emissions, was therefore treated as random error. Because the analysis focused on regional- and population-level patterns, this treatment had negligible impact on inference. Nested MANOVA based on the eight spectral-temporal parameters was used to evaluate overall differences in syllable repertoires among populations. Nested ANOVA was conducted for each parameter within each syllable type to assess population-level differences. The proportion of variance explained at regional, population, and syllable levels was then calculated. Given that different syllable types may differ in spectral-temporal structure and functional roles, responses to selective pressures may also vary (Armbruster & Schwaegerle, 1996; Baker, 2011). Nested ANOVA and MANOVA were performed using Statistica v.10.0 (Statsoft, Tulsa, USA).

Collection and analysis of ecological and genetic factors

Climatic variables were used to characterize environmental conditions at each sampling site and to evaluate associations between vocal production and local climate. Climate data for each sampling site from 1980 to 2010 were obtained using the RNCEP package in R and included mean annual temperature (MAT), mean annual relative humidity (MARH), mean annual precipitation (MAP), mean annual air pressure (MAAP), and mean annual wind velocity (MAWV). Euclidean dissimilarity matrices were then constructed to quantify climatic differences among sampling populations.

Both mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) and microsatellite data were analyzed to assess genetic contributions to the origin and maintenance of dialect structure. Samples for genetic analysis were collected from wing membranes (plagiopatagium) using 3 mm biopsy punches. Previous work has shown that 3 mm perforations in bat wings typically heal within approximately 4 weeks (Wilmer & Barratt, 1996). Procedures for tissue collection and genetic analysis are provided in the Supplementary Materials as outlined in previous study (Sun et al., 2013).

Acoustic Mahalanobis distances among syllable types in the nine populations were calculated to generate acoustic distance matrices and to determine whether geographic variation in communication calls of greater horseshoe bats was associated with geographic, genetic, climatic, and morphological distances. PCA was first performed on eight acoustic parameters for each of the 12 shared syllable types, yielding 24 PCs. These components were then used to calculate acoustic Mahalanobis distances among the nine populations. Morphological distance matrices were derived from dissimilarity matrices based on group mean forearm length for each population pair. Genetic distances were calculated using Slatkin linearized FST, expressed as FST/(1–FST). Geographic distances among populations were calculated using Geographic Distance Matrix Generator (v.1.2.2) from latitude and longitude coordinates for each site. To assess the contributions of these factors to the origin and maintenance of geographic acoustic divergence, acoustic distance matrices were compared with morphological, geographic, and genetic distance matrices using nonparametric Mantel tests and partial Mantel tests. These tests were conducted in PASSaGE v.2 (Arizona State University, USA), with significance assessed using 10 000 permutations. Sequential Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple testing with an overall significance threshold of 0.05.

Causal modeling was further conducted to reduce autocorrelation among predictor variables and to clarify relationships between candidate drivers and acoustic divergence. Five variables were included: geographic distance (D), presence of a geographic barrier (B), acoustic Mahalanobis distance (A), nuclear DNA (nDNA) genetic distance (Gn), and mtDNA genetic distance (Gm). Relationships of geographic distance, geographic barriers, and acoustic distance with the two genetic distance measures were then examined. For each genetic distance type, all possible models incorporating the candidate drivers were tested, and a model was considered supported only when all statistical expectations were satisfied. Because the objective was to evaluate effects of each factor on dialect differentiation, acoustic distance was retained in subsequent analyses when absent from an initial model. All potential causal relationships were then investigated, except those in which geographic distance or geographic barriers were treated as dependent variables, as these factors are not influenced by acoustic or genetic distance. Support for organizational models was assessed using Mantel and partial Mantel tests. Analyses were conducted across all nine populations for both acoustic and genetic datasets.

The impact of geographic isolation on acoustic variation was also examined in greater detail. Four of the nine populations were located near the Qinling Mountains (Figure 1). Notably, populations GS and SXi2 were the closest in straight-line distance, yet were separated by the Qinling Mountains. A similar relationship was observed for populations SXi1 and SXi3. The presence or absence of at least one barrier between population pairs was therefore coded in a binary matrix, with 1 indicating presence and 0 indicating absence. Shared syllable types from the four populations located on opposite sides of the Qinling Mountains were then analyzed separately. Previous studies have shown that centroid distance in signal space provides a robust measure of acoustic similarity (Boughman, 1998; Knörnschild et al., 2010; 2012b; Knörnschild et al., 2007). Within a multidimensional signal space defined by the eight acoustic parameters of shared syllables across the nine populations, Mahalanobis distances were calculated between centroids for each syllable type in the four populations near the Qinling Mountains. MANOVA was then used to compare Mahalanobis distances for each syllable type among population pairs located on either the same or opposite sides of the mountains and belonging to either the same or different genetic regions. Finally, hierarchical clustering based on complete linkage was performed using acoustic Mahalanobis distances derived from 12 syllable types across the nine populations to delineate dialect regions.

RESULTS

Acoustic features and classification of syllable types

Acoustic parameters from a total of 80 000 min of communication-call recordings were analyzed, and 2 160 syllables emitted by 127 greater horseshoe bats from nine populations were measured and classified. Based on spectral-temporal features, calls were classified into three fundamental categories: noise-burst (NB), constant-frequency (CF), and frequency-modulated (FM). On the basis of spectrogram structure and acoustic parameters, these calls were further resolved into 43 syllable types, comprising 20 simple syllable types and 23 composite syllable types (Figure 2). Simple syllables consisted of a single fundamental element, such as an isolated CF, FM, or NB segment (Figure 2A), whereas composite syllables comprised two or more fundamental elements combined without an intervening interval, with each element representing a simple CF, NB, or FM segment (Figure 2B). Acoustic parameters for each syllable type are shown in Supplementary Tables S2, S3. Spectral-temporal characteristics of each syllable type and all syllable-type abbreviations are provided in Supplementary Material S2.

Syllable types in social vocalizations of greater horseshoe bats

A: Simple syllables. B: Composite syllables. Asterisks represent syllable types shared across all populations.

The influence of the classification framework on differentiation among syllable types, together with the acoustic parameters most strongly associated with this variation, was first examined using two independent analytical approaches, PCA and MDS. PCA was conducted on a multivariate dataset comprising eight acoustic parameters to visualize relative dispersion of syllable types in two-dimensional reduced space (Figure 3). Distribution of syllable types along PC1 was primarily associated with a combination of several frequency-related variables, including peak frequency, minimum frequency, and maximum frequency. Separation along PC2 and PC3 was primarily associated with bandwidth and duration, respectively. The first two PCs accounted for approximately 86.7% of total variation, while the first three PCs together explained about 99.27% of total variation. Although the 20 simple syllable types showed partial overlap in the two-dimensional space defined by PC1 and PC2, inclusion of PC3 revealed substantially greater separation among categories.

Bivariate 75% confidence ellipses based on principal component scores of different syllable types

Major axes were defined by unbiased sample standard deviations along each axis, and ellipse orientation was determined by the sample Pearson correlation between factors plotted on X and Y axes. Orientation, overlap, and size of ellipses reflect intergroup separation and inherent variation within each syllable type as represented in multidimensional space.

Because PCA alone does not provide direct estimates of pairwise intergroup distance, mean values of each acoustic parameter for all 20 simple syllable types were used to determine maximal distances among syllable types. These values were then used to generate a three-dimensional MDS plot (Figure 4), which further resolved categories positioned close together in the first two dimensions. The MDS model yielded a stress value of 0.043, indicating good fit to the data (Kruskal, 1964). NB syllables were distributed primarily in the upper part of the plot, FM syllables occupied intermediate positions, and CF syllables were concentrated in the lower part. Taken together, results from both PCA and MDS support the validity of the syllable classification framework.

Variations in acoustic parameters across three levels

Based on quantitative syllable classification, 12 syllable types shared across the nine populations were selected for dialect analysis. These included 11 simple syllable types (BNBl, aBNB, SCF, LCF, AFMs, AFM, UFM, cUFM, DFM, cDFM, and SF) and one composite syllable type (NB-SFM). Visual differences in spectral-temporal structure were evident in spectrogram comparisons of these 12 syllable types (Figure 5; Supplementary Table S4). For example, syllables from Northeast populations generally exhibited much lower frequencies than those from Central-East and Southwest populations. When all 12 syllable types were analyzed as a whole, acoustic parameters differed significantly among populations and among regions (Table 1).

Variation in acoustic characteristics of shared syllable types

Nested ANOVA and MANOVA incorporating eight acoustic parameters were then applied across three hierarchical levels to determine whether each syllable type exhibited acoustic variation. Results indicated that, for the 12 shared syllable types, most parameters varied among populations (Table 2). Nested MANOVA further demonstrated that variation was also present at the regional level (Table 3). Total variation was then standardized to 100% and partitioned among the three hierarchical levels defined above. Most variation was attributable to random error, corresponding to the syllable level (Supplementary Table S5). However, several parameters in specific syllable types showed population-level differences, including duration in aBNB, AFMs, UFM, and cDFM, peak frequency, mean frequency, and maximum frequency in DFM and cDFM, and lower quartile in LCF. These findings indicate that acoustic variation in syllables occurred across multiple levels, including regions, populations, and individuals. Accordingly, both dialect-like structure and broader geographic variation were expressed in patterns that varied among populations and regions.

Determinants of acoustic divergence among individual syllable types

Mantel and partial Mantel tests were used to evaluate whether dialect-like variation was associated with environmental and ecological factors, including climate, morphology, geographic distance, and genetic divergence. The results of mtDNA and microsatellite analyses are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Mantel tests showed no significant correlation between variation in syllable types and either climatic or morphological factors (Supplementary Table S6). Among the 12 shared syllable types, acoustic variation in AFM, BNBl, LCF, SCF, SFM, and UFM was positively associated with geographic distance (P≤0.05). Variation in LCF, SCF, and UFM was also significantly and positively correlated with mtDNA genetic distance (P≤0.029). In contrast, variation in cUFM, NB-SFM, and SFM showed significant positive correlations with nDNA genetic distance (all P≤0.009). Partial Mantel tests further showed that, after controlling for nDNA and mtDNA genetic distance, variation in five syllable types (AFM, BNBl, LCF, SCF, and UFM) remained significantly and positively associated with geographic distance (P≤0.05; Supplementary Table S7). No correlation was detected between acoustic distance and mtDNA genetic distance once the effect of geographic distance was removed. In contrast, variation in cUFM, NB-SFM, and SFM remained significantly and positively associated with nDNA genetic distance, regardless of whether geographic distance was controlled (Mantel test: P≤0.009, partial Mantel test: P≤0.023).

According to the above results, different syllable types were affected by distinct driving factors. Based on previously reported relationships between dialect variation, geographic distance, and genetic distance (Chen et al., 2009; Deecke et al., 2000; Irwin et al., 2008; Van Schaik et al., 2003; Whiten, 2019), syllable types were grouped into four categories (Table 4). First, aBNB, AFMs, DFM, and cDFM showed no significant correlation with either geographic or genetic distance, suggesting these syllable types may be shaped primarily by cultural selection. Second, LCF, SCF, SFM, and UFM were significantly associated with both geographic and genetic distance, suggesting that these four syllable types were mainly affected by stochastic processes. Thirdly, AFM and BNBl were significantly correlated with geographic distance but not with genetic distance, suggesting a predominant role for cultural drift. Fourth, cUFM and NB-SFM were significantly associated with genetic distance but not with geographic distance, suggesting that these two syllable types may be primarily affected by genetic drift.

Analysis of factors driving acoustic divergence at the level of individual syllable type

Determinants of acoustic divergence across the full syllable repertoire

As patterns of association were not uniform across individual syllable types, acoustic variation was subsequently analyzed using all 12 shared syllable types as an integrated dataset. Two major factors were extracted from the acoustic parameters of every syllable type using factor analysis, and the extracted factors for all syllable types within each population were then combined for population-level analysis. This approach more closely reflects natural vocal production, because bat syllables are typically emitted in combinations that form phrases or complex vocal sequences rather than occurring in isolation. When acoustic distances derived from the 12 syllable types were analyzed as a whole, significant correlations were detected between acoustic distance and geographic distance, as well as between acoustic distance and mtDNA genetic distance (Figure 6). Thus, acoustic divergence among populations increased with both geographic separation and mtDNA genetic differentiation. Mantel tests further showed that acoustic distance was not significantly associated with differences in forearm length (r=−0.32, P=0.11) or climatic distance (r=0.44, P=0.07), indicating that morphology and climate did not contribute detectably to dialect formation or maintenance. This pattern is consistent with results obtained from analyses of individual syllable types. Acoustic distance was significantly correlated with geographic distance (r=0.65, P=0.029), mtDNA genetic distance (r=0.56, P=0.02), and presence of a geographic barrier (r=0.58, P=0.0001), but not with nDNA genetic distance (r=0.25, P=0.28).

To identify the causal relationships among these factors and dialect divergence, acoustic distance (A), geographic distance (D), presence of a geographic barrier (B), nDNA genetic distance (Gn), and mtDNA genetic distance (Gm) were included in causal modeling analysis. Among candidate models involving Gm, only one model containing Gm, A, and D satisfied all statistical expectations (Supplementary Table S8). Among candidate models involving Gn, only one model containing A, B, and Gn was similarly supported (Supplementary Table S9). Geographic distance and presence of geographic barriers exerted direct effects on both acoustic divergence and genetic distance (Table 5; Supplementary Tables S10, S11). Collectively, these results indicate that dialect-like variation may be shaped, in part, by cultural drift and that both the emergence and persistence of dialect structure may also be affected by geographic isolation.

Effect of geographic barriers and acoustic divergence zones

Four populations (GS, SXi1, SXi2, and SXi3) were located near the Qinling Mountains. Among these, GS belonged to the Southwest group, while the other three populations belonged to the Central-East group. Acoustic distances between populations separated by the mountain barrier (GS vs. SXi2, GS vs. SXi3, SXi1 vs. SXi2, and SXi1 vs. SXi3) were significantly greater than those between populations on the same side of the barrier (GS vs. SXi1, and SXi2 vs. SXi3) (F=5.15, P=0.026, Figure 7A). Consistent with this pattern, Mantel tests revealed a significant correlation between acoustic distance and the presence of a geographic barrier (r=0.58, P=0.0001). Acoustic distances were also compared between population pairs assigned to the same genetic region and those assigned to different genetic regions using MANOVA. No significant differences were detected between populations in different regions (GS vs. SXi1, GS vs. SXi2, and GS vs. SXi3) and populations in the same region (SXi1 vs. SXi2, SXi1 vs. SXi3, and SXi2 vs. SXi3) (F=2.52, P=0.117, Figure 7B), indicating that acoustic divergence in this comparison was not explained by genetic differentiation.

Acoustic divergence zones were further delineated using hierarchical cluster analysis based on the complete linkage algorithm. The nine populations were resolved into two main clusters. One cluster comprised the two Northeast populations (JL1 and JL2), whereas the second cluster contained the remaining seven populations from the Central-East and Southwest regions (Figure 8). Within the latter cluster, SXi1 and GS, both located on the south side of the Qinling Mountains, grouped together as a distinct subgroup, while the remaining five populations, located on the north side of the Qinling Mountains within the Central-East region, formed a separate subgroup. On this basis, three dialect regions were identified: Northeast, Central-East, and Southwest. The Qinling Mountains defined the boundary between the Central-East and Southwest dialect regions, suggesting an important role for geographic barriers in the emergence and maintenance of acoustic divergence.

DISCUSSION

Geographic and dialect-like variation in bat social calls

This study addressed three closely related questions: the extent of diversity in syllable structure within communication calls of greater horseshoe bats, the factors associated with acoustic divergence, and the presence of dialect structure across populations. Analysis of communication calls from nine populations distributed across three genetically isolated regions identified 43 syllable types. Among these, 12 syllable types were shared across populations, and their acoustic parameters differed markedly at regional, population, and syllable levels. These findings demonstrate substantial geographic variation in communication calls of greater horseshoe bats. Moreover, dialect-like vocal variation could be readily resolved into distinct groups corresponding to broad and largely exclusive geographic regions.

Sun et al. (2013) identified three monophyletic haplogroups of greater horseshoe bats in China, designated as Northeast, Central-East, and Southwest, with gene flow occurring within groups but not among them. The populations examined in the present study belonged to these three groups, and the results provide evidence that communication-call dialects exist in greater horseshoe bats. Acoustic variation among syllables within the Central-East region is appropriately described here as dialect variation because interbreeding among bats in this region has been demonstrated (Flanders et al., 2011; Sun et al., 2013). In contrast, acoustic variation among populations from more distant regions is better regarded as geographic variation, as bats from these regions do not interbreed.

Acoustic divergence and dialect structure have been previously documented in birds (Hamao et al., 2016; Slabbekoorn & Smith, 2002) and in several other mammals (Barker et al., 2021; Schlenker et al., 2014). Songbirds represent one of the most extensively studied systems for vocal divergence. Early studies showed that dialects occurred in dominant song types among bird populations (Marler & Tamura, 1962). Subsequent work further demonstrated dialect differentiation at the level of notes, song structure, and even newly derived phrases that emerged within local song traditions (Henry et al., 2015; Pipek et al., 2018; Slabbekoorn et al., 2003). Similarly, the present study identified geographic divergence in syllable acoustic parameters among bat populations. Analysis of all syllable types shared among populations as an integrated dataset likewise indicated the existence of dialects. Multiple studies have shown that ecological selection, sexual selection, and drift can all contribute to divergence in acoustic signals across birds and other animals (Henry et al., 2015; Wilkins et al., 2013). The present results suggest that cultural drift may be a dominant driver of signal divergence in greater horseshoe bats, providing a mammalian example for understanding the evolutionary processes contributing to population differentiation and speciation.

Geographic divergences in communication and echolocation calls of bats

Although vocal dialects have been demonstrated in several bat species, most previous studies have focused primarily on echolocation calls or on a single syllable type expressed in a specific social context (Montero et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2020). For example, (Esser & Schubert, 1998) described geographic variation in directive calls of female Phyllostomus discolor, providing evidence for vocal dialect structure. (Davidson & Wilkinson, 2002) reported significant divergence in reversed V-shaped syllables among four populations of Saccopteryx bilineata. Lin et al. (2022) further examined vocal divergence in five simple syllable types across nine populations of the Himalayan leaf-nosed bat and likewise identified geographic differences in acoustic parameters of communication-call syllables. In this respect, the present findings are consistent with previous work, as geographic divergence across multiple syllable types was detected in both Himalayan leaf-nosed bats and greater horseshoe bats. In both systems, acoustic distance was not significantly associated with morphological distance among populations. However, syllabic variation in Himalayan leaf-nosed bats indicated diffuse boundaries across broad geographic regions (Lin et al., 2016), whereas clear acoustic differentiation across three geographic regions was detected here in greater horseshoe bats. This contrast may reflect interspecific differences in signal diversity and vocal complexity. Greater horseshoe bats exhibited a broader vocal repertoire than Himalayan leaf-nosed bats (43 vs. 35 syllable types). In addition, a larger number and greater structural complexity of shared syllable types were identified among greater horseshoe bat populations than among Himalayan leaf-nosed bat populations (11 simple syllables and one composite syllable vs. five simple syllables). Such acoustic variation may become established through learning biases or cultural drift (Whiten, 2019; Williams & Lachlan, 2022).

Geographic divergence has been reported in both echolocation and communication calls across bat populations (Davidson & Wilkinson, 2002; Lin et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2013; Xie et al., 2017). In greater horseshoe bats, Sun et al. (2013) demonstrated significant geographic variation in echolocation calls and proposed that cultural drift may be an important force shaping this pattern. Similar results have also been reported in other bat species (Lin et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2017). By integrating a broader range of syllable types and examining multiple candidate drivers, the present study identified dialect structure in communication calls of greater horseshoe bats. Acoustic divergence across all shared syllable types was directly associated with geographic factors rather than genetic factors, supporting an important role for cultural drift. No significant correlations were detected between acoustic divergence in communication calls and either ecological or morphological factors. Previous studies have also shown that echolocation calls can function in social communication in bats (Jones, 2008; Knörnschild et al., 2012a; Voigt-Heucke et al., 2010). This functional overlap between echolocation and communication calls may expose both call categories to similar selective pressures.

Drivers of acoustic divergence

Acoustic divergence has long been recognized as a process with major implications for reproductive isolation (Mendelson & Shaw, 2012; Price, 2007; Slabbekoorn & Smith, 2002). Clarifying how acoustic divergence arises and persists is therefore essential for understanding the generation of biodiversity (Irwin et al., 2008). The present results showed no significant associations between acoustic divergence and either climatic or morphological factors, indicating that ecological selection does not explain the observed patterns of vocal differentiation. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that neither ecological nor morphological variables account for social call variation in Himalayan leaf-nosed bats (Lin et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2020). At the level of individual syllable types, different forms of vocal variation were associated with different driving factors, enabling the division of syllable types into four categories. This pattern suggests that determinants of acoustic divergence may depend, at least in part, on the specific social function of each syllable type, despite their shared role in communication. Distinct patterns of variation may reflect differences in selective pressures acting on various call types with different functions (Forstmeier et al., 2009). Similar results emerged from a previous study on social calls in 31 bat species, in which different factors showed different influences on acoustic parameters and may therefore have contributed differently to speciation (Luo et al., 2017). Although the function or meaning of each syllable type remains difficult to resolve across all behavioral contexts, future studies that integrate syllable function with geographic variation should provide deeper insight into the origins and diversification of social calls.

Shared syllable types were also analyzed collectively to identify broader drivers of acoustic divergence. Treating all shared syllable types as an integrated dataset may be more appropriate because bat communication calls are typically produced as phrases or sequences, and geographic variation in communication signals may arise in these combinations of different syllable types rather than in isolated syllables alone (Bohn et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2024). Under this framework, acoustic distance was significantly associated with geographic distance and mtDNA genetic distance but not with nDNA genetic distance. As mtDNA is maternally inherited, this pattern suggests that acoustic divergence may be influenced by maternal gene flow. Similar relationships have been reported for contact calls in leaf-roosting bats (Montero et al., 2018) and alarm calls in greenish warblers (Irwin et al., 2008). Together, these observations indicate that geographic and genetic structure can explain acoustic variation, implicating stochastic processes as important drivers of signal divergence.

Given that both acoustic and genetic data were structured in geographic space, spatial autocorrelation could violate assumptions of statistical independence, leading to spurious correlations among patterns of variation (Legendre, 1993). To address this issue, causal modeling was applied to the 12 shared syllable types analyzed as a whole. Results showed that two models were fully supported by all statistical expectations: one including Gm, A, and D, and a second including Gn, A, and B. The supported models indicated that geographic distance and presence of a geographic barrier influenced both mtDNA and nDNA genetic distance as well as acoustic distance, whereas acoustic distance was not directly affected by either mtDNA or nDNA genetic distance. Geographic distance and geographic barriers therefore emerged as direct drivers of the origin and maintenance of acoustic divergence in greater horseshoe bats. These findings provide support for the cultural drift hypothesis (Koetz et al., 2007; Whiten, 2019; Yoshino et al., 2008).

Cultural drift refers to random changes in the composition of culturally acquired and socially transmitted signals within a population, arising from stochastic differences in which variants are learned and reproduced (Wilkins et al., 2013). Vocal learning is generally defined as modification of vocal output through auditory experience (Janik & Knörnschild, 2021; Prat et al., 2017). As a key innovation in the evolution of language, vocal learning has been documented in humans, primates, and several other animal groups, including birds, cetaceans, seals, and elephants (Doupe & Kuhl, 1999; Janik & Slater, 1997; Poole et al., 2005; Tyack, 2006; Wilbrecht & Nottebohm, 2003). Evidence from several bat species supports a role for vocal learning in mediating signal convergence within social groups (Knörnschild, 2014; Prat et al., 2017). Based on these observations, it is plausible that greater horseshoe bats also possess vocal learning ability and that dialect structure may arise through population-level learning of characteristic syllable patterns by group members (Deecke et al., 2000; Prat et al., 2017).

Effect of geographic barriers

The presence of geographic barriers such as mountains, oceans, and fragmented habitats can restrict gene flow among populations and reduce opportunities for acoustic exchange among individuals. Therefore, geographic isolation plays an important role in dialect formation (Pröhl et al., 2007; Risch et al., 2007). Within a given lineage region, the presence of a geographic barrier may be especially important in shaping dialect divergence. Here, acoustic variation among four populations (GS, SXi1, SXi2, and SXi3) located on either side of the Qinling Mountains supported this interpretation. Although SXi1 belonged to the Central-East region and GS belonged to the Southwest region, their acoustic signals were more similar because both populations occurred on the same side of the Qinling Mountains. In contrast, although SXi1, SXi2, and SXi3 all belonged to the Central-East region, the acoustic signals of SXi1 differed significantly from those of SXi2 and SXi3 because these populations were separated by the mountain barrier. Comparable patterns have been reported in white-crowned sparrows (Zonotrichia leucophrys oriantha), in which populations on the eastern slopes of the Rocky Mountains produce songs that differ markedly from those of conspecific populations on the western slopes (Baptista & King, 1980). Rivers can also function as geographic barriers. In speckled ground squirrels (Spermophilus suslicus), pronounced differences in call durations exist between eastern and western populations separated by the Dnieper River (Matrosova et al., 2016). However, the role of geographic barriers as drivers of acoustic divergence has not yet been examined directly.

Populations that are genetically similar may nevertheless differ acoustically when geographic barriers restrict gene flow. In the bumblebee bat (Craseonycteris thonglongyai), geographic distance has been shown to play a primary role in limiting gene flow, thereby creating conditions favorable for divergence in echolocation calls through local adaptation (Puechmaille et al., 2011). Geographic isolation can also increase the fitness advantage of lineage-specific trait preference, potentially accelerating divergence between lineages (Hoskin et al., 2005). In the present study, causal modeling indicated that geographic barriers had direct effects on both genetic and acoustic distance. These findings suggest that geographic barriers are likely to be critical in the emergence and maintenance of acoustic divergence.

CONCLUSIONS

Analysis of communication calls in greater horseshoe bats demonstrated dialect-like variation across nine populations and broader geographic variation among three regions of China. Significant acoustic differences in shared syllable types were associated with distinct evolutionary drivers. When shared syllable types were analyzed as an integrated dataset, acoustic distance was significantly correlated with geographic distance and mtDNA genetic distance, but not with nDNA genetic distance, morphological distance, or climatic factors. Causal modeling further revealed that geographic distance and geographic barriers exerted direct effects on both acoustic divergence and genetic differentiation, whereas acoustic distance was not directly affected by genetic distance. Hierarchical clustering resolved the nine populations into three dialect regions: Northeast, Central-East, and Southwest. Together, these results support cultural drift as a dominant process underlying vocal divergence. This study advances current understanding of animal vocal evolution, acoustic diversification, and the potential contribution of cultural transmission to speciation.
