Impact of Omaveloxolone on Serum Lipids in Patients with Friedreich Ataxia: A 1-Year Real-World Analysis
Authors: Dominic Buchinger, Thomas Scherer, Daniel Dalos, Ivan Milenkovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveFriedreich ataxia is a multi-system neurodegenerative disorder with frequent cardiac and metabolic involvement. Omaveloxolone, the first approved therapy for Friedreich ataxia, improves neurological outcomes through nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 activation and exerts measurable systemic effects. However, its effects on lipid metabolism have not been systematically assessed. The present study aimed to systematically evaluate the longitudinal effects of omaveloxolone on serum lipid parameters in a real-world cohort of patients with Friedreich ataxia over a 12-month observation period.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-center, real-world observational study in adults with genetically confirmed Friedreich ataxia newly treated with omaveloxolone (150 mg/day). Serum lipid parameters, including total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, very low-density-lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, C-reactive protein, apolipoprotein A-I, and apolipoprotein B (ApoB), were measured at baseline and after 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months. Longitudinal changes were analyzed using frequentist and Bayesian linear mixed-effects models.ResultsA total of 17 patients were included in the analysis (13 male [76%]; mean age 36.1 years [range 20–55]; mean disease duration 17.4 years [range 6–33 years]). Total cholesterol increased shortly after treatment initiation and remained elevated throughout the follow-up period, driven almost entirely by a sustained rise in low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (+ 35–50 mg/dL) and ApoB (+ 20–25 mg/dL) levels, whereas high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, apolipoprotein A-I, very low-density-lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglyceride levels remained stable. Low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol and ApoB levels were strongly correlated at all timepoints (r = 0.83–0.95). The total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol ratio increased by approximately one unit and persisted throughout the follow-up period.ConclusionsIn this real-world Friedreich ataxia cohort, omaveloxolone treatment was associated with a sustained shift toward a more atherogenic lipid profile, driven almost entirely by increased low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol and ApoB levels. This pattern is consistent with impaired hepatic clearance of ApoB-containing particles rather than increased production, supporting routine lipid monitoring and further evaluation of long-term cardiovascular risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40263-026-01312-w.

Key Points

In a real-world cohort of 17 patients with Friedreich ataxia observed over 12 months, omaveloxolone treatment was associated with a sustained increase in total cholesterol, predominantly driven by rising low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol and apolipoprotein B-containing lipoproteins.In contrast, high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol, apolipoprotein A-I, very-low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglyceride levels remained stable, suggesting reduced hepatic clearance of atherogenic lipid particles rather than increased lipid production.These findings highlight lipid metabolism as a relevant systemic effect of long-term omaveloxolone therapy and support the incorporation of routine lipid monitoring into clinical follow-up examinations.

Introduction

Friedreich ataxia (FRDA) is a rare, autosomal, recessive, neurodegenerative disorder most commonly caused by biallelic intronic guanine-adenine-adenine (GAA) repeat expansions in the FXN gene, resulting in frataxin deficiency and impaired mitochondrial iron–sulfur cluster biogenesis [1]. The resulting mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress lead to progressive neurological impairment, classically manifesting as sensory and cerebellar ataxia, dysarthria, and areflexia [2]. Beyond neurological involvement, FRDA consistently involves prominent heart, skeletal, and metabolic involvement, resulting in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, skeletal deformities, insulin resistance, and diabetes mellitus [3].

In addition to these established features, evidence from clinical and experimental studies suggests that lipid pathways may be affected in FRDA. Early clinical investigations reported abnormalities in circulating lipid-related parameters, including altered lipase activity, changes in erythrocyte phospholipid fatty acid composition, and elevated plasma cholesteryl sulfate levels in subsets of patients [4–6]. Recent metabolomic and multi-omics studies have strengthened this concept, demonstrating disease-associated lipid signatures in patient-derived skin fibroblasts and identifying specific sphingolipid species, including very long-chain ceramides, as candidate plasma biomarkers associated with FRDA [7]. Consistent with these findings, tissue-based analyses have revealed alterations in phospholipid biosynthetic pathways in the FRDA brain, indicating that frataxin deficiency affects lipid metabolism at multiple biological levels [8].

Omaveloxolone, a potent activator of the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway, is the first and only approved therapy for FRDA, with demonstrated clinical efficacy in slowing the progression of neurological symptoms [9]. By enhancing antioxidant responses and modulating potential mitochondrial dysfunction, omaveloxolone targets key downstream consequences of frataxin deficiency. Clinical trial data from the MOXIe program and subsequent real-world experience have established a generally favorable safety profile, while also highlighting systemic treatment effects, most notably transient elevations in liver enzymes and dyslipidemia [9–12]. Initial studies have suggested that omaveloxolone treatment may be associated with increased low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) levels, prompting the European Medicines Agency to recommend routine lipid monitoring [13]. However, lipid outcomes and the mechanisms underlying these changes have not been a consistent focus of published studies to date. In this context, the present study aimed to characterize longitudinal changes in routine serum lipid parameters in patients with FRDA receiving omaveloxolone over a 1-year period in a real-world setting.

Methods

Patient Characteristics

This real-world, single-center, retrospective, observational cohort study was conducted between March 2024 and August 2025 at the Department of Neurology, Medical University of Vienna, following European Medicines Agency approval of omaveloxolone in February 2024. Each patient was followed for a maximum of 12 months after treatment initiation. The study included patients with FRDA who were treated with omaveloxolone as part of their routine clinical care. All patients were treatment naive with respect to omaveloxolone at study entry. Evaluation and treatment initiation were performed by an experienced board-certified movement disorder specialist. Clinical and laboratory data were obtained from routine clinical follow-ups. Data collection was performed by a structured retrospective chart review of local electronic medical records, including clinical assessments, laboratory results, and medication records. Demographic data (age, sex), genotype (GAA repeat length), disease duration, and concomitant medications with potential influence on lipid metabolism, including lipid-lowering agents such as statins, ezetimibe, fibrates, and PCSK9 inhibitors, were extracted from medical records. This study was approved by the local ethics committee of the Medical University of Vienna (approval no. 1995/2025).

All patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a genetically confirmed diagnosis of FRDA who received omaveloxolone in clinical practice and had at least 3 months of treatment and follow-ups were included in this study. All patients had an established diagnosis of FRDA prior to treatment initiation, none was newly diagnosed. Patients were not eligible for therapy if they had a modified Friedreich Ataxia Rating Scale score ≥ 80 at study initiation, severe hepatic impairment (Child–Pugh class C), an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 mL/min, or heart failure with New York Heart Association functional class > III, in accordance with the approved contraindications [13]. The included patients received oral omaveloxolone at a dose of 150 mg once daily, in accordance with standard clinical practice. None of the patients involved had therapy discontinuation or dose reduction due to an increase in liver enzymes [10].

Study Procedures and Laboratory Assessments

Blood samples were collected at baseline (maximum 6–8 weeks prior to therapy initiation) and 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months after treatment initiation. All patients were instructed to fast for a minimum of 8 h prior to blood sampling. Samples were processed within 1 h of venipuncture. At each timepoint, lipid parameters, including total cholesterol (TC), calculated LDL-C, high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides (TGs), were quantified using standardized laboratory methods. Lipid ultracentrifugation was performed to obtain all lipoprotein particles (i.e., low-density and very-low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol [VLDL-C]). In addition, apolipoprotein A-I and apolipoprotein B (ApoB) concentrations were measured, and the TC-to-HDL-C ratio was calculated as a derived index of atherogenicity. C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were measured to assess the potential inflammatory effects on TG and lipid levels. All laboratory analyses were performed at the Department of Laboratory Medicine and Division of Endocrinology, Medical University of Vienna, using validated clinical routine assays.

Statistics

All longitudinal outcomes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models to account for the within-subject correlations across repeated measurements. For each biomarker or clinical parameter, a separate mixed model was specified with the outcome value as the dependent variable, time as a categorical fixed effect (baseline, M1, M2, M3, M6, M12), and a random intercept for each participant was specified. Models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood with the lmer() function from the lme4 package. Fixed-effect coefficients are presented as estimated mean differences from the baseline with corresponding standard errors (SEs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Random-effect variances represent between-subject and residual (within-subject) variability. For descriptive interpretation, variability was categorized as low (coefficient of variation < 15%), moderate (coefficient of variation 15–40%), or high (coefficient of variation > 40%). C-reactive protein was initially modeled using linear mixed-effects models; however, the patient-specific random intercept resulted in a singular fit with negligible between-patient variance. Thus, a fixed-effects linear model with timepoints as the primary predictor was applied. The assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were checked using model residuals, and no violations were observed.

To complement the frequentist analysis, Bayesian linear mixed models with the same structure were fitted using the brms package, which implements Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling in Stan. Weakly informative priors were specified to reflect biologically plausible value ranges in the model. Each Bayesian model was run with four Markov chains (4000 iterations per chain, 2000 warm-up), yielding 8000 post-warm-up samples per model. Convergence was assessed using the potential scale reduction factor (R^= 1.00 for all parameters) and effective sample sizes. Posterior means and 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were summarized for all fixed and random effects. Model-estimated marginal means and uncertainty intervals were computed using the emmeans package and visualized with ggplot2. All statistical tests were performed in R version 4.4.2 (2024-10-31).

Results

A total of 17 patients were included in the analysis (13 male [76%]; mean age 36.1 years [range 20–55 years]). The mean time from symptom onset to study enrolment was 17.4 years (range 6–33 years). Consistent with the known multi-system involvement of FRDA, 13 patients (76%) had established cardiomyopathy and 3 (18%) had diabetes at the time of treatment initiation.

Two patients received lipid-modifying therapy during the observation period. One patient was already receiving stable lipid-lowering treatment prior to omaveloxolone initiation (rosuvastatin 20 mg daily), which remained unchanged throughout the treatment period. The second patient presented with elevated baseline lipid levels and was initially treated with atorvastatin 20 mg, which was discontinued because of myalgia and replaced with ezetimibe 10 mg once daily prior to omaveloxolone initiation. Owing to persistently increasing LDL-C levels, ezetimibe was switched to bempedoic acid 9 months after treatment initiation. First, we investigated the TC levels. Frequentist linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts (baseline as reference) showed a robust and sustained increase in TC levels over the 12-month follow-up period (black lines in Fig. 1a, Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM]). The estimated mean at baseline was 172.9 mg/dL (SE 10.6). Mean differences (±SE) versus baseline were + 35.7 ± 9.3 at M1, + 33.6 ± 9.5 at M2, + 34.6 ± 9.3 at M3, + 41.4 ± 10.3 at M6, and + 47.8 ± 10.7 mg/dL at M12 (all p < 0.001). Between-subject (standard deviation [SD] = 36.9 mg/dL) and within-subject (SD = 26.0 mg/dL) variabilities were moderate. Bayesian mixed-effects models yielded nearly identical estimates: posterior mean at baseline was 173 mg/dL (95% CrI 150–195), with posterior mean increases of + 36 (95% CrI 17–54) at M1, + 33 (95% CrI 14–52) at M2, + 35 (95% CrI 16–53) at M3, + 41 (95% CrI 21–61) at M6, and + 48 (95% CrI 28–69) mg/dL at M12 (blue ribbon and line in Fig. 1a). Between-subject and residual SDs (37 and 26 mg/dL, respectively) closely matched the frequentist results, confirming a credible increase in TC at every timepoint.

Next, we investigated which specific particles were changed. In the mixed-effects model with random intercepts for each participant, LDL-C levels increased significantly over time (black lines in Fig 1b, ESM). The estimated mean LDL-C at baseline was 99.3 mg/dL (SE 10.2). Compared to baseline, the mean differences (± SE) were + 36.8 ± 8.2 mg/dL at M1, + 31.9 ± 8.4 mg/dL at M2, + 37.1 ± 8.2 mg/dL at M3, + 37.8 ± 9.2 mg/dL at M6, and + 47.4 ± 9.5 mg/dL at M12 (all p < 0.001). Between-subject variability (SD = 36.8 mg/dL) and within-subject residual variation (SD = 23.1 mg/dL) were moderate. To complement these findings, a Bayesian mixed model with weakly informative and biologically plausible priors was used. Posterior means (95 % CrI) showed low-density lipoprotein (LDL-C) = 108 mg/dL (95% CrI 89–129) at baseline and similar increases as in the frequentist model (blue ribbon and line in Fig. 1b).

In contrast to LDL-C, HDL-C remained stable over time (Fig. 1c). In the frequentist mixed-effects model, the baseline mean was 48.8 mg/dL (SE 3.2). Mean differences (± SE) versus baseline were − 1.77 ± 2.37 at M1, − 1.33 ± 2.43 at M2, − 2.89 ± 2.37 at M3, − 3.16 ± 2.64 at M6, and − 1.56 ± 2.73 mg/dL at M12 (all p ≥ 0.60). The estimated marginal means at each visit (45.6–47.5 mg/dL) had overlapping 95% CIs with the baseline. Bayesian posterior mean at baseline was 48.3 mg/dL (95% CrI 42.0–54.9), with all posterior contrasts including zero. Together, both analytic frameworks showed no credible change in HDL-C levels across the follow-up period. We calculated the TC-to-HDL-C ratio as an index reflecting atherogenic lipid risk [15]. The baseline mean ratio was 3.83 (SE 0.38); mean differences versus baseline were significantly increased by + 0.99 ± 0.28 at M1 (p < 0.001), + 0.92 ± 0.28 at M2 (p = 0.0016), + 1.02 ± 0.27 at M3 (p < 0.001), + 1.27 ± 0.30 at M6 (p < 0.001), and + 1.04 ± 0.32 at M12 (p = 0.002). Bayesian posterior mean at baseline was 4.11 (95% CrI 3.35–4.87), with posterior mean increases of + 0.59 (95% CrI 0.09–1.08) at M1, + 0.52 (95% CrI 0.01–0.99) at M2, + 0.63 (95% CrI 0.14–1.10) at M3, + 0.81 (95% CrI 0.26–1.35) at M6, and + 0.58 (95% CrI 0.01–1.14) at M12.

We further checked the levels of apolipoproteins, which are used as markers for different lipoprotein particles (Fig. 2). Apolipoprotein A-I levels did not change significantly over time (Fig. 2a). In the mixed-effects model, the baseline mean was 127.7 mg/dL (SE 5.6), and the mean differences versus baseline ranged from − 0.5 to + 9.2 mg/dL (all p ≥ 0.08). Bayesian estimates were congruent, with posterior mean at baseline 128.5 mg/dL (95% CrI 117.8–140.0) and all timepoint contrasts including zero, confirming stable apolipoprotein A-I levels without credible change. In contrast, the ApoB levels increased significantly over 12 months (Fig. 2b). The baseline mean was 91.1 mg/dL (SE 7.3), with mean differences of + 22.3 ± 5.4 at M1, + 19.4 ± 5.8 at M2, + 19.8 ± 5.5 at M3, + 25.3 ± 6.2 at M6, and + 25.6 ± 6.2 mg/dL at M12 (all p < 0.002). Bayesian posterior estimates similarly showed credible increases at all visits (e.g., + 15.9 [6.0–25.6] mg/dL at M1, + 18.1 [6.8–28.7] mg/dL at M12). Low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol and ApoB levels were strongly correlated at all timepoints (r = 0.83–0.95, all p < 0.001), and their relationship remained stable across visits, indicating that ApoB closely tracked LDL-C (Fig. 2c).

To assess whether the increase in LDL-C was caused by increased cholesterol synthesis, we evaluated VLDL-C levels using lipid ultracentrifugation during the follow-up. Very-low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol remained stable, with a baseline mean of 29.7 mg/dL (SE 7.6) and mean differences at all timepoints between − 5.11 and + 1.51 mg/dL (all p ≥ 0.49) [Fig. 3]. Bayesian analysis confirmed no credible changes (data not shown). As TG are the main components of VLDL particles, we analyzed their levels (ESM). In the linear mixed-effects model, no timepoint showed a statistically significant change in TG levels relative to baseline (all p > 0.2), indicating no systematic longitudinal trend. The Bayesian model showed posterior distributions for all timepoint effects centered close to zero, with a wide CrI overlapping with no change. Posterior probabilities for TG increase or decrease were moderate and inconsistent across timepoints, and the probability that changes were clinically negligible (within ±10 mg/dL) was substantial at all follow-up times. Although a transient increase at 6 months was plausible, this effect was not sustained and remained highly uncertain.

As inflammation can influence plasma TG levels, CRP was assessed to determine whether inflammatory fluctuations contributed to longitudinal TG variability. C-reactive protein was analyzed using both frequentist and Bayesian longitudinal models (ESM). In frequentist analyses, the inclusion of a patient-specific random intercept in a linear mixed-effects model resulted in a singular fit due to negligible between-patient variance; therefore, CRP was analyzed using a fixed-effects linear model with timepoints as the main predictor. The estimated CRP values were 0.11 at baseline, 0.17 at month 1, 0.31 at month 2, 0.13 at month 3, 0.19 at month 6, and 0.13 at month 12. No significant differences were observed across timepoints (overall F test, p = 0.68), and all effect estimates were small, with the CI overlapping zero. Bayesian hierarchical models yielded consistent results. Across categorical, continuous, and monotonic time parameterizations, posterior estimates for CRP changes were centered near zero with a narrow CrI, and Bayesian posterior means closely matched frequentist estimates at all timepoints. Between-patient variability was minimal compared with within-patient variability.

Discussion

In this 1-year real-world analysis of patients with FRDA treated with omaveloxolone, we observed a consistent and clinically relevant shift in lipid profiles. Total cholesterol increased early after treatment initiation and remained elevated throughout the follow-up period, an effect almost entirely driven by the increase in LDL-C (35–50 mg/dL over 12 months). This increase was paralleled by an increase in ApoB, whereas HDL-C, apolipoprotein A-I, VLDL-C, and TG levels remained stable, arguing against increased lipoprotein production. Together, this pattern indicates a selective increase in atherogenic ApoB-containing lipoproteins rather than a global alteration in lipid metabolism.

The close association between LDL-C and ApoB levels across all visits provides important insights into the nature of the observed LDL increase. Because each atherogenic lipoprotein particle carries a single ApoB molecule, ApoB serves as a robust marker of particle number [14]. The strong correlation between LDL-C and ApoB indicates that the increase in LDL-C predominantly reflects an increased number of circulating atherogenic particles. The strong correlation between LDL-C and ApoB further corroborates the notion that the observed increase in cholesterol may not be due to the content per particle.

The stability of VLDL-C provides a complementary perspective on the underlying mechanisms of lipid metabolism. Although VLDL-C represents only an indirect surrogate of hepatic lipoprotein production, the absence of any upward trend argues against increased hepatic lipoprotein production as the dominant driver of the observed LDL and ApoB rise. Instead, the combination of elevated LDL-C and ApoB with unchanged VLDL-C is more consistent with the altered clearance of ApoB-containing particles from the circulation. In line with this interpretation, the absence of systematic changes in TG levels, even after accounting for inflammatory fluctuations, as reflected by stable CRP levels, further supports a clearance-dominant rather than a production- or inflammation-driven mechanism underlying the observed LDL increase. From a clinical perspective, the selective increase in LDL-C and ApoB in the absence of compensatory changes in HDL-C translated into a significant and sustained increase in the TC-to-HDL-C ratio. The ratio increased by approximately one unit, from approximately 3.8 at baseline to approximately 4.8 at 6–12 months, indicating a clear shift toward a more atherogenic lipid profile. The TC-to-HDL-C ratio integrates both pro- and anti-atherogenic lipoprotein fractions and is a well-validated predictor of cardiovascular risk, often outperforming LDL-C or HDL-C alone [15, 16]. In population-based cohorts, even a one-unit increase has been associated with a substantially higher risk of coronary events. Given that the baseline values in our cohort were already close to the upper limit of the desirable range, this sustained elevation may move some patients into higher cardiovascular risk categories, despite the absence of overt cardiovascular events during the observation period. In the context of FRDA, which is frequently accompanied by cardiac involvement, these findings highlight the importance of routine lipid and cardiovascular monitoring and management during long-term omaveloxolone therapy [13].

Importantly, omaveloxolone exerts therapeutic effects through sustained activation of the Nrf2 pathway [17]. In addition to its central role in antioxidant defense, Nrf2 has been linked to sterol regulatory networks involved in lipid homeostasis [18, 19]. Experimental studies suggest that Nrf2 signaling can suppress SREBP-dependent transcription, particularly SREBP-2, a key regulator of LDL receptor expression and cholesterol uptake [20]. Reduced SREBP-2 activity may therefore be associated with lower LDL receptor availability and decreased hepatic clearance of circulating LDL particles [21]. SREBP-2 also regulates PCSK9, promoting LDL receptor degradation. Although Nrf2 does not appear to directly regulate PCSK9, alterations in SREBP-2 activity and related sterol-responsive pathways may indirectly favor increased PCSK9 activity, further limiting LDL receptor availability [22, 23]. Within this conceptual framework, sustained Nrf2 activation by omaveloxolone may favor the accumulation of LDL/ApoB particles without a concomitant increase in VLDL secretion, a pattern consistent with lipid changes observed in our cohort.

Several aspects of the study design must be considered. First, the observational design and the absence of a propensity-matched control cohort preclude causal inferences, as disease-specific effects on lipid metabolism cannot be fully distinguished from treatment-related effects. Second, the cohort size reflects the rarity of FRDA and the real-world setting but is balanced by repeated measurements and consistent findings across frequentist and Bayesian approaches. Third, lipid assessments were obtained as part of routine clinical care, introducing some heterogeneity in sampling; nevertheless, the persistence and magnitude of the observed effects across all timepoints argue against random variation as an explanation. Fourth, medication adherence was not systematically assessed, and non-adherence (i.e., single missed doses) cannot be entirely excluded, although all patients attended regular clinical follow-up visits throughout the observation period. Finally, the proposed mechanisms are based on existing data obtained from cellular and animal models and were not directly measured in this study, highlighting an important avenue for future investigations in FRDA. In addition, two patients received lipid-modifying therapy during the observation period, either before or after the initiation of omaveloxolone. Given the small number and heterogeneous timing of these treatments, they are unlikely to account for the consistent lipid changes observed across the cohort; however, their presence should be considered when interpreting individual lipid trajectories.

Conclusions

Omaveloxolone therapy in patients with FRDA was associated with a sustained shift toward a more atherogenic lipid profile, characterized by a selective increase in LDL-C and ApoB levels over 1 year. The observed lipid pattern is consistent with altered hepatic clearance of atherogenic particles rather than a global disturbance in lipoprotein metabolism. These findings highlight lipid metabolism as a relevant systemic aspect of long-term omaveloxolone treatment and support the incorporation of routine lipid monitoring into clinical follow-up examinations. Further studies are required to clarify the long-term cardiovascular significance of this profile and to inform individualized risk assessment and management strategies.
