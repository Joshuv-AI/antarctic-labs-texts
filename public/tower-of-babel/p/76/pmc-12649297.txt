Recent Utilization of Pediatric Extrapolation and Modeling and Simulation Approaches in Pediatric Drug Development in Japan
Authors: Akinori Nakashima, Akihide Tsujimoto, Masako Saito, Jun Takeda, Makiko Natori, Satoshi Shoji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Japan, the percentage of approved drugs with pediatric indications increased to 30% in 2010‐2015, but no further increase was observed through 2020. The Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare in Japan presented draft future directions to promote pediatric drug development, where the modeling and simulation (M&S) approach was introduced as a tool to support efficacy and safety, utilizing existing data when building a flexible data package for pediatric drug approval. M&S is considered a powerful scientific tool in pediatric drug development using the pediatric extrapolation approach. We examined approval application data packages for pediatric drugs approved in Japan from January 2019 to March 2023, where 95 drug products were identified as pediatric drugs for this survey. Drugs with complete, partial, or no extrapolation accounted for 43.2%, 30.5%, and 26.3% of the total drugs, respectively. M&S was widely used as the major rationale in dose selection for pediatric clinical trials and/or the recommended dosing regimen in drug approval applications for the pediatric population (60.0%). Further use of existing data with extrapolation strategies may lead to the development of flexible and efficient data packages for pediatric drug approval. Since safety profiles may be different between pediatric and adult populations due to age‐related factors, pediatric developmental safety should be incorporated into M&S in future assessments. The pharmaceutical industry, regulatory authorities, and academia need to discuss development strategies from an early stage and share lessons learned, thereby facilitating further discussions on efficient pediatric drug development and delivering drugs for children without delay in Japan.Keywords: clinical data package, clinical pharmacology, drug development, extrapolation, model informed drug development, modeling and simulation, pediatrics, pharmaceutical R&D, pharmacometrics

Introduction

The number of approved drugs with pediatric information in their labeling increased from 20% (1999) to 41% (2009) in the US, 1 with the Best Pharmaceuticals for Children Act 2 and Pediatric Research Equity Act 3 contributing to this advancement. 4 The implementation of these laws and others led to more than 1000 labeling changes for drugs/biologics to include information for pediatric use. 5 In the EU, over 260 new medicines, including label extensions, were authorized for pediatric use from 2007 to 2016, and the number of clinical trials involving the pediatric population increased by 50% since the introduction of the Pediatric Regulation (2007 to 2016). 6 Although access to medicines for children has improved in both the US and EU, challenges remain in conducting clinical trials in children, including neonate studies and age‐specific formulations. 4 In Japan, the Evaluation Committee on Unapproved or Off‐Labeled Drugs with High Medical Needs for both adults and children was established within the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare (MHLW) in 2010, and pediatric clinical trials were subsequently conducted by pharmaceutical companies. As a result, the percentage of approved drugs with pediatric indications increased from 17.5% (2001‐2010; Ntot, total number of drugs used for the analysis = 496) to 30.2% (2010‐2015; Ntot = 629), 7 but no further increase was observed up until 2020 (2014‐2020; Ntot = 818). 8 A comprehensive report in 2018 demonstrated that only 13.5% of the package inserts registered in a database at that point (12,435 package inserts for adult and/or pediatric use) included pediatric dosage and administration information in their package inserts, and even for drugs frequently used for pediatric patients, more than half did not include such information. 9 These findings suggest that the number of pediatric drugs and drugs including pediatric information is still insufficient to establish appropriate drug treatments for children in Japan.

To address this issue, the MHLW has taken measures such as a scheme for the rapid authorization of unapproved and off‐label drugs, extension of the re‐evaluation period for drugs intended for adults, incentives for drug prices in the national health insurance, and the establishment of a pediatric clinical trial network with academia. 10 The rapid authorization scheme applies to adult and/or pediatric drugs and indications. It covers those already approved in specific Western countries but not approved in Japan, or those not approved anywhere, but whose efficacy and safety were shown to be superior to existing therapies in clinical trials. For example, a drug approval application for an additional indication can be made without all or part of new clinical studies if there are existing data sufficient to show the efficacy and safety (public knowledge‐based application). A re‐examination period for a new drug (adult indications in general) can be extended when the pediatric development plan is submitted by the completion of approval application review for the adult indication, and the planned pediatric clinical studies have been started without delay. As generics cannot be filed during the re‐examination period of the new drug, an extension of the re‐examination period can work as an extension of the exclusivity.

Recently, the MHLW issued a notification to recommend planning pediatric drug development during the development of drugs intended for adults, 11 though this is not a mandatory regulation, such as the Pediatric Study Plan in the US and the Pediatric Investigation Plan in the EU. Additionally, a representative from the Pharmaceutical Evaluation and Management Division in the MHLW presented drafts for future directions to promote pediatric drug development to a committee composed of experts in medicine, pharmacology, drug evaluation, and clinical statistics, persons from pharmaceutical industry, patient representatives, and others (a committee on regulatory policies for strengthening drug discovery capability, solving drug lag/loss issues, and so forth was held on July 10 and August 7, 2023 in Tokyo, Japan): (1) to create a system to promote simultaneous drug development for adults and pediatric patients; (2) to clarify the priority for drugs to be developed for pediatric patients; (3) to build flexible data packages in regulatory applications for approval in pediatrics; and (4) to promote the use of existing drug products with pediatric formulations (they mentioned, “it has been pointed out that even if pharmaceutical companies develop pediatric‐specific formulations, medical institutions and pharmacies in Japan do not necessarily adopt those formulations due to factors such as the limited number of patients”). 12 Specifically, direction (3) introduced the modeling and simulation (M&S) approach as a tool to support efficacy/safety utilizing existing data when building a flexible data package for pediatric drug approval.

M&S methodology has been widely implemented in drug development, initially referred to as model‐based drug development (MBDD), 13 , 14 then as model‐informed drug development and discovery 15 or model‐informed drug development (MIDD). 16 M&S is considered a powerful scientific tool, specifically for pediatric drug development, using a pediatric extrapolation approach that makes the best use of existing knowledge and data. From the late 2010s, regulatory documents on general principles and frameworks for pediatric extrapolation approach were developed, including the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) E11 (R1) guideline, 17 E11A guideline, 18 and European Medicines Agency reflection paper. 19 Additionally, some disease‐specific guidelines for the pediatric extrapolation approach were also developed for seizures/epileptic disorders 20 , 21 and anti‐infections/bacterial infections. 22 , 23 In the US, the pediatric extrapolation approach has increasingly been applied to drug development when diseases and responses to treatment are assumed to be similar between the target pediatric and reference populations. 24 , 25 , 26 Drug development with pediatric extrapolation approaches may spread further with scientific advancements and increased understanding of disease similarities and response to treatment, with M&S/MIDD playing an essential role in planning and evaluating the pediatric development programs and individual study designs.

The ICH E11A guideline describes that principles for determining the scope and extent of efficacy data to be collected in the pediatric population together with reference (adult) data can be applied to the generation of pediatric safety data. However, differences between pediatric and adult safety may limit the extrapolation, specifically due to age‐related safety concerns. In the meantime, it has been reported that mechanistic models such as quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) models for the integrated understanding of pediatric‐specific developmental biology may be useful when characteristic factors in the growth and development process of children impact the drug safety profiles of pediatric patients compared to adult patients. 27

To our knowledge, no study has reported a comprehensive evaluation or review focusing on the use of existing data (e.g., M&S) for pediatric drug products approved in Japan, although a few reports have addressed the use of M&S for drugs approved in Japan. 28 , 29 The Japan Pharmaceutical Manufacturers Association (JPMA) pediatric drug development task force has studied challenges and potential opportunities to deliver safe and effective drugs to children without approval delays from foreign countries and to provide information on the adequate usage of pediatric drugs. The objectives of this research were: (1) to examine pediatric drug approval application data packages from the past 5 years, using regulatory review reports for new drugs or drugs with label extensions approved in Japan, which are open to the public, focusing on the use of existing data, pediatric extrapolation strategies, and quantitative approaches (M&S/MIDD), and (2) to present discussion points based on these findings on efficient pediatric drug development in Japan. This article contributes to advancing the discussion on promoting pediatric drug development utilizing M&S/MIDD, not only in Japan but also in other regions with similar circumstances.

Methods

This survey examined drug approval application data packages for pediatric drug products, focusing on the utilization of existing data, specifically pediatric extrapolation and quantitative approaches. The database for approved drug products in Japan, published by the JPMA, 30 was used to select the drugs for this survey. The JPMA database identified drug products intended for the pediatric population based on the following keywords or conditions (in Japanese). 30 Drug products intended for pediatric use were determined when the following key words or conditions were included in the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency in Japan (PMDA) review report sections “Dosage and Administration” and/or “Indications”. We confirmed the PMDA review report for each product included in the database to judge whether the product was intended for pediatric use based on the keywords or conditions. Even if these keywords or weight categories were not included in the sections, drug products considered to be for pediatric use were also included in this analysis (e.g., those judged from the context of the review report as approved simultaneously for both adults and children with the same indications/dosage and administration; or those where the description “for adults” had been removed due to the addition of dosage and administration for children).

Keywords: pediatric, preterm infant, immature infant, low body weight infant, neonate, infant, child, young adult, adolescent, child patient, preschool children, school children, age greater than or equal to “X” years old (when age < 15 years old. This age limit was based on an MHLW notification about instructions for package inserts of prescription drugs, where the age range for children was mentioned as those aged 7 to less than 15 years old).

Conditions: Dosage and administration are described using body weight categories of 40 kg or lower (including cases where dosage and administration are described as mg/kg body weight and it is assumed from the maximum dose or similar information that a 40 kg or lower body weight category would be included). Additionally, even if no category for 40 kg or lower body weight is provided, drugs intended for pediatric use are included in this database.

This survey included pediatric drugs approved in Japan from January 2019 to March 2023. Among the pediatric drug products identified from the database, generic products, biosimilar products, products approved through public knowledge‐based applications, and diagnostic agents were excluded from the survey. Furthermore, concomitant drugs associated with regenerative medical products and a drug with a new administration route were excluded. The PMDA review reports for approved drugs, available on the PMDA website, 31 served as the primary sources for reviewing the data package. Other documents associated with the drug approval applications, including some parts of Common Technical Documents, were also reviewed in more detail to highlight the specific quantitative approaches used in the applications. These reference documents were also publicly available from medical drug information searches on the PMDA website. 31

Although the ICH E11A guideline defines pediatric extrapolation as a continuum, 18 we assigned the drugs into the following three categories: complete extrapolation, partial extrapolation, or no extrapolation (Table 1), to help understand/analyze the trends while aligning with prior publications. 24 , 25 , 26 This analysis summarized the classification category split by the PMDA review departments by disease area, application type (e.g., fast‐track approval, rare disease), and development strategy (e.g., simultaneous approval application with adults). This analysis also reviewed the use of existing data for each drug from the following aspects: use of M&S in pediatric dose‐setting rationale for clinical trials and/or drug approval applications, presence or absence of actual dose‐finding data in pediatric patients, statistical methodologies used for pediatric extrapolation, utilization of external data, and evaluation of ethnic/regional differences in pharmacokinetics (PK), efficacy, or safety.

Results

Between January 2019 and March 2023, a total of 605 drug products for adult and/or pediatric populations were identified as approved in Japan. Of these, 116 were approved for the pediatric population. After reviewing the PMDA reports in accordance with the steps described in the methods section, 95 drug products were identified for this survey, all of which were included in the evaluation of the extrapolation category (complete, partial, or no extrapolation). A list of the drugs included in this survey is provided in Table S1. Of these, drugs with complete, partial, or no extrapolation approaches accounted for 43.2% (41/95), 30.5% (29/95), and 26.3% (25/95), respectively (Figure 1a). The complete extrapolation strategy was the most commonly used approach. In most of the drugs categorized as complete extrapolation, pediatric patients were enrolled in or concurrently with adult trials in simultaneous drug applications (28/41), of which 17 drugs included only adolescents, while 11 drugs included younger age groups (Figure 1b). Ten of the 41 drugs (24.4%) were approved by the fast‐track review for COVID‐19, and 28 of the 41 drugs (68.3%) were approved through a special designation review (Figure 1b,c).

In the Oncology Department, complete extrapolations included molecular‐targeted drugs for small molecules (n = 2) and large molecules (n = 2). (1) Exposure matching based on population PK (PopPK) models is a widely used methodology for building a dose‐setting rationale for pediatric clinical trials and/or pediatric dose recommendations in drug applications.

(2) There were cases where more mechanism‐based/bottom‐up PK modeling, such as physiologically based PK (PBPK) models, was used for supporting dose setting (e.g., entrectinib and rivaroxaban). The pediatric dose of entrectinib, a molecular targeted drug for anticancer treatment, was investigated using body weight‐based exposure prediction from PopPK analysis to explain exposure changes during the development (growth) stage, whereas age‐based exposure prediction from PBPK analysis was conducted to describe exposure changes in the ontogeny of organ maturation (CYP3A4 expression level).

(3) Exposure‐response analysis was also used to explain pediatric safe/effective doses (e.g., ambrisentan, fesoterodine, and adalimumab).

(4) Pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) models have been used to provide a scientific rationale for the selection of dose regimens based on the mechanism of action (e.g., caplacizumab, burosumab, and mepolizumab).

(5) Innovative statistical methodologies, such as the Bayesian approach, use of historical controls, and use of external controls, were also applied to assess the pediatric efficacy of some drugs.

Regarding the statistical approach in (5), a typical use of these advanced methodologies is to extrapolate the adult (higher age) population to the pediatric (lower age) population. Examples (a), (b), and (c) below employed contrasts composed from clinical studies with intervention, and (d) and (e) without intervention (details of examples in Table S3.1 with statistical keywords in 3.2):

(a) Epileptic pediatric patients aged 4 or over and under 12 administered perampanel hydrate in one study were compared with patients in a placebo group from another study involving pediatric epileptic patients aged 12 or over and adult patients.

(b) Pediatric patients of ulcerative colitis administered adalimumab in one study were compared with the results of a meta‐analysis combining the outcomes from placebo groups in adult studies.

(c) For ambrisentan, the results of a study on pediatric patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension and another study on adult patients were combined using a Bayesian dynamic borrowing method.

(d) Patients with acid sphingomyelinase deficiency administered olipudase alfa in a previous study were compared with those in a natural history study.

(e) In a study on defibrotide sodium for veno‐occlusive disease, patients who received the drug and those who received the best supportive care selected from the participating centers by medical charts were compared.

Discussion

This survey indicated that pediatric extrapolation approaches were utilized in a certain number of pediatric drug development programs in Japan, with drugs categorized as complete or partial extrapolation accounting for 73.7%. Many cases used M&S, including PopPK, PBPK, or PK/PD analyses, to support pediatric dose selection in clinical trials and/or dose recommendations in pediatric drug approval applications. In some cases, innovative statistical methodologies were used in clinical development to interpret pediatric efficacy.

While PopPK analysis is a standard approach for both adult and pediatric populations, PK/PD analysis is also a useful approach to support dose selection and/or dose recommendations. In this survey, mechanistic PK/PD models were applied to provide a scientific rationale for selecting a dose regimen based on the mechanism of action. For example, in a case where the PK/PD model addressed the lack of data, caplacizumab, a monoclonal antibody for the treatment of acquired thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, was approved for patients, including adolescents weighing 40 kg or greater, using a PK/PD model without clinical trial data from the adolescent population.

Biomarkers play a critical role in clinical drug development for understanding the mode of action, disease progression, maturation of biological functions, and the exposure‐efficacy/safety response relationships. As quantifiable characteristics of biological processes, biomarkers can be used to support dose selection in both adult and pediatric populations. In the pediatric development of burosumab for the treatment of FGF23‐related hypophosphatemic rickets/osteomalacia, the surrogate biomarker serum phosphorus concentration was used to support the recommended dose in the drug approval application for pediatric patients aged 13‐17 years using population PK/PD modeling (efficacy in adults and pediatric patients aged 1‐12 years was evaluated in Phase 3 clinical studies). In another case, for mepolizumab used in the treatment of bronchial asthma, blood eosinophil levels were used as a response biomarker. This model supported the dose‐setting rationale for the pediatric population aged 6‐11 years to participate in the multi‐regional Phase 2 study of patients with bronchial asthma aged 6‐11 years. After the clinical data were obtained, the updated model provided the recommended dose for the drug approval application.

In some cases, drugs were approved based on the extrapolation of efficacy from the adult population to the pediatric population, even without actual clinical trial data in the pediatric population. Anti‐SARS‐CoV‐2 spike protein antibodies (tixagevimab/cilgavimab) were approved for the treatment and prevention of SARS‐CoV‐2 in both adult and adolescent populations simultaneously. Although PK data for the pediatric population had not been obtained at the time of the initial application in Japan, a PopPK model‐based analysis predicted that exposure in children aged ≥12 years and weighing ≥40 kg would be comparable to that in adult patients. This type of drug development strategy, that is, complete extrapolation without actual pediatric clinical trial data, including PK data, may be considered a special case for emergent situations (e.g., when no drugs are available for pediatric use for a specific disease or when the number of drugs is limited). In such cases, a strong rationale for pediatric use based on a detailed benefit‐risk assessment is necessary during drug development, with continuous evaluation after approval. M&S remains an essential tool throughout the assessment process.

There was a case that made a broad indication, even when certain subgroups were not enrolled, if data could be leveraged from other subgroups. For example, glecaprevir/pibrentasvir was approved for the treatment of patients with chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV) genotypes 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6, with or without compensated cirrhosis. In these filings, data on patients with HCV genotypes 5 or 6 without compensated cirrhosis and patients with any HCV genotype with compensated cirrhosis were not collected in clinical data from adolescent and pediatric patients. However, the efficacy and dosage rationale for all subgroups of pediatric patients were explained based on the similarity of disease and PK (exposure) between adults and the overall pediatric population.

This survey indicated that many cases comprehensively evaluated pediatric safety using pooled, study‐level, and/or individual‐level safety data from adult and pediatric populations and confirmed the presence or absence of safety concerns in the pediatric population (e.g., noteworthy events or trends). While exposure matching serves as a valuable initial strategy for assessing pediatric drug safety, the inherent differences in immune and metabolic profiles between children and adults, coupled with the physiological changes associated with growth and development, may result in distinct patterns of drug responsiveness in pediatric patients compared to adults. In fact, it was reported that some antipsychotic/antidepressant drugs and antiretroviral drugs showed different safety profiles between adult and pediatric populations regarding adverse event frequencies and some types of events. 32 , 33 This underscores the limitations of exposure matching as a standalone solution. To address these challenges, the adoption of quantitative approaches that account for the unique developmental characteristics of children, particularly the utilization of QSP models, along with the conventional evaluation of safety data from pediatric clinical trials and/or safety surveillance, is considered essential to advance safety evaluation in pediatric drug development. 27 Additionally, further investigations from ongoing research programs such as those in developmental biology supported by the US NIH may provide a deeper understanding of human developmental safety. 34

In this survey, approximately 40% of the drugs were categorized as complete extrapolation. Most of the drugs were approved simultaneously with adults, where pediatric patients were enrolled in or concurrently with the adult efficacy trials (28 out of 41). Most of those cases included a limited or small pediatric population in the clinical trials, although some cases were considered to include pediatric patients as a major population (e.g., treatment for epilepsy or spinal muscular atrophy). The ICH E11A guideline recommends including adolescents in adult trials when the disease, drug pharmacology, and response to treatment are sufficiently similar. 18 Additionally, in Japan, an administrative notice from the MHLW, “Considerations for the clinical evaluation of drugs in pediatric patients (10 or 12 years of age and older) who can be evaluated together with adults,” 34 includes the following diseases/drugs: type 2 diabetes mellitus, familial hypercholesterolemia, allergic diseases, antimicrobials and antivirals, and hematopoietic malignancy. The results of this survey suggest that the trend in pediatric drug development in Japan is generally consistent with the concept of the ICH E11A recommendations and notes from the MHLW. It would be an efficient strategy to simultaneously develop pediatric drugs for adults, specifically by enrolling adolescents in adult clinical trials. M&S performed for pediatric dose selection, combined with actual data obtained from adolescents, could accelerate extrapolation of efficacy and safety to younger age groups and help determine precise doses in subsequent pediatric clinical trials and/or dose recommendations in an additional drug approval application for the younger population. When these clinical trials are conducted as multinational studies, 36 it is beneficial for Japan to participate as an Asian subpopulation, especially for diseases difficult to conduct local clinical trials, such as rare diseases. To participate in multinational clinical trials without delay, factors influencing ethnic and regional differences should be discussed in advance, utilizing prior knowledge and existing data, alongside scientific discussions about disease similarity, response to treatment, and drug pharmacology between adult and pediatric populations.

Of the drugs categorized as complete extrapolation, there were no cases where the exposure‐matching approach was solely used based on pediatric PK data without including efficacy data in drug approval applications for pediatric populations, enrolling pediatric populations in adult trials, or developing the drug with adults simultaneously. However, one case (entrectinib) involved approval using the exposure‐matching approach based on data from a pediatric Phase 1 study to primarily investigate the PK and safety. This case was a simultaneous approval application with adults. Although this type of pediatric drug development (i.e., full extrapolation using pediatric PK/safety data alone) would be an efficient strategy in the complete extrapolation approach, more robust evidence and scientific rationale are required to establish an effective/safe dose in the pediatric population. Nevertheless, given that the extrapolation approach has already been introduced for seizures/epileptic disorders 20 , 21 and anti‐infections/bacterial infections, 22 , 23 the number of drugs and disease areas to be approved using this approach would increase as evidence and understanding of these diseases continue to evolve. Pediatric clinical trials used for drug approval applications were usually conducted in Japan as multinational or standalone local trials. Additionally, many cases utilized data from clinical trials conducted overseas. As participating in multinational studies is already common in adult and pediatric drug development, it would also be efficient for Japan to participate in multinational Phase 1 trials (pediatric PK/safety studies for full extrapolation) when such studies are planned.

This survey indicated that PMDA review reports usually discussed ethnic differences in PK when clinical trial data were available from both Japan and foreign countries. There were also cases where ethnic differences, not only in PK but also in terms of efficacy and/or safety, were addressed. As allele‐frequency differences between Eastern Asian and European populations (e.g., CYP2D6, CYP2C19, and UGT1A1) were reported, 37 it is important to consider the ethnic difference in PK, PD, and safety with respect to the genetic polymorphisms. When a drug has already been approved for adults in both Japan and foreign countries, but is approved for the pediatric population only in foreign countries (i.e., a pediatric drug approval lag exists in Japan), ethnic difference assessments using existing data could provide a strong rationale for planning pediatric drug development in Japan. Additionally, utilizing all existing data from adult and foreign pediatric populations, including ethnic assessment, may minimize the need for local clinical trials and deliver the drug quickly to pediatric patients in Japan. When a drug has already been approved for adults in both Japan and foreign countries, but the drug has not been available for the pediatric population in both Japan and foreign countries, participating in planned multinational clinical trials could be one option to efficiently deliver the drug to Japan without an approval lag. Utilizing existing data from adults, including ethnic assessments, could also provide a rationale for dose selection in the Japanese pediatric population and facilitate timely participation in multinational trials.

The flexible data package for pediatric drug approval application is considered a data package scientifically optimized for delivering safe/effective drugs for children while making the best use of existing data. Although a variety of extrapolation strategies and quantitative analyses were observed in this survey, further use of existing data with a quantitative approach is expected to achieve a flexible data package for future pediatric drug development in Japan. Sufficiency of the data package should be discussed from the planning stage with regulatory authorities and academic experts. The pharmaceutical industry should also collaborate with academic experts to discuss the type of clinical data that should be collected during development in actual medical settings. Regarding the experiences and discussion points obtained through pediatric drug development, it is important to share the lessons learned among the pharmaceutical industry, regulatory authorities, and academia. Accumulation of these findings may contribute to the establishment of new evidence for pediatric efficacy and safety in a specific disease area, which could be utilized in subsequent pediatric drug development. This practice may lead to the development of flexible data packages and efficient pediatric drug development that fully uses existing data and evidence, even in cases where clinical trial information in the pediatric population is insufficient.

This survey has limitations in assessing pediatric extrapolation approaches and collecting information about the use of quantitative approaches. The pediatric extrapolation approach is usually part of the overall clinical development plan. However, this survey retrospectively evaluated the type of approach/strategy that would have been planned or executed for each drug using only publicly available information (i.e., PMDA review reports). Therefore, there are limitations in accurately interpreting pediatric extrapolation approaches and development strategies. Additionally, although we assessed the extrapolation approach in accordance with three categories defined prospectively, the assessment was still subjective, and the classification of some drugs might change depending on the interpretation by the assessors. However, we believe that the assessment is generally adequate to understand the overall trends in pediatric development in Japan during the survey period, since at least a consensus on the judgment was built within the assessors while confirming one by one before finalizing the assessment. Information on the utilization of existing data with a quantitative approach was collected from PMDA review reports and associated documents. Considering the recent use of M&S and MIDD approaches in drug development, there would have been more cases using quantitative approaches that might not have been detected in the review reports. These limitations may be addressed to some extent by preparing a more systematic process for judging the classification and retrieving the necessary information from review reports and other documents.

Conclusion

This study suggests that pediatric extrapolation approaches were utilized for approximately two‐thirds of pediatric drugs approved in Japan from January 2019 to March 2023.
