Internet Addiction and Its Association with Depression, Anxiety and Quality of Life Among School-going Adolescents: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Amandeep Panghal, Jitendra Jeenger, Manu Sharma, D M Mathur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to determine the prevalence of internet addiction and explore its relationship with depression, anxiety and quality of life among adolescents.PurposeTo determine the prevalence of Internet addiction and to study its relationship with depression, anxiety and quality of life among adolescents.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 4,057 school students, aged 12–17 years. Sociodemographic information was collected using a tailored proforma. Young’s Internet Addiction Test (IAT) was employed to evaluate internet addiction, while Beck’s Depression Inventory (BDI) and Beck’s Anxiety Inventory (BAI) were used to assess depression and anxiety, respectively. The Short Form-36 (SF-36) questionnaire measured the quality of life.ResultsAmong the 4,057 adolescents surveyed, 41.2% exhibited mild internet addiction, 16.9% moderate and 1.5% severe internet addiction. The prevalence of internet addiction was significantly higher in males compared to females. A positive correlation was found between the IAT scores and the scores on BDI and BAI. Age, gender, educational level and depression emerged as significant factors associated with internet addiction.ConclusionThe findings highlight an increased prevalence of internet addiction among adolescents, which is linked to increased levels of depression, anxiety and a lower quality of life. Further multicentric studies are essential to gain a more comprehensive understanding of this disorder.Keywords: Internet addiction, depression, anxiety, quality of life, adolescents, school students

Introduction

India, which accounts for 17.5% of the world’s population, has approximately 14.5% of the global internet users. The country ranks second in internet usage, followed by China. A significant portion of India’s internet users consists of young men (53%) and students, both in urban (71%) and rural areas (55%). 1

Internet addiction (IA) has a worldwide prevalence of 14.22%, among Indian school-going adolescents estimated moderate to severe problematic internet use prevalence is 21.5%. 2 Increased use of the internet for academics, social engagement and entertainment has raised concerns about problematic usage. A systematic review from India reported varying prevalence estimates between 19.9% and 40.7%, largely influenced by differences in diagnostic tools and methodologies. 3

Similar to substance use disorders, IA is on the rise and is frequently associated with psychiatric comorbidities. Adolescents with excessive internet use are experiencing higher levels of psychiatric comorbidities, such as depression, anxiety, aggressiveness, impulsiveness, suicidal behaviour and poor self-esteem.4, 5

IA is linked to a decline in overall well-being and life satisfaction. 6 A study highlighted that individuals who frequently use the internet at night are at increased risk of developing IA, which can lead to poorer life quality and difficulty in screen time control. 7

However, these figures should be interpreted carefully, as variations in the timing of studies, sampling methods and measurement tools can impact the comparability of these findings. Despite its growing significance, there is a lacuna in published literature on IA and its psychiatric comorbidities in school-going students. Therefore, this study examines the association of IA and its impact on mental health and quality of life (QoL) among students.

Methods

The present cross-sectional study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital in Southern Rajasthan, India, between June 2015 and May 2016. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC) of Geetanjali University. The proposal was approved with Ref.: GU/HREC/EC/2015/1042. The authorities/principals at the respective schools granted permission to collect data from the students, which was included in the present study.

Multistage stratified random sampling was used to collect the data. There are 42 senior secondary English medium schools in the city, out of which 7 schools were selected. Subsequently, students of each grade from 7th to 12th in each school were selected randomly. Students who provided written informed assent and consent from parents or legal guardians were included. The exclusion criteria included students with a history of mental health difficulties or substance use disorders, excluding nicotine use, in the present or past. Additionally, adolescents with a history of any chronic medical or surgical illness were excluded. Students who were unable to comprehend the English language were also excluded from the study.

A total of 6,000 proformas, which included the parents’ consent form, sociodemographic details and questionnaires, were distributed among students in Grades 7–12. We encouraged the adolescents to complete the study forms independently, providing them with assistance from resident doctors. At the time of collecting the proforma, proformas which were not signed by a guardian, incomplete proformas and those not fulfilling the inclusion and exclusion criteria were excluded from the study. Finally, 4,057 study proformas were included in the study. Parents or legal guardians of students who screened positive for moderate to severe psychopathology were duly informed, and appropriate referral advice was provided for consultation with a school counsellor or a mental health professional.

Young’s Internet Addiction Test (IAT) was employed to evaluate IA, while Beck’s Depression Inventory (BDI) and Beck’s Anxiety Inventory (BAI) were used to assess depression and anxiety, respectively. The Short Form-36 (SF-36) questionnaire measured the QoL.

Tools Used

Young’s 20-item Internet Addiction Test 8 : The international community widely accepts this test for assessing IA. The IAT has previously been validated in Indian adolescent samples, showing acceptable internal consistency and factor structure. Cronbach’s alpha reported for an Indian school sample was 0.905. 9 This scale comprises 20 items, each assessed on a 5-point Likert scale, which evaluates compulsive use of the internet, neglect of daily activities, social interactions, sleep patterns, academic performance and emotional distress related to internet use.

Beck’s Depression Inventory 10 : This is a well-established tool for assessing levels of depression. The BDI is divided into two sections: the first evaluates psychological symptoms, while the second addresses physical symptoms. This inventory is self-reported and has 21 questions, where each item is rated on a 4-point rating scale ranging from 0 (absent) to 3 (very severe). The scoring system categorises depression severity as follows: scores between 0–13, 14–19, 20–28 and 29–63 denote minimal, mild, moderate and severe depression, respectively.

Beck’s Anxiety Inventory11, 12: This self-assessed questionnaire, meant to assess the intensity of anxiety symptoms, contains 21 items. Each question was rated on a Likert scale from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe anxiety), reflecting their experiences over the last week. The maximum score of this scale is 63, where 0–7, 8–15, 16–25 and 26–63 represent minimal, mild, moderate and severe anxiety, respectively.

Short Form-36 (SF-36) 13 : This tool was created by an American research team under the leadership of John Ware. Its purpose is to offer a tool for individuals to assess their QoL. It comprises 36 questions that assess 8 key aspects of health related to QoL: role physical, physical functioning, general health, role emotional, vitality, social functioning, bodily pain and mental health. Additionally, there is a single question that gauges perceived health changes over the past year. The number of response options for each question ranges from two to six.

Statistical Analysis: Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS-16) was used to examine the data. Categorical variables were evaluated using chi-square, and mean (±SD) were used to express the continuous variables. All analyses were performed at a 95% confidence interval, and a two-tailed p value <.05 was considered statistically significant. To measure the relationship between two variables, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used. The results were organised in a table and illustrated graphically.

Results

In the present study, 4,057 students participated, and the average age was 14.7 ± 1.5 years. The majority of adolescents belonged to the ages of 14 and 15 years (38.4%). There were more boys (64%) than girls (35.3%). The majority (91.0%) of study participants were Hindu, 57% of them belonged to nuclear families, and the majority (89.3%) of the participants resided in urban areas (Figure 1).

Sociodemographic Profiles of Adolescent Students.

Figure 2 shows that 1,673 (41.23%), 684 (16.86%) and 59 (1.45%) students were identified as having mild, moderate and severe IA, respectively. Among these participants, approximately 41.2% exhibited mild IA, while only 1.5% were categorised with severe IA. Additionally, about 27.0% of the participants reported significant depressive symptoms, with 3.2% exhibiting symptoms in the severe range. Symptoms indicative of severe anxiety were observed in 5.9% of the participants. Around 8% of adolescents reported a poor QoL.

Discussion

Indian studies reported 21.5% prevalence of moderate IAT and 2.6% for severe IAT among adolescents.3, 14 Narayanappa et al. reported combined mild, moderate and severe IA prevalence as high as ~65% in one adolescent sample. Differences in study methodologies and the evolving definition of the disorder contribute to the challenges in comparing results. 15 In the present study, the authors observed that 41%, 16.85% and 1.45% had mild, moderate and severe IA on Young’s IAT.

The current study observed that older adolescents, 14 years (59%) and above (64.7%), and boys (64.73%) had higher scores on IAT compared to girls (49.3%), a finding also noted by Lee et al. 16 Joiner et al. reported that males generally spent more time on the internet, averaging 16.02 h compared to 14.08 h for females. 17 An Indian study by Malviya et al. revealed that among 242 medical undergraduates, boys had twice the IA of girls. 18 Literature suggests that males tend to use the internet more for power, control, passion and pornography, while females use technology primarily to complete tasks. 19 This finding implies that males might be more susceptible to developing problematic internet use under similar conditions, possibly due to inherent differences in internet usage patterns. According to a meta-analysis, 12th-class students had a higher prevalence (16.7%–28.8%) of IA than junior students. We observed that 24.3% of senior secondary students had moderate to severe IA. 3

Studying IA and its psychiatric comorbidities presents challenges, as most research is cross-sectional. The relationship between IA and psychiatric comorbidity could be bidirectional. Excessive internet use is associated with increased incidence of psychiatric comorbidities.4, 5 Mental health challenges can drive individuals to excessive internet use as a coping mechanism to escape negative emotions and social isolation and to decrease stress and loneliness. 20 In the present study, 27% and 33% of school-going adolescents had depression and moderate to severe anxiety symptoms, respectively.

Another study found that more than half of the adolescents with IA also exhibited symptoms of depression, with a comorbidity rate reaching up to 50.2%. 21 Meta-analyses consistently show strong, statistically significant associations between IA and psychiatric conditions (depression, anxiety, stress, suicidal behaviour), indicating that the concern remains valid and is possibly increasing. Recent large-scale and cross-sectional studies reinforce that IA is an independent risk factor for mental health issues among adolescents globally. 22 Angane et al. found that problematic internet use is associated with higher anxiety scores. 23 People with IA exhibited elevated levels of sadness, stress, and confidence derived from internet usage compared to the non-addicted group. 22 This study concluded that IA can worsen anxiety and depressive tendencies. Bozorgkhoo et al. showed that there is a significant correlation between IA and psychological symptoms such as anxiety, depression and hostility. 24 An international study on excessive internet use was associated with higher scores suggestive of depression and anxiety, especially among adolescent males. 25

Research was conducted to assess QoL among adolescent students using SF-36 in Croatia and Germany, and they found a significant negative correlation with IA. 26 Our study also reported similar results. The magnitude of problematic internet use varies significantly across different regions of the world, and it is inversely linked to QoL, as indicated by both subjective measures, such as life satisfaction, and objective measures, such as quality of environmental conditions. 6

Limitations and Strengths

The present research has limitations due to its cross-sectional nature and reliance on responses from self-report instruments. There is no unanimity on the legitimacy of IA due to the lack of established ICD-11/DSM criteria for this condition. The study’s design precluded the determination of whether IA, depression and anxiety are causes, consequences or comorbidities. Students who had chronic medical illness, known psychiatric conditions or substance use were excluded from the study. Specific internet behaviours (gaming, social media and others) have not been assessed in this study. The findings of this study may not be generalised beyond urban school-going students due to its specific sample characteristics. Furthermore, we assessed the correlation between IA and the overall SF-36 score, excluding the subdomains of the SF-36.

This investigation’s strengths include the utilisation of standardised and validated instruments in a rather large sample of school adolescents. The evaluation of QoL in the current group of adolescents is a distinctive aspect of this study.

Conclusion

Almost 60% of school-going adolescents have reported problematic internet use, and it is more common among boys. Problematic internet use has significantly increased scores on depressive and anxiety scales. Adolescents with IA have a negative correlation with QoL. Screening students with problematic internet use for depressive and anxiety symptoms is of clinical importance. Measures to increase awareness among teachers and parents regarding this emerging condition are of public health importance.
