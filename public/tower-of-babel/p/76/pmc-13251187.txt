Identification of a potential novel Staphylococcus species via genomic sequencing: A neonatal infection case report
Authors: Fengxiang Wei, Xin Wang, Hui Lv, Huihua Xia, Guangli Gan, Xiaohang Chen, Xiaochun Liu, Huan Chen, Luyang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoagulase-negative Staphylococci (CoNS) are common symbiotic Gram-positive bacteria colonizing human skin and mucous membranes with lower virulence than Staphylococcus aureus. As crucial pathogens of neonatal infections, they often harbor multiple drug resistance genes and can induce neonatal pneumonia, sepsis, suppurative meningitis, and other clinical manifestations.Case presentationA preterm neonate at 29+1 weeks’ gestation complicated by respiratory distress syndrome and pneumonia received empirical ceftazidime and penicillin for 8 days. The condition initially improved but suddenly deteriorated on postnatal day 17 with septic shock, fever, and anemia. Routine tests suggested Staphylococcus capitis infection, and targeted anti-infective and supportive treatments relieved symptoms. Given the inconsistenty between the infection severity and that of typical Staphylococcus infections, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) were further performed, identifying a potential novel Staphylococcus species closely related to Staphylococcus warneri. Nevertheless, the origin of this potential novel species remains unclear, which needs further verification.ConclusionFor neonates with sudden clinical deterioration, intractable infection or ambiguous conventional microbial results, mNGS and WGS facilitate accurate pathogen identification and treatment adjustment. This potential novel strain discovery highlights the importance of enhanced vigilance against bacterial multidrug resistance and the emergence of potential novel pathogens in neonatal care.Keywords: Coagulase-negative Staphylococci, Staphylococcus warneri, Potential novel bacterial species, Whole-genome sequencing, Metagenomic next-generation sequencing, Microbial identification, Neonatal infection, Case report

Introduction

Staphylococcus is a genus of Gram-positive cocci widely distributed in nature. It commonly colonizes human and animal skin and mucous membranes, and shows grape-like clusters under the microscope. Among them, Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is one of the most common and widespread bacterial pathogens. It commonly colonizes the nasal vestibule, multiple skin sites, and other mucosal surfaces of the human body, and is responsible for suppurative skin and wound infections, as well as hundreds of thousands to millions of severe invasive infections globally each year [1]. It is a leading causative pathogen of pneumonia and other respiratory tract infections, surgical site infections, prosthetic joint infections, cardiovascular infections, and nosocomial bacteremia.

Coagulase-negative Staphylococci (CoNS), which colonize the skin and mucous membranes of humans and animals, are mostly classified as low-virulence or non-pathogenic Staphylococci, and are differentiated from coagulase-positive S. aureus. Their strain-specific virulence factors exhibit lower pathogenicity, and are less frequently implicated in clinical infectious manifestations. However, CoNS tend to infect preterm infants and rank among the major pathogens responsible for nosocomial infections [2]. Even worse, CoNS, as hidden reservoirs of antibiotic resistance and virulence traits, exhibit high rates of antibiotic resistance, which frequently limits clinical antimicrobial options and prolongs hospital stays [3]. Currently, vancomycin is the first-line agent for many of these pathogens, including methicillin-resistant S. aureus, penicillin-resistant Streptococcus pneumoniae, and penicillin-resistant Enterococcus spp. [4].

As a species of CoNS, Staphylococcus warneri (S. warneri) is a rare pathogenic species. Although sepsis cases caused by S. warneri infection are rarely reported, recent studies have identified that certain mutants of this species may act as emerging pathogens [5]. Herein, we report a case of neonatal infection caused by a potentially novel, clinically relevant Staphylococcus species, and describe the full diagnostic process of this isolate via conventional microbiological testing and genomic sequencing.

Case presentation

On February 14, 2023, a female preterm infant with a gestational age of 29+1 weeks and a birth weight of 0.93 kg was delivered by cesarean section in the Department of Obstetrics, Shenzhen Longgang District Maternity and Child Health Care Hospital, and was promptly admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Emergency chest radiography revealed grade II hyaline membrane disease, manifested as ground-glass opacities in both lung fields, diffuse fine granular increased density, and dendritic air bronchograms in the bilateral lower lung fields (Fig. 1A). The infant was diagnosed with stage II neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS). Immediately, porcine phospholipid injection was administered at a dose of 200 mg/kg. After aggressive resuscitation and symptomatic treatment, the infant’s vital signs became stable.

Chest radiograph. A. Grade II hyaline membrane disease. B. A new patchy blur in the lower right lung. C. Partial absorption of both pulmonary foci compared to the front.

On February 16, abnormal complete blood count (CBC) results suggested the presence of infection. Chest radiography revealed patchy, hazy opacities in the right lower lung (Fig. 1B), which supported the supplementary diagnosis of neonatal pneumonia. Empiric antimicrobial therapy with ceftazidime combined with penicillin was initiated. After 8 days of treatment, chest radiography indicated that bilateral diffuse fine granular opacities were alleviated, and the previous patchy shadows in the right lower lung were mostly absorbed (Fig. 1C). Repeated testing of infection biomarkers and blood gas analysis indicated that the infant’s clinical condition was improving. Accordingly, penicillin, and ceftazidime were discontinued.

On March 3 (postnatal day 17), the infant presented with sudden and recurrent desaturation, and was immediately intubated and placed on invasive mechanical ventilation in synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) mode. Her partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO₂) was significantly elevated, with abnormal tachycardia and marked heart rate variability, and electrocardiography revealed second-degree atrioventricular block. CBC showed a hemoglobin (Hb) level of 121 g/L (Table 1), with elevated levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT). Meanwhile, the infant had a shock score of 5, accompanied by recurrent cyanosis and pale skin. Based on these findings, the infant was diagnosed with postnatally acquired neonatal pneumonia and septic shock.

The infant’s vital signs gradually stabilized after removal of the peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC), along with implementation of acid-base correction, blood transfusion, and maintenance of internal environment homeostasis. Urgent follow-up tests, including chest radiography, CBC, coagulation profile, and blood cultures (sampled from the PICC line and peripheral vein), were performed.

A follow-up chest X-ray (Fig. 2A) showed slight progression of bilateral pulmonary lesions. Concurrently, the infant presented with increased tracheal secretions, transient fever, and tachycardia, with hs-CRP at 73.09 mg/L and PCT at 54.59 ng/mL. Given that these manifestations were attributed to infection, the vasoactive agent milrinone was administered via continuous intravenous infusion, and meropenem combined with vancomycin was initiated for antimicrobial therapy.

On March 5, Gram-positive cocci were cultured from both peripheral blood and PICC line blood samples of the infant (Fig. 3). The isolate exhibited hemolytic characteristics similar to S. aureus, and formed white, smooth, moist, and opaque colonies. The bacterial isolate from the blood culture system was identified as Staphylococcus capitis (S. capitis) using the VITEK 2 automated system. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) revealed that the isolate was susceptible to vancomycin, but resistant to oxacillin, penicillin, and erythromycin. six randomly selected bacterial isolates from blood plates, one catheter blood sample and related medical supplies, including unused disposable medical pads and indwelling needle heparin caps, were subsequently sent for metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS).

From March 6 to March 8, serial chest X-rays indicated persistent inflammatory lesions in the lungs (Fig. 2B). The infant presented with intermittent blood oxygen fluctuations. Repeated examinations revealed that infection-related biomarkers decreased markedly compared with prior levels, whereas the platelet (PLT) count continued to drop. Accordingly, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered for supportive therapy. Overall, the infant had no fever or impaired circulation, and her clinical condition was approaching normalization. Lumbar puncture was performed, and routine and biochemical testing of cerebrospinal fluid showed no obvious abnormalities. Therefore, suppurative meningitis was excluded.

On the night of March 9, the infant developed recurrent fever accompanied by tachycardia. The clinical team was vigilant for the possibility of superinfection and vancomycin-resistant S. capitis. Given this, (1,3)-β-D-glucan assay (G test) and Ureaplasma urealyticum (UU) testing were performed, and vancomycin was continued for antimicrobial therapy. In addition, mNGS results detected Staphylococcus gene sequences in the infant’s blood, which shared high similarity with sequences of S. warneri and S. aureus (Table 2), a finding inconsistent with the blood culture identification results. In contrast, no pathogenic bacteria were detected in the unused heparin caps and disposable medical pads.

Given the inconsistent identification results between blood culture and mNGS, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on the bacterial isolate for precise species identification. The sequencing data were analyzed using the fIDBAC platform [6]. Notably, at the genomic level, although the isolate shared the highest sequence similarity with S. warneri, its average nucleotide identity (ANI) value was below 95% (Table 3), which is the threshold for species delineation. Thus, the isolate was suspected to be a potential novel species of the Staphylococcus genus.

In microbiological studies, the cellular fatty acid profile of S. warneri is significantly different from that of other Staphylococcus species. To further characterize this isolate, its cellular fatty acid composition was determined. The results showed that the main cellular fatty acid components of this isolate were C16:0 (33.02%) and C18:0 (18.76%), the proportions of which were significantly higher than those of other Staphylococcus species. However, there was a clear discrepancy between this isolate and S. warneri, whose major cellular fatty acid components are C20:0 and C16:0. This indicated that this isolate also had significant differences in major cellular fatty acid composition from S. warneri[7].

At this point, the infant had received antimicrobial therapy with ceftazidime plus penicillin, and vancomycin plus meropenem for nearly 2 weeks, and the antimicrobial treatment appeared to be effective. However, the infant continued to present with tachycardia. After consultation with experts from Guangzhou Women and Children's Medical Center, a dexamethasone DART regimen was administered for 6 days, after which the infant’s heart rate returned to the normal range. Infection biomarkers returned to normal levels. Furthermore, no pathogenic bacteria were cultured from the catheter tip, and Ureaplasma urealyticum was not detected, indicating no active bacterial infection in the infant. The serum (1,3)-β-D-glucan level was 250.73 pg/mL. Although the G-test level was elevated, the infant presented without fever or circulatory dysfunction, and had a history of multiple blood transfusions. Therefore, fungal infection was excluded at this stage. On March 12, the endotracheal tube was extubated, and the infant was switched to non-invasive assisted ventilation in Neurally Adjusted Ventilatory Assist (nMCV/NAVA) mode.

On March 17, 7-day blood cultures showed no growth of common bacteria or fungi in samples from the infant. Follow-up chest X-ray showed that the pulmonary lesions had been largely absorbed (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, mechanical ventilation was discontinued. Repeated CBC showed no obvious abnormalities. Given the potential adverse effects of prolonged antibiotic therapy, and that vancomycin had been administered for 14 days, vancomycin was discontinued. Discussion

Although CoNS rarely cause infection in healthy individuals, the widespread use of interventional diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, immunosuppressants, and indwelling medical devices (including catheters and artificial joints) has significantly increased the incidence of CoNS-related infections, such as osteomyelitis, meningitis, and infective endocarditis.

The diagnosis and treatment course of this case showed that the infant’s condition deteriorated suddenly on March 3, and infection with a potential novel pathogenic bacterium was suspected. Furthermore, the preterm infant had neonatal pneumonia with multiple complications and impaired immunity, and was receiving invasive interventions including endotracheal intubation, which provides opportunities for CoNS to invade host tissues and establish infection via biofilm formation on the surface of indwelling devices [8]. In this case, the bacterial isolate cultured from the infant’s blood samples was compared with the microbial database, and was provisionally identified as S. capitis based on its biochemical reactions and phenotypic characteristics. The final test report indicated staphylococcal infection, and the isolate was classified as methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci (MRCNS), with resistance to all β-lactam antibiotics. MRCNS is a common cause of late-onset sepsis (LOS) in the NICU, which can lead to adverse outcomes including death and long-term morbidity of neonates [9], although S. capitis is generally considered a low-virulence species in normal populations. Furthermore, decreased susceptibility to vancomycin may contribute to therapeutic failure [10].

However, the bacterial colonies cultured from the infant’s blood not only exhibited hemolytic characteristics similar to S. aureus, but also had colony morphology resembling S. warneri. Subsequent mNGS testing of blood samples identified the pathogenic bacterium as most closely related to S. warneri or S. aureus at the genomic level, further supporting this observation. Finally, WGS analysis revealed that the ANI value of this isolate was less than 95%, the gold-standard threshold for bacterial species delineation. Therefore, the isolate could not be assigned to S. warneri or S. aureus, and was identified as a potentially novel Staphylococcus species. Phylogenetic tree analysis further revealed that the closest type strain of the seven sequenced isolates was S. warneri NCTC 11044 (Fig. 5). However, the genomic evidence demonstrates that the sequenced isolates belong to a single clonal lineage, but it does not definitively prove that the sequenced organism corresponds to the same organism initially identified by routine microbiology.

The pathogen most closely related to this isolate was S. warneri, and Gram-positive cocci were simultaneously cultured from both the catheter and blood samples of the infant. The bacterial isolate in this study has been properly preserved in the strain resource repository established by Longgang District Maternity & Child Healthcare Hospital of Shenzhen City. Relevant work to verify its taxonomic status and fulfill the formal criteria for bacterial species nomenclature is currently in progress.

We subsequently investigated the potential source of infection. Firstly, the infection occurred on postnatal day 17; therefore, maternal transmission was excluded, and screening of the infant’s mother was not performed. Secondly, bacterial culture was conducted for the environment of the NICU ward at that time, including the medication preparation station, medical pads, milk preparation station, skin disinfectants, incubators, heparin caps, and the hands of medical staff. No homologous bacteria were detected in any of the environmental samples.

Next, the potential transmission route was explored. Both peripheral blood culture and catheter blood culture specimens were collected from the infant. The time to positivity of catheter blood cultures was about 10 h longer than that of peripheral blood cultures, suggesting a lower likelihood of catheter-related bloodstream infection. Nevertheless, the catheter could still be considered a plausible source of infection as a result of biofilm formation. Skin sampling and testing at the PICC catheter insertion site were not performed. Combined with the environmental monitoring results at that time, no definite infection source or transmission route was identified. In addition, no colonization or infection caused by the same strain was found in other neonates in the same ward. The clinical improvement under broad-spectrum antibiotics makes attribution difficult. Therefore, the exact route of infection could not be determined, and further investigation is required.

A limitation of this study is that different single colonies were used for biochemical identification and WGS analysis. The strain was initially assigned to S. capitis by the VITEK 2 system after blood culture. Nevertheless, commercial biochemical-based identification platforms such as the VITEK 2 system exhibit limited reliability and low identification confidence for CoNS. This limitation may be attributed to the intrinsically low metabolic activity of CoNS and the short incubation time set by the instrument. A shorter incubation time may induce false-negative biochemical reactions, ultimately leading to species misidentification [11]. In addition, the databases of commercial biochemical platforms are limited and rarely updated in a timely manner, lacking reference profiles for rare species, potential novel species and regionally specific strains. Accumulated evidence from molecular studies has further verified that automated biochemical identification systems are less reliable than molecular typing approaches [12].

However, PCR-based molecular identification also has limitations: the specimen pretreatment process is cumbersome, making it difficult to routinely apply to the batch identification of every clinically isolated bacterial strain. Besides, clinical symptoms improved after empirical administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics, which makes it difficult to establish a clear causal relationship between the isolate and the clinical infection. Therefore, the clinical significance and pathogenic role of this potential novel strain cannot be fully determined based on the currently available clinical and laboratory data.

mNGS is increasingly applied in clinical microbiological practice, and offers the advantage of more accurate and rapid pathogen identification compared with conventional pathogen detection methods such as blood culture. mNGS exhibits excellent performance in identifying rare, novel, difficult-to-detect pathogens and co-infecting pathogens directly from clinical samples, and has significant potential for predicting antimicrobial resistance via sequencing of antibiotic resistance genes [13]. The most common and immediate application of WGS for clinical bacterial isolates is bacterial typing and outbreak tracing, which can significantly shorten clinical turnaround time and facilitate infection control [14]. The combination of mNGS and WGS with traditional clinical microbiological testing provides novel diagnostic evidence that can be used to guide clinical treatment regimens and optimize antimicrobial stewardship.

In conclusion, as advanced detection technologies, mNGS and WGS can facilitate the rapid and accurate identification of pathogens. In cases where patients experience sudden clinical deterioration or persistent non-resolution of infection despite antimicrobial treatment, the above technologies should be considered in a timely manner to facilitate pathogen identification and timely adjustment of clinical antimicrobial regimens. The discovery of this potential novel species also provides new insights, highlighting the need for heightened vigilance against the emergence of bacterial multidrug resistance and nosocomial infections.

Methods

Gene sequencing

DNA libraries were prepared with the Salus Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit (Salus, China). In brief, genomic DNA was fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, ligated to adapters, and PCR-amplified to incorporate sample indexes. For RNA library preparation, RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, followed by double-stranded cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. The VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, NR604) was used, and all steps were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

All libraries were quantified with the Qubit 1 × dsDNA HS Assay Kit, and their fragment size distributions were assessed on a LabChip GX Touch/GXII Touch Bioanalyzer. Qualified libraries were pooled, re-quantified, and the molar concentration of the pool was determined. Sequencing was performed on the Salus Pro sequencer (Salus) with the PRM-PE150 kit, using paired-end 150 bp, dual-barcode mode.

Data analysis

For mNGS data analysis, raw sequencing data were processed with fastp v0.24 to remove low-quality reads, adapter contamination and reads shorter than 35 bp. Human-derived reads were filtered out by alignment to the human genome sequence data with BWA v0.7.17. The remaining clean reads were aligned against an in-house curated pathogen database consisting of 16,691 bacterial, 280 fungal, 3712 viral, 514 archaeal, and 77 parasite genomes for taxonomic classification.

For WGS analysis, de novo genome assembly was performed with Unicycler v0.5.0. The assembled genome was analyzed on the fIDBAC platform, which included ANI calculation using FastANI v1.33; an ANI value < 95% was considered indicative of a potentially novel species. Phylogenomic analysis was carried out by uploading the genome to the Type (Strain) Genome Server (TYGS) for intergenomic distance calculation and construction of a phylogenomic tree against all type strain genomes of the genus Staphylococcus. For single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis at the read level, reads were mapped to the reference genome of Staphylococcus warneri NCTC 11044 (NCBI: GCF_900636385.1_41665_E01_genomic.fna) using Snippy v4.6.0, and pairwise SNP distances were computed by snp-dists v1.2.0.
