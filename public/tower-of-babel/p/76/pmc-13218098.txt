Experience, barriers and facilitators of discharge readiness in patients with acute pancreatitis: a descriptive qualitative study
Authors: Shiying Liu, Mei Xu, Wenzhe Hua, Daqiao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo explore patients’ experiences of discharge readiness following acute pancreatitis (AP) and to identify perceived barriers and facilitators influencing readiness for discharge during the transition from hospital to home.DesignA descriptive qualitative study guided by Meleis’ Transitions Theory.SettingEmergency and gastroenterology wards of Ren Ji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Shanghai, China.Participants16 patients with AP were recruited; all 16 eligible patients approached agreed to participate (100% response rate).MethodsData were collected between December 2024 and January 2025 through semistructured in-depth interviews. Data were analysed using Elo and Kyngäs’s three-step qualitative content analysis framework, integrating conventional and directed content analysis. The study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines.ResultsPatterns of response reflected variability in patients’ psychological adaptation during the transition process, including cognitive-emotional responses and relational interactions. Factors influencing discharge readiness were identified across three theoretical domains. Nature of Transitions captured the dynamics of self-management, including awareness, engagement and behavioural evolution. Transition Conditions reflected the complexity of socioecological determinants, including age and life stage, health literacy, financial resources and occupational and cultural influences. Nursing Therapeutics highlighted the discordance between professional support and patient needs, particularly in relation to information provision, dietary management and care continuity.ConclusionsBuilding on Meleis’ Transitions Theory, this study provides an empirically grounded understanding of discharge readiness among patients with AP as a dynamic, multifactorial process. Our findings extend the theory by demonstrating that discharge readiness is shaped by the interaction between individual self-management, socioecological determinants, psychological adaptations and professional support. Addressing gaps in professional health information, personalised and structured dietary management and continuity of care is essential to enhance discharge readiness and support safe, sustainable recovery.Keywords: Hospital to Home Transition, Nursing Care, Pancreatic disease, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is one of the most common emergencies in the digestive system, with a global annual incidence ranging from 3.4 to 73.4 per 100 000 population, and it continues to rise at an estimated rate of 2%–5% per year.1,3 Approximately 20% of patients develop severe AP (SAP) or experience recurrent episodes,4 with recurrence significantly increasing the risk of progression to chronic pancreatitis.5 These adverse outcomes substantially contribute to high rates of unplanned hospital readmission (up to 27.7%) and a decline in health-related quality of life.6 7 Existing evidence indicates that unplanned readmission and recurrence after AP are frequently attributable to inadequate postdischarge management, including inappropriate dietary behaviour, limited knowledge of low-fat diets, poor medication adherence and delayed recognition of symptoms.8,11 Consequently, optimising postdischarge preparation and support has emerged as a critical strategy for reducing unplanned readmissions and improving recovery outcomes among patients with AP.

Extensive evidence has demonstrated that adequate discharge readiness is associated with lower rates of unplanned readmission, shorter lengths of hospital stay and reduced all-cause mortality.12,15 The concept of discharge readiness, originally introduced by Fenwick,16 has since undergone continuous refinement and theoretical development. Galvin et al17 reconceptualised it as both a state and a dynamic process, and highlighted its role in facilitating the safe and effective transition of patients from hospital to home, community or long-term care settings. Within this framework, discharge readiness comprises four interrelated domains: (1) physical stability, including sufficient functional capacity and the ability to engage in self-care at home; (2) adequate emotional and practical support to address diverse postdischarge needs; (3) psychological preparedness, reflected in patients’ confidence and self-efficacy to manage transitional challenges and (4) access to sufficient information and knowledge to effectively cope with common problems following hospitalisation. Collectively, these multidimensional components directly address critical gaps in postdischarge management among patients with AP, thereby underscoring the potential relevance of discharge readiness in reducing readmission risk and improving longer-term outcomes.

Over the past decades, research on discharge readiness has gained increasing prominence. Initial investigations focused predominantly on vulnerable populations, including geriatric and paediatric patients, while more recent studies have extended to encompass individuals with chronic conditions and those undergoing major surgery.18,22 Notably, the latest research has begun to investigate the multifaceted and interactive determinants of discharge readiness in patients with acute myocardial infarction.23 Although the concept of discharge readiness has been widely applied across various patient populations, research focusing on AP remains limited. The few existing studies are predominantly quantitative, emphasising the measurement of discharge readiness and its demographic and disease-related influencing factors.24 While these studies objectively assess the level of discharge readiness, they fail to capture patients’ subjective experiences, behavioural adaptations, and contextual influences that shape discharge readiness. In contrast, qualitative inquiry enables in-depth exploration of how and why discharge readiness develops within specific sociocultural, interpersonal, and healthcare contexts, thereby revealing critical determinants that may not be readily captured through quantitative methods. Therefore, qualitative research is warranted to provide a more nuanced and holistic understanding of the barriers, facilitators, and experiences that shape discharge readiness among patients with AP.

Meleis’ Transitions Theory, a middle-range nursing theory, provides a comprehensive framework for understanding patients’ experiences during transitional processes. It comprises four core dimensions—Nature of Transitions, Transition Conditions, Patterns of Response, and Nursing Therapeutics.25 This theory has been extensively applied in discharge readiness research across diverse methodological approaches—ranging from qualitative studies and scale development to intervention design—providing systematic guidance to promote smooth and effective transitions from hospital to home.2026,28 In the context of discharge readiness, Nature of Transitions captures the type, pattern, and properties of change patients experience when moving from hospital to home, primarily encompassing situational and health–illness transitions; Transition Conditions represent the facilitators and barriers that influence this process; Patterns of Response reflect the patients’ adaptive outcomes and coping strategies, both psychological and behavioural, that reflect the degree of adjustment to postdischarge life; and Nursing Therapeutics denotes the targeted interventions that nurses employ to guide, support, and empower patients during this transition. Guided by this theoretical framework, the present study aims to conduct in-depth interviews with AP patients to explore their experiences and identify barriers and facilitators influencing discharge readiness, thereby generating evidence to inform the development of tailored nursing interventions.

Method

Study design

This study employed a descriptive qualitative design, grounded in the principles of naturalistic inquiry, to explore the discharge readiness experiences of patients with AP. This approach seeks to remain close to the data and participants’ own language, thereby capturing explicit facts and surface-level experiences.29 Data were analysed using a combination of conventional and directed content analysis to explore their experiences and identify barriers and facilitators of discharge readiness.

Participants and setting

Recruitment was conducted by the researcher between December 2024 and January 2025 in the emergency and gastroenterology wards of Ren Ji Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, a tertiary hospital in Shanghai, China. Most participants were admitted via the emergency department with acute abdominal pain and were subsequently managed in general wards. Treatment primarily focused on conservative medical management.

Eligible participants were adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with AP according to the diagnostic criteria of the,1 who were clinically stable, able to communicate and comprehend interview questions, and willing to participate. Patients with severe physical or psychiatric conditions that would interfere with cooperation were excluded. A total of 16 eligible patients were approached, and all (100%) agreed to participate. Participation was voluntary, and no incentives or compensations were offered.

A purposive sampling strategy was used to capture variation in age, sex, occupation, educational level, number of AP episodes, disease severity, aetiology and comorbidities. To ensure confidentiality, numerical identifiers (N1–N16) were assigned and all personal details were removed from the transcripts. A nursing researcher (SL) initially approached these participants, provided oral and written study information, and obtained their written consent. Formal interviews were then conducted 1–2 days before discharge. The sample size was guided by the principle of data saturation, ceasing recruitment when no new themes emerged.

Clinical management and discharge criteria

During hospitalisation, participants received standard medical management according to institutional protocols. Specifically, patients with biliary pancreatitis were advised on follow-up surgical consultations where appropriate; those with hypertriglyceridaemia-associated pancreatitis were initiated on lipid-lowering pharmacotherapy; and individuals with alcohol-related AP received counselling on alcohol cessation.

Patients were considered clinically suitable for discharge when they were afebrile and achieved resolution of abdominal pain, distension, nausea and vomiting. Further criteria included the return of normal bowel function, tolerance of oral intake, and significant improvement in inflammatory markers, with no evidence of complications requiring continued inpatient management.

Standardised discharge education was provided to all patients, encompassing written guidance on stepped dietary advancement from fat-free to low-fat intake, lifestyle modification, and follow-up recommendations within 2 weeks after discharge, including laboratory tests (eg, serum amylase, inflammatory markers and liver function) and imaging examinations (eg, ultrasound or CT). Follow-up care remains recommendation-based, with patients responsible for scheduling and attending these appointments.

An interview guide was developed based on a review of the literature on discharge readiness and informed by Meleis’ Transitions Theory. A pilot interview was conducted with one patient to assess the clarity and relevance of the questions. Feedback from the pilot, together with expert input from a specialist in qualitative research methods, was incorporated to refine the guide, which was finalised as shown in table 1. The guide was used as a framework for data collection while allowing flexibility to follow participants’ narratives.

Each interview was conducted face-to-face in a semistructured format, with one nursing researcher (SL) present in a quiet ward meeting room. All interviews were audio-recorded and lasted no longer than 60 min. Before each interview, participants were reminded of the study’s purpose.

During the interviews, the researcher (SL) created a supportive atmosphere and clarified that there were no right or wrong answers, encouraging the participants to speak freely. Probing questions, reflective listening, and summarising were employed to clarify or expand participants’ views. In addition to the recordings, field notes were taken to capture tone, expressions, and non-verbal behaviours. Clinical data, including demographic characteristics, AP severity, aetiology, length of hospital stay, triggers, and comorbidities, were extracted from electronic medical records to describe participants’ clinical profiles. Disease severity (mild, moderately severe, or severe) was defined according to the Revised Atlanta criteria; assessments were performed by gastroenterologists using established scoring systems (Ranson criteria and Modified CT Severity Index), integrated with routine clinical and imaging evaluations.

Data analysis

Within 24 hours after each interview, audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, with non-verbal cues (tone, facial expressions, gestures) integrated to enhance contextual understanding. Ambiguous content was returned to participants for clarification. Transcripts were imported into ATLAS.ti (Web version) for coding and analysis. Analytic memos were maintained to record coding decisions and track the development of interpretations.

A combined inductive and deductive content analysis approach30 was used by two researchers (SL and WH) during the preparation, organisation, and reporting phases31 (figure 1). In the preparation phase, transcripts were read repeatedly for immersion and holistic understanding. During the organisation phase, a bottom-up inductive process was first applied: meaningful text segments were condensed into meaning units and subsequently labelled with codes. These codes were then grouped based on similarities in meaning and further abstracted into higher-order conceptual categories. This inductive coding captured the participants’ authentic perspectives and ensured that novel insights and nuances in the data were not overlooked by theoretical assumptions. Following this, a top-down deductive process was applied, in which Meleis’ Transitions Theory was used to iteratively interpret the inductively derived conceptual categories through ongoing comparison with its subdimensions, enabling a dynamic alignment between data and theory. For example, the statement ‘I only started thinking about losing weight when the illness recurred’ was coded as ‘postponed weight management’, which, together with ‘active health information seeking’ and ‘neglected biological marker monitoring’, was interpreted as reflecting varying degrees of patients’ involvement in managing their disease, ranging from proactive engagement to delayed or absent action. This conceptualisation was aligned with the ‘Engagement’ subdimension of Meleis’ Transitions Theory, thereby informing the categorisation of these codes as ‘engagement in self-management’. Building on these categories, explanatory theme labels aligned with the corresponding theoretical dimensions were generated to capture the central meanings and underlying patterns within the data.

Analytical process of the combined inductive–deductive content analysis based on Meleis’ Transitions Theory.

While no new theoretical categories emerged, we actively examined the extent to which the data aligned with the dimensions of Meleis’ Transitions Theory through an iterative analytic process. Rather than treating discrepancies as evidence of theoretical misfit, we revisited and reinterpreted the data through ongoing comparison of empirical findings with theoretical constructs. This iterative process allowed seemingly divergent data to be understood as different expressions within the same theoretical dimensions.

In the reporting phase, the synthesised codes, categories, and themes were systematically organised into a hierarchical coding tree. These components were further categorised into three overarching domains: experiences, barriers or facilitators of discharge readiness. Finally, illustrative quotes were extracted from the transcripts to support each code and category.

Trustworthiness

The trustworthiness of this study was ensured through strategies addressing credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability.32 All interviews were conducted by SL, a clinical nurse working in a gastroenterology unit, who was undertaking a master’s degree in nursing and had received formal training in qualitative research methods. Through her clinical practice, she had observed persistently high rates of disease recurrence among patients with AP, which motivated the initial research interest. At the same time, the researcher acknowledged that her clinical background and prior experiences could influence data interpretation. To address this potential bias, reflexivity was actively practised throughout the study. Reflexivity was maintained through continuous analytic awareness throughout the study, supported by the use of field notes and analytic memos. Trusting relationships were fostered through repeated contacts and nonjudgmental listening, which encouraged participants to speak candidly. To ensure analytical rigour and consistency, original transcripts were independently coded by two researchers (SL and WH) and cross-verified by two additional researchers (MX and DZ) with expertise in gastroenterology and qualitative methods. In cases of divergent interpretations, disagreements were resolved through iterative consensus meetings. Ambiguous content was returned to participants to accurately represent their lived experiences. A thick description of the study context, participant characteristics and illustrative quotations was provided to support transferability. Rigour and transparency were ensured by adhering to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist.33

Patient and public involvement

One patient with AP was involved in the early stages of the study and contributed to the development of the interview guide.

Results

A total of 16 participants were interviewed, yielding a 100% response rate. Participants demonstrated considerable heterogeneity in demographic characteristics, disease history, and clinical background. The sample included 10 men and 6 women, aged 29–68 years. Most participants were employed (n=11) or self-employed (n=2), and the majority had attained university-level education or higher (n=12). Half of the participants (n=8) were experiencing their first episode of AP, whereas the remaining half (n=8) reported recurrent episodes. Disease severity varied across participants, including mild (n=10), moderately severe (n=1), and SAP (n=5). Metabolic and biliary conditions were common, particularly hyperlipidaemia (n=10) and gallstone disease (n=4), while hypertension (n=2) and fatty liver disease (n=1) were less frequently reported. Treatment consisted of conservative medical management in all cases. None of the participants required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU), surgical intervention, endoscopic intervention, or discharge with drains during the index hospitalisation. The length of hospital stay ranged from 6 to 19 days. The most commonly reported triggers were fatty diet (n=8), followed by heavy meal (n=3) and alcohol consumption (n=2). Collectively, this diversity provided a broad contextual basis for exploring variations in discharge readiness among patients with AP (see table 2).

Nature of transitions: dynamics of self-management

Awareness of self-management

Many patients initially demonstrated limited perception of disease severity and persistence of habitual lifestyle beliefs, often underestimating the risk of recurrence. A particularly notable barrier was the attribution of control to medication, where participants externalised the responsibility for sustained health.

N5: “I was thinking maybe I can just control it with medication.”

N1: “That morning, the doctor told me I needed to be hospitalized. But I thought it probably wasn’t a big deal—it didn’t even hurt that much at the time. After they gave me a painkiller injection and some medication, the pain went away. Since the pain was gone, I just stopped caring about it.”

Conversely, as the transition progressed, some participants showed an emerging sense of personal responsibility and increased understanding of disease-related information. They began to recognise that medical intervention was only one facet of recovery, suggesting a shift toward internalising self-management roles.

N3: “Ultimately, it all comes down to yourself. You have to depend on yourself. If you try to rely on others, they are just not reliable after all.”

Engagement in self-management

Some participants demonstrated proactive dietary planning and active health information seeking while still in the ward. They expressed a strong commitment to the avoidance of risk behaviours as part of their immediate discharge goals.

N5: “Anyway, once I go back [home], I’ve decided to quit smoking and drinking entirely. ”

However, many others, especially those with recurrent AP, reflected on their previous low adherence to postdischarge rehabilitation plans (eg, etiological modification, biological marker monitoring and weight management), which allowed disease triggers to persist and increase the risk of readmission.

N3: “Only when I got sick again did I start thinking, maybe I should lose some weight.”

N4: “After I recovered last time, I didn’t take it seriously. Otherwise, I should have come back in a month or two to check my blood glucose and lipids.”

Evolution of self-management

On the one hand, participants with a history of recurrence reflected on declining adherence over time, as evidenced by attenuated therapeutic adherence and diminished vigilance following symptom resolution. On the other hand, some participants described prospective adjustments, including intentions to establish routine self-monitoring, adopt dietary modifications and engage in regular physical activity. These patterns indicate that self-management evolves over time through fluctuating levels of engagement, underscoring the temporal nature of discharge readiness and the need for sustained motivation and ongoing professional support.

N4: “Yes, I plan to start exercising, maybe on weekends when I have time.”

N7: “The doctor prescribed lipid-lowering drugs and fish oil. At first I took them, but in the past six months I rarely take fish oil, and I haven’t really controlled my diet.”

Transition conditions: complexity of socioecological determinants

Age and life stage

Age and life stage shape both opportunities and constraints for effective discharge readiness. Younger patients, who rapidly return to work and social roles after discharge, often face competing demands between social roles and health management, limiting their time and capacity to engage in preparatory behaviours.

N14: “Young people are really busy. If I had the time, I would pay more attention to these indicators, but long-term attention isn’t realistic. The disease is just a part of my life.”

In contrast, older patients often demonstrate heightened health consciousness and a stronger motivation to maintain health to fulfil family responsibilities. However, despite this increased awareness and sense of responsibility, they frequently encounter limited physical capacity and insufficient family or social support, particularly among those living alone, which hinders their ability to sustain postdischarge adjustment.

N15: “Oh, I still have to take care of the little one(grandson). We simply can’t afford to be sick. I’ll contact you if any problems come up.”

N16: “I can’t trouble her(daughter) all the time; she has her own family. I, as an older man with poor health and a history of stroke, still have to handle the household chores. Sometimes there’s just no way…”

Health literacy

Health literacy functioned as a multidimensional determinant of discharge readiness, encompassing knowledge, practical skills and evaluative capacity. Limited health literacy was reflected in insufficient understanding of disease triggers, difficulties in preparing therapeutic meals, and challenges in evaluating health information sources, all of which constrained patients’ ability to engage effectively in self-management.

N12: “At first, I didn’t even know what blood lipids were, I didn’t know what it meant. When the doctor asked me, I had no idea, I never measured it.”

Conversely, some participants demonstrated proficiency in nutritional and dietary knowledge, which facilitated more informed decision-making and supported their readiness for postdischarge self-management.

N9: “I think there should be no problem. I usually do the cooking at home, so I am well-aware of the fat and protein content of the ingredients I use.”

Financial resources

Financial resources functioned as a key structural determinant of discharge readiness. On the one hand, substantial out-of-pocket medical costs limited patients’ willingness and ability to engage in follow-up care, leading to delays or avoidance of monitoring and treatment adjustments. On the other hand, adequate medical insurance coverage facilitated access to follow-up services and supported continuity in postdischarge care, thereby enabling more consistent engagement in recovery plans. These findings highlight that financial factors shape patients’ capacity for discharge readiness by influencing access to necessary healthcare resources.

N12: “I didn’t go back for further check-ups, partly because I was not working at the time. So my last hospitalization was fully self-paid.”

Occupational and cultural influences

High-intensity work rhythms and irregular schedules constrained patients’ capacity to maintain consistent health behaviours, while pervasive social dining culture and work-related drinking expectations introduced additional challenges to adhering to dietary recommendations. Conversely, occupational proximity to healthcare resources facilitated access to professional support and information, thereby enhancing patients’ ability to engage in self-management. These findings suggest that discharge readiness should also encompass preparation for navigating social and workplace environments, enabling patients to anticipate and manage potential conflicts between health goals and everyday social expectations.

N11: “I try to minimize social dinners, but sometimes in society you just can’t avoid it.”

N3:“It’s about mobilizing resources. I need to find out the root cause—this is the second time [it has happened]… I must mobilize all kinds of resources, from hepatobiliary and pancreatic specialists to liver experts, to gather information from multiple perspectives.”

Patterns of response: variability of psychological adaptations

Cognitive-emotional responses

Patients reflected diverse patterns of psychological adaptation during the discharge transition. Some participants expressed confidence in their capacity to manage their condition, indicating a sense of control and readiness for self-management. In contrast, others reported heightened worry about severe health outcomes, reflecting uncertainty and emotional distress in response to the illness.

N8: “Could this trigger pancreatic cancer(cry)?”

In addition, certain participants demonstrated vigilance toward potential triggers, actively monitoring risks and anticipating recurrence, whereas others exhibited a sense of futility following repeated relapse, perceiving limited effectiveness of their efforts in controlling the disease.

N6: “For me, this illness is a red light. If I don’t take it seriously, maybe (with a wry smile)…”

N10: “I’ve avoided some foods based on online info, but with repeated relapses, there’s nothing much I can do—doctors can’t fix it either.”

Another pattern involved over-reliance on emergency medical intervention, where a false sense of security derived from acute care services led to the de-prioritisation of daily self-management.

N13: “As long as I call 120 in time, I won’t die from this disease.”

Taken together, these responses illustrate the variability of cognitive–emotional adaptation, ranging from confidence and proactive vigilance to worry and perceived futility, highlighting differing levels of psychological readiness during the transition process.

Relational interactions

Relational interactions constitute an important component of the transition process. Some participants demonstrated self-protective withdrawal from social support, emphasising independence while limiting external involvement, which may reduce access to practical assistance and emotional reassurance. In contrast, others described a strong sense of belonging through family support, which provided emotional stability and reinforced their confidence to engage in discharge readiness.

N2: “I told them not to come—don’t bother. Being sick already makes me feel unlucky; if you annoy me more, it’s worse.”

N1: “My mom, dad, in-laws, sister, husband—they all care about me deeply. And my little one(son) is so good, thinking about them makes me feel content (with a smile).”

Nursing therapeutics: discordance between professional support and patient needs

Information provision

Access to health information alone did not ensure its effective use during the transition process; rather, participants’ engagement with information was shaped by its perceived credibility and relevance. Although online platforms and social media provided readily accessible information, participants often questioned the reliability and applicability of such sources, which generated uncertainty and limited their confidence in implementing recommendations. In contrast, information delivered by specialists in tertiary-level institutions was perceived as more authoritative and trustworthy, and therefore more actionable. This contrast highlights a misalignment between information accessibility and perceived credibility, where readily available resources did not necessarily meet patients’ needs for reliable, individualised guidance.

N12: “I might try to piece together information from online searches, but it’s probably not very authoritative. I prefer to consult doctors directly and follow their recommendations.”

Dietary management

Dietary management reflected challenges in translating dietary guidance into everyday practice during the discharge transition. Although structured dietary frameworks were available in hospital settings, the lack of personalised meal planning limited their applicability in patients’ daily lives. As a result, participants described cognitive gaps in operationalising dietary guidance, particularly when attempting to distinguish, quantify, and apply abstract concepts such as ‘low-fat’ or ‘fat-free’ diets in daily life. These challenges were further compounded by the presence of multiple comorbidities, where conflicting dietary demands led to confusion and uncertainty in decision-making.

N12: “The concepts of low-fat and fat-free diets…there are so many foods in daily life, I don’t know how to distinguish or measure them. I don’t have a clear boundary.……I kept wondering whether these would be the only foods I could eat from now on… that I might never be able to enjoy the oily or spicy dishes I used to love again.”

N3: “I have diabetes, and then high blood lipids, plus I’m experiencing pancreatitis again. I tried to control my diet, almost skipping regular meals and eating only high-protein foods like lamb, beef, so does that mean I can only eat vegetables and fruits from now on?”

Care continuity

Despite participants’ proactive efforts to seek specialised guidance after stabilisation, accessing appropriate follow-up care was often fragmented and difficult to navigate. Many participants expressed confusion regarding which specific subspecialty should manage their long-term recovery. In addition, inadequacy of community care further constrained continuity. Participants frequently perceived these services as insufficient and expressed low levels of trust. This situation was compounded by the absence of formalised referral pathways from higher-level hospitals, leaving patients to independently coordinate their care across different providers. Together, these barriers point to gaps in the organisation and integration of postdischarge care, where the burden of continuity is largely shifted onto patients themselves.

N6: “I look online for specialists since I don’t know anyone personally. Do you have such services?”

N2: “I won’t go [to the community center], absolutely not. Those places are just for ‘fooling’ people over 80 years old.”

Discussion

Guided by Meleis’ Transitions Theory, this study identified the transitional experiences, barriers, and facilitators influencing discharge readiness among patients with AP. The findings demonstrate that discharge readiness is shaped by individual self-management, socioecological determinants, psychological adaptations, and professional support, all of which unfold within the transition process. By mapping these dimensions within Meleis’ Transitions Theory, the study provides a foundation for developing more responsive, theory-informed nursing interventions that strengthen discharge readiness and support a smoother transition from hospital to home.

Although previous studies have suggested that lower educational attainment is associated with poorer discharge readiness,34 our findings indicate that formal education does not necessarily translate into disease-specific health literacy or effective readiness for transition in AP. Despite most participants holding a university degree, many continued to interpret AP as a transient gastrointestinal episode rather than a condition requiring sustained dietary and lifestyle management. This limited illness understanding reflects a deficient awareness of self-management and may partially explain the high recurrence rate observed among the participants. Our analysis further suggests that patients who developed an increased awareness of the need for lifestyle modification and re-evaluated their health perceptions demonstrated early engagement in self-management, which in some cases progressed toward the establishment of routine health behaviours. This pattern reflects the dynamics of self-management during the transition process, where shifts in awareness may generate intrinsic motivation that initiates engagement, and sustained engagement may further translate into more stable behavioural routines.35 36 Evidence suggests that early motivational interviewing, combined with brief action planning, can effectively promote engagement in changing high-risk health behaviours.37

Nevertheless, motivation alone is insufficient; it is shaped by a constellation of transition conditions, including age and life stage, health literacy, financial resources, occupational and cultural influences. Two studies reported age-related differences in discharge readiness, with younger patients (<60 years) often showing higher overall readiness.34 38 However, our results revealed that the peak onset age for AP typically falls around 40 years, a life stage characterised by career advancement, frequent social responsibilities, and substantial role obligations. These pressures can conflict with adherence to healthy eating patterns, participation in regular exercise, and the ability to allocate time for structured self-care. These challenges are further amplified in urban Chinese contexts, where work-related social interactions commonly involve shared meals and alcohol consumption. Such sociocultural expectations may create tensions between health-related behavioural changes and occupational or social responsibilities, making sustained lifestyle modification particularly difficult after discharge.39 Furthermore, structural features of the Chinese healthcare system, particularly the financial burden of outpatient care, may significantly shape patients’ discharge readiness. Although basic medical insurance has achieved near-universal coverage, the relatively low reimbursement rates and strict outpatient caps in tertiary hospitals create substantial barriers for vulnerable populations, such as the unemployed, rural residents and students.40 These out-of-pocket expenses for medications, laboratory tests and imaging examinations may remain substantial. This financial burden may discourage patients from engaging in recommended follow-up and long-term self-management, thereby influencing discharge readiness. These findings suggest that discharge readiness is not solely an individual attribute but is embedded within broader health systems and sociocultural contexts.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that discharge readiness is not a single patient attribute but a dynamic interplay of transition conditions and transition natures. Therefore, stratifying patients by demographic, socioeconomic and sociocultural characteristics—and incorporating these differentiated needs into discharge planning—may enhance readiness and promote safer, more sustainable transitions from hospital to home.41

Prior research has underscored the psychological dimension as a critical component of patients’ transition from hospital to home.42 Our findings further indicate that psychological adaptation functions as a process indicator of discharge readiness, reflecting whether patients are progressing toward an effective and adaptive transition. Patients with AP demonstrated diverse psychological adaptation patterns during discharge readiness, which signalled different stages of the transition process. Negative psychological responses were reflected in participants’ accounts as heightened worry about severe health outcomes, a sense of futility following recurrent episodes, a false sense of security and self-protective withdrawal from social support. These patterns suggest reduced perceived control and challenges in sustaining engagement with postdischarge self-management.43 In contrast, more adaptive responses included confidence in self-management capacity, vigilance towards potential triggers and a strengthened sense of belonging through family support, which were associated with more proactive coping and better alignment with the demands of recovery.38 44 45 In this sense, psychological responses operate as dynamic markers of transition progress, offering early signals of potential maladaptation or delayed readiness.

Recognising psychological adaptation as a process indicator enables healthcare providers to monitor patients’ transition trajectories with greater precision and to deliver timely, individualised support. Approaches such as ecological momentary assessment and just-in-time adaptive interventions can operationalise these indicators by capturing real-time fluctuations in patients’ emotional states, confidence and coping strategies.46,48 Strengthening self-efficacy and psychological resilience—through encouragement and structured feedback—has been shown to promote more favourable transition processes.3849,51 Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of incorporating psychological process indicators into discharge readiness assessment, enabling clinical nurses to detect emerging challenges early and proactively support transition competence and discharge readiness for patients with AP.

Our study highlights that dietary management during the discharge readiness for patients with AP remains insufficient. Participants frequently described cognitive gaps in operationalising dietary guidance, indicating that although recommendations were provided, they were often insufficiently specific to guide day-to-day decision-making. Given that high-quality discharge teaching is a well-established predictor of discharge readiness, the absence of detailed, stage-specific dietary guidance weakens patients’ capacity for self-care, highlighting the need for clearer and more structured content.3452,54 These findings suggest that dietary management should move beyond generic recommendations toward the provision of personalised, stage-specific dietary frameworks that clearly delineate progression from early recovery to long-term maintenance. Such personalised guidance may help reduce ambiguity, support behavioural implementation and align dietary practices with patients’ real-life contexts.

Second, participants consistently expressed strong trust in the expertise of tertiary-level hospitals and specialist clinicians. However, limited consultation time and constrained opportunities for in-depth communication reduced patients’ ability to fully understand and retain professional advice. As a result, many participants turned to online sources, including social media platforms and search engines, to supplement their knowledge. While these sources were valued for their accessibility and immediacy, participants frequently questioned their reliability, reflecting a tension between informational convenience and trustworthiness.55 This gap highlights the need for more accessible, continuous and professionally guided information delivery systems. Digital health technologies offer promising solutions to bridge this gap by enhancing the accessibility, timeliness and professionalisation of health information. The convenience of remote data collection and intelligent analysis signals transformative potential for future healthcare delivery. Mobile applications, remote monitoring systems and AI-assisted tools can track dietary behaviours, detect risks, thereby enabling nurses to provide timely, more responsive and more tailored guidance than is possible through conventional education alone.18 56 57

Third, at the system level, effective discharge support requires transitioning from isolated, inpatient-based education to an integrated continuity-of-care model. The American Hospital Association emphasises that discharge readiness is not an isolated event but a longitudinal process that continues into home recovery and therefore requires sustained professional support.58 Evidence shows that dedicated roles, such as discharge coordinators and pancreatitis specialist nurses, improve patient flow, enhance care transitions and reduce unplanned readmissions.59 60 Embedding these roles within a coordinated postdischarge framework, supported by nursing-led clinics that provide ongoing contact, referrals, resource navigation and health education, would allow discharge readiness to be extended and translated into sustainable self-management at home.

However, the availability and integration of specialist follow-up services and digital health platforms remain variable in practice. In the Chinese context, emerging models such as nurse-led specialist outpatient clinics and ‘internet+nursing services’ have been developed to extend professional support beyond hospital settings.61 While these models offer opportunities for follow-up care through both face-to-face and digital modalities, their implementation is often fragmented and largely driven by pilot or institution-specific initiatives. These services are more commonly applied to populations with chronic conditions or ongoing care needs. In comparison, patients with acute conditions—such as AP and acute myocardial infarction—may receive less structured postdischarge support, despite requiring complex and stage-specific self-management. This mismatch between service delivery models and patient needs may contribute to the gaps in professional guidance, personalisation and continuity of care identified in this study. Although the specific forms of follow-up care and digital health infrastructure differ across healthcare systems, similar challenges in supporting acute care transitions may exist and constrain discharge readiness across contexts.

Taken together, professionalising health information delivery, providing structured dietary management frameworks and establishing integrated continuity-of-care structures represent complementary strategies for optimising discharge readiness. By addressing these multidimensional needs, healthcare providers can move beyond generic instruction to foster a more resilient transition, ultimately promoting sustainable recovery and long-term health stability in patients with AP.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited exclusively from a single urban tertiary hospital, which may limit the transferability of the findings to populations in different socio-economic contexts. In urban settings, discharge readiness is uniquely challenged by high-pressure work environments and a pervasive social dining culture, which complicate dietary and lifestyle adherence. Conversely, in rural areas, different barriers may predominate, such as lower reimbursement rates for outpatient services and more constrained individual economic resources, which may prioritise financial survival over clinical follow-up. Second, most participants were recruited from general wards and were managed conservatively. Those requiring ICU admission, surgical interventions, endoscopic interventions or discharged with drains were not represented. Therefore, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of patients with more severe forms of AP who require intensive or surgical management. Third, the sample included both first-episode and recurrent AP patients. However, differences in discharge readiness between these groups were not formally examined. Future studies may further explore how prior illness experience influences patients’ perceptions and self-management needs during the transition from hospital to home. Finally, interviews were conducted 1–2 days before discharge, which may primarily capture anticipatory readiness rather than experienced readiness after returning home. Future longitudinal research is therefore needed to explore how these determinants evolve and influence transitional processes, thereby informing more targeted, theory-driven nursing interventions for AP.

Conclusions

Building on Meleis’ Transitions Theory, this study provides an empirically grounded understanding of discharge readiness among AP patients as a dynamic, multifactorial process. Our findings extend the theory by demonstrating that discharge readiness is shaped by the interaction between individual self-management, socio-ecological conditions, psychological adaptations and professional support. Addressing gaps in professional health information, structured dietary management and continuity of care is essential to strengthen discharge readiness. These findings support the development of patient-centred, theory-informed transitional care strategies to promote safe discharge and sustainable recovery.
