Concurrent Development of B‐Cell Lymphoblastic Lymphoma and Histiocytic Sarcoma in a C57BL/6 Laboratory Mouse: A Case Report
Authors: Yujeong Lee, Minkyoung Sung, Soo‐Eun Sung, Kyung‐Ku Kang, Min‐Gyeong Seo, Il‐Hwa Hong, Sijoon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a rare case of a spontaneous composite tumour with the histopathological characteristics of B‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma and histiocytic sarcoma in a 104‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mouse. It appeared to be a large, well‐circumscribed intra‐abdominal mass, presumably originating from a mesenteric lymph node. Histological examination revealed two distinct neoplastic cell populations, including small‐ to intermediate‐sized CD20‐ and Pax5‐positive lymphoid cells and large pleomorphic histiocytes expressing Iba1. Both cell types were strongly stained by proliferating cell nuclear antigen, which is indicative of high proliferative activity. No evidence of metastases was observed in other organs. While single cases of similar composite neoplasms have been reported in humans, this is the first documented case in a laboratory mouse. These findings expand the known spectrum of spontaneous hematopoietic tumours in aged mice and highlight the importance of comprehensive histopathological and immunophenotypic evaluation in diagnosing atypical lesions.

Introduction

Spontaneous hematopoietic neoplasms occur relatively frequently in aged laboratory mice, particularly those with lymphoid lineages, such as B‐ and T‐cell lymphomas (Gardner et al. 1973, Mahler et al. 1996, Ward 2006). Previous studies have reported that spontaneous neoplastic lesions, including hematopoietic tumours, occur in a substantial proportion of aged laboratory mice (approximately 55%–80% in SPF C57BL/6 and related strains), although exact incidence varies with strain, sex and study conditions (Nakamura et al. 1992). Among these, lymphoblastic lymphoma is a well‐characterised subtype, marked by the proliferation of immature lymphoid cells with a high mitotic activity, thus potentially provoking diffuse organ infiltration (Rehg et al. 2015, Sung et al. 2025, Thomas et al. 1989). Although histiocytic sarcomas are less commonly encountered, they have also been detected in mice and are characterised by pleomorphic histiocytes with a tendency to phagocytosis and variable immunophenotypes (Janke and Rehg 2023, Ohnishi et al. 2012). However, the concurrent occurrence of these two morphologically and immunophenotypically distinct hematopoietic neoplasms within a single tumour mass is relatively rare in laboratory animals (Hao et al. 2010).

In humans, rare cases of composite or histiocyte‐rich B‐cell lymphomas have been previously described, particularly for the latter, which are admixed with a large population of histiocytes (Congyang et al. 2011, Esper et al. 2021, Hancock et al. 1999, Kan et al. 2008). These lesions are of substantial diagnostic and biological value because of their heterogeneous cellular structure and potential clinical implications (Esper et al. 2021, Abramson 2006, Pittaluga and Jaffe 2010). While such composite or mixed neoplasms have been detected in humans, the spontaneous occurrences thereof in experimental animals, particularly mice, are very rarely identified.

Herein, we report a unique case of a spontaneous, large intra‐abdominal tumour in a 104‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mouse. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses revealed two distinct neoplastic populations, including immature B‐lineage lymphoid and pleomorphic histiocytic cells. To the best of our knowledge, this is an exceptionally rare instance of a spontaneous composite hematopoietic neoplasm in a laboratory mouse. Our findings potentially contribute to the broader understanding of naturally occurring tumours in aged rodent models.

Case Presentation

A 104‐week‐old male C57BL/6 mouse procured from KOATECH, Inc. (Pyeongtaek, Korea) was employed as an untreated control for background histopathological evaluation at the Laboratory Animal Resources Bank, with further subjection to terminal necropsy. After a week of acclimation, the mouse was maintained under constant temperature, humidity and light conditions (24 ± 2°C, 50% ± 20% relative humidity, and 12/12 h light/dark cycle with lights on at 07:00). Moreover, the mouse had ad libitum access to food and water. The treatment protocol and experiment were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Preclinical Research Centre of the KMEDI hub (approval no. KMEDI‐24111202‐00), and the appropriate guidelines were duly followed. The mouse had not undergone any experimental treatment during its 24‐month lifespan. Routine sentinel health monitoring of the animal facility during the study period confirmed that the colony was free of known murine infectious agents that could influence hematopoietic or lymphoid lesions.

At necropsy, the organ weights were found to be as follows: brain, 0.49; liver, 2.99; kidney, 0.58; heart, 0.28; spleen, 0.26; lung, 0.24; pituitary gland, 0.002; testis, 0.14; and epididymis, 0.10. Notably, the spleen was enlarged. A large, multilobulated intra‐abdominal mass measuring approximately 30 × 21 × 16 mm was identified occupying a substantial portion of the abdominal cavity. The mass had a firm consistency, was tan–reddish in colour, and had a relatively smooth surface with prominent vasculature, indicative of encapsulation. The contours were well‐circumscribed, and the mass displaced the adjacent visceral organs, including the intestines, which appeared to be mildly distended but intact. After removal of the surrounding tissues, the mass was found to be well‐circumscribed with a lobulated contour and prominent vasculature (Figure 1a–c). Despite its smooth external appearance, the lack of a clear internal demarcation between the lobules suggested an infiltrative growth pattern (Figure 1d). Moreover, no gross signs of haemorrhage, ascites, exudate, perforation or peritonitis were observed in the abdominal cavity. A complete gross post‐mortem examination was performed on all organs and tissues, including the brain, pituitary gland, trachea, oesophagus, thyroid and parathyroid glands, heart, lungs, stomach, duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, colon, rectum, pancreas, lymph nodes, spleen, kidneys, liver, adrenal glands, testes and epididymis. All the organs were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (BBC Biochemicals, Mount Vernon, WA, USA). For histopathologic examination of the organs and tissues, sections were obtained from the formalin‐fixed tissue samples by dehydrating them with a tissue processor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Runcorn, UK). Paraffin‐embedded tissue blocks were sectioned into 4‐µm‐thick slices and mounted onto glass slides. Hematoxylin and eosin staining was performed using an autostainer (Dako Coverstainer; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Histopathological analysis revealed a well‐demarcated and dense cellular neoplasm with two morphologically distinct cell populations, a compressed fibrous rim resembling a capsule at the tumour periphery, and residual sinusoid‐like structures in several areas adjacent to the fibrous interface (Figure 2a–c). One population consisted of small‐ to intermediate‐sized round cells with round to oval nuclei and finely dispersed chromatin, one to three prominent nucleoli and scant pale eosinophilic cytoplasm, which are characteristic features of immature lymphoid cells. The mitotic index was high, suggestive of a highly proliferative phenotype (Figure 2d). Numerous large and pleomorphic cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm and eccentrically located, reniform to oval nuclei were diffusely distributed among the monomorphic population of small‐ to intermediate‐sized lymphoid tumour cells. These larger cells did not form discrete aggregates but were evenly dispersed throughout the tumour parenchyma. Many of them exhibited marked cytologic atypia, and some were markedly enlarged cells with voluminous cytoplasm and eccentric nuclei, resembling giant cells. The overall architecture was characterised by a heterogeneous mixture of neoplastic cell types without cohesive clustering and a high mitotic index (Figure 2).

Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that the small‐ to intermediate‐sized neoplastic cells exhibited diffuse and strong nuclear positivity for CD20 (Figure 3a) and Pax5 (Figure 3b), thereby confirming their B‐cell lineage. In contrast, numerous pleomorphic cells interspersed throughout the tumour parenchyma exhibited strong cytoplasmic positivity for Iba1 (Figure 3c), which is suggestive of a histiocytic/macrophage lineage. These Iba1‐positive cells were negative for B‐cell markers (CD20 and Pax5), as well as for T‐cell (CD3), plasma cell (MUM1, CD138), and epithelial markers (MHC II, PAS), thus suggesting a distinct non‐lymphoid origin. Furthermore, strong nuclear positivity for proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) was observed in the CD20/Pax5‐positive lymphoid cells and the pleomorphic Iba1‐positive cells (Figure 3d), revealing a high proliferative index in both cellular populations. This finding further interprets both components as neoplastic and is consistent with the diagnosis of a mixed tumour composed of B‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma and a histiocytic neoplasm.

No neoplastic involvement was detected in other lymphoid or parenchymal organs and tissues, such as the spleen, bone marrow, or additional lymph nodes, which is characteristic of a localised disease process. Taken together with the histological and immunohistochemical features, the tumour was described as a spontaneous, localised mixed neoplasm with B‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma and histiocytic sarcoma features. These findings collectively support the diagnosis of a spontaneous and localised mixed tumour representing B‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma and a histiocytic sarcoma, and presumably arising from a mesenteric lymph node.

Discussion

This report describes a very rare case of a spontaneous composite hematopoietic neoplasm in a 104‐week‐old C57BL/6 male mouse, which had characteristics of B‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma and histiocytic sarcoma. The tumour presented as a large, well‐circumscribed and multilobulated intra‐abdominal mass with no evidence of systemic spread. Histopathologically, the tumour exhibited two distinct populations of neoplastic cells, including immature lymphoid cells with characteristic morphologic and immunophenotypic features of B‐cell precursors and pleomorphic large cells conforming to a histiocytic lineage. Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the dual nature of the neoplasm, where CD20 and Pax5 positivity were observed in the lymphoid component, and Iba1 expression was present in the histiocytic component. Both populations were strongly positive for nuclear PCNA staining, indicating high proliferative activity. Plasma cell and epithelial markers were included in the immunohistochemical panel to exclude alternative diagnoses such as plasma cell neoplasms or epithelial‐origin tumours and to strengthen the diagnostic distinction between the two neoplastic components.

In humans, histiocyte‐rich variants of B‐cell lymphomas and composite lymphomas comprised of dual hematopoietic lineages have been sporadically reported (Esper et al. 2021, Chen et al. 2025). These include rare cases of B‐cell lymphomas admixed with neoplastic histiocytic or dendritic cell components, as well as histiocytic/dendritic cell sarcomas that originate from pre‐existing lymphomas and undergo transdifferentiation (Skala et al. 2019, Xiao et al. 2023). However, such composite tumours remain uncommon, and their pathogenesis is not fully understood. The present case resembles these human counterparts in its heterogeneous cellular composition and mixed immunophenotype; however, it differs in that no prior treatment or experimental manipulation had been performed, suggesting truly spontaneous development. In this respect, the present murine case shares key histopathological and immunophenotypic features with reported human composite hematolymphoid neoplasms, underscoring its relevance as a comparative pathology model.

Although lymphoid and histiocytic neoplasms are well recognised as distinct entities in laboratory mice, composite tumours composed of multiple hematopoietic lineages are rarely encountered and remain poorly characterised in experimental animals. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of a spontaneous mixed tumour with B‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma and histiocytic sarcoma characteristics in a laboratory mouse. The biological relevance of this finding lies in its implications for the interpretation of spontaneous background lesions in aged laboratory mice, particularly in long‐term toxicologic or experimental pathology studies, where complex hematopoietic neoplasms may otherwise be misattributed to treatment‐related effects. While both tumour types have been described independently in aged mice, their concurrent development within a single neoplastic lesion is unprecedented (Shao et al. 2011, Willard‐Mack et al. 2019). This case may reflect the synchronous transformation of two distinct progenitor populations within a mesenteric lymph node or a divergent differentiation pathway that involves a common hematopoietic precursor (Shao et al. 2011, Weigert and Weinstock 2012). Similar mechanisms have been proposed in human composite hematolymphoid neoplasms, including transdifferentiation or clonal divergence from a shared progenitor, supporting the biological plausibility of such processes in the present case (Shi et al. 2024). The high proliferative index in both components argues in favour of their neoplastic nature and against the presence of a reactive process (Bauer et al. 2007, Zaiem et al. 2020).

This finding expands the known spectrum of spontaneously occurring hematopoietic neoplasms in ageing mouse models and emphasises the importance of comprehensive histopathological and immunophenotypic analysis of atypical tumours. Given the increasing use of aged mice in long‐term toxicological and carcinogenicity studies, the recognition of such rare composite lesions is crucial for accurate diagnostics and interpretation of study findings.

Conclusion

We report a very rare case of a spontaneous composite neoplasm with B‐cell lymphoblastic lymphoma and histiocytic sarcoma characteristics in an aged C57BL/6 mouse. The identification of two distinct neoplastic lineages within a single tumour highlights the potential complexity of spontaneous lesions occurring in aged laboratory animals. This case demonstrates the importance of thorough morphological and immunophenotypic identification in routine pathology evaluations and provides valuable reference data for future research on spontaneous or treatment‐related lymphohematopoietic neoplasms in mouse models. Awareness of such rare composite neoplasms is important in studies involving ageing mice, as these lesions may complicate diagnostic interpretation and differentiation between spontaneous background findings and treatment‐related effects in toxicologic pathology.
