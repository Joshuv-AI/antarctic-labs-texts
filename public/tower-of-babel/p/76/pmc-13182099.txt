Poorly differentiated clusters surpass tumor budding as novel prognostic factors in pancreatic cancer, especially in cases treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy
Authors: Daiki Uchihara, Shohei Shimajiri, Yoshikazu Harada, Keiichiro Kumamoto, Shinji Oe, Koichiro Miyagawa, Koichi Nakamura, Eisuke Katafuchi, Fariza Nuratdinova, Yuichi Honma, Michihiko Shibata, Masaru Harada, Toshiyuki Nakayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundsPoorly differentiated clusters (PDCs), defined as solid cancer nests comprising ≥ 5 cancer cells lacking tubular formation, may reflect the phenotype of epithelial-mesenchymal transition. The diagnostic utility in pathological practice and clinical significance in pancreatic ductal carcinoma (PDAC) has not been fully explored. This study aimed to investigate the presence of PDCs in PDAC and evaluate their clinical implications.MethodsWe examined the presence of PDCs in 102 cases with PDAC. Tumor clusters with ≥ 5 clusters were classified as high PDCs and < 5 were classified as low PDCs. The association of pathological parameters and survival was assessed for high and low PDCs. Subgroup analyses were also performed for 37 cases following neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAT).ResultsHigh PDCs were significantly associated with larger tumors, advanced pT category (≥ T3), lymphatic and vascular invasion, positive surgical margins, high tumor budding (TB), elevated CA19-9 levels, and increased recurrence risk (P = 0.014, 0.021, 0.011, 0.013, 0.026, < 0.001, 0.016, 0.027, respectively). Moreover, this was associated with worse overall survival and disease-free survival of PDAC, especially in cases following NAT (P < 0.001). Additionally, high PDCs in cases following NAT was an independent prognostic factor for shorter overall (Hazard ratio (HR): 9.131, P = 0.012) and disease-free survival (HR: 3.896, P = 0.018) in multivariable Cox regression analysis, exceeding TB in prognostic impact.ConclusionPDCs in the actual pathological diagnosis are more useful as a prognostic marker for PDAC than TB. Due to the limited sample size and broad confidence intervals, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04773-x.Keywords: Diagnosis, Pancreatic ductal carcinoma, Prognosis, Poorly differentiated cluster, Tumor budding

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer remains one of the most lethal solid tumors, with a 5-year overall survival of only 11.2% [1]. This dismal prognosis is primarily attributed to the absence of reliable early detection strategies, the difficulty of achieving complete surgical resection, and the development of chemotherapy resistance through multiple mechanisms [2]. Therefore, the pathological diagnosis after surgery is crucial for predicting the prognosis.

Approximately 90% of pancreatic cancers are pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) [3]. PDAC is a tubular adenocarcinoma pathologically characterized by mucus-producing cuboidal or columnar epithelial cells that proliferate invasively within a rich fibrous stroma. Histological grade is classified into three types: well, moderately, and poorly differentiated [4]. However, PDAC is a histologically heterogeneous cancer. It is not uncommon to observe cases diagnosed as well or moderately differentiated type that contain focal areas of poorly differentiated components. Therefore, this conventional grading system may not accurately reflect the biological behavior or prognosis of the tumor. In recent years, neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has become an important treatment option for localized cancer, including borderline resectable and locally advanced PDAC [5, 6]. NAT frequently induces marked fibrosis, stromal remodeling, cytological atypia, and architectural distortion, which may obscure residual tumor structures and complicate histopathological interpretation.

Poorly differentiated clusters (PDCs) are defined as solid cancer nests comprising ≥ 5 cancer cells and without tubular formation. PDCs represent the dynamic process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition, which is reflected in their ability to invade and metastasize, alongside tumor budding (TB) [7]. TB is defined as a single tumor cell or a cluster of up to four cells in the stroma at the outer edge of the tumor. The presence or absence of luminal formation was not considered a determining factor in the assessment of TB. These PDC characteristics are focused on histologic features of colorectal carcinoma (CRC) and serve as parameters reflecting CRC metastatic potential [8]. However, their significance in PDAC remains unclear, and it is still uncertain whether PDCs or TB serve as superior prognostic indicators.

This study aimed to evaluate PDCs in surgically resected specimens, including cases following NAT. We assessed the usefulness as a treatment efficacy assessment and prognostic factor in PDAC.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective study, conducted at a single center, received approval from the Ethics Committee of University of Occupational and Environmental Health (protocol no. CR24-016). All procedures conformed to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and an opt-out mechanism was provided to ensure adequate notification and allow patients to decline the use of their clinical information via our institutional website.

Patient selection and follow up

We retrospectively reviewed 102 patients with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) who underwent surgical resection at the University of Occupational and Environmental Health Hospital between January 2007 and December 2023. Among them, 37 patients received neoadjuvant therapy (NAT). The NAT regimen included a combination of gemcitabine and nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (GnP) or a combination of gemcitabine and S-1 (GS). Among these patients, 12 received GnP, 22 received GS, and 3 received other regimens. The follow-up period was defined as the interval from the diagnostic date to death or the last documented visit in March 2024. Overall survival was defined as the time from the diagnostic date to death from any cause, whereas disease-free survival was defined as the duration from surgery to the first confirmed recurrence. Recurrence was determined by radiologic imaging and categorized as locoregional (involving the pancreatic remnant, peripancreatic tissue, or regional lymph node metastases) or distant (including hepatic and pulmonary metastases or peritoneal carcinomatosis).

Pathologic examination

Histological evaluation was performed on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)–stained sections from 102 PDAC specimens. Two board-certified pathologists (SS and TN), certified by the Japanese Medical Specialty Board, independently assessed the slides without access to any clinical information. PDCs were classified as well-defined cancer cell clusters (≥ 5 cancer cells) that infiltrated the stroma, with the absence of gland formation. Grading of the PDCs were determined according to a previous report [9]. In brief, we checked all H&E slides and selected those containing the highest concentration of PDCs (Fig. 1A). Clusters were then counted under a × 20 objective lens (field diameter: 1 mm, area: 0.785 mm2), and tumor grading was determined based on the numbers present (Fig. 1B). Positive determination was made regardless of the location within or outside lymphatic channels or whether surrounded by lacunar spaces associated with the so-called invasive micropapillary pattern (Fig. 1C). Cancer clusters, showing tubular formation, containing intracytoplasmic lumina, structures morphologically similar to intracytoplasmic mucin, or floating in a large mucin pool, were excluded (Fig. 1D). Fragmented cancer cell clusters due to inflammatory cell infiltration or necrosis were not included in PDCs (Fig. 1E). When preparing a specimen, if part of the glandular duct appears blind-ended on the section, it may resemble cancer cell clusters in the absence of gland formation, which is called pseudo PDCs, but it should not be diagnosed as PDCs (Fig. 1F).

All H&E slides prepared from a single longitudinal section of the whole tumor were first scanned at low-power magnification to identify areas with the greatest number of PDCs (A, H&E, × 4 objective lens). Clusters were then counted under a × 20 objective lens surrounded by a red circle (field diameter: 1 mm, area: 0.785 mm.2). There are the typical findings of PDCs that is defined as solid nest comprising ≥ 5 cancer cells and the absence of gland formation (B, H&E, × 20 objective lens), regardless of the location within or outside lymphatic channels or whether surrounded by lacunar spaces (C, H&E, × 20 objective lens). We exclude from PDCs, cancer clusters containing intracytoplasmic lumina and structures morphologically similar to intracytoplasmic mucin (D, H&E, × 20 objective lens), tumor budding (E, H&E, × 20 objective lens), and pseudo PDCs by cutting the blind-ended on the section (F, H&E, × 20 objective lens)

Tumor clusters with < 5, 5 to 9, ≥ 10 clusters were classified as grade 1 (Fig. 2A), grade 2 (Fig. 2B), and grade 3 (Fig. 2C), respectively. In addition, we defined grade 1 as PDCs low, grade 2 and grade 3 as PDCs high. Tumor budding (TB), which is defined as a single tumor cell or a cluster of up to four cells in the stroma at the outer edge of the tumor, was also assessed. With reference to ITBCC recommendation, TB was assessed in a filed measuring 0.785 mm2 in one hot spot under a × 20 objective lens [10]. We used the following three-tier system to categorize bud counts: TB 1 (0–4 buds, Fig. 2D), TB 2 (5–9 buds, Fig. 2E), and TB 3 (≥ 10 buds, Fig. 2F). In addition, we defined TB 1 and 2 as TB low, and TB 3 as TB high according to the previous report [11]. The pathologic features of the pancreatic specimens, including the pathological diagnosis, tumor differentiation, tumor size, primary tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, lymphatic invasion, vascular invasion, neuroinvasion, and margin status, were carefully reevaluated. If the section margin was very close (within one mm), the margin status was regarded as positive. In specimens of cases following NAT, treatment efficacy was reevaluated by two certified pathologists based on the area of residual tumor (ART) grading system [12–14], Evans`s grading system [15], College of American Pathologists (CAP) grading system [16], M.D. Anderson Cancer Center group (MDA) grading system [17], and the Japan Pancreas Society (JPS) grading system [18].

PDCs & TB grading (H&E, × 20 objective lens). PDCs were graded according to the number of clustered tumor cells as follows: Grade 1, clusters containing fewer than 5 cells (A, red arrowhead); Grade 2, clusters composed of 5–9 cells (B, red arrowhead); and Grade 3, clusters comprising 10 or more cells (C, red arrowhead). TB was graded by counting the number of buds in a single hot-spot field as follows: TB 1, 0–4 buds (D, yellow arrowhead); TB 2, 5–9 buds (E, yellow arrowhead); and TB 3, 10 or more buds (F, yellow arrowhead)

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25 software (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). All continuous and categorical variables were described respectively as the mean and standard deviation or frequency and percentage. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using Student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney test. The chi-square test or Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables between the two groups. Survival curves were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between the curves were analyzed using the log-rank test. The association of multiple prognostic factors with disease-free survival was assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard model analyses with a forced entry method. Considering the sample size, factors with P-values < 0.2 in univariate analyses were included into the multivariate analysis. In the subgroup analyses of cases following NAT, given the anticipated the small sample size, the analysis was limited to four variables—PDCs, TB, serum CA19-9 levels, and lymphatic invasion (or lymph node metastasis)—based on prior evidence demonstrating their strong association with overall survival [19]. Treatment response was excluded from the multivariable analysis because it showed a low hazard ratio and lacked statistical significance in the univariable analysis. Differences were considered statistically significant at P-values of < 0.05.

Results

Cases of PDCs in PDAC

A total of 102 patients with PDAC were included in the study. Based on histopathological evaluation, there were 49 cases with high PDCs (PDCs grade 2: 23 cases, PDCs grade 3: 26 cases) and 53 cases with low PDCs (PDCs grade 1). Of the 102 cases, 37 cases underwent NAT. In 37 cases following NAT, there were 16 cases with high PDCs (PDCs grade 2: 11 cases, PDCs grade 3: 5 cases) and 21 cases with low PDCs (PDCs grade 1). PDAC is highly heterogeneous within the tissue, and even when most of the components were well differentiated or moderately differentiated, many specimens contained locally PDCs when evaluated in detail (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Correlation of PDCs with clinicopathological parameters

The relationships between PDCs and clinicopathological parameters in PDAC are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the patients was 72 years (range: 33–90 years), with 66 (64.7%) males and 36 (35.3%) females. The mean tumor size was 25.0 mm (range: 0–80 mm). High PDCs were significantly associated with larger tumor size, advanced pT category (≥ T3), lymphatic and vascular invasion, positive surgical margins, high TB, elevated CA19-9 levels, and an increased risk of recurrence (P = 0.014, 0.021, 0.011, 0.013, 0.026, < 0.001, 0.016, 0.027, respectively). Supplementary Table 1 showed a subgroup analysis limited to cases following NAT. High PDCs were associated with vascular invasion, positive surgical margins, high TB, and an increased risk of recurrence (P = 0.026, 0.03, 0.024, 0.004, respectively).

Correlation of clinicopathologic parameters and PDCs with survival

The median follow-up duration of the entire cohort was 22.6 months (range, 3.3–219 months). Among the patients, 66 (64.7%) developed recurrence or metastasis, and 52 (51.0%) had died. The median overall survival and disease-free survival were 22.6 months and 13.2 months, respectively. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that high PDCs were significantly associated with poorer overall survival and disease-free survival (log-rank, both P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A, 3B). The same results were observed in cases following NAT (Fig. 3C, 3D). Analysis classified as Grade 1–3 by PDCs was shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. Supplementary Fig. 3 shows the histogram of the follow-up duration. We compared the follow-up duration between the high-PDCs and the low-PDCs groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. There were no significant differences between the two groups, suggesting that the effect of survival bias was negligible. The same results were observed in the subgroups of analysis following NAT.

The results of the univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses are presented in Table 2. In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, high PDCs were identified as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival (Hazard ratio (HR) = 2.055, 95% Confidence interval (CI): 1.044–4.045, P = 0.037), whereas TB showed no statistical significance (HR = 1.563, 95% CI: 0.812–3.011, P = 0.181). In disease-free survival (Supplementary Table 2), lymphatic invasion was the most significant prognostic factor (HR = 4.272, 95% CI: 1.260–14.483, P = 0.020) in PDAC, but high TB was the second most significant prognostic factor (HR = 1.982, 95% CI: 1.061–3.702, P = 0.032). In contrast, high PDCs were not statistically associated with shorter disease-free survival (HR = 1.599, 95% CI: 0.885–2.891, P = 0.120). A subgroup analysis limited to patients (37 cases) following NAT is presented in Table 3 and Supplementary Table 3. In the multivariable Cox regression analysis, compared to the analysis of the entire cohort, high PDCs were associated with higher HR for both overall survival (HR = 9.131, 95% CI: 1.637–50.930, P = 0.012) and disease-free survival (Supplementary Table 3, HR = 3.813, 95% CI: 1.294–11.234, P = 0.018). The power was 0.96, confirming that the result was statistically significant. High TB was not statistically associated with shorter overall survival (HR = 2.566, 95% CI: 0.457–14.395, P = 0.284) and disease-free survival (HR = 2.652, 95% CI: 0.732–9.615, P = 0.412).

Discussion

In the present study, we revealed that high PDCs were associated with worse overall survival and disease-free survival of PDAC, especially in cases following NAT.

Several studies have evaluated PDCs as prognostic factors for CRC. The PDC grading system aligns more strongly with the UICC TNM classification [8]. In pT1 CRC, the presence of PDCs, submucosal invasion depth ≥ 1000 µm, and lymphatic vascular invasion were significantly associated with lymph node metastasis [20]. KRAS mutation was involved in the formation of PDCs [21]. The presence of high PDCs in a pretreatment tumor is significantly associated with a poor response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [22]. On the other hand, TB, defined as single tumor cells or tumor cell clusters of up to four cancer cells [23], has also been reported as a prognostic factor. Both PDCs and TB represent the dynamic process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition which is reflected in their ability to invade and metastasize [7]. However, PDCs demonstrated superior predictive value for recurrence compared to TB, desmoplastic reaction, perineural invasion, and WHO grade. In addition, interobserver agreement among pathologists for PDCs evaluation was high [23].

In PDAC, several studies have revealed that TB is a prognostic marker; however, no such studies have been conducted for PDCs. It remains unclear whether PDCs or TB serve as the superior prognostic marker. High TB was associated with advanced pT classification, lymphatic invasion, and shorter overall survival and disease-free survival [11]. In the multivariate analyses of our total cohort, TB remained the stronger predictor of disease-free survival than PDCs, whereas PDCs were independently associated with overall survival. In specimens following NAT, high-grade TB was associated with worse disease-free survival in the previous report [24]. On the other hand, when the analysis was restricted to cases following NAT of our cohort, PDCs emerged as a stronger prognostic factor than TB for overall survival and disease-free survival. In specimens following NAT, therapy-induced stromal reaction tends to be more pronounced, resulting in dense fibrosis and scattered degenerating tumor cells [25]. Unlike gastric and cololectal cancer, the pancreas exhibits a distinct histopathological process termed acinar-to-ductal metaplasia (ADM), which plays a pivotal role in pancreatic injury response and tumorigenesis [26]. In the pathological diagnosis of PDAC, small duct-like structures undergoing ADM are frequently observed adjacent to the tumor, and often associated with surrounding inflammation and fibrosis [27]. These changes can occasionally resemble scattered invasive tumor foci, particularly when residual carcinoma cells are sparse. Our specimens revealed similar findings (Supplementary Fig. 4). Therefore, in specimens following NAT, marked stromal fibrosis and therapy-induced cytological alterations can obscure the morphological boundaries between fragmented cancer cells and benign glandular elements derived from ADM. This may lead to a diagnostic pitfall, especially in the assessment of TB, potentially resulting in overestimation of TB. Although PDCs and TB are distinguished by being defined as ≥ 5 and < 5 cancer cells, respectively, the two markers likely belong in the same spectrum of dedifferentiated biological features. Because PDCs are larger in size and the absence of a grand formation, PDCs likely require less time and effort to identify. Although not directly evaluated in the present study, ADM generally comprises smaller gland-forming structures with preserved luminal architecture, which could potentially aid in differentiating it from PDCs. Even if specimens follow NAT, there is a lower possibility of confusion with ADM than with TB. Various grading systems are used to evaluate treatment effects [12–18]. These systems are generally used to estimate the amount of residual tumor, but it may be challenging to reach consensus among pathologists and there are potential issues with the reproducibility of the results. Additionally, these did not focus on morphological characteristics.

Our study had some limitations. First, the applicability of PDC assessment criteria established in colorectal cancer directly to pancreatic cancer remains uncertain. This represents a limitation of our study and should be addressed through future tumor-specific validation. And this study was conducted at a single center with a relatively small sample size and broad confidence intervals, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In the cases following NAT, given the wide 95% confidence intervals and the small sample size, the results should be interpreted with caution and regarded as exploratory. An additional limitation relates to potential assessment bias. Because PDCs consist of larger cohesive nests, they may be identified with greater consistency, especially in specimens affected by fibrosis or NAT. Conversely, the evaluation of TB, defined by single cells or small clusters, may be more susceptible to interpretative variability in the presence of therapy-induced alterations or ADM. Therefore, methodological factors rather than inherent biological differences may partly account for the observed prognostic discrepancy. And as PDCs and TB were assessed by consensus between two pathologists, formal measurement of interobserver agreement was not possible in this study. Further studies with larger sample sizes and future multicenter investigations incorporating independent assessments by several pathologists are required.

In conclusion, our findings suggest a potential association between high PDCs and adverse survival outcomes, especially in cases following NAT. Given the exploratory nature of this study, these observations should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmation in future investigations. In actual pathological diagnosis, rather than vaguely noting the degree of differentiation of specimens of histologically heterogeneous findings, it would be more beneficial to share information with clinicians by focusing on morphological characteristics such as PDCs in order to improve patient care in clinical practice.
