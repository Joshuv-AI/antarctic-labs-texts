A collection of experimental data from a multiphase plant simulating oil and gas transport
Authors: Giovanni Mazzuto, Ilaria Pietrangeli, Marco Ortenzi, Vincenzo Foti, Filippo Emanuele Ciarapica, Maurizio Bevilacqua
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper details the data gathered from an experimental plant designed to simulate an oil and gas transportation system. The plant was configured to replicate various working conditions, including both normal and anomalous operational states. Different scenarios were created to reflect realistic conditions encountered in oil and gas transportation, such as steady-state operations, typical operational fluctuations, and a range of anomalies like leakages, obstructions, and working parameter modifications.The data provide a comprehensive view of the plant behaviour under these diverse conditions. This information can be instrumental for applications in machine learning, where it can aid in the development of predictive maintenance algorithms and anomaly detection models. In the realm of control theory, the data can support the design and validation of advanced control strategies to ensure efficient and safe operations. Additionally, the data enhance process comprehension, offering insights into the mechanisms governing oil and gas transportation systems.This dataset can be helpful for the analysis of the dynamic responses of an oil and gas transportation system under various operational scenarios. The structured data can be used to model and simulate system behaviour, providing a foundation for improving process resilience, efficiency and reliability. The information captured in this dataset can be useful for improving theoretical and practical understanding in the fields of machine learning, control theory, and process engineering.Keywords: Industrial processes, Operational data, Process engineering, System dynamics, Measurement data

Background

The dataset was created to support the authors in developing a digital model that can accurately replicate both normal and abnormal behaviours of the system [1]. This model aims to study and evaluate corrective actions during operation. By simulating various operational scenarios, the digital model helps identify potential issues and assess the effectiveness of different corrective measures [2]. This approach is essential for understanding how the system responds to both expected and unexpected conditions. The dataset provides a comprehensive foundation for this digital modelling [3], encompassing a wide range of data points captured under diverse conditions to improve the system resilience by enabling proactive measures and informed decision-making during actual operations.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The experimental plant and the acquisition system

The experimental plant is located at the Department of Industrial Engineering and Mathematical Sciences (DIISM) of the Università Politecnica delle Marche in Ancona, Italy. Fig. 6 depicts the 3D model of the plant.

The 3D model of the experimental plant.

This setup simulates the extraction of oil and natural gas from depleted wells. The useful life of hydrocarbon reservoirs is determined by their potential and operating costs. A well is considered depleted if it contains oil/gas in quantities that make extraction impractical, or if the production volumes of hydrocarbons are too low to justify the high operating costs, making production economically unsustainable. Indeed, in typical scenarios, the most apparent solution would involve installing high-cost pumps both on the surface and at the base of the oil well to manage extraction.

However, the system under study offers a more efficient alternative. This approach uses the pressure from a hypothetical reservoir at the peak of its productive life to extract from a depleted reservoir. The higher pressure of the productive reservoir, due to its physical characteristics, creates into an ejector a suction effect on the depleted reservoir, which lacks sufficient pressure for transport.

Gas-liquid ejector in the system mix two phases at different pressures—one from a depleted well and one from a productive well—and provide the necessary transport energy. While in real operations, the fluids involved are crude oil and natural gas, for safety reasons, the experimental plant uses water and ambient air. Here, water in a tank and a positive displacement pump simulate a pressurized well, while ambient air represents natural gas from a depleted reservoir.

As shown in Fig. 7, pressurized water enters the ejector through the inlet water line (blue dashed line), creating a vacuum that draws in air from the environment (the inlet air line, red dashed line), forming a two-phase mixture (the mixture inlet line, green dashed line). This mixture is then directed into a vertical tank acting as a slug catcher, which separates the liquid (outlet water line, black dashed line) and gas (outlet air line, orange dashed line) phases. Additionally, all the “VM” denote manual valves used to simulate system anomalies. The detail of the valves is shown in Fig. 10.

At this point, all the plant sensors and valves are connected to a Revolution Pi (RevPi) device, shown in Fig. 12, to acquire the status value and send it to data management system based on MySQL and MQTT protocol.

RevPi device for data acquisition and communication.

Specifically, RevPi device is an industrial-grade programmable logic controller (PLC) based on the Raspberry Pi platform, designed for industrial automation and control. It features a modular design for adding various I/O modules, runs on an open-source Raspberry Pi OS, and supports multiple programming languages. Built for harsh industrial environments, it offers robust hardware and various connectivity options like Ethernet and USB, making it suitable for applications such as machinery control, data logging, and building automation.

The entire system is controlled through a desktop application and also monitored via a smartphone app. Specifically, the working condition tests were conducted by setting control parameters through the section enclosed in the dashed rectangle in Fig. 13, where the setpoint values for the actuated valves are shown.

A view of the app desktop for controlling the plant.

To create the anomaly dataset, two operators were needed: one on the line and one using the desktop app. The operator on the line closes one of the valves under examination, as shown in Fig. 14(c). Specifically, different degrees of valve closure generate anomalies of varying severity. Fig. 14(b) shows a normally open valve that is partially closed. During the test, the control operator notes which valve has been closed to record it in the dataset Fig. 14(a).

To enhance readability and reproducibility, we provide a schematic mapping from raw files to system behavior: raw JSON/TXT include sim_time, sample_time, sensors S1–S8, valve states AV1–AV3, PID logs, and VMx tags etc. For each test, traces are time-aligned and compared. The system behavior has been analysed via key channels—pressures (S1, S5), level (S6), and flows (S2, S7–S8)—and then apply the VM1–VM7 signatures below to characterize normal versus anomalous dynamics. In particular,

•VM1 – inlet-water obstruction: closing the valve upstream of the ejector deprives the motive stream: inlet water pressure S1 falls and the water inflow S2 collapses; downstream air/water mixing becomes erratic (S7–S8). The mixture cannot overcome the height difference, and the air line is partially flooded.

•VM2 – ejector obstruction: the mixture cannot reach the tank; S6 does not rise as expected, while air lines flood, causing strong instabilities in S7–S8 and fluctuating pressures around the ejector path. The test is stopped early for safety.

Limitations

The only limitation can be identified in the creation of the anomaly dataset, as mentioned before. Since it is created through the cooperation of two operators, one on the line and one at the control app, there may be a slight asynchrony (lead/lag) between when one closes the valve and the other records it in the file. However, this can be resolved through statistical preprocessing of the data.

Since anomalies are introduced manually, a slight time offset may occur between the physical valve operation and the corresponding dataset entry. To facilitate alignment, users can rely on the anomaly flags (VM1–VM7) embedded in the JSON files, which indicate the expected anomaly time window. When needed, simple preprocessing can be applied by shifting the anomaly flag to match the observed deviations in key variables (e.g., pressure, flow rate). Additionally, statistical techniques such as Hotelling’s T² can be employed to detect the precise instant when sensor readings diverge significantly from the normal condition, thus improving synchronization between sensor signals and manually introduced anomalies.
