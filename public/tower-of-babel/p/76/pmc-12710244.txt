Old germs, novel approaches – A systematic review on pathogen distribution and therapeutic intervention strategies in periprosthetic joint infections
Authors: Anina Lange, Soraya Mousavi, Stefan Bereswill, Markus M Heimesaat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic degenerative diseases including osteoarthritis are on the rise leading to a growing demand for joint replacement surgery in elderly and often multimorbid patients. Periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) constitute serious complications following endoprosthetic surgery. Increasing prevalences of PJIs by multi-drug resistant and/or biofilm-producing bacteria hinder sufficient anti-infectious treatment especially in vulnerable patients. Hence, alternative and/or adjunct therapeutic approaches appear crucial in the combat of difficult-to-treat PJIs. In our review we summarize recent evidence for changes in the spectrum of PJI-associated pathogens over time and elucidate treatment concepts beyond established standard therapies. Our literature search revealed that the spectrum of bacterial pathogens can vary considerably depending on the time course post-surgery, the geographical region, and the patient population. While standard antibiotic therapy besides surgical revision remains the corner stone of treatment, alternative/adjunct antibiotics-independent methods are increasingly coming to the fore. These include the targeted dissolution of bacterial biofilms, enzyme-based approaches, and enhanced infection prevention measures upon risk assessment of the patient. Despite promising methodological approaches clinical evidence of their therapeutic value in everyday care is scarce. Hence, optimized early pathogen detection measures, individually tailored treatment concepts and their application in interdisciplinary settings will be important in the combat of difficult-to-treat PJIs.Keywords: periprosthetic joint infections, multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria, biofilm-producing pathogens, shift in pathogen spectrum, alternative antibiotics-independent treatment options

Introduction

Medical relevance of endoprostheses

Demographic changes result in a progressively aging population in high-income countries such as Europe. According to estimates by the United Nations, the proportion of people over the age of 60 was around 25% in 2017, and this figure is expected to rise to around 35% by 2050 [1]. A similar trend can be observed in Germany, in particular, which has a direct impact on medical care for the elderly. With increasing life expectancy, chronic degenerative diseases such as osteoarthritis are on the rise. This development is leading to a steadily growing demand for joint replacement surgery. Hip and knee joint prostheses are among the most common elective surgical procedures in orthopedics. In 2016 alone, around 230,000 hip and approximately 170,000 knee prostheses were implanted in Germany [1]. Professional associations and epidemiological models assume that these figures will continue to rise in the coming decades. The primary aim of these surgical interventions is to reduce pain and restore mobility. Many patients report a significantly improved quality of life and a rapid return to everyday life after endoprosthetic procedures. Long-term data show that modern endoprostheses function reliably and remain stable for many years. Despite these successes, the procedures are not without risks. In a small but clinically relevant number of cases, complications arise. These include prosthesis loosening, dislocation, or infection of the implanted joint. Periprosthetic joint infections (PJIs) in particular, are considered serious and costly complications, as they often require repeated surgery and extended antibiotic treatment. Against the backdrop of rising case numbers, the topic of infection prevention and treatment is becoming increasingly important [2].

Periprosthetic joint infection – definition and common pathogens

PJIs are bacterial infections that develop within or around an artificial joint. They are mainly caused by bacterial microorganisms entering the tissue around a joint prosthesis and are considered serious complications following endoprosthetic replacement. The infection can occur either early or late after surgery and often presents with inflammatory clinical signs such as pain, swelling, hyperthermia, or abscess formation. In many cases, the PJI initially goes unnoticed, especially if it is caused by slow-growing or less virulent bacteria. Early detection of the PJI is crucial, as untreated infections can lead to serious complications, limited function, and loss of the prosthesis. The most common pathogens causing PJIs are Gram-positive bacteria, especially Staphylococcus aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci (CNS) including Staphylococcus epidermidis. In recent years, however, also Gram-negative bacterial species such as Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa have been more frequently identified as causative agents of PJIs [3].

Biofilms - the invisible refuge of pathogens hampering diagnosis and treatment of PJIs

Biofilms play a central role in the development and persistence of PJIs. They represent structured bacterial communities that colonize the surface of foreign materials such as joint prostheses. The bacteria are integrated into an extracellular molecular biofilm matrix consisting of polysaccharides, proteins, and DNA. This matrix protects the pathogens from host immune defense measures, but also from the antimicrobial effects of many antibiotics [4]. If compared to free-living planktonic bacteria, the microorganisms integrated in biofilms exhibit different metabolic features given that their overall metabolism is down-regulated (i.e., reduced) mounting in much slower bacterial growth. Whereas the biofilm-forming bacteria show enhanced tolerance to antimicrobial substances, the complex structure of the biofilm itself hinders the penetration of antibiotics, which significantly limits bacterial eradication [4]. Distinct strains of both, Gram-positive bacterial species such as S. aureus, CNS including S. epidermidis, Streptococcus viridans, and Enterococcus faecalis, as well as Gram-negative bacteria including E. coli, P. aeruginosa, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis may exhibit biofilm-forming abilities [5]. PJIs particularly caused by biofilm-producing pathogens constitute major challenges for timely and appropriate diagnosis and treatment in prosthetic intervention, particularly during chronic inflammatory processes [5]. One reason is that PJIs due to biofilm-forming bacteria often manifest through non-specific or masked clinical signs given that persistent pain, loosening or dysfunction of the prosthesis might be prominent, while classic inflammatory features such as redness, warming, and swelling of the skin in the area around the implanted artificial joint, or fever are often absent. Furthermore, biofilm-associated infections often remain localized and become chronic if unnoticed [6]. The microbiological diagnosis might be challenging since the pathogens embedded in the biofilm do not only exhibit different morphological features, but also changed metabolic characteristics and might thus be missed when applying conventional culture methods due to poor growth capabilities. New detection methods include sonication of explanted prostheses in an ultrasound bath with subsequent cultural analyses. Although in any positive cultural event infection should be suspected, contamination needs to be taken into consideration. Therefore, a cut-off of 20–100 colony-forming units (CFU) per mL has been set to consider the detected pathogen as diagnostic [7]. One needs to take into consideration, however, that the equipment and expertise needed are not necessarily available in routine settings. To further increase diagnostic sensitivity, especially in the event of culture-negative PJIs, molecular detection methods such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) are applied to the sonicates [8]. However, given its very high sensitivity, PCR might yield false-positive results. Furthermore, specific primers detecting only single afore-hand defined microorganisms are needed. Even though multiplex PCR with several defined primers are available, they were shown to exhibit even lower sensitivity and were not necessarily superior to culture [7]. For an effective treatment and cure of PJIs, antibiotic treatment alone is not sufficient. Instead, complete removal of the infected implant, careful surgical debridement in combination with systemic, targeted antibiotic therapy are mandatory to cure PJIs, irrespective whether the causative agent produces biofilm or not [9].

Multi-drug resistant pathogens – when antibiotics fail

Multi-drug resistant (MDR) pathogens are defined as bacteria that are resistant to at least three classes of antibiotics relevant for antimicrobial therapy [3]. This classification allows for a practical assessment of the resistance situation and serves as a basis for hygiene measures including isolation strategies, therapeutic decision making as well as infection surveillance. Among the most important MDR pathogens, methicillin-resistant S. aureus strains (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), and Gram-negative bacteria that produce extended spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBL) or even carbapenemases need to be considered in clinical conditions when initially calculated antibiotic therapies fail mounting in difficult-to-treat infections and ultimately death [3, 10]. MDR bacterial pathogens are of increasing importance as infectious agents of PJIs. Here, they do not only prolong the duration of treatment but also often require complex, multi-stage therapeutic including surgical procedures [11]. The increase in prevalence of MDR pathogens underscores the importance of careful antibiotic management and a consistent infection control strategy further highlighting the need to critically review existing treatment approaches and focus more on novel, alternative treatment options as well as prevention measures [12].

Epidemiology and health care burdens of PJIs

PJIs are among the most serious complications following hip and knee arthroplasty. The PJI incidence in primary arthroplasty is approximately 0.5–2%, and it is even much higher in revision surgery [13] constituting a significant burden for both, the patients' health and the healthcare system given that affected individuals often require several surgical procedures and spend considerably more time in the hospital than patients without infection. In a retrospective analysis by a research group at the Charité - Universitätsmedizin Berlin, Germany, the average length of hospital stay for patients with infected knee and hip prostheses was around 14 and 22 days, respectively [13]. PJIs often lead to significant functional impairment and chronic pain, and furthermore, reduced quality of life given that PJI patients more frequently require walking aids, report increased limitations in everyday life, and achieve significantly worse scores in established quality of life measurements if compared to non-infected arthroplasty patients [14]. Of note, the mortality risk was calculated to be up to 5-fold higher in patients with PJI versus uninfected control subjects [15]. Furthermore, the economic consequences also place a considerable burden on the healthcare system. In the above-mentioned Charité analysis, the average cost per PJI case was around €16,300, and in the case of an infected hip joint prosthesis requiring a two-stage replacement, the costs exceeded €38,000 [13]. At the same time, however, the reimbursement under the German flat-rate payment system did not cover the expenses: the average shortfall was over €3,000 per case, with an average of €8,585 for hip prosthesis infections. This funding gap constitutes a major economic challenge for specialized clinics [13].

Risk factors for the development of PJIs

When performing risk assessment for the development of PJIs both, modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors including patient-related, pre-and post-operative as well as surgery-related parameters need to be taken into consideration [16]. A meta-analysis, for instance, revealed that (i) male gender; (ii) increased age; (iii) alcohol abuse; (iv) metabolic disorders including obesity and diabetes mellitus; (v) rheumatoid arthritis; (vi) urinary tract infections; (vii) ASA scores >2; (viii) prolonged operation time (exceeding 90 min); and (ix) drain usage were significant risk factors for the development of PJIs [17]. In addition, intra-articular injections of glucocorticoids, anaesthetics or hyaluronic acid three months before endoprosthetic surgery, and immune-suppressive morbidities and medications were increasing the risk for subsequent PJIs [18]. Also high degree valgus, varus, and flexion deformities, post-operative development of seroma, hematoma, and wound dehiscence need to be considered as PJI-promoting risk factors [16].

Current treatment concepts of PJIs

To date, the so-called DAIR procedure (i.e., debridement, antibiotic therapy, and implant retention) constitutes the classical treatment option in PJIs. If compared to revision arthroplasty, this procedure is technically less invasive and can preserve a well-fixed endoprosthesis. In Europe, the one-stage exchange is common practice in immune-competent patients by which the infected protheses is removed and replaced by a new implant within the same session [16]. To minimize the risk for PJIs, endoprotheses covered by antibiotics, silver, and iodine, for instance, have been applied, but with conflicting effects [19].

Aim of the study

The aim of this study was to provide a structured overview of the current knowledge on PJIs in hip and knee joint endoprostheses addressing (i) which pathogens were predominantly causative infectious agents and how their spectrum changed over time; and (ii) which therapeutic measures were currently state of the art and if promising alternative and/or adjunct options were available for PJI treatment.

Methods

Search strategy and data collection

Between May 15 and June 3, 2025, a structured literature search was conducted using the online database PubMed. The search was carried out as a free-text search using the terms “prosthetic joint arthroplasty” AND “infection” AND “multi-drug resistant,” resulting in 32 hits. These studies were transferred to a table for systematic evaluation. The following data were extracted: pathogen spectrum, applied therapy, study country or location, type of publication, study design, time of infection, and limitations. After initial screening, the studies were assessed based on defined inclusion and exclusion criteria as follows.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were (i) studies published between 2014 and 2025; (ii) free full-text availability; (iii) English or German language; (iv) reporting in vitro and/or in vivo including animal and human studies; (v) of therapeutic relevance regarding either pathogen; (vi) presenting conventional and/or alternative treatment strategies. Excluded were, however, studies (i) focusing exclusively on diagnostic, but not therapeutic aspects; (ii) addressing only standard therapies without innovative or alternative approaches; (iii) presenting cases due to rare or atypical pathogens without broader clinical relevance; (iv) addressing rather historic, i.e., outdated therapeutic concepts. After applying these criteria, 3 systematic reviews and 14 original studies were excluded. A total of 15 studies remained for final content analysis (Fig. 1).

Results

Pathogen spectrum in PJIs

In a retrospective multicenter cohort study, 2,524 patients with PJIs diagnosed in 19 Spanish hospitals were analyzed over a period from 2003 to 2012 [20]. In 90.6% of infections affecting hip or knee prostheses, a pathogen could be identified of which 35.7% were acute postoperative infections, 52.7% were chronic infections, and 11.6% of hematogenous origin. The most frequently detected pathogens were Gram-positive cocci (i.e., 77.7% of cases), whereas 27.6% of the PJIs were caused by Gram-negative bacteria. Over the observation period, a significant increase in the proportion of Gram-negative pathogens from 25% (2003–2004) to 33.3% (2011–2012) could be observed, whereas the proportion of Gram-positive species decreased from 80.3% to 74.3%. The most common pathogen species were S. aureus (28.1%) and CNS (39.6%) including S. epidermidis (23.3%). Enterobacteriaceae accounting for 20.4% of pathogens, included E. coli (9.1%), Proteus spp. (4.8%), Enterobacter spp. (4.2%), and Klebsiella spp. (2.5%). Among non-fermenting Gram-negative bacteria, P. aeruginosa were the most common ones, accounting for 8.8%. The infection rates with MDR pathogens increased from 9.3% in the period 2003–2004 to 15.8% in 2011–2012 that were mainly due to an increase in MDR Gram-negative bacilli (from 5.3 to 8.2%), whereas MRSA bacteria constituted 7.9% of all isolated pathogens, with their proportion initially increasing and then slightly declining in the last two-year period. Of note, ciprofloxacin-resistant Gram-negative isolates increased from 3.0 to 6.4%. Among the rarer pathogens, the proportion of fungal infections rose from 0.7 to 1.5% during the survey. Other pathogens such as anaerobic bacteria, mycobacteria, and corynebacteria were also identified, but no clear changes over time could be noticed.

Zajonz et al. [21] conducted a retrospective single-center study in 2016 involving a total of 320 patients with PJIs who were treated at the Leipzig University Hospital (Germany) between 2001 and 2012. The aim of the study was to analyze the spectrum of bacterial pathogens in infected endoprostheses and the development of MDR pathogens over time. Of these cases (median age of included patients 71 years), 172 involved hip endoprostheses and 148 knee endoprostheses. A total of 58% of the PJIs were caused by staphylococci of which S. epidermidis was the most commonly detected pathogen, accounting for 25% of the isolates. Notably, the number of methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis strains increased significantly during the observation period given that not a single methicillin-resistant strain was isolated in the first three years of the study, whereas the detection rate increased up to 74% of the cultured S. epidermidis isolates between 2010 and 2012. MRSA bacteria contributed to 3.0% of all cases and accounted for 11.0% of all S. aureus isolates, but the incidence remained relatively constant over the study period. Furthermore, 6% of the PJIs were caused by Gram-negative bacteria such as enterobacteriaceae (i.e., E. coli, Klebsiella oxytoca, and Citrobacter koseri) of which 25% were ESBL producers. Other pathogen groups were streptococci (7%), enterococci (3%), and anaerobes (1%), whereas 13% were mixed infections, and another 13% were culture-negative cases. Of note, the detection rate of resistant strains primarily of methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis (accounting for 18% of the isolates) increased over time, which could, however, not be observed in case of Gram-negative bacterial infections [21].

In a retrospective monocentric study [22], 318 patients with PJIs treated at the Chinese Zhengzhou Orthopedics Hospital between 2012 and 2018 were analyzed. A total of 148 infections of hip prostheses and 170 of knee prostheses were documented. The average age of the patients was 56.4 years for hip infections and 68.6 years for knee infections. The infections were classified according to their timing after surgery as early (<3 months), delayed (3–12 months), and late (>1 year) infections. In 72.9% of the cases, the pathogens could be detected by culture, while 27.1% of cultures remained negative. Gram-positive pathogens accounted for 65.1% of all infections, while Gram-negative bacteria were detected in 7.9% of cases. The most commonly isolated pathogens were CNS (25.5%), followed by S. aureus (21.1%), whereas other Gram-positive bacteria included other staphylococci (10.7%), streptococci (4.4%) and E. faecalis (3.4%). Among the Gram-negative bacilli, 2.8% were identified as E. coli (2.8%), 1.6% as K. pneumoniae (1.6%), and 0.09% as P. aeruginosa. Fungal infections occurred in 4.8% of cases, and interestingly, almost exclusively in knee replacements. Polymicrobial infections were detected in 21.7% of cases. The methicillin resistance rate among the staphylococci was 28.9%, with methicillin-resistant CNS accounting for 19.8% and MRSA for 9.1%. The regression analysis revealed a declining trend in MRSA detecting rates over the study period, while methicillin-resistant CNS showed an increasing trend. Thus, also in this survey no fundamental shift from Gram-positive to Gram-negative pathogens responsible for PJIs could be observed [22].

Da Silva and Salles analyzed 98 cases of PJIs that had been diagnosed in an orthopedic center in Brazil between 2014 and 2018 [23]. The retrospective monocentric cohort study included patients (average age of 67.3 years) with proven infection after hip or knee replacement, based on at least two culture-positive samples. The majority of cases involved hip prostheses, in most cases after revision surgery. Around 69% were early infections with symptoms manifesting within three months post implantation. In 56 of the 98 cases (i.e., 57.1%), MDR Gram-negative pathogens were detected of which even 26 cases were due to infections by extensively drug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens. The most frequently identified Gram-negative pathogen was Acinetobacter baumannii, followed by Enterobacter aerogenes, K. pneumoniae, E. coli, P. mirabilis, and P. aeruginosa. With respect to Gram-positive bacteria as infectious agents of the PJIs, 15.4% of cases were caused by methicillin-sensitive S. aureus, while 4.8% were MRSA. Hence, the observed spectrum of pathogens suggests that Gram-negative pathogens played a predominant role in causing PJIs in this particular patients cohort [23].

McCulloch et al. [24] conducted a retrospective analysis of 63 patients who had to undergo multiple revision surgeries due to persistent PJIs. The study focused on how the microbiological profile of causative infectious agents were changing over time. Overall, the most common isolates were CNS and S. aureus. After six revision surgeries, 90% of the patients showed a different causative organism compared to the first revision. Remarkably, 53% of the cultured bacteria were MDR isolates. These findings underline that repeated surgery and applied antibiotic treatments may alter the microbiological profile of infectious agents of PJIs. Therefore, the authors recommended to perform microbiological cultures at each revision and to adjust antibiotic therapy accordingly [24].

Another retrospective study by the same group compared PJI cases following standard knee arthroplasty with those following oncologic limb salvage procedures [25]. The cohort included 106 patients. While CNS and E. coli were the most frequently isolated pathogens in both groups, MDR microorganisms were significantly more common in the oncological patient cohort if compared to the arthroplasty group (36% versus 17%). The study highlights that certain patient populations, especially those with a history of chemotherapy or radiation, may be at higher risk for infections with MDR microorganisms. Although the spectrum of isolated bacterial species remained relatively stable, the resistance patterns varied between the two groups [25].

Månsson and colleagues included 66 patients scheduled for elective hip or knee arthroplasty in their prospective observational study [26]. Nasal and skin swabs were taken before hospital admission to evaluate the patients' colonization with MDR S. epidermidis shown to be often responsible for PJIs. The study found that 45% of patients were already colonized with methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis, and 15% carried even MDR S. epidermidis strains that had previously been associated with PJIs. The authors concluded that common preoperative prophylaxis methods, such as chlorhexidine body washes and beta-lactam antibiotics, may reduce loads of sensitive S. epidermidis strains but allow resistant ones to persist. This could explain why PJIs often involve MDR microorganisms, even in early postoperative infections [26]. Collectively, these studies suggest that the bacterial profile in PJI cases can shift over time due to prior treatments and patient-related factors. Resistant strains may already be present before surgery or emerge after repeated revisions. This highlights the need for individualized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies [26].

Therapy in transition

We further addressed what kind of alternative therapeutic approaches exist to date to tackle PJIs. Therefore, we specifically identified studies that focused on novel drug or combinatory treatment strategies going beyond the classic approach consisting of debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR), and investigated their modes of action, especially in infectious scenarios caused by biofilm-producing and MDR pathogens.

Vassena et al. tested a photodynamic therapy with the newly developed photosensitizer RLP068/Cl in vitro [27]. Therefore, plastic surfaces were subjected to bacterial biofilms-producing clinical S. aureus and P. aeruginosa isolates. The intervention consisted of local application of the active ingredient RLP068/Cl, followed by irradiation with light at a wavelength of 690 nm. The authors were able to show that the photodynamic therapy resulted in the elimination of up to 99% of the bacteria within the biofilm, with more prominent effects achieved against the P. aeruginosa biofilms being greatly reduced after 30 s of irradiation only. Of note, both planktonic bacteria and early as well as mature biofilm forms were significantly reduced by the treatment. The authors hypothesized that the antibacterial effect was based on the formation of reactive oxygen species through light activation, which caused structural cell damage to the pathogens. In fact, control groups that lacked either the active ingredient or the light did not show such effects. The study thus provides initial evidence of the potential efficacy of photodynamic therapy against biofilm-associated pathogens, although clinical data are not yet available [27].

Slane and colleagues followed a similar experimental approach but used a different strategy for biofilm inhibition [28]. Here, the effect of silver nanoparticles on the antibacterial properties of bone cement was investigated in vitro. Commercial Palacos-R cement was used, which was produced in four variants: without silver and with 0.2%, 0.5%, and 1.0% silver content. Five cement samples were prepared for each group, and reference strains and clinical isolates of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa applied, respectively. The results showed a clear, concentration-dependent inhibition of biofilm formation on the cement surface. Notably, a significant reduction in biofilm thickness was already apparent at 0.5% silver. At the same time, the mechanical properties of the cement were retained. In support of the report by Vassena et al. [27], this study also provides a preclinical concept aimed at biofilm control, yet with a permanent active ingredient integrated into the material instead of a light-activated therapy [28].

Vila and coworkers reported a small case series of three patients with early hip PJIs caused by MDR A. baumannii [29]. All patients were treated with a DAIR strategy, applying high-dose tigecycline. One patient also received intravenous colistin. In all three cases, the infection was successfully eradicated, and no relapses occurred during follow-up. Although the sample size was small, the study suggests that implant retention might be an option in selected patients with MDR infections, provided that the antibiotic regimen is aggressive and closely monitored.

Fagotti et al. retrospectively assessed risk factors and treatment strategies in patients with failed two-stage revisions [30]. The authors discussed several alternative approaches, such as long-term antibiotic suppression and resection arthroplasty and emphasized that patients with immunosuppressive comorbidities, and those with infections caused by MDR organisms, are at higher risk for treatment failure. In such cases, long-term antimicrobial suppression may be the only realistic option to combat the chronic inflammatory scenario. This study further underlines the importance of tailoring treatment to individual risk profiles rather than following rigid protocols [30].

Angerett and coworkers presented a single case report involving a patient who developed Clostridioides difficile toxin-associated enterocolitis after receiving oral cefadroxil as extended prophylaxis following total knee arthroplasty [31]. The gastrointestinal infection was followed by a MDR E. coli bacteremia and subsequent MDR E. coli-associated PJI, most likely due to bacterial translocation from the intestinal tract. Besides the E. coli bacteria, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron constituting an obligate anaerobic commensal member of the gut microbiota could be isolated from the infected joint. The authors concluded that extended use of oral antibiotics may disrupt the gut microbiota and increase the risk for opportunistic infections. The case highlights that even preventive strategies can sometimes lead to serious complications and should be carefully evaluated. Together, these studies provide insight into alternative approaches when standard surgical strategies fail or are inappropriate. They also show that antibiotic strategies need to be carefully chosen to avoid unintended consequences such as resistance development or secondary infections [30].

In contrast to the two afore-mentioned in vitro studies [30], Ji et al. investigated an established surgical procedure in a clinical setting [32]. The retrospective cohort study was conducted at a Chinese university hospital. Of the 126 patients who underwent surgery due to chronic periprosthetic hip joint infection, 111 were included in the evaluation. The patients (average age 58.7 years) met the MSIS criteria for chronic PJIs (which are widely accepted as an international standard) and had a mean symptom duration of 10.8 months. The intervention consisted of a single-stage revision with a cementless prosthesis, extensive debridement, and local administration of vancomycin in powder form. In high-risk cases, intra-articular antibiotic infusion was additionally administered. The results revealed that Gram-positive bacteria dominated among the identified pathogens (51.4%) including MRSA and methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis, followed by Gram-negative pathogens (20.7%) and fungi (3.6%), whereas 20.7% of the cases were due to culture-negative infections. The treatment success rate was 89.2% indicative for infection-free cases over a mean follow-up period of 58 months. The Harris Hip Score assessing function, improvements in mobility, and pain after surgery improved on average from 40.2 to 79.6 points. In comparison to the previous reports, the focus of this study was not on biofilm inhibition, but rather on an integrative surgical treatment concept with adjuvant local antibiotic administration [32].

Another alternative method was presented by Huang et al. [33]. In this study, the authors tested the synthetic antimicrobial peptide PLG0206 that had been designed to act specifically against MDR pathogens and bacterial biofilms both in vitro and in vivo. By applying a rabbit PJI model, an infectious inoculum of methicillin-sensitive S. aureus was introduced in the knee joint via a Kirschner wire implantation. Two days after infection, surgical revision with debridement and irrigation was performed, in accordance with the DAIR procedure in human PJIs. The infected joint space was additionally treated intraoperatively with PLG0206 by local application of the peptide to the infected site, followed by a washout after an exposure time of 5.0, 7.5, or 15.0 min. The results revealed that the PLG0206 treatment led to a significant reduction in the bacterial loads on the implant and surrounding tissues, in some cases even resulting in cultural sterility. In addition, a long-term survival analysis was performed in which one group was treated with PLG0206 in combination with cefazolin, a standard antibiotic against methicillin-sensitive S. aureus. This combined therapy resulted in a significantly higher survival rate compared to the control group treated with antibiotics alone. It is noteworthy that PLG0206 was also able to exert a rapid bactericidal effect in biofilm-associated infections [33].

A very recent case report from 2025 by Marcheggiani Muccioli and colleagues describes the successful treatment of a periprosthetic infection of the knee joint caused by methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis in a multimorbid patient presenting with dysmetabolic myopathy, celiac disease, and multiple allergies [34]. The patient was treated with oral rifampicin in combination with continuously infused fosfomycin via a portable elastomer pump as part of an outpatient therapy concept. The entire antibiotic therapy was carried out for 7 weeks in total. This period was divided into several phases with therapy breaks and adjustments due to temporary side effects. After 4 months with an antibiotic-containing cement spacer, a hypo-allergenic cemented knee prosthesis was reimplanted. The functional result was remarkable given that the patient achieved a range of motion of 0-0-125 for his previously infected knee joint indicating an extension of 0° and a flexion of 125°. This allowed pain-free mobility and completely preserved the patient's quality of life. Just one year after the operation, the patient was active in sports again and very satisfied with the result [34].

Discussion

Pathogen spectrum in PJIs and changes over time

The reviewed studies revealed that the spectrum of pathogens causing PJIs might not only vary regionally but also change over time. A Spanish multicenter study, for instance, showed a significant increase in MDR pathogens and particularly in Gram-negative bacteria [20]. Likewise, Da Silva and Salles [23] found an increase in PJIs caused by MDR Gram-negative bacilli and particularly reported A. baumannii as endemic, suggesting a persistent nosocomial source indicating that difficult-to-treat Gram-negative bacteria play a central role in the pathogen spectrum of PJIs in the investigated Brazilian patient cohort. In a German survey [21], however, the pathogen spectrum of PJI-causing agents remained quite stable over a period of twelve years. The authors highlighted a pronounced increase in methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis, accounting for 74% of all S. epidermidis isolates during the last observation period, whereas no increase in Gram-negative pathogens was documented [21]. In line, a Chine study did not find a significant shift of the pathogen spectrum toward Gram-negative bacteria [22]. Whereas there was a decline in MRSA cases over a 6-year observation period, methicillin-resistant CNS including S. epidermidis increased over time. This development could indicate changes in hygiene management, antibiotic use, or biofilm-based infection dynamics [22]. The highly varying distributions of Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens in international comparisons suggest that geographical location, hospital structure, antibiotic practice, and patient populations are key factors influencing the microbiological profile of PJIs. A global shift in the pathogen spectrum cannot be inferred from the available data, but regional differences and temporal developments among individual study regions can be identified.

Therapeutic concepts of PJIs including alternative treatment options

Several studies addressed novel alternative or adjunct therapeutic approaches in the treatment of PJIs ranging from new antimicrobial molecules and physically based methods to modified surgical procedures. What all these concepts have in common is that they have mainly been tested in preclinical trials and hence, are not yet part of established clinical standards. One antibiotics-independent approach that appears particularly promising for biofilm-associated infections was investigated by Vassena et al. [27]. The applied photodynamic therapy reduced both planktonic and biofilm-forming pathogens through formation of reactive oxygen species causing bacterial cell damage. Another preclinical concept of using silver nanoparticles on bone cement proved potent anti-biofilm properties that could find access to clinical practice as adjunct strategy to prevent primary joint infections [28]. Furthermore, promising results were derived from a study addressing a surgical treatment alternative and revealed that cementless reconstruction with local antibiotic application can also be successful in treating chronic infections given good functional outcomes and low reinfection rates. The study thus provides evidence that standard surgical procedures can be modified without compromising the quality of treatment [32]. A study by Huang and colleagues revealed promising efficacies of an antibiotics-independent molecule, namely the synthetic antimicrobial peptide PLG0206, which exerts its antimicrobial effect by destroying the bacterial cell membrane. Remarkably, the substance achieved a pronounced antibacterial effect against both, Gram-positive and Gram-negative biofilm-forming bacteria in in vitro as well as in vivo including clinicial studies. Importantly, no development of resistance against PLG0206 was observed [33]. Collectively, it can be said that alternative therapeutic approaches for PJIs are at various stages of development. While some concepts, such as PLG0206 or photodynamic methods, rely primarily on novel antimicrobial molecules, others focus on optimizing surgical strategies or modifying surfaces to prevent biofilm growth. Hence, there are several potential ways to supplement or improve existing treatment standards. However, intensive future research including clinical trials are needed before the bench-to-bedside transfer.

A central challenge for the appropriate treatment of PJIs is due to the inadequate and often delayed identification of the causative pathogen. A retrospective analysis showed that in more than a quarter of cases, no pathogen could be detected by conventional cultural methods [22]. This inevitably leads to an empirical broad-spectrum therapy, often with a cephalosporin and vancomycin, without the possibility of targeted antimicrobial control and in consequence, promotes antibiotic resistance development as collateral damage. It is therefore mandatory to call for the consistent use of optimized established pre-analytic procedures and microbiological identification methods such as sonication, extended culture times, and molecular techniques resulting in an increase in sensitivity of pathogen detection. This is further underscored by a study showing that in patients with multiple revised hip and knee prostheses, different pathogens were detected in over 90% of cases after six revisions if compared to the initial surgical procedure, whereas even after the second revision, a different infectious agent was isolated in almost half of the PJI patients [24]. This dynamic cannot be explained by new infections alone, but also points to possible gaps in the initial diagnosis. In addition, the number of MDR bacterial pathogens increased significantly with every following revision. This further underscores that a concise microbiological diagnostic work-up according to optimal standardized protocols of pre-analytic and analytic procedures should be initiated upon each revision, particularly in complex or persistent infections. In addition, a close interdisciplinary exchange of clinicians and microbiologists regarding diagnostic, preventive, and therapeutic measures should be implemented in everyday clinical practice to improve outcomes of PJIs. This is further underscored by a very recent Dutch study from 2025 [35]. In a specialized center, a total of 1,321 treatment decisions were made for 509 patients with bone and joint infections by an interdisciplinary board comprising orthopedic surgeons, clinical infectious disease specialists, microbiologists, and other experts. The implementation rate of these joint recommendations was over 90%. However, in cases where the board's treatment recommendation was not implemented or only partially implemented, the cure rate fell significantly from over 70% to just 33% [35]. This result underlines the clinical benefits of structured and binding interdisciplinary decision-making and shows that microbiological expertise should not only be complementary, but also integrated and actively involved in treatment.

Månsson et al. [26] provided evidence that the prophylactic strategies currently used in endoprosthetics may unintentionally contribute to the selection of resistant bacterial pathogens. The study showed that common preoperative prophylaxis procedures such as antiseptic washings and antibiotic prophylaxis might affect distinct commensal bacteria colonizing the skin or mucosal surfaces, induce resistance mechanisms, and promote clonal expansion of methicillin-resistant S. epidermidis or MRSA strains. These resistant bacteria might translocate on a later time point via the blood stream to other body compartments and cause PJIs. In support, a case study reported that a patient developed a PJI upon knee joint replacement that was associated with a C. difficile toxin-associated enterocolitis triggered by postoperative administration of cefadroxil [31]. During this inflammatory scenario commensal bacterial were able to translocate from the gastrointestinal tract across the inflamed intestinal mucosa via the bloodstream resulting in a hematogenous PJI by a MDR E. coli strain and B. thetaiotaomicron. The authors questioned the extended postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis that is standard practice for high-risk patients in many institutions. The case further illustrates that this measure can have not only advantages but also significant adverse effects and hence, the benefits should be critically weighed against the risks, especially in older and multimorbid patients with a potentially compromised intestinal epithelial barrier.

A case series by Vila and colleagues provided evidence how targeted and individually tailored antibiotic therapy can control infection even in the case of MDR pathogens [29]. Three patients with PJI caused by MDR A. baumannii were successfully treated with high-dose tigecycline in combination with debridement without the need for prosthesis replacement. The therapy was well-tolerated and no serious side effects developed. All patients remained symptom-free and the implanted hip prostheses remained functional. Although the number of cases was very small and the lack of a control group significantly limits the generalization of the results, the study still provides important inspiration for decisions in individual cases. This is particularly true for complex infections where conventional treatment strategies reach their limits or prosthesis replacement is not feasible.

More than just an infection – the PJI patient in the overall clinical context

An association of preexisting morbidities of the patient and an unfavorable outcome of a two-stage revision procedure in periprosthetic hip joint infections was clearly demonstrated by Fagotti et al. [30]. The authors found that immune-compromising factors such obesity, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal failure, rheumatoid arthritis, malignancies, and other immunosuppressive conditions did not only impair infection control but also postoperative healing and resulted in higher rates of persistent or relapsing infections. This implies the need for a careful preoperative risk assessment and an individualized treatment strategy to support the positive outcome of the two-stage revision procedures in vulnerable groups [30]. This is further underscored by a study demonstrating that patients with tumor-associated endoprostheses and a history of radio- and/or chemotherapy were more frequently infected with MDR pathogens including Gram-negative bacilli if compared to individuals with conventional knee prostheses [25]. The authors hypothesized that besides the more reduced physical conditions of the patient, more complex surgical procedures, cytotoxic therapies, and hence, compromised local and systemic immune responses increased the risk for PJIs [25]. In line, a large-scale study from 2024 [36] revealed that patients who received chemotherapy within the first year after knee, hip, or shoulder endoprosthesis implantation more often developed PJIs than individuals without such compromising treatment further underlining the necessity for an individual preoperative risk assessment to any endoprosthetic procedure and a rather tailored intervention beyond uniform regimens. A structured, prevention-oriented approach such as the “search and destroy” concept established in Scandinavian countries offers a promising approach in this context. Although this is not the individualized strategy for infection prevention that is required, it is a concept that can be easily established and has proven to be practicable. This model includes the systematic identification of persons at risk, preoperative screening for pathogens such as MRSA, targeted decolonization with mupirocin nasal ointment and chlorhexidine washes, for instance, if necessary, and adjustment of the perioperative antibiotic therapy based on microbiological findings [37]. The current guideline of the European Society of Clinical Microbiology and Infectious Diseases (ESCMID) [38] explicitly emphasizes the importance of this strategy and recommends that elective endoprosthetic procedures in countries with a high MRSA prevalence should be preceded by preoperative screening and, if necessary, decolonization to prevent nosocomial infections later-on. In Sweden, Norway, and Denmark, this approach has been implemented for long as standard clinical practice and is supported by national surveillance and quality programs also outside Scandinavia [37]. Even though the incidence rates of MRSA infections have been declining in recent years, there is still a need for optimizing prevention strategies [39].

Possibilities and challenges of phage therapy in PJIs

Very recent review articles [40, 41] summarized studies addressing the use of bacteriophages in PJIs of hip and knee prostheses particularly in difficult-to-treat patients who mostly suffered from protracted infections caused by bacteria including MDR and biofilm-producing strains. The phages were often injected locally into the joint during surgery and, in some cases, additionally given intravenously. As a result, significant clinical improvement could be observed and the phages could sufficiently counteract and even clear infections caused by CNS, S. aureus including MRSA, and even P. aeruginosa. In some cases, an initially planned removal of the prosthesis could be avoided upon phage application. Overall, the treatment was well tolerated. Despite these positive findings, the available data is still limited. Most of the publications to date are based on individual cases or very small patient groups. Randomized controlled studies are currently missing. There is also no general agreement on the standardized selection of suitable phages, the optimal dosage, and the form of application. In addition, phage preparations may currently only be used in individual treatment tests, which makes their general use in everyday clinical practice difficult. Despite these limitations, the cases documented up to now show that phages could be a valuable addition to current therapies. Phage therapy seems to be a promising strategy, especially in situations where conventional methods have reached their limits. However, further preclinical studies and controlled clinical trials are necessary also to address long-term safety issues [40, 41].

Limitations

Like any scientific work, the present review is subject to certain methodological and content-related limitations. For instance, several of the included studies present individual case reports or case series with very low numbers of patients. In addition, given differences in experiment designs such as inclusion criteria of patients and control subjects, treatment strategies, applied bacterial strains, for instance, studies might not be directly comparable making a quantitative meta-analysis virtually impossible. Furthermore, PubMed was the only data base used, so scientific work indexed elsewhere might have been missed. Finally, search and analysis of the publications were performed by only one scientist bearing the risk for a selection bias. Hence, despite the careful approach and precise work, mistakes cannot be rules out.

Conclusions

PJIs are serious complications that require extensive treatment, particularly in vulnerable such as multimorbid patient groups suffering from compromised immune functions. S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and P. aeruginosa are among the most common infectious agents with varying prevalence rates, particularly of MDR strains depending on the clinical course of infection (acute/chronic), patient populations, and regional aspects. Many PJI-associated pathogens produce biofilms further complicating eradication and sufficient treatment. While standard antibiotic therapy besides surgical revision remains the main corner stone of treatment, the use of alternative/adjunct antibiotics-independent methods are increasingly coming to the fore. These include the targeted dissolution of bacterial biofilms, enzyme-based approaches, application of bacteriophages, and infection prevention measures following initial risk assessment of the patient. Studies published to date yet show promising approaches, but there is still a lack of clinical evidence of their therapeutic value in everyday care. Future research should focus on optimized early pathogen detection measures, whereas individually tailored treatment concepts and their application in interdisciplinary settings will be important in the combat of PJIs. Finally, a close interdisciplinary exchange of clinicians and microbiologists regarding diagnostic, preventive, and therapeutic measures should be implemented in everyday clinical practice to improve the outcomes of PJIs.
