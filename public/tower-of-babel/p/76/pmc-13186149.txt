Predictors of Mortality and Outcomes in Older Adult Burn Patient Subgroups: A Comparative Meta-analysis of Age, Burn Severity, and Intervention Strategies
Authors: Yomna E. Dean, Mohamed I. Mohamed, Alaa Alqadi, Abu Omayer, Dina Elsayed, Abdulrahman Shokri, Rashed W. Alweshah, Shree Rath, Israa Alam, Moussa Nassar, Kevin McGarry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Older adult burn patients experience disproportionately high morbidity and mortality due to age-related physiological decline and comorbidities. Understanding predictors of mortality is essential for risk stratification and improving outcomes. This meta-analysis evaluates mortality predictors and clinical outcomes among older adult burn patients, stratified into a younger cohort (60-79 y) and an older cohort (80+ y).Methods:A systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was conducted on February 2, 2024. Meta-analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.1) with random-effects models. Relative risks (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, and certainty of evidence was evaluated using GRADE.Results:Fifty-eight studies comprising 37,732 older adult burn patients were included. Patients aged 60-79 had significantly lower intensive care unit admission risk (RR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.63–0.88) and lower mortality (RR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.58–0.73) compared with those aged 80 or older. Total burn surface area (TBSA) strongly predicted mortality: TBSA 1%–9% versus 10%–19% (RR: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.21–0.44) and 10%–19% versus ≥20% (RR: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.11–0.27). Flame burns significantly increased mortality compared with scald burns (RR: 3.98, 95% CI: 2.68–5.91). Inhalation injury (RR: 2.95) and intubation (RR: 5.66) further elevated mortality risk. Grafting reduced mortality compared with conservative management (RR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.54–0.82).Conclusions:Age 80 years or older, larger TBSA, flame burns, inhalation injury, and intubation substantially increase mortality in older adult burn patients, whereas grafting is associated with improved survival. These findings support age-specific risk stratification and tailored management strategies to optimize outcomes.

Takeaways

Question: What factors influence mortality in older adult burn patients, and how do outcomes differ between younger (60–79) and older (80+) groups?

Findings: Inhalation injury, flame burns, and intubation significantly increased mortality. Grafting reduced mortality risk. Mortality was lower in the younger group despite similar total burn surface area and burn etiology distributions.

Meaning: Key predictors of mortality in older adult burn patients, such as total burn surface area, burn etiology, and respiratory complications, should guide clinical decisions. Age should inform—but not dictate—burn management, and grafting may offer a survival benefit, even in older adults.

INTRODUCTION

Burn injury management presents a critical clinical challenge in older adult populations.1 Previous studies have identified that individuals 60 years of age and older face disproportionately higher mortality rates compared with younger age groups.2,3 The heightened vulnerability of older adult burn patients stems from increased environmental risk, age-related physiological changes, preexisting comorbidities, and diminished recovery capacity, all of which can negatively affect clinical outcome.1 Studies indicate that burns involving more than 40% of the total burn surface area (TBSA), which are eminently survivable in younger patients, are associated with a nearly 100% fatality rate in older patients.4,5 This meta-analysis further investigates the injury- and patient-based factors contributing to this finding (Fig. 1).

Both patient- and injury-related factors have already been identified as significantly influencing mortality and recovery in the older adult burn patient population. These include TBSA, burn etiology, inhalational injuries, and intervention strategies.2,4,5 Inhalation injuries alone can increase mortality risk by nearly 3-fold,3 whereas timely surgical interventions such as grafting have been shown to reduce mortality.5 However, the unique challenges posed by age-related vulnerabilities mean that outcomes vary widely across different age subsets within the older adult population.6 Emerging evidence suggests that patients aged 60-79 years may have better survival outcomes than those aged 80 years or older,3,6 highlighting the importance of stratifying this population to identify differential risks and optimize care strategies.

Despite advancements in burn care, current research often treats older adult patients as a homogeneous group, overlooking critical differences in outcomes based on age and other predictors. This lack of stratification limits the ability to tailor interventions effectively, potentially leading to suboptimal outcomes for the most vulnerable patients. Furthermore, although certain predictors of mortality, such as TBSA and burn etiology, are well-established, their interaction with age and the efficacy of specific interventions still need to be explored.

This study aimed to bridge these knowledge gaps by conducting a comprehensive meta-analysis to evaluate predictors of mortality and outcomes in older adult burn patients. Patients were stratified into a younger cohort (60-79 y) and an older cohort (80+ y) and by outcomes examined through subgroup analysis of epidemiological characteristics, mortality rates, TBSA, and intervention strategies. We hypothesize that patients aged 60-79 years exhibit better outcomes than those aged 80 years or older and that targeted clinical interventions, particularly surgical grafting, will significantly reduce mortality risks in both groups. This research seeks to inform age-specific treatment protocols and improve survival and recovery for older adult burn patients by addressing these gaps.

METHODS

Study Design

This is a systematic review and meta-analysis that follows the regulations of the preferred reporting items of systematic reviews and meta-analysis (PRISMA),7 and its protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024510651).

We considered the studies eligible for inclusion if their population was burn patients aged older than 60 years, and if they included any of the following outcomes: mortality and survival, types of burns (flame and scald), TBSA, length of hospital stay, burns stratified by sex, intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, treatment (conservative versus grafting), inhalational injuries, and intubations. Mortality-related outcomes (by age subgroup and by TBSA) were designated as primary outcomes, reflecting their central prognostic importance in older adult burn patients. Other outcomes, including burn etiology, ICU admission, inhalation injury, intubation, treatment approach, length of stay, and sex differences, were considered secondary outcomes. Studies with other age groups (children and adults younger than 60 y of age) as their population were excluded. Only cohort, case-control, and cross-sectional studies were deemed eligible for inclusion, whereas reviews, editorials, meta-analyses, and animal studies were excluded.

Literature Search and Data Extraction

A literature search was performed on February 2, 2024, on the following databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, using keywords such as “burn,” “thermal injuries,” and “geriatric.” (View the full search strategy in the supplementary material.)

Two independent reviewers screened the studies on Covidence; in case of a conflict, a third reviewer was consulted.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

For the baseline and summary, 2 independent coauthors extracted the following data: the last name of the first author, year of publication, study design, country, sample size, sex, age, comorbidities, and hospital setting. We extracted the following data for the outcomes: burn etiology, TBSA, mortality, intubations, treatment (conservative versus grafting), and ICU admissions. Similarly, 2 independent extractors worked on each included study, and a third independent reviewer referred to the original article to settle any discrepancies identified.

The study population was further divided into 2 subgroups: a younger group (60–79 y) and an older group (80+ y). The cutoff value for the younger group was chosen as 60 years of age or older, conforming to the United Nations’ definition of older persons.8 The decision to categorize older adults as such was based on the age group stratifications published in the National Burn Repository by the American Burn Association, where the 60–60.9 age group represents the youngest older adults subgroup, and the 80 and older age group represents the oldest subgroup.9 This repository represents the most comprehensive epidemiological collection of burn-related injuries in North America. Several studies have also utilized similar subgroupings, such as an epidemiological cohort from central China’s largest burn center10 and a retrospective chart review from a verified level I trauma and burn center in New York.11 We additionally stratified the study population into survivors and nonsurvivors.

Using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) items,12 the risk of bias was assessed, with a total score of 9 points, to evaluate the quality of the observational studies. We determined that observational studies with an NOS score of greater than or equal to 7 stars are of high quality, and an NOS score of less than 7 stars are of low quality.

The certainty of evidence for outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE approach via GRADEpro GDT, taking into account risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Statistical Analysis

We conducted a meta-analysis on the odds ratios (ORs), relative risk (RR), and 95% confidence intervals using the R software (version 4.4.1), and the significance threshold was set at a P value less than 0.05. The inverse variance (IV) and Mantel–Haenszel methods, which provide study weight based on the IV calculation of each study, were utilized to aggregate the results of the meta-analysis. Studies with smaller variance were given greater weight in the finalized pooled estimate calculation. Under the random-effects model, these weights were further adjusted for between-study variance (tau2 and I2) to account for heterogeneity. We further utilized the leave-one-out test and subgroup analyses to address heterogeneity. For publication bias detection, we plotted contour-enhanced funnel plots and calculated the linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry (Egger test).13 Whenever heterogeneity was attributed to a single study, we utilized a sensitivity analysis to exclude that study and calculated the effect estimates following the exclusion.

Predictors of Mortality

TBSA emerged as a significant predictor of mortality in older adult patients. Those with a TBSA of 1%–9% had a 69% lower mortality risk than patients with a TBSA of 10%–19% (RR: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.21–0.44, I2 = 28%). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 9, which displays a forest plot showing the association between TBSA and mortality, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E831.) Similarly, patients with a TBSA of 10%–19% experienced an 83% reduction in mortality risk compared with those with a TBSA of 20% or more (RR: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.11–0.27, I2 = 60%) (Fig. 3). Additionally, older adult patients with flame burns had a 298% higher mortality risk than those with scald burns (3.98; 95% CI: 2.68–5.91, I2 = 79%). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 10, which displays a forest plot showing association between burn etiology and mortality, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E832.) Gender did not significantly affect mortality risk (RR: 1.09, 95% CI: 0.92–1.30, I2 = 69%). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 11, which displays a forest showing an association between gender and mortality, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E833.) Patients with inhalation injuries had a 195% increased mortality risk compared with those without (RR: 2.95, 95% CI: 2.31–3.78, I2 = 86%). (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 12, which displays a forest plot showing an association between inhalation injury and mortality, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E834.) Moreover, intubated patients exhibited a 466% increased mortality risk relative to those who were not intubated (RR: 5.66, 95% CI: 2.61–12.30, I2 = 84%). Finally, the use of grafts was associated with a 33% reduction in mortality risk compared with conservative management (RR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.54–0.82, I2 = 54%) (Fig. 4). )

Publication Bias

By visual inspection of the funnel plots generated for the analyses, we noticed a slight asymmetry of the plots, which indicates publication bias. However, upon conducting the Egger test, all generated P values were greater than 0.05, indicating undetectable publication bias. DISCUSSION

A number of important clinical findings were revealed in the analysis, increasing TBSA percentages predicted greater mortality rates than expected in older adults. Furthermore, flame burns resulted in almost 4 times the mortality compared with scald burns. Grafting significantly reduced mortality rates compared with conservative management. The younger group, however, demonstrated significantly lower odds of ICU admissions and lower mortalities. Factors predisposing older adult burn patients to poor outcomes following burn injury are summarized in the supplementary file.

Age-related Discrepancies

The World Health Organization predicts that adults older than 60 will represent around 22% by the year 2050, with those older than 80 forming over 400 million of the world’s population. This places a substantial burden on healthcare systems to keep up with their wellness and healthcare demands.70 Despite this rapid projected rise in the older adult population, the literature seems to have loose definitions of “older adults,” “elderly,” “geriatrics,” “aged,” or “seniors,”70 with cutoff values clearly variable and fluctuating in the literature from 45 to 70.1 In our comparative analysis of those aged 60-79 and 80 or older, we used the United Nations definition of older persons as those 60 years of age or older.8 Older adult burn patients should not be treated as a homogeneous group.71 Rather, as illustrated in our analysis, both patients aged 60-79 and the “super-elderly”72 (age 80+) displayed clear variations in their mortality and ICU admission odds.

Comparison of Younger and Older Subgroups

Similar to the Brazilian cohort of 219,365 burn patients from 2009 to 2019,70 the burn etiologies between both the younger and older study groups were comparable. Mortality increases in those older than 80 years of age, with 4.71 more deaths for every 1000 inhabitants in the 80 years or older group. Daronch et al70 also reported significantly greater hospitalization rates and average hospital expenses (P < 0.0001) in older groups, underscoring the greater burden this could have on healthcare systems with limited specialized burn facilities. In line with our findings, a nationwide Dutch analysis by Goei et al involving 505 older adult patients,5 as well as a large Australian study of 2000 older adult patients,73 both reported no statistically significant differences in the percentage of TBSA affected across different age groups (P = 0.121 and 0.87, respectively).

Additionally, Goei et al5 noted how the younger group (65–74 y) experienced significantly lower mortalities than the older group (85+ y), with percentages of 3.1 and 23.8, respectively. Furthermore, the Dutch group compared ICU admissions among their burn patients; their analysis concluded that both younger and older groups had insignificant differences in admissions, mean ICU stay, and mechanical ventilation, disagreeing with our finding of 25% lower risk of ICU admissions in the younger group. This might be due to their smaller sample size in the younger (n = 249) and older groups (n = 77) and the fact that they utilized an analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare 4 different age groups, including a nonelderly group. Similar to Daronch et al,70 the oldest age group had a greater healthcare burden—defined as the median length of stay per TBSA percentage—further highlighting how different age groups among older adults seem to be heterogeneous in terms of their burn injury care.

A Middle Eastern retrospective review55 conducted from 1997 to 2016 gathered data from 5 burn facilities and had similar conclusions to ours, as follows: It appears that the older than 80 subgroup experienced significantly greater mortality rates than their younger counterparts, with a 3-fold increase in burn-related mortalities between the 60–69 and 80+ age groups. Evidence from Harats et al55 also points to insignificant differences in flame burns across the different older adult age groups (P: 0.3). Conversely, they reported how scald burns were more common in the 70–79 and 80+ age groups. Finally, they revealed how increasing age subgroups in older adults predispose them to a greater risk of ICU admissions, confirming our meta-analytic finding.

Overall, it emerges that literature—from different continents and different levels of specialization of burn care facilities—generally conforms with our meta-analytic results. Increasing age among older adults is a nonmodifiable risk factor for poorer burn injury outcomes.

Risk Factors for Mortality Among Older Adults: Burn Etiology

Flame-related burns carrying a higher risk of mortality than scald burns appears to be a common finding across the literature, with Secanho et al29 reporting a significantly greater risk of death from flames, and Harats et al55 illustrating how 58.9% of their burn-related deaths were attributed to flames. Flame burns are linked to greater burns index and prognostic burn index scores, as concluded by Morita et al37 in their 6-year analysis of 35 Japanese older adult burn patients. Additionally, flames usually contribute to more extensive burn injuries and are sometimes associated with inhalation injuries, an established and consistently reported predictor of lethality in older adult burn patients.37

Risk Factors for Mortality Among Older Adults: Inhalational Injuries

Smoke inhalation irreversibly damages the respiratory tract’s epithelium and subjects burn patients to dreaded complications such as pneumonia, an important contributor to multiple organ dysfunction and subsequent mortality in burn patients.57 Furthermore, the hypermetabolic and systemic inflammatory responses in burn patients are greatly increased with inhalation injuries, possibly leading to lung edema.74 Therefore, these facts reinforce our meta-analytic results and highlight inhalational injuries as a major mortality risk in older adult patients.

Risk Factors for Mortality Among Older Adults: TBSA

Unsurprisingly, there is a consensus that indicates heightened mortalities with increasing burn size. The epidemiological analysis by Gurbuz et al56 of burn patients admitted to an inpatient unit showed a TBSA increase of over 3-fold in older adult nonsurvivors compared with survivors (37.8% and 11.4%, respectively). Despite a greater percentage of TBSA being considered an important contributor to mortality in older adults, the evidence appears to be conflicting regarding its role in different timeframes. Lundgren et al75 analyzed 325 seniors with burn injuries in 2009, where TBSA predicted longer hospital stays and greater in-hospital mortalities (P < 0.001 for both) but did not predict long-term mortalities 1 year following discharge in their multivariate model (P: 0.32). This trend is also demonstrated by Cords et al63 in their 1-year and 5-year univariate regression models, underscoring the significance of stratifying mortality predictors based on the timeframe being examined.

Risk Factors for Mortality Among Older Adults: Intubation

Intubated older adult patients exhibited a 466% increased risk of mortality (RR: 5.66), which is considerably higher than that observed in the adult population. For comparison, Kim et al reported a 277.4% higher likelihood of death among intubated adults (OR: 3.774),76whereas pediatric patients remain the most vulnerable, with a 1032% increased risk compared with nonventilated children.77 This strong association between intubation and mortality likely reflects the severity of the underlying burn injury rather than intubation alone. Older adult patients often require intubation in the context of extensive TBSA involvement, deeper burns, or concomitant inhalation injuries, all of which are independent predictors of poor prognosis. Furthermore, age-related physiological decline, comorbidities, and decreased pulmonary reserve make this group particularly susceptible to complications such as pneumonia, sepsis, and multi-organ failure following prolonged ventilation. Therefore, the high mortality associated with intubation may indicate both a marker of disease severity and an increased vulnerability to ventilation-related complications in older adult patients.

Risk Factors for Mortality Among Older Adults: Surgery

Decision-making regarding conservative management versus excision and grafting in older adults remains contentious among plastic surgeons.78 Although early excision and grafting is the established standard of care in younger burn patients, yielding significantly improved survival outcomes,79,80 the situation is more complex in older adults. Their preexisting comorbidities and reduced physiological reserve often raise concerns about surgical tolerance, leading some to advocate conservative treatment approaches.53 The findings of our analysis suggest that grafting is associated with lower mortality in older adult burn patients; however, due to the observational nature of included studies, potential confounding factors, and heterogeneity, this should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of efficacy. Further phase III, multidisciplinary, multicenter clinical trials are needed to clarify the optimal surgical strategy for this population.81

CONCLUSIONS

Our meta-analysis highlights the substantial impact of age and burn characteristics on outcomes in older adult patients. The findings reinforce that increasing TBSA, flame burns, inhalation injuries, and intubation significantly elevate mortality risk, whereas grafting appears to reduce it. Additionally, although younger and older subgroups share similar burn characteristics, patients aged 80 or older face significantly higher ICU admissions and mortality rates. Given the projected rise in the older adult population worldwide, these findings emphasize the urgent need for improved preventive strategies, early intervention, multidisciplinary teams involving palliative care, and specialized burn care protocols tailored to the unique vulnerabilities of older patients. Future research should focus on refining risk stratification models and exploring interventions to optimize outcomes for this high-risk population.
