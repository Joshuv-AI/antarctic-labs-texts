Neferine inhibits the development of lung cancer cells by downregulating TGF‐β to regulate MST1/ROS‐induced pyroptosis
Authors: Peng‐Cheng Zhong, Zhi‐Wen Liu, Qi‐Chang Xing, Jia Chen, Rui‐Pei Yang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for ~85% of all lung cancer cases. Neferine is used as a traditional Chinese medicine with many pharmacological effects, including antitumor properties; however, it has not been reported whether neferine plays an anticancer role by causing pyroptosis in NSCLC cells. We used two typical lung cancer cell lines, A549 and H1299, and 42 lung cancer tissue samples to investigate the regulatory effects of neferine on TGF‐β and MST1. We also treated lung cancer cells with different concentrations of neferine to study its effects on lung cancer cell survival, migration, invasion, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) as well as on pyroptosis. Lentivirus‐mediated gain‐of‐function studies of TGF‐β and MST1 were applied to validate the roles of TGF‐β and MST1 in lung cancer. Next, we used murine transplanted tumor models to evaluate the effect of neferine treatment on the metastatic capacity of lung cancer tissues. With increasing neferine concentration, the viability, migration, invasion, and EMT capacity of A549 and H1299 cells decreased, whereas pyroptosis increased. Neferine repressed TGF‐β expression to modulate the induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by MST1. Overexpression of TGF‐β in either in vitro or mouse‐transplanted A549 cells restored the inhibitory effect of neferine on tumor development. Overexpression of MST1 clearly enhanced pyroptosis. Neferine contributed to pyroptosis by regulating MST1 expression through downregulation of TGF‐β to induce ROS formation. Therefore, our study shows that neferine can serve as an adjuvant therapy for NSCLC patients.

INTRODUCTION

Lung cancer is the most common type of cancer and is a major contributor to cancer‐related deaths worldwide. 1 , 2 Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for ~85% of all lung cancer patients. Among these, lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma are the most common. 3 Because most patients with NSCLC are diagnosed at a late stage, chemotherapy remains the principal means of treatment and has a marked impact on survival and prognosis. This has also led to resistance to chemotherapy in NSCLC, predominantly because anticancer therapy fails to trigger apoptotic or nonapoptotic cell death. 4 Therefore, resistance to apoptosis is a major barrier to chemotherapy failure. Bypassing apoptotic signals to initiate cancer cell death has been suggested as a prospective strategy to overcome this problem. 5 Pyroptosis is a newly discovered, nonapoptosis‐regulated cell death mechanism with a novel molecular profile. Pyroptosis is a type of programmed cell death characterized by cell swelling, the formation of bulky vacuoles across the plasma membrane, and damage to the plasma membrane. Proinflammatory cytokines that trigger pyroptosis include caspase‐1, ‐4, and ‐5 in humans. While caspase‐1‐mediated pyroptosis is usually initiated by classical inflammatory vesicles, caspase‐4/5‐mediated pyroptosis is commonly induced by non‐classical inflammatory vesicles. 6 , 7 , 8 Transformation of apoptosis into pyroptosis has the potential to overcome resistance to apoptosis. In addition, experimental evidence indicates that pyroptosis and tumor immunity interact within the tumor microenvironment. 9 , 10 In lung cancer, pyroptosis triggers antitumor immune cell infiltration and enhances cisplatin sensitivity in regressing NSCLC. 11 Currently, there are relatively few reports on pyroptosis in NSCLC, and its mechanisms are not well understood.

Neferine is the main bisbenzylisoquinoline alkaloid isolated from the green plant Nelumbo nucifera Gaerth (Lotus), which is consumed as a dietary staple in traditional Chinese medicine. 12 Previous studies have revealed that neferine has many pharmacological effects, including antiarrhythmic, antihypertensive, antitumor, and cholinesterase inhibitory effects. 13 , 14 It has been reported that neferine inhibits the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells. 15 Neferine may reduce bleomycin‐induced pulmonary fibrosis. 16 Neferine also inhibits the proliferation of A549 cells by inducing autophagy. 17 Furthermore, neferine regulates pyroptosis and improves neuroinflammation and oxidative stress injury in hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy. 13 However, whether neferine plays an anticancer role by inducing pyroptosis in NSCLC cells has not been reported.

Transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) signaling is a critical pathway in cancer. 18 TGF‐β is thought to promote tumor growth. There are many reports using breast cancer cells to study the cooperation between mutated RAS and TGF‐β in triggering epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) or enhancing cell aggression. 19 Similarly, TGF‐β is highly expressed in NSCLC. 20 Previous studies have shown that TGF‐β promotes the invasion of NSCLC cell invasion. 21 Inhibition of TGF‐β signaling was shown to accelerate pyroptosis in triple‐negative breast cancer. 22 In studies of liver and lung fibrosis, neferine was found to reduce the development of fibrosis by downregulating TGF‐β. 23 It would be worthwhile to study whether neferine suppresses the development of lung cancer, and especially NSCLC, by downregulating TGF‐β signaling and affecting pyroptosis.

Mammalian STE20‐like kinase 1 (MST1) acts upstream of the core Hippo pathway and controls its activity. MST1 was originally reported as a proapoptotic cytoplasmic kinase essential for amplifying apoptotic signaling. 24 Recent studies have revealed that MST1 is a putative tumor suppressor in various types of cancer, including breast and lung cancers. 25 , 26 These findings suggest that MST1 acts as a tumor suppressor by regulating the apoptosis, migration, and proliferation of cancer cells. In a study on pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, MST1 inhibited the progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells through ROS (reactive oxygen species)‐induced pyroptosis. 27 However, there are no reports indicating that MST1 regulates pyroptosis to inhibit the development of lung cancer. Recent studies have shown that TGF‐β promotes the development of glial malignancies by mediating the loss of MST1 expression. 28 Therefore, we speculated that a similar regulatory mechanism exists in lung cancer, which is the main focus of this study.

Herein, we observed high expression of TGF‐β and low expression of MST1 in clinical lung cancer tissues with reduced pyroptosis. Neferine induces pyroptosis in vitro and in mice, preventing NSCLC cell development and EMT. This mechanism was attributed to the regulation of MST1 by neferine via the downregulation of TGF‐β. MST1 overexpression mediates ROS‐induced pyroptosis, leading to proliferation, metastasis, and blockage of EMT in NSCLC cells. Therefore, neferine promotes MST1 expression by downregulating TGF‐β, which promotes pyroptosis in NSCLC cells and blocks cell proliferation, metastasis, and EMT.

METHODS

Clinical specimen collections

Lung cancer and normal paracancerous tissues (n = 42) were collected at Xiangtan Central Hospital. This clinical trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangtan Central Hospital. All the patients who underwent surgical treatment signed an informed consent form.

Cell culture and treatment

The Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences provided the human lung cancer cell lines A549 and H1299 (Shanghai, China). BALB/c fetuses were removed by cesarean section and stored in ice‐cold D‐Hank's buffer. The epidermal tissue was removed and chopped into 1‐mm fragments. The tissue was then digested in the presence of type I collagenase (200 U/mL; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and hyaluronidase (100 U/mL, Sigma‐Aldrich) at 37°C with agitation, centrifuged to remove the supernatant, and resuspended in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) for culturing. All cells were grown in DMEM (Sigma‐Aldrich), which typically comprised 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), 100 U/mL of penicillin and streptomycin, and 3 mM glutamine, at 37°C and 5% CO2. For neferine (HY‐N0441, purity 99.92%; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ)‐treated lung cancer cell experiments, cells were raised to approximately 60% density and then treated with different gradient concentrations of neferine for 48 h. Cells were treated with 3 mM NAC (ROS inhibitor, 50303ES05; Yeasen, Shanghai, China), 100 μM z‐VAD‐FMK (apoptosis inhibitor, HY‐16658, MedChemExpress), 30 μM necrostatin‐1 (programmed Necrosis Inhibitors, HY‐15760, MedChemExpress), 4 mM 3‐methyladenine (autophagy inhibitor, HY‐19312, MedChemExpress), 2 μM ferrostatin‐1 (ferroptosis Inhibitor, HY‐100579, MedChemExpress), and 10 μM disulfiram (pyroptosis inhibitor, HY‐B0240, MedChemExpress).

Plasmid construction and lentiviral envelopes

Human TGF‐β and MSX1 cDNA sequences were ligated into the pLV‐EF1a‐IRES‐Puro lentiviral vector (85,132; Addgene, Watertown, MA) to generate plasmids that overexpressed the two proteins. The lentiviruses were packaged into 293 T cells. Lentiviruses can infect lung cancer cells (multiplicity of infection: 5). Using real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) and immunoblotting, overexpression efficiencies were verified after stable cell lines were selected for 7 days using 2 g/mL puromycin.

Animal assays

Trophic Animal Feed High‐Tech Co. (Nantong, China) supplied the BALB/c nude mice, aged 6–8 weeks. Mice were randomly divided into four groups of five mice each for the tumorigenicity experiments. A549 cells were suspended in 100 μL of DMEM containing 50% Matrigel (356,230; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and injected subcutaneously into each mouse's right or left axilla. Neferine was solubilized in 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma‐Aldrich) and 90% corn oil (Aladdin, Shanghai, China). The same solvents were used as controls. The drug was administered intraperitoneally (10, 20, or 30 mg/kg daily). Tumor size was measured at 15, 19, 23, 27, 31, and 35 days. Tumors were collected from mice on day 35. For the liver metastasis experiments, nude mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 per group). Each nude mouse received a tail vein injection of 2 × 106 with or without TGF‐β overexpressing A549 cells (20 mg/kg, daily). Simultaneously, neferine was used for treatment (20 mg/kg daily) was administered. After 8 weeks, the liver tissues were removed, and the number of nodules on the liver surface was counted to determine tumor metastasis. Liver tissue was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological examination. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the regulations of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine Animal Resource Center.

Cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8) assay

CCK‐8 was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Cell viability was measured according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, A549 and H1299 cells to be detected were cultured in a 96‐well plate and adhered to the wall. Then, 10 μL of CCK‐8 reagent and 90 μL of medium were included in each well and cells were further incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The optical density was measured at 460 nm. Cell viability was measured as the absorbance in the experimental group divided by that in the blank group.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Cell suspensions (3 × 104 cells/well) were transferred to an empty upper chamber (Transwell; Corning Inc., Corning, NY) for migration tests or to a precoated Matrigel chamber for invasion assays to evaluate the migratory and invasive capabilities of lung cancer cells. Then, 20% FBS was added to the medium in the lower chamber. The cells were immersed in fixative solution for 30 min, incubated for 24 h, stained with crystal violet, and washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) (5 min). The migration and invasion of lung cancer cells were recorded and counted using an Olympus (Tokyo, Japan) inverted microscope.

Western blot analysis

Lung cancer cells or tumor tissue samples were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer, and protein quantification was performed using the bicinchonic acid method. The extracted protein samples were separated using sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were placed at 4°C and incubated overnight with the following primary antibodies: TGF‐β1 (3709; Cell Signaling Technologies, Danvers, MA), MSX1 (720,172, Thermo Fisher Scientific), pro‐caspase‐1 (2225, Cell Signaling Technologies), cleaved caspase‐1 (89,332, Cell Signaling Technologies), IL‐1β (9722, Abcam), IL‐18 (ab191860, Abcam), E‐cadherin (4065, Cell Signaling Technologies), N‐cadherin (sc‐59,987; Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, Dallas, TX), vimentin (sc‐373,717, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies), IL‐1β (12,242, Cell Signaling Technologies), IL‐18 (67,775, Cell Signaling Technologies), GSDMD (69,469, Cell Signaling Technologies), cleaved‐GSDMD (36,425, Cell Signaling Technologies), and GAPDH (97,166, Cell Signaling Technologies). The membranes were washed with Tris‐buffered saline containing Tween and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using a Tanon 5200 series fully automated chemiluminescence analysis system (Tanon, Shanghai, China) with enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (E411; Vazyme, Nanjing, China).

RT‐qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from lung cells or lung cancer tissue samples using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), and first‐strand cDNA synthesis was performed using a high‐volume RNA‐to‐cDNA kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). The ABI QuantStudio 5 (Applied Biosystems) system was used to measure the fluorescence intensity of the RT‐qPCR reactions using Power‐SYBR green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). The relative number of genes was counted by the 2−∆∆C method and was normalized to GAPDH. The corresponding primers are as follows: TGF‐β1, F: ACTGCAAGTGGACATCAACG, R: CAGAGCCCAGAGCCATTGTC; MSX1, F: GACATGACTTCTTTGCCACTCG, R: CAGGAGCGAAGGGGACACTTT; GAPDH, F: CTGGGCTACACTGAGCACC, R: AAGTGGTCGTTGAGGGCAATG.

HE and ROS detection

Mouse liver metastatic nodules were formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded, sectioned, and stained by HE. All images were captured using an Olympus microscope. ROS levels were assayed by immunofluorescence. A549 and H1299 cells were washed with cold PBS and incubated with the ROS probe, 5‐(and ‐6)‐chloromethyl‐2,7‐dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCHF‐DA; Sigma‐Aldrich), for 20 min at 37°C in the dark. After washing with PBS, ROS production was measured using an Olympus microscope.

Microscopy

Lung cancer cells were plated in 6‐well plates. After treatment of A549 and H1299 cells with different concentrations of neferine, stationary bright‐field images were taken using an Olympus microscope in a blinded experiment. Images were processed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Statistical analysis

Data were reported as mean ± standard deviation in at least three distinct studies. Student's t‐test was used to establish any variation between the two sets of groups. One‐way analysis of variance was used to compare the differences between groups. Comparisons were made using Spearman's correlation test; p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

RESULTS

Neferine induces pyroptosis to prevent NSCLC cell development and EMT in vitro

To investigate the effect of neferine on NSCLC cells, we selected two types of lung cancer cells, A549 and H1299, and then treated the cells with different concentration gradients of neferine to observe cell viability. The IC50 values of neferine in A549 and H1299 cells were 8.073 and 7.009 μM, respectively (Figure 1A). The effects of neferine at 2.5, 5, and 10 μM on the viability of normal lung epithelial cells were not significant, while their viability was inhibited at high concentrations of 20 μM (Figure S2A). Cell viability decreased with increasing neferine concentrations (Figure 1B). We subsequently treated lung cancer cells with 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 μM neferine to observe the migration and invasion ability of the cells, and noticed that cell migration and invasion weakened with increasing concentration of neferine (Figure 1C).

To explore the influence of neferine on the viability, migration, and invasion ability of lung cancer cells, we imaged cells treated with neferine and revealed that treatment with neferine along with the apoptosis inhibitor z‐VAD‐FMK, programmed necrosis inhibitor necrostatin‐1, autophagy inhibitor 3‐methyladenine, ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin‐1, and pyroptosis inhibitor disulfiram increased cellular activity only after the addition of the pyroptosis inhibitor, suggesting that neferine may affect cancer cell development through pyroptosis (Figure S2B). Thus, neferine concentrations higher than 5 μM caused pyroptosis in lung cancer cells (Figure 1D). The expression of pyroptosis‐related proteins (pro‐caspase‐1, cleaved caspase‐1, IL‐1β, IL‐18, GSDMD, and cleaved GSDMD) was determined by immunoblotting: the expression of pro‐caspase‐1 was obviously reduced, while the expression of cleaved caspase‐1, IL‐1β, and IL‐18 was remarkably enhanced (Figure 1E). In addition, the effect of neferine treatment on cellular EMT was evaluated by assessing changes in the expression of EMT‐related proteins (E‐cadherin, N‐cadherin, and vimentin); the hallmark of EMT is the upregulation of E‐cadherin, followed by the downregulation of N‐cadherin and vimentin (Figure 1F). Collectively, these in vitro results indicate that neferine diminishes the viability, migration, and invasion of lung cancer cells by stimulating pyroptosis, as well as by inhibiting EMT.

Neferine promotes pyroptosis in mice to suppress NSCLC development and metastasis

To further study the effectiveness of neferine on the tumorigenic ability of NSCLC cell‐transplanted mice, we selected 10, 20, and 30 mg/kg of neferine for intraperitoneal injection into immunodeficient mice transplanted with A549 cells and found that tumor growth was slower in mice after neferine treatment (Figure 2A). The size of the transplanted tumors was clearly smaller after neferine treatment, and the growth arrest effect of neferine at 30 mg/kg was particularly dramatic in the transplanted tumors (Figure 2B). Examination of liver metastatic nodule sections by HE staining revealed that neferine treatment resulted in a reduction in the number of liver metastatic nodules (Figure 2C). The above‐mentioned treatment of A549 cells with neferine in vitro led to the induction of pyroptosis, and in vivo, neferine therapy impeded the growth and aggravation of transplanted tumors by driving pyroptosis. Changes in the expression of pyroptosis‐related proteins were detected by western blotting, which was consistent with the in vitro experimental results. Pro‐caspase‐1 expression was obviously reduced, whereas the expression of cleaved caspase‐1, IL‐1β, IL‐18, GSDMD, and cleaved GSDMD was remarkably enhanced. This indicates that tumor‐cell pyroptosis increased after neferine therapy (Figure 2D). In addition, western blotting revealed that the expression of E‐cadherin, a marker of EMT in transplanted tumors, was upregulated; however, both N‐cadherin and Vimentin were downregulated, indicating that neferine treatment inhibited EMT in tumor cells (Figure 2E). In conclusion, neferine promotes pyroptosis in mice to inhibit the development and metastasis of NSCLC cells.

Neferine impacts NSCLC pyroptosis, progression, and EMT via downregulation of TGF‐β

A previous study has reported that TGF‐β is expressed at high levels in NSCLC. 20 TGF‐β can promote NSCLC cell invasion. 21 To study the effect of TGF‐β expression in neferine‐treated lung cancer cells, we measured changes in TGF‐β expression at the RNA and protein levels by RT‐qPCR and found that TGF‐β expression gradually decreased with increasing concentrations of neferine (Figure 3A). We successfully established TGF‐β‐overexpressing lung cancer cell lines (Figure S3A,B). After simultaneous treatment of lung cancer cells with neferine and overexpressed TGF‐β, we reversed not only the suppressive effect of neferine on TGF‐β, but also the influence of neferine on the viability of tumor cells (Figure 3B,C). The effect of TGF‐β overexpression on cell migration and invasion capacity after neferine treatment was examined by a Transwell assay, which showed that TGF‐β overexpression restored the inhibitory effect of neferine treatment on cell migration and invasion (Figure 3D). Furthermore, TGF‐β overexpression reinstated not only the facilitating role of neferine treatment on pyroptosis but also the repressive action of neferine treatment on EMT (Figure 3E,F). We also found that TGF‐β overexpression increased cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (Figure S3). Taken together, these results suggest that neferine affects pyroptosis and development in NSCLC cells by downregulating TGF‐β.

High TGF‐β expression and low MST1 expression in clinical tissues of lung cancer with reduced pyroptosis

To further explore the expression levels of TGF‐β and MST1, we collected 42 lung cancer tissues and paraneoplastic tissues and assayed the expression of TGF‐β and MST1 in these tissues using RT‐qPCR, which revealed high expression of TGF‐β and low expression of MST1 in lung cancer tissues compared to paraneoplastic tissues (Figure 4A). Correlation analysis of TGF‐β and MST1 expression in tumor tissues revealed that TGF‐β and MST1 expression were negatively correlated (Figure 4B). Western blotting of lung cancer tissue samples was performed to detect the expression of TGF‐β and MST1, which was consistent with the RT‐qPCR results. Furthermore, western blotting of lung cancer tissue samples to detect the expression of pyroptosis‐related proteins demonstrated that the pro‐caspase‐1 levels were remarkably reduced, while the levels of cleaved caspase‐1, IL‐1β, IL‐18, GSDMD, and cleaved GSDMD were obviously increased (Figure 4C). In short, high expression of TGF‐β and low expression of MST1 in clinical lung cancer tissues resulted in decreased pyroptosis.

MST1 overexpression mediates ROS‐induced pyroptosis leading to proliferation, metastasis, and blockage of EMT in NSCLC

Recent studies have indicated that MST1 is a putative tumor suppressor in various cancers. 25 , 26 Here, we revealed that MST1 is expressed at low levels in lung cancer tissues (Figure 4A). MST1 overexpression in A549 and H1299 cells significantly reduced cell viability (Figure 5A,B). Similarly, the impact of MST1 overexpression on the migration and invasion capacity of lung cancer cells was examined using a Transwell assay, which showed that MST1 overexpression in A549 and H1299 cells clearly reduced the migration and invasion potential of the cells (Figure 5C). Previous studies found that MST1 inhibited the progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma through ROS‐induced pyroptosis. 27 In our study on lung cancer cells, immunofluorescence was used to test ROS levels in cancer cells that overexpressed MST1, and it was found that MST1 was able to increase ROS levels in NSCLC cells (Figure 5D). Western blotting was used to detect the expression of pyroptosis‐related proteins after overexpression of MST1. The expression of proteins associated with enhanced pyroptosis was significantly upregulated in A549 and H1299 cells after MST1 overexpression (Figure 5E). In addition, western blotting was used to examine EMT changes in lung cancer cells after MST1 overexpression. The results showed that the expression of proteins associated with reduced EMT was significantly downregulated in A549 and H1299 cells after MST1 overexpression. MST1 overexpression further increased neferine‐induced pyroptosis and inhibited cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (Figure S4). This indicated that MST1 limits EMT in cancer cells (Figure 5F). In summary, MST1 overexpression mediates ROS‐induced pyroptosis in lung cancer cells, thereby inhibiting NSCLC proliferation, metastasis, and EMT.

Neferine promoted MST1 expression through downregulation of TGF‐β to promote NSCLC pyroptosis and arrest cell proliferation, metastasis, and EMT

To further study the role of neferine in the regulation between TGF‐β and MST1, we overexpressed both TGF‐β and MST1 in the context of neferine treatment in A549 and H1299 cells. RT‐qPCR showed that the neferine+oe‐TGF‐β + oe‐MST1 experimental group was able to reverse the changes in MST1 compared with the neferine +oe‐TGF‐β group (Figure 6A). Follow‐up results with CCK‐8 and Transwell assays showed that the neferine+oe‐TGF‐β + oe‐MST1 experimental group was able to restore the effect of neferine treatment only on the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of NSCLC cells (Figure 6B,C). Thus, neferine promotes MST1 expression in NSCLC cells by downregulating TGF‐β expression. Furthermore, immunofluorescence for ROS levels and immunoblotting for pyroptosis‐related proteins showed that TGF‐β overexpression reduced ROS levels and pyroptosis in neferine‐suppressed lung cancer cells, whereas MST1 overexpression reversed the effect of TGF‐β overexpression (Figure 6D,E). In addition, TGF‐β overexpression enhanced EMT in neferine‐suppressed lung cancer cells, whereas MST1 overexpression reversed this effect (Figure 6F). In general, neferine affects NSCLC pyroptosis as well as cell proliferation, metastasis, and EMT by regulating MST1 expression.

Neferine induces NSCLC pyroptosis and inhibits tumor growth and liver metastasis in mice in vivo through downregulation of TGF‐β expression

We overexpressed TGF‐β in neferine‐treated A549 cells, injected them into immunodeficient mice to form transplanted tumors, and observed the size of the tumors. The statistical results showed that overexpression of TGF‐β reversed the inhibitory effect of neferine on mouse lung cancer transplants (Figure 7A,B). TGF‐β was upregulated in neferine‐treated mouse tumor tissue samples after TGF‐β overexpression, as determined by immunoblotting, while MST1 expression was markedly downregulated, which was consistent with our in vitro results (Figure 7C). Examination of liver metastatic nodule sections from the liver tissue of an NSCLC metastasis tumor model by HE staining showed that TGF‐β overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of neferine treatment on metastatic liver nodules from mouse lung cancer (Figure 7D). Western blot detected changes in the expression of proteins associated with cell death, consistent with the results of the in vitro experiments. TGF‐β overexpression reversed the promoting effect of neferine on cell death in mouse tumors (Figure 7E). In addition, E‐cadherin was downregulated in mouse tumor tissue samples after TGF‐β overexpression and neferine treatment; however, N‐cadherin and vimentin were both upregulated, indicating that TGF‐β overexpression reversed the inhibitory effect of neferine treatment on EMT in mouse tumors (Figure 7F). Taken together, these results indicated that neferine affects NSCLC pyroptosis, cancer cell growth, and metastasis in mice by downregulating TGF‐β.

DISCUSSION

Lung cancer is the most common type of cancer and is a major contributor to cancer‐related deaths. Approximately 85% of patients with lung cancer have NSCLC. 1 , 2 As most patients are diagnosed at a late stage, which significantly affects survival and prognosis, chemotherapy continues to be the treatment of choice. The failure of anticancer therapy to induce apoptotic or non‐apoptotic cell death has also resulted in resistance to chemotherapy in NSCLC. 4 Therefore, apoptosis resistance is one of the main obstacles to chemotherapy failure. A potential solution to this issue has been suggested to be passing apoptotic signals to initiate cancer cell death. 5 Pyroptosis is a recently discovered nonapoptotic controlled cell‐killing mechanism with a distinct molecular profile. Apoptosis resistance can be overcome by switching from apoptosis to pyroptosis. 9 , 10 Pyroptosis in lung cancer can cause antitumor immune cell infiltration, thereby increasing cisplatin sensitivity in regressing NSCLC. 11 In this study, we demonstrated that neferine treatment in A549 and H1299 NSCLC cells stimulated pyroptosis and inhibited EMT, thereby reducing lung cancer cell viability, migration, and invasion. Moreover, treatment with neferine in a transplanted tumor model of A549 cells in mice arrested the development and metastasis of NSCLC cells by promoting pyroptosis.

We collected tissue samples from 42 patients with lung cancer and found that TGF‐β was significantly hyperexpressed in these cases, which is consistent with previous studies. 20 Neferine was found to reduce the development of fibrosis by downregulating TGF‐β in previous studies of liver fibrosis and lung fibrosis. 23 In the present study, neferine treatment of NSCLC significantly reduced TGF‐β expression. Inhibition of TGF‐β signaling was shown to accelerate pyroptosis in triple‐negative breast cancer. 22 Therefore, the repressive effect of neferine on TGF‐β expression may be responsible for the observed decrease in NSCLC cell proliferation, metastasis, and EMT. TGF‐β overexpression reversed neferine‐induced inhibition of EMT in murine lung cancer tumors. Furthermore, in a study on triple‐negative breast cancer, the suppression of TGF‐β signaling accelerated pyroptosis. 22 In hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, neferine modulates pyroptosis and ameliorates neuroinflammation and oxidative stress injury. 13 Here, we showed that ROS inhibitors increased cell viability after neferine treatment (Figure S1). As shown here, neferine‐induced pyroptosis in NSCLC cells is due to reduced expression of TGF‐β. TGF‐β overexpression was capable of restoring neferine‐induced cell death.

MST1 regulates the activity of the central Hippo pathway upstream. MST1 was initially identified as a proapoptotic cytoplasmic kinase required for enhancing apoptotic signaling. 24 MST1 is a potential tumor suppressor in several malignancies, according to recent studies. 25 , 26 Here, we found that MST1 is expressed at low levels in lung cancer tissues. MST1 inhibits the progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma via ROS‐induced pyroptosis. 27 In our study on lung carcinoma cells, MST1 increased ROS levels in NSCLC cells. Pyroptosis in lung cancer cells was enhanced by MST1 overexpression. Previous studies revealed that TGF‐β promotes the development of glial malignancies by reducing MST1 expression. 28 When NSCLC cells were treated with neferine, TGF‐β was downregulated and MST1 was upregulated, whereas MST1 expression was depressed after overexpression of TGF‐β, indicating that neferine regulates MST1 by downregulating TGF‐β to oppose the proliferation, metastasis, and EMT of NSCLC cells.

In summary, we provide the first evidence that neferine induces pyroptosis to prevent the proliferation, metastasis, and EMT of NSCLC cells, both in vitro and in mice. Neferine contributes to pyroptosis by regulating MST1 through the downregulation of TGF‐β to induce ROS formation. Therefore, our findings suggest that neferine can be used as an adjuvant therapy for patients with NSCLC to reduce tumor recurrence and prolong survival after treatment in lung cancer patients.
