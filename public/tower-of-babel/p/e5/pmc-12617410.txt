Development and Validation of Photonumeric Scales for Glabellar, Lateral Canthal, and Forehead Lines
Authors: Z Paul Lorenc, Stacy Smith, Lawrence S Bass, Jason D Bloom, Jeanine Downie, Derek Jones, Amir Moradi, Wooshun Lee, Seolhui Go
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUpper facial lines are early signs of aging that impact self-image, underscoring the need for objective, evidence-based tools to support the growing demand for aesthetic interventions.ObjectivesThe authors of this study aim to develop and validate 4-point photonumeric scales for clinical assessment of glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead lines at rest and dynamic expression, and evaluate their clinical relevance and alignment with patient-reported perceptions of wrinkle severity.MethodsSix 4-point photonumeric scales were developed to assess glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead lines at rest and during maximum expression. Validation was conducted by 5 board-certified physicians over 2 live rating sessions, spaced at least 2 weeks apart. Participants (n = 96-114 per scale) reflected a diverse range of ages, sexes, and Fitzpatrick skin types. Intrarater and interrater reliability were evaluated using weighted kappa and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), respectively. Correlation with participant self-assessments and the Facial Line Distress Scale (FINE) assessed patient alignment. Clinical relevance was confirmed through physician rating of image pairs with known score differences.ResultsAll scales demonstrated high intrarater (mean κ = 0.83-0.91) and interrater reliability (ICC = 0.94-0.98), indicating “almost-perfect” agreement. FINE scores showed a strong correlation with physician and participant scale ratings. Clinically meaningful 1-grade score differences were supported by nonoverlapping 95% CIs between image pairs rated as clinically different and not clinically different, with statistically significant mean differences exceeding 1 point.ConclusionsThe validated scales demonstrated high reliability, clinical relevance, and strong alignment with patient-reported outcomes, supporting their use as objective tools for clinical assessment.Level of Evidence: 5 (Therapeutic)

Among the most visible signs of aging are changes in the upper face, including the development of glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead lines.1 These lines are primarily caused by repetitive muscle contractions linked to common facial expressions such as frowning, smiling, and raising the eyebrows.1 Specifically, glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead lines correspond to the activity of the procerus and corrugator, orbicularis oculi, and frontalis muscles, respectively.1 Over time, dynamic lines that appear during movement can progress to static lines visible at rest.2 This transition reflects intrinsic aging processes, such as collagen depletion, muscle hyperactivity, and reduced skin elasticity, compounded by extrinsic factors, including sun exposure and smoking.3 Such facial changes can negatively affect perceived attractiveness, self-esteem, and overall well-being.4,5

As a result, demand for minimally invasive cosmetic procedures, such as botulinum toxin and dermal fillers, has grown significantly over the past 2 decades as patients seek to correct age-related facial changes.6 These aesthetic treatments are associated with improvements in patient-reported outcomes (PROs), including satisfaction with facial appearance, psychological well-being, and social functioning.7 Because rejuvenating procedures are becoming more common, there is a growing need for objective, validated scales to assess treatment outcomes and support evidence-based practice.8,9 In the absence of such tools, clinicians and patients must rely primarily on subjective impressions of treatment effectiveness.9

Photonumeric wrinkle assessment scales provide a standardized, objective approach to evaluate treatment outcomes and guide clinical decision making. Reliable scales must demonstrate consistent scoring across different users and time points, which can be evaluated using measurements of intrarater and interrater reliability.10-12 Intrarater reliability is typically assessed using Cohen's weighted kappa coefficient, which ranges from 0 (slight agreement) to 1 (almost-perfect agreement).13 Interrater reliability is most often evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which, like kappa, ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values reflecting stronger consistency among raters.

To support objective evaluation of aesthetic treatments and promote consistency in clinical decision making, we developed and validated six 4-point photonumeric scales for clinical use to assess glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead lines at rest and during corresponding maximum expression. The scales were also evaluated for clinical relevance and alignment with patient-reported wrinkle severity to support their utility in real-world practice. Publication of these scales supports transparency in clinical research and facilitates standardized use in regulatory and clinical settings.

METHODS

The Glabellar Lines Scale, Lateral Canthal Lines Scale, and Forehead Lines Scale are 4-point photonumeric tools designed to assess the severity of corresponding facial lines at rest and during maximum contraction (dynamic). Approximately 200 adult participants with healthy facial skin and a BMI below 35 volunteered and provided informed consent to be photographed. The cohort was selected to ensure representation across all Fitzpatrick skin types and both sexes. Exclusion criteria included recent cosmetic procedures, previous facial surgery, or any features that could interfere with visual assessment or make the participant easily recognizable, such as permanent fillers, tattoos, or facial piercings. Standardized, full-face, 2-dimensional images were captured using a standardized imaging system (VISIA-CR Gen 5; Canfield Scientific, Inc, Parsippany, NJ). All images were reviewed for technical quality and cropped to focus on the specific area of interest (AOI) before inclusion in the photographic database used for scale development and validation. The scale name reflects the developer and is not intended for product promotion. All participants provided written informed consent for the capture and use of their images. Procedures were conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. Because this study involved collecting Facial Line Distress Scale (FINE) questionnaires from participants, IRB approval was obtained from Salus IRB.

Scale Development

In collaboration with the Canfield project team, participant images representing each grade of glabellar lines, lateral canthal lines, and forehead lines at maximum expression (dynamic) and at rest were reviewed by 2 scale developers (board-certified dermatologists or facial plastic surgeons). Images were selected to ensure diversity in sex and Fitzpatrick skin types across all grades.

The scale developers drafted grade-specific descriptors for each of the 4 grades. These preliminary descriptors were reviewed and discussed by the sponsor and scale developers. Final grade definitions were established through consensus.

To support the development of the photonumeric grading scales, an AOI diagram was created using sponsor-provided anatomical landmarks to delineate the glabellar line, lateral canthal line, and forehead line regions. Before scale development, scale developers conducted an independent, web-based review of captured images for each indication, using a 4-point grading system. These evaluations informed the selection of representative real-world images that demonstrated clear distinctions between grades and helped guide the creation of scale descriptors.

Base images for each scale were selected by the scale developers and subsequently morphed by Canfield graphics technicians to visually match the defined descriptors for each grade. This iterative process ensured alignment between morphs and verbal descriptors. Final scales for each of the 3 facial line areas (glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead) were developed separately for at rest and maximum expression. Each scale included 2 real-world participant images per grade, selected to reflect diversity in sex and Fitzpatrick skin types, along with grade-specific descriptors, AOI diagrams, and 1 set of morphed images representing all grades (Figures 1-6).

Scale Validation

A subset of previously photographed participants meeting specific eligibility criteria was invited to participate in live validation sessions. Inclusion required adults able to follow study instructions and provide informed consent, whereas exclusions included facial asymmetry, recent or planned aesthetic procedures, and features that could interfere with visual assessment. Sample size for the live validation was determined based on interrater reliability using Bonett's method.14 To achieve a 95% CI width of 0.2 with 5 raters and an ICC of 0.5, 88 participants were needed, and 92 were enrolled (to account for attrition) with approximately equal numbers of each grade included.

To evaluate the interrater and intrarater reliability of the final scales, a live-participant validation study was conducted with 5 physician raters experienced in using aesthetic photonumeric scales. These physicians were not involved in scale development and participated in 2 live validation sessions held at least 2 weeks apart. Before the first session, all raters received standardized training on scale use. During each session, participants were independently evaluated by each physician using printed copies of the scales, and scores were entered into an electronic data capture system. Raters assessed participants individually and were not permitted to discuss their evaluations with each other or with participants. Participants were rerandomized to a different grading order for the second session.

As part of the live validation process, participants completed self-assessments of their facial line severity using the same photonumeric scale as the physicians. These self-ratings were conducted across 2 sessions held at least 2 weeks apart. In addition to grading their lines, participants also completed the FINE (FINE-GL, FINE-LCL, and FINE-FH), a 5-point Likert scale PRO measure developed according to FDA guidance.15 The scale assesses the perceived impact and aesthetic concerns related to glabellar lines, lateral canthal lines, and forehead lines, capturing both emotional and behavioral aspects of facial line distress. Participants completed the questionnaire independently in a quiet setting.

Clinical Significance Determination

To assess the clinical significance of a 1-grade difference on each photonumeric scale, a web-based image review was conducted by 3 independent experts. Multiple image pairs were selected to reflect a range of severity differences, from no difference to a 3-grade difference. Experts were asked to evaluate each pair and determine whether the difference was clinically meaningful (categorized as “Clinically different” or “Not clinically different”). Following this assessment, all reviewed images were randomly sequenced using a standardized randomization program. Each expert then independently assigned a numeric score to each image using the corresponding photonumeric scale, enabling evaluation of clinically distinguishable differences across paired images.

Data Analysis and Statistical Methods

Statistical analyses included descriptive summaries for continuous variables using mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, and maximum values, and for categorical variables by number and percentage of participants.

To evaluate the clinical relevance of the scale grades, absolute score differences between image pairs classified as “clinically different” or “not clinically different” were summarized using descriptive statistics and 95% CIs. Intrarater reliability was assessed by comparing ratings from Sessions 1 and 2 using weighted kappa statistics (Fleiss–Cohen weights).13 Kappa values above 0.6 were considered acceptable, with 0.61 to 0.80 indicating substantial agreement and 0.81 to 1.00 indicating almost-perfect agreement. Interrater agreement was evaluated by calculating the ICCs and corresponding 95% CIs based on the Shrout and Fleiss method.16

Agreement between physician raters and participant self-assessments was measured using kappa and weighted kappa statistics (Cicchetti–Allison weights), with interpretive thresholds of <0.4 (poor), 0.4 to 0.75 (fair to good), and >0.75 (excellent).17 To address potential directional bias in ordinal data, symptom grades were also dichotomized (eg, serious vs nonserious) to support interpretation and reduce the impact of asymmetry in scoring.

Additionally, to explore correlations between FINE scores and scale grades, Jonckheere–Terpstra tests were used to assess for ordered differences across the 4-point scale.18

Intrarater Reliability

Intrarater reliability was high across all 3 facial line scales (Table 2). For the Glabellar Lines Scale, mean kappa values demonstrated almost-perfect agreement both at rest (κ = 0.91; 95% CI, 0.88-0.94) and during maximum frown (κ = 0.83; 95% CI, 0.80-0.86). Similarly, the Lateral Canthal Lines Scale showed almost-perfect agreement at rest for both the right and left sides (mean κ = 0.90; 95% CI, 0.88-0.92 and 0.90; 95% CI, 0.87-0.92, respectively), as well as during maximum smiling (κ = 0.85 for both sides; 95% CI, 0.82-0.88). For the Forehead Lines Scale, mean kappa values also indicated almost-perfect agreement at rest (κ = 0.89; 95% CI, 0.86-0.91) and during maximum eyebrow elevation (κ = 0.87; 95% CI, 0.84-0.90). Results from individual physician raters are presented in Table 2, with all values reflecting substantial to almost-perfect agreement across facial regions and expressions. These findings suggest strong consistency in ratings by individual physicians across all 3 scales.

Intrarater Reliability of Line Scales

Interrater Reliability

Interrater reliability was assessed using ICCs among 5 physician raters across 2 live validation sessions. Agreement was consistently high across all scales and conditions. For the Glabellar Lines Scale, ICCs indicated almost-perfect agreement at rest and during maximum frown, ranging from 0.94 to 0.98 across sessions (Table 3). For the Lateral Canthal Lines Scale, interrater agreement was also almost perfect for both the right and left sides. At rest, ICCs were 0.97 across both sessions, whereas during maximum smile, values ranged from 0.95 to 0.97. For the Forehead Lines Scale, ICCs were similarly high, with 0.97 at rest and 0.96 to 0.97 during maximum eyebrow elevation in both sessions (Table 3). Thus, physician assessments using all 3 scales demonstrated consistency across multiple raters and time points.

Correlation Between Physician Raters’ Assessment and Participants’ Self-Assessment

Agreement between physician raters and participant self-assessments was high across all scales and facial expressions (Table 4). For the Glabellar Lines Scale, kappa values indicated almost-perfect agreement at rest (κ = 0.86, 95% CI, 0.83-0.90) and substantial agreement during maximum frown (κ = 0.74, 95% CI, 0.68-0.81) in both sessions. For the Lateral Canthal Lines Scale, agreement at rest and during maximum smile was substantial, with kappa values ranging from 0.77 to 0.81 for both sides across sessions. For the Forehead Lines Scale, agreement at rest was almost perfect (κ = 0.85-0.86), whereas during maximum eyebrow elevation, agreement remained substantial (κ = 0.72-0.78) across sessions (Table 4). The results indicate consistent alignment between clinician assessments and patient self-ratings across all 3 scales.

Clinical Significance Determination by Physician Raters

Clinical significance was assessed by comparing mean score differences between image pairs that physician raters deemed clinically different vs those considered not clinically different. For glabellar lines, lateral canthal lines, and forehead lines, mean score differences were markedly higher in pairs deemed clinically different (Table 6). At rest, mean differences ranged from 1.47 to 1.71 for clinically different pairs, compared with 0.20 to 0.28 for nondifferent pairs (Table 6). During maximum facial expression (frown for glabellar lines, smile for lateral canthal lines, or eyebrow elevation for forehead lines), mean differences for clinically different pairs ranged from 1.48 to 1.75, vs 0.17 to 0.30 for nondifferent pairs. Across photo pair selections, the 95% CIs for clinically different image pairs did not overlap with those of the nondifferent pairs, supporting the conclusion that a 1-point difference on the scale is clinically meaningful (Table 6). Additionally, the 95% CIs for the mean difference between the 2 groups were consistently >1 point and statistically significant, reinforcing the clinical relevance of a 1-grade change on the scale. These findings support the scale's ability to distinguish meaningful differences in facial line severity.

DISCUSSION

The Medytox Glabellar Line, Lateral Canthal Line, and Forehead Line Scales demonstrated high reliability and strong alignment with patient-reported perceptions of facial line severity. Intrarater and interrater reliability were consistently high across all assessed facial regions and expression states, with kappa values and ICCs reflecting substantial to almost-perfect agreement. These results support the consistency and reproducibility of the scales across raters and time points, reinforcing their utility in both clinical and research settings.

Beyond rater reliability, strong agreement between physician ratings and participant self-assessments, along with high correlations between scale ratings and FINE scores, further support the clinical relevance of these tools. The FINE PRO measure, developed in accordance with FDA guidance, was recently created to assess glabellar line severity and distress, demonstrating high reliability and validity.19 In our study, FINE scores correlated closely with both physician and participant ratings across glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead lines, suggesting that the scales reflect patient perceptions. Notably, the “appraisal of the line” subdomain showed particularly strong correlation, indicating the scales capture wrinkle severity in ways that are meaningful to patients. Additionally, statistically significant score differences between image pairs deemed clinically different vs not clinically different confirm that the scales are sensitive to perceptible changes in clinical practice and support that a 1-point difference on the scale is clinically meaningful.

Together, these findings indicate the Medytox validated scales are reliable tools that reflect patient perspectives and are relevant for real-world use, supporting standardized assessment in aesthetic dermatology. By offering an objective method for evaluating dynamic and static facial lines, the scales may also enhance patient communication and satisfaction by aligning clinical assessments with individual perceptions and treatment goals. Such systems are critical for evaluating treatment outcomes in real-world practice, where objective grading tools help establish baselines and treatment goals, enhance patient understanding, and support realistic expectations for nonsurgical aesthetic procedures.20

Although several validated scales for assessing facial lines, including glabellar, lateral canthal, and forehead lines, have been developed, the photonumeric scales presented here offer key advancements that address limitations of earlier tools.9-12,21-24 Early foundational work used basic validation methods, and later efforts, such as the Merz 5-point scales and the Allergan Forehead Lines Scale, introduced standardized photo-based ratings with descriptive anchors and statistical validation using interrater metrics like ICCs.9,10,21,22 More recent lateral canthal and forehead line scales, developed for both static and dynamic states, have further advanced the field by incorporating diverse images representing various ages, sexes, and Fitzpatrick skin types, thereby improving their generalizability across patient populations.11,12,23

The Medytox Glabellar Line, Lateral Canthal Line, and Forehead Line Scales complement existing validated tools by providing a standardized, photonumeric set for all 3 facial regions, developed from a single image library with consistent methods to support broad utilization across products and clinical settings. The scales reflect a diverse range of real-world participants and feature both rest and maximum expression states, adding further value by incorporating grade-specific descriptors, AOI diagrams, and clear, well-composed images that effectively highlight the facial lines being evaluated. Together, these enhancements support accurate, reproducible wrinkle severity assessments and promote applicability in real-world aesthetic practice. Furthermore, their alignment with patient perception and ability to detect clinically meaningful differences between image pairs reinforce their clinical relevance. Importantly, this validation includes 5, rather than 3, raters as well as more rigorous statistical testing. The scales will be used in future botulinum toxin research conducted by the study sponsor.

Limitations

As with any study, it is important to consider several limitations. Although participants were selected to reflect a range of Fitzpatrick skin types, ages, and sexes, the final sample was still predominantly composed of White females, which may limit generalizability to broader populations. Furthermore, assessments were conducted at a single site under controlled conditions, which may not reflect real-world variability. Finally, although intrarater and interrater reliability was high, physician assessments remain inherently subjective, and variability may increase among less experienced users or in different practice settings. Despite these limitations, the consistency of the findings and the strong alignment with patient perspectives support the relevance and robustness of the scales. In addition, the strong correlation of participant and grader scoring is important, particularly as both static and dynamic assessments were included, the latter of which can be challenging for patients. This agreement is central to positive outcomes and patient satisfaction in clinical practice, making it an important aspect of these findings.

CONCLUSIONS

The Medytox Glabellar Line, Lateral Canthal Line, and Forehead Line Scales demonstrated high reliability, clinical relevance, and strong alignment with FINE PROs. These validated tools provide a standardized and objective approach for assessing dynamic and static facial lines, supporting their utility in both clinical practice and aesthetic research. By incorporating diverse, representative images and aligning with patient perspectives, the scales offer a real-world tool for evaluating outcomes and helping patients understand their treatment journey.
