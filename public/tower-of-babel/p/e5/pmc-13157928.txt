Assessing the efficacy and safety of transcranial magnetic stimulation and vagus nerve stimulation for drug-resistant epilepsy: A systematic review
Authors: Dhvanit Rajdeep, Ahmed Saleh Ahmed Abdelkader, Hashim Mohamed Siraj, Farhad Islam, Vedika Saxena, Nirjhar Yaduvanshi, Shabbeer Imtiaz Ahamed, Ahmed Usama Rizvi, Shreya Vassan, Nikhila Liz Aby, Syed Khaja Ayaanuddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), characterized by inadequate seizure control despite adequate trials of multiple antiepileptic medications, affects a substantial proportion of the global epilepsy population. Recent advances in neuromodulation techniques have demonstrated promise as adjunctive therapeutic approaches for managing treatment-resistant epilepsy. Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) represent emerging interventions for patients who fail conventional pharmacological management.Methods: A comprehensive search was performed for multiple databases such as PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Search was done for studies from 1995 to 2024, which will meet our inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria. We selected 36 studies that include randomized controlled trials, observational studies, and retrospective analyses. Our primary outcome is seizure recurrence, and the secondary outcomes are adverse events. Our systematic review is registered in PROSPERO, and its ID is CRD42025629381.Results: Approximately 47% of patients treated with either TMS or VNS were found to have a mean seizure reduction of over 50%, which also contributed to the results of our data interpretation. Even in several institutional studies, it was additionally found that more than 60% of patients showed significant improvements in seizure control.Conclusion: Evidence reported that both TMS and VNS greatly improve seizure control in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, potentially enhancing quality of life and reducing seizure frequency. A large amount of our findings support the integration of neuromodulation techniques into future treatment regimens for drug-resistant epilepsy, which might be accompanied by calls for future clinical trials.

INTRODUCTION

Clinically, epilepsy is defined by the occurrence of two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart, or a single unprovoked seizure with a documented recurrence risk of at least 60% within the next 10 years, or by identification of an epilepsy syndrome.[19] Neurologically, individuals with epilepsy are predisposed to spontaneous seizures, often accompanied by substantial neurological, cognitive, and psychosocial consequences. Globally, approximately 50 million people have epilepsy, with the vast majority residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where healthcare resources are severely limited.[28] Despite the availability of cost-effective first-line antiepileptic drug (AED) treatments, a persistent treatment gap remains in both high-income and resource-limited settings.[16,28] In India, for example, an estimated 6-10 million individuals live with active epilepsy, yet less than half receive appropriate and sufficient treatment.[28] Among those treated with standard antiepileptic drug interventions, approximately 20-40% do not achieve adequate seizure control, developing drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), a treatment-resistant state analogous to other neuropsychiatric conditions.[27] Novel therapeutic approaches for managing DRE have emerged, including advanced neuromodulation techniques such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), along with artificial intelligence-based algorithms for seizure prediction and forecasting to improve clinical outcomes.[27,31] These innovative interventions represent the frontier of epilepsy management in treatment-resistant populations.[16,19,28]

In recent times, neuromodulation has become a tool of use as an adjunct to other standard approaches, which have shown promise for patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE). In studies, it was reported that non-invasive transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) uses magnetic pulses which are targeted toward specific brain regions to influence neuronal activity and decrease seizure frequency.[10] Now, clinically vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) has been used as an adjunct, and the way it works is that it involves the surgical implantation of a device to stimulate the vagus nerve, which modulates brain function to help control seizures. Now both of the neuromodulation techniques work through different mechanisms, TMS mainly regulates our cortical excitability and neuroplasticity, while VNS works by affecting our broader neural networks, which are involved in generating seizures. Even after some research on both the treatment modalities to consider them as additional treatment options for DRE as they have gathered significant interest in recent times, a comparison of both modalities qualitatively is still not explored.[31]

This systematic review is based on examining the safety and efficacy of TMS and VNS as treatment options for DRE. Our primary objective is to qualitatively examine outcomes such as seizure frequency reduction and the incidence of adverse events associated with both of these interventions. Through gathering data across diverse patient populations, which includes both pediatric and adult samples of populations, this review aims to focus on providing a qualitative comparison of these neuromodulatory approaches for different clinical settings, which might give us qualitative information to improve clinical outcomes and help in identifying new areas of research for the management of DRE.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Eligibility criteria

We selected studies that examined the efficacy and safety of TMS and VNS in patients with DRE. Studies that were eligible for our systematic review were randomized controlled trial (RCTs), observational studies, and retrospective analyses published between 1995 and 2024. Population samples which were eligible for inclusion in our study were individuals diagnosed with epilepsy, specifically those with drug-resistant or refractory forms and without restrictions regarding treatment duration, frequency, intensity, and other intervention protocols. Exclusion criteria which we used for excluding studies included those studies that did not report on seizure reduction outcomes and which were presented as theses or letters to the editor. Our systematic review is registered in PROSPERO, and its PROSPERO ID is CRD42025629381.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was performed by our team for multiple databases such as PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Search was done for studies from 1995 to 2024, which will also meet our inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria. The search strategy combined keywords and medical subject headings including, “Vagus Nerve Stimulation,” “Drug-Resistant Epilepsy,” “Medication Resistant Epilepsy,” “Drug Refractory Epilepsy,” “Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation,” “Efficacy,” and “Safety.” The search approach used a variety of combinations of these terms (e.g., “Vagus Nerve Stimulation AND Epilepsy AND Efficacy”). Boolean operators such as “AND” were used to find the publications that met our inclusion criteria.

Study selection

Two to three independent reviewers participated in the selection process, evaluating the search results to ascertain the study’s eligibility. In the beginning, titles and abstracts were screened to check if they met our inclusion criteria, which was then followed by a full-text review. Disagreements were settled through discussion after each reviewer evaluated the documents on their own. Letters to the editor and research not related to our topic of interest and which did not include relevant outcomes of the study were excluded. Studies that met the inclusion criteria based on the defined parameters of TMS, VNS, and DRE were included. This thorough screening ensured that only studies that met our inclusion criteria were included in our study.

Data extraction

Data extraction was carried out independently by two reviewers, separately extracting relevant information from each included study. To guarantee correctness and comprehensiveness, cross-checking was used during the data collection process. The extracted data included study characteristics (e.g., study design, sample size, and participant demographics), intervention details (such as TMS settings and VNS parameters), and outcomes such as seizure frequency reduction and adverse events. We had also gathered information regarding the country of study, and reported treatment response rates were also collected. Any incomplete, confusing, and lacking in evidence-based information was exempted from the study

Risk of bias (RoB) assessment

The quality and RoB of the included studies were independently evaluated by two reviewers using established tools. For cohort and case-control studies, the Newcastle-Ottawa scale (NOS) was utilized, while randomized clinical trials were assessed with the RoB tool. Disagreements were settled through discussion after each reviewer evaluated the documents on their own. We wanted to check study quality rigorously, which might support the reliability of the gathered evidence regarding the safety and efficacy of TMS and VNS in managing DRE in our study.

RESULTS

Preliminary scans in numerous databases yielded 1276 articles. 568 studies were evaluated from this group after removing duplicates. After considering the title and abstract, 472 articles were eliminated, leaving 96 papers that could be regarded as plausible for this Systematic Review after passing the article review. Based on a thorough review and inclusion criteria, 36 articles totaling 1676 patients were eventually included in this Systematic Review. Outcomes of VNS

During our assessment of each study for the outcomes of seizure reduction and adverse effects for the management of epilepsy with the help of VNS, we found a great deal of evidence from several studies showed that VNS is effective in reducing seizure frequency in refractory epilepsy among diverse patient populations, including adults and pediatric groups. Moreover, from the included studies, it was found that median seizure frequency reductions usually ranged from 34% to 55% at diverse follow-up periods (3 months–12 months). Furthermore, mainly responder rates, which can be defined as accomplishing ≥50% seizure reduction, usually ranged from 35% to 77% among studies.[1,3,4,6,8,11,13,17,18,22,23,26,30,31,35,39-42] In addition, we found that most often reported adverse effects among many studies were transient and involved voice changes (57.9%), hoarseness, throat discomfort, coughing (37.9%), paresthesias, and mild gastrointestinal symptoms;[4,8,11,13,14,22,23,32,35,39,40] generally, these adverse events were endurable. Importantly, many studies have found improvements in quality of life and alertness, mainly in Landau-Kleffner syndrome and pediatric groups with autism. Outcomes of TMS

During our assessment of TMS Studies, it was found that TMS had demonstrated more heterogeneous outcomes with substantial instability, which was based mainly on parameters such as patient populations, coil configurations, and stimulation variables. Importantly, it was reported that the outcome of seizure reduction rates ranged from 4.5% to 79.8% among many studies. Moreover, few studies had found their results to have non-significant effects (P > 0.05); in contrast, the other studies showed statistically significant reductions.[7,15,20,21,25,34,37,38] Moreover, it was reported that low-intensity protocols (2.3% reduction, 20% resting motor threshold) showed significantly inferior efficacy in comparison to high-intensity stimulation protocols (90% resting Motor Threshold [rMT]).[36] Furthermore, a few low-frequency repetitive TMS (0.3–1.0 Hz) demonstrated irregular but commonly positive effects such as during the reporting of results in a few studies, it was found that, in them, the seizure reduction rate had ranged from 19 > 1% to 72% usually determined by target locations and treatment periods.[7,21,25] Certainly, studies assessing TMS in patient populations who have cortical malformations showed significant reductions in both seizure frequency (51.2–57.3%) and epileptiform discharges (42.1–46.4%).[20] Certain neurophysiological studies reported that TMS can produce remarkable elevation in short-latency intracortical inhibition (P = 0.016), which usually indicates potential mechanistic pathways for seizure control.[15] Mainly, it was found that, in particular, for crossover studies comparing different coil configurations, they had significantly diverse individual patient responses.[34] Furthermore, the adverse effects that were usually associated with TMS are mild and include headaches, mild discomfort, and transient tinnitus.[21,34,37,38]

In very few studies, it was found that patients had shown an increase in transient seizures or seizure frequency following stimulation.[21,34] More detailed study characteristics, involving sample sizes, intervention parameters, seizure types, and outcomes for the intervention VNS are presented in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

A systematic review was performed, which synthesized data from 36 studies that examined the efficacy and safety of TMS and VNS in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy.

A great piece of evidence from VNS studies showed a strong therapeutic effect across diverse populations. In a 12-month prospective study involving 195 patients, it was clearly shown that their outcome comprised median seizure frequency reductions of 34% at 3 months and 45% at 12 months, with 35% of patients achieving over a 50% reduction and 20% attaining greater than a 75% reduction;[13] now, this development from 3 to 12 months indicates a accumulated therapeutic effect which usually would reflect the progressive neuroplastic variations in vagal nerve adjustments and thalamocortical circuits. Moreover, objectively, it indicates that maximal benefit might not be found in early treatment phases, as this time-dependent efficacy demonstrates the need for the practice of long trial periods (at least 6–12 months) before determining VNS therapy inefficient. During retrospective analysis of 154 patients, it was even further amplified by these findings, which showed significant modifications to stimulation parameters (P < 0.0001) alongside only some mild adverse effects which were hoarseness and paresthesias,[14] In particular, shorter off-times ≤1.1 min should be considered as variable for identification of optimal stimulation due to its importance in individualized programming to optimizing efficacy along with sustaining endurability, which n might also indicate that structed variable refinement can improve response rates during follow-up for initially non-responding patients. In the pediatric population, a large retrospective study of 125 patients reported average seizure reductions of 36.1% at 3 months and 44.7% at 6 months, alongside stimulation-induced voice alteration (57.9%) and coughing (37.9%).[22] While VNS demonstrates parallel effectiveness across diverse age groups, the elevated incidence of voice-related adverse effects in children necessitates thorough parental counseling prior to implantation. Similar cumulative efficacy and tolerability are observed in adult cohorts, including those with pharmacoresistant generalized epilepsy.[23] Furthermore, the improvement for 3–6 months can also be found in adults, showing and proving that similar protocols are effective. Adult cohorts exhibit similar improvements from 3 to 6 months, validating the efficacy of these protocols and showing mild, transient side effects.[40] In pediatric populations, VNS adverse events remained predominantly mild and transient and correlated with an improved quality of life.[4,8,11,32,35] VNS’s promising safety profile and its largely stimulation-related adverse effects distinguish it from systemic antiepileptic drugs, which frequently cause weight gain, cognitive slowing, and metabolic issues. Moreover, the improvement shown in quality of life does broaden beyond seizure reduction, even possibly demonstrating the other benefits such as elevated alertness and mood through modulation of the vagus nerve through noradrenergic and serotonergic systems.

For TMS studies, we found very contrasting evidence that showed that they have more heterogeneous outcomes due to the influence of variations in stimulation protocols and study designs. One randomized double-blind trial of 24 patients with localization-related epilepsy reported a mean seizure reduction of 4.5 ± 13% over an eight-week follow-up in the active TMS group, compared to a 0.4 ± 20% increase in the placebo group (P = 0.11).[38] This non-significant result and small effect size likely stem from the extremely short treatment duration (only 1 week of stimulation) and limited coil penetration depth. Unlike the continuous neuromodulation of VNS, short TMS courses provide only transient cortical inhibition, highlighting the need for more intensive and prolonged protocols to achieve sustained antiepileptic effects. Conversely, an open-label study of eight patients with drug-resistant epilepsy secondary to malformations of cortical development (MCD) demonstrated that a single session of low-frequency rTMS yielded significant reductions in epileptiform discharges of 46.4% at 15 days and 42.1% at 30 days. This electrophysiological improvement was accompanied by clinical seizure frequency declines of 57.3% and 51.2%, respectively.[20] This parallel reduction validates the neurophysiological mechanism of TMS, which likely operates by reducing cortical hyperexcitability within the malformed tissue. Moreover, the efficient MCD patients in particular are very significant given the contrasting nature due to the structural abnormalities which may stop those intrinsically epileptogenic cortices, indicating that TMS might be able to directly modulate the zone which generated the seizure if properly targeted. Other pilot studies reported varied clinical effects, including a 22.8% seizure reduction in a five-patient cohort (P < 0.01)[7] and an overall, non-significant 19.1% reduction in a cohort of seven patients with extratemporal lobe epilepsy.[25] Furthermore, separate literature evaluating short-latency intracortical inhibition supports the mechanistic understanding of TMS, reflecting increased GABA-A receptor-mediated inhibitory neurotransmission within cortical circuits. Furthermore, this biomarker of physiological nature does show the potential to serve as a predictive marker for TMS responsiveness and giving chance to clinicians to find the patient population for optimizing individualized patient treatments. A subsequent randomized, sham-controlled trial corroborated these MCD findings, reporting robust seizure reductions of 72%, 53% (p = 0.002), and 58% (p = 0.001) in the active rTMS group at weeks 2, 4, and 8, respectively.[37] Furthermore, a controlled trial evaluating stimulation parameters in refractory partial epilepsy achieved a 79.8% seizure reduction utilizing high-intensity stimulation (90% of resting motor threshold) compared to only a 2.3% reduction with low-intensity stimulation [25]. This superior efficacy of high-intensity stimulation highlights a critical dose-response relationship, which may explain the heterogeneous results across the broader TMS literature. However, clinicians must balance treatment intensity against the increased risk of adverse effects, including seizure exacerbation in susceptible individuals. Conversely, a randomized, sham-controlled crossover trial evaluating neuronavigated rTMS in refractory neocortical epilepsy found no overall seizure reduction and no significant efficacy difference between figure-8 and round coils.[34] Individual responses varied drastically; while one patient achieved an 18% seizure reduction with the figure-8 coil and a 48% reduction with the round coil, another patient suffered an acute fourfold seizure exacerbation during treatment. Furthermore, one patient experienced a severe rebound in seizure frequency accompanied by intense headaches following an initial post-treatment reduction. This profound interindividual variability, coupled with the risk of paradoxical seizure exacerbation, necessitates rigorous patient monitoring and questions the broad applicability of current rTMS protocols.

Direct comparison reveals substantial disparities in safety profiles and clinical efficacy between these modalities. While TMS yields heterogeneous outcomes, ranging from a non-significant 4.5% reduction[38] to significant decreases under high-intensity protocols,[37] its episodic nature often produces only transient inhibition. Conversely, VNS achieves robust and persistent seizure control, with responder rates between 35% and 77%,[13,42] reflecting its chronic neuromodulatory influence on thalamocortical circuits. The inferiority in TMS might be due to its mechanism having more focal and transient cortical effects, while the VNS uses the mechanism of bilateral thalamic activation and extensive cortical effects by mounting vagal extrapolations to the nucleus tractus solitarius and respective monoaminergic pathways. Methodological disparities between these modalities complicate direct comparisons. TMS research typically relies on small pilot investigations of 5 to 24 patients with limited follow-up periods ranging from one to twelve weeks.[7,21,25,38] Conversely, VNS investigators utilize larger cohorts of 60 to 195 patients and extended observation periods exceeding 12 months.[13,14,39] These longitudinal VNS designs allow for the detection of cumulative therapeutic benefits that the shorter duration of current TMS trials may fail to capture. A major distinguishing factor between the two neuromodulation approaches is their level of methodological standardization. TMS currently lacks methodological standardization, as investigators continue to debate optimal treatment parameters. These include stimulation intensities ranging from 20% to 90% of the motor threshold,[21,25] the efficacy of figure-8 versus round coils,[7] and the selection of frequencies between 0.3 and 1.0 Hz.[7,25] In contrast, VNS represents a mature therapeutic modality supported by decades of clinical refinement. Its FDA-approved devices utilize standardized titration protocols that effectively reduce seizure frequency[13,39] and modulate interictal epileptiform activity,[40] benefits validated across diverse adult and pediatric populations.[14] Practical considerations further differentiate these modalities regarding clinical implementation and patient burden. While noninvasive, TMS necessitates intensive and supervised therapy, often requiring twice-weekly sessions for several months, which may restrict geographic accessibility and compromise long-term compliance.[7] Conversely, VNS requires an initial surgical implantation but subsequently delivers passive, continuous neuromodulation that does not rely on daily patient adherence or frequent clinical visits.[13,14] Adverse event profiles also exhibit distinct clinical characteristics. TMS primarily associates with localized headaches and, in rare instances, transient seizure exacerbation.[34,37] In contrast, VNS generates predictable, stimulus-dependent side effects, including hoarseness, coughing, and throat discomfort.[8,22] These VNS effects are generally well-tolerated and lack the systemic metabolic or cognitive complications associated with conventional antiepileptic drugs, such as weight gain or cognitive slowing.[14,39] TMS and VNS serve as complementary rather than competing neuromodulatory strategies. Clinicians typically utilize VNS for patients with multifocal or generalized epilepsy, as well as comorbid developmental conditions, who are ineligible for resective surgery.[3,30] VNS provides cumulative, long-term seizure suppression and may even serve as a bridge to localize foci for future resections in complex pathologies like tuberous sclerosis.[18] Conversely, focal rTMS offers a non-invasive alternative for patients with well-defined neocortical malformations, effectively reducing clinical seizures and interictal discharges where precise targeting is possible.[20] Integrating these modalities into a staged treatment algorithm could optimize outcomes for partial responders, though this synergistic approach requires further investigation.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged while taking into account the outcomes discussed in our study. The substantial variability in study design, patient populations, and methodologies across the 36 included studies prevented the synthesis of results in a meta-analysis. Heterogeneity might have contributed to the observed variation in outcomes, especially in TMS studies. In addition, potential biases in observational studies cannot be entirely ruled out, and publication bias may exist.

CONCLUSION

Due to findings from our study, we can conclude that future research should prioritize head-to-head RCTs that compare VNS and TMS in similar patient populations with standard measures, along with developing predictive biomarkers such as baseline intracortical inhibition measures to give individualized patient treatments and optimize the use of each modality. Combination approaches should also be examined, VNS plus TMS, which can accomplish synergistic effects, and finally, the cost-effective analysis of both modalities for the optimization of seizure-related healthcare should be considered as a variable to improve the quality of life, and long-term follow-ups for TMS should be considered to improve the evidence-based approach for its use for a long period of time. Ultimately, new and emerging technologies such as transcutaneous VNS and neuronavigated TMS, along with individualized targeting, should be studied systematically due to their potential as being more accessible alternatives to our current approaches.
