INTRODUCTION

Transfusion has faced many changes since the first ‘vein‐to‐vein’ humanist attempt to preserve human life. Even though it has evolved for plasma into market‐driven plasma‐derived medicinal products (PDMPs), blood donors are still needed. Nonetheless, since PDMPs are classified as pharmaceutical medicines [1], the question of their availability has been shifted to that of access to care. Discussions regarding volumes of plasma required for the production and whether or not to remunerate donors are usually oversimplified, with these factors being considered as the only parameters to be evaluated. As a result, policies and political decisions are often focused on access to treatment for patients, whereas health consequences for donors may be considered secondary. Unfortunately, this leads to an inability to meet today's challenges, both in terms of access for patients to treatment and donor protection.

Here we analyse how the current challenges and patients' and donors' risks can be held under two major human fundamental rights: the right to health protection [2] and the right to human dignity [3] and its associated ethical principles, relating to patients and donors (autonomy, beneficence, non‐maleficence and justice) [4]. We advocate for the view that making decisions rooted in those human rights can protect both patients and donors while strengthening the efficiency of PDMP supply. By adopting a holistic approach that prioritizes the human rights of everyone involved, the entire donor‐to‐patient chain can be improved.

THE BLOOD CHAIN AND ITS SPECIFIC RISKS

Here we will briefly discuss how the definitions of risks associated to transfusion, their intensity and the way scientific, societal and political communities appreciate and deal with them have all evolved since the modern human transfusions [5, 6]. However, one risk seems to equally persist over time, namely the one associated with an insufficient protection of human rights, both for patients and donors.

Managing donor risks

Since surgical vein‐to‐vein transfusion was discontinued, along with the associated risks for donors, attention to donor health was primarily seen as relevant to mitigate risks for blood component recipients. In the early days of transfusion, donors were chosen for their availability in the event of a haemorrhagic emergency and on the basis of being in general good health. Physical and moral appearance were the primary selection criteria. Countries have individually [7], or at times collectively [8], implemented regulations for the medical screening of donors, primarily motivated by the risks of infection. This was notably the case in Europe, following the tainted blood scandal that resulted in the infection of tens of thousands of patients through blood transfusions or PDMPs [9].

The side effects of blood donation have long been seen as secondary and rarely serious for the donor. However, this has changed over the past decades because it is increasingly evident that donations can have short‐ and long‐term health consequences on donors' physiology. Furthermore, the potential negative health effects of donation have always been underestimated due to the ‘healthy donor effect’: donors represent a relatively healthy subset of the general population because of self‐selection and donor screening criteria [10, 11, 12]. Immediate adverse reactions, both local and general, can include dizziness or fainting, bleeding, pain or swelling at the injection site and hypersensitivity reactions to the anticoagulants used with apheresis [13, 14]. Additionally, long‐term effects have increasingly become subjects of study, mostly relating to the risk of depletion of iron with whole‐blood donations [15]. The substantial increase in plasma donations by apheresis over the last few decades to meet the demand for production of PDMPs related to the increased patient needs has also raised the question of the impact of repeated plasma donations on the levels (i.e., the depletion effect) of plasma proteins such as immunoglobulins (Igs) [16]. There currently is a need for studies on health effects of frequent and long‐term plasma donations, as the few existing studies show deleterious effects but are of insufficient size or quality [17, 18]. This observation is also true for blood component donations (as is the case of platelet donations, where lower CD4 cell counts are, for example, noticed in donors after frequent platelet apheresis collections performed with some technologies) [19].

Managing patient transfusion‐transmitted infection risks

For patients receiving all types of blood products, immunological and transfusion‐transmitted infection (TTI) risks are now much better controlled than in the past, and safety is a constant concern for every professional involved. This high level of safety notably results from donor eligibility criteria, laboratory testing mandated by legislation [20], sensitive tests for relevant pathogens as well as optimized PDMP manufacturing processes including efficient steps to remove or inactivate pathogens. For PDMPs, no transmission of relevant blood‐borne pathogens has been documented in the last three decades [21]. It is also worth considering that transfusion medicine has faced recent new risks from both emerging and re‐emerging pathogens that were not present—or present to a lesser extent—in the early 2000s (such as arboviruses, hepatitis E virus, parvovirus B19, coronavirus). The need for continuous monitoring and screening of risks related to blood‐borne pathogens is of specific importance in an era of climate change, which has an effect on how pathogens spread and evolve [22].

A contemporary residual risk related to ethics and public health

Since the first so‐called ‘transfusion episode’ of Pope Innocent VIII in 1492 with three non‐voluntary young shepherds, transfusion has always carried the fear of exploitation of vulnerable people for the benefit of others. Motivations that are guiding blood donation are complex and have to be thought of as a whole, meaning through a national political, economic and social context that cannot be totally universalized [23]. The ethical principle of autonomy is then questioned when a donation is supposed to be based on voluntary consent, that is, without any kind of external factors that could unduly influence the altruistic behaviour of donors. Because the ethical principles overwhelming blood donation are universally guided by the respect of human dignity, this implies that it should always be considered whether a decision runs the risk of exploiting the most vulnerable groups in society, especially when it has been and is still largely discussed whether remuneration contributes to increasing the burden of donation on the most disadvantaged people [24] or not [25]. Moreover, a high national plasma collection rate, including paid plasma, even exceeding the amount theoretically needed for the country, does not necessarily mean that access to PDMPs is secured in that country [26]. In countries where the health system provides blood collection and access to PDMPs, there is a need to have systems in place to reinforce the link between national plasma collection and the PDMP supply; otherwise, the plasma may predominantly be used to serve a global market, regardless of the local public health strategy [27].

In this global context, the ethical principles pertaining to patients are also questioned, underlying the major current risk related to the unmet needs for therapy and medicine shortages. Europe, for example, is dependent on plasma collected in the United States for almost 40% of its supply. Indeed, one of the effects of PDMPs being globally marketed is the lack of geographical unity and equity between plasma collection, plasma fractionation and access to treatment [28]. Self‐sufficiency in PDMPs is therefore considered within the context of access to care by national or regional (European) health policies and arrangements including public healthcare budgets. The problem is even more acute in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), where the shortages in PDMPs affect the patients most severely. This can notably be explained by an insufficient domestic supply of plasma of a quality suitable for non‐domestic fractionation and from a lack of technical and financial capacity to implement a domestic plasma fractionation programme [29].

THE NEED FOR A HOLISTIC APPROACH

This global status and associated international regulation of PDMPs has led to two paradoxical effects. On one hand, it has improved the quality and safety standards to ensure safe and effective medicines, theoretically enabling supply in every country regardless of their fractionation capacities. On the other hand, it has also weakened some regional organizations [30], increasing international interdependence. As a result, patients' access to treatment is uncertain and unbalanced, and the burden on blood and plasma donors is inequitably distributed. The European Medicine Agency (EMA) has issued evidence‐based guidelines for Igs, the main driver of plasma demand; however, off‐label use for non‐EMA Ig indications co‐exists, based on a country's national consensus or new incoming scientific evidence. Moreover, explicit Ig starting criteria, next to stopping criteria, need to be included to prevent unnecessary usage [31].

Furthermore, the prism of analysis is too often limited to a ‘supply and demand’ perspective if PDMPs are considered as pharmaceutical products, because needs for patients, expressed in tonnes of PDMPs, are supposed to be met solely by a corresponding number of plasma donations, regardless of the other parameters that influence the global need. The debate seems to be too often focused on the opposition between whether or not donors should be remunerated, with an increasing pressure on donors (frequency, volume of the donations, exposure to incentives, etc.) and a persistent scarcity of products for patients. This comes with a risk to forgo the ethical principle of non‐maleficence towards donors, while obscuring the other inputs participating to the global supply of PDMPs to support the annual increase in Ig demand. Geopolitical developments, inadequacy of national healthcare organizations (diagnosis, reimbursement, etc.), highly centralized production units, unnecessary plasma wastage, suboptimal extraction yields, inappropriate uses and pandemics pose risks that contribute to unequal access to PDMPs. Assessing each of these parameters and identifying and internationally disseminating good practices could add helpful highlights on how to become strategically independent at a regional [30] or national level [29]. The burden to donate would then be distributed across more than just a few populations by choosing a holistic approach, thereby strengthening PDMP supply for patients.

TOWARDS A RECONCILIATION UNDER THE FUNDAMENTAL HUMAN RIGHT OF HEALTH PROTECTION: PROTECTING HEALTH IN ITS ENTIRETY

These observations force us to go beyond a ‘supply and demand’ perspective and think in terms of fundamental human rights, as rights that guide human behaviour in society and social relationships. They are defined and guaranteed in a state governed by rules and democracy, sometimes at a geographical level [2]. Although these rights are not strictly universal because they depend on local policy, their protection is a common goal shared by numerous democratic countries across the world, at the image of dignity of a human person that forms the very basis of every fundamental right. Blood and plasma bring together many of them, such as the right to personal integrity and autonomy, the right to non‐discrimination [32], the rejection of human exploitation and the right to health protection. As health protection is based on prevention, access to and continuity of care and the best possible health preservation, it means, in our perspective, that it should be considered as a whole, including both patient health with access to PDMPs and donor health that cannot be disproportionately affected.

We propose to reposition the fundamental right to health protection as an input parameter for decisions on the transfusion and plasma supply chain, as this would make it possible to reconcile both patient access to these therapies and the protection of donors, and finally go beyond the sole prism of donor remuneration. This objective means that priority actions must be taken on parameters that do not negatively impact human rights, so that patients are optimally cared for, the plasma donated is fully exploited and the donors are adequately protected.

In that perspective, studies to investigate how to personalize plasma donation intervals in order to optimize efficiency and donor health protection would contribute to ensuring the integrity and ethics surrounding a safe and sustainable plasma collection. An open and transparent communication about the results is required so that the trust of donors and public can be maintained. It is important to note that the new substance of human origin (SoHO) European regulation is now introducing standards and rules to improve donor health protection [33]. Due to the lack of evidence on long‐term health effects of high‐frequency donations, we are unable to meet this ethical obligation [18]. Special attention must also be paid to donors who are exposed to additional burden, either because they have a blood or platelet group of particular interest or because their plasma donation is essential for the manufacture of hyperimmunoglobulin and anti‐RH1 Ig, sometimes even requiring active re‐immunization. Recent access to big data registries, the ability to link transfusion and public health datasets and randomized controlled trials should help us to assess these potential deleterious effects. The European Directorate for the Quality of Medicines & Healthcare, through its recommendations in the Blood Guide and the European Health Data Space (EHDS), will be key pillars to reach this objective in Europe [34].

Research and investments, for instance, on recombinant therapies or monoclonal antibodies, have to be pursued to move away from human‐derived products to reduce the reliance on donated plasma. Meanwhile, it is essential that the use of PDMPs aligns with established and emerging evidence on indications and treatment regimens. This helps to minimize unnecessary risks to patients receiving human‐derived products [21] and to avoid unwarranted donations [35], thereby preventing potential adverse effects on donor health. We want to emphasize that while access to care is of very high importance, donor protection is always a prerequisite that ultimately ensures the resilience of patient access to donor‐derived medicines. To increase resilience of the donation capacity and lower the risk for single donor populations, a wide base of low‐ to medium‐frequency donors shall therefore be preferred over a narrow one of very high frequency donations [36].

In the same way, while the pre‐donation selection criteria are frequently reviewed in light of scientific findings, epidemiological monitoring and technological developments, they also call for a principle of proportionality. A fair proportionality in policy making means to carefully weigh the balance between the efficacy of a safety measure, the collective interest and the individual impact. That is why the risk assessment approach aims to conciliate the need to improve patient safety while mitigating donor risks by improving policies that could negatively impact donor health or donation behaviour.

Finally, protecting health means addressing each non‐human parameter of this global human chain that could enhance knowledge, good practices, efficiency and resilience of the chain, meaning that all initiatives in that perspective shall be supported, like the European union SUPPLY project or the World Health Organization resolutions [37, 38] (Figure 1).

CONCLUSION

At a time when international efforts are and should be increasingly made to limit trades that involve taking advantage of people subject to forms of social vulnerability [39], it is appropriate to consider donation and the supply of PDMPs within the remit of human rights, to protect health in the broadest sense, regardless of whether donors are paid or not. Decisions could then be made within this objective, and finally go over the opposition between access to care and donor health protection. By doing so, we also improve access to PDMPs for patients. As PDMPs have created a kind of international human interdependence, it is our responsibility to place both patients and donors under the umbrella of fundamental human rights, to act not as if a choice has to be made between the two but in ways that protect them both (Table 1).
