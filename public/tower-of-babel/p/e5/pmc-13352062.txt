Physics-embedded CycleGAN for robust metabolite mapping in short-readout deuterium metabolic imaging
Authors: Shuang Huang, Gang Chen, Xinjie Liu, Hongkang Chu, Hong Shi, Zhi Zhang, Xin Zhou, Maili Liu, Chaoyang Liu, Qingjia Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deuterium metabolic imaging (DMI) enables noninvasive, spatially resolved mapping of in vivo metabolism by tracking deuterium-labeled substrates and downstream products, yet it is frequently signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) limiteddue to the low gyromagnetic ratio of 2H and low metabolite concentration. Balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) can improve scan-time SNR efficiency for DMI, but operation in the short repetition time (TR)regime restricts readout duration and the number of acquired free induction decay (FID) points. The resulting loss of spectral resolvability increases metabolite overlap, renders separation ill-conditioned, and destabilizes fast Fourier transform (FFT)-basedprocessing and IDEAL-type model-based least-squares decomposition via strong cross-talk. We propose a physics-embedded CycleGAN for robust metabolite mapping in short-readout DMI using unpaired training. The key innovation is to embed an analytical bSSFP-DMI forward model directly into the cycle-consistency pathway by replacing one generator with a deterministic physics operator, thereby enforcing measurement consistency without requiring paired ground truth. A U-Net generator estimates metabolite maps and an off-resonance field map, with an off-resonance field map (B0) constrained to be spatially smooth via spline-basis parameterization. The physics operator synthesizes voxel wise time-domain signals, enabling a signal-domain cycle loss. Across simulation stress tests and in vivo mouse experiments, our method consistently suppresses inter-metabolite leakage and preserves spatial structure as readout length decreases, outperforming FFT peak integration and IDEAL-type fitting in the short-readout regime.

Introduction

Deuterium metabolic imaging is a stable-isotope MRI/MRSI technique that enables noninvasive, spatially resolved probing of metabolism in vivo by tracking administered deuterium-labeled substrates and their downstream products [1,2]. Because labeled resonances have known chemical shifts, Deuterium metabolic imaging (DMI) provides metabolite-specific readouts of substrate uptake and downstream conversion—pathway-level information not accessible from anatomical MRI. With deuterated glucose, deuterated water (HDO), glucose (Glc), and lactate (Lac) signals can be mapped to probe regional glycolysis and metabolic routing, enabling studies of tumor metabolism and treatment response [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

In practice, DMI is often sensitivity-limited: the lower gyromagnetic ratio of deuterium and the intrinsically low metabolite concentrations produce weak signals, so achieving reliable detection requires highly SNR-efficient acquisitions within a fixed scan time [9]. Balanced steady-state free precession is therefore attractive for DMI: by maintaining coherent steady-state transverse magnetization and operating with short repetition time (TR), bSSFP can provide substantially higher signal per unit time than spoiled gradient-recalled echo (SPGR)and conventional chemical shift imaging (CSI) strategies [10,11]. Short TR can also widen the bSSFP passband, improving tolerance to off-resonance and mitigating the severity and spatial extent of B0-induced banding artifacts. However, the same short-TR design that makes bSSFP SNR-efficient introduces a spectral processing bottleneck. Short TR typically forces a short readout and thus a limited number of time-domain samples, reducing effective spectral resolution and increasing overlap between metabolite peaks. Meanwhile, residual off-resonance continues to modulate the bSSFP steady-state amplitude and phase. Together, these factors make voxel-wise metabolite separation substantially more ill-conditioned under the low-SNR, limited-sampling regime typical of DMI. As a result, conventional FFT-based post-processing, which relies on sufficient time-domain support to resolve closely spaced resonances, can become unreliable when only a short FID is acquired [12].

To move beyond FFT-limited resolution, a broad class of model-based methods has been developed to separate metabolites by fitting measured signals to a parametric forward model. In DMI, these approaches typically represent each metabolite contribution as a complex exponential with known chemical shift (optionally with prior linewidth/phase constraints), while incorporating relaxation, baseline terms, and voxel-dependent B0-induced frequency shifts; metabolite amplitudes are then estimated via least-squares, maximum-likelihood, or regularized fitting [5,10,[13], [14], [15]]. Related formulations include IDEAL-type decompositions adapted from water–fat separation [[15], [16], [17], [18]], dictionary/subspace representations [[19], [20], [21], [22]] that reduce effective degrees of freedom, and joint spatial–spectral regularization to stabilize estimation in low-SNR settings [17,23,24]. In parallel, the NMR/MRS literature has long used linear prediction (LP) and related parametric extrapolation to extend truncated FIDs and sharpen spectra [11,25,26]. While these strategies can, in principle, mitigate FFT resolution limits, their performance can degrade in bSSFP-DMI when the time-domain sampling is sparse and the metabolite encoding becomes highly correlated—especially in the presence of off-resonance–dependent steady-state modulation—leading to unstable solutions and strong cross-talk between overlapping metabolites.

Recently, deep learning (DL) has emerged as an alternative pathway for DMI reconstruction and quantification by leveraging data-driven priors to improve robustness at low SNR [[27], [28], [29]]. Reported directions include denoising and de-aliasing for MRSI/DMI, learning-based spectral fitting or parameter regression, and super-resolution strategies that aim to recover sharper spectra or higher-quality metabolite maps from limited measurements [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Deep models can be advantageous in ill-conditioned regimes by suppressing noise-driven solutions and exploiting spatial context [34,35]. However, purely data-driven learning faces two key barriers in DMI: (i) paired ground truth metabolite maps are rarely available in vivo, limiting supervised training; and (ii) without explicit physics constraints, networks can generate visually plausible outputs that are measurement-inconsistent, particularly under severe spectral overlap and off-resonance effects.

Here we propose a physics-informed CycleGAN for robust metabolite separation in short-TR bSSFP-DMI using unpaired training. While CycleGAN enables learning across domains without paired examples [36,37], we further embed the analytical bSSFP-DMI forward signal model into the cycle-consistency pathway by replacing one generator with a deterministic physics operator. A U-Net maps the complex bSSFP-DMI data to metabolite amplitude maps (HDO, glucose, and lactate) and an off-resonance field map, which is parameterized with a spline basis to enforce spatial smoothness. The physics operator then synthesizes voxel-wise time-domain signals from these estimates, and a signal-domain cycle loss enforces measurement consistency while the adversarial loss provides a learned prior that suppresses noise-driven cross-talk in ill-conditioned regimes. Across large-scale simulations and in vivo 9.4 T mouse bSSFP-DMI, the proposed method yields more accurate and stable metabolite maps than two representative baselines—conventional FFT peak integration and IDEAL-type least-squares decomposition—with the largest gains under severely shortened FID sampling.

Methods

bSSFP based DMI forward signal model (bSSFP-CSI)

In bSSFP-CSI for DMI, a balanced SSFP readout is used for each phase-encoded k-space location. After spatial reconstruction (inverse FFT along k-space), each voxel x yields a short complex time-domain signal (a truncated FID) that contains overlapping metabolite resonances. Because bSSFP maintains coherent steady-state transverse magnetization, the measured samples are governed by chemical-shift evolution during the readout and by a metabolite-dependent bSSFP steady-state complex response modulated by local B0 off-resonance.

Let y(x,m)∈C denote the complex sample at voxel x and time index m=1,...,M with sampling time tm=(m−1)Δt (Δt is dwell time, bandwidth =1/Δt). For N metabolites (here N=3: HDO, Glucose, Lactate), we model the voxel-wise signal as(1)y(x,m)=∑n=1Nρn(x)SbSSFP,n(ϕn(x))e−i2πδfn(x)tme−tm/T2n∗+ε(x,m)where ρn(x) is the complex amplitude of the nth metabolite (proportional to concentration and receive sensitivity), fn (Hz) is its chemical-shift offset relative to the carrier, Δf0(x) (Hz) is the voxel-wise off-resonance due to B0 inhomogeneity, and δfn(x)=fn+Δf0(x) is the total frequency offset. The bSSFP phase accrued per TR is(2)ϕn(x)=2πδfn(x)TR+ϕpcwhere TR is the repetition time and ϕpc is an optional RF phase-cycling term (for RF phase alternation, ϕpc=π). The factor e−tm/T2n∗ models effective within-readout decay/linewidth broadening, and ε(x,m) is circular complex Gaussian noise.

We use the standard analytical balanced-SSFP steady-state response evaluated at a centered echo (typically TE≈TR/2). Defining E1n=e−TR/T1n and E2n=e−TR/T2n, the metabolite-specific complex response is(3)SbSSFP,n(ϕn)=E2ne−iϕ/2(1−E1n)(1−E2ne−iϕ)sinαCncosϕ+Dnwith flip angle α and Cn=E2n(E1n−1)(1+cosα) and Dn=(1−E1ncosα)−(E1n−cosα)E2n2.Equations (1), (2), (3) define the deterministic bSSFP-CSI forward operator used in this work. In the passband regime, SbSSFP,n(ϕn) varies relatively slowly with ϕ near a resonance and can be interpreted as a metabolite-dependent complex gain (amplitude and phase). However, with short readouts and limited M, spectral overlap increases and the inverse separation becomes ill-conditioned, motivating the physics-informed constraints introduced in the proposed reconstruction framework.

Stacking samples across m=1,...,M yields y(x)∈CM. Defining an encoding matrix A(δf(x))∈CM×N with N = 3 metabolites, the forward model becomes:(4)y(x)=A(δf(x))ρ(x)+ε(x)where ρ(x)=[ρHDO(x),ρGlc(x),ρLac(x)]T. When M is small and metabolite frequencies are close, columns of A can become highly correlated, making least-squares estimation unstable and sensitive to noise. This motivates a constrained inverse mapping that leverages measurement consistency and appropriate priors.

Physics-informed CycleGAN for bSSFP-DMI metabolite separation

Given the voxel-wise complex bSSFP-DMI time-domain measurements y(x,m) defined in Eqs. (1), (2), (3), our goal is to estimate spatial maps of metabolite amplitude maps ρ(x)=[ρHDO(x),ρGlc(x),ρLac(x)]T together with the off-resonance field map Δf0(x). This inverse problem is particularly ill-conditioned for short-TR bSSFP-DMI because only a limited number of readout samples are acquired per voxel, which increases spectral overlap and makes the signal strongly sensitive to residual off-resonance through the bSSFP steady-state response SbSSFP,n(ϕn(x)), where ϕn(x)=2π(fn+Δf0(x))TR+ϕpc.

Paired ground-truth metabolite maps are rarely available in vivo; we therefore adopt an unpaired training strategy (Fig. 1(b)). We define two domains: the measurement domain Y containing voxel-wise complex bSSFP-DMI time series y, and the map domain Z containing metabolite amplitude maps (and a B0 map) z(x)={ρHDO(x),ρGlc(x),ρLac(x),Δf0(x)}. A generator Gθ:Y→Z is trained to infer zˆ=Gθ(y) from y. Crucially, instead of learning an explicit reverse mapping Z→Y with a second generator as in conventional CycleGAN, we replace it with a deterministic physics operator F:Z→Y implemented by the analytical bSSFP-CSI forward model in Section 2.1 (Eqs. (1)–(3)). This physics-embedded design forces the learned inverse mapping to remain measurement-consistent and markedly stabilizes training in low-SNR, short-FID regimes. In Fig. 1(b), this corresponds to the two closed loops: (i) y→Gθzˆ=Gθ(y)→Fyˆ=F(Gθ(y)) (signal-consistency loop), and (ii) z→Fy˜=F(z)→Gθz˜=Gθ(F(z)) (map-consistency loop). The generator architecture is a U-Net–style encoder–decoder that takes the complex bSSFP readout (stacked real/imaginary channels over M time samples) and outputs three metabolite amplitude maps plus one field map, with a dedicated multi-scale spline back-projection branch that converts decoder features at several resolutions into intermediate field components and combines them to produce the final smooth field map (Fig. 1(d)).Fig. 1Overview of the proposed physics-guided unpaired adversarial framework for bSSFP-DMI metabolite separation. To impose a compact smoothness prior, we parameterize Δf0(x) using a multi-scale B-spline basis representation (Fig. 1(c)) [38,39]. At each scale k, the generator predicts a low-dimensional coefficient map Ck on a coarse k×k grid, which is then deterministically interpolated to full resolution by fixed, separable B-spline basis matrices. For a single scale, this projection can be written in separable matrix form as(5)Ψk=1γkAkCkBkTwhere the full image has size h×w, Ck∈Rk×k denotes the network-predicted coefficient matrix at scale k, Ak∈Rh×k and Bk∈Rw×k are precomputed one-dimensional B-spline basis matrices evaluated at the full-resolution sampling locations (along the y- and x-axes respectively), and Ψk∈Rh×w is the resulting full-resolution contribution from scale k. The final full-resolution off-resonance map is obtained by summing contributions across all scales:(6)Δf0=∑k=1KΨk

In our implementation, we use first-order (linear) B-splines and a multi-scale representation for the 32×32 image geometry used in this work. The spline supports from coarse to fine are {32,24,18,14,10,7} pixels, corresponding to coefficient grid sizes of {3,4,5,6,7,9} respectively. This low-dimensional parameterization substantially reduces the degrees of freedom relative to direct full-resolution B0 regression and markedly improves robustness in the short-FID, low-SNR regime.

Training is driven by the two physics-defined cycles in Fig. 1(b). First, a signal-domain cycle loss enforces measurement consistency by comparing the measured signal y with the synthesized signal yˆ=F(zˆ)=F(Gθ(y)):(7)Lsig=Ey∼Y[‖F(Gθ(y))−y‖1]

Second, a map-domain cycle loss regularizes the inverse mapping using unpaired samples z∼Z (e.g., simulated metabolite/ Δf0 maps), by requiring that applying Gθ to the forward-simulated signal F(z) recovers the original maps:(8)Lmap=Ez∼Z[‖Gθ(F(z))−z‖1]here, Ey∼Y and Ez∼Z denote expectations, implemented in practice as empirical averages over samples drawn from the measurement domain Y and the map domain Z, respectively.

To encourage realistic metabolite-map statistics, we employ a discriminator Dψ on the map domain and use an adversarial loss (LSGAN) between generated maps zˆ=Gθ(y) and samples from Z:(9)Ladv(Gθ,Dψ)=Ez∼Z[(Dψ(z)−1)2]+Ey∼Y[(Dψ(Gθ(y)))2]

The total objective is(10)minθmaxψλsigLsig+λmapLmap+λadvLadvwhere λsig, λmap and λadv balance measurement consistency, map consistency, and realism, respectively.

At inference, only the generator Gθ is used: given a measured voxel-wise bSSFP-DMI time series y(x,m), the network outputs metabolite maps {ρˆHDO,ρˆGlc,ρˆLac} and Δf0(x). The embedded forward operator F is not required during inference, but it provides a physics-based constraint during training that is especially beneficial for short-FID, off-resonance–sensitive bSSFP-DMI. A brief note on computational resources and training time is provided in Experimental Details (Section 3.3).

Experimental details

Simulations (data generation, short-FID protocol, and Monte Carlo SNR analysis)

To validate the performance of the metabolic separation, we constructed a simulation pipeline that generates unpaired map samples z(x)={ρHDO(x),ρGlc(x),ρLac(x),Δf0(x)} and the corresponding complex bSSFP-DMI voxel time series y(x,m) using the forward model in Eqs. (1), (2), (3). Brain extraction and tissue segmentation were performed using FSL (FMRIB Software Library), including the Brain Extraction Tool (BET) and FMRIB’s Automated Segmentation Tool (FAST)to obtain CSF/GM/WM(cerebrospinal fluid/gray matter/white matter) masks on the public IXI dataset (http://brain-development.org/ixi-dataset/). Metabolite maps for HDO, glucose, and lactate were synthesized by linear combinations of these tissue masks to mimic tissue-dependent distributions reported in the literature [40,41]; chemical shifts and relaxation parameters were set to literature-consistent values [1,10]. Complex circular Gaussian noise was added in the time domain. To increase diversity, we applied random affine augmentations and also generated synthetic phantom samples with randomized geometric shapes and metabolite patterns. In total, 10,000 simulated samples were generated (8000 brain-derived and 2000 phantom), split 80/10/10 for training/validation/testing.

The B0 maps used in simulation were not directly extracted from measured in vivo B0 scans. Instead, they were synthesized using a parametric multi-scale spline random-field model, chosen to reflect the predominantly smooth off-resonance structure typically observed in localized-shimmed 9.4 T small-animal imaging and to remain consistent with the spline-based B0 representation described in Section 2.2. Specifically, at each spline scale, a low-dimensional coefficient map was sampled from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with scale-dependent amplitude, such that coarser scales dominated the global smooth offset while finer scales contributed only weaker local perturbations. These coefficient maps were then projected to full resolution using the same fixed separable B-spline basis functions used in the network field-map branch, and the multi-scale contributions were summed to form the final B0 field. To ensure realism, the resulting full-resolution B0 maps were further amplitude-constrained to lie within the empirically observed range from our 9.4 T mouse experiments (approximately [−20,20] Hz). Thus, the measured in vivo B0 information was used to calibrate the plausible range/statistics of the simulated fields, but not as direct field-map samples in the training set.

To probe the short-readout regime, we varied the number of acquired samples per voxel Mto emulate truncated FIDs. Unless otherwise stated, we used M={128,72,48,36}, corresponding to effective TR values of 31.69 ms, 17.91 ms, 14.96 ms, and 12.00 ms in our sequence timing. To keep the network input size fixed, signals with fewer samples were zero-padded to 128 points before being fed into the generator (zero-padding introduces no additional information).

To quantify scan-time SNR efficiency, we performed Monte Carlo simulations comparing bSSFP with a conventional CSI reference modeled by an RF-spoiled GRE/SPGR steady-state signal under matched total scan time. Each condition used 50 trials; SNR gain distributions are summarized by the median and variability intervals. Off-resonance Δf0 was sampled over a range relevant to 9.4 T small-animal imaging.

bSSFP-DMI acquisition on phantom and in vivo mouse

All phantom and in vivo experiments was performed on a Bruker BioSpec 9.4 T system using a lab-built single-channel transmit/receive surface coil. Phantom measurements were acquired on a dedicated calibration phantom. In vivo experiments were carried out on a cohort of five 8-week-old BALB/C mice (N = 5), with one imaging session per animal. Animal handling and procedures were approved by the institutional animal care committee (protocol APM23022T) and followed National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines. Body temperature was maintained at approximately 38 °C throughout imaging; anesthesia was induced and maintained with ∼1.5 % isoflurane in oxygen (0.5 L/min), and respiration was continuously monitored (30–50 breaths/min).

For positioning and planning, a T2-weighted fast spin-echo anatomical scan was acquired first, followed by localized shimming over the imaging slice. A baseline bSSFP-DMI scan was acquired prior to substrate administration. In addition, five non-localized spectroscopy (NSPECT) scans were recorded during the session; deuterated glucose was injected slowly via an indwelling catheter at the end of the second NSPECT acquisition, and dynamic bSSFP-DMI data were subsequently acquired.

The bSSFP-DMI acquisition used a fixed sampling bandwidth of 4065 Hz (dwell time Δt = 0.246 ms) and four TR settings (31.69, 17.91, 14.96, and 12.00 ms), corresponding to M = 128, 72, 48, and 36 complex time points per voxel, respectively. Sequence parameters were: flip angle 60°, field of view (FOV) 35 × 35 mm2, matrix 32 × 32, single slice, yielding 1.1 × 1.1 × 6 mm3 resolution.

Baselines, network training, and evaluation

Baselines

We compared the proposed method with two representative baselines for limited-sampling spectroscopic separation: conventional FFT peak integration and an IDEAL-type model-based least-squares decomposition. For FFT peak integration, voxel-wise complex FIDs were Fourier transformed and metabolite amplitudes were estimated by integrating signal within predefined frequency windows around expected resonances. For IDEAL-type decomposition, metabolite amplitudes were estimated by least-squares (or regularized least-squares) fitting using known chemical shifts; under short M and low SNR, the encoding matrix becomes highly correlated, leading to cross-talk and unstable estimates.

Network and optimization

The generator Gθ is a U-Net encoder–decoder operating on stacked complex-valued voxel time series (real/imaginary channels), and the discriminator Dψ follows a PatchGAN design. We used a least-squares GAN (LSGAN) adversarial loss together with signal- and map-domain cycle-consistency losses (Section 2.2). The field map Δf0(x) is parameterized via a spline basis: the network predicts spline coefficients on a coarse grid, which are back-projected to full resolution using fixed B-spline basis functions (Fig. 1(c)).

The model was implemented in TensorFlow and optimized with Adam optimizer (batch size 32, initial learning rate = 2×10−4, β1=0.5, β2=0.999), Training ran for 200 epochs with linear learning-rate decay after epoch 100. Loss weights were set to balance adversarial and cycle terms, specifically λsig=100, λmap=10, and λadv=1. Training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU under Ubuntu 22.04 with CUDA 12.2.

Metrics

For simulations, we report peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index measure (SSIM) on metabolite maps when applicable. PSNR quantifies voxel-wise fidelity by measuring the logarithmic ratio between the reference signal intensity and the reconstruction error, with higher values indicating smaller deviations from the reference. SSIM evaluates perceptual similarity by jointly assessing luminance, contrast, and structural consistency, with higher values indicating better preservation of image structure. For phantom and in vivo experiments, where pixel-wise ground-truth metabolite maps are unavailable for the acquired data, we primarily assess qualitative map plausibility, and compare against baselines under matched sampling.

Computational cost and runtime

The model was trained on the workstation specified above using 8000 simulated training samples. Training under the reported configuration typically required approximately 7 h 12 min on the NVIDIA RTX 3090. Single-sample runtime comparisons with FFT peak integration and IDEAL-type fitting are reported in the Discussion.

Results

Simulation results

We first evaluated the proposed physics-guided unpaired framework on a large-scale simulation dataset generated with the bSSFP-CSI forward model (Eqs. (1)–(3)), which includes diverse metabolite-map patterns and spatially varying Δf0(x) to emulate realistic bSSFP steady-state modulation. Representative examples are shown in Fig. 2. The fully sampled reference condition (M = 128) is included in Fig. 2 primarily as a visual reference, since under this relatively well-resolved condition spectral resolvability is still good and the differences among methods are comparatively small. For a well-sampled readout (M = 72), all methods recover the dominant HDO pattern, but FFT peak integration and IDEAL-type fitting already show noticeable cross-talk and spatial noise in the weaker glucose and lactate maps. As the readout is progressively shortened (M = 48,36), these baselines degrade rapidly—exhibiting blurred structures, spurious leakage between metabolites (particularly into lactate), and loss of contrast in low-signal regions. In contrast, the proposed method remains visually stable across all M, producing cleaner maps with sharper boundaries and substantially reduced inter-metabolite leakage. Discussion

This work targets a practical bottleneck in DMI: while bSSFP-CSI can substantially improve scan-time SNR efficiency, short-TR operation inevitably shortens the readout and reduces the number of acquired time-domain samples per voxel, which degrades effective spectral resolution and makes metabolite separation ill-conditioned. We addressed this challenge with a physics-informed CycleGAN that enables unpaired learning while enforcing measurement consistency through an embedded analytical bSSFP-DMI forward operator and a structured (spline-based) off-resonance field parameterization.

Beyond reconstruction quality, computational cost matters for practical DMI workflows. On our development workstation (see Methods for details), average single-sample processing times were: physics-informed CycleGAN (GPU inference) approximately 0.0615 s/sample, FFT peak-picking approximately 0.0977 s/sample, and IDEAL-type fitting approximately 1.9088 s/sample. These results show that our DL approach already delivers near-real-time inference on GPU and is substantially faster than pixel-wise iterative fitting, while offering markedly greater robustness in short-FID, low-SNR regimes. Absolute timings depend on implementation and hardware; both classical and deep learning methods can be further optimized (e.g., parallelization, batching), so the numbers should be taken as a practical demonstration of relative cost and optimization potential rather than a definitive ranking.

Below, we discuss (i) why bSSFP-CSI is advantageous for DMI, (ii) what elements of the proposed framework drive performance, supported by ablations, and (iii) limitations and future directions.

Why bSSFP-CSI is advantageous for DMI under fixed scan time

DMI is intrinsically SNR-limited because of the low gyromagnetic ratio of H2 and weak detectable signal levels. Under a fixed scan-time budget, improving SNR efficiency is therefore a central objective. bSSFP-CSI is particularly attractive because it maintains coherent steady-state transverse magnetization and can operate at short TR, yielding higher signal per unit time than spoiled GRE/CSI strategies [26,42,43]. In addition, shorter TR increases off-resonance tolerance by widening the bSSFP passband and increasing stopband spacing, which can reduce the severity and spatial extent of B0-induced banding. Our Monte Carlo analysis under 9.4 T conditions (Fig. 5) supports this expectation: across the tested short-TR range, bSSFP provided consistently higher scan-time–normalized SNR than an RF-spoiled GRE (SPGR) reference, with especially pronounced gains for the lower-SNR metabolites (glucose and lactate). However, bSSFP’s short-TR advantage comes with a downstream reconstruction penalty. Short TR and short readouts limit the number of acquired FID points M, reducing spectral resolution and increasing metabolite peak overlap. Residual off-resonance further modulates the steady-state amplitude and phase, making separation sensitive to B0 and increasing the conditioning of voxel-wise model fitting. This is precisely the regime in which FFT-based processing becomes unreliable and least-squares model-based decomposition can become unstable and cross-talk–prone. The proposed method is designed to retain bSSFP’s SNR efficiency while mitigating these short-FID separation instabilities.

What drives performance: ablation studies

A key design choice is to replace one CycleGAN generator with a deterministic physics operator (the analytical bSSFP-CSI forward model in Eqs. (1), (2), (3)). This modification converts standard cycle consistency into a measurement-model–consistent cycle: the generator must produce metabolite maps and an off-resonance field that, when passed through the known forward operator, reproduce the acquired complex bSSFP-DMI time series. The second design choice is to represent the off-resonance field using a spline basis, explicitly enforcing spatial smoothness consistent with the physical nature of B0 inhomogeneity. Our ablation experiments isolate these two contributions.

Ablation 1: removing the physics operator

When the physics operator is removed and replaced by a learned inverse generator (i.e., a conventional CycleGAN trained between the measurement domain and the metabolite-map domain), performance degrades substantially in the short-FID regime. Qualitatively, the resulting metabolite maps show increased noise-driven structure and more frequent metabolite leakage, particularly between Glucose and Lactate, where spectral overlap is strongest under limited sampling. Quantitatively, this ablation (‘w/o Physics’ in Table 2) yields lower PSNR/SSIM and higher error metrics relative to the full model (Table 2). This behavior is consistent with the fact that, under sparse sampling, the inverse mapping is highly non-unique: a purely learned cycle can satisfy adversarial constraints while drifting away from measurement consistency, producing plausible-looking but physically inconsistent metabolite maps. Ablation 2: removing spline-based B0 parameterization

When the spline field-map parameterization is removed and Δf0(x) is directly regressed at full resolution, we observe reduced stability and increased sensitivity to noise and local artifacts. The predicted field maps become less smooth and may contain high-frequency components that are not physically plausible, which in turn propagate through the bSSFP steady-state term and degrade metabolite separation. This effect is most apparent near regions with lower SNR or stronger off-resonance, where the network can trade off unrealistic B0 variations against metabolite amplitudes to satisfy the signal-domain loss. Enforcing a spline basis reduces the effective degrees of freedom of the B0 estimate and biases solutions toward physically plausible, smooth fields, thereby improving robustness of joint metabolite/ B0 estimation and reducing cross-talk. In our experience, this structured parameterization is a practical mechanism to stabilize training and inference without requiring explicit B0 ground truth.

Taken together, these ablations support the central thesis of this work: in short-TR bSSFP-DMI, robustness is driven by (i) physics-embedded cycle consistency that enforces measurement-model agreement, and (ii) structured B0 modeling that reduces ambiguity in the inverse problem and prevents the network from using nonphysical field variations as a shortcut.

Relationship to FFT-based and model-based separation

It is useful to contrast the proposed approach with established baselines. FFT-based approaches implicitly assume that the acquired time series provides sufficient spectral resolution to separate resonances; this assumption fails when M is small, causing peak overlap and unstable peak integration. Model-based least-squares methods explicitly incorporate chemical shifts and can partially compensate for limited spectral resolution, but their conditioning deteriorates rapidly when sampling is sparse and the bSSFP steady-state response introduces additional off-resonance–dependent modulation. In this regime, small perturbations in the data (noise, drift, residual phase errors) can translate into large fluctuations in estimated metabolite amplitudes, producing cross-talk. The proposed method can be viewed as a hybrid: it leverages the same forward signal model as model-based fitting to ensure physical consistency, while using a learned prior (via adversarial training and a deep generator) to regularize the ill-conditioned inverse and suppress noise-driven solutions. This combination is particularly beneficial for bSSFP-DMI where the forward model is known but inversion is unstable under short-FID acquisition.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, although the embedded physics operator enforces consistency with the assumed bSSFP-CSI signal model, mismatch between the model and the true acquisition physics can still affect quantification accuracy. Examples include imperfect knowledge of metabolite relaxation parameters, deviations from the assumed echo timing or phase-cycling convention, and additional system effects such as eddy currents, gradient delays, and coil-dependent phase offsets. Importantly, in the present short-readout bSSFP-CSI setting, the dominant source of metabolite discriminability is the complex temporal evolution during the acquired FID, driven primarily by chemical-shift differences together with local off-resonance. By comparison, relaxation-related parameters mainly modulate the bSSFP steady-state complex response and the decay/linewidth envelope. Therefore, mismatch in T1 is expected to affect quantitative scaling more directly, whereas mismatch in T2∗ may alter linewidth and effective spectral overlap, thereby increasing cross-talk. These effects are expected to be more pronounced for lower-concentration metabolites such as glucose and lactate, because their lower effective SNR and stronger spectral overlap make them more sensitive to modeling errors. Future work could incorporate explicit nuisance-parameter modeling (e.g., coil-phase terms), uncertainty-aware relaxation priors, or calibration scans to reduce model mismatch.

Second, the present framework estimates a spatially smooth B0 map via spline parameterization, which is well matched to typical off-resonance structure but may under-represent sharp local variations in the presence of susceptibility boundaries or severe field distortions. In such regions, the resulting field-model mismatch could introduce local errors in metabolite separation and quantification; this should be viewed as a trade-off for the improved global stability afforded by the smooth spline prior, particularly in the low-SNR, short-FID regime, and may become especially relevant in future applications involving lesions with stronger susceptibility heterogeneity, such as glioma. Multi-scale or adaptive basis strategies, or combining spline parameterization with a weak total-variation prior, may improve flexibility while preserving stability. When available, integrating an independently measured B0 map as a soft constraint could further reduce ambiguity.

Third, although our training uses large-scale simulations to enable unpaired learning, generalization can still be sensitive to the realism of the simulation distribution, including metabolite spatial statistics, noise characteristics, and off-resonance ranges. In principle, subject-specific self-supervised adaptation using only the signal-domain consistency loss with the fixed physics operator is feasible in our framework. Related physics-consistency–based unsupervised strategies have also been shown to be effective in closely related MRI decomposition problems such as water-fat separation, where unsupervised deep neural networks trained using the physical forward model were reported to achieve accurate separation. However, a consistency-only strategy mainly relies on the physical signal model together with the implicit regularization induced by the network architecture, and does not fully exploit the population-level statistical priors that can be learned from large-scale data. As a result, while such self-supervised adaptation may help compensate for scanner-specific imperfections or subject-specific B0 variations not fully represented in simulations, its robustness may still be limited in highly ill-conditioned short-FID settings, especially under low SNR or residual model mismatch. A more effective practical direction may therefore be a hybrid two-stage strategy: first pretrain the network on large-scale simulated and/or real datasets to learn a strong prior for metabolite-map structure and signal statistics, and then perform subject-specific refinement using the physics-consistency loss to better match the actual acquisition. In our view, this combination of population-level pretraining and physics-based adaptation is likely to offer a better balance between generalization and subject-specific accuracy than using consistency loss alone.

Finally, the current study focuses on three dominant components (HDO, glucose and lactate). Extending to additional metabolites or broader spectral bandwidths may require revisiting identifiability under short-FID sampling and may benefit from richer priors or joint spatiotemporal modeling across dynamic frames. Incorporating temporal redundancy (e.g., low-rank or recurrent priors) within the same physics-informed training framework is a promising direction for further improving stability in very low-SNR regimes.

Conclusion

Overall, our results support bSSFP-CSI as an SNR-efficient acquisition strategy for DMI under realistic scan-time constraints, but also highlight that short-TR operation creates a challenging inverse problem due to limited FID sampling and off-resonance–dependent steady-state modulation. By embedding the analytical bSSFP-DMI forward model into a CycleGAN training loop and imposing a physically motivated spline parameterization for the B0 field, the proposed method addresses these challenges and improves robustness of metabolite separation in the short-FID regime.

Beyond the present mouse bSSFP-DMI setting, this framework may provide practical value for accelerated or time-constrained DMI studies, where maintaining metabolite separability under limited readout is critical. More broadly, the proposed physics-guided unpaired learning strategy may be extensible to dynamic metabolic imaging and other limited-sampling MR spectroscopic separation problems, offering a potential path toward more robust preclinical and future translational applications.
