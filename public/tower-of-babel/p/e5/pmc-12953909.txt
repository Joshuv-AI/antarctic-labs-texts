Deep learning-based identification and maturation assessment of the zygomaticomaxillary suture in cone-beam computed tomography images
Authors: Zehua Jin, Yuhua Shan, Jianxing Feng, Jingtao Li, Zhelin Shi, Haotian Wu, Xiaochen Fan, Ruomei Li, Zhenqi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study addresses maxillary deficiency management by developing a deep learning-based system for zygomaticomaxillary suture assessment. As the maturation status serves as a pivotal indicator for optimal maxillary protraction timing, its accurate evaluation is crucial for treatment success. The proposed cone-beam computed tomography-based system overcomes current limitations of subjective manual staging, offering quantitative and reproducible maturation analysis to guide clinically critical decisions in maxillary protraction therapy.MethodsA dataset of 505 cone-beam computed tomography scans was collected and annotated by three orthodontists to locate and stage the suture. Data augmentation and various loss functions were applied to optimize the model. The framework employs dual-network architecture: (1) 3D U-Net for precise suture identification, followed by (2) 3D ResNet50-based classification network for maturation stage prediction. Model performance was validated using multi-dimensional metrics including classification accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and area under the curve values from the receiver operating characteristic curve, with benchmark comparisons against expert evaluators’ assessments.ResultsThe model assessment took about 46 s for two tasks. 3D U-Net showed an average Euclidean distance of 2.31 ± 1.57 mm between the located and annotated zygomaticomaxillary suture centers. For classification tasks, the two-stage (ABC/DE) task had an accuracy of 0.922 and an F1-score of 0.921; the three-stage (A/BC/DE) task had an accuracy of 0.833 and an F1-score of 0.838; and the five-stage (A/B/C/D/E) task had an accuracy of 0.611 and an F1-score of 0.604.ConclusionThe dual-network deep learning system can automatically locate and assess the maturation of the zygomaticomaxillary suture in three dimensions from CBCT files, providing a critical foundation for clinical decision-making in maxillary protraction therapy. This deep learning-model improves diagnostic workflow by reducing assessment time and minimizing inter-observer variability, providing clinicians with a reliable and reproducible reference for optimal maxillary protraction timing—avoiding over-treatment of the very young (stage A) while ensuring timely intervention before sutural closure (stages D and E).Keywords: Maxillary protraction, Zygomaticomaxillary suture, Maturation staging, Cone-beam computed tomography, Convolutional neural network

Background

Maxillary deficiency, a primary etiologic factor in skeletal class III malocclusion, requires timely intervention during growth periods [1, 2]. Prepubertal maxillary protraction demonstrates significant therapeutic potential [3–8], the efficacy of which depends on the maturation of circummaxillary sutures, especially the zygomaticomaxillary suture (ZMS), which is the longest and biomechanically dominant circummaxillary articulation [9–13].

Angelieri et al. established a cone-beam computed tomography(CBCT)–based classification that grades ZMS maturation from stages A to E, there by offering a quantifiable ‘suture age’ indicator for determining the optimal orthopedic intervention window [14]. Their follow-up study confirmed that stages A and B, in which the suture remains patent, yield the greater sagittal maxillary advancement, whereas stage C, defined by the initial appearance of bony bridges, constitutes a critical window beyond which incremental gains diminish [15]. Notwithstanding these histological data, clinical observation reveals that stage A patients are predominantly in deciduous dentition; intervention at this juncture is associated with reduced patient compliance, compromised appliance stability, elevated relapse rates, and a frequent need for retreatment. Consequently, although the biological window is technically open during stage A, behavioral and stability-related factors indicate that it is premature for routine therapy [16, 17]. The preferred interval remains the pre-pubertal phase encompassing stages B or C, when cooperative capacity is improved and the dentition is more mature, thereby optimizing the balance between skeletal correction and treatment reliability [18–22]. For individuals in late puberty or adulthood (stages D and E), with limited growth potential, are less likely to benefit from this treatment [23].

The interpretation of the maturation stages requires extensive professional knowledge and experience, demanding systematic training and clinical practice [24, 25]. The assessment of ZMS maturity is particularly demanding: its irregular three-dimensional geometry necessitates multi-planar reorientation to identify the optimal imaging plane, prolonging evaluation time and compromising reproducibility. Additionally, suture development is a complex biological process influenced by genetics and the environment, with maturation assessment involving some ambiguity [25]. The aforementioned technical constraints have prevented widespread adoption of ZMS maturation assessment, significantly impeding its clinical implementation [14].

In recent years, deep learning has made remarkable progress in medical image analysis. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have shown excellence in image recognition and segmentation tasks and have been successfully applied in oral and maxillofacial image analysis [26–28]. 3D U-Net, with its strong feature extraction and segmentation accuracy, was used in this study for ZMS localization [29, 30]. 3D Resnet50, a deep CNN effective for 3D data processing and feature extraction, was applied for ZMS maturation analysis [28]. This study aims to develop an automated ZMS maturation detection system based on deep learning. By leveraging deep learning technology, we aim to improve the accuracy, efficiency, and reproducibility of ZMS maturation assessment, thereby providing more precise guidance for clinical treatment.

Materials and methods

Dataset

Approved by the Independent Ethics Committee of the Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, affiliated with the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval No. SH9H-2024-T271-1), this study collected CBCT files of 570 patients imaged by the iCAT FLX (DEXIS, USA). These scans were originally acquired for justified clinical diagnostic purposes. Our research utilizes these pre-existing images in full compliance with ethical standards to investigate the maturation process of the ZMS. Images, exported in DICOM format and imported into proprietary software, had a field of view of 16 × 13 cm, scan time of 20–30 s, and slice thickness of 0.25 mm. Inclusion criteria: aged 3–25 years old, underwent iCAT FLX CBCT imaging. Exclusion criteria: blurred ZMS images, previous orthodontic or orthognathic treatment, maxillofacial trauma history. After excluding 65 volumes, 505 CBCT data sets were retained for analysis.

ZMS localization network

A researcher annotated the left and right ZMS locations in 331 CBCT images, generating 662 regions of interest (ROI) data entries. When annotating, the researcher was blinded to the CBCT sample information. In the ZMS localization network, the input was the preprocessed CBCT image, and the output was the predicted ZMS location. During preprocessing, the volumes were sampled from a region of 300 × 300 × 300 voxels centered on the suture, then resized to 128 × 128 × 128 for model inputting. Data augmentation techniques, such as random cropping and random rotation (± 15° around the x, y, and z axes), were used to expand the training dataset. Left suture images were flipped to mirror right suture images. The Adaptive Wing Loss function was adopted to enhance the network’s ability to identify small sutures. After training, the network’s performance was evaluated using a validation set and finally tested on a test set.

ZMS maturation analysis network

The localization network generated maximum observation sections of the ZMS for all 505 cases, yielding 1010 datasets for classifier training, validation, and testing. Two experienced orthodontists annotated the data, with a third researcher resolving discrepancies to establish a machine-learning gold standard. Labeled images underwent supervised learning with Mean Square Error as the loss function. Model performance was evaluated via a validation set and ultimately tested on a test set.

Evaluation metrics

Figure 1 shows the schematic diagram of the training workflow based on two-stage (ABC/DE), three-stage (A/BC/DE), and five-stage (A/B/C/D/E) maturation classifications. The deep learning framework for ZMS identification and maturation assessment was implemented based on the proposed dual-network architecture. The core algorithm of this framework has been registered for computer software copyright (Registration Number: 2024SR1858191). The localization algorithm generated a heatmap matching the input-image size. Each heatmap pixel corresponded to an original-image pixel with a confidence value. Higher confidence implied a greater probability of suture presence at that pixel. The top 5% highest-confidence pixels were clustered and median-ranked to estimate the suture-center position. Euclidean distance between the model-predicted and researcher-annotated suture-center positions assessed model localization accuracy. For the maturation-analysis network, metrics include accuracy, weighted recall, weighted precision, weighted F1-score, and receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) area under the curve (AUC) evaluated model performance in two-stage, three-stage, and five-stage classifications. Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX GPU using the Pytorch framework. The network was trained using a stochastic gradient descent optimizer with a learning rate of 0.01, a batch size of 16, and a maximum of 200 training epochs.

ZMS localization performance

3D U-Net demonstrated excellent performance in localizing the ZMS, with an average Euclidean distance of 2.31 ± 1.57 mm between the model predicted suture center position and the researcher annotated suture segmentation results. This indicates that the model can accurately locate the ZMS in three-dimensional space.

The ROC curves and AUC values for the three classification tasks are shown in Fig. 3. In the two-stage classification task, the model exhibited good discriminative ability, which decreased with increasing task complexity. The AUC value for the two-stage classification task was 0.98, indicating that the model can effectively distinguish between patients with open sutures (A, B, C stages) and those with partially or fully closed sutures (D, E stages). In the three-stage classification task, stage A corresponds to patients who are too young and in the transitional phase between deciduous and early mixed dentition, achieving an AUC value of 0.93. For stages B and C, representing the critical treatment window, the model demonstrated an AUC value of 0.83, effectively differentiating B and C from the closed stages of D and E. In the five-stage classification task, the AUC values for stages B and C were relatively low (0.76 and 0.79, respectively), indicating reduced model discriminability for transitional stages.

Model assessment time

The model took approximately 46 s from CBCT data inputting to outputting the maturation results of both ZMSs, which was significantly shorter than the average time required for researcher assessment.

Discussion

Maxillary protraction is an effective treatment for early skeletal Class III maxillary deficiency. Regardless of the type of anchorage, some degree of maxillary advancement can be achieved, improving maxilla-mandible relationships and occlusion [8, 20, 31]. While bone-anchored maxillary protraction can achieve greater maxillary advancement, it carries risks such as postoperative infection, titanium plate loosening, and impacts on tooth germ from the implant procedure [31–33]. Importantly, the choice of anchorage seems less critical than treatment timing [34]. Early maxillary protraction can significantly reduce the need for adult orthognathic surgery. Within two years of treatment, 90% of patients can maintain a positive overjet [35, 36] However, the concept of early intervention requires precise definition. initiation that is excessively early—such as treatment begun at the deciduous dentition stage—yields the anticipated bony response be offset by elevated relapse rates, compromised appliance retention, and diminished patient compliance [16, 37]. Conversely, late treatment may result in attenuated skeletal adaptation and increased dento-alveolar compensation [33, 34]. Thus, accurate treatment timing is crucial for enhancing therapeutic outcomes and minimizing complications.

Age is a traditional indicator for determining treatment timing, yet there is no consensus on the optimal treatment age. For instance, Hickham and McNamara suggested that maxillary protraction before age 8 could yield more significant bony changes and reduce dental compensation [38, 39]. Wells and Kim recommended treatment before age 10 [40, 41], while Franchi and Takada advocated for treatment before the pubertal spurt [23, 42]. Current expert consensus recommends initiating treatment in the mixed dentition after the eruption of the permanent incisors, typically around 8–9 years of age [17]. Different studies have also proposed varying indicators based on wrist-bone age and cervical-vertebrae maturation [43–45]. However, discrepancies between bone age and chronological age, as well as differences in growth-spurt timing, limit the ability of these indicators to accurately reflect maxillary growth potential [20, 46]. In contrast, suture maturation status provides a more direct reflection of maxillary growth potential. CT has advantages in assessing suture status, enabling accurate identification of suture opening and closure [47]. With the development of imaging technologies, CBCT has become routinely used in orthodontic diagnosis and treatment. It offers lower radiation doses, lower costs, and clearer imaging of fine oral and maxillofacial structures [48–50]. Consequently, Angelieri referenced the palatal suture maturation staging method to propose a ZMS maturation staging method, offering a more precise basis for clinical decisions [14]. Patients in the A and B stages of ZMS maturation achieve better maxillary protraction outcomes compared to those in stage C. Stage C and earlier are considered appropriate for orthopedic treatment. However, in patients with ZMS in stages D and E, the sutures begin to close, leading to suboptimal maxillary protraction effects and potential treatment failure [15].

The image interpretation of ZMS maturation requires a high level of professional knowledge and experience. In the original study proposing the maturation staging, four experienced orthodontists demonstrated an intra-and inter-examiner weighted kappa coefficient ranging from 0.774 to 0.953 [14]. In a similar study on midpalatal suture maturation, Barbosa et al. reported weak overall inter-examiner agreement (k = 0.34–0.37) among a group of 11 orthodontists and 10 maxillofacial radiologists, with a complete agreement rate of 43.2% [25]. Chhatwani et al. found that the consistency of student examiners was minimal (k = 0.34), that of orthodontic resident physicians was weak (k = 0.46), and that of orthodontists was moderate (k = 0.68) [24]. This indicates that there are differences in suture maturation assessment among researchers with varying levels of experience. Compared to midpalatal sutures, ZMS maturation staging is more complex, requiring observers to perform multi-angle positioning and rotation to determine the optimal assessment perspective. This assessment process is technically demanding and relies on the observer’s experience and subjective judgment, which has restricted its widespread clinical application.

The deep learning model developed in this study performed exceptionally well in assessing ZMS maturation. In two-stage classification tasks (ABC/DE), the model achieved an accuracy of 0.922, comparable to or even surpassing experienced orthodontists. This indicates that the model can effectively distinguish between patients with open sutures (A, B, C stages) and those with partially or fully closed sutures (D, E stages). For clinicians, this distinction holds significant reference value, as patients in stages D and E typically require more complex treatment plans, whereas those in stages A, B, and C can achieve favorable outcomes with maxillary protraction. The model’s visualization feature enables physicians to comprehend its decision-making process, enhancing the interpretability of clinical applications and reducing human error. It provides clinicians with a reliable reference, aiding in the formulation of more precise treatment plans.

The three-stage classification (A/BC/DE) provides a more clinically relevant framework for decision-making. In this classification, stage A corresponds to patients typically aged 6.3–6.5 years on average, who are considered too young for maxillary protraction [51, 52]. Treatment initiated at this stage, which is the transition between deciduous and early mixed dentition, is prone to protraction and increasing complexity, driven by continuing mandibular growth, exfoliation of primary incisors, and variable patient compliance [17]. Stages B and C, on the other hand, involve patients in the mixed dentition period, averaging 7.9–8.5 and 10.9–11.7 years of age, respectively [51, 52]. These stages represent the optimal treatment window where the ZMS remains sufficiently open to respond to orthopedic forces, enabling effective maxillary advancement. By merging Stages B and C into a single category, clinicians can focus on this critical treatment window while differentiating it from the early (A) and late (D and E) stages, thus avoiding overtreatment in very young patients and ensuring timely intervention before the sutures begin to close.

In five-stage classification tasks, the model exhibited varying degrees of discriminative ability across different stages. The AUC values for stages A, D, and E were high (0.95, 0.94, and 0.98, respectively), indicating strong model accuracy in identifying these stages. However, the AUC values for stages B and C were relatively low (0.76 and 0.79, respectively), suggesting weaker model performance in distinguishing transitional stages. Stages B and C both fall under the category of open sutures, with stage B characterized by more active suture remodeling. As a biological process, ZMS maturation does not abruptly transition from one stage to another. Therefore, it is acceptable to anticipate and tolerate discrepancies within a single stage during analysis [25]. Although the model’s ability to distinguish between stages B and C is limited, the clinical significance of this distinction is relatively minor. In stages B and C, maxillary protraction treatment is generally still effective, although the treatment outcome in stage C may be slightly inferior to that in stage B. Stage B represents the optimal therapeutic window, whereas stage C is suboptimal yet clinically acceptable. Thus, despite an overall accuracy of 0.611 in five-stage classification tasks, the model remains clinically valuable, particularly in differentiating between stages C and D. This transition is critical as it may signal a change in treatment plans and prognosis. Once the suture closes to stage D or E, conventional maxillary protraction may no longer yield satisfactory results.

The model’s performance in two-stage and three-stage classification tasks was comparable to that of experienced orthodontists. However, in more complex five-stage classification tasks, the model’s performance was slightly inferior to the two researchers. Orthodontists, with their extensive clinical experience, can make judgments by observing and analyzing subtle features in CBCT images. In contrast, deep learning models learn patterns from limited training data, leading to a gap between model precision and the gold standard in complex tasks. Nevertheless, model performance can be continuously improved through methods such as expanding the dataset, incorporating multi-modal information, and employing semi-supervised learning, which are directions for future research. It is worth noting that as the complexity of classification tasks increases, the consistency between the two researchers also shows a significant downward trend. In contrast, the model demonstrated relatively stable performance across all tasks. This stability gives the model a unique advantage in clinical applications, as it can reduce misdiagnoses caused by subjective differences among evaluators. Particularly in controversial cases, it can provide an objective reference, forming a complementary relationship with clinicians.

It should be noted that this study has certain limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, and the distribution across stages is uneven, which may affect model performance in some classification tasks. Although we have adopted weighted averaging to mitigate the imbalance, future studies should further enlarge the sample and refine its distribution to enhance the model’s generalizability. We plan to achieve this through multi-center collaborations to ethically collect more data, particularly targeting under-represented stages, and by exploring more advanced algorithms designed for imbalanced learning. Second, this study only assessed the maturation of the ZMS and did not combine it with other clinical indicators. Future research could further explore the integration of age, gender, bone density, and other multidimensional information to develop a more comprehensive prediction model. Additionally, the model could be applied to the maturation assessment of other circummaxillary sutures to expand its clinical application scope.

The findings of this study provide new technical support for maxillary protraction in the treatment of skeletal Class III malocclusion. With the assistance of the deep learning model, clinicians can more accurately assess the maturation of the ZMS, thereby selecting the optimal treatment timing. Furthermore, the model can serve as a clinical teaching and training tool, assisting young physicians in rapidly mastering the skills of suture maturation assessment. Through visualization techniques, physicians can gain a more intuitive understanding of the model’s decision-making process, thereby improving their diagnostic abilities. Future research will continue to optimize model performance and explore its applications in a broader range of clinical scenarios.

Conclusion

This study has successfully developed an automated deep learning-based system for ZMS maturation assessment, demonstrating significant advancements through a dual-network architecture where 3D U-Net achieved precise suture localization with an average Euclidean distance of 2.31 ± 1.57 mm and 3D ResNet50 accomplished efficient maturation staging with accuracies of 0.922, 0.833, and 0.611 for two-stage, three-stage, and five-stage classifications respectively. The system completes comprehensive assessments in approximately 46 s, significantly enhancing diagnostic workflow by reducing inter-observer variability and providing reproducible results comparable to or surpassing expert evaluations in key classification tasks. By enabling objective differentiation between open and closed suture stages, this approach provides clinicians with reliable guidance for optimal maxillary protraction timing, effectively avoiding overtreatment in early stages while ensuring timely intervention before sutural closure. The model’s consistent performance across complexity levels and its visualization capabilities further support clinical decision-making and training, establishing a valuable tool for advancing precision orthodontics in skeletal Class III malocclusion management, with future potential for expansion through larger datasets and multimodal data integration.
