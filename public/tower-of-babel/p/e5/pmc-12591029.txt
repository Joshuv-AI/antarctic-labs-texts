PLAYshop Randomized Controlled Trial: Efficacy and Implementation of a Virtually Delivered Parent‐Focused Physical Literacy Intervention for Early Childhood
Authors: Valerie Carson, Morgan Potter, Yeongho Hwang, E Jean Buckler, Madison Boyd, Ryan E Rhodes, Sam Liu, Ramiah Moldenhauer, Joshua Li, Jayleen Hills, Patti‐Jean Naylor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurpose Examine the efficacy of a virtually delivered, parent‐focused physical literacy (PL) intervention for early childhood called PLAYshop and examine the level of and factors that influenced implementation. Methods A randomized controlled trial was conducted with 122 preschool‐aged children (3–5 years) and their parents from western Canada. PLAYshop included a virtual workshop, educational and material resources, and follow‐up support via an app with four booster lessons (1, 3, 5, 7 weeks post‐workshop). Primary and secondary efficacy outcomes were measured at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up. Tertiary efficacy outcomes were also measured post‐workshop (intervention group)/1‐week post baseline questionnaire (control group). Tertiary implementation outcomes were measured via parental questionnaire and explored via parent and workshop leader interviews. Repeated measures Analysis of Covariance/Analysis of Variance and Generalized Estimating Equations were conducted. Results Intervention efficacy was not observed for children's PL (fundamental movement skills, motivation, enjoyment, confidence; primary outcome) or physical activity (PA; secondary outcome). Increases in other secondary and tertiary outcomes were observed in the intervention group, compared to the control group, including parent–child co‐participation in PA (partial η 2 = 0.062), parental PA modeling (partial η 2 = 0.049) as well as parental capability (partial η 2 = 0.039), opportunity (partial η 2 = 0.089), and motivation (partial η 2 = 0.026–0.045) to support children's PL development. High usefulness (87%) and satisfaction (96%) were reported by parents. Several themes were identified supporting the implementation of the intervention. Booster lesson completion was low (17%–30%). Conclusion Efficacy was observed for some parent but not child outcomes. Implementation was highly feasible at the delivery level but parent engagement in boosters had challenges. Introduction

Physical activity (PA) during childhood has numerous immediate and long‐term physical, social–emotional, and mental health benefits [1, 2]. Unfortunately, insufficient PA has been observed in children globally [3]. In Canada, findings based on nationally representative samples with device‐based measures of PA, indicated that 38% of children aged 3–4 years and 51% of children aged 5–11 years were at increased health risk because they did not meet PA recommendations [4, 5]. In the World Health Organization (WHO) global action plan to address the worldwide public health issue of physical inactivity, physical literacy (PL) is identified as a key element [6]. PL is defined by the International PL Association as: “the motivation, confidence, physical competence, knowledge and understanding to value and take responsibility for engagement in physical activities for life” [7]. Canada's PL Consensus Statement breaks down this definition into four elements or components: affective (motivation and confidence), physical (physical competence), cognitive (knowledge and understanding), and behavioral (engagement in physical activities for life) [8]. Enjoyment has also been identified as another important PL element or component [9]. In Cairney and colleagues' evidence‐informed conceptual model, these various PL components interact to influence PA and subsequent health [10]. Consequently, PL is increasingly becoming a health promotion target [10] and PL interventions have grown exponentially since 2014 [11].

PL development occurs throughout life in different stages. Early childhood or the active start stage, in particular the preschool years (3–5 years), has been identified as a critical stage [9, 10, 12]. Specifically, PL development during this time period is foundational in supporting PA, including sport participation, and subsequent health benefits throughout childhood and beyond [9, 10, 12]. To date, few studies have explored interventions that target PL in early childhood. In one systematic review, researchers identified only four PL interventions, all within the childcare setting, that focused specifically on preschool‐aged children (3–5 years) [11]. The content of all four interventions was found to lack extensive links with the various PL components, defined in the article as physical competence, motivation and confidence, and knowledge and understanding [11]. Similarly, in another review on PA and PL interventions in the childcare setting, all but one PL intervention exclusively focused on physical competence [13]. Additionally, researchers have often only measured fundamental movement skills (FMS), a sub‐component of physical competence, to assess PL in these interventions [11, 13, 14]. Holistically, measuring PL in this age group is challenging. For instance, researchers have argued it is not developmentally appropriate to measure the cognitive component of PL (i.e., knowledge regarding the importance of PA), given the cognitive capacity of young children [14]. Furthermore, tools to measure PL sub‐components motivation, enjoyment, and confidence in preschool‐aged children are limited [14].

In addition to limitations related to PL intervention content and PL measurement in early childhood, there are also some major gaps. For instance, despite the various key facilitators in the development of PL in early childhood, including parents and caregivers, early childhood educators, and sport and recreation coaches and instructors [9], PL interventions have heavily focused on the childcare setting only [11, 13]. In line with PA evidence [15, 16], parents can play a key role in supporting PL development, in particular in this age group, where parents have the largest influence on children's development [17]. Therefore, parent‐focused PL interventions in preschool‐aged children are needed. PLAYshop, a novel, brief, theory‐based, and parent‐focused PL intervention for early childhood, was developed to fill this important evidence gap. The theoretical frame for PLAYshop is the Capability‐Opportunity‐Motivation = Behavior (COM‐B) model [18]. COM‐B was designed from an amalgamation of 19 frameworks distilled to the essential conditions responsible for behavior change [18]. Within the model, capability includes physical (e.g., skills) and psychological (e.g., knowledge) capability, opportunity includes physical (e.g., resources) and social (e.g., norms) opportunity, and motivation includes automatic (e.g., reinforcement) and reflective (e.g., beliefs about capabilities) motivation [18].

PLAYshop has undergone extensive pilot and feasibility testing [19, 20, 21]. In the Carl and colleagues systematic review, previously mentioned [11], the first iteration of PLAYshop [20] was defined as “theory‐driven” as PLAYshop intervention content aimed at parents purposefully addresses physical competence, motivation and confidence, and knowledge and understanding. Due to the COVID‐19 pandemic, PLAYshop was converted to be delivered virtually and a virtual protocol for measuring FMS was developed and tested [21]. In terms of feasibility, most parents have reported that the intervention was very/extremely useful (82%–84%) and were satisfied/extremely satisfied with its content (93%–96%) and delivery (94%–96%), regardless of whether PLAYshop was delivered in‐person or virtual [20, 21]. Furthermore, pilot findings suggested PLAYshop, whether delivered in‐person or virtual was efficacious for increasing parental capability (i.e., knowledge), opportunity (i.e., perceived availability of resources), and motivation (i.e., confidence) to support children's PL development [19, 20, 21]. Furthermore, the virtual FMS measurement protocol was found to be feasible, and preliminary positive changes were observed (i.e., increase in children's hopping skills) [21]. Building on this pilot and feasibility work, important next steps are to examine the efficacy of the virtually delivered PLAYshop intervention on holistic child PL outcomes as well as examine the efficacy of parent outcomes using an adequately powered randomized controlled trial (RCT).

The primary objective of this study was to examine if the virtually delivered PLAYshop intervention increased preschool‐aged children's PL, including FMS, motivation, enjoyment, and confidence over 2 months, compared to controls. The secondary objective was to examine if the virtually delivered PLAYshop intervention increased preschool‐aged children's PA, parent–child co‐participation in PA, and parental PA modeling over 2 months, compared to controls. The tertiary objectives were to examine: (1) if the virtually delivered PLAYshop intervention increased parental capability, opportunity, and motivation to support preschool‐aged children's PL development over 2 months, compared to controls and (2) the level of implementation and factors that influenced implementation at the parent and program delivery level.

It was hypothesized that increases in preschool‐aged children's PL, PA, and parent–child co‐participation in PA and parental PA modeling would be greater in the intervention group, compared to the control group. Additionally, it was hypothesized that increases in parents' capability, opportunity, and motivation to support preschool‐aged children's PL development would be greater in the intervention group, compared to the control group.

Materials and Methods

A detailed description of study methods has been previously reported [22, 23]. This trial is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) Framework [24]. The trial was registered with the Clinical Trials Registry maintained at the National Library of Medicine at the National Institutes of Health (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05255250).

Trial Design

Study objectives were addressed with a single‐blind, parallel group, two‐arm, superiority RCT design. After baseline measures were completed, parent–child dyads were assigned by research coordinators to the intervention group (Group 1) or control group (Group 2) using a computer‐generated 1:1 sequence. It was considered a single‐blind trial because participants were not told whether they were assigned to the intervention or control group but rather whether they were assigned to Group 1 or Group 2. However, participants may have been able to determine whether they were in the intervention or control group based on whether they received the intervention immediately or after completing study measures. It was not considered a double‐blind trial as research coordinators were aware of the group allocations as a part of organizing random assignment and scheduling participants for intervention components and measures. Additionally, though research assistants scoring FMS were not told group assignment, the same research assistants helped with data collection and during scoring they may have recalled from a previous data collection session whether the family participated in the intervention or control group procedures.

Participants

The inclusion criteria for families to participate in the trial were having a child aged 3–5 years and living in a population centre of at least 1000 people within the provinces of Alberta and British Columbia [25]. The inclusion criterion ensured there would be adequate internet bandwidth to deliver the virtual intervention. In instances where families had more than one eligible child (n = 11), all children were invited to participate and data from one child was randomly selected to be included in the analysis. The trial exclusion criteria for families were: (1) previous participation in the PLAYshop pilot or feasibility study, (2) having a child who was experiencing a developmental delay or disorder/condition that may affect gross motor development or limit their ability to be physically active, (3) not comfortably speaking or reading English, and (4) not having access to a smartphone/tablet with a camera and microphone. Participants were primarily recruited through targeted paid social media advertising (95%). Other sources of recruitment were word of mouth (2%) and referral from another unrelated laboratory study (2%), and a few participants (1%) did not remember how they heard about the study. Research staff who led a PLAYshop workshop, as described in the next section, were also recruited. Recruitment took place from February, 2022 to January, 2023 and follow‐up continued until August, 2023. The study was approved by the University of Alberta (00093764) and University of Victoria (16–444) Research Ethics Boards. Written informed consent was obtained from parents and workshop leaders. Parents and workshop leaders also provided verbal consent prior to audio and video recordings (parents only) as outlined in the procedures section. All data was collected virtually and data collection was coordinated from the University of Alberta site.

Intervention Condition

The overall aim of PLAYshop is to build parental capability (i.e., knowledge), opportunity (i.e., perceived availability of resources), and motivation (i.e., confidence) to support their children in developing PL and acquiring PA through purposeful play. To achieve this aim, PLAYshop includes four intervention strategies. A detailed description of the implementation strategies, how they align with COM‐B, and the behavior change techniques utilized have been previously published [19, 21]. Briefly, the first and main strategy was educational training that occurred via a 60‐min virtual synchronous workshop, reduced from 75 min when previously delivered in‐person. It was delivered by trained workshop leaders (i.e., research staff). The workshop included fun interactive play activities for parents and children to engage in. Play activities were designed to support the development of children's FMS, including their locomotor, manipulative, and balance skills along with supporting their motivation and confidence. Between activities, educational messages embedded within child‐level PL components were delivered to parents. The specific messages and their links with child‐level PL components have been previously published [19]. As one example, a specific message that linked to children's motivation, enjoyment, and confidence was the importance of choice. Specifically, parents were encouraged to follow along with the interests of their child and let them dictate the challenge level of the activity or game to align with what the child was most comfortable with [19]. The workshop was originally designed by a research team member in partnership with a community PL organization using evidence‐based behavior change techniques [18], adult education/training practices, and recommendations from PL experts [8, 9]. The second intervention strategy was the distribution of educational resources via hardcopy handouts, including the Canadian 24‐h Movement Guidelines [26, 27] and additional activity ideas. The third intervention strategy was providing material resources via a goody bag of basic inexpensive active play equipment. The educational and material resources were shipped to families prior to the workshop, and families were asked not to open the materials until the workshop. The equipment was used during the interactive play activities in the workshop. The fourth strategy was providing follow‐up support post‐workshop via access to a digital app. The app was built using a no‐code app builder platform called Pathverse [28], and included an online toolkit and four booster lessons. The booster lessons were released bi‐weekly and parents were notified about them via text message notifications.

Control Condition

After follow‐up measures were complete, the control group received access to all four PLAYshop intervention strategies, except the four bi‐weekly boosters, which served a dual purpose of both enhancing and measuring dose in the intervention group.

Procedures

A flow chart of study components and timelines as well as details on outcome measures has been previously published [23]. Briefly, baseline measures were first completed for all interested and eligible participants. Prior to baseline data collection, an online consent form was completed by parents. After baseline data collection, participants were randomly assigned to intervention (Group 1) or control (Group 2). Intervention participants were scheduled into a virtual workshop via Zoom within 2 weeks of their baseline data collection, with the exception of one participant where the workshop was within 3 weeks. Workshops ranged from 1 to 8 participants. Parents completed a questionnaire at baseline, post‐workshop (intervention group)/1‐week post baseline questionnaire (control group), and at 2‐month follow‐up. Additionally, accelerometers were mailed to families for the preschool‐aged child and the designated parent to wear on a belt around their waist during waking hours for seven consecutive days at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up. The designated parent was defined as the parent who spent the most time with their child during play activities. To encourage children's accelerometer compliance, at both time points, parents were given seven stickers, a sticker chart to mark accelerometer wear each day, and a small toy for the end of the 7 days. Parent–child dyads also participated in a recorded virtual assessment session at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up. Research assistants tried to schedule 2‐month follow‐up assessments within a window of −5 to +10 days. Four intervention group and four control participants had their recorded virtual assessment session past +10 days and two intervention participants wore their accelerometers past the +10 days. For the intervention group only, parents were sent bi‐weekly (i.e., 1, 3, 5, 7 weeks post workshop) booster lessons via the app that included brief check‐in questions. Additionally, at the end of the 2‐month virtual session brief semi‐structured interviews were conducted with parents. The control group was scheduled into a workshop or emailed a workshop recording (n = 17) after completing all study measures. Post‐workshop, they were also given access to the app. Booster lessons with the check‐in questions were not sent to the control group. Finally, workshop leaders completed brief semi‐structured interviews after all study workshops were completed. Specific procedures for the primary, secondary, and tertiary measures are described in the next section.

Measures

Primary Efficacy Outcome

The primary efficacy outcome was preschool‐aged children's PL, including FMS, motivation, enjoyment, and confidence sub‐components measured at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up. Given a lack of existing tools to holistically assess PL in early childhood, especially in the home setting, and the challenges of in‐person data collection during COVID‐19, a virtual protocol was developed to assess the multiple sub‐components of children's PL using a combination of existing tools [14, 29, 30, 31]. The cognitive PL component (e.g., knowledge regarding the importance of PA), was not assessed in children due to the children's age and development stage [14]. As noted in the introduction, pilot testing found that the virtual protocol for assessing children's FMS was feasible. Specifically, high completion rates (> 90%) and inter‐rater reliability scores (Intra‐class correlation coefficients (ICC) = 0.79–0.99) were observed [21]. Five FMS were assessed during the virtual assessment session, including two manipulative/ball skills (i.e., overhand throw, underhand throw) and two locomotor skills (i.e., horizontal jump, hop) from the Test of Gross Motor Development—Third Edition (TGMD‐3) tool [31] as well as one balance/stability skill (i.e., one‐leg balance) from the Movement Assessment Battery for Children‐Second Edition (MABC‐2) tool [29]. A composite of children's motivation, enjoyment, and confidence for active play was assessed in the parental questionnaire using items from the Preschool PL Assessment (PrePLAy) [14]. Children's enjoyment for active play was also assessed via self‐report using an adapted Five Degrees of Happiness Likert scale for children [30].

Due to the virtual assessment, it was not possible to do the full battery of FMS from these tools. The five skills were selected due to the minimal space and equipment required to perform the skill, their alignment with the FMS covered in the PLAYshop workshop, and their established psychometric properties. For instance, a recent systematic review found high‐quality evidence that the TGMD‐3 is reliable, in terms of test re‐test, inter‐rater reliability, and intra‐rater reliability [32]. Similar findings were observed in another systematic review that examined individual skills [33]. The one‐leg balance test has been found to be the most reliable balance test in preschool children across studies, with test re‐test scores for the MABC‐2 ranging from an intra‐class correlation (ICC) of 0.85–0.97 in previous studies [34, 35]. Additionally, in a previous study of preschool‐aged children, the selected TGMD‐3 skills had significant correlations with total motor skills and large effect sizes (0.5–0.7) were observed [36, 37].

At the virtual assessment session, participants watched a pre‐recorded treasure hunt adventure video, which included a demonstration of each skill. Guided by a research assistant leading the virtual assessment session, after each video demonstration of the skill, parents then prepared the space for children to perform the skill (e.g., setting up markers, double‐checking the area was safe, ensuring they had the correct camera angle). Adult balled‐up socks were used for the ball/manipulative skills. Parents used their smartphone, tablet, or laptop that the virtual assessment session was occurring on to film the skills. A research assistant later scored the skills from the recorded video. The virtual assessment protocol and scoring aligned with the TGMD‐3 and MAB‐C protocols [29, 31]. Specifically, for the locomotor and ball/manipulative skills, children performed one practice trial and two test trials and parents were instructed not to provide any assistance to children, whether verbally or through demonstration, during the assessment [31]. Scores for each skill ranged from 0 to 8, with higher scores indicating more advanced FMS. For the balance/stability skill, children completed one practice trial and one timed test trial on each leg to a maximum of 30 s [29]. The final score was the longest time on either leg, with a longer score indicating more advanced FMS [29]. For the present study, the inter‐rater reliability for 10% of baseline and follow‐up TGMD‐3 scores (n = 26) ranged from an ICC of 0.88–0.96. However, the internal consistency reliability of TGMD‐3 scores was below α = 0.6 at baseline (α = 0.58) and follow‐up (α = 0.52). In terms of convergent validity, in a previous baseline study of PLAYshop, total PA (r s = 0.20–0.24) and age (r s = 0.27–0.64) were significantly associated with all FMS, with the exception of total PA and horizontal jump [38].

The Pre‐PLAy tool was selected because it is the only tool that includes measures of motivation, enjoyment, and confidence PL sub‐components in preschool‐aged children [14]. Pre‐PLAy includes four items, including one item for perceived motivation, one item for enjoyment, and two items for confidence [14]. There were five response options for each item, ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree [14]. In line with the Pre‐PLAy tool, motivation, enjoyment, and confidence were summed as a composite score, after reverse scoring one of the confidence items, with higher scores indicating greater perceived children's motivation/enjoyment/confidence during active play. This tool was originally developed to be completed by early childhood educators and psychometric properties have previously been reported for this population, including internal consistency reliability (α = 0.84), test re‐test reliability (ICC = 0.47–0.74), and convergent validity (significantly associated with accelerometer‐derived moderate‐ to vigorous‐PA (MVPA) in girls [B = 0.32; p < 0.01] but not boys [B = 0.04; p = 0.63]) [14]. In terms of the present study, where parents completed the four items about their child, the internal consistency reliability was α = 0.73 at baseline and α = 0.55 at follow‐up, When dropping the reserved score confidence item, internal consistency reliability, was similar at baseline (α = 0.72) and improved at follow‐up (α = 0.62). Furthermore, participants also completed this scale post‐workshop (intervention group)/1‐week post baseline questionnaire (control group), to assess 1–2‐week test re‐test reliability, which was ICC = 0.74 (four items) or 0.70 (three items). Finally, in terms of convergent validity, in a previous baseline study of PLAYshop, accelerometer‐derived MVPA was significantly associated with the composite motivation/enjoyment/confidence scale (r s = 0.22) [38].

The adapted Five Degrees of Happiness Likert scale was chosen because face scales have been used with children as young as 3 years old in other fields (e.g., pediatric pain field) [39, 40]. The scale included one item with five smiley face response options that ranged from a slightly happy face (active play is okay but I'd rather be doing something else) to a very happy face (really love being active and it is my favorite thing to do), with higher scores indicating greater enjoyment during active play [30]. At the virtual assessment session, children were asked to point to the face that looks most like them when doing active play like running, jumping, throwing, chasing, or dancing. Research assistants explained the response options to children. This particular scale was adapted from a research study in 9–11 year‐olds that observed within and between variance among children, with all response options being used due to positive emotions being displayed for all response options [30]. The research team adapted the description of faces and the question to make it relevant for active play and preschool‐aged children. In terms of convergent validity, in a previous baseline study of PLAYshop, no significant associations were observed with PA or age and this self‐reported Likert scale.

Secondary Efficacy Outcomes

The secondary efficacy outcomes were children's PA, parental PA modeling, and parent–child co‐participation in PA measured at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up. Children's light‐intensity PA (LPA), MVPA, and total PA (TPA) were measured with Actigraph wGT3X‐BT accelerometers. The data were downloaded in 15‐s epochs to capture the sporadic and intermittent activity patterns of children [41]. Non‐wear time was defined as ≥ 80 consecutive 15‐s intervals of zero counts (equivalent to ≥ 20 min of consecutive zero counts) [42]. It was assumed that any daytime naps were removed with non‐wear time. To be included in the analyses, participants were required to have ≥ 3 days with ≥ 1440 total 15‐s intervals (equivalent to ≥ 6 h) of wear time. This valid data definition maximized the sample size along with reliability [43, 44]. Validated cut‐points for preschoolers were used to classify counts into LPA (26–419 counts per 15 s) and MVPA (≥ 420 counts per 15 s) [45]. Minutes per day were calculated. TPA was calculated by adding minutes per day of LPA and MVPA. Finally, to adjust for wear time, standardized PA variables were calculated [46].

Parental PA modeling was measured via Actigraph wGT3X‐BT accelerometers (proxy measure) and parental questionnaire. For the accelerometer measures, parental PA modeling was defined as average minutes per day of parents' LPA, MVPA, and TPA. Of note, in error only the questionnaire measure of parental PA modeling was registered a priori [23]. Parent data were also downloaded in 15‐s epochs to capture the sporadic and intermittent activity patterns of children they care for [41]. Non‐wear time was defined as ≥ 240 consecutive 15‐s intervals of zero counts (equivalent to ≥ 60 min of consecutive zero counts), allowing for ≤ 8 consecutive 15‐s counts (equivalent to ≤ 2 min) between 0 and 24 [47]. To be included in the analyses, participants were required to have ≥ 3 days with ≥ 2440 total 15‐s intervals (equivalent to ≥ 10 h) of wear time [48]. Validated cut‐points for adults were used to classify counts into LPA (25–504 counts per 15 s) and MVPA (≥ 505 counts per 15 s) [47]. Minutes per day were calculated. TPA was calculated by adding minutes per day of LPA and MVPA. Standardized PA variables were also calculated [46].

For the questionnaire measure, there were three items asking parents to describe themselves (e.g., “I encourage my child to be physically active by leading by example [by role modeling]”) with four response options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The scores for the three items were summed, with higher summary scores indicating higher parental PA modeling. The items were from the Activity Support Scale for Multiple Groups (ACTS‐MG) [49]. Internal consistency reliability at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up was α = 0.84 and α = 0.88, respectively. Participants also completed this scale post‐workshop (intervention group)/1‐week post baseline questionnaire (control group) to assess 1–2‐week test‐retest reliability, which was ICC = 0.84. Parental MVPA modeling was significantly correlated with the questionnaire measure at 2‐month follow‐up (r = 0.28) but significant correlations were not observed for parental LPA or TPA modeling at baseline.

Parent–child co‐participation in PA was measured via Actigraph wGT3X‐BT accelerometers and parental questionnaire. For the accelerometer measure, it was defined as the average minutes per day parents and children were both engaged in MVPA (co‐MVPA) or both engaged in TPA (co‐TPA) in close proximity. The Bluetooth proximity detection feature was set so parents' accelerometers were beacons and children's accelerometers were receivers. Children's accelerometers recorded whether a signal was detected (i.e., close proximity) from the continuously emitted Bluetooth signal of the parents' accelerometers once per minute [50, 51]. Data were coded as 1 = with when a signal was detected and 0 = away if a signal was not detected for each minute. In line with previous work [50, 51], rolling 5‐min averages were calculated for each 1‐min epoch and if the average equaled > 0 then that epoch was reclassified as with and if the average equaled 0 then the epoch was reclassified as away. It was reported in previous research that this method had 82% sensitivity and 81% specificity for determining the presence of close proximity between parents and preschoolers using this feature when a 5‐min window was used [50]. Standardized co‐participation variables were also calculated to adjust for wear time [46].

For the questionnaire measure of parent–child co‐participation in PA there were four items asking about the frequency of parent–child co‐participation in PA over the past month (e.g., In the past month, how often have you participated in any active play with your child?). Items were adapted from an expert‐informed concept mapping analysis of parenting practices for 5–12‐year‐olds [52]. Response options ranged from 1 (never) to 5 (4 or more times per week) and scores for the four items were summed, with higher summary scores indicating more frequent parent–child co‐participation in PA. Internal consistency reliability at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up was α = 0.79 and α = 0.78, respectively. Participants also completed this scale post‐workshop (intervention group)/1‐week post baseline questionnaire (control group), to assess 1–2‐week test–retest reliability, which was ICC = 0.78. The questionnaire and device‐based measures of parent–child co‐participation were not significantly correlated.

Tertiary Efficacy Outcomes

The tertiary efficacy outcomes were parental capability (i.e., knowledge), opportunity (i.e., perceived availability of resources, perceived barriers), and motivation (i.e., confidence, beliefs, outcome expectations, intentions, perceived behavioral control) to support preschool‐aged children's PL development. Parental capability, opportunity, and motivation were measured in the parental questionnaire at baseline, post‐workshop (intervention group)/1‐week post baseline questionnaire (control group), and at 2‐month follow‐up.

For parental capability, the knowledge measure included 9 items (e.g., locomotor skills), with five response options ranging from 1 (no knowledge) to 5 (a lot of knowledge). Internal consistency was α = 0.95, α = 0.93 and α = 0.94 at the three time points. For opportunity, the perceived availability of resource measure had one item (i.e., Do you feel you have the resources (e.g., information, equipment, space, etc.) you need to promote PA and PL for your preschool child?), with five response options ranging from 1 (Yes, I have all the resources I need) to 5 (No, I don't have all the resources I need). This item was reverse scored. The perceived barriers measures had five items (e.g., My child is not able to participate in some activities because they are too expensive), with five response options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Internal consistency was α = 0.51, α = 0.39, and α = 0.46 at the three time points, so these items were analyzed separately. For motivation, the confidence measure included 11 items (e.g., If I wanted to, I am confident in my ability to provide activities to my preschool child that promote locomotor skills), with five response options ranging from 1 (no confidence) to 5 (a lot of confidence). Internal consistency was α = 0.95 at each of the three time points. The beliefs measure included four items (e.g., Children who do regular PA learn better), with five response options, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Internal consistency was α = 0.84, α = 0.89, and α = 0.90 at the three time points. The outcome expectations measure in relation to doing activities that promote PA and PL with their preschool‐aged child included 3 items (e.g., It would enhance my connection with my child), with five response options, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Internal consistency was α = 0.77, α = 0.75, and α = 0.82 at the three time points. The intention measure included two items (e.g., In the next 2 months, I intend to do PA and PL activities with my preschool child), with five response options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Internal consistency was α = 0.65, α = 0.80, and α = 0.72 at the three time points. Finally, the perceived behavioral control measure had four items (e.g., In the next 2 months, doing PA and PL activities is under my personal control) with five response options ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Internal consistency was α = 0.48, α = 0.55, and α = 0.48 at the three time points so these items were analyzed separately. Scores for each scale were summed, except for barriers and perceived behavioral control, with higher scores indicating higher capability, opportunity, and motivation to support preschool‐aged children's PL development. These items were adopted from previous research (i.e., perceived barriers, beliefs, outcome expectations, intentions) [53, 54] or developed specifically for PLAYshop (i.e., knowledge, perceived availability of resources, confidence, perceived behavioral control) [19, 20, 21].

Tertiary Implementation Outcomes

The tertiary implementation outcomes (intervention group only) were parental satisfaction and perceived usefulness, facilitators and barriers, and dose. Parental satisfaction and perceived usefulness were measured in the parental questionnaire post‐workshop and explored via brief semi‐structured interviews with parents at the 2‐month follow‐up. Facilitators and barriers were also explored via brief semi‐structured interviews with parents at the 2‐month follow‐up and with workshop leaders after all study workshops were completed. Finally, dose was explored via: (1) brief semi‐structured interviews with parents at the 2‐month follow‐up, (2) via check‐in questions as part of the bi‐weekly booster lessons, and (3) by app usage data across the 2‐month follow‐up.

The questionnaire measures of parental satisfaction and perceived usefulness included three items (i.e., rate your overall level of satisfaction with the workshop content, rate your overall level of satisfaction with the workshop delivery, how useful do you feel this training will be to you?) with five response options, ranging from 1 (not satisfied or not useful) to 5 (extremely satisfied or extremely useful) that were used in previous PLAYshop work [20, 21]. In terms of dose, the main check‐in question at each booster lesson asked parents how many active play sessions they have done with their child in the past 2 weeks, and participants could select a value from 0 (none) to 20 (20 or more). App usage data included when parents accessed the app, booster lessons, and resources.

Semi‐structured interviews with parents and workshop leaders were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim using a web‐based transcription program (Digital Anarchy Inc., San Francisco, CA, USA). Participants were then given access to their interview transcripts and were asked to verify the accuracy of the transcription. Open‐ended interview questions for parents focused on the application of workshop learnings, intentions, barriers, and facilitators to continuing with PL‐related active play, and thoughts on the resources provided. Open‐ended interview questions for workshop leaders focused on barriers and facilitators to implementation and potential improvements.

Other Measures

Participant characteristics of the child including, age (months) sex (male, female), ethnicity (White, other), number of siblings (0, 1, ≥ 2), non‐parental care time, started elementary/primary school (yes, no) were collected in the baseline questionnaire. Participant characteristics of the parent including, age (years), sex assigned at birth (male or female), gender identity (male, female, something else), marital status (married/common law, other), born in Canada (yes, no), highest level of education (Above Bachelor's degree, Bachelor's degree, Below Bachelor's degree), and previous PL‐related training (yes, no) were also collected in the baseline questionnaire. Some response options were collapsed due to frequency distributions. Additionally, to protect confidentiality, data for parental sex and gender identity were not presented because some cell sizes were below 5. The questions have previously been used in PLAYshop and are adopted from Statistics Canada or previous research [55, 56].

Weather Characteristics, including mean temperature and mean precipitation were calculated at baseline and 2‐month follow‐up for each parent–child dyad using publicly available data from Environment Canada. Specifically, the mean daily temperature (i.e., average of the maximum and minimum temperature during a day) and daily total precipitation at the weather stations nearest to the participant's municipality, with complete data, across the 7 days of accelerometer wear were calculated. The 7 days was based on the first day that the parent–child dyads started wearing the accelerometer.

Statistical Analyses and Sample Size

Details for the statistical analysis and sample size calculation of this trial have been previously published [23]. The trial was powered to detect a medium effect size f = 0.31 (approximately d = 0.60), which was estimated from a previous childcare PA intervention in preschoolers where FMS was a secondary outcome [57]. The number of participants needed to detect this effect size with 80% power at a probability of 0.05 for an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) interaction was 100 [58, 59]. We oversampled by approximately 30% to account for missing data based on previous parent–child accelerometer research [51].

Quantitative data analyses were performed using SPSS Version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and statistical significance was defined a priori as p < 0.05. The patterns of missingness in the analytic sample were examined and assumed to follow the Missing at Random (MAR) mechanism [60, 61]. The missing values were subsequently estimated using the Expectation–Maximization (EM) technique (baseline outcome variables: n = 1–11; baseline participant characteristics: n = 1; baseline weather: n = 1; follow‐up outcome variables: n = 2–27; follow‐up weather: n = 10) [62]. Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize participant characteristics and weather data. Baseline variables (participant characteristics and outcomes) were compared between the adherers and dropouts and between intervention and control groups using Independent T‐Test/Mann–Whitney test (continuous variables) or Pearson Chi‐Square test/Fisher's Exact Test/Fisher–Freeman–Halton Exact Test (categorical variables).

To address primary, secondary and the first tertiary objectives, intention‐to‐treat analyses were conducted where the nine participants who dropped out after baseline remained in their randomized group and Expectation–Maximization (EM) technique was used to estimate their follow‐up value [62]. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted using a complete case analysis. Assumptions underlying the statistical tests were assessed prior to the analyses. For the primary outcome (children's PL), repeated measures (RM) multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was initially planned [23]. However, the assumptions required for RM MANCOVA were not met (i.e., multivariate normality, moderate correlations between outcome variables). Given the potential for invalid results of RM MANCOVA under these conditions, RM Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) for each individual PL sub‐component was conducted. As well RM ANCOVA or RM Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) were conducted for secondary and relevant tertiary outcomes (i.e., capability, opportunity, motivation). For the tertiary efficacy outcome variables collected over three time points, time was modeled as a within‐subjects factor with three levels, and omnibus group × time interactions were tested. In addition, planned within‐subject contrasts were conducted to compare differences between time points 1 and 2, as well as time points 1 and 3. The normality assumption was visually assessed using Q‐Q plots and histograms. This assumption was satisfied for all outcome variables except for the five FMS variables, which were consequently analyzed using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEEs). The FMS variables were analyzed separately instead of a composite score because of the low internal consistency reliability reported in the measures section. The Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied where the assumption of sphericity was violated for RM ANCOVAs. Effect sizes were estimated for both GEEs and RM AN(C)OVAs: Cohen's d for GEEs and partial η 2 for RM AN(C)OVAs. Cohen's d was interpreted as small (|d| = 0.20), medium (|d| = 0.50), and large (|d| = 0.80) [37, 63]. Partial η 2 were interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14) [64].

To address the second tertiary objective, descriptive analyses were performed for the perceived usefulness and satisfaction as well as for app data on dose. Additionally, qualitative data analysis was performed using NVivo software (QSR International Pty Ltd., Burlington, MA, USA) following an inductive framework approach for analyzing qualitative data in health research [65]. After thorough familiarization with the interview transcripts, one research staff created a preliminary set of codes and an analytic framework that were reviewed and agreed upon by multiple members of the research team. Two research staff independently coded a random sample of 25% of the parent interviews resulting in 95.5% agreement between the two coders' responses. The same framework was then used to code the remaining parent interviews by one research staff. Qualitative data was then summarized into a matrix to assist with interpretations. The research team collectively worked towards a consensus regarding coding categorizations and interpretations. Finally, one research team member who was not involved in the delivery of workshops independently coded and summarized the workshop leader interviews into the main themes.

Results

A total of 408 families expressed interest in receiving more information regarding the PLAYshop study via an online REDCap link [66] or email. Of those, 151 families were interested in participating and moved forward with eligibility screening. A participant flow chart is provided in Figure 1 for the primary research objective. Briefly, of the 151 families assessed for eligibility, 17 were excluded because they were ineligible, three declined participation, and eight were excluded for other reasons, resulting in 123 families being randomized (intervention = 61; control = 62). After baseline assessments, 9 participants were lost to follow‐up/discontinued the intervention/did not receive allocated intervention (intervention = 4; control = 5). However, these participants were maintained in the analytic sample with the intention‐to‐treat analysis. Marital status was the only baseline variable that showed a significant difference between adherers (n = 114) and drop‐outs (n = 9), with a higher percentage of the “other” group dropping out (n = 3; 23%) compared to the “married/common law” group (n = 6; 6%). One participant was excluded from the analytic sample due to missing data for child sex. Since the family chose not to answer this question, the missing value was not imputed using the Expectation–Maximization (EM) technique. As a result, the full analytic sample for the primary research objective was 122 (intervention = 60; control = 62). An accelerometer sub‐sample (n = 115; intervention = 58; control = 57) was also created to address the secondary research objectives involving accelerometer data. Seven participants were missing child accelerometer data and/or parent accelerometer data at both time points. Finally, for the qualitative analysis, 57 participants from the intervention group participated in the 2‐month follow‐up interviews; of those, 41 responded back confirming the accuracy of the transcripts, and 16 did not reply to the request to verify the transcription. All four workshop leaders participated in end‐of‐study interviews.

Under the category of parent satisfaction and usefulness themes were: increased content knowledge and confidence, connection with child, fun with child, skill and confidence growth (child), and positive and negative feedback on electronic and paper resources. Overall, parents valued the information that the workshop provided, with several parents mentioning increases in their own confidence and knowledge base (25% of parents). Some parents also valued the connection (33% of parents) and fun nature (26% of parents) of the PL‐related active play, and reported connecting with their children in new ways, learning more about their child's interests and abilities, and bonding. Additionally, some parents reported seeing notable changes in their children's skill and confidence levels, both in relation to workshop‐specific games and other PL‐related active play (30% of parents). Finally, resource preference varied, with approximately half of participants having positive comments regarding the electronic or app‐based content (47% of families) and paper‐based resources (56% of families), such as the app being user‐friendly and the paper‐based resources being easier to share with family members. However, some families found these resources less useful (electronic: 14%; paper: 5%), such as forgetting to use the app versus others misplacing paper resources. Specifically, while 77% of parents mentioned using the app, 30% of these mentioned only opening the app initially.

Within the category of intervention dose (parent implementation) themes were manipulative skill games, locomotor skill games, balance stability games, and understanding and use of key workshop messages, which also had five subthemes: game modifications/adaptations, easy/simple equipment, fundamental movement skills, confidence and motivation, and outdoor play. Almost all parents (98%) shared ways that they have incorporated PL‐related active play, since the workshop, with 91% of parents referring specifically to manipulative skill games, followed by locomotor (72% of parents), and balance (32% of parents). Many parents retained key workshop messaging around PL‐related active play. This was evident as they mentioned workshop take‐away messaging unprompted during the interview.

The top three facilitators and barriers to implementing PL‐related active play are the representative themes for the facilitators and barriers category. The top three facilitators were: the PLAYshop app, weather, and how easy the PLAYshop resources and activities were to implement. The top three barriers were: time/busy schedules, child mood or lack of motivation, and weather. Weather/season was both a commonly mentioned facilitator (26% of families) and barrier (35% of families). Some parents felt that warmer summer weather facilitated more PL‐related active play because the time spent in outdoor play was greater, more time at parks, and more free play for their children. However, in other cases parents felt that PL‐related active play was easier in the winter because with the increased indoor time due to unpleasant weather they were able to focus on the PLAYshop games and activities.

Although not related to a specific outcome of our study, it is important to note in terms of sustainability, the majority of parents (51/57, 89%) expressed intent to continue PL‐related active play with their child in the future, and 77% (40/52) of those families demonstrated certainty in their response using phrases such as “100%,” “very likely,” or “definitely.”

Workshop leader qualitative themes, key quotes, and the number of participants who raised the issues are outlined in Table 7. For the workshop leaders, four facilitators to workshop success were identified: goody bag, interactivity, parent response, increased access with virtual delivery; and two barriers were identified: technology challenges and unknown implementation. Although the virtual delivery facilitated greater access, challenges related to technology, including poor internet connections and parents keeping their video cameras turned off were perceived as barriers. In particular when the camera was turned off, workshop leaders were unsure if parents were understanding the material presented. In terms of unknown implementation, this was mentioned by one workshop leader and did not pertain to the workshop delivery itself but to their own uncertainty about what parents did after the workshop.

Discussion

The objectives of this study were to examine the efficacy of the PLAYshop intervention and the level of implementation and factors that influenced implementation of the intervention at the parent and delivery levels. To our knowledge, PLAYshop is the first parent‐focused PL intervention in preschool‐aged children. Across settings, it has been identified as the only PL intervention in this age group that purposefully addresses physical competence, motivation and confidence, and knowledge and understanding [11]. The present study built on previous PLAYshop pilot and feasibility studies by using an adequately powered RCT design and including child outcomes. Despite the intervention being well‐received and efficacious at changing parent‐level secondary and tertiary outcomes it did not have an impact on child‐level outcomes, including the primary outcome of PL. In summary, increases in parent–child co‐participation in PA (questionnaire‐only) and parental PA modeling (accelerometer‐derived MVPA only) were observed in the intervention group, compared to the control group. The intervention group also had increases in several tertiary outcomes, including parental capability (i.e., parental knowledge), opportunity (i.e., parental perceived availability of resources), and motivation (i.e., parental confidence, beliefs, outcome expectations, intentions, perceived behavioral control [general control, time consuming]) to support children's PL development. In terms of implementation outcomes at the parent level, PLAYshop had high acceptability among intervention parents; however, parental engagement with the app and related booster lessons was challenging. At the delivery level, implementation of the PLAYshop intervention was highly feasible. These findings are discussed more extensively in the following paragraphs.

In terms of the primary outcome, in contrast to what was hypothesized, the efficacy of the intervention was not observed for preschool‐aged children's PL. There are several potential reasons for this finding. For instance, the dose of the intervention may have been too low to influence changes at the child‐level. PLAYshop was specifically designed as a brief intervention (i.e., one workshop and boosters) addressing the parental barrier of lack of time observed in previous PA research [19, 67]. However, active play sessions with their children still required time and consistent with previous PLAYshop studies [19, 20], time was identified as a barrier to parent implementation of PL‐related active play in the present study. Findings of the present study suggest the workshop was feasible, acceptable, and effective at the level of the parent. For instance, high parental satisfaction and perceived usefulness of the workshop were observed with both quantitative and qualitative data and many parents had unprompted recall of workshop PL concepts during the 2‐month follow‐up interviews. Previous parent feedback during piloting suggested more support after the workshop would be helpful [20]. Thus the virtual workshop was supplemented by booster lessons via an app to increase the intervention delivered to the parent and quantify the dose of active play they engaged in with their children following the workshop. However, less than half of families accessed this content and the response rate to the check‐in questions was even lower. The families that did respond to the check‐in questions reported engaging in multiple active play sessions with their child per week (on average it was the equivalent of 5×/week) but the amount of total time remains unknown as does the dose provided by over half of the families that did not report. The qualitative findings suggest all but one intervention family implemented some PL‐related active play post‐workshop. Overall, parents may need further follow‐up and reminders to engage with the app content to ensure adequate intervention dose and implementation tracking. Considering a small percentage of families found the app less useful, continuing to have a combination of electronic and paper‐based resources could meet varying needs of different families.

Given the novelty of the PLAYshop intervention, there is minimal existing evidence to compare the intervention dose used to other studies. For instance, when just considering FMS, one sub‐component of PL, a systematic review of FMS interventions identified only one parent‐focused intervention that exclusively took place in the home setting with preschool‐aged children (mean age < 5 years) [68]. Of note, this parent‐focused virtual intervention asked parents to engage with their child in 12 h of targeted and structured FMS instruction (a mean of 9.5 h was delivered), and a statistically significant and practically significant increase in children's FMS (measured via the TGMD‐3) in the intervention group, compared to the control group, was found [69]. Beyond differences in the approach to dose (requested hours versus no specific dose requirement), the role suggested to parents was also different between the two interventions in regards to FMS development. In PLAYshop, a play‐based approach was adopted; whereby, parents were encouraged to engage in positive purposeful active play with their child that incorporated foundation activities to support FMS development (e.g., games involving scarfs and balloons to build the foundation for tracking and catching) but that also incorporated PL concepts related to motivation (e.g., using a variety of activities), enjoyment (e.g., following the child's interest), and confidence (e.g., modifying activities to the appropriate challenge level) [19]. In comparison, the FMS intervention focused on parents providing targeted and structured FMS instruction [69]. With longer term follow‐up it is possible that the mastering of foundational skills through play encouraged by PLAYshop, even at the current dose, may contribute to FMS improvement, motivation, enjoyment, and confidence over time, which was not captured at the 2‐month follow‐up.

The lack of intervention effect on the primary outcome could also be due to measurement issues. Since there is no existing tool to holistically assess PL among preschool‐aged children in the home setting, we adapted several existing tools and combined them for assessment. It is common for PL interventions in this age group to only measure FMS as an outcome and not other sub‐components of PL [13] because of the inherent challenges in this age group. For instance, within a systematic review of PL interventions in the childcare setting [13], only one study was included that had a measure other than FMS [68]. Specifically, in this study, knowledge, attitude, and habit scores related to PA were measured, and higher change scores were observed in the intervention group, compared to the control group, after 1 year [70]. Focusing on only one sub‐component of PL has been critiqued in the literature base [14]. The present study addressed this gap by incorporating motivation, enjoyment, and confidence PL sub‐components, in addition to FMS, in the outcome measure. However, moderate correlations were not observed between PL outcome variables, indicating these variables did not represent a composite PL variable. This may be due to limitations of the measures themselves. For instance, convergent validity was not observed for the children's self‐reported enjoyment measure at baseline [38]. Further testing is needed on self‐report PL measures in this age group. Additionally, while the virtual assessment of FMS allowed a greater geographic reach as well as access to families during the COVID‐19 pandemic, we were only able to measure five FMS due to space and equipment limitations. While good inter‐rater reliability was observed for the TGMD‐3 skills, low internal consistency reliability was found among the five FMS. Performing the full battery of skills would have given a more comprehensive picture of FMS development, which may be particularly important for the development stage of the preschool‐aged children in the study. For instance, for some of the skills used in this study, such as overhand throw, fully mature skill patterns don't typically appear until children are older (i.e., 7–8 years of age) [71]. Of note, qualitative findings revealed that some parents observed improvements to their children's skill and confidence levels, related to PL‐related active play. Furthermore, borderline non‐significant intervention effects, of small to medium effect size, were observed with the parental‐reported children's enjoyment/motivation/confidence measure. However, the study was powered for a medium effect size based on a previous childcare FMS intervention [57]. This study can provide some insight on effect size estimates for future trials. Overall, PL is challenging to measure in preschool‐aged children and future research needs to continue to focus on developing a reliable, valid, and holistic formative construct assessment for this age group, as exists in older age groups [72]. Given PL is a multidimensional construct this tool should enable the calculation of both a composite total score as well as domain‐specific scores for a more nuanced approach [73]. This work should continue to consider the larger psychological literature, where definitions and distinctions have been developed for affect‐related constructs in relation to physical activity [74], and distinctions have been outlined between motivation and confidence constructs [75].

Intervention effects were observed for some secondary and tertiary outcomes. These findings align with previous PLAYshop findings [19, 20, 21] and partially support study hypotheses. The findings that parent–child co‐participation increased along with parental MVPA modeling suggest that the intervention was successful in increasing the amount of purposive active play that parents were engaging in with their child. The qualitative data further support this finding with some families reporting they valued the connection and fun nature of the PL‐based play, connecting with their children in new ways, learning more about their child's interests and abilities, and bonding. Quantitative findings for co‐participation and modeling were not consistent across device‐based and questionnaire measures. In terms of parent–child co‐participation, the device‐based measure might include co‐participation in activities that were not active play related (e.g., both child and parent walking into a store). Active play is one domain of PA in preschool‐aged children [76]. Therefore, it is possible the questionnaire measure better captures co‐participation within the active play domain rather than co‐participation in total PA that the device‐based measure captured. Conversely, in regard to parental modeling, parental MVPA minutes are a proxy for parental modeling but a significant correlation was observed between the device‐based parental MVPA modeling and the questionnaire measure of parental PA modeling, which was not a proxy. The discrepancy in efficacy findings by mode of measurement is unclear. Overall, the intervention effects regarding these secondary outcomes are positive findings of this trial as the intervention was directed at parents.

Consistent with previous PLAYshop feasibility and pilot studies [19, 20, 21], and what was hypothesized, the intervention was successful in increasing parental competence (i.e., knowledge), opportunity (i.e., perceived availability of resources), and motivation (i.e., confidence) to support children's PL development. This quantitative finding was further supported with qualitative findings where some parents mentioned increases in their confidence and knowledge base after the workshop. We previously classified parental perceived barriers, beliefs, outcome expectations, perceived availability of resources as theoretical mechanisms of change [19]. Similarly, perceived behavioral control and intentions could also be considered theoretical mechanisms of change [18]. Findings were more mixed with these variables, with some intervention effects being observed but not maintained at the 2‐month follow‐up. This may reflect the impact of the day‐to‐day implementation challenges a parent faces over time. Further consideration should be given to how to maintain these PLAYshop intervention effects overtime.

This study had a number of strengths and limitations. The main strength was the RCT study design. Furthermore, the focus on parents in the home setting is novel in this age group. The virtual delivery and assessment was also a strength as it allowed for the study to be conducted during COVID‐19 as well as enabling a wider intervention across two Canadian provinces. While the exclusion of rural participants and families without electronic devices may limit generalizability, it should be noted that in Canada it is rare for an adult of average child‐rearing age to not own a smartphone [77]. The inclusion of other sub‐components of PL beyond FMS is another strength. However, measuring PL in preschool‐aged children remains a challenge. A previous study using baseline data from this PLAYshop RCT suggests findings regarding the children's self‐reported enjoyment measure should be interpreted with caution [38]. Although the virtual assessment has strengths, as previously noted, it was less standardized in terms of equipment, set‐up, and camera angles. It was also not possible to conduct the full battery of TGMD‐3 or MABC‐2 measures and a composite score could not be computed because of low internal consistency reliability. Finally, the use of device‐based measures as well as quantitative and qualitative measures for the secondary and tertiary outcomes was a notable strength. Questionnaire measures are prone to social desirability and recall bias but the majority of measures had acceptable reliability. Finally, approximately a third of the sample had previous PL training (based on a yes/no question); it is possible that families who signed up for the PLAYshop intervention had higher interest in PL, compared to the average Canadian family.

Perspective

Targeting PL in the preschool years is key to improving PA levels and supporting sport participation of children [6, 14]. The PLAYshop intervention takes a novel approach by targeting parents' capability, opportunity, and motivation to support children's PL in the home setting. The efficacy of the PLAYshop intervention was again demonstrated for parent level but not child‐level outcomes. Implementation of PLAYshop was highly feasible at the delivery level and highly acceptable at the parent‐level but ongoing parent engagement through boosters was problematic. Study findings provide important insight into PL assessment of preschool‐aged children and areas to support the improvement and expansion of the PLAYshop intervention. Overall, PLAYshop is a promising intervention that supports parents so they can provide PL‐related active play opportunities that support their children's PL journey. Findings can inform future research, such as a scaled‐up trial that focuses on the effectiveness, implementation, as well as the longer‐term maintenance of outcomes associated with PLAYshop.
