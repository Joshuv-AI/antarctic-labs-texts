The evolutionary consequences of behavioural plasticity
Authors: Carlos A. Botero
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
When environments change and phenotypes become suboptimal, organisms often mitigate the costs of maladaptation with behavioural adjustments. Although this “buffering” of natural selection is widespread, its evolutionary consequences are poorly understood. Here, I use evolutionary simulations to explore how behavioural plasticity shapes the tempo and mode of adaptive evolution. My results indicate that this fast-acting form of plasticity enhances persistence under suboptimal conditions and facilitates the accumulation of genetic variation within populations. Over short timescales, or when opportunities for niche expansion are limited, the combined action of these effects leads to slower evolution of morphology and physiology. Over longer timescales, or when ecological opportunities abound, it can instead lead to a “Goldilocks” pattern, where evolution is fastest in moderately plastic lineages, whose medium buffering capacity enables persistence in suboptimal conditions without severely constraining subsequent optimizing selection. These findings expose key differences in scope and mechanism relative to other forms of phenotypic plasticity and suggest that behavioural plasticity may more broadly influence the evolutionary trajectories of individual lineages. More generally, they help clarify our understanding of how short-term flexibility shapes long-term adaptation and underscore the need to reassess current risk-assessment frameworks that equate slower evolutionary rates with greater vulnerability to environmental change.

Introduction

Adaptation to environmental change often unfolds over multiple generations and is constrained by the availability of suitable genetic variants, either already present in the population or arising through mutation. In the interim, behavioural plasticity can offer a more immediate mechanism for mitigating the fitness costs of phenotypic mismatch1–3. For example, when insulation becomes suboptimal, individuals may reduce thermal stress by selecting different microhabitats4, adjusting their activity patterns5, huddling6 or tolerating closer proximity to conspecifics7. Likewise, thermally mismatched individuals can offset the energetic costs of increased thermoregulation by modifying their foraging strategies to favour more energy-rich prey choices8. Similar behavioural buffers have been documented across a wide range of taxa and ecological contexts, but their evolutionary consequences, particularly in the context of adaptation to rapid environmental change9, remain poorly understood. As climates shift, urban footprints expand, and anthropogenic pressures accelerate environmental degradation10, clarifying the role of early behavioural plasticity on population persistence and genetic adaptation is both timely and essential1,3,11,12.

A key challenge in understanding the effects of behavioural plasticity on the evolutionary process is that behavioural responses to altered environments vary widely within and among species12. For example, some red squirrel populations, Tamiasciurus hudsonicus, have responded to climate change by shifting their phenology13, whereas others have increased instead their food hoarding and territoriality14. Such divergent responses to similar stressors raise questions about the equivalence and evolutionary implications of alternative behavioural strategies. Similarly striking qualitative differences in behavioural response are common among species that differ in morphology, habitat, or natural history, further complicating the derivation of general principles from individual case studies.

One practical way to circumvent the variability in observed behaviours is to focus our comparisons on the general capacity to buffer selection through behaviour (hereafter ‘behavioural buffering’) rather than on the specifics of individual response15–19. Two contrasting perspectives can help frame this discussion. The cognitive buffer hypothesis states that behavioural flexibility shields individuals from the fitness cost of phenotypic mismatch20–23 and therefore predicts a decrease in rates of genetic evolution due to weaker selection16. For example, the selective use of animal skins allowed early hominins with different levels of insulation to experience similar thermoregulation costs in cold environments, thereby weakening selection on traits like fur and body fat24. In contrast, the behavioural drive hypothesis15 notes that behavioural buffering rarely nullifies selection, and therefore makes the opposite prediction that rates of genetic evolution should be faster in more behaviourally plastic lineages because improved abilities to occupy new habitats and ecological niches are likely to provide more frequent opportunities for optimizing selection to act upon the phenotype15,18,25–28. The conditions under which behavioural buffering shifts from constraining to accelerating genetic evolution are poorly understood and remain a key unresolved challenge in cognitive ecology.

The contrasting views on the evolutionary consequences of behavioural plasticity echo long-standing debates on whether phenotypic plasticity generally accelerates or decelerates evolution3,11,12,29–36. Such similarity is not surprising because behaviour is part of the phenotype and behavioural flexibility is simply a form of phenotypic plasticity3,29. Nevertheless, behavioural adjustments can be typically expressed and reversed on much faster timescales than morphological and even physiological change9. Moreover, investments into behavioural plasticity (e.g., bigger brains) can more easily buffer selection in a greater variety of traits (even in entirely new contexts), whereas more structural forms of phenotypic plasticity tend to exert narrower, stage-dependent, or more context-specific effects. These key differences in both timing and scope suggest that behavioural plasticity may occupy a distinct position within the broader spectrum of phenotypic plasticity, with unique implications for how populations navigate and adapt to changing environments9,37–40.

Earlier theoretical work on phenotypic plasticity has provided important insights on its evolutionary consequences, but has not typically distinguished between behavioural and more structural forms of plasticity28,29,41–44. Likewise, the studies that have explicitly made such distinction have focused primarily on the evolution of plasticity itself rather than on its downstream consequences on the evolution of other components of the phenotype37,45,46. Here, I integrate theoretical and empirical insights from animal behaviour, cognitive ecology, and evolutionary biology to identify conditions under which behavioural plasticity shapes genetic adaptation and influences the evolution of other phenotypic traits. Using evolutionary simulations and digital experiments, I show that increasing effectiveness in behavioural buffering is generally advantageous in changing environments, but that its downstream effects on genetic adaptation are complex and sometimes counterintuitive. I begin by showing that behavioural plasticity facilitates resilience to environmental change and promotes the accumulation of genetic variation within populations. I then show that although these dual effects lead to slower rates of morphological and physiological change over short timescales, on longer timescales they can result instead in a ‘Goldilocks’ pattern47, where the rate of morphological and physiological change peaks at intermediate levels of behavioural flexibility. At that peak, intermediate plasticity facilitates evolution by enabling persistence in a wide range of suboptimal conditions without severely constraining the optimizing selection that will subsequently ensue. These findings help explain empirical discrepancies in the observed effects of behavioural plasticity on evolutionary rates and provide a better understanding of how behavioural flexibility influences genetic adaption in the natural world. They also provide new mechanistic insights that help explain the broad empirical associations that have been observed between brain size, environmental unpredictability48–50, and diversification potential15,17.

Results

Modelling framework

To investigate the evolutionary consequences of behavioural plasticity, I developed an individual-based simulation model that tracks the evolution of a quantitative morphological trait, I, in a sexually reproducing population with non-overlapping generations and density-dependent regulation. Selection on I is stabilizing, such that fitness is maximized when trait values match local environmental conditions. For illustration purposes, I hereby frame the model in terms of thermal insulation: thick coats are favoured in cold habitats and impose costs in warm ones, whereas thin coats exhibit the opposite pattern. However, readers should note that this model is intentionally general and applies to many other traits under selection toward an environmentally defined optimum.

For each combination of parameter values, I simulated the evolution of insulation in 100 independent replicate populations. Each population was initialized with 5000 individuals defined by a single continuous genetic trait, whose value directly determines the phenotype. Starting genotypes were randomly drawn from a uniform distribution on [0, 1]. Simulations proceeded through three sequential phases: (i) a 500-generation burn-in period with an initial environmental target equal to the mid-value of the seeded genotypes (E = 0.5, aimed at establishing realistic levels of standing genetic variation), (ii) a 1000-generation evolutionary phase with a new environmental target of E = 0.3, and (iii) a 500-generation diaspora phase in which a small subset of randomly selected individuals experienced an extreme environmental change to an environmental target of E = −1.3. Absolute fitness was determined by the mismatch between an individual’s genotype, I, and its current environmental target, E, scaled by a population-wide buffering parameter, b (i.e., the lineage’s potential for behavioural plasticity). Evolutionary dynamics

Stable levels of standing genetic variation were attained in all simulation replicates across all parameter combinations by the end of the burn-in period (Fig. 1). The only exception was at b = 1, where a complete lack of selection led to evolutionary dynamics that approximated a random walk (e.g., green trajectory in Fig. 1B). After environmental changes were implemented in generation 500 (dashed vertical lines in Fig. 1B, D, and IF), the evolutionary dynamics of insulation became strongly correlated with the effectiveness of the behavioural buffer. Specifically, across parameter combinations, the mean population value of insulation phenotypes approached the new target more slowly in simulations with higher b.

Inter-population variability, measured as the spread among replicate trajectories, generally declined over time except for simulations with complete buffering (i.e., b = 1). This trend can be observed in the confidence intervals of Fig. 1B, D, and IF, where the gap between upper and lower bounds is widest in early generations and narrower thereafter. At equilibrium, lineages with higher b displayed greater residual variability, suggesting that more behaviourally plastic species should exhibit stronger geographic variation in morphological and physiological response to environmental stress.

Interactions with known drivers of the evolutionary process

To assess the robustness of my findings, I repeated these analyses under different selection strengths and mutation rates. As expected, stronger selection (smaller d) accelerated genetic evolution and reduced standing variation (Fig. 3A, B). Similarly, the pace of genetic adaptation continued to be negatively associated with behavioural buffering across d values, but the decline was considerably steeper in simulations with stronger selection (Fig. 3A). Likewise, the positive association between behavioural plasticity and genetic variance persisted across d values, but overall levels of variation decreased when selection was more intense (Fig. 3B). Close inspection of this pattern indicates that strength of selection had a pronounced effect at low b but minimal or no effect at high b. The effects of mutation rate on the evolution of insulation were similarly affected by behavioural buffering (Fig. 3D–F). As expected, higher rates of mutation accumulation generally fuelled faster adaptation, but this effect weakened in lineages with stronger behavioural buffers (Fig. 3D). Similarly, standing genetic variation increased conspicuously with mutation rate (Fig. 3E) and continued to be dependent on b. Here too, the increase in standing genetic variation with b was more pronounced at higher mutation rates (Fig. 3F), suggesting that behavioural plasticity potentiates the effects of mutation by preventing the loss of extreme genetic variants during periods of stability.

Macro-evolutionary implications

The above analyses reveal that although behavioural plasticity can decelerate phenotypic evolution, it can also increase the diversity of potentially adaptive variants before environments change. To explore the macro-evolutionary implications of these contrasting effects, I examined how small population diasporas fared when introduced to significantly different environments or ecological niches. Specifically, I randomly selected 100 individuals at generation 1500 and allowed these nascent populations to evolve an additional 500 generations in a severely altered environment. Across model parameterizations, diasporas generally collapsed when behavioural buffering was not very effective but easily survived when it was (Fig. 4). Furthermore, population survival increased in a stepwise manner, reinforcing the idea that under extreme ∆E, population resilience to environmental shock relies largely on the pre-existence of genetic variants that are either already adaptive or at least sufficiently close to the new optimum to be meaningfully shielded by behavioural buffering. As expected, population survival was more likely when environmental change was less intense (Fig. 4A), selection was weaker (Fig. 4B), or mutations accumulated at a faster rate (Fig. 4C). With the possible exception of Fig. 4B, these effects were visibly weaker than those of behavioural plasticity, further suggesting that behaviour can play a critical role in protecting populations against collapse during rapid environmental change. Discussion

My analyses suggest that behavioural plasticity exerts dual and time-dependent effects on the tempo and mode of adaptive evolution: it slows down the microevolutionary process and simultaneously prevents the loss of genetic variation within populations. On short evolutionary timescales, or when opportunities for niche expansion are limited, my results suggest that these effects are likely to translate into reduced rates of morphological and physiological evolution (Fig. 5). However, over longer timescales, or when opportunities for ecological diversification abound, they suggest instead a Goldilocks pattern in which the pace of evolutionary change will peak at intermediate levels of behavioural plasticity (Fig. 5). The model developed here is intentionally simple, a choice that entails both limitations and advantages. Some of its limitations include the assumption that behavioural plasticity is cost-free and non-evolving, and the examination of evolutionary consequences on a single quantitative trait under stabilizing selection. Future extensions could relax these assumptions by allowing plasticity itself to evolve, incorporating energetic or opportunity costs of flexible behaviour, modelling effects on multiple traits with conflicting selective demands, or embedding the process in spatially structured or temporally autocorrelated environments. Such extensions could further refine our understanding of the limits of behavioural buffering.

There are also, however, some important conceptual benefits to the model’s generality. Given that behavioural plasticity was broadly abstracted as a general buffering agent that helps reduce the fitness costs of environmental mismatch, the model’s insights can potentially be extended far beyond behaviour alone. For example, rapid physiological adjustments (e.g., acclimation), human‑mediated interventions (such as conservation actions), and even ecological interactions44 (e.g., microbiome shifts or symbioses) can presumably buffer the costs of phenotypic mismatch in similar ways, and could therefore result in similar evolutionary dynamics.

Overall, my findings parallel ongoing debate on the potential accelerating and decelerating effects of phenotypic plasticity (broadly defined) on evolution29,54,55. However, they also highlight some unique aspects of behavioural plasticity that suggest that behaviour may have a more pervasive and long-lasting influence on the evolutionary trajectories of individual lineages. Most notably, it was recently suggested that phenotypic plasticity homogenizes genetic variation within populations29,31,34,56 (but see in ref. 43). In contrast, here I show that behavioural plasticity tends to increase it (Fig. 2). This key finding implies that behaviourally plastic lineages are likely to develop deeper reservoirs of genetic variation during periods of stability, enabling them to survive and respond more quickly to a wider range of environmental changes. While not directly tested in this study, the general scope of behavioural buffering suggests that organisms may be able use it to reshape the selective landscape of a broader suite of traits than in more structural forms of phenotypic plasticity36,57.

Testing these theoretical predictions in natural systems will require reliable estimates of behavioural flexibility and an understanding of how cognitive capacities vary across species. General proxies such as relative brain size58 or neuron numbers59–61 offer promising starting points. For example, among birds, relative brain size correlates strongly with behavioural flexibility22,62,63, innovation rate64, and the ability to devise behavioural solutions to novel ecological challenges63,65,66. Thus, species with larger relative brain sizes can reasonably be assumed to enjoy greater protection from environmental change. Nevertheless, we must bear in mind that the buffering capacity of behaviour is not unlimited, and that even highly intelligent species can sometimes be ineffective at countering certain selective pressures. For example, despite our unparalleled cognitive capacity, our own species has not been able to fully eliminate some forms of natural selection. Thus, when translating this model’s findings to the natural world, we must bear in mind that the range of b values that are biologically attainable in Fig. 5A may not necessarily extend to the theoretical upper limit in every taxon or every context. Whenever this is the case, the Goldilocks pattern described earlier could manifest itself incompletely, leading to a perception of a simpler, threshold-like increase in diversification rates rather than a clear peak in lineages with intermediate brain sizes.

Current evidence in corvids17, and potentially across all birds15,67, largely aligns with this view. Specifically, crows and ravens (Corvus spp.) rank among the most behaviourally flexible non-human animals and possess some of the largest relative brain sizes in birds. Nevertheless, their behavioural buffering capacity is limited, as evidenced by their weaker development of material culture and technological innovation relative to humans. Consistent with my predictions, the broader Corvidae exhibits a single threshold-like shift toward higher rates of phenotypic and species diversification at larger relative brain sizes17. Notably, this transition coincided with the evolution of exceptional flight performance in ancestral Corvus lineages, reinforcing the notion that elevated behavioural plasticity must be coupled with exposure to new ecological opportunities to drive an increase in macroevolutionary rates17.

In conclusion, the analyses presented here indicate that behavioural plasticity is an important modulator of evolutionary responses to environmental change. By mitigating the costs of phenotypic mismatch, this fast-acting form of phenotypic plasticity enhances short-term persistence under environmental stress and strengthens long-term resilience through the accumulation of genetic variation beyond what is typically supplied by mutation. The observed short-term deceleration of phenotypic change driven by behavioural flexibility also calls for a re-evaluation of recent conservation and risk-assessment standards, which view slower morphological or physiological evolution as evidence of limited evolutionary adaptive capacity68,69. Specifically, as shown here and in recent empirical work16, behaviourally flexible lineages are likely to exhibit slower rates of phenotypic evolution not because they are unable to evolve, but because they are less affected by selection. More broadly, these findings indicate that a more explicit consideration of the wide-reaching effects of behavioural plasticity on adaptive landscapes can lead to more accurate assessments of relative risks and better prioritization of conservation and management practices in this era of rapid environmental change.
