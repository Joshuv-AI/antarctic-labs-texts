Best evidence synthesis of preoperative prehabilitation for older patients undergoing gastrointestinal cancer surgery
Authors: Huixiang Lai, Manli Liu, Wen Zhang, Qingran Lin, Minping Deng, Mei Feng, Jinfen Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify, evaluate, and synthesize the best available evidence on preoperative rehabilitation for older patients undergoing gastrointestinal tumor surgery.MethodsFollowing the “6S” evidence model, a systematic search was conducted for clinical decisions, guidelines, systematic reviews, expert consensus statements, evidence summaries, and randomized controlled trials on preoperative prehabilitation for older patients with gastrointestinal tumors. Searches covered major domestic and international databases as well as professional websites, with literature collected up to 30 November 2024. Two researchers independently screened the studies, assessed methodological quality, and extracted data, with input from expert judgment.ResultsA total of 22 publications were included: 1 clinical decision, 6 clinical guidelines, 5 systematic reviews, 5 expert consensus statements, 3 evidence summaries, and 2 randomized controlled trials. From these, 33 key evidence items were synthesized across seven domains: implementation timing and team composition, preoperative risk assessment, nutritional intervention, exercise intervention, psychological support, specialized management, and supervision and evaluation.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive and methodologically consistent summary of the available evidence. Healthcare professionals should individualize evidence-based prehabilitation strategies according to patients’ clinical characteristics and preferences to improve surgical outcomes.Keywords: Older patients, Gastrointestinal tumour surgery, Preoperative care, Prehabilitation, Evidence-based care

Introduction

The global trend of population aging poses unprecedented challenges to oncology care. The World Health Organization predicts that by 2050, the number of people aged 65 and over will reach 2.1 billion. China, with its older population accounting for 40% of Asia and 25% of the world, is one of the countries experiencing the fastest aging rate.1,2 Gastrointestinal tumors are among the most common malignant tumors in China's digestive system. Among them, gastric cancer accounts for about 10.25% of all malignant tumor cases, while colorectal cancer makes up 9.87%, ranking third and second, respectively, in the spectrum of malignant tumor incidence in the country. Approximately 60% of gastrointestinal tumors occur in patients over 65 years old, and nearly 20% affect individuals aged 80 and above.3

The surge in disease burden is closely linked to age-related physiological vulnerabilities (such as frailty, sarcopenia, and polypharmacy). Frailty increases perioperative mortality, prolongs hospital stays, reduces postoperative quality of life, and raises disability rates in older patients with gastrointestinal tumors. Sarcopenia is common in 30% to 50% of older cancer patients and is associated with extended hospital stays, postoperative complications, and reduced survival rates. Polypharmacy (more than five medications) exacerbates drug interactions and metabolic stress, further complicating perioperative management. The combined effect of these three factors significantly increases surgical risks.4,5

Given the requisite recovery period following surgery, accelerating patient recovery during the perioperative period while enhancing their quality of life and comfort remains a key objective for clinical healthcare providers. The Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) concept optimizes perioperative management using evidence-based medicine. Its goals are to reduce postoperative complications, shorten hospital stays, lower medical costs, and mitigate surgical stress responses, enabling patients to achieve rapid recovery.6 Prehabilitation is a multimodal, individualised preoperative intervention process designed to optimise a patient's physical, nutritional, and psychological status. This optimisation aims to enhance functional capacity and resilience, improving the patient's ability to withstand surgical stress and facilitating postoperative recovery. Consequently, prehabilitation constitutes a core component within the broader ERAS framework.7 Minnella et al. pointed out that multimodal preoperative rehabilitation care can improve patients' functional capabilities.8 A study confirmed that applying preoperative rehabilitation concepts in older patients with colorectal cancer can shorten catheter removal time and hospital stay, reduce postoperative complications, promote early ambulation after surgery, and improve psychological well-being.9 Although prehabilitation shows potential in improving functional reserves and reducing complications, there is still a lack of unified, standardized operating guidelines and reliable evidence-based support, leading to certain obstacles and delays in its clinical practice.10 Existing evidence is often fragmented across disciplines (surgery, geriatrics, nutrition, physiotherapy), may contain conflicting recommendations, or lacks clear guidance on practical application within the complex context of aging physiology and multimorbidity common in this population.

Therefore, this study seeks to synthesize the most effective preoperative rehabilitation evidence through systematic evidence retrieval, literature quality assessment, evidence extraction, thereby offering a reference for developing a scientific, effective, and feasible prehabilitation program, formulating prehabilitation strategies, optimizing preoperative management, and enhancing the overall rehabilitation outcomes and satisfaction of older patients with gastrointestinal tumors.

Methods

Search resources and strategies

The evidence-based problem establishment tool PIPOST11 was used to form the evidence-based practice problems. According to the “6S Evidence Resource Pyramid Model,” which prioritizes higher-quality evidence in the upper layers of the pyramid,12 we utilized a combination of subject and free terms to conduct a comprehensive search across 29 databases using a top-down approach. (i) The Guide website comprises Guidelines International Network (GIN), National Guideline Clearinghouse (NGC), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), Registered Nurses' Association of Ontario (RNAO), and Medlive. (ii) Professional websites: American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS), National Cancer Database (NCDB), American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN), European Society of Intensive Care Medicine (ESICM), European Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ESPEN), European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), Cancer Care Ontario (CCO), Cancer Council of Australia (CCA), Chinese Nutrition Society, and Chinese Anti-Cancer Association. (iii) Evidence-based databases comprise BMJ Best Practice, Up To Date, the Cochrane Library, and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Centre for Evidence-based Health Care Database in Australia. (iv) Comprehensive databases include CINAHL, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, VIP Database, and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (SinoMed).

Search terms combined MeSH and free-text terms for neoplasms (e.g., “Stomach Neoplasms,” “Colonic Neoplasms,” “Rectal Neoplasms,” “Colorectal Neoplasms,” “Gastrointestinal Neoplasms”) with surgical procedure terms (e.g., “digestive system surgical procedures,” “Gastrectomy,” “Colectomy,” “Proctectomy”), perioperative timing terms (“preoperative,” “perioperative”), and guideline/evidence terms (e.g., guid∗, evidence∗, “expert consensus,” “randomized controlled trial,” practice, “systematic review,” recommendation∗). Searches covered each database from inception to 30 November 2024. The full search strategy is provided in the Appendix; PubMed is shown as an example.

Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (i) Subject matter: preoperative rehabilitation for geriatric patients undergoing gastrointestinal tumor surgery; (ii) Study design: The categories of literature included clinical decisions, recommendations, systematic reviews, expert consensus, evidence summaries and Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs); (iii) Research setting: hospital; (iv) Language restriction: English and Chinese; (v) Limitations on dissemination: public dissemination.

Exclusion criteria: (i) Study subject: Subjects who have undergone surgical procedures unrelated to gastrointestinal cancers; (ii) Research design: primary research; (iii) Publication limitations: grey literature; the complete text of the literature is inaccessible; Insufficient literature; Redundant publications; Research proposals, manuscripts, reports, and summaries.

Literature quality evaluation criteria

Clinical decision making

Given that BMJ Best Practice and Up to Date are premier evidence-based medicine databases, characterized by high levels of evidence and quality, the project group adopted evidence that aligns with clinical realities in China following thorough debate and analysis.

Clinical guidelines

Clinical guidelines utilize the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation, AGREE II, which was revised in 2017. There are six fields (23 items) and two complete evaluation criteria, encompassing scope and purpose, participation, rigor, clarity, application, and editorial independence, with each item assessed on a scale of 1–7 points. The score for each field is calculated as the sum of all entries inside that field and is expressed as a percentage of the maximum achievable score for that field. The superior the quality of the guidance. Standardization percentage in each field of each guide is calculated as follows: (obtained score - minimum possible score) / (maximum potential score - minimum possible score) ​× ​100%. The third domain, pertaining to the rigor of guideline formulation, is assessed initially, and a score of ≥ 70% in this domain permits scoring in other domains. The guide's recommendation level is categorized into three tiers: a score of ≥ 60% in six areas constitutes grade A; a score of 30% or more in three or more areas below 60% is classified as grade B. A score below 30% in three or more categories constitutes a C grade.13

Systematic review, expert consensus, and randomized controlled trials

Systematic reviews, expert consensus, and RCTs were assessed utilizing the quality evaluation method from the 2016 edition of the JBI Evidence-Based Health Care Center System Evaluation in Australia. The assessment criteria for each question were “yes”, “no”, “unclear”, and “not applicable".14

Evidence summary

The quality assessment of Evidence Summaries is conducted by examining the original research literature that underpins the evidence and employing the Critical Appraisal for Evidence Summaries (CASE) created by Margaret's team.

Evidence extraction, integration

The literature was separately assessed by two researchers who had received comprehensive training in evidence-based methods. Subsequent to extraction, the evidence was verified, augmented, and consolidated. The tenets of evidence integration are as follows: (i) When the content of the evidence is consistent, evidence that adheres to professional standards and is easily comprehensible is prioritized. Several studies7,15,16 indicated the timing for the implementation of prehabilitation, ultimately concluding that it should occur 1–2 weeks prior to surgery, with an extension to 2–4 weeks for patients exhibiting poor physical condition, severe malnutrition, or significant underlying health issues. (ii) When the elements of the evidence are complementary, they are amalgamated into a singular body of evidence based on their logical interrelation. Wenying Gu et al.17 and Rongrong Li et al.18 articulated the initiation of enteral nutrition as follows: “Enteral nutrition should be considered when oral nutritional supplement (ONS) intake has failed to enhance nutritional status or to fulfill 60% of the target energy requirement for over one week.” (iii) In cases of conflicting results from various sources, prioritize evidence based on the principles of evidence-based practice, favor high-quality evidence, and consider freshly published authoritative evidence first. Keller DS et al.19 and Leping Li et al.7 both addressed the time of prehabilitation smoking cessation and alcohol withdrawal, together with the daily protein intake for cancer patients. According to the principle of prioritizing evidence-based data, the selected evidence indicated to “initiate smoking cessation and alcohol withdrawal at least 4 weeks prior to surgery” and “protein intake should be 4.184–8.368 kcal/(kg·d)."In instances of conflicting evaluative opinions, the senior researcher intervenes to facilitate arbitration or discussion in order to achieve consensus.

Discussion

A solid foundation for prehabilitation of older patients with gastrointestinal tumor surgery

Timing matters

The first evidence emphasizes that the ideal time for prehabilitation is 1–2 weeks before surgery. For frail older patients, nurses can utilize validated frailty assessment tools (e.g., Clinical Frailty Scale) to determine individualized prehabilitation duration.7 For patients with poor physical condition, severe malnutrition, or complicated with serious underlying diseases, the prehabilitation time can be appropriately extended to 2–4 weeks before surgery.15,16 This approach aligns with ERAS Society recommendations while addressing age-specific vulnerabilities through nurse-led risk stratification.

Multidisciplinary collaboration

Article 2 evidence emphasizes prehabilitation practitioners and indicates the need for collaboration among multidisciplinary professionals.16, 17, 18,20,29 The creation of an effective prehabilitation team collaboration framework can guarantee that members from various disciplines (including oncology, nutrition, rehabilitation, psychology, etc.) engage in continuous communication and efficient cooperation, collectively formulating and executing tailored prehabilitation strategies aligned with patients' rehabilitation objectives. Regular case discussions, information sharing, and collaborative ward rounds facilitate knowledge and experience exchange within the team, thereby delivering comprehensive, multi-faceted, scientific, and rational prehabilitation services to patients. This ultimately aims to expedite recovery, minimize complications, enhance surgical success rates, and improve patients' quality of life.

A prehabilitation team led by nurses was established

Nurses serve as the primary implementers of the prehabilitation program, responsible for patient screening, evaluation, feedback, and oversight and modification during program execution. Given the essential role of nurses in prehabilitation , it is advisable to enhance the normative training of nurses in the gastrointestinal surgery unit. The training material must encompass fundamental concepts, principles, methodologies, and detailed implementation strategies for prehabilitation while integrating knowledge of gastrointestinal surgery to enhance the professional competence and practical skills of nurses. Nurses can assume a pivotal role in prehabilitation through training, hence facilitating the effective implementation of prehabilitation programs in clinical practice. A robust prehabilitation team collaboration system must be built to facilitate effective communication and cooperation among multidisciplinary team members, hence delivering complete and scientifically grounded prehabilitation services to patients.

Comprehensive strategies for prehabilitation of older patients with gastrointestinal tumor surgery

Risk assessment

Preoperative risk assessment is the basis and guide of the preoperative prehabilitation strategy. Through comprehensive evaluation of patients' tumor stage, comorbidities, lifestyle, physiological indicators, and psychological status, various risks that patients may face are accurately identified. Behavioral risk mitigation (e.g., Smoking and drinking) provides the essential foundation for functional interventions.

Nutritional intervention

Nutritional intervention is an important part of the preoperative prehabilitation strategy.36 According to evidence 15, nutritional risk screening tools such as Nutritional risk screening 2002 (NRS2002) and malnutrition universal screening tool (MUST) can accurately identify patients in need of nutritional support.7,24,26,27,29 According to evidence in article 16, combining the nutritional needs of the older in different age groups and different tumor stages, the development of a personalized nutritional treatment plan, including dietary adjustment, ONS, and enteral/parenteral nutritional support, can effectively improve the nutritional status of patients and enhance the tolerance of surgery.7,17,26,35

Exercise intervention

Exercise intervention aims at improving the patient's physical fitness and motor ability. According to articles 23 and 24 of the evidence, by issuing personalized exercise prescriptions, including aerobic training, resistance strength training, etc. In addition, traditional Chinese medicine exercises (such as Baduanjin) should be appropriately included in the exercise prescription, and three measures should be taken together, combined with exercise monitoring and adjustment, the patient's physical fitness level can be gradually improved to prepare for surgery.16,17,28,30 However, it is important to note that the exercise intervention should be appropriately adjusted according to the patient's physical condition and the surgical plan to avoid adverse effects on the surgery.

Psychological intervention

Psychological intervention is of great significance in relieving patients' surgical anxiety and fear.37 According to evidence 28, a patient's mental state can be accurately assessed through tools such as the Mental Health Assessment Scale. Providing patients with psychological support, cognitive behavioral therapy, and other psychological interventions can help enhance patients' confidence in coping with surgery and rehabilitation and reduce the negative impact of anxiety and fear on surgery and rehabilitation.38 On the basis of scale evaluation, TCM five-element music therapy and TCM emotional regulation methods such as ear acupoint pressing beans were integrated.

Antithrombotic management

According to evidence 29, older patients are at a higher risk of thrombosis, and antithrombotic management is an integral part of preoperative rehabilitation strategies. Tools such as the Caprini assessment table can accurately assess the risk level of patients' thrombosis and the risk of pulmonary embolism, ensuring the safety of surgery.7

Blood glucose management

High blood sugar status increases the risk of surgery. Blood glucose management is essential to ensure the safety of surgery. According to Article 30 evidence, regular monitoring of blood glucose levels and formulation of personalized blood glucose control programs for patients, including diet adjustment and drug therapy, can effectively control blood glucose levels and reduce surgical risks.7,25

Anemia management

Anemia can reduce the tolerance of patients to surgery, and anemia management is important to improve the surgical success rate. The cause and type of anemia can be determined through the examination of anemia-related indicators. According to Article 31 evidence, a personalized anemia treatment plan should be developed for patients, including supplementation of nutrients such as iron and vitamin B12, to correct the anemia state and improve the tolerance of surgery.24

Effectiveness of prehabilitation in older patients with gastrointestinal tumor surgery

Strict supervision and regular evaluation of the prehabilitation program, as well as dynamic adjustment according to the evaluation results, are the key to ensure that the prehabilitation measures can effectively improve the patient's surgical tolerance and postoperative recovery quality. According to evidence 15, patients with an NRS2002 score ≥ 3 should use appropriate tools for nutritional status assessment, and patients with an NRS2002 score ≥ 5 or patient-generated subjective global assessment (PG-SGA) score ≥ 9 with severe malnutrition and requiring major surgery should be strengthened with prehabilitation management before surgery.7,24,26,27,29 Nutritional assessment is not limited to simple changes in weight and appetite but should also reflect the true nutritional status of patients through biochemical indicators (such as serum albumin, transferrin, etc.).36 According to Exhibit 23, the assessment of motor capacity measures the patient's physical reserve and functional recovery through specific motor tests.16,17,30 The results of each evaluation should be used as a basis for subsequent intervention adjustments to ensure that the program meets the individual needs of the patient.39 According to evidence 31, all gastric cancer patients receiving prehabilitation management need to be re-evaluated after prehabilitation, including nutritional status, exercise ability, psychological state, and functions of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular, liver, kidney, lung, and other important organs.7,20 If the surgical requirements are not met, prehabilitation can be performed again; if the surgical standard is not met after the second cycle of prehabilitation , the treatment plan needs to be adjusted.7

The implementation of a preoperative prehabilitation program does not only rely on the initial intervention but also requires continuous tracking and dynamic adjustment throughout the rehabilitation process to ensure that each intervention can be timely optimized according to the actual changes of patients. Through regular evaluation, feedback, and adjustment, the quality of preoperative preparation of patients can be effectively improved, and finally the safety of surgery and the speed of postoperative recovery can be improved.

Strengths and limitations

This study employs a rigorous, multi-methodological approach to evidence synthesis, combining systematic literature reviews, dual independent assessments, standardized quality appraisals, and expert consultations to minimize bias and enhance analytical validity. By integrating clinical expertise with empirical evidence, the findings generate actionable recommendations that address the translational gap between research and practice. Additionally, the development of patient-centered guidelines tailored to older adults considers contextual factors (e.g., institutional resources, cultural diversity) and incorporates interdisciplinary insights from geriatrics, nutrition, psychology, and rehabilitation sciences. This holistic framework captures the complexity of perioperative care for heterogeneous older populations and promotes collaborative care models. However, three primary limitations warrant consideration: Application in resource-constrained settings is challenged by staffing shortages (e.g., geriatric/rehabilitation specialists), high-cost interventions, and infrastructure limitations affecting protocol adherence. Adaptation strategies such as task-shifting to nurses, utilizing phone-based support, and prioritizing core components could enhance feasibility in these contexts. Another limitation is the framework's initial assumption of population homogeneity underestimates cultural variables (e.g., mental health stigma, family decision-making norms) that critically impact psychological intervention efficacy. Implementing culturally tailored protocols, including family-inclusive approaches where appropriate, is therefore essential. Furthermore, Traditional Chinese Medicine components (Baduanjin, five-element music therapy, acupressure) face cross-cultural implementation barriers due to: (i) Philosophical concept misalignment (e.g., Qi, meridians), (ii) Scarcity of trained practitioners/resources, (iii) Regulatory misalignment in non-TCM healthcare systems. Therefore, this involves adapting or substituting these elements with locally relevant, evidence-based, and culturally congruent alternatives that align with regional beliefs, healthcare practices, and patient preferences to ensure acceptability and effectiveness.

Clinical implications for oncology nursing practice

The findings of this study translate into several key actionable recommendations for oncology nurses caring for older patients undergoing gastrointestinal tumor surgery. Firstly, nurses play a pivotal role in the early identification and risk stratification of patients using validated tools (e.g., clinical frailty scale [CFS] for frailty, NRS2002/MUST for nutrition) to determine optimal prehabilitation timing (1–4 weeks pre-surgery) and intensity. Secondly, nurses are central to orchestrating and facilitating multidisciplinary collaboration, ensuring seamless communication between surgeons, nutritionists, physiotherapists, psychologists, and other specialists to develop and implement cohesive, individualized prehabilitation plans. Thirdly, nurses are the primary implementers and monitors of core interventions: executing tailored nutritional support, guiding safe exercise regimens (including integrating TCM practices like Baduanjin where appropriate), providing psychological support (utilizing cognitive-behavioral techniques and TCM methods like five-element music therapy), and managing key parameters like blood glucose, thrombosis risk, and anemia, while diligently tracking patient progress and adherence. Crucially, nurses must dynamically evaluate patient response using relevant metrics and feed back to the team to enable timely adjustments to the prehabilitation strategy. Finally, ongoing patient and family education regarding the goals and components of prehabilitation is essential to foster engagement. To effectively fulfill these roles, commitment to continuous professional development in geriatric oncology prehabilitation principles and practices is paramount. Even in resource-constrained settings, nurses can prioritize fundamental screening, essential education, core nutritional support, and promotion of feasible physical activity.

Conclusions

This study systematically summarized 33 key pieces of evidence for preoperative prehabilitation of older patients with gastrointestinal tumor surgery, covering 7 core dimensions: Implementation timing and team, Preoperative risk Assessment, Nutritional Intervention, Exercise Intervention, Psychological Support, Specialized Management and Supervision & Evaluation. In clinical practice, a complete prehabilitation management system for older patients undergoing gastrointestinal tumor surgery should be built based on the management regulations of hospitals and departments, the actual situation of patients, and the feasibility and applicability of various evidences, so as to ensure that each patient can obtain the most suitable prehabilitation program, so as to maximize the success rate of surgery and the quality of life of patients. While the proposed rehabilitation framework is applicable to global geriatric surgical populations, the integration of TCM interventions offers unique advantages in Chinese clinical settings. We recommend healthcare professionals worldwide prioritize cultural adaptability, particularly concerning psychological support and other culturally embedded interventions, when implementing protocols across diverse regions. This requires careful consideration of local beliefs, practices, resource availability, and patient preferences to ensure relevance and effectiveness.
