How Early Is Early?
Authors: Peter Calvert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: ablation, atrial fibrillation, cryoballoon, early

Atrial fibrillation (AF) may cause a variety of symptoms—from palpitations to breathlessness, fatigue to clinical heart failure (HF). The coexistence of AF and HF is not infrequently seen in practice,1 and may significantly worsen both symptoms and prognosis.2,3 AF and HF, individually and together, result in complex neurohormonal and hemodynamic effects, with adverse cardiac remodeling.4 In many cases, restoration of sinus rhythm may ameliorate symptoms and reverse the remodeling process, substantially improving both quality of life and longer-term prognosis.5

At what stage to intervene remains an important question. In this issue of JACC: Asia, Ando et al6 present a study analyzing outcomes of early cryoballoon ablation for AF in patients with HF. This editorial seeks to put their findings into the greater context of AF management, as well as the recent concept of “early rhythm control.”

Does Rhythm Control Outperform Rate Control?

Seminal studies failed to demonstrate any clear benefit of rhythm control over and above rate control. For example, AFFIRM (Atrial Fibrillation Follow-up Investigation of Rhythm Management) in 2002 showed no difference in mortality, stroke, major bleeding, or cardiac arrest, and in fact found a higher rate of adverse events with antiarrhythmic drugs (AADs).7 AFFIRM focused on patients without HF, however, and was limited by high rates of crossover in both arms. The later AF-CHF (Atrial Fibrillation and Congestive Heart Failure) trial in 2008 studied patients with reduced ejection fraction HF with similar study limitations.8

A further limitation of both of these older studies was the virtual absence of catheter ablation, which is now recognized to be superior to AADs for maintaining sinus rhythm.9,10 In addition, AADs tend to be more toxic than rate control agents, and long-term use may be detrimental, offsetting the potential benefits of restoring sinus rhythm. This concept is supported by later studies such as CASTLE-AF (Catheter Ablation versus Standard Conventional Therapy in Patients with Left Ventricular Dysfunction and Atrial Fibrillation), which demonstrated improvements in mortality, HF hospitalization, and ventricular functional recovery with ablation over medical (70% rate control) therapy.11 Furthermore, a subanalysis of pharmacologically treated rate vs rhythm control patients in CASTLE-AF concurred with earlier studies, finding no benefit of drug-based rhythm control.12

These findings support the concept that catheter ablation may be of significant symptomatic and prognostic benefit to those with combined AF and HF. This is especially true where AF is itself the cause of HF—so-called “tachycardiomyopathy”—where guidelines give a Class I recommendation for catheter ablation.13

Is Early Rhythm Control Beneficial?

It is well-established that prolonged exposure to AF is undesirable. Over time, the atria dilate, stretching the atrioventricular valve annuli and causing functional regurgitation. Electroanatomic mapping in such patients often demonstrates areas of fibrotic atrial scar, and patients with longer-standing AF are more challenging to maintain in sinus rhythm.14 Much like a malignancy, therefore, it stands to reason that early intervention should be better, catching the disease before it can progress. Although this is an oversimplification of a complex underlying metabolic pathology (ablation does not, for example, improve obesity or diabetic control), it remains true that rhythm outcomes in paroxysmal AF are superior to those in persistent AF and may prevent progression of the arrhythmia.15

The recent EAST AF-NET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention Trial 4) randomized more than 2,700 patients to rate or rhythm (mostly drug-based) control in patients with AF diagnosed in the prior 12 months. The study was stopped early for efficacy, demonstrating a significant reduction in a composite of cardiovascular death, stroke, HF hospitalization, or hospitalization for acute coronary syndrome.16 Although there were issues with adherence and loss to follow-up, the findings are compelling and support the logic established previously, and later retrospective evidence showed that the study generalizes well to larger populations.17 The EARLY-AF (Early Aggressive Invasive Intervention for Atrial Fibrillation) study also showed that catheter ablation outperformed antiarrhythmic therapy for arrhythmia suppression when initiated within 2 years of AF diagnosis.18 A retrospective study from Taiwan also demonstrated the utility of early rhythm control in Asian patients, finding reductions in stroke, HF, myocardial infarction, and mortality.19

HF and Early Catheter Ablation

So far, a few premises have been established in relation to patients with combined HF and AF. First, restoration of sinus rhythm is beneficial, both in terms of symptoms and prognosis. Second, catheter ablation maintains sinus rhythm better than AADs while avoiding toxic side effects. Third, early rhythm control works better, when less adverse remodeling has occurred. It therefore stands to reason that early AF ablation should benefit patients with combined AF and HF.

A few factors remain uncertain—in particular, the optimal timing for catheter ablation in these patients. This is what Ando et al6 set out to analyze in the present study. The investigators retrospectively studied 543 patients with HF across 17 centers in Japan. They found that cryoballoon ablation within 6 months of AF diagnosis was associated with a 10% absolute reduction in AF recurrence across follow-up (P = 0.018). The 6-month cutoff was chosen as the median time between AF diagnosis and cryoablation—notably, the “early” group was ablated a median of 0.3 years post-AF diagnosis, compared with 2 years in the “late” group, although some cases in the latter group extended beyond 7 years—subanalysis stratifying by differing time-to-ablation showed a consistent effect, with the longest times having worse arrhythmia recurrence.

Prognostic outcomes (mortality and HF hospitalization) were not improved by early ablation in this study, contrasting with what may be expected based on prior randomized studies. Furthermore, most cases of HF involved preserved ejection fraction (390 of 543 [72%]), and subanalysis found no difference in the low ejection fraction cohort.

The rate of major complications was high at approximately 4%, mostly driven by persistent phrenic nerve palsy—a recognized problem with cryoablation. Touch-up ablation for incomplete cryoballoon pulmonary vein isolation was required in more than a quarter of patients, highlighting the inflexibility of the cryoballoon. With these limitations, and those inherent to a retrospective study, the optimal timing of ablation remains uncertain, although this study suggests that earlier may be better.

Remaining Uncertainties

Aside from optimal ablation timing, at least 2 other questions are important to consider. First, what burden of AF should prompt consideration of early ablation? This is particularly relevant, as many patients with HF have implanted devices, with recent studies beginning to analyze outcomes in those with “subclinical AF.” Second, how should we decide if a patient is suitable for catheter ablation? Meaningful restoration of sinus rhythm may be unlikely in those with severe atrial dilatation, in which case a pace-and-ablate strategy may be more optimal.4 Early pulmonary vein ablation is not a solution for all.

Finally, rhythm control is but one part of the guideline-recommended “ABC” pathway for managing AF.20,21 Adherence to the pathway has been shown to improve outcomes in AF.22,23 Studies assessing the benefits of early rhythm control alongside optimal “ABC” pathway adherence may be of benefit.

Conclusions

Catheter ablation of AF for patients with concomitant HF may provide both symptomatic and prognostic benefit. Optimal patient selection is important, and mounting evidence suggests that ablation early in the course of AF—before adverse atrial remodeling—may provide greater benefit. However, the degree of AF required to benefit, along with optimal timing, requires further study.
