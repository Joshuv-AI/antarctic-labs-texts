Engineered novel dually cross-linked hydrogel system embedded with Apigenin–cyclodextrin complex for surgical wound healing
Authors: Zakir Ali, Fatima Zahid, Ainy Butt, Muhammad Owais Ahmad, Ali H Alamri, Adel Al Fatease, Zahrah Ali Asiri, Nabil K Alruwaili, Muhammad Aamir Zohaib, Fakhar ud Din
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurgical wounds present a critical clinical challenge often requiring advanced therapeutic strategies to accelerate healing and enhance tissue regeneration. Herein, we engineered novel dually cross-linked hydrogel system incorporating an Apigenin-β-cyclodextrin (API–βCD) inclusion complex for enhanced wound therapy. First, the API–βCD inclusion complex was prepared and characterized comprehensively through nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffractometry (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), molecular docking and phase solubility studies. Then, dually cross-linked hydrogel matrix embedded with the API–βCD complex was synthesized and assessed for comprehensive structural, mechanical and physiochemical properties. In vitro release, ex vivo permeation, cell viability, antibacterial activity, wound closure and anti-inflammatory cells analyses of the API–βCD hydrogel were performed. Results confirmed the formation of successful inclusion complex. Phase solubility study demonstrated 66.8 ~ fold increase in the aqueous solubility of API. FTIR analysis confirmed successful crosslinking within the hydrogel matrix. Moreover, API–βCD hydrogel exhibited enhanced mechanical properties, porous architecture and optimal swelling and degradation behaviors. In vitro and ex vivo studies demonstrated sustained release, minimal permeation and enhanced disposition (57%) of API in skin layers, higher cell viability, and augmented antibacterial potential. Additionally, API–βCD hydrogel demonstrated significantly accelerated wound healing with 97% wound closure and noticeable downregulation of inflammatory parameters, such as TNF-α and IL-1β. These results concluded that API–βCD hydrogel may improve the solubility, dissolution of API and holds promising potential for regenerative wound management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56677-1.Keywords: Apigenin, Solubility, Polygalacturonic acid, Hydrogel system, Wound healingSubject terms: Biotechnology, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Materials science

Introduction

Wound management is a substantial global healthcare challenge imposing a significant economic burden on global healthcare expenditures amounting to billions of dollars annually. Recently, an increasing focus has been paid to the progression of innovative wound dressing technologies1. It was reported earlier2 that significantly accelerated re-epithelialization was achieved in both acute and partial-thickness wounds, when preserved in a moist milieu. According to hasan et al., 20233, ideal wound dressing foster a moist environment at the wound site, possess good tissue compatibility, adequate mechanical and physical stability and prevent the intrusion of external bacteria. Among contemporary wound dressing materials, hydrogels offer exceptional advantage by maintaining a moist environment, absorbing excess exudate, delivering therapeutic agents and promote a physiologically promising milieu for cellular proliferation and tissue regeneration4.

Polysaccharides based hydrogels have garnered a vital role in biomedical applications due to their intrinsic biocompatibility, biodegradability and significant potential to simulate natural 3D tissue environments to support cellular functions, including cell adhesion and proliferation. Polygalacturonic acid (PGA) is one such naturally occurring polysaccharide abundantly found in the cell walls of ripe fruits and vegetables. The biocompatible and non-toxic nature of PGA underscores it as versatile functional biopolymer with numerous applications, including composite membranes for bone tissue engineering, anti-adhesion films and drug delivery systems5. Moreover, the three-dimensional scaffolds or hydrogels are formed by using chemical cross-linkers such as calcium (Ca2⁺) which is a capable of crosslinking with COOH functionality of polysaccharides6. However, limited stability in physiological environment, poor mechanical strength and inadequate structural integrity for long-term applications are the major limitations of these physically cross-linked polysaccharide hydrogels7. To accomplish an optimal balance between mechanical robustness and controlled degradability, dual cross-linking strategy has recently gained significant scientific focus8. Therefore, glutaraldehyde (GA) was selected for dual cross-linking in this study. It is a commonly employed bi-functional crosslinking agent with the ability to form covalent bonds with nucleophilic functional groups, like hydroxyl and amine, in polymeric networks. Also it is a well-known crosslinking agent for polysaccharides9.

Natural compounds, such as polyphenols, have emerged as a substitute to synthetic chemicals in a wide range of applications such as food preservation, nutraceutical development and alternative medicine10. Moreover, they have demonstrated anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, neuroprotective, potent antioxidant and anticancer properties. Apigenin (API), is one such polyphenolic flavonoid, found abundantly in the chamomile plant, various culinary herbs like thyme, celery and parsley and dietary sources such as honey and various fruits including lemon, grapes and oranges11. API has multifaceted pharmacological actions, most prominently its potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, neuroprotective and anticancer potential has attracted substantial scientific attention12,13. Despite its significant pharmacological promise, clinical utility of API remains hindered primarily due to its extremely low water solubility (2.16 μg/mL), and limited lipid solubility (0.001–1.63 mg/mL)14, which required advance delivery strategies in order to improve its therapeutic efficacy.

Multiple strategic approaches have been explored to enhance solubility, including particle size reduction, micro-environmental pH adjustment, and co encapsulation techniques, molecular complexation and supercritical fluid technologies15. Among these, cyclodextrin complexation has emerged as a compelling approach due to simplicity, efficiency and versatility. These cyclic oligosaccharides have the capability to form host–guest complex based on non-covalent linkage with wide range of hydrophobic compounds, organic ligands and metal ions. Among the cyclodextrins, Beta-cyclodextrin (βCD) is commonly employed in pharmaceutical formulations as stabilizer and carrier to augment the stability, solubility and bioavailability of hydrophobic drugs16. The FDA has classified it as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) agents. Owing to its suitable cavity dimension, it has the capability to accommodate the drugs with varied molecular weights17.

In this study, we engineered novel dually cross-linked hydrogel system by incorporating API-βCD complex into bio-functional PGA matrix for surgical wound healing. The novelty of this work lies in combining three functional components within a single system: solubility enhancement through βCD inclusion complex, mechanical reinforcement via dual cross-linking, and localized delivery within a PGA hydrogel matrix. While each component has been studied independently, to our knowledge their integrated implementation in this specific structured configuration for surgical wound management has not been previously reported. The comparative advantages of the API-βCD hydrogel over the available hydrogel systems is summarized in Supplementary table ST1. This system was rationally designed, extensively characterized and evaluated in in vitro, ex vivo and in vivo settings to ensure localized delivery and drug retention with sustained release profile that support wound tissue regeneration. Full thickness incisional wound model was established to evaluate the in vivo efficacy of the API-βCD hydrogel in mice, allowing evaluation of wound contraction and tissue regeneration.

Material and methods

Chemicals and utilizable

Β-cyclodextrin (Molecular weight (MW): 1134.98), Calcium chloride dihydrate and Di-potassium hydrogen phosphate were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Steinheim, Germany). Polygalacturonic acid (degree of esterification (< 25%) and MW: 25000–5000) and Apigenin (API, MW: 270.24) were procured from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Glutaraldehyde (25% aqueous solution), and Sodium hydroxide were purchased from Icon Chemicals (Schlüchtern, Germany). Potassium dihydrogen phosphate was purchased from Duksan Pure Chemicals Co., Ltd (Ansan, South Korea). Semi permeable membrane was obtained from Membrane solution USA. All other chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade purity.

Cell culture

The scratch assay and cytotoxicity studies were conducted on human keratinocyte (HaCaT) cells. Culture facility of the College of Pharmaceutical Sciences Soochow University, (Suzhou, China) was used for cell culturing. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin–streptomycin (1% v/v) was used to support optimal growth and maintenance of the cultured cells. In case of cytotoxicity assay, cell suspension at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL were seeded in each well plate of the 96-well plates. In case of scratch assay, cell suspension at a density of 1.5 × 10⁶ cells/mL were seeded per well of 6-well plates. Standard culture conditions of 37 °C, 5% CO₂ and 95% relative humidity were used for cells growth.

Animals

Male BALB/c mice weighing 25–30 g were sourced from the National Institute of Health (NIH), Islamabad, Pakistan. They were housed under standard laboratory conditions with ad libitum access to standard rodent chow and drinking water. Environmental parameters such as temperature and relative humidity were maintained at 25 ± 2 °C and 50–60%, respectively. All animal study procedures were conducted in a pathogen free environment in full compliance with the NIH Animal Welfare Policy and the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines, following the consent of Bioethical Committee of Quaid-i-Azam University, Islamabad, Pakistan via approval# BEC-FBS-QAU2024-591.

Preparation of API-βCD inclusion complex

Co-precipitation method was adapted for the fabrication of API-βCD complex as described by Jiang (2019)18. Briefly, βCD (0.0176 M) was completely solubilized under thermal stimulation at 60 °C in double distilled water utilizing a hot plate. Afterwards, βCD solution was gradually cooled to 40 °C. To promote host–guest molecular recognition and complex nucleation, an ethanolic solution of API was meticulously added with continuous stirring to the βCD solution. This mixture was then allowed to stir in a dark milieu for 5 h and incubated subsequently at 4 °C overnight to promote precipitation of the inclusion complex. Isolation of the precipitate was carried out via vacuum filtration, washed carefully with 30% ethanolic solution (Thrice) to remove un-complexed API from βCD surface and lyophilized to acquire a dry, purified API-βCD complex for further analysis.

Determination of the complexation efficiency

The API encapsulated content in the βCD host matrix was determined by dissolving 10 mg of API-βCD complex in 20 mL ethanol, followed by sonication for 2 min to facilitate complete disruption of the complex and quantitative extraction of API. Quantification of API was accomplished via UV spectrophotometer (UV-1601, Shimadzu, Japan) at wavelength of 267 nm. Characterization of API-βCD complex

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy

NMR (1H and 13C NMR) analysis was conducted to confirm the formation of the API-βCD complex using Bruker Avance III 400 MHz Spectrometer. Briefly, 10 mg of the lyophilized complex, API and βCD were dissolved in deuterated dimethyl-sulfoxide (DMSO-d₆) and transferred to NMR tubes. 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectra were recorded at 400 and 100 MHz, respectively at ambient temperature. Chemical shifts (δ) were reported in parts per million (ppm) referenced to the residual DMSO-d₆ peak (2.48 ppm for 1H and 39.52 ppm for 13C)20.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

FTIR analysis was carried out using a Shimadzu IRAffinity-1S spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto 604–8511, Japan) furnished with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. FTIR spectra of API, βCD and API-βCD complex were acquired in mid infra-red region (4000–500 cm⁻1) with 32 scans at spectral resolution of 4 cm⁻1. Samples in solid form were positioned directly onto the ATR crystal surface, and to ensure optimal contact and reproducibility of spectra, they were pressed uniformly using the built-in pressure applicator21.

X-ray diffractometry (XRD) analysis

XRD (Rigaku Co., Tokyo, Japan) analysis was conducted to investigate the crystalline characteristics of pure API, βCD and API-βCD complex in order to gain insight into changes in crystallinity accompanying complex formation. XRD fortified with a Ni-filtered Cu-Kα monochromatic radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å), operated at an accelerating voltage of 40 kV and a constant filament current of 100 mA was utilized for this purpose. Samples were scanned at ambient temperature and 2θ range of 10° to 80° with step size of 0.01822.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

SEM (JEOLJSM-6490A, Japan) analysis of pure API, βCD and API-βCD complex was conducted to examine any morphological changes accompanying inclusion complex formation. Double-sided carbon adhesive tape was used to affix the samples onto aluminum stubs and subsequently sputter-coated with thin layer of gold to ensure optimal conductivity. SEM was operated at an excitation voltage of 30 kV23.

Solubility studies

Solubility analysis of pure API and API-βCD complex was carried out in distilled water at 25 ± 1 °C24. API and API-βCD complex in excess were dispersed in 10 mL distilled water independently in sealed glass vials and stirred for 24 h. After attaining equilibrium, solutions were filtered through a membrane filter (0.45 µm) and quantified via UV spectrophotometer. All analysis were carried out in triplicate (n = 3).

Molecular docking

Host–guest complex interaction of API and βCD was elucidated using AutoDock Vina (v1.1.2). PubChem repository was used to retrieve the 3-D coordinates of βCD (PubChem CID: 444041) and API (PubChem CID: 5280443) in SDF format. To facilitate molecular docking, SDF files were subsequently converted to PDB format using PyMOL (v2.5.2). Moreover, preparation of molecular structure was carried out in AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6), where both ligands were protonated at physiological pH and Gasteiger charges were allocated. API was designated as ligand and βCD was defined as receptor (macromolecule). All hydrophobic hydrogen atoms were merged, and rotatable bonds were identified to signify ligand torsional flexibility. To comprehend the prime binding site the docking grid was positioned to enclose the cavity of βCD. Based on the anticipated binding affinities the top-ranked binding poses were evaluated in Discovery Studio Visualizer (v2021) to ascertain key intermolecular interactions with the βCD cavity including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and π–π stacking25.

Preparation and optimization of API-βCD-embedded dual cross-linked hydrogel

Dual cross-linked calcium, polygalacturonic acid and glutaraldehyde (Ca-PGA-GA) hydrogel was fabricated by employing a two-step crosslinking strategy. Initially, Ca-PGA ionic assembly was engineered, followed by covalent GA bridging to reinforcing the nascent network, to develop a porous, yet mechanically robust scaffold. Briefly, PGA (2%) was dissolved in water with continuous magnetic stirring until a homogeneous solution was obtained. CaCl2 solution at different molar concentrations (0.25, 0.5 and 1 relative to PGA concentration) were prepared separately. To initiate prompt gelation through Ca2⁺–carboxylate coordination CaCl2 solution was introduced drop wise into PGA solution. To ensure the attainment of uniform ionic crosslinking, this mixture was allowed to stir for 30 min. Thereafter, it was transferred to a mildly acidic GA solution and allowed to crosslink with residual hydroxyl group of PGA under mild stirring to achieve optimum integrity of the scaffold. After reaction completion washing was carried out with deionized water thrice. For comparative evaluation PGA-Ca single cross-linked hydrogel (PGA-Ca hydrogel) and PGA-GA single crossed linked hydrogel (PGA-GA hydrogel) were also prepared as per the above protocols. Similarly, API–βCD hydrogel and API hydrogel were prepared by adding API–βCD/API after the formation of the ionic Ca–PGA hydrogel network under gentle stirring to ensure homogeneous distribution of the drug within the matrix followed by covalent crosslinking with GA.

Characterization of the hydrogel

FTIR and SEM analysis of the hydrogels were conducted following the procedure described in the previous section. FTIR analysis was conducted for individual components like CaCl2.2H2O, PGA and GA along with ionically and dually cross-linked hydrogels.

Mechanical strength and tissue adhesion properties

Cylindrical hydrogel samples (diameter: 10 mm; thickness: 6 mm) were subjected to mechanical evaluation using a universal testing apparatus (CMT6202, MTS Systems Co., Ltd., China). Compressive characterization was performed with a 200 N load cell at a crosshead velocity of 20 mm/min. The maximum compressive stress at specimen failure was recorded as compressive strength. All the values were presented as mean ± SD (n = 3)27. Tissue adhesion evaluation employed fresh porcine skin specimens (1 × 2 cm2) with subcutaneous fatty tissue removed and rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) for 2 h. Hydrogel formulations were deposited onto the adipose layer of the prepared skin samples. Two treated skin specimens were positioned with their adhesive surfaces in contact, establishing an overlap region of 1 × 1 cm2. Bio-adhesive strength was quantified using a tensile testing instrument equipped with a 10 N load cell operating at a displacement rate of 10 mm/min. Five independent replicates of each hydrogel were tested27.

Amplitude sweep rheological analysis

The mechanical resilience of the prepared hydrogels was evaluated using amplitude sweep rheological testing on a controlled-stress rheometer (Anton Paar MCR 302) equipped with parallel-plate geometry (25 mm diameter, 1–2 mm gap) at 25 ± 1 °C. Amplitude sweeps were conducted by progressively increasing the oscillatory strain amplitude from 0.1 to 100% at a constant angular frequency of 1 rad/s (or 0.1 Hz), with the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) recorded at each strain interval. The critical strain (xStrain) at the G′/G″ crossover point—where G′ equals G″—was identified as the yield point, representing the gel-to-sol transition28.

Hydrogel volume was estimated from the difference in ethanol level before and after hydrogel immersion. Physiological stability

A systematic weight loss study of the hydrogel system was conducted to evaluate its degradation kinetics under physiological conditions. Freeze-dried hydrogel scaffolds were weighed (initial weight W1) and immersed in PBS (pH 7.4) and incubated at 37 °C. Samples were retrieved at predetermined time intervals, surface moisture was removed with careful blotting via filter paper and freeze-dried subsequently to acquire residual dry weight (W2). The extent of hydrogel degradation over time was assessed quantitatively by calculating the percentage weight loss31.

In vitro release study

Dialysis bag diffusion method was employed to evaluate the drug release kinetics of pure API, API-βCD complex and API-βCD hydrogel at pH 6.8, corresponding to the pH of acute wound. Pre-soaked dialysis membranes were carefully filled with each formulation (equivalent to 5 mg of API) and sealed at both ends to prevent leakage. The sealed bags were then placed in a beaker containing 100 mL of the release media. The beakers were kept in a thermostatically controlled shaking water bath with continuous agitation at 100 rpm and maintained at 37 ± 1 °C. At predetermined time points (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12 and 24 h), 2 mL of the release media was collected and immediately replenished with same volume of pre-warmed fresh buffer. Amount of drug released in each sample was quantitatively determined via UV–visible spectrophotometer, followed by calculation of cumulative drug release. The drug release mechanism was further elucidated by applying various mathematical models i.e. Zero order, First order, Korysmeyer-Peppas, Weibull, Higuchi and Hixson–Crowell. Statistical parameters, including Model Selection Criterion (MSC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the coefficient of determination (R2) were used to evaluate the model fitting and predictive accuracy. The release kinetics and drug release mechanisms were best explained by the model demonstrating lowest AIC, highest R2 and MSC values32.

Ex vivo permeation analysis

Ex vivo permeation profiles of API-βCD hydrogel and API hydrogel were assessed using a Franz diffusion cell apparatus (PermeGear, USA) with effective diffusion area of 0.77 cm2. Excised rat skin was utilized as diffusion membrane to evaluate drug permeation33. The prepared skin was mounted securely between donor and receptor chamber of the Franz cells, with stratum corneum facing the donor chambers. PBS (pH 7.4; 5 mL) was added to the receptor chambers and maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C with continuous stirring using a magnetic bar. Precisely weighed 0.5 g of both API-βCD hydrogel and API hydrogel (equivalent to 2 mg of API) were placed in individual donor compartment. One mL of receptor solution was withdrawn at different time intervals (0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h) and instantly replenished with the same volume of pre-warmed fresh buffer. Drug content in all samples was analyzed via UV–visible spectrophotometer.

Skin deposition study

After 24 h of ex vivo permeation analysis, the excised skin portion was collected and rinsed with PBS (pH 7.4). The cleaned skin was subsequently sectioned into small pieces followed by incubation in 2 mL ethanol for 24 h. The tissues were then subjected to homogenization using a high shear mixer (D-91126, Heidolph, Germany) and the homogenate was filtered through a 0.22-µm syringe filter followed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 15 min34. The API was quantified in the supernatant as discussed earlier.

Preparation of hydrogel leachate

The extract solution of blank and API–βCD hydrogels were obtained by incubating the hydrogels in DMEM for 24 h at a concentration of 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 g/mL. The resulted extract solutions were then subjected to sterilization using 0.22 µm syringe filters.

Determination of residual GA concentration

The residual GA content in the hydrogel was determined through 3-methyl-2-benzothiazolinone hydrazone hydrochloride (MBTH) analytical assay as reported by35. GA react with MTBH to form azine followed by subsequent oxidation by ferric acid in acidic medium resulting in the formation of blue-green chromophore detectable by UV–Visible spectrophotometry. Briefly, 10 g of hydrogel sample was immersed in 20 mL distilled water and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under gentle shaking to extract residual GA followed by centrifugation to obtain GA extract. MBTH solution and ferric ammonium sulfate solution were prepared by dissolving 200 mg/L MTBH in water and 1800 mg/mL ferric ammonium sulfate in 0.2 mol/L HCl, respectively. Then 1 mL of hydrogel extract was mixed with 1 mL of MTBH solution followed by incubation for 15 min at 30 °C to facilitate the formation of azine intermediate. This was followed by the addition of ferric ammonium sulfate solution (1 mL) and incubated for 40 min at 40 °C for color development. The absorbance was checked at 604 nm using UV- visible spectrophotometer. The concentration of residual GA was calculated from the calibration curve.

Scratch assay

The HaCaT cells were allowed to incubate until they reached 90–100% confluence. Then the culture medium was aspirated followed by gentle washing of the cell layer with PBS. Afterward, a straight-line scratch was created with the help of sterile 200 µL pipette tip. To ensure clean area and to remove the dislodged cells, wells were washed with PBS thrice. Consequently, 1 mL of API–βCD solution, API- hydrogel extract and API–βCD hydrogel extract were added to each well and cells were allowed to incubate for 24 h. For model control group, 1 mL of DMEM without FBS was added to stimulate nutrients deficient conditions. To assess cell migration, images of the scratched area at 0-, 12- and 24- h were captured via inverted microscope29.

Anti-bacterial assay

The antibacterial activity of the prepared formulations was evaluated against Escherichia coli (E.coli) ATCC 25922, Staphylococcus aureus (S.aureus) ATCC 6538 and Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) ATCC 43300. These bacterial cultures were grown in nutrient broth overnight at 37 °C, and to obtain inoculum density of 1.5 × 105 CFU/mL, they were subsequently diluted. Afterward, 195 μL aliquot of the bacterial suspension and varying concentration (3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 and 200 µg/mL) of API, API-βCD and API-βCD hydrogel in 5 μL of PBS were added into each well of 96-well plate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. Then the optical density was recorded using a microplate reader at wavelength of 620 nm at 24 h32.

In vivo wound healing model

Therapeutic potential of API-βCD hydrogel was evaluated by inducing full-thickness excisional wound healing in BALB/c mice. Briefly, a combination of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) was administered to anesthetize each mouse. Hair was depilated from the dorsal surface to ensure aseptic conditions. Full thickness wounds with uniform geometry and depth (10 × 10 mm) were created along the paravertebral axis via surgical excision. The mice were then divided into three groups (n = 6), including untreated control (Negative control), API-βCD hydrogel and API hydrogel treated groups at a dose of 20 mg/kg on a daily basis36. Sterile adhesive bandage was utilized to assure proper coverage and retention of hydrogel over the wound microenvironment. To preclude cross contamination, each mouse was housed individually under controlled laboratory conditions in sterile cage. Post-surgery, on day 0, 4, 7, 9, 11 and 14, macroscopic assessment was conducted via high resolution camera and quantitative analysis of wound area was performed through image J software. The primary metric of therapeutic efficacy was based on temporal evaluation of wound closure, calculated as the % wound area shrinkage comparative to the initial wound area (day 0). Histological analysis

Animals (n = 3) were euthanized on day 14 followed by careful excision of wound tissue encompassing full-thickness dermal and hypodermal layers. To preserve cellular and extracellular architecture, tissues were immediately fixed in 10% buffered formalin solution. Tissues were then subjected to standard histological techniques comprising of paraffin embedding followed by serial tissue sectioning of 3–4 µm thickness using a microtome. Representative sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining for histopathological assessment and quantification of collagen deposition, respectively38. Tissue sections were examined under an inverted phase contrast microscope (Olympus IMT-2, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of pro-inflammatory cytokines

Assessment of the inflammatory cascade in wound tissue was performed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) by quantifying the levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). Homogenization of the collected tissue samples was performed in radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer. The clear supernatant was collected after centrifugation of the homogenate at 14000 rpm for 30 min. ELISA kits (REF: PRS-20505Mo and REF:PRS-20295Mo, Biosharp Ltd.) were utilized for quantification of TNF-α and IL-1β levels, respectively39. The accuracy of the data was ensured by conducting each experiment in triplicate (n = 3).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad prism (Version 8.0.2) was used for statistical analysis. Statistical significance among groups was determined through One-way or Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. The data was plotted as mean ± SD (n = 3, n = 6). P values < 0.05 were considered significantly different between the tested groups.

Results and discussion

The co-precipitation method was opted for the development of API-βCD complexation, owing to its simplicity, operational convenience, high encapsulation and complexation efficiency along with stronger molecular interactions40. The quantity of guest molecules successfully entrapped within the host architecture (βCD cavity) was estimated in terms of %CE. A high %CE of 93.15 ± 0.9% was demonstrated by API recovery data, thereby demonstrating enhanced solubility and chemical stability of API, as reported earlier19. Comparable findings were observed by41, with 90.3 ± 0.9% entrapment for garlic oil-βCD inclusion complexes prepared by this method.

Evaluation of API-βCD complexation

NMR analysis

The formation of the API-βCD inclusion complex was confirmed through 1H-NMR Fig. 1IA-C and 13C-NMR Fig. 1IIA–C spectroscopy in DMSO-d₆, with comparative analysis of chemical shifts providing evidence for host–guest interaction and hydrogen bonding between the two components. The 1H-NMR spectrum (400 MHz) of the API-βCD inclusion complex displayed characteristic signals from both API and βCD, with notable chemical shift changes indicating successful complexation. The API (in complex) hydroxyl protons appeared at δ 12.95, 10.83, and 10.35 ppm, showing a limited cognizable shift compared to free API (δ 12.95, 10.84, 10.36 ppm), suggesting that the phenolic OH groups remain exposed thereby partaking in intermolecular hydrogen bonding with the CD cavity. The aromatic protons of API exhibited slight downfield shifts, with the B-ring protons resonating at δ 7.93 (+ 0.02) ppm and δ 6.92 ppm (J = 8.5 Hz), and the H-3 proton appearing at δ 6.78 ppm (+ 0.02), while the A-ring proton H-6 was observed at δ 6.19 (+ 0.01) ppm (J = 2.1 Hz). These subtle shifts indicated the encapsulation of API aromatic system within the hydrophobic βCD cavity. The βCD signals appeared alongside the API peaks, with the anomeric proton resonating as a singlet at δ 4.83 ppm, slightly shifted from the doublet observed in free βCD (δ 4.82 ppm, J = 3.6 Hz), suggesting conformational changes upon complexation. The βCD ring protons displayed characteristic signals as doublets at δ 3.67 ppm, multiplets at δ 3.59–3.67 ppm, a doublet at δ 3.56 ppm (J = 11.0 Hz), a singlet at δ 3.37 ppm, and a doublet at δ 3.30 ppm (J = 7.0 Hz), with minor shifts for H5 (-0.04), H4 (+ 0.01) and H3 (−0.02), compared to free β-cyclodextrin, indicating host–guest interactions stabilized by hydrogen bonding between API hydroxyl groups and βCD glycosidic oxygens. The 13C-NMR spectrum (101 MHz, DMSO-d₆) for the API-βCD inclusion complex revealed nineteen distinct carbon signals representing both molecular components, with API carbons appearing at δ 182.22 (C-4 carbonyl), δ 164.59, 164.21, 161.90, 161.63, 157.77 (oxygenated aromatic carbons), δ 128.95, 121.63, 116.42 (aromatic carbons), δ 104.15, 103.30, 99.29, 94.43 (C-10, C-3, C-6, C-8), showing negligible shifts from free API, indicating that electronic environments of API remains largely unperturbed. The βCD carbons resonated at δ 102.40 (C-1), δ 81.99 (C-4), δ 73.51, 72.87, 72.49 (C-3, C-5, C-2), and δ 60.37 (C-6), exhibiting slight up-field shifts compared to free βCD, particularly for the internal cavity carbons C-3 and C-5, providing further evidence of API insertion into the hydrophobic cavity and stabilization through van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding interactions. PXRD analysis

Crystallographic features of pure API, βCD and API-βCD complex were assessed via PXRD (Fig. 2II) to elucidate any polymorphic changes associated with inclusion complex formation47. API exhibited several sharp and intense diffraction peaks mainly in the 2θ ranges of 10.7°, 13.8°, 15.4° and 26.3° confirming its well-ordered lattice arrangement and crystalline nature48. βCD also demonstrated several sharp and discrete peaks in the 2θ ranges of 12.1°, 15.4°, 17.5°, 19.1°, 21°, 23°, 25°, 27°, 30° and 34° confirming its structural regularity49. In marked contrast, API-βCD complex exhibited a significantly different diffractogram. The pattern was dominated by a broad, diffused halo, where sharp peaks attributed to API and βCD were absent or diminished significantly. The considerable reduction in crystallinity indicated that API had undergone a phase transition and was molecularly encapsulated within the hydrophobic cavity of βCD. The crystallization potential of API was efficiently suppressed by intimate molecular interaction and steric constraints enforced by the complexation process resulting in disordered or amorphous system. Conclusively, the PXRD findings authenticated the development of a true inclusion complex, instead of a simple physical mixture. Similar findings were reported for βCD inclusion complexes, containing essential oil47 and didymin50.

SEM analysis

The surface morphology and structural transitions associated with inclusion complex formation were assessed via SEM. The SEM images illustrated clear differences in API, βCD and API-βCD complex morphology providing a visual insight into the physical interaction between API and βCD (Fig. 2III–V). βCD depicted a highly ordered structure with well-defined crystalline particles, sharp edges and smooth facets (Fig. 2III). However, API exhibited agglomerated irregularly shaped particles, with rough, coarse and fragmented surface revealing its crystalline nature (Fig. 2IV). Nevertheless, a profound morphological transformation was observed in case of API-βCD complex (Fig. 2V). The crystalline characteristics observed in both API and βCD were absent in API-βCD complex and a more amorphous and diffused matrix was formed with indistinct boundaries. The development of a homogenized surface topology and disruption of the native crystal lattice supported the successful molecular level interaction between API and βCD. Similar findings were observed by23 for metronidazole inclusion complexes and50 for didymin inclusion complexes.

Molecular docking

The molecular docking analysis showed that API and βCD (1:1) form a stable and strong inclusion complex with a binding affinity of –8.1 kcal/mol. The docking conformation exhibited the effective encapsulation of API within the hydrophobic cavity of βCD, exhibiting excellent structural complementarity (Fig. 3II & III). Key non-covalent interactions were highlighted by detailed visualization of the complex including the involvement of βCD polar rims in hydrogen bonding with API hydroxyl groups. Van der Waals forces and π–π interactions also contributed to the stabilization of complex52. The thermodynamic stability of the complex was ensured by the collective contribution of these non-covalent forces. The well oriented fit of API within βCD cavity and promising binding energy demonstrated that βCD through the provision of a suitable molecular environment may enhance API physiochemical properties such as aqueous solubility and stability. Strong molecular level evidence was provided by these in silico findings for the effective host–guest complex formation that supported the potential application of βCD as a delivery vehicle for API into advanced drug delivery system.

Preparation and evaluation of API–βCD hydrogel

To improve the structural stability of the hydrogel while maintaining its functional adaptability, dual cross-linking strategies are adopted which involve two distinct crosslinking mechanisms, commonly a combination of covalent and secondary physical interactions. Dual cross-linked hydrogel systems have been widely reported providing a balance between mechanical strength and flexibility, making them suitable for biomedical applications53,54. In contrast, three or more simultaneous crosslinking mechanisms are involved in a multi-crosslinked hydrogel system, resulting in a more complex network architecture55. Although mechanical robustness may be enhanced in such systems, challenges related to network heterogeneity, degradation predictability, swelling behavior and reproducibility associated with drug release kinetics may be introduced. In comparison, a simpler and more controllable formulation strategy is offered by dual cross-linked hydrogels, while the desirable physicochemical and biological properties relevant to wound dressing applications are maintained56. Based on this rationale dual cross-linked PGA hydrogel was developed. Dually cross-linked hydrogel of PGA was prepared successfully through ionic crosslinking achieved with Ca2⁺ and further stabilization was achieved by covalent crosslinking with GA, as shown in supplementary Figs. SF3 and SF4. Initially, ionic crosslinking density was optimized using varying concentrations of calcium chloride (0.25, 0.5, and 1.0 M) relative to PGA concentration (Table 1). A mildly cross-linked, loose structured hydrogel was obtained at 0.25 M Ca2⁺, which may be due to insufficient ionic crosslinking between PGA chains57. When the concentration of Ca2⁺ was increased to 0.5 M, a well-formed hydrogel was obtained, indicating sufficient crosslinking to maintain the hydrogel structural integrity. However, with further increase in Ca2⁺ to 1.0 M, the hydrogel appeared brittle and exhibited cracking, which may be due to excessive crosslinking and reduced flexibility57. Therefore, PGA hydrogel with 0.5 M Ca2⁺ was selected for further cross-linking with GA to obtain a mechanically stable hydrogel matrix. GA was used in 2.5 and 3.5% (v/v) concentrations (from 25% aqueous solution of GA) for covalent crosslinking. Excellent structural integrity and homogeneity were observed for hydrogel with 2.5% (v/v) of a 25% aqueous GA stock solution, corresponding to an effective GA concentration of 0.625% (v/v), which demonstrated a balanced covalent cross-linked framework that strengthened the gel structure without compromising elasticity. In contrast, 3.5% (v/v) of a 25% aqueous GA stock solution, corresponding to an effective GA concentration of 0.875% (v/v), showed structural breakdown and syneresis (Table 1), which could be attributed to the over cross-linking, leading to expulsion of water, shrinkage and higher internal stress within the hydrogel matrix58.

FTIR analysis for the confirmation of cross-linking

The FTIR spectrum of API-βCD gel and its individual constituents, API, Ca, PGA, Ca-PGA and Ca-PGA-GA hydrogel were evaluated. The results convincingly demonstrated hierarchical crosslinking, initially through ionic crosslinking by Ca2+ followed by covalent GA crosslinking (Fig. 3IV). The PGA spectrum features distinct peaks in the region of 3350, 2926, 2852, 1735 1625, 1440 and 1234 cm-1 attributable to O-H, C-H, C = O and COO- (symmetric and asymmetric) stretching59. The CaCl₂ spectrum exhibited peaks in the region of 3360, 1630 cm-1 corresponding to lattice bound water and 655 cm-1 due to Ca-Cl stretching. Ca-PGA spectrum showed three distinct changes upon complexation, including a narrowed and slightly shifted O–H stretching, reduction in C = O band intensity and enhancement in COO- (both symmetric and asymmetric) band intensity with slight shift from 1625 to 1610 cm-1 and 1440 to 1434 cm-1. Similarly, It can be concluded from all these spectral changes that an ionic cross-linked architecture has been established by the coordination of Ca2⁺ ions to the PGA carboxylate moieties60. The pristine GA spectrum demonstrated signature peaks in the region of 3380, 1640 and 670 cm-1 due to characteristic vibration of OH, C = O and CH (out of plane) groups. The disappearance of C = O stretching band at 1640 cm-1 concomitant with a marked reduction in OH stretching band, collectively established the covalent acetal linkages development between GA aldehyde functionality and the hydroxyl group of PGA. Also, the retention of COO- stretching band at 1610 cm-1 in Ca-PGA-GA, endorsed the persistence of ionic coordination between divalent calcium ions and PGA carboxyl group. These findings validated the dual cross-linking mechanism within the hydrogel matrix encompassing both ionic and covalent interaction.

Morphological analysis

SEM analysis of API-βCD-Ca-PGA-GA hydrogel (API-βCD hydrogel) revealed a distinctive layered and lamellar architecture, suggesting a well-formed 3D porous matrix (Figs. 3V and SF5I & II). The dense network of folds and stratified microstructures, indicative of effective network formation may be attributed to the dual cross-linking mechanism i.e. ionic interactions between PGA’s carboxyl functionalities and divalent calcium ions coupled with covalent crosslinking facilitated by GA. Substantial structural integrity has evidently been conferred to the hydrogel by this dual cross-linking strategy as evidenced from persistently intact walls and an interconnected porous framework. The abundant network of micro- and nano-scale crevices observed across the hydrogel surface, is indicative of high porosity. In wound management, such a structural architecture is of prime importance, because it facilitates moisture retention, efficient exudate management and controlled therapeutic delivery, which are key attributes for effective and accelerated wound healing61.

Rheological characterization

The rheological properties of the hydrogel formulations, including API-βCD-PGA-GA, API-βCD-PGA-Ca, and API-βCD hydrogel, were characterized using steady-state shear flow analysis across a shear rate range of 0 to 3000 s⁻1. All three formulations exhibited pronounced shear-thinning (pseudoplastic) behavior (Fig. 4 I), characterized by a progressive decrease in apparent viscosity with increasing shear rate. This non-Newtonian flow behavior is desirable for pharmaceutical hydrogel formulations intended for topical wound dressing applications. At low shear rates (near 0 s⁻1), the API–βCD hydrogel demonstrated a highest initial viscosity (39876 ± 112 cP), followed by API-βCD-PGA-Ca (37899 ± 111 cP), while the API-βCD-PGA-GA formulation showed a lower initial viscosity (35718 ± 96 cP). This hierarchy reflected the superior network density and intermolecular interactions present in the API–βCD hydrogel formulation at rest. As shear rate increased, all formulations exhibited pronounced viscosity reduction, consistent with pseudoplastic flow behavior. However, the rate and extent of viscosity decline varied significantly among formulations. At higher shear rates (> 1500 s⁻1), the viscosity values approached similar plateaus, with API-βCD hydrogel, API–βCD-PGA-GA and API-βCD-PGA-Ca showing corresponding residual viscosity of 3000–4000 cP, 2000–2500 cP, and 1000–1500 cP at 3000 s⁻1. The API-βCD hydrogel maintained consistently higher viscosity values across the entire shear rate spectrum compared to single-polymer formulations, indicating superior network architecture and hydrogel structure. The elevated initial viscosity (~ 40,000 cP at near-zero shear) of the API–βCD hydrogel reflected the presence of a dual-polymer network wherein both Ca and GA components, appears to enhance network density through multiple types of interactions. The calcium-mediated electrostatic crosslinks, combined with hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions between the two polymers, create a more cohesive network structure resistant to flow-induced disruption62. The consistent maintenance of higher viscosity by the API–βCD hydrogel across the entire shear rate spectrum indicated superior network strength and more effective polymer chain interactions compared to single-polymer formulations.

Mechanical strength and tissue adhesion properties

The synthesized hydrogel formulations exhibited substantial elastic deformation capacity (Fig. 4II), with elongation-at-break values reaching 60%, coupled with rapid and complete shape recovery upon release of compressive loading, indicating favorable viscoelastic performance63. Quantitative assessment of uniaxial compression strength yielded the following values: API-βCD-PGA-GA (21.1 ± 0.98 kPa), API–βCD-PGA-Ca (23.15 ± 0.91 kPa) and API–βCD hydrogel (29.02 ± 0.78 kPa) (Fig. 4III). The enhanced mechanical characteristics observed in these formulations were attributed to multiple interaction mechanisms, including hydrogen bonding and ionic interactions. The measured bio-adhesive strengths were observed to be 6.7 ± 0.91, 7.8 ± 0.84 and 11.78 ± 0.6 kPa for API-βCD-PGA-GA, API–βCD-PGA-Ca and API–βCD hydrogel, respectively (Fig. 4IV). The adhesive functionality on the dermal tissue could be due to the formation of strong covalent linkage between aldehyde groups of GA and the terminal amine groups of the lysine and cystine residues to the skin. Moreover, the PGA has been reported to form hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions due to the free carboxy terminuses with the lysine/arginine residues and other dermal proteins64. These findings collectively demonstrated that dually cross-linked hydrogels exhibit superior adhesive properties and structural adaptability, rendering them suitable candidates for advanced wound protection.

Amplitude sweep rheological analysis

The mechanical resilience and viscoelastic capacity of the prepared hydrogel formulations is reported in Fig. 4V. The API–βCD hydrogel demonstrated a highest critical strain (65%) at the G′/G″ crossover point (G′ = G″), indicating superior network stability and mechanical resilience. In contrast, both the API–βCD-PGA-Ca hydrogel and API–βCD-PGA-GA hydrogel exhibited crossover points at 50% strain, corresponding to a 15-percentage-point differential compared to the API–βCD hydrogel. This significant difference in critical strain reflected the evidently different mechanical behavior of the three formulations under progressively increasing deformation. Beyond their respective crossover points (yield point), G″ exceeded G′ in all formulations, indicating the transition to predominantly viscous, liquid-like behavior. These results collectively demonstrated that the API–βCD hydrogel formulation maintained its elastic hydrogel-like network structure over a substantially wider strain range, endowing it with superior mechanical robustness65.

Porosity determination

API–βCD hydrogel exhibited porosity of 69.4 ± 1.27%, which is strategically situated within the optimal porosity range (60–90%) to facilitate robust cellular and neovascularization, crucial for tissue regeneration. It has been reported earlier that matrices exhibiting high porosity, typically 60–90% significantly enhance cellular infiltration for effective tissue regeneration66.

Swelling analysis

The swelling analysis of API–βCD hydrogel was performed in citrate buffer (pH 4.5), distilled water and PBS (pH 7.4) as reported in Fig. 5I. Rapid swelling of the hydrogel was observed in citrate buffer, reaching to a maximum (1050%) in 2 h, followed by significant weight reduction at 4–6 h. Two concomitant acid mediated phenomena may explain these findings. Firstly, reduction in the strength of Ca2⁺ mediated ionic crosslinking may occur due to protonation of carboxylate COO- group of PGA leading to loosened network and high influx of water67. Secondly, the ionic junction may be further disrupted by the presence of citrate ions, which act as a chelating agent and binds to free Ca2⁺ thereby resulting in initial high swelling. Erosion of gel matrix begins when ionic crosslinking is adequately compromised. PGA network may no longer be held together by Ca2⁺ resulting in weight loss, as observed after 4–6 h. Under basic conditions (PBS 7.4), the hydrogel exhibited a prominent initial uptake and swelled up to 1344% followed by a gradual decline. Initial rapid water uptake (6 h) may be driven by electrostatic repulsion within the hydrogel matrix and osmotic pressure. After the attainment of an equilibrated internal osmotic gradient, gradual leaching of the loosely bound polymer chains and (Ca2⁺ ↔ Na⁺) ion exchange predominated, thereby reducing the crosslinking density and causing expulsion of excess water, resulting in weight loss68. In water, a steady swelling plateau was observed after 4 h with hydrogel swelling up to 1156% and no significant reduction in weight was observed over 24 h. The crosslinking remained intact due to the absence of ion exchange and acid-catalyzed protonation68.

Degradation analysis

The degradation profile of API–βCD hydrogel is presented in Fig. 5II. A modest degradation of 7.19 ± 1.37% was observed on the first day, reaching 18.60 ± 2.10% on day 3, demonstrating that the structural integrity of hydrogel was maintained during the initial phase. Such early stability is advantageous for supporting cell adhesion and proliferation. The API–βCD hydrogel exhibited a degradation of 34.27 ± 3.84% on day 7. This steady degradation could be attributed to the exchange of divalent Ca2+ ions in the scaffold with Na+ ions present in PBS leading to fewer ionic crosslinking points in the hydrogel matrix. This ultimately may enhance the porosity of scaffold, facilitating cell infiltration, thereby leading to effective tissue regeneration69. Moreover, degradation was increased to 59.68 ± 2.72% on day 14, signifying a substantial but not complete breakdown. This degradation could be credited to the complete or near-complete dissociation of Ca2⁺ ion, and the progressive hydrolysis of acetal bonds, leading to destabilization of hydrogel network, which eventually leads to mass loss and softening. It has been reported earlier that hydrogels with a controlled degradation profile show superior wound management compared to hydrogel that degrade too rapidly or too slowly70.

In vitro release analysis

A comparative analysis of the in vitro release profiles of API was assessed for API suspension, API-βCD complex and API-βCD hydrogel (Fig. 5III). API suspension demonstrated poor release (10.33 ± 1.2%) in initial 2 h, followed by 36.5 ± 1.31% in 24 h. This limited release may be attributed to the poor aqueous dissolution profile of API, leading to restricted diffusion in physiological conditions. In contrast, significantly enhanced release profile (p-value < 0.001) was exhibited by API-βCD complex, where 67.91 ± 1.39% of the drug was released in first 2 h, reaching a plateau of 100% release in 4 h. This significant enhancement in drug release profile of API may be attributed to the development of an inclusion complex with βCD resulting in enhanced aqueous solubility, transition toward a more amorphous state and improved wettability, leading to rapid and efficient dissolution71. Notably, the release profile of API-βCD hydrogel exhibited sustained release behavior, and only 33.07 ± 1.34% release was observed in initial 2 h, followed by a gradual increase, reaching 96.48 ± 1.58% in 24 h. The release of API from API-βCD hydrogel was significantly enhanced (p-value < 0.001) as compared to the API suspension, while its release was significantly sustained (p-value < 0.001) as compared to the API-βCD complex. This extended release may be attributed to the secondary diffusion barrier provided by the hydrogel matrix through swelling and erosion-controlled mechanism, leading to prolonged release72. These results indicated a significant progression in API solubility, dissolution and a sustained release profile jointly by the βCD complexation and hydrogel matrix.

The underlying mechanisms of drug release were evaluated via kinetic modeling. The results of kinetic modeling are depicted in Table 2. The drug release from the API-βCD hydrogel system follows a two-compartment sequential release model, wherein free API generation within the gel (Stage 1) is coupled with free API diffusion from gel into dissolution media (Stage 2). The Korsmeyer-Peppas model (n = 0.654, indicating anomalous transport) quantitatively demonstrates that drug release during the principal phase (25–75% released) is governed by both complex dissociation and matrix diffusion simultaneously, rather than by a single dominant mechanism73. Specifically, the n-value of 0.654 (0.5 < n < 1.0) indicates that neither pure Fickian diffusion (n = 0.5) nor pure polymer relaxation (n ≥ 1.0) alone describes the kinetics, but rather both processes operate in tandem. At early time points, API-βCD complex dissociation at the gel-medium interface represents the rate-limiting step, generating free API within the gel matrix that subsequently accumulates and creates an intra-gel concentration gradient. Progressive gel degradation and chain relaxation synergistically reduce physical barriers to both complex dissociation and free API diffusion, effectively coupling these two processes into a unified anomalous transport mechanism73.

Ex vivo permeation analysis

An ex vivo permeation study was conducted to evaluate the effect of βCD complexation on the potential of API to cross the skin barrier. Figure 5IV depicted the results of ex vivo permeation analysis, demonstrating minimal permeation of 30.54 ± 1.14 µg/cm2 (1.5%) from API hydrogel over a period of 24 h, which could be attributed to its limited water solubility and restricted mobility in an aqueous environment. API-βCD hydrogel showed cumulative permeation of 108.18 ± 1.14 µg/cm2. The cumulative permeation values were normalized to 1 cm2, as conventionally reported for Franz diffusion studies, although the effective diffusion area of the Franz cell was 0.77 cm2. Accordingly, the actual permeated amounts for API–βCD hydrogel and API hydrogel were approximately 87.5 and 23.52 µg, corresponding to 4.16 and 1.18% of the applied 2 mg dose, respectively. This modest increase in permeation of API may be credited to its enhanced aqueous solubility and thermodynamic activity at skin interface. The enhancement in permeation following βCD complexation can be attributed to improved aqueous solubility and increased thermodynamic activity of API at the skin interface, facilitating its partitioning and diffusion across the stratum corneum. The overall permeation remained minimal in absolute terms, even if a reasonable improvement in permeation was conferred by βCD complexation, emphasizing that higher solubilization alone fails to overcome restrictive permeability of the stratum corneum. Additionally, despite the relative increase, the absolute permeation values were still low compared to systemic absorption thresholds, thereby ensuring local retention at the wound site. This local retention is advantageous for wound healing applications, as it minimizes systemic exposure while maintaining therapeutic concentrations at the target site. An earlier study reported no enhancement in percutaneous permeation of piroxicam when complexed with HP-ßCD74. Moreover, that study further explained that a higher flux observed with piroxicam HP-ßCD hydrogel was due to enhanced aqueous solubility conferred by complexation, compared to hydrogel in which the drug was incorporated as physical mixture.

Skin retention study

Significantly enhanced drug deposition (p-value = 0.001) was exhibited by API–βCD hydrogel as compared to API hydrogel (Fig. 5V). It was demonstrated by the findings of qualitative analysis of skin retention that 57.33 ± 2.49% of API was retained by the API–βCD hydrogel as compared to API hydrogel which showed only 13.43 ± 0.57% drug retention. Two complementary factors might have contributed to the significantly enhanced dermal localization, including the enhanced solubility of API and a prolonged residence time of the API–βCD hydrogel, which serve as a secondary diffusion barrier. An optimized microenvironment for wound healing may be created by sustained topical delivery and minimal systemic exposure. Moreover, the formulation’s limited permeation (5.4%) ensures sustained local retention of API at the wound site, which is critical for maximizing its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-collagen effects in situ. This localized delivery minimizes systemic variability and reduces the risk of off-target effects, thereby prioritizing site-specific efficacy. At the same time, it is important to acknowledge that API has well-documented systemic anti-inflammatory benefits, which could theoretically support broader tissue recovery following surgical trauma. In this context, our approach represents a strategic trade-off: by favoring local retention, the formulation ensures consistent therapeutic concentrations at the site of injury. Together these results demonstrated that API–βCD hydrogel acts as a highly efficient drug dermal depot, transforming physicochemical improvements into clinically relevant drug availability at the wound site.

Cytotoxicity test

Glutaraldehyde (GA) is commonly employed as chemical crosslinking agent in the fabrication of biomaterial fabrication due to its capacity to form stable covalent bonds. Nevertheless, concerns about its potential cytotoxicity and tissue irritation, which resulted from residual unreacted aldehyde groups have been well documented. Residual GA may interact with cellular membrane proteins and extracellular matrix components, potentially triggering inflammatory responses if not thoroughly removed or neutralized. Consequently, minimizing residual GA is essential to ensuring the biocompatibility of GA-crosslinked biomaterials intended for wound-contact applications75. In the present study, post-fabrication washing steps were employed to remove unreacted GA, thereby reducing the likelihood of residual cytotoxicity. Biocompatibility of the prepared formulations was performed via the cell viability count to evaluate whether the post-fabrication washing effectively eliminated any potential cytotoxic effects of residual GA. According to the ISO recommendations (ISO 10993–5), a cell viability of ≥ 70% for a component or material indicates non-cytotoxicity, relative to a negative control76. As reported in Fig. 6I, the hydrogel groups demonstrated a high HaCaT cells viability (around 100%) in CCK-8 assay. Even enhanced cell proliferation was observed in the hydrogel groups as compared to control group. The enhanced cell proliferation may be attributed to the biocompatible nature of PGA and the ECM-like environment that promotes cell growth and adhesion77. Also, the API-βCD complex provide antioxidant protection resulting in an enhanced cell viability. For efficient re-epithelialization in wound healing, it is essential to maintain the viability and proliferative potential of keratinocytes. A high cell viability demonstrated the provision of favorable microenvironment by API-βCD hydrogel thereby supporting keratinocytes growth and migration. These findings demonstrated the excellent cytocompatibility of API-βCD hydrogel and highlighted its potential for safe application in wound healing27.

Determination of residual GA concentration

MBTH analytical assay was used to determine the residual GA content in the API-βCD hydrogel. The results demonstrated 0.0001723% (W/W) GA concentration in optimized API-βCD hydrogel showing efficient removal of GA during washing step. This minimal residual concentration is well below the reported cytotoxic threshold in biomaterial application75. The finding of this assay along with the higher cell viability confirmed that the hydrogel possesses excellent cytocompatibility and is suitable for wound healing applications.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration results (Fig. 6II) demonstrated that API-βCD hydrogel significantly enhanced keratinocyte migration in 24 h period as compared to API-hydrogel and API-βCD complex. The enhanced keratinocyte migration may be ascribed to the improved aqueous solubility following complexation with βCD, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of API, which likely facilitated re-epithelialization by promoting cellular migration and proliferation. Qing et al. observed similar findings for bilirubin CD complex loaded hydrogels78.

Anti-bacterial assay

A concentration dependent anti-bacterial activity was observed for all the test formulations, including API, API–βCD and API–βCD hydrogel. However, a significantly enhanced antibacterial activity was demonstrated by API–βCD hydrogel (Fig. 6III). API exhibited IC50 of 175.0 ± 12.32, 80.85 ± 9.87 and 103.10 ± 14.20 µg/mL against E.coli, MRSA and S. aureus, respectively. API–βCD demonstrated IC50 of 31.79 ± 3.84, 14.78 ± 2.61 and 20.54 ± 4.52 µg/mL against E.coli, MRSA and S. aureus, respectively. Specifically, the results demonstrate that API alone exhibits only short-term activity due to rapid diffusion, while the API–βCD inclusion complex moderately prolongs activity by enhancing solubility and stability. However, significantly reduced IC50 values were observed for API–βCD hydrogel, including 22.60 ± 2.49, 11.45 ± 3.11 and 15.32 ± 1.96 µg/mL against E.coli, MRSA and S.aureus, respectively. The significantly enhanced anti-bacterial activity (p < 0.001) of API–βCD and API–βCD hydrogel may be attributed to the enhanced solubility as compared to API (Fig. 6III). Stancu et al. reported similar findings of essential oil after complexation with βCD79. The API-βCD hydrogel showed significantly lower (p-value) IC50, in case of E.coli, while non-significant difference was observed for MRSA and S.aureus as compared to API–βCD. The lower IC50 of API–βCD hydrogel compared to API–βCD may be attributed to the intrinsic antibacterial activity of PGA.

In vivo wound healing

The API-βCD hydrogel was evaluated for its therapeutic potential in full-thickness excisional wound model. Compared to API hydrogel and Negative control group, API-βCD hydrogel exhibited a statistically significant improvement in healing kinetics based on the quantitative evaluation of wound contraction over 14 days period (Fig. 7I & II). On day 4, a significantly enhanced (p < 0.001) wound contraction was observed in API-βCD hydrogel (~ 35 ± 2.11%) as compared to API hydrogel (9 ± 1.50%) and un-treated group (4 ± 1.41%). A similar observation was detected throughout the treatment period (Day 7, 9, 11 and 14). More specifically, on day 14, a 97 ± 1.0% wound closure (p < 0.001) was observed in API-βCD hydrogel treated group, when compared with API hydrogel (54 ± 1.51%) and untreated group (37 ± 1.70%). The significant healing effect of API-βCD hydrogel may be credited to the dual performance of βCD, provision of moist microenvironment, and 14-days continued hydrogel-based sustained drug delivery. It has been reported earlier that βCD inclusion complex with bilirubin showed optimum wound healing capability80. It is also worth mentioning, that our findings demonstrate relative improvements within our experimental framework, while direct comparisons with existing wound care products remain an important direction for future work.

Quantification of pro-inflammatory cytokines

TNF-α and IL-1β levels were quantified in wound tissue through ELISA as reported in Fig. 7III & IV. Persistent high levels of both cytokines were observed in untreated group suggesting sustained inflammatory markers and impaired healing dynamics. API hydrogel treated group exhibited significant reduction (p < 0.05) in TNF-α and IL-1β levels as compared to negative control group, emphasizing the inherent anti-inflammatory potential of API. Most pronounced suppression of TNF-α and IL-1β levels was observed with API–βCD hydrogel treated group as compared to negative control group (p < 0.001) and API hydrogel (p-value < 0.05). This improved anti-inflammatory activity of API may be attributed to βCD complexation, resulting in enhanced aqueous solubility, sustained release and improved local tissue penetration. The findings of ELISA were consistent with histopathological analysis and supported the conclusion that API–βCD hydrogel modulates the wound milieu by attenuation of the inflammatory cytokines.

Histopathological analysis

H&E staining

H&E staining was performed on collected skin tissue to evaluate the extent of epidermal remodeling and tissue regeneration (Fig. 8I). The normal group exhibited well-organized stratified epidermis with dense dermal matrix and several sebaceous glands and hair follicles were evident (black arrows) (Fig. 8IA). In contrast, marked histopathological changes were observed in untreated group, including severely fragmented epidermis with focal epithelial continuity, dilated blood vessels and marked infiltration of the inflammatory cells. Persistent inflammation and delayed healing are evident from loss of structural organization in the dermis (Fig. 8IB). A considerable improvement in tissue regeneration was observed in API hydrogel treated group. Figure 8IC depicted a substantial epithelial regeneration, and partial reappearance of hair follicles, visible reduction in inflammatory infiltrate, demonstrating progression towards resolution of inflammation and initiation of tissue repair. API–βCD hydrogel treated group exhibited near-complete restoration of normal skin architecture, including re-epithelialization and higher density of re-formed skin appendages (Fig. 8ID). These results demonstrated an enhanced regenerative efficacy API–βCD hydrogel, which may be credited to the enhanced solubilization and sustained release of API conferred by βCD complexation and its encapsulation within the hydrogel matrix. Similar results have been reported by Chen et al., using gelatin methacrylate, adenine acrylate and CuCl2 based hydrogel for diabetic wounds healing81.

Masson’s trichrome staining

Collagen deposition within the wound site and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling were evaluated through Masson’s trichrome staining (Fig. 8II). The normal group displayed a compact, highly organized collagen network uniformly distributed throughout the dermis, as evident from the deep blue staining (Fig. 8IIA). However, dermal architecture was markedly disrupted in untreated group (Fig. 8IIB). Insufficient fibroblast activation and ECM synthesis was evident from sparse, poorly stained and loose collagen fibers. However, moderate collagen deposition was observed in API hydrogel treated group (Fig. 8IIC). A more coherent fiber alignment was observed, when associated with untreated group, however, partial restoration of the dermal scaffold and active fibroblast-mediated remodeling was evident from the moderately enhanced fiber organization. On the other hand, most pronounced matrix remodeling was observed in API–βCD hydrogel-treated group (Fig. 8IID). Moreover, uniformly distributed and densely packed collagen fibers aligned parallel to the epidermis were observed. They were closely mimicking the native dermal architecture. These findings demonstrate enhanced therapeutic potential of API when complexed with βCD and embedded in dually cross-linked hydrogel. Enhanced solubilization, sustained tissue exposure and localized anti-inflammatory action may have facilitated collagen synthesis, ECM reorganization and enhanced fibroblast proliferation, resulting in accelerated wound healing. Similar findings have been reported earlier with platelet rich plasma derived exosomes gelatin/ βCD hydrogel for promoting diabetic wound healing82.

Inclusively, the outcomes of in vivo wound contraction and biochemical analysis were substantiated by the histological findings demonstrating that the API–βCD hydrogel exhibited a superior regenerative potential. Consequently, promising translational potential of API–βCD hydrogel in topical wound care was revealed. Favorable rheological behavior, excellent cytocompatibility, high local retention and ease of application support its direct use as hydrogel dressing without the need for a supplementary carrier. These features along with excellent wound healing efficacy supported its feasibility for clinical application in future as topically applied formulation for wound healing. However, some of the limitations of this study include the absence of power analyses to justify the animal size and to strengthen statistical rigor. Similarly, the use of controlled animal model is another limitation, which may not fully replicate the complexity of human wound physiology. Additional comparative animal groups and comparison with the marketed products and standard wound-healing therapeutics may be included to allow a more comprehensive evaluation of treatment specific effects, intergroup variability for direct clinical benchmarking. Besides, the mechanistic insight into cellular pathways needs to be investigated to establish the efficacy of the prepared dually cross-linked hydrogel system. Extended long-term biocompatibility evaluation would further strengthen the safety profile and should be included in future investigations. Likewise, detailed visualization of bacterial morphological changes using SEM or CLSM needs to be performed, and mechanistic insights to be investigated, even if a significant antibacterial activity was demonstrated through quantitative assays. Also, a positive antibiotic control (e.g., gentamicin, amoxicillin) may be utilized for antibacterial assay.

Conclusion

This study reported a strategically engineered novel dually cross-linked hydrogel system embedded with the apigenin–β-cyclodextrin (API-βCD) complex as a multifunctional, next-generation platform for surgical wound management. Poor solubility of API was addressed by integrating it into a molecular complexation with βCD. While sustained release profile, optimal swelling and favorable degradation were provided by the hydrogel matrix. The API-βCD hydrogel demonstrated better permeation when compared API gel, Moreover, enhanced disposition of API in skin layers from the API-βCD hydrogel, and a higher cell viability signify its topical administration with safety towards HaCaT cells. Furthermore, significantly enhanced antibacterial effect, wound closure, tissue regeneration and anti-inflammatory activity of API-βCD hydrogel compared to API hydrogel exhibited the therapeutic efficacy of the prepared system. Therefore, it may be concluded that this multidisciplinary approach, merging drug delivery chemistry with advanced biomaterial engineering offers a promising strategy for clinical translation in regenerative medicine, as observed for API in the management of incisional wound. Additionally, it may serve as a foundation model for future translational research in regenerative medicine.
