Characteristics, medical management, and outcomes of gender-based violence survivors in Neno, Malawi: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Manuel Mulwafu, Moses Banda Aron, Vera Kabanda, Stellar Chibvunde, Rachel Mwanza, Willy Chisindo, Jimmy Tamani, Grace Momba, Kondwani Mpinga, Fabien Munyaneza, Chiyembekezo Kachimanga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is limited research describing survivors of gender-based violence (GBV) in Malawi. This study examined characteristics, medical management, and outcomes of GBV survivors presenting at health facilities in Neno district, Malawi.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study, using facility-based GBV registers at 14 health facilities in Neno district, Malawi. We categorized all variables and used frequencies and percentages. We used Chi-square and Fisher exact to test the association between sexual abuse and other variables.ResultsOf 465 GBV survivors, 59.1% were sexually abused, and 56.8% sought help within 72 h after the incident. The majority of survivors were female (n = 413, 88.8%), unmarried (n = 334, 71.8%), and aged 18 years or younger (n = 282, 60.6%). Psychological and trauma counselling was provided to 85.6% and 84.1% of survivors, respectively. Of 275 SGBV survivors, 74.9% tested HIV negative, and 25.8% (n = 71) tested Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) positive. Of the 33 eligible women, 40% tested pregnancy positive. Only 26.6% of the eligible 275 SGBV survivors received STI prophylaxis, and 47.1% of the eligible 85 SGBV survivors received post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). Emergency contraception was given to twelve of 29 eligible women. We found an association between sexual abuse and reporting to the health facility within or after 72 h, age of the victim, sex of the victim, marital status, and referral to social welfare (p < 0.001).ConclusionsGBV survivors district-wide were mainly at or below 18 years of age, female, and unmarried. A majority of the survivors were sexually abused, and over half of the survivors reported cases within 72 h of the incident taking place. Our study demonstrates that providing standard, and coordinated GBV care in a rural setting is possible. Background

Gender-based violence (GBV) refers to harmful acts directed at an individual based on gender and comes in several forms, including sexual, physical, emotional, and neglect [1, 2]. According to the World Health Organization, one in three women worldwide is subjected to GBV in their lifetime [3]. A comprehensive review of 307 studies conducted on physical or sexual violence against women from 2000 to 2018 revealed significantly higher rates, ranging from 33% to 55% (with an average of 44%), in many lower and middle-income countries (LMICs) compared to high-income countries [4]. A systematic review of 50 studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa revealed that various factors at the individual, family, community, societal, and structural levels are associated with GBV experienced by women [5]. Studies have also shown that the COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated this situation in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, ranging from lockdowns – forcing many individuals to stay in close quarters with their abusers – to economic strain and reduced access to services [6–12]. Throughout the pandemic, GBV case reporting increased by 48% in parts of East Africa and by 35% in Central Africa [12].

Malawi, a southern African country, has documented widespread GBV [13–17]. This includes direct acts of violence and indirect consequences of GBV—early marriage and teen pregnancies. In 2020, teen pregnancies increased by 16% in one eastern district of Malawi [18]. Moreover, a recent study conducted in Malawi found that adolescents experience high rates of violence [19]. Rigorous data collection on types and frequency of GBV is essential for tailoring efforts to improve interventions [11]. Data on GBV in Malawi has been increasingly available, including from nationwide studies such as the Malawi Demographic and Health Surveys. This is a testament to the Malawi Government and various stakeholders’ recognition of the physical and psychological health burden imposed by GBV. However, significant shortcomings remain. Mellish and colleagues (2015) conducted a literature review and found significant information gaps on efficacy and outcomes associated with GBV interventions [11]. Furthermore, the authors noted that multiple studies have consistently found a generally poor knowledge of GBV [20, 21] and that survivors are rarely asked what services they want or need.

From a historical perspective, comprehensive and integrated medical, legal, and social services for GBV have not existed in Malawi [22–25]. However, in 2010 Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital established a multidisciplinary model to address GBV– comprising medical, social welfare, police victim support, and volunteer counsellor officers. The multidisciplinary model ensures that GBV cases are addressed promptly, with the aim of holding perpetrators accountable and providing essential support to those affected. For instance, sexually abused individuals are tested for HIV and receive appropriate treatment within a 72-hour window to reduce the risk of infection. This model, also known as the One Stop Centre, has been rolled out across multiple districts. Yet, there is limited evidence of the benefits of the model [22]. The Neno Ministry of Health (MOH), with the support of Partners in Health Malawi (PIH), has been offering comprehensive care to GBV survivors in Neno since January 2020 using the multidisciplinary model. To our knowledge, no studies have been published from Malawi on the characteristics of GBV survivors, medical management, and outcomes following management in a rural setting. We aim to describe the characteristics, medical management, and outcomes of GBV survivors who presented at health facilities in Neno District to better understand the quality of care they receive. Notably, in this study, outcomes are defined as essential follow up such as receiving counselling, referral to social welfare, etc. and not outcomes after treatment.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study reviewing GBV registers between January 2020 and December 2022.

Study site

We conducted the study in Neno District, located in southern Malawi, with an estimated population of 150, 211 in 2022 [26]. Neno district has two hospitals, ten health centres, and two dispensaries (Fig. 1). The district has a poverty rate of over 40% [27], typical of the majority of Malawi. Accessibility to health services is also a challenge. For example, over 75% of women of reproductive age reported at least one problem accessing health services in 2015 [28] due to socioeconomic barriers and challenging terrain. In conjunction with MOH, PIH supports health facilities in Neno, offering community, primary and secondary health care – including GBV support services targeting survivors at all levels [29, 30]. At the community level, PIH works with Community Victim Support Units, community health workers, health surveillance assistants, and other local structures to raise awareness, identify survivors, and refer them to primary and secondary levels of care. At the primary care level, PIH supports GBV service delivery at health centres where survivors report and receive first-line clinical management and referral. At the secondary care level, it supports comprehensive GBV services at Neno District Hospital and Lisungwi Community Hospital, which include One Stop Centre (OSC) services that provide coordinated medical, legal, and social welfare support [29, 30]. Survivors report incidents at any level of care; however, OSC are centralized at secondary level facilities, and referrals from the community and primary levels are intended to ensure timely access to comprehensive care.

Study participants

This study included all GBV survivors who sought services at health facilities in Neno district between January 2020 (when the GBV register was implemented) to December 2022, regardless of their district of origin or age. We defined survivors as people who experienced any form of GBV and sought care. We excluded people who experienced GBV but did not report at a health facility in Neno district or were outside the study timeframe.

GBV program in Neno district

Before January 2020, various challenges hindered the provision of GBV health services in the Neno district, including the lack of coordination among service providers, lack of proper referral pathways, poor infrastructure, a limited number of trained service providers, and lack of standardized sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV) guidelines and data collection tools [29]. A multidisciplinary team led by the MOH was established to address these challenges, with the support of PIH and enabled by a Global Affairs Canada (GAC) funded No Woman or Girl Left Behind (NWOGLB) Program. The NWOGLB Program aims to end SGBV through strengthening, prevention, and treatment for SGBV survivors [29, 30]. The team comprised MOH leadership, health care providers, police, social welfare, judiciary, PIH staff, and other implementing partners.

Initially, the team conducted a benchmarking process at Malawi’s first GBV One Stop Centre (OSC) [22], located at the Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital, to learn best practices on case management for GBV survivors. The development of GBV guidelines and registers, admission and reporting forms, referral pathways from home to facilities, and referral pathways between the health facilities and other units within the district followed this. During that time, these tools only existed at Ministry of Health Headquarters and not at individual district health offices. Over 70 public service personnel and community leaders across Neno received training in GBV care. This resulted in establishing a significant network of GBV service providers, developing a broader range of GBV packages of care, and improving data collection of GBV cases.

Significant challenges to the timely and seamless GBV survivor service provision were (1) the need for physical infrastructure and (2) the long wait times experienced by survivors. To address these issues, a virtual infrastructure (‘OSC’) was established through a protected WhatsApp group. Unlike the conventional OSC which provides easy physical access for survivors to report cases and seek services, the virtual ‘OSC’ in Neno was only accessible by a trained and select team of GBV service providers to facilitate seamless support services and communication. Only de-identified, general information on new-found GBV cases is shared to alert and prepare members of this group who provide direct support. This eliminates the need for physical infrastructure. The members of this group originate from health, social welfare, Victim Support Unit members of Neno Police station, judiciary, PIH staff, and other partner organization members. The forum also facilitates case follow-up among various GBV service providers in Neno. Once a case is identified by any GBV care government or partner agencies, it is immediately shared with other service providers on the WhatsApp group. This allows them to be alert and prepare to handle incoming cases quickly, thereby avoiding unnecessary delays and long queues. The multidisciplinary team meets monthly to discuss and address the challenges faced in providing GBV care.

Initially, comprehensive GBV care was provided in January 2020 in four facilities. The program expanded to the remaining ten health facilities in Neno District based on increased need. Trained personnel provided medical care to survivors who presented at the health facility, and their information was recorded in GBV registers designed for this purpose. History, physical examination, and medical treatment, including psychological and trauma counselling, were provided to all survivors according to standardized guidelines and protocols [31, 32]. Survivors were referred to the police for further follow-up, and referral for social support was based on the individual’s needs. To better support people, community awareness activities of the referral pathway are conducted (Fig. 2).

All providers who performed the tests mentioned above received additional training on the Management of Sexual Violence Survivors in Malawi. In handling all survivors with trauma, this training enabled them to perform the tests while ensuring professionalism and dignity, safety, and well-being of survivors.

From the community perspective, the program instituted an awareness campaign to raise awareness of the GBV. These campaigns were conducted in various settings including mass gatherings of different kinds, at churches and schools with the sole purpose of ensuring that the community is conversant with the process flow of GBV support.

Data collection

We developed a database in Microsoft Excel and trained a research assistant to use the tool. Between July 2022 and January 2023, a research assistant extracted the data from GBV registers across all 14 facilities in Neno. The tool had information on (i) Socio-demographic characteristics: age (in years), gender (male or female), and marital status (married or not married). (ii) Details of clinic visit: type of visit (new visit or revisit) and type of abuse (sexual, physical, neglect, or emotional abuse). (iii) Medical care provided: psychological support (yes or no) and trauma counselling (yes or no) and (iv) Referrals to other services: Referred to social welfare (yes or no) and referred to the police (yes or no). For all sexual abuse survivors, we further included (i) Medical evaluation: pregnancy test results (yes or no), HIV test results (positive and on treatment, positive but not on treatment, Non-reactive or HIV test not done), syphilis rapid tests results using VDRL (positive, negative or not done), and pregnancy test results (positive, negative, not done or not applicable). (ii) Medical treatment for SGBV survivors: Received pre-exposure prophylaxis for HIV (yes or no), received emergency contraceptives (yes, no, or not applicable), and received STI treatment (yes or no).

Data analysis

We imported data from a Microsoft Excel database to R version 4.2.2 for cleaning and analysis [33, 34]. We dropped two cases that had missing case report date representing 0.4% of our total sample, However, we recorded all other missing values in the variables as “missing” and included them in the analysis. We plotted the total number of GBV cases recorded each quarter between January 2020 and December 2022. We categorized all variables and used frequencies and percentages to present the summary statistics in a table format. We further used the Pearson Chi-square test and Fishers exact to test the association between sexual abuse and the following variable: incident report, visit type, age of the victim, sex, district, referral source, marital status, psychological support, trauma counselling, referral to social welfare and referral to police. We consider a p-value of 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics, forms of abuse, and support received by GBV survivors

We reviewed 467 records of GBV cases that were documented and treated across 14 healthcare facilities in Neno district from January 2020 to December 2022, excluding two cases that had missing dates. We observed an increase in the number of survivors of GBV, with cases rising from less than 10 per quarter in 2020 quarter 1 to over 70 per quarter in 2022 quarter 3 (Fig. 3).

Distribution of GBV cases recorded in Neno District between 2020 and 2022

Among the 465 survivors, sexual abuse was the most common form of abuse, accounting for 59.1% (n = 275), followed by physical abuse (39.4%, n = 183), neglect (4.1%, n = 19), and emotional abuse (0.6%, n = 3). Most survivors (n = 264, 56.8%) sought medical attention within 72 h of the incident, and the vast majority (n = 456, 98.1%) were new patients at the health facility. Of the survivors, 60% (n = 282) were aged 18 years old or younger. Most (n = 334, 71.8%) were unmarried at the time of the incident, and over three-quarters (n = 413, 88.8%) were females. Almost all survivors (n = 459, 98.7%) were from Neno district. More than half (n = 243, 52.3%) were referred to the health facility by the police, while 26.9% (n = 125) presented themselves to the health facilities directly. Psychological support and trauma counselling were provided to 85.6% (n = 398) and 84.1% (n = 391) of GBV survivors, respectively. The majority (n = 403, 86.7%) and n = 434, (93.3%) of the survivors were referred to social welfare and police services, respectively, for further assistance (Table 1).

Survivors of sexual abuse were eligible for a series of medical assessments and interventions (Fig. 4). Those who had experienced sexual abuse and were within the reproductive age group were eligible for pregnancy tests and emergency contraception. Additionally, all survivors, regardless of age, were qualified for HIV and VDRL tests, as well as STI prophylaxis. Individuals who were not HIV positive were also eligible for Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP).

Out of the 275 survivors of sexual abuse, the majority (n = 206, 74.9%) tested negative for HIV, while one-fifth (n = 57, 20.7%) did not undergo HIV testing. Among the 275 survivors eligible for the VDRL test, a quarter (n = 71, 25.8%) tested positive, one person refused the test, and 29.1% (n = 80) did not receive the test. Out of the 275 survivors of SGBV who were eligible for STI prophylaxis, only 73 (26.6%) received it. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is among the few studies done in Malawi [34] and the first district-wide study in Malawi to describe GBV survivors’ characteristics, medical management, and outcomes [22, 23]. This study’s findings help us understand who survivors of GBV are, medical assistance provided and their outcomes which are defined as essential follow up such receiving counselling and referral to social welfare. We found a steady increase of cases from quarter one of 2020 with a drop in quarter four of 2022. The GBV survivors were mainly young unmarried females and the majority were sexually abused.

The increase in GBV survivors reported to the health facility within the study period can be explained by awareness of the community as it was part of program activities. Communities were sensitized about GBV through dialogue sessions, campaigns, mass gatherings including during football games and churches. While there has been no community-based study to estimate the incidence, the steady increase in our study would suggest that there are cases that would have remained not reported to the hospital which has a serious implication on the survivors. We observed a decline in the last quarter of our study period. While there is no clear explanation for this, we suggest that the full implementation and rollout of preventive interventions across the district during this quarter may have contributed to the decrease. However, further study is warranted to determine whether this represents a continued trend. Notably, the 72-hour window applies only to survivors of sexual violence because prophylactic treatments are time-sensitive. These include post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) and prophylactic treatment for sexually transmitted infections (STIs).

The predominance of sexual abuse cases, especially among younger females and them seeking health and other services tells of the strides made in SGBV awareness and care, particularly concerning sexual abuse in Malawi. This suggests that people are becoming more aware of the available support systems, as evidenced by the fact that the police are the primary source of referrals for cases documented at the facility. Further, community sensitization campaigns may help to explain this phenomenon. This is in contrast to a study done in Ghana which found that survivors rarely seek institutional help, particularly from the police [32]. In our study, despite the small percentage of male survivors, our findings align with the prevalence reported in another study conducted in Malawi, which found a prevalence rate of 10.4% [35]. However, it’s worth noting that a survey conducted in the Democratic Republic of Congo found that male survivors of SGBV often did not seek care due to feelings of fear, shame, and guilt. This could account for our study’s low number of male GBV survivors [36].

Altogether, these findings pose both a critical challenge and an opportunity. The challenge is that sexual violence against predominantly younger unmarried females, which bears significant mental and physical health consequences such as post-traumatic stress disorder or unhealthy behaviours [36], warrants appropriate interventions from providers and relevant stakeholders—the opportunity from here on lies in designing tailored interventions for this specific population. Cognitive-behavioural therapy effectively prevents and treats post-traumatic stress disorder elsewhere [36]. Neno GBV services provision can consider this type of therapy as part of the care given to survivors. Interventions encouraging help-seeking in the male community following SGBV should also be intensified. A study in Uganda found retrogressive norms to be a chief obstacle to help-seeking and suggested context-specific social and behavioural change interventions to encourage help-seeking [37].

The uptake of essential laboratory tests for sexually abused survivors depended on the type, with one in five to one in four clients not receiving the essential tests. HIV tests done were relatively higher compared to VDLR and pregnancy tests. However, the STI prophylaxis and PEP provision fell short of optimal levels. Several factors could account for this, including (1) delayed reporting of sexual assault cases beyond the standard 5-day window period for effective emergency contraception (2), pre-menarche status (3), menstruation at the time of case presentation (4), the survivor being identified as already pregnant (5), reliable use of prescribed contraceptive methods (6), refusal by the survivor (7), cases referred but not documented at the referral facility (8), and drug and diagnostic stock-outs at health facilities. As a policy implication, health facility staff should receive training and mentorship to ensure the delivery of quality and consistent services. Since managing SGBV cases was a new concept to providers in the district, there is a possibility that some eligible clients were mistakenly deemed ineligible for certain tests. Our findings contrast with those of a study in Kenya, which reported optimal uptake of medical treatment (PEP and STI treatment) and an emergency contraception non-compliance rate of only 17% [34]. Moving forward, addressing gaps in documentation – particularly instances of incorrect or missing entries – is critical. Regular case conferencing and data quality assessments may help alleviate these issues, as they directly affect the continuum of care. We further suggest conducting a qualitative study to explore the reasons behind less-than-optimal uptake of STI prophylaxis and PEP.

Survivors were primarily referred to Police and Welfare services following evaluation and medical management. However, whether they accessed these subsequent services remains unclear and should be documented in the current version of the register. To facilitate the seamless care provision, we recommend enhanced data collection at this stage, and future studies should aim to bridge this gap.

The study has several strengths, including its district-wide scope in a rural setting, its coverage of various forms of GBV, and its assessment of the provision of GBV care following Malawi GBV guidelines. Neno was one of the country’s first districts to implement the GBV register in 2020, and based on this, the Neno team supported MOH with the national GBV register and OSC guidelines.

Our study has some limitations. First, the data may not fully represent the community, as not all survivors seek help from formal support services. Consequently, the actual number of cases may be higher than those reported during the study period. Secondly, no follow-up of clients was conducted to provide continuous supportive services, which are essential given the far-reaching mental and physical complications of GBV. It was difficult to retrospectively link hospital cases with those reported to the police. Therefore, a second register should be placed in all stations where survivors may seek additional services. Thirdly, because this was retrospective data, other forms of abuse may have been underreported. Reporting depended on whether service providers probed for additional abuses or documented only the abuse initially presented by the survivor. Continued mentorship for services providers would therefore improve data capture. Lastly, missing data may have impacted the study’s findings. We therefore suggest improving documentation in GBV registers to ensure the collection of quality data.

Conclusion

Our study describes all GBV survivors across a district in rural Malawi and provides critical information on areas for improvement in the GBV service provision continuum. While the administration of tests was acceptable, the provision of prophylaxis could be improved through monthly data reporting against indicators for data completion. Additional information on why particular services, such as prophylaxis, were not provided should also be added to the GBV register. In addition, documentation should be improved to ensure rich information on the survivors. Given trained personnel, this study shows that it is possible to provide standard, comprehensive, and coordinated GBV care in a rural setting.
