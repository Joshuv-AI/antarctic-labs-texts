Prognostic prediction and immune microenvironment analysis in colorectal cancer using exosome-related lncRNA signatures
Authors: Haohui Li, Xiaoping Huang, Zhenchao Luo, Fangfang Zhou, Yunyao Deng, Canliang Tan, Yiyi Jin, Jian Yan, Gang Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExosomes play a crucial role in tumor microenvironment (TME) by mediating cell-cell communication, but their role in colorectal cancer (CRC) remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate exosome-related lncRNAs (ER-lncRNAs) in CRC.MethodsmRNA profiles and clinical data from TCGA and GEO, microbiome data from TCMAand exosome-related genes from ExoCarta were analyzed. Consensus clustering, ER-lncRNA-related risk signature, and nomogram were developed.ResultsA total of 797 differentially expressed lncRNAs (DE-lncRNAs)were identified, with 490 ER-lncRNAs selected based on their correlation with exosome-related mRNAs. Consensus clustering stratified CRC samples into four molecular subtypes, with Cluster 2 exhibiting the most favorable prognosis and Cluster 1 the poorest. These subtypes showed significant differences in survival outcomes, immune cell infiltration, and therapeutic responses. Nine ER-lncRNAs were identified as prognostic biomarkers and used to develop a risk score model. Furthermore, a nomogram incorporating the risk score and clinical parameters was constructed to predict individual prognosis.ConclusionThese findings highlight the clinical relevance of ER-lncRNAs as in CRC and underscores their potential as novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04755-1.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC), which includes colon cancer (COAD) and rectal cancer (READ), is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with over 1.9 million new cases and 904,000 deaths reported in 2022 [1]. In China, both the incidence and mortality of CRC are increasing, posing significant challenges to disease management [2, 3]. Risk factors such as aging, Western dietary habits, obesity, lack of physical exercise, and smoking contribute to the increasing prevalence of CRC [4]. Despite recent advances in therapeutic strategies that have extended the median overall survival for advanced CRC to approximately three years, the prognosis remains poor [5]. Drug-tolerant cancer cell populations are known to contribute to treatment failure and tumor recurrence, which are major causes of cancer-related mortality [6, 7]. CRC development and progression involve various alterations in molecular functions that influence tumorigenesis, progression, drug resistance, immune homeostasis [8], and clinical outcomes [9, 10]. Although some biomarkers associated with CRC progression and drug resistance have been validated for routine clinical application [11]. However, the potential biomarkers and underlying mechanisms in CRC are still not fully understood. Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore reliable prognostic biomarkers to improve prognosis prediction and guide treatment strategies in CRC.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs), which are released from all cells and range in size from 40 to 160 nm transport protein, DNA, RNA, lipids, metabolites, and cytosolic and cell-surface proteins [12, 13]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that exosomes play key roles in tumor initiation, tumorigenesis, metastasis, immunity, angiogenesis, tumor microenvironment remodeling, and drug resistance [14–17]. Given their biogenesis, secretion by cells, and cancer cell-specific molecular and genetic contents, exosomes have emerged as promising, clinically valuable, and minimally invasive biomarkers for monitoring of the disease progression and treatment efficacy in cancer patients [18, 19].

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), which are non-coding RNAs with longer than 200 nucleotides and lacking important open reading frames, play critical roles in many tumors [20, 21]. For example, DiGeorge syndrome critical region gene 5 (DGCR5) exhibits a dual role in tumors, functioning either as a tumor suppressor or an oncogene to regulate tumor progression [22]. LINC00239 contributes to tumorigenesis and development by inhibiting ferroptosis of CRC cells [23], whereas GAS5 acts as a tumor suppressor in CRC by triggering YAP phosphorylation [24]. Increasing evidence have shown that exosomal noncoding RNAs are abundantly present in multiple tumor types and hold considerable clinical potential [25–27]. Specifically, exosomal lncRNAs such as SNHG10 [28], LINC00659 [29], and LINC01315 [30] have been identified in the CRC. Nevertheless, a substantial number of exosome-related lncRNAs (ER-lncRNAs) remains to be discovered.

In this study, we screened the differentially expressed exosome-related genes (DE-ER-mRNAs) and then identified their associated differentially expressed exosome-related long non-coding RNAs (DE-ER-lncRNAs). Subsequently, we systematically explored the molecular clusters related toER-lncRNAs, along with their associated clinical characteristics, biological functions, landscape of immune cell infiltration, and responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy. Moreover, we constructed a prognostic risk signature based on the DE-ER-lncRNAs and evaluated its predictive performance. We also explored its biological significance, immune landscape, therapeutic responses, and associated microbiota profiles using integrative multi-omics data from publicly available databases.

Methods

Data acquisition

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data and corresponding clinical information for COAD (n = 462) and READ (n = 156) tumor samples, along with 51 normal colon and rectum tissues, were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). After merging the TCGA-COAD and TCGA-READ cohorts, a total of 618 CRC tumor samples with complete mRNA expression data and clinical information were included as the training set for subsequent analyses. Additionally, mRNA expression profiles and corresponding clinical information for 177 CRC tumorous samples were retrieved from GSE17536 dataset in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) based on the GPL570 platform. A list of 1,619 exosome-related genes (ER-mRNAs) were downloaded from the ExoCarta database (http://exocarta.org/index.html). Microbiome data for the TCGA-COAD/READ cohort were obtained from the Cancer Microbiome Atlas (TCMA) [31]. Detailed information on all data sources used in this study can be found in Table S1.

Identification of differentially expressed transcripts

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially expressed lncRNAs (DE-lncRNAs) between CRC tumor samples and normal samples were identified using the Limma package in R, with cutoff values set at |log2 fold change (FC)| > 0.585 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. The Volcano plot of the DEGs was visualized using the ggplot2 package in R, while heatmap was visualized using the pheatmap package in R. Differentially expressed ER-mRNAs (DE-ER-mRNAs) were then identified by intersecting the DEGs with the 1,619 ER-mRNAs obtained from the ExoCarta database.

Selection of ER-lncRNA-mRNA pairs

ER-lncRNA-mRNA pairs were selected based on a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.4 and a p-value less than 0.05 between DE-lncRNAs and DE-ER-mRNAs.

Consensus clustering

Consensus clustering was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus package in R, applying Spearman distance and the partitioning around medoids (PAM) clustering algorithm. To ensure the classification stability, 1,000 iterations were performed. Consensus clustering was performed based on the expression profiles of ER-lncRNAs, with the number of groups ranging from 1 to 5. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the consensus matrix and cumulative distribution function (CDF). The Kaplan-Meier method was used to assess differences in overall survival (OS) between clusters. The distribution of categorical data (age, gender, stages, and histology) among clusters was analyzed using Wilcoxon’s test. DEGs between clusters were identified using the Limma package in R, with cutoff values of |log2 FC| > 0.585 and FDR < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses [32–34] were performed using the clusterProfiler package in R [35], significant pathways were identified by an adjusted p-value of < 0.05. In the present study, only the KEGG pathway names and identifiers were used, and no copyright permission from KEGG was required.

Comparison of the immune characteristics and immunotherapy responses between different clusters

To investigate the tumor immune microenvironment of colorectal cancer, both ESTIMATE analysis and single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) were performed. The ESTIMATE score, stromal score, immune score, and tumor purity for each sample were calculated using the ESTIMATE package in R [36]. Differences between groups were assessed using Wilcoxon’s test. ssGSEA was performed with the GSVA package in R to compute the rank score of each immune cell-specific marker gene [37]. The immune-related gene set for 28 types of immune cells was sourced from previous studies [38]. The correlation between different types of immune cells and clusters was determined using the Spearman correlation test. Differences in proportions of immune cell types between groups were analyzed using Wilcoxon’s test. Pathways with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Analysis of the immunotherapy and chemotherapy response

Differences in immune checkpoint expression among clusters were assessed using Wilcoxon’s test. T cell dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) scores [39] were calculated using the tidepy Python package, while immunephenoscore (IPS) was computed using the IOBR package in R to evaluate immune checkpoint blockade therapy. Differences in TIDE scores and IPS among clusters were analyzed using Wilcoxon’s test. The Submap algorithm was utilized to assess sensitivity to anti-PD1 and anti-CTLA4 treatments for each sample from different groups, based on GenePattern (https://www.genepattern.org/). Moreover, half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values for commonly used chemotherapeutic and targeted agents were calculated using the oncoPredict R package, relying on data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC2) database (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). Differences in drug sensitivity among clusters were evaluated using Wilcoxon’s test. A p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Development of an ER-lncRNA-related risk score (ERrisk score) and validation of the ERrisk score in CRC

To assess the prognostic value of ER-lncRNAs, the TCGA cohort was used as the training set, while the GSE17536 dataset served as the external validation set. Univariate Cox analysis was performed to identify ER-lncRNAs associated with Overall survival (OS), using a cutoff of P < 0.1. Subsequently, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression was conducted using the glmnet package in R to establish a prognostic gene signature. This approach helps reduce redundancy among genes and prevents model overfitting [40]. Next, the risk score for each sample was calculated using the formula: risk score = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sum}_{i=1}^{n}\left(Expi*Coei\right)$$\end{document}, where Expi represents the expression level of each gene and Coei represents the coefficient of each gene derived from LASSO Cox regression. Patients in the training set were stratified into high- and low-risk groups based on the median value of the risk score. Using the same formula, risk scores were calculated for patients in the test set and external validation set using the same formula, and they were also divided into high- and low-risk groups accordingly. OS differences between these risk groups were assessed using the survival package in R. Time-dependent ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were generated to evaluate the predictive accuracy of the risk score, with a higher area under the curve (AUC) indicating better accuracy.

DEGs between ERrisk groups, functional analysis, immune characteristics of the tumor microenvironment (TME), and response to immunotherapy were determined using previously described methods. Additionally, differences in microbiome data between risk groups were assessed using Wilcoxon’s test. A p-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Samples collection

A total 7 CRC tumor and 7 normal colon and rectum samples were collected from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University between March 20, 2025 to April 20, 2025. All participants provided written informed consent, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (approval number: 2025-ER-047). Tissue specimens were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Reverse transcription was performed with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted in triplicate using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The reaction conditions were: warm start for 10 min at 95℃,15s at 95℃, and 1 min at 60℃ (40 cycles in together). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

Primer sequences of the genes analyzed were SNHG16 5′-GCTGCACCTGACCTCTACCT-3′, 5′-CAGGTGGCTTGTGGTTTGAT-3′; H19 5′-TGCTGCACTTTACAACCATGC-3′, 5′-TGGGTTCCTCATCTTTGCCA-3′; LINC00174 5′-CAGCAAGGACAAGGCTGAAG-3′, 5′-GCTGGAGGAGGAGGTTGTTC-3′; SNHG7 5′-GGAGGCCAGTTTGTTTGAGG-3′, 5′-CCTTCCCAAGTCCTCTTCCA-3′; LINC01315 5′-TCCTCACCCTGTCTTCCTCA-3′, 5′-AGGCACAGAGCAGGAACAAA-3′; FOXD3-AS1 5′-GGAGGCTGAAGTGGAGAAGG-3′, 5′-CCTGGTGTTGCTGTTGCTTC-3′; FENDRR 5′-CCAGGAGCAGCAGTTTCAAG-3′, 5′-GCTGGTGTTGCTGAGGTTGT-3′; HAND2-AS1 5′-GCTGAGGCTGAGTTGGAGAA-3′, 5′-CAGGCAGGTGTTGAGTTGGT-3′; OGFRP1 5′-TGGAAGCCTTCAGGAGCTAA-3′, 5′-CCAGGTCCAGGTTCTTCAGT-3′ GAPDH 5’-TGTTCGTCATGGGTGTGAAC-3′ and 5′-ATGGCATGGACTGTGGTCAT-3′.

Development of a nomogram

Independent prognostic factors were identified using univariate and multivariate Cox analyses, incorporating clinical characteristics such as age, gender, tumor grade, and p-T/N/M stages. These factors were then used to develop a predictive nomogram for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS. Concordance indices (C indices), calibration curves, and ROC curves for 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS were plotted to evaluate the sensitivity and accuracy of the predictive model.

Results

Identification of the exosomal DEGs and their related lncRNAs in CRC

A total of 797 DE-lncRNAs were identified between tumor and normal samples, with 538 upregulated and 259 downregulated, using cutoff values of |log2 FC| > 0.585 and FDR < 0.05 (Fig. 1A–B, Table S2). Similarly, a total of 6,006 DEGs were identified, with 3,577 upregulated and 2,429 downregulated, using the same cutoff values (Fig. 1C–D, Table S3). To identify DE-ER-mRNAs, 6,006 DEGs were overlapped with 1,619 ER-mRNAs from the ExoCarta database (http://exocarta.org/index.html), resulting in 622 DE-ER-mRNAs (Fig. 1E, Table S4). Additionally, 490 ER-lncRNAs were identified through co-expression analysis of the 797 DE-lncRNAs and 622 DE-ER-mRNAs (Table S5).

Consensus clustering analysis of the ER-lncRNA-related molecular clusters

To explore the molecular characteristics of 490 ER-lncRNA in CRC, consensus clustering analysis were performed. CRC patients were classified into four distinct clusters with high consistency and stability (Fig. 2A–C). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that Cluster 2 exhibited the best prognosis, while Cluster 1 and 4 had the poorest prognosis (Fig. 2D). We observed significant differences in the distribution of patients based on age and tumor stage across the clusters (Fig. 2E). These findings suggest that the ER-lncRNA-related molecular clusters are strongly associated with patient prognosis and clinical characteristics in CRC.

Functional analysis of four molecular clusters

Previous analyses revealed significant differences in survival, age, and cancer stages among the four clusters. Subsequently, differential expression analysis identified 2,055 DEGs in Cluster 1, 335 DEGs in Cluster 2, 919 DEGs in Cluster 3, and 385 DEGs in Cluster 4 (Table S6-9). Functional enrichment analysis further demonstrated distinct biological characteristics for each cluster. Cluster 1 was predominantly enriched in immune-related process, including leukocyte migration, chemotaxis, activation, leukocyte-mediated immunity, chemokine signaling pathways, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions (Fig. 3A–D). Cluster 2 was associated with immune responses, focal adhesion, complement and coagulation cascades (Fig. 3E–H). Cluster 3 was involved in responses to metal ions and hypoxia, organic acid and carboxylic acid transport, wnt signaling pathway, pancreatic secretion, and arachidonic acid metabolism (Fig. 3I–L). Cluster 4 was significantly associated with antigen processing and presentation via MHC class I and II, and intestinal immune network for IgA production (Fig. 3M–P). These findings highlight the unique biological functions and immune characteristics of each ER-lncRNA-related molecular cluster in CRC.

Immune characteristics of tumor microenvironment for four molecular clusters

Immune cell infiltration is a hallmark for tumor and play both pro- and anti-tumorigenic roles in cancer progression [38, 41]. Therefore, we subsequently investigated the immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment (TME) among the four molecular clusters. ESTIMATE analysis revealed significant differences in ESTIMATE score, immune score, stromal score, and tumor purity among the four clusters (Fig. 4A–D). Cluster 1 exhibited the highest ESTIMATE score, immune score, and stromal score, along with the lowest tumor purity. Conversely, cluster 4 showed the lowest ESTIMATE score, immune score, and stromal score, with the highest tumor purity. Consistent with these findings, analysis of immune cell infiltration indicated significant differences in immune cell types among the clusters. Cluster 1 demonstrated abundant immune cell enrichment, whereas cluster 4 exhibited the lowest immune cell infiltration (Fig. 4E–F).

Immunotherapy and chemotherapy responses of four molecular clusters

Distinct immune checkpoint expression patterns were observed among the four clusters, with most checkpoints upregulated in Cluster 1 but significantly downregulated in Cluster 4 (Fig. 5A–B). TIDE scores varied among the clusters, suggesting differential T cell dysfunction and exclusion profiles, while IPS scores did not show significant differences (Fig. 5C–D). Submap analysis further indicated that patients in Cluster 1 might potentially benefit from anti-PD-1 therapy (Fig. 5E). In terms of chemotherapy response, we employed the the oncoPredict R package to evaluate the sensitivity of each cluster to 198 chemotherapy agents. Cluster 1 patients exhibited the lowest estimated IC50 values for several drugs, including Alisertib, Dactinomycin, Epirubicin, Mitoxantrone, Sabutoclax, and Teniposide, suggesting greater drug sensitivity. In contract, patients in Cluster 3 exhibited the highest IC50 values, indicative of potential resistance to these treatments (Fig. 5F–G). These findings suggest that Cluster 1 patients may benefit more from both immunotherapy and chemotherapy, while Cluster 3 patients might be less responsive to conventional chemotherapeutic approaches.

Development and validation of the ER-lncRNA-related risk score in CRC

To further explore the association of the 490 ER-lncRNAs with OS in CRC patients, a univariate Cox regression analysis was conducted to identify 77 ER-lncRNAs significantly associated with OS in the training set (P < 0.1) (Fig. 6A, Table S11). These 77 ER-lncRNAs were then included in a LASSO regression model, resulting in the selection of nine ER-lncRNAs (SNHG16, H19, FOXD3-AS1, LINC00174, SNHG7, FENDRR, LINC01315, HAND2-AS1, and OGFRP1) based on their coefficients (Fig. 6B–D, Table S12). Using the median risk score calculated from the expression and coefficients of these nine ER-lncRNAs, CRC patients in both the training and validation cohorts were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups (Fig. 6E–F and I–K). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicated that patients in the high-risk group had significantly poorer survival outcomes compared to those in the low-risk group in both datasets (Fig. 6G and J). Time-dependent ROC analysis confirmed the predictive capability of the model, with AUC values for 1-, 3-, and 5-year ROC curves in the training set reaching 0.665, 0.685, and 0.681, respectively (Fig. 6H). In the external validation set, the AUC values were 0.720, 0.638, and 0.630 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year ROC curves, respectively (Fig. 6L). Additionally, we validated the expression levels of the nine ER-lncRNAs in clinical CRC samples. As shown in Fig. 7A–F, OGFRP1, SNHG16, H19, LINC00174, SNHG7, and LINC01315 were significantly upregulated in tumor tissues, whereas FOXD3-AS1, FENDRR, and HAND2-AS1 were notably downregulated, supporting the relevance of these ER-lncRNAs in CRC progression.

Development of a nomogram for CRC

Based on univariate Cox (Fig. 8A–B) and multivariate Cox (Fig. 8C–D) analyses in both the training and validation sets, the risk score, age, and cancer stage were identified as independent prognostic factors (Fig. 8A–D). Subsequently, these factors were used to develop a nomogram based on the training set (Fig. 8E). The time-dependent ROC curves showed that the nomogram had robust predictive performance for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival, with AUC values of 0.768, 0.808, and 0.819, respectively (Fig. 8F). Calibration plots demonstrated the accurate predictive ability of the nomogram for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival (Fig. 8G). Decision curve analysis confirmed that the predictive model provided clinical benefit across the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival predictions (Fig. 8H).

Identification of the ERrisk score associated immune features and immunotherapy responses

Further analysis of immune cell infiltration revealed notable differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups. Specifically, CD56bright natural killer cells and central memory CD4 T cells were increased, whereas activated CD4 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cells, immature dendritic cells (DCs), mast cells, memory B cells, Type 17 T helper cells, and Type 2 T helper (Th2) cells were decreased in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 10A–B). In terms of immune checkpoint expression, the high-risk group exhibited upregulation of ADORA2A, BTN2A1, TNFRSF14, and TNFRSF4, but the expression of BTLA, CD209, CD226, CD274, CD28, CD40LG, CD47, CD80, CD86, CD96, ICOS, IDO1, PDCD1LG2, TNFRSF9, and TNFRSF18 were decreased in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 10C–D). Moreover, TIDE score analysis indicated that patients in the high-risk group had higher TIDE scores, suggesting a greater likelihood of immune evasion and potentially reduced responsiveness to immunotherapy. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in IPS scores between the two groups (Fig. 10E–F). Submap analysis suggested that patients in the high-risk group might be more responsive to anti-CTLA4 treatment (Fig. 10G). These findings collectively highlight distinct immune landscapes between risk groups and offer insights into potential immunotherapeutic strategies for CRC patients stratified by ERrisk score.

Identification of the ERrisk score associated with microbiota landscapes

Microbiotic data from the TCMA corresponding to samples in TCGA enabled a microbial-host multi-omics analysis in this study. Our findings revealed significant alterations in the microbiota composition between the high-risk and low-risk groups. Specifically, the abundance of Firmicutes were significantly decreased in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 11A–B). Additionally, Clostridia levels were lower, while Bacteroidia were upregulated in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group (Fig. 11C–D). Furthermore, Clostridiales showed a significant decrease, whereas Bacteroidales were notably increased in the high-risk group relative to the low-risk group (Fig. 11E–F). These microbiota shifts suggest a potential association between the ERrisk score and alterations in the gut microbiome, which may contribute to the host’s immune response and cancer progression.

Discussion

Exosomal lncRNAs exert the key roles in the regulation of several cancer processes, including tumor growth, development, angiogenesis, metastasis, drug resistance, and immunomodulation [42–45]. Exsomoe lncRNAs not only involved in the molecular regulating of cancer, but they have also been identified as biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of cancer, as well as targets for chemotherapy and immunotherapy [46–48]. Depending on the cell origin, exosomal lncRNAs are packaged by exosome, which provide an enriched and protective environment for these lncRNAs cargos, enhancing their stability and functionality. As a results, exosomal lncRNAs serve as reliable tools for diagnostic and therapeutic applications in cancer [49]. In the present study, we conducted a comprehensive exploration of ER-lncRNAs from two perspectives, their molecular characteristics and their prognostic value.

Firstly, 490 DE-ER-lncRNAs were identified in CRC and used for consensus clustering analysis. There were four ER-lncRNA-related molecular clusters were clustered and showed a significant distinction among the four clusters. Cluster 1 and 4 showed the worse prognosis. Despite Cluster 1 exhibiting the worst prognosis, immune infiltration results showed abundant immune cell enrichment, and patients in this cluster were more sensitive to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. This paradox can be explained by the complex interplay between immune cell infiltration and tumor microenvironment characteristics, which may contribute to increased immune surveillance and heightened responsiveness to treatment in Cluster 1, despite the poor overall prognosis. The enhanced immune response could be linked to tumor-promoting inflammation, which, although boosting therapeutic sensitivity, may also lead to aggressive disease progression. Cluster 2 exhibited the best prognosis and was associated with immune response pathways. Cluster 3 was involved in several pathways related to energy metabolism and revealed resistance to chemotherapy. cluster 4 participated in antigen presentation-related pathway, showed the lowest ESTIMATE score, immune score, stromal score, and the highest tumor purity, and lowest immune cell enrichment in cluster 4. Overall, these results highlight that the differential expression of ER-lncRNAs leads to substantial heterogeneity among CRC patients, influencing both tumor behavior and therapeutic response.

Recently, an increasing number of molecular mechanisms and biomarkers involved in pathological processes have been identified, such as non-synonymous and deleterious variants in CRC [50], as well as Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fragmentation in CRC [51]. To further explore the prognostic ER-lncRNAs in CRC, nine ER-lncRNAs (SNHG16, H19, FOXD3-AS1, LINC00174, SNHG7, FENDRR, LINC01315, HAND2-AS1, and OGFRP1) were selected as the prognostic signature. LncRNA SNHG16 and lncRNA H19 were detected in exosomes from sera of 18 CRC patients and significantly upregulated in an advanced stage of CRC samples compared to the early stages [52]. LncRNA H19 functions as a oncogene in CRC [53], involved in tumorigenesis, stemness, EMT, and chemoresistance [54–56]. LncRNA FOXD3-AS1 also functions as an oncogene in multiple cancers, such as glioblastoma [57], lung cancer [58], nasopharyngeal carcinoma [59], and CRC [60]. LncRNA FOXD3-AS1 is closely related to immune infiltration in glioma and can be used as a prognostic biomarker [61]. LINC00174 also acts as an oncogene by facilitating cell proliferation, migration, and invasion and regulating inflammation and glycolysis [62–64]. LncRNA SNHG7 is a key oncogene in various cancers, for example, exosomal SNHG7 promotes docetaxel resistance of LUAD cells [65], and accelerates tumor progression and cisplatin resistance in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [66]. SNHG7 has been identified as an oncogene and potential prognostic biomarker in CRC [67, 68]. LINC01315 is upregulated in CRC and involved in aggressive phenotypes of CRC [69–71]. Exosomal LINC01315 exerts as an oncogene in CRC by regulating the proliferation, migration, and stemness [30]. LncRNA OGFRP1 also functions as an oncogene in CRC by regulating the aggressive phenotypes of CRC [72]. In contrast, lncRNA FENDRR and HAND2-AS1 act as tumor suppressors, and serves as a prognostic biomarker in CRC [73, 74]. Notably, lncRNA HAND2-AS1 plays a tumor suppressor in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [75, 76] and CRC [77].

LncRNAs not only regulate the molecular mechanisms in cancer but also interact with the gut microbiota to promote its reprogramming. For instance, LncRNA SNHG9 mediates the interaction between gut microbiota and host metabolism through immune regulation [78]. Enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) is a well-known tumor-inducing bacterium in the human gut [79], lncRNA BFAL1 mediates ETBF-related carcinogenesis in CRC [80]. F. nucleatum promotes lncRNA ENO1-IT1 to accelerate high glucose metabolism in patients with CRC [81, 82]. Here, we also explored the different microbiotas associated with the ER-lncrisk score. We observed that Firmicutes were significantly decreased in in high-risk group compared to the low-risk group. Furthermore, clostridiales were decreased, but bacteroidales were increased in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group. Although Firmicutes are rarely associated with CRC, some species, such as Clostridium septicum and Bacteroides fragilis, have been identified as promoting colorectal carcinogenesis [83–85].

Although our findings identified DE-ER-lncRNAs and subsequently established a risk signature that may serve as a predictive tool for survival and a potential target for diagnosis and treatment, several limitations remain. First, the identification of ER-lncRNAs was based on Pearson correlation analysis with exosomal mRNAs from public databases, which can only suggest potential co-expression relationships but cannot directly demonstrate causal or locational evidence of exosomal enrichment. Direct experimental validation, such as exosome isolation, was not performed in this study. However, we examined the expression of ER-lncRNAs in clinical samples by qPCR, and we plan to further address this limitation in future research through more comprehensive experimental approaches. Second, although multivariable Cox regression was applied to adjust for clinical factors such as age, sex, and stage, incomplete clinical information in public datasets means that potential confounders could not be fully excluded. Similarly, in the microbiome analysis, key covariates such as tumor location, disease stage, and treatment history were not adequately controlled, which may have introduced bias. Third, due to the resolution of available data, our microbiome analysis was mainly restricted to the phylum, class, and order levels, while species-level associations remain to be further explored.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study systematically identified ER-lncRNA-related molecular phenotypes and revealed the underlying heterogeneity among CRC patients driven by ER-lncRNA expression profiles. We further identified key prognostic ER-lncRNAs and comprehensively evaluated their prognostic significance as well as their interaction with the gut microbiota. Our findings provide novel insights into the regulatory role of ER-lncRNAs within the tumor microenvironment and highlight the potential link between ER-lncRNAs and host–microbiota interactions in colorectal cancer.
