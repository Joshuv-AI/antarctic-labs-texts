Shorter or biomarker-guided antibiotic durations for common serious neonatal infections: a collection of non-inferiority meta-analyses
Authors: Sourabh Dutta, Nandkishor S Kabra, Shiv Sajan Saini, Rajendra Prasad Anne, Sandeep Kadam, Monisha Rameshbabu, Supreet Khurana, Sai Kiran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundUnnecessary antibiotic prolongation promotes antimicrobial resistance. Evidence-based guidance on antibiotic durations for treating neonatal infections is lacking. Noninferiority meta-analyses evaluating shorter durations or biomarker-guided durations have not been conducted.MethodsWe conducted systematic reviews and meta-analyses using a unified search strategy (MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, January 1990–December 2024) to determine whether short-course antibiotics are noninferior to “standard” courses for culture-positive sepsis, culture-negative sepsis, UTIs, uncomplicated/complicated meningitis, and fungal sepsis. Likewise, we determined whether biomarker-guided antibiotic durations are noninferior to “standard” courses for any sepsis. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) without language restriction comparing short-courses or biomarker-guided courses versus standard courses for treating the above neonatal infections. Minimum clinically important differences (MCIDs) for critical outcomes were pre-specified [mortality (28-day, in-hospital or 12-month corrected age) = +1%, culture-positive relapse = +3%, culture-negative relapse = +5%]. We evaluated risk of bias (Cochrane RoB 2.0) and certainty of evidence (CoE) using GRADE. Registration: PROSPERO CRD42023311895.FindingsWe reviewed 146 full-text articles and included 26 in the review. For culture-positive sepsis (7 randomized controlled trials [RCTs]), 7–10-day courses were noninferior to longer courses for 28-day mortality [upper bound of 95% CI for risk difference +0.85%, below 1% MCID (low CoE)], in-hospital mortality [+0.51% (moderate CoE)], culture-positive relapse [+0.75%, below 3% MCID (low CoE)], culture-negative relapse [+4.7%, below 5% MCID (very low CoE)]. For culture-negative and -positive sepsis, biomarker-guided courses (5 RCTs) were noninferior to standard courses for mortality and relapses (all very low CoE). For culture-negative sepsis (6 RCTs), shorter courses (3–4 days) were noninferior for culture-positive relapses (very low CoE) but not noninferior for other outcomes (low to very low CoE). Conclusions could not be drawn for UTI (6 observational studies) or meningitis (1 RCT). There were no data for complicated meningitis and fungal sepsis.InterpretationCompared to standard durations, 7–10-day antibiotic courses and biomarker-guided courses may be noninferior for treating culture-positive neonatal sepsis and any sepsis, respectively. Three-to-four-day courses may not be noninferior for culture-negative sepsis. Given the low to very low CoE, these conclusions are not definitive. No conclusions can be drawn for UTIs, meningitis, and fungal sepsis.FundingNot funded.Keywords: Neonate, Sepsis, Antibiotics, Duration, Noninferiority, Meta-analysis, Relapse

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Since antibiotic durations for serious neonatal systemic infections are often recommended without robust evidence, we searched PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and grey literature databases up to 2022 before initiating this study, and we identified the absence of systematic reviews evaluating the noninferiority of shorter antibiotic durations compared to standard durations for: (i) culture-proven sepsis, (ii) culture-negative sepsis, (iii) urinary tract infections, (iv) meningitis, (v) ventriculitis, (vi) fungal sepsis, and noninferiority of biomarker-guided antibiotic duration compared to standard durations.

Recently, Aljarbou et al. (2024) and Singh et al. (2024) published separate meta-analyses comparing short versus standard durations in culture-proven neonatal sepsis, finding no difference in treatment failure [relative risk (RR) 1.47, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.48–4.50] and RR 2.45 (0.93–6.47), respectively; and Devi et al. (2024) performed a meta-analysis comparing short versus standard courses for culture-negative sepsis, finding no differences in treatment failure rates [RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.55–1.86]. None of these recent meta-analyses were conducted as noninferiority meta-analyses, although such systematic reviews should ideally adhere to noninferiority guidelines (Treadwell 2012).

Added value of this study

We employed a novel unified search strategy to concurrently answer seven PICO questions, performed aggregate data noninferiority meta-analyses, determined the minimum clinically important differences (MCIDs) of critical outcomes a priori [risk differences (RDs): +1% for mortality (by day 28 of life, during hospital stay or by 12 months corrected age), +3% for culture-positive relapses, +5% for culture-negative relapses], conducted per-protocol primary analyses, and interpreted the 95% CIs of the RDs relative to the MCIDs.

Implications of all the available evidence

Shorter courses of antibiotics are noninferior to standard courses for treating blood-culture positive sepsis with respect to short-term mortality [low to moderate certainty of evidence (CoE)] and relapses (very low to low CoE). Biomarker-guided courses are noninferior to standard courses for treating sepsis, regarding short-term mortality and relapses [all very low CoE]. Shorter courses (3–4 days) are noninferior to standard courses for treating culture-negative sepsis regarding culture-positive relapses (very low CoE), but are not noninferior regarding short-term mortality or culture-negative relapses (low to very low CoE). The noninferiority of shorter courses cannot be established for urinary tract infections, uncomplicated meningitis, complicated meningitis and fungal. Given the overall very low to low CoE, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn regarding the noninferiority of shorter courses of antibiotics for treating serious neonatal infections.

Introduction

Sepsis is a significant cause of neonatal mortality worldwide.1 Due to its varied presentations, antibiotics are often overused during the neonatal period, contributing to a rising problem of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) among neonates.2,3 In practice, “standard” antimicrobial durations for common neonatal infections—such as culture-positive sepsis, culture-negative sepsis/pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTI), meningitis, ventriculitis, and fungemia—are generally 10–14 days, 5–7 days, 10–14 days, 21 days, 4–6 weeks, and 14 days, respectively.4, 5, 6, 7 However, evidence-based guidelines on the optimal durations for these conditions are lacking. If shorter courses prove as effective as standard courses, they could replace the latter, potentially reducing AMR, adverse events (AEs), hospitalization duration, and costs. However, inadequate treatment duration may increase the risk of relapse and mortality.8

A recent superiority meta-analysis on culture-positive neonatal sepsis (six randomized controlled trials [RCTs], 573 neonates) concluded that shorter antibiotic courses (7–10 days) reduced hospital stay compared to the standard 14-day course [low certainty of evidence (CoE)] but did not affect treatment failure rates (very low CoE).9 Another comparing short (2–4 days) versus standard (5–7 days) courses for culture-negative sepsis (seven RCTs, 729 neonates) found similar treatment failure rates for both courses (very low CoE).10 A systematic review on meningitis included only one small trial comparing antibiotic durations11 and other small RCTs showed no difference in treatment failure with either short or long courses.12,13 No systematic review of RCTs has specifically compared antibiotic durations for neonatal UTIs.14,15 Additionally, no systematic reviews exist for the optimal antimicrobial duration for treating ventriculitis, fungal sepsis, or biomarker-guided antibiotic courses for bacterial sepsis. None of the previous meta-analyses on shorter versus “standard” antimicrobial courses in neonates adhered to noninferiority guidelines.9,10,16

RCTs comparing shorter versus standard antimicrobial durations should ideally be conducted as noninferiority trials (NITs), and meta-analyses should also follow noninferiority guidelines. Guidelines for NITs and noninferiority meta-analyses (NIMAs) differ substantially from those for superiority trials and meta-analyses.17, 18, 19 Unfortunately, statistically nonsignificant results from superiority trials are often misinterpreted as evidence of noninferiority, and confidence intervals of meta-analyses are not interpreted against pre-specified noninferiority margins, leading to misleading conclusions.20 Given these knowledge gaps and the need for evidence-based recommendations for optimal antimicrobial durations, we conducted updated systematic reviews and NIMAs, addressing several serious neonatal infections concurrently. We assessed the certainty of evidence based on the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) approach.

Methods

We conducted systematic reviews and aggregate data meta-analyses referring to guidelines on conducting NIMAs.19 A team of eight meta-analysts concurrently addressed seven PICO questions. The questions were selected based on the high priority assigned by 49 neonatologists in an online survey (eTable 1). Two primary meta-analysts addressed each question, with a third serving as arbitrator.

Eligibility criteria

We included RCTs (without language restriction) comparing short versus standard antimicrobial courses or biomarker-guided versus standard antibiotic courses for treating neonates with the serious infections covered in questions 1–7 and which included at least one of the pre-specified outcomes. As per the guidelines, RCTs self-reported as superiority trials were not excluded if the key underlying question was noninferiority; however, wherever applicable, these RCTs were penalized for risk of bias (RoB) in domains specific to NITs.19 Observational studies were included only if RCTs were not available. We did not have any additional restrictions on the eligibility or sample size requirements for observational studies. We searched the bibliographies of included studies for additional articles.

Outcomes

For all questions, critical and important outcomes were evaluated. Identifying outcomes as critical or important was done by a 24-member panel of Neonatologists and methodologists who followed the standard GRADE methodology. Outcomes with median ratings of 7–9 were “critical” and median ratings of 4–6 “important”. Outcomes where short-course antibiotics were a priori expected to have a decreased risk were categorized as “superiority outcomes”, and where they had the potential of an increased risk, as “noninferiority outcomes”.

Literature search

We searched EMBASE, MEDLINE, and the Cochrane Library from January 1990 to December 2024. The initial search was conducted on 13 December 2022 and updated on 30 June 2023, 30 April 2024, and 31 December 2024 (search strategy in eTable 2). The systematic review was registered (PROSPERO CRD42023311895, dated 19/2/23, version 1.0). In versions 1.1, 1.2, and 1.3, the search dates and the anticipated review completion dates were updated without making any changes to the protocol.

Selection of articles and data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts, reviewed the shortlisted full-text articles, and extracted data from included studies (e.g., author, publication year, country, study type, eligibility criteria, expected baseline risk and risk difference, antimicrobial duration, sample size, number analyzed per protocol [PP] and intention-to-treat [ITT], sex distribution, and average gestational age, birth weight, and age at randomization). We identified duplicate publications by assessing the authors' names, study location and setting, study dates and duration, and the specific details of population and intervention(s). Questions 1 and 3 were reviewed by SD and SSS; questions 4 and 7 by NSK and Sandeep K; question 2 by MR and Supreet K; questions 5 and 6 by RPA and Sai K. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, failing which, arbitration by a senior reviewer. Questions 4, 5, 6, and 7 were arbitrated by SD. Questions 1, 2, and 3 were arbitrated by NSK. Authors' PP and ITT analyses, if reported, were used without modification. Otherwise, a “worst-case scenario” ITT analysis was performed by imputing outcomes for patients with missing data.

Risk of bias assessment

We used the Cochrane risk of bias (RoB) 2.0 for RCTs21 and the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies.22,23

We also assessed RoB in domains specific to NITs: (1) inconsistent application of eligibility criteria, (2) selection bias, (3) protocol adherence, (4) impact of concomitant treatments, (5) protocol violations, (6) outcome measurement inadequacies, and (7) failure to report PP and ITT analyses in cases of attrition.19,24 Risk of bias was assessed by two independent reviewers, and disagreements resolved as mentioned above. Authors of original studies were not contacted in case of incomplete data.

Minimum clinically important difference (MCID)

An MCID (similar to the noninferiority margin for NITs), was determined a priori for each outcome.19 The authors arrived at a consensus regarding MCIDs for each outcome, partly based on noninferiority margins extracted from published NITs.25, 26, 27, 28, 29 The following principles guided the choice of MCIDs: (a) the smallest published margins, (b) outcomes with similar severity were assigned the same MCID (e.g., all outcomes including mortality had the same MCID), (c) less serious outcomes had a larger MCID (e.g., culture-negative relapse had a higher MCID compared to culture-positive relapse). The MCID was 1% for mortality by day 28 of life, mortality during hospital stay, mortality by 12 months of corrected age, death or moderate or severe NDI at or after 12 months of age, and for moderate or severe NDI at or after 12 months of age. The MCID was 3% for relapse with culture-positive sepsis or meningitis, relapse with culture-positive UTI, seizures needing >1 anticonvulsant, chronic renal failure and for hydrocephalus requiring surgical intervention. The MCID was 5% for relapse with culture-negative sepsis or meningitis, and relapse with culture-negative UTI (Table 1). The noninferiority margin is conceptually like the MCID, but not synonymous with it. Ideally, the noninferiority margin should be equal to the MCID, however, researchers sometimes choose a more liberal NI margin (“not much worse”) than a strict MCID (“smallest difference that would be meaningful to patients”).30,31

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted if at least two eligible studies were available, which had similar eligibility criteria, interventions, comparisons, and outcomes. We carefully considered clinical, methodological and statistical homogeneity before combining studies (identified by the systematic review) in the meta-analysis. Both PP and ITT analyses were reported, with the primary analysis being PP. Since MCIDs are typically expressed as risk differences (RDs), the upper bounds (UBs) of two-sided 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of RDs were compared with the respective MCIDs to determine noninferiority. Risk ratio (RR) meta-analyses have the advantage over risk difference (RDs) meta-analyses as the former are less affected by differences in baseline risks across studies. Hence, our principal approach was to obtain a pooled RR by a RR meta-analysis and derive absolute effects (expressed as RDs) from the pooled RR using the auto-calculate option in GRADE Pro. RR meta-analysis was performed using the software “R” with continuity correction of 0.5 for RCTs with zero cell counts. The secondary approach was to directly conduct RD meta-analyses in RevMan 5.4.19 Random effects models were used as per guidelines, as the confidence interval of a fixed effects model may be inappropriately narrow.19 Pooled effects in observational studies were expressed as odds ratios (OR). Continuous data were compared using mean differences (MDs). The I2 statistic was used to assess statistical heterogeneity for binary outcomes, and Tau2 for continuous outcomes. An I2 ≥50% was considered significant. τ2 (tau-squared) test does not have accepted cut-off values and was used only to compare heterogeneity.32

Publication bias

We planned to construct funnel plots if the number of studies exceeded ten.33

Assessment of certainty of evidence

Certainty of evidence (CoE) was assessed using the GRADE approach, using the GRADEPro GDT software.34 It was based on risk-of-bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness and other considerations. Risk of bias was assessed by two independent reviewers, and disagreements resolved as mentioned above. The literature lacks adequate guidance on formulating recommendations based on NIMAs.

Role of the funding source

No funding was received.

Results

19,541 records were identified, and 146 full-text articles were retrieved and categorized by question. After exclusions, 26 studies were included across five questions. No studies were identified for questions 6 and 7 (Fig. 1). Since none of the questions had more than ten studies, funnel plots were not constructed. The studies included in the meta-analyses for questions 1 to 4 had acceptable clinical, methodological and statistical homogeneity (barring certain caveats mentioned in the section on UTI).

The results of direct RD meta-analysis were on similar lines as above (eFigures 2A, 3A, 4A, and 5A, Fig. 6B).

Only two trials reported adverse events (AE's).25,27 Fursule et al. reported median episodes of extravasation in the short vs. long course groups [3 (2, 3) vs. 5 (4, 7), p < 0.001], phlebitis [2 (2, 4) vs. 3 (2, 3), p = 0.76], and hematomas [1 (0, 1) vs. 1 (0, 2), p = 0.35].25 Dutta et al. evaluated a broad spectrum of AE's.27 In the short vs. standard course groups, there were 5.26 vs. 5.03 episodes of any AE's per 1000 patient-days [IRR (95% CI) = 1.05 (0.46, 2.39), p = 0.8] and 0.91 vs. 1.53 episodes of serious AE's (SAE's) per 1000 patient-days [IRR (95% CI) = 0.59 (0.10, 3.47), p = 0.4].

The pooled mean difference (MD) from two RCT's comparing antibiotic durations was −5.41 days (95% CI: −7.62, −3.19), favoring the short course25,27 (eFigure 6A). Tau2 was 2.47. All RCT's reported hospital stay duration,25, 26, 27,35, 36, 37, 38 and the pooled MD was significantly shorter in the short-course arm [−3.69 (95% CI: −4.42, −2.97)] (eFigure 7A). Tau2 was 0.39, indicating less heterogeneity. No other outcomes were reported.

The results of RR meta-analysis by ITT are shown in eFigures 8A, 9A, 10A, and 11A. Based on the pooled RRs, the CIs for RD were derived using GRADE Pro (Fig. 6C). The UBs of 95% CIs for mortality by day 28 (+3.13%), culture-positive relapse (+4.82%) and culture-negative relapse (+5.33%) crossed the respective MCID's, whereas that for mortality by hospital discharge (+0.82%) was below the MCID.

PP subgroup analyses were performed for the trials comparing 7 versus 14 days,27,35 10 versus 14 days25,26,36,38 and 7 versus 10 days37 (eFigure 12A–D). The p values of the tests of interaction for mortality by day 28, mortality by hospital discharge, culture-positive relapse and culture-negative relapse were 0.89, 0.92, 0.98, and 0.83, respectively, demonstrating no subgroup effects.

A short course of antibiotics was noninferior to the standard course regarding mortality by day 28 (low CoE), mortality by hospital discharge (moderate CoE), culture-positive relapse (low CoE) and culture-negative relapse (very low CoE) [Table 3]. The durations of antibiotic therapy (high CoE) and hospitalization (moderate CoE) were significantly shorter with a short course.

Of 32 shortlisted citations, none were RCTs, and 25 articles were excluded after full-text review (eTable 3D), leaving seven studies (Table 8).14,49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54 Six of the 7 were primary studies.49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54 Although all primary studies were included in the meta-analysis, caution is warranted because one was a case-control study while the rest were retrospective cohort studies, and one study each included bacteremia and pyelonephritis beyond febrile UTI. Nama (2021) was a living systematic review that included five of the six primary studies.14 While the primary objective of the systematic review (≤3 vs. >3 days of parenteral antibiotics in UTI) was not directly relevant, the secondary objective comparing total antibiotic duration (short vs. standard) was of interest. The authors did not mention total antibiotic duration in the original articles; however, Nama et al. obtained unpublished data by contacting authors, allowing for comparison.49, 50, 51, 52, 53 Using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, all six studies had high RoB (main biases due to selection, misclassification, detection, and immortal time biases)49, 50, 51, 52, 53, 54 (Fig. 2D). Bias related to NITs was not applicable since there were no eligible RCTs.

Discussion

We conducted a collection of meta-analyses addressing seven related PICO questions comparing various durations of antimicrobials and biomarker-guided versus standard durations in serious neonatal infections. Across the meta-analyses, high risk of bias was prevalent. Few original trials were explicitly conducted as noninferiority trials (NITs), and the sample sizes generally fell short of the respective optimal information sizes. Results of NITs and NIMA need to be interpreted carefully. In the context of NITs, “noninferior” means a new treatment is not worse than a standard treatment by more than a predefined margin, while “not noninferior” means the results are inconclusive (the CI crosses the noninferiority margin), while “inferior” means the new treatment is worse than the standard treatment (entire CI on higher side of noninferiority margin, thus favoring the standard treatment).58

Our collection of meta-analyses offers several unique features. By addressing closely related questions, we applied a unified search strategy and chose common outcomes (barring a few question-specific outcomes), thus improving efficiency compared to conducting seven separate meta-analyses. To the best of our knowledge, our meta-analyses are the first in this field to explicitly address noninferiority questions. We prespecified MCIDs, extracted PP and ITT data from individual studies and interpreted the absolute effects compared to the respective MCIDs. For most outcomes, the certainty of evidence (CoE) was low to very low, which reflected high risk of bias and small sample sizes. However, statistical heterogeneity across studies was generally low. To our knowledge, there is no previous meta-analysis comparing biomarker-guided versus standard durations of antibiotics in neonates. NIMAs require assessment of RoB in certain domains specific to NITs, which we duly reported. Recently several meta-analyses have been published on neonatal infections,9, 10, 11,14,16 none of which were carried out using a noninferiority approach. Not only was our methodological approach distinctly different from the previous meta-analyses, but our conclusions also differed from them and gave a novel perspective.

The literature supports shorter antibiotic durations for older age groups and different organ systems.59,60 Evidence suggests that unnecessarily prolonged antibiotic courses are linked to increased adverse events (AEs) and the development of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).61,62

Clinical cure defined objectively and comprehensively is the most patient-centric metric to define cure. Therefore, the comparison of treatment failure rates between antibiotic regimens remains the most practical method for determining optimal durations. Recent systematic reviews have assessed antibiotic regimens for managing serious bacterial infections, pneumonia, and meningitis in infants aged 0–59 days.11,63,64 However, none compared short versus standard antibiotic durations.

Our meta-analyses have certain limitations. There are challenges with using the GRADE approach for noninferiority trials. For example, the implications of CI imprecision, large effect size, and dose-response gradient differ between superiority and NITs. Our search strategy was limited to three major databases. Although the Cochrane database includes trial protocols and Embase includes conference abstracts, we may have missed some registered trials and conference proceedings. We did not include the parent/caregiver's perspective while selecting the outcomes. Thus, we failed to include parent-centric outcomes, such as “parental quality of life”. Although the MCIDs were narrow and decided a priori, a broader consensus through a Delphi process involving more neonatologists would have been preferable. The MCID for the superiority outcomes expressed as numerical variables (duration of antibiotics and of hospital stay) could have been decided a priori by an anchor-based or distribution-based method followed by triangulation, but this was not performed in our study.65

The observed event rates for most outcomes in the standard treatment arms were considerably lower than anticipated, rendering the pre-specified MCIDs disproportionately large. For example, mortality by day 28 in the standard treatment arms was only 0.6% for culture-positive sepsis, 0.12% for biomarker-guided treatment, and 0% for culture-negative sepsis.

The authors of several primary RCTs had kept patients in the short-course arm for an extra period of observation in hospital after antibiotic completion, potentially introducing a detection bias. In the RCTs on culture-positive sepsis, culture-negative sepsis, and meningitis, those patients were included who had remitted within a duration equal to the short-course. Since the point of randomization to “no further antibiotics” versus continued antibiotics was after the completion of duration equal to the short-course, mortality and relapse events were rare.

Bradburn et al. evaluated meta-analytical methods for pooling rare events (in superiority meta-analyses) and concluded that for event rates below 1%, the Peto one-step OR method yielded less bias and greater statistical power than others.66 The disadvantage of the Peto OR is that it eliminates studies that have zero events in both arms (not an uncommon finding in NIMAs on antibiotic durations) and cannot be performed with a random-effects model. Considering all options, the most optimal primary approach was random-effects pooled RR with 0.5 continuity correction for zero events performed in “R”. In addition, we reported RD meta-analyses. RD meta-analysis has the drawback of combining RCTs with varying baseline risks, but it can include RCTs with zero events obviating the need for continuity corrections.

We did not contact investigators of primary studies for incomplete data because of the practical difficulties in doing this for 7 concurrent meta-analyses. Many studies lacked data to calculate intention-to-treat (ITT) results, so we used a worst-case scenario analysis for imputing outcomes, which is an overly conservative approach. Most studies on antibiotic duration for culture-positive sepsis, culture-negative sepsis, and meningitis were conducted in India. Given the distinct pathogen profile and high AMR prevalence in India, generalizability to high-income countries may be limited.67, 68, 69

The meta-analysis on UTI posed unique problems because all the studies were retrospective. Ideally, outcomes should have been adjusted for a common set of potential confounders, but this is possible only with individual patient data meta-analysis. No primary study had addressed the issue of immortal time bias.

We could not find any eligible studies on fungemia. There are four sets of expert guidelines,70, 71, 72, 73, 74 and one review of guidelines.75 The IDSA Guidelines have been revised periodically.71,76, 77, 78 Most guidelines suggest administering antifungal therapy for 14 days after the fungal blood culture turns sterile (eTable 5).70, 71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78 This recommendation has been derived from the protocol of an RCT comparing antifungals in adults, rather than comparing durations of therapy.79

We conclude that shorter and biomarker-guided antibiotic durations are possibly noninferior to standard durations for the treatment of culture-positive neonatal sepsis and any sepsis (i.e., culture-negative or -positive sepsis), respectively. However, given the low CoE, we cannot be definitive about our findings. We recommend conducting large-scale, multicenter trials globally to determine the optimal duration of antibiotics for serious neonatal infections.
