Acoustic water display
Authors: Félix Sisombat, Thibaut Devaux, Samuel Callé, Jere Hyvönen, Axi Holmström, Ari Salmi, Lionel Haumesser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCross-boundary communication between media with high impedance mismatch, such as air and water, remains a major challenge for conventional wireless transmission systems. At such interfaces, most of the emitted signal energy is reflected at the media boundaries. Here, we propose a novel display technology for optical information transmission across such an interface, using acoustic radiation pressure to dynamically shape the water–air boundary. The design is based on the combination of spatiotemporal deformations of a water-air interface induced by acoustic radiation pressure. It allows to shape a water-air interface in real-time. By tuning an incident acoustic field at the interface, we demonstrate the ability to generate contactless complex deformation shapes, such as ellipsoidal deformation. Subsequently, we demonstrate the creation of a virtual seven-segment display on the water surface to display numeric values from “0” to “9”. This work paves the way for new opportunities to transfer information across the boundary between two media.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-16755-2.Subject terms: Acoustics, Fluid dynamics

Introduction

The rapid evolution of wireless communication technologies demands the development of robust and reliable solutions to meet the growing complexity and scale of modern systems. In communication systems, the propagation channel is one of the elementary elements. For wireless communication, assuring energy transmission of the information without distortion across medium boundaries in the channel remains a critical challenge1–3. Establishing a communication link between an underwater system and an in-air system is one of these challenges due to the signal reflection at the water-air interface. Because of their agility, fluid interfaces can be deformed in space and time. These mechanical deformations have inspired their use as screens4. Here, we propose a novel type of display to visually convey information across a water–air interface using acoustic radiation force. The acoustic radiation force allows to use the water surface as a screen to display messages. The application of acoustic radiation force upstream of the water-air interface results in the creation of protuberances at the boundary5–7. Time-dependent perturbations of the surface generated from the underwater side can be detected by an airborne receiver2,8,9 but the data transmission efficiency is limited in space by the punctual nature of the wave excitation. The advantage of this method lies in the utilisation of a liquid, which enables the possibility to program and reconfigure a screen in real-time4,10. In parallel, recent advancements in meta-surfaces have allowed for improved functionality and energy transmission at fluid interfaces1,11–13. By locating complex structures at the surface, meta-surfaces can enhance the acoustic transparency of the water-air interface, but transmission is restricted to the nearfield regime.

The creation of protuberances on the surface of water by the force of acoustic radiation is a phenomenon that was demonstrated as early as the 20th century3,6,14–25. It has since been the subject of numerous applications, such as the displacement of particles in microfluidics26,27and more recently ultrasound-enhanced electrospinning28. The spatiotemporal characteristics of the interface deformation induced by acoustic radiation pressure (ARP) can be tailored by driving the parameters of the incident ultrasonic excitation5,7,18,19,29. To achieve this, the acoustic pressure level, the type of beam and its orientation are adapted to each application. Recent work by Melde has shown that ARP enables patterning particles at the water surface into a variety of shapes using acoustic holograms30. While this work lays the foundation for forming smooth geometries on a water surface, real-time reconfigurability is limited by the printed hologram. Notably, previous demonstrations of acoustic-induced surface deformations have also been explored for haptic feedback applications21,23where the tactile sensation rather than visual display was the primary goal.

The shape of the protuberance relies on the spatial distribution of the incident pressure field5,7. Tailoring the incident pressure field in space and in time provides new insight for shaping complex patterns at the water surface. However, interface deformation is commonly studied for a normal-incident focused ultrasound beam, resulting in an interface deformation with a radial symmetry around the focal spot17. This axisymmetric deformation limits the reconfigurable shape variety of the fluid interface deformation. To overcome this symmetry, characterization of the deformation resulting from an oblique incident beam10 has been proposed.

In this work, we propose to combine multiple incident beams to generate complex patterns on a water-air interface. The reconfigurability, in real time, of this pattern allows to create a water display. Based on the theoretical expression of the pressure field induced by a single planar transducer, experiments are first carried out to investigate the shape of the deformation induced by an obliquely incident ultrasonic beam. The resulting elementary interface deformation is subsequently used as a unit segment of the water display. Then, the interface deformation induced by the combination of the pressure fields generated by two transducers is investigated by means of numerical simulations and compared to the experimental results. Finally, the proof-of-concept of a water display based on ARP is demonstrated. The combination of seven incident pressure fields allows to display seven independent segments in real time. By combining those segments, numerical values from “0” to “9” are displayed. The on-water display is then experimentally characterized using confocal measurements and a digital camera. This concept shows the possibility to achieve cross-boundary data encoding and communication through the water-air interface.

Results

Principle of the water display

The concept of the water-based display is illustrated in Fig. 1. A bi-layer water-air system is employed. By generating an acoustic field towards the water-air interface, interface deformations are expected6. The deformation shape is contingent upon the pattern of the pressure field impinging on the interface. The deformation of the interface is proportional to the square of the amplitude of the incident pressure field. The control of the incoming two-dimensional pressure field in time and space enables the display of a complex shape, such as the number “5” (depicted in Fig. 1). The induced patterns can therefore be detected using any optical device such as a camera or electromagnetic sensor5,7 (confocal sensor, laser light). By shaping the water-air interface in real time, we propose here a robust solution to facilitate communications between underwater systems and in-air systems.

Schematic representation of a water display using acoustic radiation pressure. A source generates an acoustic field from water toward the interface with air. The induced acoustic radiation pressure results in elevation of water according to the 2D shape of the wavefront at the interface. The generated pattern is detected with an optical device allowing to transmit data through the interface.

It can be observed that the measured interface deformation formed by both transducers (solid red line) is close to the sum of the deformation H1 (blue dashed line) and H2 (green dashed line) denoted by the black dashed line. When the two incident pressure fields slightly overlap, the resulting interface deformation is a linear combination of the two induced deformations. Simulation results show a good agreement with the measured deformation. Minor variations can be attributed to the positioning of the hydrophone measurements, which serve as inputs for the simulation model (see Methods section for details).

This shows the possibility to obtain complex geometry at the water surface depending on the shape of the incident pressure field. By combining several pressure fields from different transducers, it is then possible to achieve experimentally complex shaping of the water surface by ARP.

7-segments display

The conception of a display is investigated using seven transducers to generate seven segments independently addressable in time. The experimental setup used to characterise the display is presented in Fig. 4(a).

Experimental configuration of the 7-segment acoustic display. (a) Experimental setup. (b) Expected segments generated by each transducer. (c) Measured pressure field with simultaneous emissions of all transducers and (d) induced interface deformation measured, alternatively, with the confocal displacement sensor and a digital camera.

The display design is based on 7 ultrasound transducers positioned in a water tank as shown in Fig. 4(b). Each transducer allows controlling one segment of the 7 segments necessary to display numbers from “0” to “9”. Transducers are directed towards the water-air interface of the water tank top. Each elliptical shape represents one interface deformation. This spatial configuration enables to create a 7-segment display pattern. The pressure field is measured when all the transducers are turned on (Fig. 4(c)). It can be observed that the pressure presents the shape of the digit “8”.

By optimizing the position, angle and input pressure field of the transducer, a mm-scale water protrusion at the water-air interface is expected5. This deformation is induced by the mean incident acoustic energy and relies on the shape and angle of the incident beam (Eq. 2). Due to the inclination of the transducer, deformation with an elongated shape (10 mm x 5 mm) is expected as shown on Fig. 2.

On Fig. 4(d), the 8-digit shape of the interface deformation, consistent with its dependence on the square of the incident pressure, is also observed through confocal measurements of the deformation height.

The 7 segments can be controlled independently, which enables real-time reconfiguration of the information displayed. Although measurements with a confocal laser enables obtaining precise estimation of the induced interface deformation, performing the 2D scan results in long measurements and so in a long time to detect the full-digit display.

To have an instantaneous detection of the digits displayed, it is possible to use a camera positioned above the interface and triggering a flash. The convex shape of the deformed water surface enables to focus the light on the bottom of the water tank as depicted in Fig. 5(1)-(10). Results are presented in the Fig. 5 (a)-(i) for confocal measurements and (1)-(10) photos representing numbers between “0” to “9”. (Dynamic reconfiguration can be observed in Supplementary Movie 1.)

Experimental evidence of digits “0” to “9” using ARP. By switching ON and OFF the ultrasonic transducers, digits from “0” to “9” can be displayed. (a)-(i) confocal measurement of the water elevation induced by ARP. Color scale denotes a variation of the protrusion height between 0 to 0.3 mm. (1)-(10) Photos of light focused on the water tank floor through refraction by the convex water-air interface deformation induced by ARP.

It is possible to observe that the pattern can be satisfactorily measured by both camera and confocal laser. The ten patterns corresponding to digits “0” to “9” are generated. Once each deformation is set-up, the deformations are stable (See Supplementary Movie 1). It shows the robustness of the proof-of-concept water display. The spatial resolution of the display is limited by the width of each segment (induced by the piezoelectric transducer active surface). The reconfiguration of each pattern in less than 100 microseconds is governed by the interplay between the shape of the incident pressure field and the liquid surface tension5. It offers new ways of communicating across the interface, with the possibility to vary the reconfigurable 2D pattern in real time.

Discussion

Acoustic radiation pressure has demonstrated its robustness to remotely generate time and space-controlled protrusions at water-air interfaces without any contact5,7. A proof-of-concept demonstration of the possibility to achieve complex and reconfigurable water-air interface deformation is shown. It enables the visual transmission of information across material boundaries with high acoustic impedance mismatch. By combining seven transducers, we have shown the ability to shape of the pressure field through the positioning and inclination of each transducer. The design of one segment of the display has been studied through the possibility to induce a non-radial symmetry around a focal point, typically seen with a normally incident beam, using obliquely incident excitation. This result illustrates the possibility to achieve ellipsoidal deformation with a single element.

Overlapping the incident pressure fields to generate a linear combination of interface deformations has been studied in Fig. 3. It highlights that the superposition of the incident acoustic beams does not lead to instability or interferences in the water surface elevation. Close agreement with simulations attests the possibility of using multiple transducers to generate a combination of interface deformations for complex patterning of water-air interface deformation. Extending the study to seven transducers validates the conception of the 7-segment display.

Experimental and numerical results have shown the possibility to display up to 128 different shapes comprising ten digits (“0” to “9”) by the use of seven transducers. Owing to the transient characteristic of the ARP, each of the 7 segments induced can be controlled in real-time in amplitude. It allows to combine in-space and in-real-time information encoding and transmission through the water-air interface. More advanced shaping of the pressure fields could be used with phased array transducers or dynamic holograms offering the ability to elevate more complex patterns. Extending prior work based on single-point deformation for communication through water-air interface2this study could open the way to spatial modulation as a new degree of freedom for communication.

Methods

Experimental apparatus

The transducers are composed of flat piezoelectric disk (PZT4 - Soar Piezo) of 25 mm diameter corresponding to the ultrasonic active region. The thickness is 1.26 mm. The central frequency of the ultrasonic transducer is 1.75 MHz. The backing of the transducers has been made by 3D printing material (polylactic acid filament) corresponding to a volume of 6.51 cm3. All experiments have been performed in a water tank 60 cm x 58 cm x 16 cm dimensions.

The radiation pattern of the pressure field emitted by the ultrasound (US) transducer have been characterized using classical hydrophone measurements. A vertically positioned hydrophone (needle hydrophone, Precision Acoustics LTD) is used. The distance between the hydrophone needle and the transducer is set as d = 3 cm. An angle of =60° is chosen arbitrarily to avoid reflections at the water tank boundaries and to maximize the size of the deformation. A 2D-scan of the acoustic pressure field has been performed along the x-y plane using a motorized stage with a step of 0.1 cm.

To study the interface deformation generated by an oblique incidence, a single transducer is placed at 3 cm below the water surface with angles of =[0; 60]°, as illustrated by Fig. 2(a). The shape of the interface deformation induced by this incident pressure field is measured at the center of the deformation along the x-axis with a step of 1 mm and a total length of 40 mm corresponding to 41 samples. The height of the deformation is tracked with polychromatic confocal sensor5.

To study the cumulative effect on the water-air interface deformation (Fig. 3), two transducers with opposite tilt angle (60° and − 60°) are placed 3 cm below the interface. The distance between the piezoelectric disk centres is set as 100 mm along the x-axis. The shape of the interface deformation induced by this incident pressure field is measured at the center of the deformation along the x-axis with a step of 1 mm and a total length of 100 mm corresponding to 101 samples.

For results shown in Figs. 2 and 3, the excitation signal consists of emitting 20 periods of a 1.75 MHz sine burst. The excitation signal is generated by an amplifier capable of delivering an electrical signal to up to 16 channels, with independent control of amplitude and phase. Each channel of the amplifier consisted of a power MOSFET connected to a 1:4 transformer whose output was connected to an LC low pass filter. The transformer was used to step-up the voltage and the filter was used to remove harmonics from the output, resulting in a sinusoidal excitation signal.

To determine the acoustic field generated by each of the 7 identical transducers, a 2D-scan of the acoustic pressure field has been performed along the x-y plane using a motorized stage with a step of 1 mm. A 20 periods sine burst of 1.75 MHz frequency is emitted by each of the seven transducers. The amplitude of the signal on each channel is adjusted to match the impedance of individual transducers, ensuring uniform pressure amplitude delivery at the interface. To ensure spatially continuous deformations over the water surface, the transducers were symmetrically arranged around the display area. Grouping all transducers in a single location was avoided, as their finite size, limited numbers, and required tilt angles would have introduced discontinuities between adjacent deformations. More advanced pressure field shaping, using phased array transducers or dynamic holograms, could enable the elevation of more complex surface patterns.

The measurement of the resulting surface displacement is ensured by a confocal displacement sensor mounted on a motorised stage. A 2D scan is performed on along 100 mm in the x and y direction. Pictures shown in Fig. 5 have been taken with a Nikon D5600 camera. Millimetric graph paper has been added to the bottom of the water tank to help to visualize the water surface deformations. Additional lighting with normal incidence was only used to enhance top-view image capture for comparison with simulations. However, real-time visualization is possible under standard ambient light conditions without any specific lighting setup. We estimate the acoustic display to be robust to typical water disturbances, as long as ripples or waves remain below ± 0.3 mm, less than the height of the deformations required for readable patterns.

Numerical model

Simulations are performed using finite element methods with the Comsol Multiphysics software. The numerical model uses ‘laminar two-phase flow’ physics to solve the Navier-Stokes equations using a moving mesh. The acoustic radiation pressure is set as an external force applied on a free surface calculated using the incident pressure field measured while the two transducers emit simultaneously (solid purple line in Fig. 3(b)). The boundaries of the domain along x are set wide enough to avoid interference of retropropagating waves (100 mm). The depth along z is set at 10 mm to be in capillary waves regime. The resulting interface deformation can be observed in Fig. 3(b).
