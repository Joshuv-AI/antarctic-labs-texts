A plastoglobuli-localized enzyme links phenylalanine biosynthesis to translational homeostasis in maize
Authors: Xing Huang, Yuwei Cao, Qiong Wang, Xingguo Wu, Yu Su, Changsheng Li, Jiaojiao Feng, Yujia Yang, Wenqin Wang, Yongrui Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAromatic amino acids are essential precursors for numerous plant metabolites, with phenylalanine (Phe) forming the basis of the phenylpropanoid pathway. Here, we reveal a critical mechanism for Phe biosynthesis in maize, demonstrating that all seven arogenate dehydratases (ADTs) are specifically localized to plastoglobuli (PGs) in chloroplasts, and ADT2.2 shows high catalytic activity towards arogenate and prephenate. This discovery establishes PGs as a site for Phe synthesis. Genetic analysis confirms that only ADT2.2 is indispensable for plant and seed development, with its loss causing a severe Phe deficiency in seeds. This metabolic blockage directly reduced the tRNAPhe-GAA charging, thereby repressing protein translation. Crucially, we uncover that Phe starvation disproportionately affects the decoding efficiency of wobble-paired codons, increasing ribosome pausing. Our work provides biochemical and genetic evidence that PGs-localized ADT2.2 catalyzes Phe synthesis and reveals a link between amino acid availability and codon-specific translation dynamics.Subject terms: Agricultural genetics, Transgenic plants, Chloroplasts, Secondary metabolism

Introduction

Aromatic amino acids (AAAs), L-phenylalanine (Phe), L-tyrosine (Tyr), and L-tryptophan (Trp) are essential precursors for nitrogen assimilation and a vast array of aromatic secondary metabolites in plants. Phe, for instance, is the precursor to phenylpropanoids, a diverse class of compounds that includes salicylic acid (SA), alkaloids, flavonoids, and aromatic antibiotics, as well as lignin, one of the most abundant biopolymers on Earth1–7. Furthermore, as an essential amino acid for humans, Phe is crucial for enhancing net protein balance, promoting muscle anabolism, and mitigating muscle wasting associated with aging and disease8,9.

In plants, AAAs are synthesized from chorismate, the final product of the shikimate pathway. Chorismate mutase 1 converts chorismate to prephenate10,11, which serves as a precursor for two alternative Phe synthesis pathways: the plastidial arogenate pathway and the cytosolic phenylpyruvate (PPY) pathway12,13. Decades of research have broadly defined the core AAA biosynthetic pathways within plastids via the arogenate pathway14–16. In this pathway, prephenate is transaminated to arogenate by prephenate aminotransferase (PPA-AT) and subsequently dehydrated and decarboxylated to Phe by ADT15–17.

Maize (Zea mays) Big embryo 6 (Bige6) encodes a plastid-localized PPA-AT that can directly catalyze prephenate transamination to arogenate18. Mutations in Bige6 impair both endosperm development and plant growth, while knockout of its Arabidopsis homolog results in female gametophyte abortion12,18. The importance of the subsequent step, catalyzed by ADT, is underscored by the fact that Arabidopsis mutants for adt2 arrest at the globular embryo stage19. Adt gene families are diverse, with six, nine, four, and three members identified in Arabidopsis, pine, rice, and petunia, respectively20–23. Furthermore, their functions are highly differentiated; in Petunia hybrida, an alternative transcription start site (TSS) in PhAdt3 generates a functional cytosolic prephenate dehydratase (PDT) that enables an alternative Phe synthesis route24. Collectively, these findings suggest that Adts have evolved differentiated functions and multiple, integrated regulatory mechanisms to precisely control Phe synthesis19,20.

PGs are dynamic lipoprotein particles known to store tocopherols and other isoprenoid-derived metabolites25. Beyond storage, PGs are active sites for prenyl lipid metabolism, redox and photosynthetic regulation, plastid biogenesis, and senescence26–29. However, whether PGs are involved in amino acid metabolism remains unclear. Maize is one of the most important crops; seven Adt genes were annotated in the maize genome, but none of their activity and biological function have been well illustrated.

In this study, we show that maize ADTs assemble into homo- and heteromeric complexes that localize to PGs. We demonstrate that ADT2.2 is essential for Phe synthesis, as its knockout severely depletes Phe, especially in the endosperm, causing a filling deficiency and metabolic imbalance. This Phe starvation represses protein translation by dramatically reducing the aminoacylation of tRNAPhe-GAA, leading to ribosome pausing at TTT codons. We identified 12,321 genes in adt2.2 endosperm with TTT A-site-specific translation pausing, implicating a wide range of biological processes involved. Notably, we found that decoding of wobble-paired codons is more sensitive to Phe deficiency, revealing an intriguing mechanism for translational response. Concurrently, the GCN2-eIF2α (S51) pathway is activated to mitigate the stress of Phe starvation. Overall, our findings establish ADT2.2 as a crucial enzyme for Phe synthesis and provide a paradigm for investigating amino acid metabolism, offering targets to enhance crop nutritional quality, defense, and nitrogen use efficiency for sustainable agriculture.

Result

Identification of the Adt gene family in maize

To identify the maize genes responsible for converting arogenate to Phe, we searched the MAIZEGDB database for sequences homologous to the six annotated Arabidopsis thaliana ADT homologs (ADT1-6)20. This search identified seven candidate genes. Based on phylogenetic analysis, Zm00001d021611 and Zm00001d048561, which were most similar to Arabidopsis ADT1, were designated ADT1.1 and ADT1.2, respectively. Similarly, Zm00001d047755 and Zm00001d028712 were named ADT2.1 and ADT2.2 due to their similarity to Arabidopsis ADT2, while Zm00001d003830, Zm00001d025333, and Zm00001d005351 were designated ADT6.1, ADT6.2, and ADT6.3 based on their homology to Arabidopsis ADT6 (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Fig. 2).

In silico analysis using TargetP-2.0 predicted that all seven maize ADTs possess an N-terminal chloroplast transit peptide (cTP) (Fig. 1a). Domain analysis revealed that each protein contains the conserved PDT catalytic domain and the aspartate kinase-Chorismate mutase-TyrA (ACT) regulatory domain20 (Fig. 1a). Gene expression analysis showed that all seven Adts are expressed in both leaves and seeds, with Adt2.2 most highly expressed in leaves and Adt2.1 in seeds (Fig. 1b). Yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays demonstrated that all ADTs can interact with each other and with themselves, indicating the potential to form both homo- and heteromultimers (Fig. 1c). To confirm their multimeric state in vivo, we purified native ADT2.2 complexes using a FLAG-tagged bait. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) of the purified complexes identified all seven ADT family members, confirming that ADT2.2 forms multimeric complexes in planta (Fig. 1d). Consistent with this, size exclusion chromatography (SEC) of purified recombinant ADT2.2 showed that it forms multimers in solution. (Fig. 1e).

Maize ADTs localize to PGs

To experimentally determine the subcellular localization of maize ADT proteins, the coding sequences (CDS) of each gene were fused to the N-terminus of green fluorescent protein (GFP) and transiently expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaf cells using the Agrobacterium infiltration method. All ADT proteins are localized to chloroplasts and form punctate structures (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3). Punctate localization of the enzyme may allow it to catalyze its reaction at a site where its substrate is most readily available30–32. The ADT proteins of Petunia also exhibited a similar localization patterns16. However, the specific subchloroplast compartmentalization of these puncta remains unresolved.

PGs, which are thylakoid-membrane-associated lipid droplets, form a functional metabolic link between the inner envelope and thylakoid membranes and play an essential role in chloroplast metabolic regulation33. To identify these puncta in Fig. 2a, we performed co-localization studies with SOUL4 (SOUL heme–binding protein 4), a known component of Arabidopsis PGs34. The ADT signals co-localized with SOUL4-TDT (tandem dimer Tomato), confirming that maize ADTs are PGs-associated (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3). This localization is dependent on an N-terminal cTP. Once the cTPs were deleted, the ADT2.1ΔN-GFP and ADT2.2ΔN-GFP mainly accumulated in the cytosol and co-localized with the OCD1-TDT, which encodes a cytosolic Oxalyl-CoA decarboxylase135 (Fig. 2b). The other five truncated members were accumulated in the nucleus and the cytosol (Fig. 2b). These findings provide strong evidence that maize ADTs are components of PGs, supporting the hypothesis that the Phe biosynthesis occurs within PGs.

ADT2.2 efficiently catalyzes Phe synthesis

Given that all ADT members are localized to the PGs, we questioned whether their functions might be redundant (Fig. 2a). To test this, we synthesized the disodium salt of arogenate in seven steps (Fig. 3a). Because the disodium salt was prone to re-aromatization under acidic conditions, it was purified on a Hypercarb™ column using a 10 mM Na2CO3/NaHCO3 (pH 9.6) mobile phase36. The product had a retention peak time of 3.20 min (Fig. 3b). The 1H NMR (Proton Nuclear Magnetic Resonance) spectrum was consistent with the literature report36 (Fig. 3c).

To reconstitute the arogenate dehydratase and decarboxylase activities of ADTs in vitro, we expressed all ADT members lacking their N-terminal cTPs in an Escherichia coli system. Highly purified recombinant ADT proteins were prepared (Supplementary Fig. 4), and Trigger Factor (TF) tags were removed by tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease to avoid confounding effects. We established a stable catalytic reaction system, in which arogenate remained stable in the absence of ADT proteins; an equivalent concentration of bovine serum albumin (BSA) was used as a negative control. Incubation of 30 pmol of each ADT protein with arogenate in phosphate-buffered solution (pH 8.0), followed by HPLC analysis of the reaction mixtures, revealed that only ADT2.2 was capable of converting arogenate to Phe in vitro (31.43% ± 0.17%). The other six ADT members exhibited negligible catalytic activity under the same conditions (Fig. 3d). When the protein amount was increased to 120 pmol, ADT2.2-mediated conversion was markedly enhanced (44.31% ± 0.55%); moreover, ADT6.1 (5.32% ± 0.46%), ADT6.2 (25.70% ± 0.64%) and ADT6.3 (32.86% ± 0.99%) showed detectable activity at this elevated protein input, albeit at substantially lower levels than ADT2.2 (Fig. 3e). In microbes, PDT converts prephenate to PPY, which is then transaminated to Phe by phenylpyruvate aminotransferase (PPY-AT)24. However, no dedicated PDTs have been identified in plants, although certain plant ADTs can utilize prephenate as substrate20. We next asked whether maize ADTs can use prephenate as a substrate. High-purity prephenate was purified from commercially available prephenic acid barium salt (Fig. 3f). Conversion of prephenate to PPY was observed for ADT2.2 at a protein input of 30 pmol (25.87 ± 0.70%, Fig. 3g). Upon increasing the protein amount to 120 pmol, the conversion ratio of ADT2.2 increased to 50.70 ± 1.63%, whereas other ADT members still remained catalytically inactive (Fig. 3h). Collectively, these findings suggest that Phe synthesis in maize may proceed via two pathways: the arogenate route and the PPY pathway, with ADT2.2 serving as a key node in both.

ADT2.2 governs plant development

To genetically validate that ADT2.2 is crucial for the Phe synthesis, we generated single mutant lines of maize Adt members in the B104 inbred background using CRISPR/Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and CRISPR-associated protein 9) technology. These mutants (adt1.1, adt1.2, adt2.1, adt2.2, adt6.1, adt6.2, and adt6.3) each had at least two independent alleles (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 5). Phenotypic analysis revealed that, compared to wild-type B104, only the homozygous adt2.2 mutants exhibited a noticeable dwarfing phenotype, whereas the other single mutants showed no substantial defects (Fig. 4a). Accordingly, we measured plant height and dry weight and found that only adt2.2 showed a significant reduction in both metrics (Fig. 4b). To determine if these developmental defects resulted from a deficiency in Phe synthesis, we measured Phe and PPY content using Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry (UPLC-MS). Compared with the wild-type, in adt2.2 leaves, the Phe content decreased to 56.67%, and the PPY content decreased to 42.36%. (Fig. 4c).

Phe is the primary precursor for phenylpropanoids, especially lignin, a major component of the plant cell wall that contributes to plant growth, lodging resistance, and responses to various biotic and abiotic stresses37. Targeted metabolomic analysis of the lignin biosynthetic pathway revealed a pronounced downregulation of its constituent metabolites in adt2.2 mutants (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data 1), suggesting that lignin synthesis was repressed. Both Acetyl Bromide Soluble Lignin (ABSL) quantification and phloroglucinol-HCl staining confirmed that knockout of Adt2.2 directly repressed lignin synthesis, while other single mutants showed no significant differences (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 6). Collectively, these genetic and phenotypic analyses confirm that ADT2.2 is essential for Phe synthesis and, consequently, for maize plant development.

ADT2.2 controls endosperm Phe and storage reserve biosynthesis

As homozygous adt2.2 plants could not self-pollinate, we were unable to obtain homozygous adt2.2 ears (Fig. 4f). Nevertheless, self-pollinated adt2.2/+ ears segregated seeds with a typical filling-deficiency phenotype. Genetic linkage analysis established a direct link between the filling-defective phenotype and homozygous genotype (Supplementary Fig. 7). In contrast, the other single homozygous mutants were fertile and produced ears with no obvious phenotype (Fig. 4f). The adt2.2 kernels were smaller and had less vitreous endosperm (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 7). Scanning electron microscopy analysis evealed that the size of starch granules (SGs) in the mature endosperm was sharply decreased in adt2.2 (Fig. 4g). We then examined endosperm development at 18 and 24 days after pollination (DAP) using semi-sections. In wild-type B104, starchy endosperm cells at 18 DAP already contained numerous SGs. By 24 DAP, the cells were densely packed and completely filled with SGs, particularly in the peripheral endosperm (Fig. 4h). However, in adt2.2 endosperm, SG accumulation was significantly repressed, especially in the central starchy endosperm, where only a few small SGs were observed at 24 DAP (Fig. 4h). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that the morphology of the SGs is also aberrant in adt2.2 endosperm (Supplementary Fig. 8a). As mature endosperm is composed of nearly 60% starch38,39, this impaired granule formation led to a substantial decline in hundred-kernel weight (Fig. 4i).

Notably, although ADT2.2 catalyzes the conversion of prephenate to PPY in vitro, its loss of function resulted in only a mild reduction in PPY content to 70.27% of that in B104 in the 18-DAP endosperm. Strikingly, the Phe content in the adt2.2 endosperm decreased to 4.31% of the B104 level at 18 DAP, representing a much more pronounced reduction than that observed for PPY (Fig. 4j). Given the markedly reduced Phe content in the adt2.2 endosperm, we next examined storage protein accumulation in adt2.2 seeds. Because zeins are the predominant storage proteins, which assemble into protein bodies (PBs) in the endosperm, we examined the PB biogenesis. We found that both the number and size of PBs were markedly decreased in the mutant, with many exhibiting aberrant structures containing large cavities (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Consistent with these morphological defects, SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed a substantial reduction in total zein content (Fig. 4k). Intriguingly, this was accompanied by a compensatory increase in non-zein proteins (Fig. 4k). Together, these results demonstrate that ADT2.2 directly mediates endosperm-localized Phe synthesis and governs storage reserve biosynthesis.

ADT2.2 orchestrates endosperm metabolic homeostasis

Given the substantial reduction in Phe content (Fig. 4j), we investigated potential metabolic disturbances in the adt2.2 endosperm. To this end, we performed a widely-targeted metabolomics analysis on B104 and adt2.2 endosperms at 18 DAP. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the Adt2.2 mutation profoundly affected the maize endosperm metabolome, clearly separating the mutant from the wild-type (Fig. 5a). In total, 423 metabolites exhibited significantly altered abundance in the adt2.2 endosperm compared to B104, with 254 increased and 169 decreased (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Data 2). These altered metabolites were systematically categorized by their biosynthetic pathways. The Adt2.2 mutation altered the levels of numerous metabolites across several key pathways, including secondary metabolism, amino acid and derivative metabolism, nucleotide and derivative metabolism, and carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 5c).

To visualize the functional impact, we used Cytoscape to cluster the most significantly affected metabolites40. Strikingly, the metabolites that showed the most significant increases belonged to the aromatic amino acid, lysine, sulfur-containing amino acid, branched-chain amino acid, and proline/hydroxyproline metabolic pathways (Fig. 5d). We speculate that this accumulation results from feedback regulation in response to Phe starvation in the adt2.2 endosperm. Substantial changes were also observed in secondary metabolism. Although Phe content was reduced to 4.31%, leading us to expect a comprehensive decrease in phenolic acids and flavonoids, the pools of these metabolites exhibited more complex alterations, with both increases and decreases observed (Fig. 5d). This suggests that a more intricate network governs the regulation of Phe-derived secondary metabolites. Collectively, these findings highlight the essential role of ADT2.2 in maintaining metabolic balance in the endosperm.

Phe deficiency repressed endosperm translation

To further correlate endosperm filling with ADT2.2 function, we performed RNA-seq analysis on B104 and adt2.2 endosperm at 18 DAP. We identified 5525 upregulated genes but only 878 downregulated genes (|Fold change| ≥ 1.5, padj < 0.05). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the upregulated genes in adt2.2 endosperm were primarily associated with translation, mRNA processing, tRNA metabolic processes, rRNA processing, ribosome assembly, and the cell cycle (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Data 3).

Strikingly, 185 ribosome-coding genes were significantly upregulated, while only one (RPS23A) was downregulated (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Data 3). A similar transcriptional response has been reported in the bige6 mutant, which encodes a plastid PPA-AT18. Although ribosomes are essential for protein synthesis and cell growth41,42, their encoding genes are often upregulated as a compensatory response when translation is repressed43. We hypothesized that the Phe deficiency in adt2.2 endosperm was causing translational repression. To test this, we performed polysome profiling using 15–45% sucrose gradient centrifugation on 18-DAP endosperm. Compared with wild-type B104, we found that the accumulation of 40S and 60S ribosomal subunits changed only slightly, while the level of 80S monosomes increased dramatically. (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 9). The polysome-to-monosome (P/M) ratio is a quantitative measure of active translation44,45. We found that this ratio was significantly decreased in the adt2.2 endosperm, confirming that global translation was repressed in this tissue (Fig. 6d).

Aminoacylated tRNAPhe-GAA dramatically decreased in adt2.2 endosperm

Transfer RNAs (tRNAs) play an essential role in mRNA decoding. The accurate translation of mRNA into protein requires the correct synthesis of aminoacyl-tRNAs. This reaction, known as tRNA aminoacylation or charging, is catalyzed by Aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (ARSs), which ligate an amino acid to the 3′-terminal cytidine-adenosine motif of tRNAs46–48. In maize, the Phe codons TTT and TTC are decoded by a single tRNA species, tRNAPhe-GAA, as maize lacks a tRNAPhe-AAA (https://gtrnadb.org/). This indicates that the TTT codon is decoded via wobble (G-T) pairing49,50.

The fraction of aminoacylated tRNA directly impacts translation speed and efficiency, with the most abundant tRNA isodecoders typically being highly charged51,52. Given the significant decrease in Phe content in the adt2.2 endosperm, we speculated that the fraction of charged tRNAPhe-GAA would also be reduced (Fig. 4j). To verify this, we first examined the total tRNAPhe‑GAA content by northern blotting and found that levels were comparable between B104 and adt2.2 endosperms (Fig. 6e). Next, we assessed tRNAPhe‑GAA charging levels by using an acidic northern blotting. In wild-type endosperms, tRNAPhe‑GAA was nearly fully charged, whereas in adt2.2 endosperms, tRNAPhe‑GAA charging was markedly reduced (Fig. 6f). Quantitative analysis using the periodate oxidation and β‑elimination method confirmed this defect, showing that charged tRNAPhe‑GAA levels in adt2.2 endosperms were less than one-seventh of those in wild-type endosperms (Fig. 6g and Supplementary Fig. 10). Taken together, these results strongly suggest that a loss of ADT2.2 function leads to a severe decrease in the fraction of aminoacylated tRNAPhe-GAA, thereby repressing translation.

Translation pausing increased in adt2.2 endosperm

To further dissect the translational status in the adt2.2 mutant, we performed Ribo-seq analysis by using the B104 and adt2.2 18-DAP endosperms. Quality control metrics, including ribosome-protected fragments (RPFs) size distribution, 3-nt periodicity, and read coverage, confirmed the high quality of the data and the successful capture of translating ribosomes (Fig. 6h and Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Given that charged tRNAPhe-GAA was significantly reduced in the adt2.2 endosperm, we hypothesized that translation-pausing events would increase (Fig. 6e–g). Our analysis of the Ribo-seq data confirmed this, revealing a global increase in site-specific pausing in the mutant, with a particularly strong signal at the TTT codon (Supplementary Fig. 11c).

To investigate the pausing mechanism in greater detail, we examined ribosome occupancy at the A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl), and E (exit) sites, responsible for accepting incoming tRNA, holding the polypeptide chain, and releasing the deacylated tRNA, respectively53. Given the scarcity of charged tRNAPhe-GAA, we inferred that ribosome occupancy would increase at cognate Phe codons at the A-site. Intriguingly, the increase in ribosome occupancy was most pronounced at the A-site, with a nearly fourfold increase at the TTT codon compared to an approximately onefold increase at the TTC codon (Fig. 6i). By contrast, only minimal differences were observed between the P- and E-sites (Supplementary Fig. 11d, e). Meanwhile, we calculated the translation pausing scores of the TTT and TTC codons in the B104 18-DAP endosperm and observed only a minor difference in ribosome pausing scores between the two codons (Supplementary Fig. 12). As the TTT codon was decoded via wobble pairing, we speculated that the amino acid starvation disproportionately reduces the decoding efficiency of wobble-paired codons compared to their Watson-Crick-paired counterparts.

We next defined a gene set exhibiting global TTT A-site-specific translational perturbation. Our analysis identified 12,321 genes in adt2.2 18-DAP endosperm with TTT A-site-specific translation pausing, a number that far exceeded the 1074 genes showing translational acceleration. This highlights the pronounced TTT A-site-specific translational pausing in adt2.2 endosperm (Supplementary Data 4). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these pausing-associated genes were significantly enriched in RNA splicing (GO:0008380, 7.14 × 10−35), ribosome biogenesis (GO:0042254, 1.05 × 10−32), mRNA binding (GO:0003729, 7.99 × 10−29), rRNA processing (GO:0006364, 5.16 × 10−25), endosome (GO:0005768, 9.15 × 10−22), spliceosomal complex, (GO:0005681, 5.95 × 10−20) and other biological process (Fig. 6j). Collectively, these data show that Phe starvation in adt2.2 endosperm directly impairs ribosome decoding at Phe codons, with a pronounced effect on the TTT codon at the ribosome A-site. This codon-specific translational stalling, in turn, disrupts the extensive biological processes of the endosperm.

Feedback regulation of Phe deficiency in adt2.2 endosperm

The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a master regulator of protein translation that couples nutrient signaling to cell growth. When amino acids are abundant, the mTORC1 complex is activated and phosphorylates ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K) at threonine 449 (T449). This, in turn, leads to the phosphorylation of S6K, activating downstream signaling54,55. As expected, phosphorylation of S6K-T449 was significantly decreased in the adt2.2 endosperm; this result suggested that the adt2.2 endosperm is undergoing extreme Phe starvation (Fig. 6k). Conversely, amino acid deficiency, an increased fraction of uncharged tRNAs, and ribosome collisions activate the nutrient-sensing kinase general control nonderepressible 2 (GCN2)56,57. Activated GCN2 phosphorylates the α subunit of eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2α) at serine 51 (S51). This event triggers a transcriptional program to manage metabolic stress, including the upregulation of genes involved in amino acid metabolism, redox maintenance, and autophagy58,59. We found that while total eIF2α levels were comparable, phosphorylated eIF2α-S51 was almost undetectable in wild-type B104 endosperm but highly accumulated in the adt2.2 endosperm (Fig. 6k). This indicated that the GCN2 pathway was activated in the mutant to alleviate the stress of Phe deficiency.

Our widely targeted metabolomics had previously revealed that amino acids and their derivatives were significantly elevated in the adt2.2 endosperm (Fig. 5d). To confirm this, we measured the absolute content of 19 different amino acids. Intriguingly, only Phe and L-lysine (Lys) were decreased in the adt2.2 18-DAP endosperm, while all other amino acids were significantly increased (Fig. 6l). A total of 13 amino acids showed more than a onefold increase, with L-glutamine (Gln) and L-leucine (Leu) increasing by over sevenfold. A similar result was obtained using 24-DAP endosperm (Supplementary Fig. 13). Furthermore, we found that many transcripts for ARS coding genes were significantly upregulated in the adt2.2 endosperm, including the Phenylalanyl-tRNA synthetase (FARS) (Fig. 6m and Supplementary Data 3). Together, these results imply that multiple, interconnected pathways are activated in the adt2.2 endosperm to manage translational and metabolic stress.

Discussion

AAAs are essential for protein biosynthesis and serve as precursors for numerous primary and secondary metabolites. Phe, in particular, is a precursor to over 8000 phenolic compounds. It provides the carbon skeleton for the phenylpropanoid pathway, yielding diverse chemicals critical for plant growth, development, reproduction, and defense3,60. The most abundant Phe-derived compound is lignin, which can constitute up to 30% of plant dry weight and is vital for strengthening and waterproofing secondary cell walls61,62. The AAA pathways have mosaic origins, assembled from enzymes derived from both eukaryotic and diverse bacterial ancestors63,64. While prokaryotes predominantly use AAAs for protein biosynthesis, eukaryotes must precisely regulate their flux to meet the competing demands of protein synthesis and secondary metabolite production23. In plants, Phe is synthesized from the shikimate pathway product prephenate via two routes: the plastid arogenate pathway and the cytosolic PPY pathway12,13,15,18,19,62. Key regulatory enzymes in the AAA pathways, especially ADTs, exhibit high isoform diversification. Most green algae possess a single Adt gene, but gene expansion and duplication events in flowering plants have resulted in multiple Adts with differentiated functions23,62.

While ADT-GFP fusion proteins have been widely reported to form punctate structures, their precise suborganellar location has remained unknown16. The two most studied punctate structures in chloroplasts are nucleoids65,66 and PGs32. Our previous research reported that the nucleoid-localized PEN1 mediates the removal of RNA primers during chloroplast DNA replication67. As the ADT-GFP puncta were significantly larger than nucleoids, we investigated whether ADTs are components of PGs. Our data revealed that ADTs are localized to PGs via their cTPs (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 3). This microcompartmentation is a highly evolved, dynamic strategy to enhance specific biological processes30,68,69. Our research is the first to demonstrate that PGs are involved in Phe synthesis, thereby expanding their known biological functions.

All maize ADTs contain typical PDT and ACT domains and exhibit high protein sequence similarity (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). Notably, ADT2.2 shares 85% sequence similarity with ADT2.1, yet we detected no catalytic activity from ADT2.1 under the same conditions (Figs. 1a and 3d, e). It is plausible that mutations in amino acid residues essential for catalysis occurred during evolution, rendering the other ADT members (ADT1.1, ADT1.2, ADT2.1) catalytically inactive. Analysis of single mutants of maize Adt members revealed that adt2.2 exhibited plant and endosperm deficiency phenotypes compared to the B104 wild-type (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. 6 and 8). The Phe content in adt2.2 leaves decreased to 56.67%, leading to a partial repression of lignin synthesis in the stem, suggesting the presence of alternative pathways in leaves (Fig. 4c–e and Supplementary Fig. 6). Although ADT6.1, ADT6.2, and ADT6.3 exhibit partial catalytic activity at high concentrations, the homozygous adt6.1, adt6.2 and adt6.3 plants display no obvious phenotypes, and the Phe content without significantly changes in their leaves (Figs. 3e and 4a–e). These results suggest that the contributions of ADT6.1, ADT6.2, and ADT6.3 to in vivo Phe synthesis are minor. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that they may function under specific physiological contexts or in particular cell types, which warrants further investigation.

In Arabidopsis, the cytosolic aminotransferase VAS1 (REVERSAL OF SAV3 PHENOTYPE 1) can use phenylpyruvic acid, 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvic acid, and 3-indolepropionic acid as substrates to synthesize Phe in the cytoplasm13. Whether alternative pathways for Phe synthesis exist in maize leaves warrants further investigation. The whole cytosolic PPY pathway has been well demonstrated in Petunia hybrida. The cytosolic Chorismate mutase 2 catalyzes the conversion of chorismate to prephenate, and the plastid phADT3 transcribed from an alternative TSS, yields a cytosolic PhADT3 isoform that mediates the conversion of prephenate to PPY24. Finally, the cytosolic PhPPY-AT converts PPY to Phe70. Downregulation of either PhPPY-AT or cytosolic PhADT3 leads to reduced Phe levels and a corresponding decrease in Phe-derived volatiles24,70. A recent study revealed that the plastid Arabidopsis AspAT3 can directly convert PPY to Phe by using Met (Methionine) or Glu (Glutamate) as an amino donor, respectively71. Maize plastid ADT2.2 can directly mediate the conversion of prephenate to PPY in PGs, and the mutation in Adt2.2 directly resulted in PPY content decreased to 42.36% of the B104 in the leaves (Fig. 4c). These observations raised the possibility that the PPY pathway also functions in plastids.

Intriguingly, the PPY content in adt2.2 endosperm decreased to 70.27% of that in B104, whereas the Phe content dropped to 4.31% of the B104 level, suggesting that the arogenate pathway is primarily responsible for Phe synthesis in maize endosperm (Fig. 4j). Maize BIGE6 acts upstream of ADT2.2 in the metabolic pathway. Loss of its function severely impairs endosperm development, further supporting our conclusion18. This severe, tissue-specific phenotype of adt2.2 is reminiscent of the defective kernel 20 (dek20) mutant, which encodes the rate-limiting enzyme in serine biosynthesis. The dek20 endosperm, segregated from heterozygous ears, has a significantly reduced serine content, leading to embryo lethality72. These findings underscore the crucial role of local amino acid synthesis in maize endosperm development.

Our findings reveal that loss of Adt2.2 function directly represses translation, as evidenced by a reduced P/M ratio (Fig. 6c, d). Canonically, wobble pairing allows a single tRNA to recognize multiple codons, balancing fidelity with the genetic code’s degeneracy. The slower elongation speeds associated with wobble codons have been documented73,74. Here, we reported that under Phe starvation, the decoding efficiency of wobble-paired codons is more severely repressed than that of Watson-Crick-paired codons, leading to increased ribosome pausing in the maize (Fig. 6h–j and Supplementary Fig. 11). We speculated that under normal charged tRNAPhe-GAA abundance, the intrinsic kinetic disadvantage of wobble pairing may be masked (Supplementary Fig. 12). However, under the extreme Phe limitation in the adt2.2 endosperm (Phe reduced to 4.31% of wild-type), this intrinsic difference becomes dramatically amplified (Fig. 6i). These findings uncover an intriguing mechanism of translation regulation under nutrient stress and provide a perspective on codon‑specific translational sensitivity.

Collectively, our work provides biochemical and genetic evidence that PG-localized ADT2.2 mediated the Phe synthesis (Fig. 7). Functional ADT2.2 is essential for maintaining metabolic balance and translational homeostasis. This research provides an in-depth understanding of the pathway and regulation of Phe synthesis in plants and identifies potential targets for bioengineering to manipulate crop traits.

A working model showing ADT2.2 synthesis of Phe in PGs, which is essential for plant development and endosperm development.

ADT2.2 catalyses the conversion of arogenate to Phe in PGs, and also converts prephenate to PPY, thereby providing an alternative route for Phe synthesis. Genetic disruption of ADT2.2 elicits tissue-specific effects. In leaves, loss of ADT2.2 function reduces Phe levels by 43.33% relative to wild-type, impairing lignin biosynthesis. In contrast, in the adt2.2 endosperm, Phe levels drop to only 4.31% of wild-type levels, leading to metabolic disruption and widespread accumulation of translational pausing events.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The maize adt CRISPR-Cas9-targeted mutants were created in the B104 inbred line via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Two sgRNAs were synthesized for each gene (http://www.e-crisp.org/E-CRISP/designcrispr.html). The sgRNA was inserted into pCPB-ZmUbi-hspCas9 (the promoter of ZmUbi was used to drive human-codon optimized Cas9 (hspCas9); the RNA polymerase III promoter expressed sgRNA, including 20 bp target and 74 bp sgRNA scaffold). The CRISPR/Cas9 construct was removed by segregation during backcrossing. After backcrossing, homozygous mutant ears were generated by self-pollination for two generations. After each generation of backcrossing and self-pollination, PCR and sequencing were used to select progeny that contained the desired mutations. Since the homozygous adt2.2 cannot be self-pollinated, it can only be isolated from the self-pollinated heterozygous ears. All primers used are listed in Supplementary Data 5.

To create the Ubi: Adt2.2-Flag construct, the Adt2.2 CDS was amplified and inserted into WM0001. The Ubi: Adt2.2-Flag was transformed into the B104 inbred line via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation by Wimi Biotechnology (Jiangsu).

All maize genetic materials were grown in the Song Jiang experimental field (30.5°N, 121.1°E) in Shanghai, China, and a field in Sanya (18.2°N, 109.3°E), China. Wild-type N. benthamiana was grown in a growth chamber with soil at 22 °C and 70% humidity under 16-h light and 8-h dark photoperiod using Philips TLD 36 W/865 and 36 W/830 bulbs (90 μmol/m2/s).

Phylogenetic analysis

Maize and Arabidopsis ADT protein sequences were downloaded from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using Clustal X software with default parameters. The phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA X using the neighbor-joining method, with Poisson correction, pairwise deletion, and bootstrap (1000 replicates).

Immunoblotting analysis

The primary antibodies Anti-ADT2.2 (the 16–29 aa was used for immunizing, ABclonal, Wuhan), Anti-eIF2α (Agrisera, AS204371), Anti-eIF2α (S51, Abclonal, AP0692), Anti-S6 K (Agrisera, AS121855), Anti-S6 K (T449, Abcam, ab207399), and Anti-Actin (Abmart, M20009L) were all used at a 1:1000 dilution. The secondary antibodies Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG-HRP (Abmart, M21002L) and Goat Anti-Mouse IgG-HRP (Abmart, M21001L) were all used at a 1:5000 dilution.

Endosperm tissue harvested at 18 DAP was ground to a fine powder. An aliquot of 0.1 g powder was suspended in 400 μL extraction buffer containing 5% SDS, 5% β-mercaptoethanol, and 125 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), extracted for 2 h, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min to obtain the supernatant containing soluble proteins. The protein was separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to the PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad, 162-0177) for immunoblotting analysis. The signal was detected using the Omni-ECL™ Enhanced Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (Epizyme, SQ101) and visualized by the Tanon Chemi Dog Ultra Imaging System (Tanon Science and Technology, China).

Y2H assay

To analyze the interaction between ADT members, the CDSs of the Adt genes were amplified and cloned into the pGADT7 and pGBKT7 plasmids, respectively. The bait and prey constructs were co-transformed into the yeast Y2HGold according to the Yeastmaker Yeast Transformation System 2 (Clontech). The yeast strains were grown on selection medium lacking SD/−Trp/−Leu (DDO) and SD/−Trp/−Leu/−His/−Ade (QDO), respectively.

IP for LC-MS/MS assays

5 g samples of ADT2-FLAG transgenic and B104 18-DAP endosperm were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen samples were ground into powder and lysed in 10 mL of extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% v/v glycerol, 0.1% v/v NP40, 5 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). The homogenate was incubated on a rotary mixer for 60 min (4 °C, 50 rpm) and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min again. Then, the supernatant was mixed with FLAG agarose beads (Smart-Lifesciences, SA042005) and incubated on a rotary mixer for 120 min (4 °C, 50 rpm). After incubation, the beads were washed 6 times with washing buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 1% v/v glycerol, 0.1% v/v NP40, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail). The resulting beads were eluted with 200 μg/mL Flag in Buffer B (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1% v/v glycerol) at 4 °C for 2 h.

Protein samples (two experimental groups and one control group) were digested using a modified FASP protocol. Briefly, 200 µL of UA buffer (8 M urea in 50 mM ABC) was added directly into each sample tube to bring the total volume to 220 µL. The entire mixture was transferred onto a 10 kDa ultrafiltration centrifugal filter unit (Sartorius) and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min to remove the SDS. For reduction and alkylation, 100 µL of UA buffer containing 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) and 50 mM chloroacetamide (CAA) was added to the filter unit, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min with shaking at 650 rpm.

Thereafter, the filters were washed twice with 300 µL of UA buffer (14,000 × g, 30 min each), followed by buffer exchange via two consecutive washes with 300 µL of 50 mM ABC buffer (14,000 × g, 20 min each). Protein digestion was initiated by adding 1 µg of trypsin in 100 µL of 50 mM ABC buffer onto the filter, with subsequent incubation at 37 °C for 16 h under constant shaking at 650 rpm.

Post-digestion, the resulting peptides were collected by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min. To maximize peptide recovery, the filter was washed twice with 100 µL of 0.1% formic acid (FA), with each elution step followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min. The combined eluate was then strongly acidified by the addition of 5 µL of 20% trifluoroacetic acid to ensure the final pH was below. Finally, the acidified peptides were desalted using C18 cartridges (Thermo Scientific) and quantified via a Pierce Quantitative Colorimetric Peptide Assay (Thermo Scientific) before downstream LC-MS/MS analysis.

Experiments were performed on a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer that was coupled to Easy nLC (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were reconstituted in 0.1% Formic acid in HPLC-grade water (0.1% FA). The peptide mixture was loaded onto the C18-reversed phase column (25 cm long, 75 μm inner diameter) packed in-house with RP-C18 1.9 μm resin in buffer A (0.1% Formic acid in HPLC-grade water) and separated with a 60 min linear gradient of buffer B (0.1% Formic acid in 80% acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 600 nL/min. MS data was acquired using a data-dependent top20 method dynamically choosing the most abundant precursor ions from the survey scan (375–1500 m/z) for HCD fragmentation. Determination of the target value is based on predictive Automatic Gain Control. Dynamic exclusion duration was 45 s. Survey scans were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 with AGC target value of 3 × 106 ions or a maximum integration time of 50 ms, and resolution for HCD spectra was set to 15,000 with AGC target value of 1 × 105 ions or a maximum integration time of 50 ms. Normalized collision energy was 28 eV. The fixed first m/z was 200, and the isolation window was 1.6 m/z.

The MS data were analyzed using Thermo Proteome Discoverer (2.5.0.400) software. MS data were searched against the Maize_UP000007305_4577 database. An initial search was set at a precursor mass window of 10 ppm. The search followed an enzymatic cleavage rule of Trypsin and allowed maximal two missed cleavage sites and a mass tolerance of 10 ppm for precursor and 0.02 Da for fragment ions. Carbamidomethylation of cysteines was defined as a fixed modification, while protein N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation were defined as variable modifications for database searching. The cutoff of global false discovery rate for peptide and protein identification was set to 0.01.

Arogenate synthesis and purification

The arogenate was synthesized according to the previously reported literature36. First, the methyl ester was converted to the corresponding methyl amide, which was then condensed with an aldehyde. Under the condition of triphosgene, the amide attacked the imine and underwent further acylation to produce an aminoacyl chloride intermediate. Subsequent hydrogenation over Pd/C revealed the phenolic group, which, under basic conditions, underwent a dearomatization-cyclization sequence to construct the all-carbon quaternary center required for the target. Finally, a Luche reduction followed by amide hydrolysis delivered the target compound. The arogenate was purified by a Hypercarb™ column (Thermo Scientific, 35005-254630) with a 10 mM Na2CO3/NaHCO3 (pH 9.6) mobile phase. The fraction eluting between 3.2 and 3.4 min was collected for lyophilization. HRMS-ESI (Electrospray Ionization High-resolution Mass Spectrometry): calcd. for C10H13O5NNa [M+Na]+ 250.0686, found 250.0691.

The reaction mixtures (40 µL) contained 250 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 8.0) and 1 mM arogenate. After preincubation at 37 °C for 5 min, 30 or 120 pmol of the respective ADT recombinant protein was added. The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. After centrifugation, 10 µL of the mixture was analyzed by HPLC using a Hypercarb™ column. The mobile phase consists of 10 mM Na2CO3/NaHCO3 (pH 9.6, solvent A) and acetonitrile (solvent B). Metabolomic analysis

The 18-DAP B104 and adt2.2 endosperm were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The endosperm was then ground into fine powder. Weigh 30 mg of sample powder using an electronic balance and add 1500 μL of −20 °C pre-cooled 70% methanolic aqueous internal standard extract. Vortex once every 30 min for 30 s, for a total of 6 times. After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 5 min), the supernatant was aspirated, and the sample was filtered through a microporous membrane (0.22 μm) and stored in the injection vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

The sample extracts were analyzed using a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC™ AD, https://sciex.com.cn/) and a tandem mass spectrometry system (https://sciex.com.cn/). The analytical conditions were as follows: UPLC: column, Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm); The mobile phase consisted of solvent A, pure water with 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B, acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Sample measurements were performed with a gradient program that employed the starting conditions of 95% A, 5% B. Within 9 min, a linear gradient to 5% A, 95% B was programmed, and a composition of 5% A, 95% B was kept for 1 min. Subsequently, a composition of 95% A, 5.0% B was adjusted within 1.1 min and kept for 2.9 min. The flow velocity was set as 0.35 mL per minute; The column oven was set to 40 °C; The injection volume was 2 μL. The effluent was alternatively connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS.

LIT and triple quadrupole (QQQ) scans were acquired on a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer (Q TRAP), API 6500 Q TRAP UPLC/MS/MS System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, operating in positive and negative ion mode and controlled by Analyst 1.6.3 software (ABSciex). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: ion source, turbo spray; source temperature 550 °C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (positive ion mode)/−4500 V (negative ion mode); ion source gas I (GSI), gas II (GSII), CUR were set at 50, 60, and 30.0 psi, respectively; the collision gas(CAD) was high. Instrument tuning and mass calibration were performed with 10 and 100 μmol/L polypropylene glycol solutions in QQQ and LIT modes, respectively. QQQ scans were acquired as MRM experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to 5 psi. DP and CE for individual MRM transitions were done with further DP and CE optimization. A specific set of MRM transitions was monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period.

Based on the in-house database Metware Database, qualitative analysis was performed using the MS1 and MS2 spectra obtained from mass spectrometry detection. For the identification of certain substances, isotopic signals, redundant signals from K⁺, Na⁺, and NH₄⁺ adduct ions, as well as redundant signals originating from fragment ions of other larger-molecular-weight compounds, were removed during analysis. The raw data files generated after mass spectrometry analysis were opened and reviewed using Analyst 1.6.3 software, and subsequently used for qualitative and quantitative analysis. Quantification was carried out using the MRM mode of a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (as shown in the figure below). In MRM mode, the first quadrupole selects the precursor ion (parent ion) of the target compound, excluding ions corresponding to other molecular weights to preliminarily eliminate interferences. The precursor ions are then induced to dissociate in the collision cell, generating numerous fragment ions. The third quadrupole filters and selects one specific characteristic fragment ion, thereby excluding non-target ion interferences, which results in more accurate quantification and better reproducibility. After obtaining the metabolomic mass spectrometry data from different samples, peak area integration was performed for all metabolite peaks, and the chromatographic peaks of the same metabolite across different samples were subjected to integration correction.

PCA was performed using the Metware Cloud, a free online data analysis platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). The hierarchical cluster analysis results of samples and metabolites were presented as heatmaps with dendrograms. For the two-group analysis, differential metabolites were identified using VIP (VIP > 1) and absolute Log2FC (|Log2FC| ≥ 1.0). VIP values were extracted from the OPLS-DA (Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis) result, which also included score and permutation plots, generated using the R package MetaboAnalystR. The data were log-transformed (Log2) and mean-centered before OPLS-DA. To avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed. Identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG Compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), and the annotated metabolites were then mapped to the KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). The annotated differential metabolites were displayed by Cytoscape.

Polysome profiling

The 18-DAP B104 and adt2.2 were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The endosperm was then ground into fine powder with a mortar. An amount of 100 mg powder was hydrated in 300 μL lysis buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.0, 200 mM KCl, 25 mM EGTA, 35 mM MgCl2, 1% Brij-35, 1% Triton ×-100, 1% Tween 20, 1% Igepal CA-630, 1% deoxycholic acid, 1% polyoxyethylene-10-tridecyl ether, 1 mM PMSF, 0.5 mg/mL heparin, 5 mM DTT, 50 μg/mL cycloheximide, 50 μg/mL chloramphenicol, and 40 U mL−1 RNase inhibitor. After incubation in the ice bath for 1 h, the mixtures were centrifuged for 10 min at 13,000 × g. Then, 100 μL of supernatants were loaded onto a 10–45% sucrose gradient. After ultracentrifugation at 222,200 × g for 3 h at 4 °C (Beckman Optima XE-100), the A260 absorbance was recorded using a piston gradient fractionator (Biocomp, Canada) equipped with a Bio-Rad Econo UV monitor. All experiments were repeated three times and yielded similar results.

tRNA northern blotting

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol/chloroform, followed by precipitation with absolute ethanol. The RNA pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of RNase-free water. The extracted RNA samples were quantified using Nanodrop and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Northern blot analysis was performed to examine tRNA levels. Briefly, 2–5 μg of total RNA was separated on an 8% urea-PAGE gel and transferred onto a Hybond-N+ positively charged nylon membrane (GE Healthcare, RPN303B) using a semi-dry transfer system in 0.5 × TBE buffer at 300 mA for 30 min. The membrane was crosslinked with a UV crosslinker (UVP Laboratory Products), followed by hybridization with biotin-labeled probes specific for target tRNAs. Signals were detected using the Chemiluminescent Nucleic Acid Detection Module kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 89880). 5S rRNA was used as a loading control and detected with a specific probe.

For tRNA charging assays, RNA samples were subjected to acid or alkaline treatment. Acid treatment was performed by adding 50 μL of 10 mM NaOAc (pH 5.0) and incubating at 37 °C for 5 min before storage at 4 °C. Alkaline treatment involved adding 50 μL of 0.2 M Tris-HCl (pH 9.5), incubating at 37 °C for 3 h, followed by NaOAc precipitation and resuspension in 50 μL of 10 mM NaOAc (pH 5.0). Subsequently, 2 μg of total RNA was separated on a 12% acid-urea PAGE gel, and membrane transfer, hybridization, and chemiluminescent detection were performed as described for northern blot. The tRNA northern blotting was performed by XingYi Bio Co., Ltd (Wuhan).

tRNA aminoacylation assay by qPCR

The tRNA aminoacylation assay was performed based on previous research, but with some modifications52. The 18-DAP B104 and adt2.2 were collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The endosperm was then ground into fine powder and lysed with 1 mL TRIzol (Life Technologies, 15596018) on ice for 10 min. Lysates were shaken with chloroform 5:1, centrifuged at 18,600 × g, and precipitated with 2.7 × volumes of cold ethanol in the presence of 30 μg of GlycoBlue Coprecipitant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM9515) overnight. Spin down precipitated samples for 30 min at 18,600 × g at 4 °C. Remove the supernatant and resuspend the pellet in 0.3 M acetate buffer (pH 4.5) containing 10 mM EDTA (Invitrogen, AM9260G). Then, precipitate the sample with 2.7 times the volume of cold ethanol overnight. The next day, samples were resuspended in 10 mM acetate buffer with 1 mM EDTA. Of each RNA sample, 2 μg RNA was treated with 10 mM of either sodium periodate (“oxidized sample”, Sigma-Aldrich, 311448) or sodium chloride (“non-oxidized sample”) and incubated for 20 min at room temperature in the dark. Reactions were quenched with glucose for 15 min.

A synthesized Escherichia coli tRNAPro (70 pg) was added to each sample as a spike-in control, which was then precipitated with ethanol. Samples were resuspended in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 9, Sigma-Aldrich, T2819-100ML), incubated for 50 min at 37 °C, quenched with acetate buffer, and precipitated. Finally, samples were resuspended in RNase-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A57775) and ligated to a 5′-adenylated DNA adaptor (5′-/5rApp/TGGAATTCTCGGGTGCCAAGG/3ddC/-3′, synthesized in Sangon Biotech Shanghai Co., Ltd.) using truncated KQ mutant T4 RNA ligase 2 (New England Biolabs, M0373) for 3 h at room temperature. Reverse transcription was performed with SuperScript™ IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, 18090050), with a primer complementary to the DNA adaptor.

cDNA samples were subjected to quantitative PCR with tRNA isodecoder-specific primers designed so that the forward (FW) primer was complementary to the 5′ end of the tRNA, and the reverse (RV) primer spanned the junction between the 3′ end of the tRNA and the ligated adaptor. The charged fraction value was calculated from a relative difference between a delta-Ct value from non-oxidized (representing total) and oxidized (representing charged) samples for each primer pair. Ct values obtained with primers specific for Escherichia coli tRNAPro were subtracted from those obtained with primers specific for an iso-decoder of interest. The charged fraction value was calculated from a relative difference between a delta-Ct value from a non-oxidized (representing total) and oxidized (representing charged) sample for each primer pair.

Ribo-seq

Tissues were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground to a powder in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle, then dissolved in 400 μL of lysis buffer. Ribo-seq was performed as reported previously76. The resuspended extracts in lysis buffer were transferred to new microtubes, pipetted several times, and incubated on ice for 10 min. The lysate was then triturated 10 times through a 26-G needle. The lysate was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. To capture RPFs, 10 μL of RNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EN0601) and 6 μL of DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EN0521) were added to 400 μL of lysate, and the mixture was incubated for 45 min at room temperature with gentle mixing using a Nutator mixer. Nuclease digestion was stopped by adding 10 μL of SUPERase·In RNase inhibitor (Invitrogen, AM2694). Size-exclusion columns (MicroSpin S-400 HR, Cytia, 27514001) were equilibrated with 3 mL of lysis buffer by gravity flow, followed by centrifugation at 600 × g for 4 min at room temperature. 100 μL of digested RPFs was added to the column, which was then centrifuged at 600 g for 2 min. Next, 10 μL of 10% (w/v) SDS was added to the elution, and RPFs greater than 17 nt were isolated using the RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research, R1017). rRNA was removed using the method previously reported77. Briefly, short (50–80 bases) antisense DNA probes complementary to rRNA sequences were added to a solution containing RPFs; RNase H (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 18021014) and DNase I were then added to digest rRNA and residual DNA probes. Finally, RPFs were further purified using magnetic beads (Vazyme, N412-01). After obtaining ribosome footprints above, Ribo-seq libraries were constructed using NEBNext® Multiple Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina® (New England Biolabs, E7300L).

Low-quality reads were filtered by fastp78. The short-read alignment tool Bowtie2 was used to map reads to the ribosomal RNA (rRNA) database, GenBank, and the Rfam database79. Reads mapping to rRNAs, tRNAs, small nuclear RNAs, small nucleolar RNAs, and miRNAs were removed. Processed Ribo-seq reads were aligned to the genome using STAR with the 2-pass setting enabled80. Reads mapping to classical noncoding RNAs (including long noncoding RNAs and microRNA precursors) were discarded. The remaining RPFs were assigned to different genomic features (5′UTR, CDS, 3′UTR, and intron) based on the position of the 5’ end of the alignment. Three-nucleotide periodicity was plotted using the riboWaltz R package as described in ref. 81.

Because ribosomes translocate along the mRNA one codon at a time, the potential for single-codon resolution pauses was investigated. For codon occupancy analysis, A-site codons were inferred from 28 to 32 nt reads aligned in the zero frame to the CDS, using an offset of 15 nt from the 5’ end. Reads mapping within the first 15 codons were excluded to avoid biases from sample preparation. The occupancy of each A-site codon was then normalized to its basal occupancy, calculated as the average occupancy of the three downstream codons at the +1, +2, and +3 positions82. The means of total A-site occupancy of TTT codons were calculated for every canonical transcript. A gene was defined as having significant TTT A-site-specific translation pausing if the ratio of its total TTT A-site codon occupancy in adt2.2 over that in B104 was ≥ 2. In contrast, a gene was defined as having significant TTT A-site-specific translation acceleration if the ratio of its total TTT A-site codon occupancy in adt2.2 over that in B104 was ≤ 2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism v.10.2.0 was used for the statistical analyses. One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test was applied for comparisons involving more than two groups, while two-sided Student’s t-tests were used for pairwise comparisons.
