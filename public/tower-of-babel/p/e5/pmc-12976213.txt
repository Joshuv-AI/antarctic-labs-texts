Proteomic profiling of amylases secreted by Penicillium crustosum UEM-45 and characterization of two major enzymes
Authors: Ketty Carolina Araujo Pacheco, Ione Parra Barbosa-Tessmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the industrial relevance of fungal amylases, those from Penicillium crustosum remain poorly characterized. This study aimed to use a proteomics-based approach to identify and characterize extracellular amylases from P. crustosum UEM-45. The extracellular proteome of P. crustosum UEM-45 was analyzed by LC–MS/MS, resulting in the identification of seven amylases among 1,641 extracellular proteins. An α-amylase (CAZy GH13_1) and a glucoamylase (CAZy GH15) were partially purified and characterized. Both enzymes exhibited molecular masses of approximately 67 kDa, were predicted to be monomeric, and to contain a CBM20 module in addition to their catalytic domains. The α-amylase exhibited optimal activity at pH 6.5 and 40 °C, whereas the glucoamylase showed optimal activity at 45 °C. The melting temperatures (Tm) of the α-amylase and glucoamylase were 50.16 °C and 47.8 °C, respectively. Both enzymes were stable at 4 °C. The KM values for starch were 0.98 mg/mL for the α-amylase and 4.51 mg/mL for the glucoamylase, whereas the Vmax was 0.12 mg/min of starch consumed by the α-amylase and 0.3 µmol/min of glucose released by the glucoamylase. Regulatory and substrate specificity assays revealed distinct profiles. When combined, the enzymes efficiently converted starch into glucose and degraded raw starch. Overall, this study demonstrates that proteomics is effective for discovering novel amylolytic enzymes in P. crustosum. Notably, the characterization of a glucoamylase from this fungus contributes to the limited body of knowledge on this enzyme in Penicillium. Introduction

Currently, enzyme production is a rapidly growing area, and the industrial enzyme market is projected to reach USD 8.7 billion by 2026 (Mesbah 2022). Hydrolases account for approximately 75% of the total enzyme market, with amylases representing about 25‒30% due to their extensive application in baking, brewing, textile, and paper industries (Pandey et al. 2000; van der Maarel et al. 2002; Gupta et al. 2003; Läufer 2017; Chapman et al. 2018; Paul et al. 2021; Mesbah 2022).

Amylases catalyze the hydrolysis of starch and are classified as endo- or exo-acting enzymes according to their mechanism of action (Pandey et al. 2000; van der Maarel et al. 2002; Gupta et al. 2003; Singh et al. 2016). In industrial starch processing, gelatinized starch is liquefied by α-amylases, which hydrolyze internal α(1 → 4) glycosidic bonds, producing dextrins that are subsequently converted into glucose through saccharification by glucoamylases, which act from non-reducing ends (Pandey et al. 2000; Norouzian et al. 2006; Kumar and Satyanarayana 2009; Läufer 2017; Karim and Tasnim 2018). Enzymatic hydrolysis is preferred over acid hydrolysis due to lower waste generation and higher economic efficiency (Singh et al. 2016; Läufer 2017).

Most industrial amylases are of microbial origin, as these enzymes show high substrate specificity, greater stability, and can be efficiently produced by fermentation processes (Souza and Magalhães 2010; Gurung et al. 2013; Singh et al. 2016). Filamentous fungi are particularly attractive producers because of their high protein secretion capacity and ability to grow on diverse substrates (Souza and Magalhães 2010; Gurung et al. 2013; Singh et al. 2016). Amylase-producing microorganisms are widely distributed in nature, with fungi of the genera Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Rhizopus, and bacteria of the genus Bacillus, being the main sources (Gupta et al. 2003; Norouzian et al. 2006; Kumar and Satyanarayana 2009; Marín-Navarro and Polaina 2011; Gurung et al. 2013; Mehta and Satyanarayana 2016; Karim and Tasnim 2018; Paul et al. 2021). Despite the extensive literature on amylases from Aspergillus species, amylase production by fungi of the genus Penicillium remains poorly understood. Reports are limited to a few species, with α-amylase being the main enzyme described (Doyle et al. 1989; Afifi et al. 2008; Metin et al. 2010; Dar et al. 2015). In addition, comprehensive analyses of the extracellular proteome of filamentous fungi, particularly those addressing the diversity and relative abundance of secreted amylolytic enzymes, remain scarce.

Penicillium crustosum is a blue-green or blue-grey fungus associated with food spoilage in protein-rich products such as meat and cheese and is known for producing neurotoxic mycotoxins, including penitrems and thomitrems (Rundberget et al. 2004; Moldes-Anaya et al. 2012). This species also produces antibacterial compounds such as viridicatol (Wang et al. 2025) and several biotechnologically relevant enzymes, including lipases, tannases, pectinases, and cellulases (Xiong et al. 2017; Hasnaoui et al. 2022; Núñez-Serrano et al. 2024; Thakur et al. 2024). However, information regarding its amylolytic enzyme repertoire and secretion profile remains limited.

The exploration of microbial diversity to identify novel enzymes with improved catalytic efficiency or distinctive biochemical properties is of considerable scientific and industrial interest. An isolate of P. crustosum previously obtained in our laboratory was identified as an amylase producer (Souza and Barbosa-Tessmann 2024). Therefore, the aim of this study was to perform a proteomic profiling of the amylases secreted by P. crustosum UEM-45 using LC–MS/MS analysis. In addition, the two most abundant amylases identified in the extracellular proteome were partially purified and biochemically characterized.

Materials and methods

Microorganism

The P. crustosum UEM-45 isolate was originally obtained as an airborne contaminant on a Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) Petri dish in 1992 (Souza and Barbosa-Tessmann 2024). Its spores were lyophilized at that time and subsequently reactivated in 2020. The fungus has been preserved on PDA slants, including under mineral oil. Species identification was confirmed by DNA barcoding (Souza and Barbosa-Tessmann 2024).

Morphologic characterization and production of amylolytic activity in solid medium

Macro- and micromorphological characteristics of the P. crustosum UEM-45 isolate were examined in four-day-old colonies grown on Czapek-Dox Agar and PDA in 10-cm Petri dishes at 25 °C with a 12-h light/dark photoperiod. Amylase activity was assessed by culturing P. crustosum UEM-45 for three days on starch-containing nutrient agar (Mendonça et al. 2023). Plates were then flooded with iodine solution [2% KI; 0.2% I₂] and rinsed with distilled water to visualize starch hydrolysis halos.

Enzyme production over time

Submerged culture was carried out in a liquid medium containing 0.2% soluble starch (Lago et al. 2021). Cultures were prepared with 25 mL of medium in 125-mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The inoculum consisted of 1 mL of a spore suspension (3.2 × 10⁶ spores) obtained by shaking a PDA culture fragment (≈1.0 cm3) in 20 mL of sterile distilled water. Cultures were incubated at 25 °C and 100 rpm. Every 24 h, the cultures of three flasks were filtered through filter paper. The retained mycelium was dried at 50 °C to determine fungal growth, while enzymatic activity was measured in the filtrate.

Enzymatic assays

α-Amylase activity was determined using the iodine–starch complex decolorization method (Palanivelu 2001). The reaction mixture consisted of 500 µL of enzyme sample, 1.0 mL of 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and 500 µL of 0.1% gelatinized soluble starch prepared in the same buffer. The mixture was incubated at 40 °C for 10 min, and the reaction was stopped by adding 500 µL of 100 mM HCl. Residual starch was quantified by adding 500 µL of iodine solution, followed by measurement of absorbance at 690 nm. One unit of α-amylase activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to reduce the absorbance by 20% per minute under the assay conditions.

Glucoamylase activity was measured using the glucose oxidase method (Trinder 1969), with a commercial kit (Interteck-Katal, Belo Horizonte, Brazil). A sample aliquot of 50 µL was incubated with 50 µL of 0.5% starch prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and gelatinized by heating, at 50 °C for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 10 µL of 6.1 M trichloroacetic acid (TCA), resulting in a final concentration of 0.55 M. Samples were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 5 min to remove precipitated proteins. An aliquot of 10 or 25 µL of the supernatant was transferred to 1.0 mL of Trinder reagent (Trinder 1969) and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 505 nm. A control reaction was performed to account for background glucose present in the used starch substrate, using 50 µL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 50 µL of the 0.5% starch solution. The blank reaction consisted of 25 µL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1.0 mL of the Trinder reagent. A glucose calibration curve (0.05–1.33 µmol) was used to quantify glucose release. One international unit of glucoamylase was defined as the amount of enzyme that released 1 μmol of glucose per minute under the assay conditions.

Extracellular proteins precipitation and size-exclusion chromatography

Culture filtrates from sixteen 125-mL Erlenmeyer flasks, each containing 25 mL of liquid medium, were combined, and extracellular proteins were precipitated by the addition of ammonium sulfate to 90% saturation. Precipitated proteins were recovered by centrifugation (12,000 g, 5 min) and dialyzed three times against 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) using cellulose dialysis tubing with a 14-kDa molecular weight cutoff. After brief centrifugation (4,000 g, 2 min), the dialysate was loaded to a Sephacryl S-200 HR column (1.9 × 60 cm; GE Healthcare Life Sciences, cat. 17,058,410, USA) equilibrated with 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Elution was performed with the same buffer at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, and 4-mL fractions were collected. Protein elution was monitored by measuring absorbance at 280 nm, and enzymatic activity assays were performed for each fraction. Fractions exhibiting the highest α-amylase and glucoamylase activities were pooled and stored at − 20 °C until use.

Electrophoretic analyses

SDS-PAGE was performed to analyze protein profiles and to prepare samples for Liquid Chromatography coupled with tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay (Bradford 1976), with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard. Aliquots of dialysate proteins (179 μg) and pooled proteins from the size-exclusion chromatography fractions (14.7 µg) were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The precipitated proteins were washed with ethanol and resuspended in 20 µL of sample buffer containing 20% glycerol, 1% SDS, 0.03 mg/mL bromophenol blue, 125 mM Tris-base (pH 6.8) and 0.72 M β-mercaptoethanol. After boiling in a water bath for 10 min, samples were loaded onto a 7.5% SDS-PAGE gel according to Laemmli (Laemmli 1970). The Broad Range Molecular Marker (BioRad, USA) was also boiled before loading. Gels were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250.

Native PAGE was performed under conditions similar to SDS-PAGE, except that SDS and β-mercaptoethanol were omitted from all solutions and samples were not heat-denatured. Native proteins samples (1.3 µg of the column fractions containing amylases and 64 µg of the dialysate proteins) were mixed with sample buffer at 1:1 ratio and loaded on the 7.5% polyacrylamide gels. Three equal gels were run simultaneously. One gel was silver-stained (Blum et al. 1987). For α-amylase zymography, the second gel was incubated for 30–40 min at room temperature in 50 mL of 0.5% starch prepared in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). After incubation, the gel was stained with iodine solution (Murakami et al. 2007) to visualize hydrolysis zones. For glucoamylase zymography, proteins from the third gel were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane by descending capillary transfer for 1.0 h. The transfer assembly consisted of a nitrocellulose membrane placed beneath the gel within a filter paper sandwich, positioned on a stack of paper towels, with a cellulose sponge soaked in Tris–glycine buffer placed on top. After transfer, the membrane was incubated overnight with a mixture of 1% starch in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and Trinder reagent at a 9:1 ratio (Dojnov and Vujčić 2012).

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis

Aliquots of dialysate proteins (180 μg) and pooled size-exclusion chromatography fractions exhibiting amylolytic activity (22 µg) were TCA precipitated. Precipitated proteins were washed with ethanol and resuspended in 20 µL of sample dilution buffer. After boiling in a water bath for 10 min, sample were loaded onto a 7.5% discontinuous SDS-PAGE gel, as described above. Electrophoresis was performed only until the samples entered the resolving gel. Following Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining, single gel bands containing all proteins from the dialysate or pooled eluate fractions were excised and sent to the University of São Paulo – ESALQ (Piracicaba, São Paulo, Brazil) for LC–MS/MS analysis.

At the LC–MS/MS facility, Coomassie-stained SDS-PAGE protein bands were excised from the gels and destained by washing with 50% acetonitrile. The gel pieces were then dehydrated with 100% acetonitrile, dried, and rehydrated with 20 mM dithiothreitol. After a further dehydration step with 100% acetonitrile, alkylation was performed using 55 mM iodoacetamide, followed by washes with 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate and dehydration with 100% acetonitrile. Trypsin digestion was then carried out in the dehydrated gel fragments. The trypsin reaction was stopped by adding formic acid-acetonitrile (1:10). Peptides were extracted by successive washes with formic acid–methanol (1:60), formic acid-acetonitrile (1:50), and acetonitrile. All wash solutions were combined and concentrated to 1.0 µL using a SpeedVac concentrator at room temperature. The concentrated samples were resuspended in 10 µL of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, desalted using a ZipTip pipette tip (Merck, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, which was eluted with 10 µL of 1% formic acid. An aliquot of 1.0 µL of the final solution was injected into the mass spectrometer.

LC–MS/MS was performed on a NanoElute system coupled online to a hybrid trapped ion mobility spectrometry (TIMS)-quadrupole time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometer (timsTOF Pro; Bruker Daltonik, Germany) via a nano-electrospray ion source (Captive Spray; Bruker Daltonik, Germany) (Meier et al. 2018). Peptides were separated using a short gradient (22 min total run time) on an Aurora reversed-phase column (15 cm × 75 µm inner diameter, 1.9 µm particle size; Ion Opticks, Australia) at a flow rate of 500 nL/min, with the column oven maintained at 50 °C. The mass spectrometer was operated in data-dependent parallel accumulation–serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode (Meier et al. 2015), acquiring one TIMS-MS survey scan followed by four PASEF MS/MS scans per acquisition cycle. The ion mobility range analyzed was 1/K0 = 0.85–1.3 V·s·cm2‒, using equal ion accumulation and ramp times of 100 ms each in the dual TIMS analyzer. Precursor ions were isolated using a quadrupole isolation window of 2 Th for m/z < 700 and 3 Th for m/z > 700, synchronized with precursor elution from the TIMS device. Collision energy was applied as a function of ion mobility, ranging from 27 eV at 1/K0 = 0.85 V·s·cm2‒ to 45 eV at 1/K0 = 1.3 V·s·cm2‒. Peptide and fragment ion mass tolerances were set to 20 ppm and 0.05 Da, respectively. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 1% at both peptide and protein levels, requiring at least one unique peptide for protein identification.

Raw data processing, protein identification, and relative quantitative analyses were performed using PEAKS studio software (Version 10.6, Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Canada). Protein identification was conducted by searching against the P. crustosum G10 proteome database (Uniprot UP000701341, GenBank GCA_014621375.1). All protein identifications were accepted with confidence values higher than 95%. The PeptideMass tool available at ExPASy (Gasteiger et al. 2005) was used to predict tryptic peptide masses and sequences. Protein functions were assigned based on literature data, curated databases, and publicly available online resources. Functional categories were defined according to protein occurrence.

Sequence analysis, structural model, and phylogeny

Protein sequences were aligned with homologous proteins in the Clustal omega program from European Bioinformatics Institute (Madeira et al. 2024). Protein domains were identified using the Conserved Domain Database (CDD) at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (Marchler-Bauer et al. 2012). N-glycosylation sites were predicted using the NetNGlyc-1.0 server (Gupta and Brunak 2002). Disulfide bond prediction was performed using the DisLocate web server (Savojardo et al. 2011). The theoretical molecular weight of the proteins was calculated using the Compute pI/MW tool at ExPASy (Gasteiger et al. 2005).

Structural models for the P. crustosum G10 α-amylase and glucoamylase were generated using SWISS-MODEL (ExPASy) (Waterhouse et al. 2018). The selected templates were Penicillium oxalicum α-amylase (AlphaFold S7Z6T2_PENO1) and Aspergillus niger glucoamylase (AlphaFold G3Y7U0). Sequence identity, excluding signal peptides, was 77.35% between the P. crustosum and P. oxalicum α-amylases and 71.7% between the P. crustosum and A. niger glucoamylases. The oligomeric organization of the templates was used to predict the structural organization of the modeled proteins. Model stereochemical quality was evaluated using MolProbity v4.4 (ExPASy) and secondary structure content was analyzed with VADAR 1.8 (Willard 2003). Structures were visualized and colored in CCP4MG (McNicholas et al. 2011).

For the phylogenetic analysis, amylolytic enzymes sequences were retrieved from the GenBank and UniProt database via the Carbohydrate-Active enZymes (CAZy) Database. Proteins belonging to the glucoamylases GH15 family and the α-amylases GH13_1 and GH13_5 subfamilies were included. A Phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining method (Saitou and Nei 1987) implemented in MEGA 11 program (Tamura et al. 2021). Branch support was assessed by bootstrap analysis with 1000 heuristic replicates (Felsenstein 1985). Phylogenetic groupings were considered reliable when bootstrap values exceeded 70%. Evolutionary distances were calculated using the Poisson correction method (Zuckerkandl and Pauling 1965) and are expressed as the number of amino acid substitutions per site.

Biochemical kinetics

The optimal pH for α-amylase activity was determined using the iodine–starch complex decolorization method in 0.1 M phosphate buffer over a pH range of 5.0–8.0. The optimal pH for glucoamylase was not determined due to the influence of pH on the glucose oxidase assay.

Optimal temperatures were determined by measuring enzyme activity at different temperatures at pH 7.0. Thermal stability was evaluated by incubating the enzymes at various temperatures for 10 min in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), followed by determination of residual activity. The thermal stability data were best fitted with a third-order polynomial regression curve, and the resulting equation was used to calculate the temperature at which the enzyme lost 50% of its activity (Tm).

Thermal stability was also investigated over a 72 h period at 40 °C and 4 °C in 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Aliquots were collected at defined intervals and assayed to determine residual α-amylase or glucoamylase activity. The experimental data were best fitted with a second-order polynomial regression curve, and the resulting equation was used to calculate the enzymes half-life at 40 °C.

Saturation curves were generated for P. crustosum UEM-45 α-amylase and glucoamylase. The α-amylase saturation curve was obtained with 0.72 µg of enzyme and starch concentrations of 0.2 to 2.0 mg/mL, applying the starch–iodine decolorization method. The glucoamylase saturation curve was generated with 7.2 µg of enzyme and starch concentrations of 1.25 to 20 mg/mL, measured with the glucose oxidase method. Residual starch concentrations for α-amylase were obtained from a starch calibration curve, and initial reaction rates were expressed as mg starch consumed per mL per minute. For glucoamylase, rates were expressed as µmol glucose released per minute. Kinetic parameters (KM and Vmax) were determined by nonlinear regression using the Solver tool in Microsoft Excel.

Enzyme activity on different substrates

The substrate specificity of α-amylase was evaluated using soluble starch, maltodextrin (dextrose equivalent 4.0–7.0; Sigma-Aldrich 419,672, USA), and maize amylopectin (Sigma-Aldrich 10,120, Germany), each at 0.1% , using the starch–iodine decolorization method with 0.72 µg enzyme. Glucoamylase specificity was determined with the same substrates, in addition to β-cyclodextrin and maltose, each at 0.5%, employing the glucose oxidase method with 7.2 µg of enzyme.

Enzyme substrate specificity was further assessed using synthetic substrates. For this assay, 50 µL of the partially purified enzyme preparation (7.2 µg) were added to 450 µL of the 4 mM 4-nitrophenyl α-D-glucopyranoside (p-NPG, Sigma-Aldrich N1377, USA) or blocked p-nitrophenyl α-D-maltoheptaoside (BpNPG7, Sigma-Aldrich N5665, USA) prepared in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Reactions were incubated for 4 h at 40 °C in a rotatory mixer (10 rpm). The reactions were stopped by the addition of 500 µL of 2% sodium carbonate, and absorbance was measured at 410 nm.

A calibration curve was constructed using 5‒50 μM of p-nitrophenol (p-NP) in 500 µL of 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 500 µL of 2% sodium carbonate. One unit of enzyme activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 μmol of p-NP per minute under the assay conditions.

Product analysis of enzymatic reaction

An aliquot of 300 µL (43 µg) of the partially purified enzyme preparation was added to 700 µL of 1.0% soluble starch prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). A starch-only control reaction was prepared with 300 µL of buffer and 700 µL of starch solution, while an enzyme-only control reaction consisted of 300 µL of enzyme preparation and 700 µL of buffer. All reactions were incubated for 18 h at 40 °C in a rotary mixer (10 rpm).

Aliquots of 10 µL from reactions and standards (100 mM D-( +)-anhydrous glucose and D-( +)-maltose monohydrate) were spotted onto a silica gel 60 thin layer chromatography (TLC) plate (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Ascending chromatography was performed using the butanol:ethanol:water system (5:3:2). After drying, the plate was sprayed with 0.2% orcinol in sulfuric acid:methanol solution (1:9) and heated at 80 °C (Benassi et al. 2014).

Impact of metal ions and chemicals on enzyme activity

The effects of potential activators and inhibitors on the partially purified α-amylase and glucoamylase were evaluated using the starch–iodine decolorization method and the glucose oxidase assay, respectively. All assays were performed in 0.1 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.5) to prevent precipitation. Metal salts, EDTA, and SDS were tested at a final concentration of 5.0 mM. Enzyme activity was expressed as a percentage relative to the control assay performed in the absence of any added compound.

Raw starch degradation

The glucose oxidase method (Trinder 1969) was employed to evaluate the ability of the amylolytic enzymes to hydrolyze raw starch. For this assay, 50 µL of enzyme solution were incubated for 1 h at 40 °C with 50 µL of 10% suspension of raw starch (rice, cassava, corn, wheat, or potato) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), without prior gelatinization. Subsequent procedures followed those described for the standard enzyme assays. Enzyme units obtained were used to calculate specific activity relative to protein concentration (mg).

Statistics

Results were evaluated by analysis of variance (ANOVA), and mean comparisons were performed using Tukey’s test in SASM–Agri software (Canteri et al. 2001). Means followed by the same letter did not differ significantly at α = 0.01.

Results and discussion

Morphology and amylase production by P. crustosum UEM-45

The P. crustosum UEM-45 strain displayed cultural and morphological characteristics typical of the genus Penicillium (Fig. 1A and B). Colonies grew rapidly on Czapek-Dox Agar and PDA, displaying green pigmentation. Microscopic analysis revealed single-celled conidia formed on phialides supported by metulae arranged in branched conidiophores (Fig. 1C). Growth on starch-containing agar followed by iodine staining produced clear hydrolytic halos, confirming amylase secretion (Fig. 1D).

In liquid culture, P. crustosum UEM-45 did not sporulate (Fig. 1E), allowing direct assessment of extracellular amylolytic activity in culture filtrates. Both α-amylase and glucoamylase activities increased during cultivation and reached maximum levels between 72 and 96 h; therefore, 72 h was selected for all subsequent experiments.

Several species of the genus Penicillium have been described as producers of α-amylase, although reported activity levels vary widely depending on the strain and cultivation conditions (Doyle et al. 1989; Ertan et al. 2006; Afifi et al. 2008; Metin et al. 2010; Balkan and Ertan 2010; Dar et al. 2015). In this context, P. crustosum UEM-45 produced α-amylase at levels ranging from 5.5–6.4 U/mL of filtrate after three days of culture, as determined by the starch–iodine decolorization method (Fig. 1E, Table 1). These values are higher than those reported for Penicillium olsonii (0.5 U/mL) but lower than activities described for Penicillium chrysogenum (550 U/mL), Penicillium citrinum (180 U/mL), and Penicillium expansum (40 U/mL), which were determined using the reducing sugar method (Doyle et al. 1989; Afifi et al. 2008; Metin et al. 2010; Dar et al. 2015).

Glucoamylase production has been reported for fewer Penicillium species, such as P. oxalicum and Penicillium verruculosum (Volkov et al. 2013; Xu et al. 2016). In P. crustosum UEM-45, glucoamylase activity remained low during the initial cultivation period and increased after 72 h, reaching 0.5–1.0 U/mL as determined by the glucose oxidase method (Fig. 1E, Table 1). A comparable glucoamylase activity of 6.2 U/mL, has been reported for P. oxalicum in submerged culture using the reducing sugar method (Xu et al. 2016). In contrast, substantially higher activities have been described for A. niger (137 U/mL) under similar fermentation conditions and activity evaluation (Wang et al. 2008).

Exclusion chromatography and electrophoretic analyses

The elution profile of the Sephacryl S-200 HR column shows that the amylolytic enzymes were eluted in only one peak (Fig. 2A). This purification experiment was repeated twice (not shown), and the proteins were consistently found to have the same purification profile. The average yield of the partially purified α-amylase was 1122.2 ± 138.1 U/330 mL of culture medium (2.4 U/mL of culture medium) with a specific activity of 308.9 U/mg (Table 1). The average yield of the partially purified glucoamylase was 134.9 ± 4.1 U/330 mL of culture medium (0.4 U/mL of culture medium) with a specific activity of 37.5 U/mg (Table 1). There was a recovery of 52.9% of the α-amylase and 40% of the glucoamylase, with specific activity increments of 3.1 and 1.8-fold, respectively (Table 1). However, SDS-PAGE analysis revealed that several proteins were present in the pooled fractions (F) containing amylolytic activity (Fig. 2B).

The partially purified α-amylase and glucoamylase ran as a more concentrated single band in the SDS‐PAGE, with an approximate molecular mass of 68.7 kDa (Fig. 2B). However, there were other protein bands (main ones at 87, 75, 53, and 29 kDa) in the same lane of the eluate, indicating that other proteins co-purified.

LC–MS/MS analyses of extracellular proteins

LC–MS/MS combined with TIMS-TOF and PASEF provides high sensitivity and throughput and has become a standard approach in proteomic studies (Bian et al. 2022). Using this strategy, a total of 1,641 proteins were identified in the one-dimensional SDS-PAGE dialysate (D) proteins band from the extracellular proteome of P. crustosum UEM-45 (Fig. 3). This number includes distinct protein isoforms rather than entirely unique proteins, reflecting the complexity of the fungal secretome.

LC–MS/MS analysis of proteins identified in the SDS–PAGE band from the P. crustosum UEM-45 dialysate. The 1641 identified proteins were classified according to function and are displayed as a pie chart. Functional categories are indicated adjacent to each slice, along with the number of proteins in each class

The most abundant extracellular enzymes in the culture filtrate were an α-glucosidase and a peptidase (Supplementary Table 1), indicating a strong capacity of P. crustosum UEM-45 to secrete hydrolytic enzymes. This feature is further supported by the identification of six additional α-glucosidases in the extracellular proteome. Importantly, LC–MS/MS analysis of the dialysate (D) also revealed the presence of five α-amylases and two glucoamylases (Table 2), demonstrating the diversity of secreted amylolytic enzymes. A similar predominance of an α-glucosidase followed by amylolytic enzymes has been reported in the transcriptome of P. oxalicum (Zhang et al. 2019).

Amylases from P. crustosum UEM-45 identified by mass spectrometry

Proteins identified in the extracellular proteome of P. crustosum UEM-45 were classified into functional categories, as summarized in Fig. 3. The most abundant class comprised proteins involved in protein and peptide metabolism (224 proteins), including components related to translation, protein folding, post-translational modification, and degradation pathways. A large proportion of proteins were also assigned as uncharacterized or of unknown function (217 proteins).

Other major categories included proteins associated with amino acid, nitrogen, urea, and heme metabolism (131 proteins), as well as carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZy) (130 proteins), predominantly represented by glycoside hydrolases (97 proteins). Proteins related to central energy metabolism (113 proteins) and nucleic acid structure and metabolism (112 proteins) were also highly represented.

Additional functional classes comprised proteins involved in lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, redox homeostasis and oxidoreductase activity, and general enzymatic functions, while proteins associated with signaling processes, host interaction and defense, stress response, transport systems, cellular structure, and secondary metabolism, among others, were also detected in lower proportions.

Overall, these results indicate that a substantial fraction of the proteins detected in the extracellular proteome are associated with intracellular metabolic processes, suggesting that part of this protein set may reach the extracellular environment through mechanisms other than classical secretion. In contrast, many of the identified glycoside hydrolases are expected to be actively secreted. A similar profile, characterized by the presence of both CAZy enzymes and proteins related to intracellular processes, has been reported for the extracellular proteome of P. chrysogenum (Jami et al. 2010).

LC–MS/MS analyses of the eluate fractions with amylolytic activity

A heat map analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1A) of the protein in the dialysate (D) and in the column eluate fractions (F) with amylolytic activity revealed a marked reduction in proteome complexity after purification. While 1,641 proteins were detected in the dialysate, only 877 proteins were identified in the eluate fractions, indicating that more than half of the extracellular proteins were removed during exclusion chromatography. A Venn diagram analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1B) showed that most proteins detected in the eluate (850 proteins) overlapped with those identified in the dialysate, whereas 27 proteins were exclusively detected after purification, likely due to protein concentration effects.

Although the chromatographic step did not result in high purification efficiency, LC–MS/MS analysis of the eluate fractions proved essential for prioritizing the enzymes responsible for the observed amylolytic activity and revealed their relative enrichment after chromatography. Two major enzymes were detected: an α-amylase with a predicted molecular mass of 66.52 kDa and pI 4.63, and a glucoamylase with a predicted mass of 67.98 kDa and pI 4.58 (Table 2). The close similarity in molecular weight and isoelectric point between these enzymes explains the difficulty in achieving their separation through additional purification steps.

In addition to the two predominant enzymes, four other α-amylases and one additional glucoamylase were detected in the eluate fractions (Supplementary Table 1; Table 2), albeit at lower relative abundance compared with the dialysate. The most abundant α-amylase and glucoamylase corresponded to UniProt entries A0A9P5KX09 and A0A9P5L1K9, respectively, both encoded in the sequenced genome of P. crustosum G10 (GenBank PRJNA606544). The α-amylase was assigned to glycoside hydrolase family GH13_1, whereas the glucoamylase belonged to GH15, according to CAZy classification. The additional α-amylases detected were also classified mainly within GH13_1, with one enzyme assigned to GH13_5, while the additional glucoamylase was likewise classified as GH15 (Table 2).

The proteomic results are consistent with the zymographic analyses, as the multiple α-amylase activity bands observed in the iodine-stained zymogram (Fig. 2D) and the two glucoamylase activity bands detected using the Trinder reagent (Fig. 2E) correspond to the number of enzymes identified by LC–MS/MS. Together, these results demonstrate that extracellular proteomics is a powerful complementary approach for validating purification strategies and confidently assigning enzymatic activities to specific proteins within complex fungal secretomes. The predominant α-amylase and glucoamylase identified by LC–MS/MS and identified in the sequenced genome of P. crustosum G10 (UniProt A0A9P5KX09 and A0A9P5L1K9, respectively) are therefore most likely the partially purified enzymes subjected to biochemical characterization in this study.

Sequence and structural analysis of the P. crustosum G10 α-amylase

The amino acid sequence of the studied P. crustosum G10 α-amylase (UniProt A0A9P5KX09) was aligned with α-amylases from P. oxalicum (GenBank EPS26265.1) and A. clavatus (UniProt A1CFQ9) (Supplementary Fig. 2), revealing sequence identities of 76.18% and 63.09%, respectively. After removal of the signal peptide, the predicted molecular mass and pI of the 598-amino-acid enzyme were 64.4 kDa and 4.54.

Conserved domain analysis (CDD) showed that the enzyme contains the typical α-amylase architecture, comprising an N-terminal catalytic domain A, a protruding domain B, a C-terminal domain C, and an additional C-terminal carbohydrate-binding module of family CBM20 (Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, this CBM20 domain was absent from the other α-amylases identified in the LC–MS/MS analysis (Table 2), suggesting a distinct substrate interaction capability. Although not universal, CBM20 domains have been described in a limited number of fungal α-amylases (Stam et al. 2006; Janeček and Gabriško 2016; Janeček et al. 2019).

The catalytic residues characteristic of α-amylases—a nucleophilic Asp, a proton-donating Glu, and a stabilizing Asp (Paul et al. 2021)—were conserved in the P. crustosum G10 enzyme (Asp206, Glu230, and Asp285; Supplementary Fig. 2). Additional conserved features, including calcium-binding residues (Asn121, Asp175, His210), the seven conserved sequences (CSR) in α-amylases, predicted N-glycosylation sites (Asn197, Asn577), substrate-binding aromatic residues in the CBM20 module (Trp524, Trp572, Tyr508, Tyr537), and cysteines potentially forming disulfide bridges (Cys30-Cys38; Cys150-Cys164), are highlighted in Supplementary Fig. 2. The presence of predicted N-glycosylation sites agrees with previous reports of N-glycosylation in A. oryzae α-amylase (Minobe et al. 1979).

Structural modeling of the P. crustosum G10 α-amylase (Fig. 4A) was performed using the P. oxalicum α-amylase AlphaFold model (S7Z6T2_PENO1) as a template, yielding 100% residue coverage and a high GMQE score (94%). Model quality evaluation using MolProbity v4.4 indicated that 94.8% of residues located in favored regions (Supplementary Fig. 3). The predicted structure is monomeric and consists of 22% α-helices, 35% β-strands, and 42% coils, as determined by VADAR analysis.

The structural model (Fig. 4A) reveals the classical (β/α)₈-barrel catalytic domain A, domain B, domain C, and a C-terminal CBM20 connected by a linker region (Christiansen et al. 2009; Janeček et al. 2019). The active site is located within domain A, whereas calcium-binding residues are positioned at the interface between domains A and B, a structural arrangement conserved among α-amylases (Stam et al. 2006; Janeček and Gabriško 2016).

Carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs) are auxiliary domains that enhance enzyme–substrate interactions by promoting carbohydrate binding (Janeček et al. 2019; Sidar et al. 2020). CBM20 is a well-characterized module widely distributed among amylolytic enzymes (Christiansen et al. 2009; Ngo et al. 2019). However, structural information for CBM20-containing fungal α-amylases remains scarce. Thus, the modeled structure of the P. crustosum G10 α-amylase provides novel structural insight into this subgroup of fungal amylolytic enzymes.

Sequence and structure analysis of the P. crustosum G10 glucoamylase

The amino acid sequence of the P. crustosum G10 glucoamylase (UniProt A0A9P5L1K9) was aligned with homologous enzymes from A. niger (AlphaFold G3Y7U0) and Hypocrea jecorina (Trichoderma reesei) (PDB 2VN7), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. Sequence identity values of 68.17% and 51.7% were observed in comparison with A. niger and H. jecorina, respectively, including signal peptide regions. Excluding signal peptide, the predicted molecular mass and pI of the for the 614-amino-acid P. crustosum G10 glucoamylase are 64.9 kDa and 4.45, respectively.

Domain analysis using the CDD platform revealed a typical glucoamylase architecture consisting of an N-terminal GH15 catalytic domain and a C-terminal CBM20 domain connected by a linker (Supplementary Fig. 4), in agreement with the modular organization commonly described for fungal glucoamylases (Marín-Navarro and Polaina 2011). Conserved catalytic residues (Glu181, Glu402), predicted N‐glycosylation sites (Asn95. Asn173, Asn593), substrate-binding residues within the CBM20 domain (Trp541, Trp588, Tyr525, Tyr554), and cysteine residues involved in four putative disulfide bridges (Cys212-Cys215, Cys224-Cys451; Cys264-Cys272; Cys507-Cys602) were identified and highlighted in Supplementary Fig. 4.

The three-dimensional structure of the P. crustosum G10 glucoamylase was modeled using the A. niger glucoamylase (AlphaFold G3Y7U0) as a template, achieving 100% residue coverage and a GMQE score of 93% (Fig. 4B). Model quality assessment using MolProbity v4.4 indicated that 96.06% of residues were in the most favorable regions (Supplementary Fig. 5). VADAR analysis revealed a secondary structure composition of 37% α-helix, 20% β-strand, 41% coil, and 26% turn. Swiss-Model analysis indicated a monomeric organization, consistent with other fungal glucoamylases.

Structural inspection of the catalytic domain revealed the canonical GH15 α-barrel fold with a six-helical hairpin toroidal arrangement (Fig. 4B), similar to that reported for P. oxalicum glucoamylase (Roth et al. 2018). Several loops and short β-strands contribute to structural stability, reinforced by conserved disulfide bridges, as described for other fungal glucoamylases (Aleshin et al. 1994, 1996). The conserved catalytic acid–base mechanism characteristic of GH15 enzymes was confirmed, with Glu181 and Glu402 corresponding to the catalytic acid and base, respectively (Fig. 4B).

The P. crustosum G10 glucoamylase CBM20 domain adopts a β-sandwich fold (Fig. 4B), as reported for P. oxalicum glucoamylase (Roth et al. 2018). Conserved aromatic residues involved in substrate binding in A. niger glucoamylase (Sorimachi et al. 1997; Christiansen et al. 2009; Janeček et al. 2019) are also present in the P. crustosum G10 enzyme (Fig. 4B), suggesting a similar role in starch interaction.

Predicted N-glycosylation sites located in both the catalytic domain and the CBM20 module (Fig. 4B) correspond to conserved positions previously reported for P. oxalicum glucoamylase (Roth et al. 2018). Glycosylation is a hallmark of fungal glucoamylases, contributing up to 10–20% of their molecular mass and playing critical roles in secretion efficiency, structural stability, and thermal tolerance (Norouzian et al. 2006; Kumar and Satyanarayana 2009; Karim and Tasnim 2018).

Phylogeny

Phylogenetic analysis showed that the α-amylase characterized in this study clustered within the GH13_1 subfamily, while the glucoamylase grouped with members of the GH15 family (Supplementary Fig. 6). The additional α-amylases and glucoamylases identified by LC–MS/MS were also assigned to GH13_1 and GH15, respectively, except for one α-amylase belonging to the GH13_5 subfamily, which formed a distinct clade. Overall, three main amylase clades were observed, corresponding to GH13_1, GH13_5, and GH15, consistent with established glycoside hydrolase family classifications and phylogenetic relationships (Henrissat 1991; Stam et al. 2006).

Characterization of the partially purified enzymes

The α-amylase produced by P. crustosum UEM-45 exhibited optimal activity at pH 6.5 (Fig. 5A), which is consistent with the acidic pH optima reported for for α-amylases from other Penicillium species (Afifi et al. 2008; Metin et al. 2010; Balkan and Ertan 2010).

The optimal temperatures were 40 °C for the α-amylase and 45 °C for the glucoamylase (Fig. 5B). These values are within the range commonly reported for fungal amylases, including the α-amylase from Penicillium griseofulvum (Ertan et al. 2006), although higher optima temperature have been described for some glucoamylases, such as that from P. oxalicum of 65 °C (Xu et al. 2016).

Thermal stability analyses revealed a gradual loss of activity above 40 °C for both enzymes, with melting temperatures (Tm), calculated by third-order polynomial regression of 50.16 °C for the α-amylase and 47.8 °C for the glucoamylase (Fig. 5C). This thermal profile is comparable to that reported for the α-amylase from Penicillium brevicompactum and the glucoamylase from P. oxalicum (Balkan and Ertan 2010; Xu et al. 2016) and reflects the generally lower thermostability of fungal amylolytic enzymes relative to bacterial counterparts (Gupta et al. 2003; Kumar and Satyanarayana 2009; Paul et al. 2021).

At 40 °C, the P. crustosum UEM-45 α-amylase displayed a half-life of 31.1 h, retaining approximately 70% of its activity after 24 h and 76% after 72 h at 4 °C (Fig. 5D). A similar thermal stability was reported for the α-amylase from P. citrinum, which remained 70% active at 40 °C after 24 h (Metin et al. 2010).The glucoamylase showed a shorter half-life at 40 °C (11.15 h) but retained full activity after prolonged storage at 4 °C (Fig. 5D), indicating good stability under refrigeration conditions.

Both amylases from P. crustosum UEM-45 followed Michaelis–Menten kinetics. The α-amylase exhibited a KM of 0.98 mg/mL and a Vmax of 0.12 mg starch degraded/min (Fig. 5E). This KM value is comparable to the one reported for the α-amylase of P. olsonii (0.55 mg/mL) but lower than those for P. brevicompactum (5.71 mg/mL) and P. griseofulvum (9.1 mg/mL) α-amylases (Ertan et al. 2006; Balkan and Ertan 2010). The glucoamylase exhibited a KM of 4.51 mg/mL for starch and a Vmax of 0.38 µmol glucose released/min (Fig. 5F). This KM value falls within the range of 0.11–4.1 mg/mL described for fungal glucoamylases (Kumar and Satyanarayana 2009). Direct comparisons of Vmax values remain limited due to methodological differences among studies.

Substrate preference and starch hydrolysis products

The α-amylase produced by P. crustosum UEM-45 showed the highest activity toward starch and amylopectin, as determined by the starch–iodine decolorization assay (Fig. 6A). Substrate specificity among microbial α-amylases is known to vary considerably (Gupta et al. 2003), and similar preferences for complex polysaccharides have been reported for the α-amylase from P. citrinum (Metin et al. 2010).

The P. crustosum UEM-45 glucoamylase exhibited its highest activity toward amylopectin and maltodextrin, as measured by the glucose oxidase method (Fig. 6B). A preference for branched or partially hydrolyzed starch substrates over amylose has also been described for glucoamylases from P. verruculosum and P. oxalicum (Yamasaki et al. 1977; Volkov et al. 2013). No activity was detected toward β-cyclodextrin, whereas low but measurable activity was observed with maltose (Fig. 6B), consistent with previous reports for fungal glucoamylases (Yamasaki et al. 1977; Volkov et al. 2013). Activity toward the chromogenic substrates BpNPG7 and p-NPG further confirmed the presence of α-amylase and glucoamylase activities, respectively (Fig. 6C).

Thin-layer chromatography analysis revealed glucose as the main product of starch hydrolysis by the partially purified enzyme preparation (Fig. 6D), supporting the presence of a glucoamylase activity. This finding is consistent with reports showing glucose as the major product of amylose degradation by glucoamylases from P. oxalicum (Yamasaki et al. 1977), whereas glucose and maltose as the main hydrolysis products of α-amylases from P. griseofulvum and P. citrinum (Ertan et al. 2006; Metin et al. 2010). Any maltose generated by the α-amylase from P. crustosum UEM-45 is likely further converted to glucose by the glucoamylase present in the preparation.

Overall, the combined action of the α-amylase and glucoamylase from P. crustosum UEM-45 constitutes an efficient enzymatic system for starch liquefaction and saccharification, resulting in effective glucose production (van der Maarel et al. 2002).

Activators and inhibitors of the partially purified enzymes

The α-amylase from P. crustosum UEM-45 was strongly inhibited by EDTA, SDS, selenite (), Hg2+, and Pb2+, whereas Zn2+ caused only slight but statistically significant (α = 0.01) inhibition (Fig. 7A). Inhibition of α-amylases by various metal ions, particularly heavy metals, has been well documented (Gupta et al. 2003; Paul et al. 2021). Similar sensitivities have been reported for the α-amylase from P. olsonii, which is inhibited by Hg2+ and Cu2+ (Afifi et al. 2008), and the α-amylase from P. brevicompactum, which is inhibited by K+, Fe3+, and Mg2+ (Balkan and Ertan 2010).

All reagents were added to a final concentration of 5 mM, and enzyme activity was measured using the starch–iodine complex decolorization assay in 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.5). B Effects of potential inhibitors and activators on glucoamylase activity. All reagents were added to a final concentration of 5 mM, and enzyme activity was measured using the glucose oxidase assay in 0.1 M sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.5). All data represent the mean ± SD of three independent experimental sets. Bars with different letters indicate statistically significant differences according to Tukey’s test (α = 0.01)

Although most α-amylases are metalloenzymes that depend on Ca2+ for structural integrity and catalytic efficiency (Gupta et al. 2003; Paul et al. 2021), the α-amylase from P. crustosum UEM-45 retained activity in the absence of calcium (Fig. 7A). Considering the presence of conserved calcium-binding residues in the P. crustosum G10 α-amylase, Ca2+ may play a predominantly structural rather than catalytic role. Calcium-independent activity has also been reported for α-amylases from other Penicillium species, such as P. olsonii and P. brevicompactum (Afifi et al. 2008; Balkan and Ertan 2010), whereas stimulation by Ca2+ has been described for the enzyme from P. citrinum (Metin et al. 2010).

The P. crustosum UEM-45 glucoamylase was strongly stimulated by heptamolybdate () and moderately activated by Zn2+, Pb2+, and Mn2+, while EDTA, selenite, SDS, and Hg2+ caused significant inhibition (α = 0.01) (Fig. 7B). Variable responses of fungal glucoamylases to metal ions have been widely reported and appear to be enzyme- and species-specific (Kumar and Satyanarayana 2009). Consistently, glucoamylases from P. oxalicum are known to be inhibited by Hg2+ and Pb2+ (Yamasaki et al. 1977).

EDTA partially inhibited the α-amylase and glucoamylase activities of P. crustosum UEM-45 (Fig. 7A and B), indicating the involvement of metal ions in enzyme stability or catalysis. Similar inhibitory effects of EDTA have been described for α-amylases from P. brevicompactum and P. citrinum, as well as for a glucoamylase from P. oxalicum (Yamasaki et al. 1977; Metin et al. 2010; Balkan and Ertan 2010). SDS caused strong inhibition of the α-amylase and partial inhibition of the glucoamylase from P. crustosum UEM-45 (Fig. 7A and B), consistent with its partial inhibitory effect on the α-amylase of P. citrinum (Metin et al. 2010).

Hydrolysis of raw starch

The partially purified amylases exhibited broad substrate specificity, effectively degrading all tested raw starches. Notably, enzymatic activity toward raw rice starch was significantly higher than that observed for the other substrates (Table 3). However, hydrolysis efficiency on raw rice starch was lower than that observed for gelatinized potato starch (Table 3). Raw Starch-Degrading Enzymes (RSDEs) are of considerable industrial interest, as they can reduce processing costs by eliminating the need for heat pretreatment and liquefaction steps.

The specific activity of the P. crustosum UEM-45 partially purified amylases toward raw rice starch was 10.5 U/mg, which is comparable to that reported for a glucoamylase from P. oxalicum (24.3 U/mg), measured using the reducing sugar assay with the same substrate (Xu et al. 2016). However, this activity is substantially lower than that reported for an α-amylase from a marine metagenomic library (GH13_37 subfamily), which exhibited 150.1 U/mg on raw rice starch using the reducing sugar method (Peng et al. 2018), and also lower than the 362.8 U/mg reported for a glucoamylase from A. clavatus measured by the glucose oxidase assay (Mendonça et al. 2023).

Many fungal α-amylases and glucoamylases contain carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs), predominantly CBM20 or CBM21, which facilitate binding to and hydrolysis of raw (thermally untreated) starch (Christiansen et al. 2009; Janíčková and Janeček 2020). The CBM20 modules of the P. crustosum G10 α-amylase and glucoamylase contain conserved raw starch-binding sites composed of two tryptophan and two tyrosine residues (Fig. 4A and B; Supplementary Figs. 2 and 4). Removal of CBMs from glucoamylases has been shown to preserve activity toward soluble starch while markedly reducing activity against insoluble starch (Takahashi et al. 1985; Vu et al. 2014). Nevertheless, fungal glucoamylases naturally lack CBMs have also been reported (Sidar et al. 2020).

Conclusions

Seven extracellular amylases secreted by P. crustosum UEM-45 were identified by LC–MS/MS within a comprehensive extracellular proteome comprising 1,641 proteins. Beyond the identification of individual amylases, this study offers, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive proteomic overview of the extracellular protein repertoire of P. crustosum, allowing the evaluation of amylase diversity, secretion dynamics, and relative abundance in a complex biological system. This proteomic approach allowed the identification of the major amylolytic components secreted under the tested conditions, guiding the selection of an α-amylase and a glucoamylase for biochemical characterization. Both enzymes displayed molecular masses of approximately 67 kDa, were predicted to be monomeric, and contained a CBM20 module in addition to their catalytic domains, a feature consistent with efficient starch binding and degradation. The enzymes showed optimal activity at around 40 °C, remained stable for several hours at 4 °C, and could hydrolyze gelatinized as well as raw starch substrates, producing glucose as the main fermentable sugar. Importantly, the combined action of these two enzymes confers both liquefaction and saccharification capabilities, demonstrating the functional relevance of the amylolytic profile revealed by the extracellular proteome. Importantly, the identification and biochemical characterization of a glucoamylase from P. crustosum UEM-45 address a relevant knowledge gap, as glucoamylases from species of the genus Penicillium have been rarely studied. Therefore, this study highlights extracellular proteomics as a powerful strategy not only for enzyme discovery but also for prioritizing biologically and industrially relevant enzymes. Considering the efficient enzyme production by P. crustosum UEM-45 in an inexpensive liquid culture medium, these findings expand current knowledge on the amylolytic potential of Penicillium species and underscore the biotechnological relevance of this strain for industrial starch processing applications.
