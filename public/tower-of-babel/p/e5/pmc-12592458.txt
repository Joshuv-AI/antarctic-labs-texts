Ultra-broadband wide-angle anti-reflection scheme utilizing multi-layer resonant metasurfaces
Authors: Yehuda Baum, Dotan Arad, Rachel Gringols Yarden, Sharon Karepov, Jacob Scheuer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuppressing reflection at the interface separating media with different optical properties has always been an objective, common to many disciplines involving wave phenomena. This task constitutes an even greater challenge for beams exhibiting complex spatiotemporal profiles as broadband (spectral and spatial) operation is required. Here we present a new approach for obtaining broadband antireflection properties by utilizing nanostructure arrays in a multi-layer metasurface configuration with periodicity in the order of a wavelength. As a concrete example, we show the ability to attain ultra-broadband reflection suppression exceeding –30dB in a Silicon-Air interface, over a bandwidth of ~ 400 nm at telecom wavelengths and an incidence angle of ± 30˚. This method offers several inherent advantages over moth-eye-like approaches as it allows for a more repetitive fabrication process while facilitating a monolithic, single-material antireflection structure. Introduction

When light transitions from one medium to another, differences in refractive indices cause partial reflection which reduces the intensity of the transmitted light. Partial reflection is common to all types of waves and in the optical regime can significantly degrade the performance of optical devices such as lenses, solar panels, displays, and even eyeglasses. Thus, anti-reflection (AR) techniques have become ubiquitous and are employed in numerous applications.

Approaches for reflection suppression have been developed since the 19th century following the pioneering works of Raleigh and Fraunhofer1,2 on utilizing graded index layer for AR purposes. Approaches such as thin-film AR coating3–5, adiabatic graded index layers6–11, metamaterials12, and resonant nanostructures13,14 have been proposed and demonstrated. The growing interest in spatiotemporal shaping of optical beams15–17 poses significant challenges to AR structures design as such beams are characterized by broadband spectral and spatial profiles. Recent developments in graded index layers and three-dimensional configurations have been shown to support polarization-insensitive and broadband reflection suppression18,19. Particularly, moth-eye-inspired20 nanostructures mimicking biological AR have drawn significant interest. More recently, a broadband reflection suppression approach utilizing white light cavities and exceptional points has been suggested and demonstrated in RF21,22.

Many of the more advanced AR techniques exhibit excellent performances in terms of bandwidth (spectral and spatial), particularly those employing gradient index profiles or 3D nanostructures. Such approaches can indeed cope with the challenges associated with beams exhibiting complex spatiotemporal shapes. However, the practical realization of such AR structures is quite complex, requiring extreme control capabilities over fabrication processes, and is often not very repetitive. Moth-eye-like AR structures, for example, rely on continuously varying surface profiles with very fine features that are difficult to optimize and to fabricate accurately or in a repetitive manner. To date, the commonly used AR approach in optics is based on thin films. The basic AR coating employs a single layer placed between one medium (e.g., air) and another (e.g., glass). By choosing the layer thickness to be a quarter wavelength and its refractive index to be the geometric mean of the indices of the two media, it is possible to cancel completely the reflectivity at a specific wavelength. Employing multilayer coatings can yield broadband AR. However, achieving this in practice is challenging due to the difficulty in obtaining transparent materials with specific refractive indices that do not necessarily exist23. Nevertheless, optimal performances can be traded for realizability by employing intensive numerical optimization procedures based on existing materials.

Despite the practical success of thin-films AR coating, there are applications for which a monolithic approach (i.e. the AR coating consists of a single material) is advantageous24. For example, practical AR for high-power applications25,26 is difficult to attain, mainly because of the different thermal expansion coefficients associated with different materials. Consequently, thin-film AR coatings are susceptible to thermal damage under high-power illumination conditions27.

In this paper, we present a hybrid broadband (spectral and spatial) AR scheme, utilizing nanostructure arrays in a multi-layer metasurface configuration with periodicity in the order of a wavelength (see Fig. 1). This scheme offers a rigorous design and fabrication approach, allowing for a quick realization flow with good control over the spectral reflectivity. It also allows for optimizing the geometry and obtain more repetitive realization (in terms of obtaining as specific design) than that of moth-eye-like approaches. In parallel, the monolithic, single-material, nature of structure supports high-power applications as well as operation at very high temperature conditions. Furthermore, such AR structures can also be realized using additive manufacturing techniques such as 3D printing and Nano-Imprint Lithography.

AR structure concept and design approach

The multilayer metasurface consists of a periodic pyramid-like unit cell comprising a discrete stair-case structure (as shown in Fig. 1). It should be noted that Fig. 1 shows only one possible implementation and that additional configurations employing other geometries (e.g. cylindrical nanostructures) and arrangements (e.g. hexagonal lattice) are possible and discussed in the following sections. Figure 1 outlines the design parameter space. For polarization insensitivity, the metasurface is symmetric in the horizontal dimension such that each layer is defined by the side, Si, and height, hi, of the box (for cylindrical nanostructures – the diameter and height). The period of the array, Λ, is identical for all layers.

For an n-layer design, there are 2n + 1 design parameters. Even for the simplest case of a two-layer AR structure (the single layer is equivalent to a quarter-wavelength AR film), the optimization of the design is quite complex. As the optimization problem is not necessarily convex, we chose to employ stochastic optimization, specifically particle swarm optimization (PSO)28. PSO29,30 is a population-based optimization algorithm inspired by the social behavior of birds flocking or fish schooling. Each particle represents a potential design in the parameters space, and it adjusts its position based on its own experience and the experiences of other particles. The algorithm constantly updates the velocity and position of the particles to converge toward an optimal solution. The actual optimization is carried out on the geometrical parameters of the nanostructures in each layer (height and side/diameter), where the periodicity, Λ, is considered a fixed parameter. The reason not to optimize Λ stems from the fact that for practical realization reasons, it is advantageous to have the periodicity as large as possible. However, the periodicity should be smaller than the wavelength in the material in order to eliminate all Bragg diffraction lobes except for the 0th order.

An important part of any numeric optimization scheme is the choice of the cost function to be minimized. For broadband AR applications, two potential cost functions to be minimized come to mind: (1) the maximal reflectivity within the band; (2) the overall reflectivity in the band. As discussed in Sect. 3, the differences between the designs originating from the two cost functions are relatively minor.

As a concrete example, we focus on designing AR structures for Silicon-Air interface at the telecom band (1300–1700 nm). We chose this example because of the large index contrast between the two media which leads to substantial reflection (~ 31%), thus rendering broadband AR a challenging task.For the analysis and the simulations, we use undoped silicon. The experimental study presented in the Supplementary Information, utilizes undoped silicon wafers as well.

Broadband AR metasurfaces designs and performances

Reflectivity analysis and parameter optimization.

The calculation of the reflectivity is carried out by the Rigorous coupled-wave analysis (RCWA) method31,32. As noted above, the periodicity of the structure is considered to be a design parameter that is not being optimized. In order to avoid the excitation of a higher Bragg scattering order (larger than 0) under perpendicular illumination, the periodicity must be smaller than the wavelength in the material. The shortest wavelength in the optimization band is 1300 nm and the refractive index of the medium (Silicon) is ~ 3.5. Consequently, the periodicity must be smaller than ~ 371 nm.

Figure 2 depicts the calculated spectral reflectivity for a dual layer metasurface consisting of rectangular meta-atoms, with various periodicities, following the optimization procedure described above. For the calculations, the complete dispersive properties of Silicon are used. The dimensions obtained for each structure are detailed in Table 1. The vertical dashed lines indicate the spectral band for which the designs are optimized. The largest periodicity is 355 nm which is still below the upper bound discussed above. As shown in the figure, the impact of the periodicity on the obtained AR performances is almost negligible (Consider, for example, the curves corresponding to Λ= 355 nm and Λ= 305 nm). Thus, throughout the rest of this paper, we focus on structures with a periodicity of Λ= 355 nm. Nevertheless, we note that the results for each periodicity were obtained by stochastic optimization and there is no guarantee that the presented results correspond to the global minimum of the reflectivity.

A broadband anti-reflection effect can be achieved by designing the nanostructure dimensions such that the transmission resonances are closely spaced, resulting in a wide transmission band. As shown in Fig. 2, the two-layer structure produces two resonances, and when the wavelengths of these are sufficiently close, their combined effect leads to the desired broadband response. (see also Appendix C in the SI for additional analysis).

Note that at certain wavelengths (~ 1240 nm for Λ= 355 nm and ~ 1070 nm for Λ= 305 nm) the reflectivity curves “break”, leading to reflectivity of ~ –10dB. This is because the periodicity at these wavelengths exceeds the wavelength in the material, leading to the excitation of the m = ± 1 Bragg orders in the Silicon substrate. Another impact of the periodicity (not observed in Fig. 2) is the angular bandwidth of the reflectivity suppression. Under an oblique impinging field, higher Bragg orders are expected to be excited at smaller angles for larger periodicities. Thus, it is expected that structures with larger periodicity exhibit narrower angular bandwidth.

The impact of lattice and meta-atoms geometry.

The approach exhibited in Fig. 1 can be realized using various meta-atoms geometries and lattice configurations. Figure 3(a) depicts the spectral reflectivities of three AR dual layer metasurfaces optimized for reducing to a minimum the maximal reflectivity in the band: Design (1) Square meta-atoms arranged in a square lattice (blue); Design (2) Circular meta-atoms arranged in a square lattice (cyan); Design (3) Circular meta-atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice (green). The structures parameters are detailed in Table 2. Although the spectral profiles are quite different, the performances of the three structures are very similar in terms of the optimization criterion (the maximal reflectivity in the band for all structures is approximately –30dB with a minor advantage to the hexagonal arrangement). This is because the maximal reflectivity in the band (which is the parameter being minimized in the optimization process) is obtained at the edges of the band ( λ= 1300 nm and λ= 1700 nm). Nevertheless, considering the overall reflection in the band, it is clear that the hexagonal lattice configuration comprising circular meta-atoms performs better than the other options. However, we note that the optimization converges to a local minimum of the cost function, and it is possible that a better solution exists for the square lattice configurations (employing either circular or square meta-atoms). It is also important to know that the hexagonal lattice design does not exhibit the abrupt increase in the reflectivity at λ≈ 1240 nm discussed in the previous section. This is because of the different lattice geometry which modifies the Bragg condition.

Sensitivity to incidence angle and fabrication errors

The designs presented in Sects. 2 and 3 have been optimized for perpendicular illumination. In any practical application, AR structures are expected to operate for a finite acceptance angle determined by the specific application. Thus, it is important to analyze the performances of the AR metasurfaces as a function of the incidence angle.

Figure 4 (a) depicts the spectral reflectivity of an optimized dual-layer metasurface comprising square meta-atoms in a square lattice as a function of the incidence angle of the impinging field. As can be seen, when the incidence angle is increased the AR performances of the structure degrade, though high reflection suppression (<−30dB) is obtained for incidence angles of up to 10˚.

Dependence of the reflection suppression on the incidence angle: (a) spectral reflectivity of an optimized dual-layer metasurface consisting of square meta-atoms arranged in a square lattice (design 1); (b) highest reflectivity in the band as a function of the incidence angle for the three designs discussed here.

Figure 4(b) shows the largest reflectivity in the band as a function of the incidence angle, for the three optimized metasurfaces designs detailed in Table 2. The figure shows that good AR performances are maintained for both square lattices structures up to an incidence angle of ~ 10˚. Beyond this angle, the reflectivity exhibits an abrupt increase, and continues to increase, reaching −18dB and −23dB at an incidence angle of 20˚ for the circular and rectangular meta-atoms, respectively. We attribute the abrupt jump in the reflectivity to the excitation of higher Bragg reflection order in the Silicon substrate, similar to the abrupt increase in the reflectivity at shorter wavelengths (see Figs. 2 and 3). We note that even though design 2 (circular meta-atoms and square lattice) exhibits better AR performances than those of design 1, the abrupt degradation in the performances of design 2 for incidence angles larger than 10˚ is significantly larger than that of design 1. The reason for the apparent better robustness of design 1 to changes in incidence angle is not clear. It is possible that the optimization of design 2 (for perpendicular incidence) turned out to be better than that of design 1, thus rendering the first more sensitive to the incidence angle. This may indicate that it is possible to optimize the design also for a broader angular range, probably at the expense of the reflection suppression.

In contrast to the square lattice designs, the hexagonal lattice design appears to support larger incident angles, maintaining reflectivity levels below −30db even at 20˚. As discussed in Sect. 3, this is attributed to the different lattice geometry, which suppresses the excitation of higher Bragg diffraction orders.

Any realistic device is susceptible to fabrication errors and tolerances. Such errors could be roughly separated into two types: random deviations (or “noise”) and bias. “Noise” corresponds to random deviations of the individual meta-atoms from the optimal design parameters. Because of the large number of meta-atoms in the metasurface, the impact of such noise is expected to average out and exhibit a relatively minor impact on the device performances, unless the variance is large. Bias, on the other hand, corresponds to deviations of all meta-atoms from the optimal design. Such deviations could be highly detrimental to the device performances and, therefore, the impact of bias on the presented AR devices must be analyzed.

Figure 5 depicts the sensitivity of the reflectivity (maximal value in the band) of the devices in Table 2 to small variations in the geometrical parameters of the meta-atoms. The color maps show the highest reflectivity within the optimization band (1300–1700 nm). It seems that the sensitivity of the devices’ performances to variations in the side/diameter of the two layers (panels a, c, and e) are correlated. When the sides/diameters of the nanostructures in both layers either increase or decrease, the impact on the AR performances is more moderate. Deviations of ± 5 nm in (both) diameters of the pillars (panels c and e) and ± 10 nm in the sides of the squares (panel a) seem to exhibit almost negligible impact on the performances. The sensitivity of the reflectivity to the thicknesses of the nano-pillars (panels b, d, and f) is relatively small allowing for variations in the order of 10–20 nm (depending on the specific design) without significant impact on the performances. It should be noted that the ability to determine the thicknesses of the pillar is better and more repeatable than the ability to set their diameters/sides.

Optimization for wide-angle incidence

As noted in Sect. 4, in many applications AR structures are required to operate over a range of acceptance angles. For such applications it is desired to optimize the design for broadband and wide-angle operation simultaneously. Our design approach supports such optimization simply by modifying the optimization cost function. To attain broadband and wide-angle operation, we choose the cost function (to be minimized) as the sum of the maximal reflectivity within the band (1300–1700 nm) at 5 incidence angles: 0˚, 8˚, 18˚, 25˚, and 30˚ at TE polarization. The optimization was carried out in the same way discussed in the previous sections. The choice to optimize the reflectivity over a non-uniform distribution of incident angles originates from the results shown in Fig. 4. Note that the spectral reflectivity varies gradually at near-normal incidence and more steeply at larger angles. Therefore, in order not to bias the optimization towards the small angles case we chose a non-uniform set of incidence angles to better emphasize performance at larger angles.

The optimization is carried out for a dual layer metasurface composed of cylindrical meta-atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice. This is because this structure exhibited less sensitivity to the incidence angle (see Fig. 4(b)). Following the optimization procedure, the design parameters were: S1 = 331 nm, S2 = 203 nm, h1 = 147 nm, and h2 = 258 nm. Note that this design differs from that of Design 3 in Table 2, reflecting the modification of the optimization target. Conclusions

We presented a new approach for broadband anti-reflective (AR) structures using nanostructure arrays in a multi-layer metasurface configuration. The method is based on optimizing the meta-atoms dimensions to tailor the resonances of the metasurface and attains broad AR performances. This method offers several advantages over contemporary approaches, such as moth-eye-like nanostructures and thin-film AR coatings. The repetitive and scalable fabrication process, using monolithic, single-material metasurfaces, simplifies the realization of structures compared to moth-eye-like approach. The main advantage of our metasurface technique over traditional thin-film techniques is its suitability for high-power applications where thermal stability is critical.

As a concrete example, we studied three variants of AR metasurfaces utilizing various meta-atom geometries and lattice configurations, which were optimized to suppress reflection at the interface between Silicon and air at the telecom band. The proposed metasurface design demonstrates highly efficient reflection suppression over a broad bandwidth (1300–1700 nm) and incidence angles of up to ± 20° for circular meta-atoms in a hexagonal lattice, and ± 10° for square lattice designs, achieving spectral and spatial reflection suppression exceeding −30dB for each wavelength in the band. The design can be further optimized for wide-angle operation, allowing for total reflection suppression exceeding −32dB in the band over incidence angle in the range of ± 30˚. It should be emphasized that the reflection suppression bandwidth presented here (400 nm) is limited primarily by the choice of the AR requirement (−30dB or more). Relaxing this requirement to −20dB (which is completely sufficient for many applications) would have extended to bandwidth to ~700 nm and extend the angular range. To verify the validity of our design approach (specifically the RCWA simulation tool), we carried out a preliminary experimental study of single-level AR metasurfaces, demonstrating very good agreement with the predictions of the RCWA tool (see Appendix A).

We also conducted an in-depth study of the sensitivity of the three designs to fabrication variations and errors. The analysis showed that all designs exhibited relatively high tolerance to fabrication errors. Variations in the dimensions of the nanostructures exhibited relatively minor impact on the overall AR performance. Specifically, deviations in the thickness of the meta-atoms of up to ± 10–20 nm, and ± 5 nm in the sides/diameter for designs 2 (circular meta-atoms, square lattice) and 3 (circular meta-atoms, hexagonal lattice), and ± 10 nm for design 1 (square meta-atoms, square lattice), did not affect significantly the reflection suppression. This demonstrates the robustness of the designs across a range of fabrication inaccuracies, with what appears as a slight advantage for design 1. We note, however, that this apparent better robustness might stem from a less optimal design. Nevertheless, this robustness makes these metasurface designs highly suitable for practical, large-scale manufacturing processes.

Finally, we note that in addition to monolithic structure, the approach can also be implemented by utilizing a metasurface composed of a different material than that of the substrate. Consequently, the technique is compatible with additive manufacturing methods, including 3D printing and Nano-Imprint Lithography, enhancing its practicality for various optical systems.

The proposed AR concept is attractive for several practical applications. For example, in high-power laser systems, where conventional multilayer coatings often degrade due to thermal expansion mismatch between layers. A monolithic (single material) design, as discussed here, is inherently more stable under such conditions (see also Appendix B in the SI). Another relevant application is in optical systems operating across various spectral ranges, where minimizing reflection over a broad bandwidth and wide angular range is essential. This includes imaging systems, display technologies, optical sensors, and scientific instrumentation. While the designs presented here focus on the near-infrared regime, the same design principles are expected to apply at other wavelength ranges, provided the structural dimensions are scaled accordingly. Finally, photovoltaic panels, which operate under varying temperature conditions and illumination angles and require low reflectivity across a broad spectral range. The monolithic nature of multi-layer metasurface based AR structures and the ability to realize them using scalable methods such as nanoimprint lithography render them a highly attractive solution for PV.
