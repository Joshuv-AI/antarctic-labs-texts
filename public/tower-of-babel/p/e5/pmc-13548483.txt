Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 as a novel bioinoculant: promoting growth and modulating the metabolic profile of Ashwagandha (Withania somnifera (L.) Dunal)
Authors: Afrah E. Mohammed, Nahaa M. Alotaibi, Maha H. Khalaf, Seham M. Hamed, Marcelo Carvalho Minhoto Teixeira Filho, Nashwa Hagagy, Modhi O. Alotaibi, Ahmed M. Mahmoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the medicinal importance of Withania somnifera (L.) Dunal (Ashwagandha), a significant knowledge gap remains regarding how endophytic microbes mechanistically reprogram host metabolism to enhance both growth and biological value. This study addresses this gap by exploring the biofortification capacity of Jidongwangia sp. JTT04, a newly isolated endophytic actinobacterium from Tribulus terrestris, in enhancing the growth, biochemical profile, and functional properties of Ashwagandha. Inoculation with JTT04 significantly improved shoot biomass and enhanced carbon assimilation provides the essential precursors for primary metabolism, driving a significant surge in glucose, fructose, and starch. These carbohydrates serve as the carbon skeleton for the accelerated synthesis of organic acids (citric and succinic) and amino acids, alongside an enriched pool of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids. This robust primary metabolic foundation subsequently fuels the shikimate and phenylpropanoid pathways, triggering the overproduction of high-value secondary metabolites, including phenolic acids (caffeic, rosmarinic) and flavonoids (rutin, kaempferol). Consequently, this integrated metabolic redirection directly translates into superior biochemical and functional values, evidenced by a 1.51-fold increase in antioxidant capacity (FRAP), potent anti-inflammatory effects (Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and lipoxygenase (LOX) inhibition), and broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. By mechanistically linking nutrient assimilation to biological value, JTT04 offers a transformative strategy for the sustainable biofortification of Ashwagandha.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09371-3.

Introduction

Withania somnifera (Ashwagandha) is a widely studied medicinal plant known for its rich phytochemical profile and broad therapeutic potential. It contains a variety of bioactive compounds, including withanolides (e.g., withaferin A, withanolide D), alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and phytosterols, which collectively contribute to its anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, immunomodulatory, and neuroprotective properties [1–4]. The neuroprotective and adaptogenic activities of its alkaloids support the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s [3, 5], while its antioxidant constituents help mitigate oxidative stress and reduce chronic disease risk [4]. Phytosterols also confer hepatoprotective effects by modulating cholesterol metabolism [5]. Additionally, W. somnifera exhibits antidiabetic activity through the regulation of glucose metabolism, and its use extends to managing conditions such as fever, arthritis, gastrointestinal disorders, and stress-related cognitive decline [6–8]. Its diverse metabolite composition, including steroids, saponins, glycosides, and phenolics, further enhances its value as a functional herbal remedy [6, 7].

Optimizing growth conditions is essential for improving the yield and quality of medicinal plants [9]. To address the rising demand for sustainable agriculture and water treatment, research is focusing on eco-friendly alternatives [10, 11]. Endophytic actinobacteria have emerged as promising biopriming agents, enhancing crop productivity in major cereals like maize, rice, and wheat [12]. These bacteria colonize the rhizosphere and internal plant tissues, stimulating growth and improving disease resistance through sustained plant–microbe interactions [13, 14]. Actinobacteria contribute to plant health by supplying nutrients, boosting bioactive metabolite production, and enhancing resistance to soil-borne pathogens [15]. They are also known for producing a broad array of secondary metabolites, including antimicrobials, antioxidants, enzymes, and phytohormones like indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and gibberellic acid (GA), and for their abilities to fix nitrogen and solubilize phosphate [16–19]. A wide spectrum of endophytic actinobacteria has been identified across diverse plant hosts, including established genera such as Streptomyces, Microbispora, and Micromonospora, as well as novel ones like Jidongwangia, Koreibacter, and Actinophytocola [20, 21]. In Ashwagandha, limited studies suggest that actinobacterial inoculation can improve yield, nutrient uptake, and phytochemical composition. For instance, inoculation reduced the need for chemical fertilizers by up to 25% and enhanced phenolic acid levels, supporting the plant’s antioxidant capacity [22]. Jidongwangia sp., a recently described genus in the family Micromonosporaceae, remains understudied in medicinal plants. Although its plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits are documented in this study, its influence on Ashwagandha, especially regarding yield, metabolic enhancement, and bioactivity, has not been reported. In this study, Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 was isolated from Tribulus terrestris, a medicinal plant native to arid regions like Saudi Arabia [23]. T. terrestris hosts endophytic actinobacteria and is valued for its saponin- and flavonoid-rich profile, which confers cardiovascular, antioxidant, hepatoprotective, and diuretic benefits [24–26].

Given the increasing need for sustainable approaches in medicinal plant cultivation, this study evaluates the biofortification potential of Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 in Ashwagandha. We assessed its effects on biomass, metabolite accumulation (sugars, amino acids, organic and fatty acids), and biological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial responses. Through integrated physiological and biochemical analyses, we explored Jidongwangia’s capacity to enhance agronomic performance and therapeutic quality in Ashwagandha under controlled conditions.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolation

Endophytic bacteria were obtained from Tribulus terrestris leaves following a sterilization protocol that involved immersion in 70% ethanol for 3 min, then in 0.1% mercuric chloride for 5 min, with five subsequent washes using sterile distilled water to remove surface contaminants. The sterilized leaf tissues were crushed and suspended in 2 mL of physiological saline, and 100 µL of this homogenate was plated on yeast-extract mannitol agar (YEMA) and incubated at 30 °C for 72 h. Morphologically distinct bacterial colonies were isolated, purified, and characterized based on colony traits as described by AbdElgawad et al. [27]. A bacterial culture, prepared in double-distilled water and adjusted to an OD600 standard, was used to inoculate sterilized Ashwagandha seeds in triplicate assays. Among the isolates, Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 was chosen for further biofortification evaluation due to its metabolic adaptability, particularly its ability to metabolize diverse carbon and nitrogen substrates, including sugars, amino acids, and organic acids. Additionally, JTT04 demonstrated key plant growth-promoting properties, including the synthesis of phytohormones like IAA and GA, along with notable antioxidant production (Table 1). Furthermore, morphological characterization of the bioinoculant was performed using both culture and microscopic imaging (Figure S1) to document the phenotype of the Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 strain used in this stud.

Molecular Identification of the bacterial isolate

Genomic DNA was extracted following the protocol described by Chen et al. [28] to facilitate subsequent gene amplification. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified in a 25 µL PCR reaction containing 1.5 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM of each deoxynucleotide triphosphate (dNTP), 0.5 µM of forward and reverse primers, 0.625 units of Taq DNA polymerase, and 5 µL of the extracted DNA template (~ 5–10 ng/µL). The primers used for amplification were 27 F (5′-CAGAGTTTGATCCTGGCT-3′) and 1492R (5′-AGGAGGTGATCCAGCCGCA-3′) [29]. The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 amplification cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, annealing at 49 °C for 45 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 90 s. A final extension was carried out at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were purified and sequenced, and the resulting sequences were compared to known sequences in the GenBank database using BLAST for taxonomic identification. Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis were performed using MEGA version 7, using the Neighbor-Joining method with the Kimura 2-parameter model, and node reliability was assessed using 1,000 bootstrap replicates [30].

Plant material and experimental conditions

Ashwagandha seeds were obtained from the Agricultural Research Center in Dokki, Giza, Egypt. A pot experiment was conducted under controlled conditions using a completely randomized design with two treatments: (1) untreated control and (2) biofertilization with the JTT04 strain. For the biofertilization treatment, Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 was cultured in nutrient broth at 29 °C for 24 h. The culture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min, and the resulting pellet was washed and resuspended in sterile water to a final density of 10⁶ CFU mL⁻¹ (OD₆₀₀ = 0.6–0.7). This inoculum was applied to the soil before sowing and subsequently added biweekly. Ashwagandha seeds were surface-sterilized with 5% NaOCl for 20 min, rinsed with distilled water, and sown in pots (60 cm × 45 cm) containing 1.5 kg of a 1:1 mixture of loamy soil and organic compost, pre-adjusted to 65% of soil water capacity. Seeds were divided into two treatment groups: one inoculated with JTT04 and one non-inoculated control. Each treatment consisted of six pots, with four plants per pot. Every two pots served as one biological replicate, yielding three replicates per treatment. Pots were maintained at > 65% water holding capacity by daily weighing and were kept in a growth chamber under standardized conditions: 60% humidity, 250 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ PAR, 22/18°C Day/night temperature, and a 16/8 h photoperiod. The experiment was repeated twice for consistency. After four weeks, shoots were harvested, fresh mass was recorded, and tissues were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent biochemical analysis [31]. Following four weeks of growth, the entire aerial shoot system was harvested by severing the stems at the soil interface to evaluate primary vegetative vigor.

Evaluation of sugars

Sugar profiling was conducted using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Monosaccharides and disaccharides were extracted from 0.5 g of fresh shoot tissue using 50% aqueous acetonitrile at 60 °C. The extracts were filtered, and the supernatant was analyzed using an HPLC system equipped with a SUPELCOSIL LC-NH₂ column (250 × 4.6 mm), eluted with 75% aqueous acetonitrile. Individual sugar forms were identified and quantified based on the peak areas of corresponding standards. Total soluble and insoluble sugars were extracted in 80% aqueous ethanol and quantified using Nelson’s method [32].

Amino acid determination

For the extraction of amino acids, 200 mg of shoot tissue was homogenized in 1 mL of 80% ethanol supplemented with norvaline as an internal standard to facilitate accurate quantification via mass spectrometry. The homogenate was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 30 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected and evaporated to complete dryness under vacuum. To eliminate lipophilic interferences, the dried residue was resuspended in 1 mL of chloroform. A subsequent aqueous extraction was then performed by adding 500 µL of ultrapure water directly to the chloroform suspension to induce liquid-liquid phase partitioning. Following vigorous vortexing to ensure maximum analyte transfer, the biphasic mixture was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min. The aqueous phase was passed through 0.2 μm Millipore filters to remove particulates before analysis. Amino acid separation was performed using a Waters Acquity UPLC-TQD platform equipped with a BEH amide column (2.1 × 50 mm). A 10 µL sample was injected and separated using a solvent gradient consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water and acetonitrile. The column and autosampler were maintained at 30 °C and 20 °C, respectively [33].

Analyses of organic and fatty acids

Fatty acid extraction, identification, and quantification from Ashwagandha shoot tissue were conducted according to Torras-Claveria et al. [34]. Dried, powdered shoot material (200 mg) was extracted with 50% aqueous methanol at 25 °C, with nonadecanoic acid as an internal standard. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis was performed on a Hewlett-Packard 6890 system (Palo Alto, CA, USA) equipped with an HP-5 MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Fatty acid identification was achieved by comparing the resulting spectra against the NIST 05 and Golm Metabolome Database. Organic acid analysis was carried out via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) according to Saleh et al. [35]. A Dionex chromatographic system (Sunnyvale, CA, USA), consisting of an LPG-3400 A pump, TCC-3000SD thermostat, EWPS-3000SI autosampler, and an Ultimate 3000 LED detector, was employed. Samples (200 mg) were analyzed with an injection volume of 20 µL and eluted isocratically with 0.001 N sulfuric acid at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min⁻¹, with detection monitored at a wavelength of 210 nm. Separation was achieved using an Aminex HPH-87 H column (300 × 7.8 mm) maintained at 65 °C, preceded by a Bio-Rad IG Cation H precolumn (30 × 4.6 mm). Quantification of citric, succinic, fumaric, and malic acids was based on UV detection at 210 nm and subsequent analysis using Chromeleon v6.8 software (LaChrom, Tokyo, Japan).

Evaluation of phenol and flavonoid contents

Phenolic compound quantification was performed using ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) [36]. Dried, powdered plant material was extracted in 80% ethanol at 70 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 30 min. The resulting dry residue was accurately weighed and redissolved in HPLC-grade methanol to achieve a final crude extract concentration of 1 mg mL⁻¹ (1,000 ppm, w/v). Chromatographic separation was carried out on an Acquity UPLC system (Waters, USA) with a BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm). A binary mobile phase system consisting of ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid (eluent A) and acetonitrile (eluent B) was employed at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min with an injection volume of 20 µL. A linear gradient was applied, increasing eluent B from 3% to 100% over 10 min. 3,5-Dichloro-4-hydroxybenzoic acid served as the internal standard. Total flavonoid content was determined using a microplate AlCl₃ colorimetric assay with quercetin as the standard [37]. Total phenolic content was measured via the Folin-Ciocalteu method, using gallic acid as the reference standard [38].

HPLC analysis of withanolides level

W. somnifera shoots were extracted in methanol (analytical grade) via sonication-assisted extraction for 30 min. The resulting crude extracts were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was then collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter to estimate the contents of withanolide A, withanolide D, and withaferin A. Analysis was performed using an HPLC system (Waters 2475) equipped with a reverse-phase C-18 column (250 × 9.4 mm; 5 μm; Phenomenex Luna) subjected to binary gradient elution [39]. The concentration of withanolides was estimated by comparison with external standards, and the obtained results were expressed as mg/g DW.

Biological activities

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activities of JTT04-treated Ashwagandha shoot extracts were assessed using multiple assays, including diphenylpicrylhydrazyl (DPPH; % inhibition), ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP; µmol Fe²⁺/g DW), oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC; µmol Trolox equivalents (TE)/g DW), and inhibition of LDL oxidation (TBARS; % inhibition) and conjugated diene measurements [40–42]. In the FRAP assay, 180 µL of FRAP reagent and 20 µL of ethanolic extract were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and absorbance was recorded at 593 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy Mx, Biotek Instruments, USA), with Trolox as the reference standard. For the ORAC assay, 120 µL of fluorescein (112 nM) in phosphate buffer (75 mM) was mixed with 20 µL of sample, blank (phosphate buffer), or Trolox, followed by a 15-minute incubation at 37 °C. Subsequently, 80 µL of AAPH (62 mM) was added, and fluorescence was measured at 485/520 nm. Antioxidant capacity was calculated based on the difference between initial and final readings relative to the Trolox standard. LDL oxidation was evaluated using dialyzed LDL (100 µg protein/mL) diluted in PBS (pH 7.4), incubated at 37 °C with or without 10 µM CuSO₄, in the presence or absence of bovine colostrum proteins. Lipid peroxidation was determined by measuring TBARS at 532/600 nm using 1,1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane as a standard, and conjugated dienes were assessed at 234 nm.

Anti-inflammatory activity

Lipoxygenase (LOX) assay

Antilipoxygenase activity was evaluated using the lipoxygenase (LOX) enzyme with linoleic acid as the substrate. LOX (400 U/mL) was prepared in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 6.5). The substrate, linoleic acid, was dissolved in ethanol and diluted in the same buffer to obtain a final concentration of 0.4 mM. JTT04-treated Ashwagandha shoot extract was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; ≤1% v/v final concentration), and 10 µL of this solution was incubated with the enzyme in the dark at 25 °C for 5 min. The reaction was initiated by adding linoleic acid and allowed to proceed for 20 min at 25 °C. Subsequently, 100 µL of freshly prepared ferrous orange xylenol (FOX) reagent was added, and the mixture was further incubated for 30 min at 25 °C. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm, and the percentage of enzyme inhibition was calculated.

Evaluation of cyclooxygenase 2

Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) activity was determined using a commercial COX assay kit (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications. The reaction mixture (200 µL per well) comprised assay buffer, heme solution, COX-2 enzyme, and JTT04-treated Ashwagandha shoot extract dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; ≤1% v/v final concentration). After pre-incubation at 25 °C for 5 min, the reaction was initiated by adding arachidonic acid substrate (~ 100 µM final concentration) along with the chromogenic co-substrate. The plate was incubated at room temperature with gentle shaking for 18 h, followed by incubation in the dark for 1.5 h at 25 °C for color development. Absorbance was measured at 420 nm using a microplate reader. All assays were performed in triplicate, with DMSO as a solvent control and standard inhibitors (e.g., indomethacin or celecoxib) as positive controls.

Antibacterial activities

Antibacterial activity of ethanolic extracts from JTT04-treated Ashwagandha shoot extracts was evaluated using the disc diffusion assay against 13 bacterial strains, including Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Proteus vulgaris, and Enterobacter aerogenes. Bacterial suspensions (10⁶ CFU/mL) were uniformly spread onto Mueller-Hinton agar plates. Sterile filter paper discs impregnated with 5 µg of each extract were applied to the plates, with ethanol serving as the negative control. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, zones of inhibition were measured using a Vernier caliper [43].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R (v4.3.2), SPSS (v26.0), and GraphPad Prism (v8.4.2). All experiments were independently repeated twice, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene’s tests, respectively, in SPSS (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons, while Student’s t-test was used for pairwise differences. Histograms were created using GraphPad Prism. Principal component analysis (PCA) of continuous variables was performed in R using the “FactoMineR” and “factoextra” packages [44, 45]. Boxplots were generated with the “ggplot2” package, and correlation matrices were visualized using “corrplot” [46]. Radar plots were also constructed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Characterization and identification of the isolate

The 16S rRNA gene sequencing method is essential for accurately identifying and classifying endophytic bacteria. It has detected diverse plant-associated genera, such as Bacillus and Stenotrophomonas, known for their beneficial traits [47]. A single PCR amplicon (~ 1.5 kb) of the 16S rRNA gene was obtained and sequenced. Phylogenetic analysis performed using MEGA 7 demonstrated that the isolate JTT04 clusters within the genus Jidongwangia, showing closest similarity to Jidongwangia harbinensis at 95.90% sequence similarity (Fig. 1). These results align with recent findings highlighting medicinal plants as novel actinobacteria reservoirs. For example, Nouioui et al. [48] used genome-based taxonomy to describe Nocardia alni sp. nov. from actinorhizal nodules, supporting the potential of species like Tribulus terrestris as sources of uncharacterized, bioactive endophytes. Phenotypic analysis of strain JTT04 revealed it to be rod-shaped, Gram-positive, non-motile, oxidase- and catalase-positive, non-fermentative, and capable of producing fluorescent pigments. The strain grows in 2.5–7.5% NaCl, tolerates 30–55 °C, forms biofilms, and exhibits proteolytic activity. It also showed strong antioxidant activity (FRAP) and demonstrated plant growth-promoting traits, notably through indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production (Table 1).

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 improved the biomass of Ashwagandha

Inoculation with Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 significantly (p < 0.05) enhanced both the fresh weight (FW) and dry weight (DW) of Ashwagandha plants compared to the untreated controls (Fig. 2). Consistent with our findings, actinobacteria inoculants in Brassica species have improved biomass accumulation, germination rates, and increased both shoot and root development [27]. Actinobacterial inoculants promote plant growth through various mechanisms, including the production of phytohormones like auxins, improved uptake of nitrate, solubilization of phosphate, oxidation of sulfur, and secretion of beneficial organic compounds. These traits collectively enhance root permeability and facilitate plant-bacterial interactions, ultimately leading to improved plant growth and biomass [49]. For example, a study by Mohamad et al. [50] demonstrated that 88% of tested actinobacterial strains produced auxins, leading to significant biomass increases in tomato and cotton plants. Similarly, Saleh et al. [51] reported that endophytic actinobacteria with phosphate-solubilizing abilities enhanced nutrient uptake and promoted significant biomass accumulation. Also, enhanced nutrient use efficiency, facilitated by endophytic bacteria, has been linked to increased biomass production in sugarcane [52]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that actinobacteria contribute to robust plant development and biomass gains through nutrient mobilization, physiological regulation, and enhanced photosynthesis [53]. The improved biomass accumulation of Withania somnifera with Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 aligns with sustainable agricultural practices aimed at enhancing crop productivity while minimizing reliance on chemical fertilizers.

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 improved the accumulation of primary metabolites in Ashwagandha

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 impact on sugars

Our results indicate that Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 significantly improved carbohydrate profiles in Ashwagandha. Glucose, fructose, sucrose, and starch levels increased by 1.03-, 1.06-, 1.02-, and 1.04-fold, respectively, compared to controls. Total soluble sugars and overall carbohydrates increased by 1.06- and 1.11-fold (Fig. 3). These findings align with previous studies that reported higher soluble sugar levels following bacterial inoculation in Arabidopsis thaliana [54], strawberries [55], and lettuce [56]. This enhancement is likely due to increased starch breakdown, pectin metabolism, and better nutrient availability. Furthermore, Dermacoccus abyssi has been shown to boost soluble sugars in tomatoes under salinity stress [57]. Sugar accumulation may result from increased carbon and nitrogen uptake driven by elevated photosynthetic activity post-inoculation [58]. These sugars serve as energy sources and carbon skeletons for synthesizing essential biomolecules, such as amino acids, fatty acids, and secondary metabolites, thereby contributing to improved nutritional quality [59]. Moreover, actinobacteria enhance the solubilization of key nutrients like phosphate and zinc, which are vital for carbohydrate metabolism [60]. The accumulation of sugars and carbohydrates can improve the taste and quality of edible plant parts and enhance plant development, stress tolerance, and physiological function [58].

Jidongwangiasp. JTT04 impact on amino acids

Amino acid content is crucial for plant nutritional value [32]. For instance, methionine contributes to human health as it’s involved in choline synthesis, lipid metabolism, and antioxidant defense [61]. Also, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan are precursors for indolic and aromatic glucosinolates, while aliphatic glucosinolates derive from alanine, leucine, isoleucine, valine, and methionine [61]. Furthermore, phenylalanine is essential for phenolic biosynthesis via the phenylpropanoid pathway [62]. Our observations revealed that JTT04-treated Ashwagandha accumulated higher levels of all 20 amino acids investigated than untreated controls, which aligns with previous reports [31, 63]. The principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. 4) showed distinct clustering of amino acid profiles in Ashwagandha following treatment with Jidongwangia sp. JTT04. Clear separation was observed between the JTT04-treated and control groups. Control plants clustered tightly along the negative axis of principal component 1 (Dim1), while JTT04-treated plants clustered primarily along the positive axes of both Dim1 and Dim2, exhibiting positive associations with several amino acids, including arginine, proline, lysine, leucine, cystine, coumaric acid, caffeic acid, and phenylalanine. Endophytic actinobacteria could enhance plant amino acid contents in various ways, including increased carbohydrate availability due to enhanced photosynthesis which provides precursors for amino acid biosynthesis, improved nitrogen availability or direct excretion by the strain into the plant tissues [64], and upregulation of key enzymes involved in ammonium assimilation, such as glutamine synthetase and glutamate synthase [65]. These results highlight the potential of JTT04 as a bio-priming agent for enhancing the nutritional value of Ashwagandha.

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 impact on organic and fatty acids

Actinobacteria are well-recognized for secreting organic acids that contribute to nutrient chelation and mineral solubilization, enhancing soil fertility and plant nutrition [66, 67]. In the current study, Ashwagandha plants treated with Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 exhibited significant accumulation of several organic acids, particularly succinic and citric acids, which increased by 1.53- and 1.86-fold, respectively, compared to controls. Also, non-significant elevations in malic and fumaric acids were observed (Fig. 5). These increases may be linked to elevated carbohydrate content and enhanced tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity following inoculation, consistent with sugar accumulation patterns noted in this study [63]. Our findings align with earlier studies where actinobacterial inoculation in tomato and chili plants was associated with increased biomass and higher organic acid levels [62, 68]. Similarly, Streptomyces sp. enhanced drought tolerance in Zea mays by elevating ascorbic acid and other stress-related metabolites [69]. Actinobacteria have also been shown to release organic acids such as pyruvate, lactate, oxalate, malate, and succinate into the rhizosphere, which can be taken up and accumulated in plant tissues [70]. Moreover, strains like Streptomyces griseorubens BC10 and Nocardiopsis alba BC11 have been reported to facilitate phosphorus and potassium solubilization through organic acid-mediated metal chelation [10, 66].

In this study, inoculation with Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 significantly increased the levels of several fatty acids (µg/g DW). ) in Ashwagandha, including dodecanoic acid (C12:0; 0.236 vs. 0.149), tetradecanoic acid (C14:0; 32.608 vs. 29.211), pentadecanoic acid (C15:0; 4.768 vs. 3.111), heptadecanoic acid (C17:0; 2.561 vs. 2.013), octadecanoic acid (C18:0; 5.025 vs. 4.602), eicosanoic acid (C20:5; 0.866 vs. 0.651), tetracosanoic acid (C24:0; 0.159 vs. 0.106), tricosanoic acid (C23:0; 0.089 vs. 0.028), and pentacosanoic acid (C25:0; 7.550 vs. 3.965) (Fig. 6). These elevations are typically associated with enhanced plant vigor, as fatty acids play essential roles in stress tolerance, membrane integrity, and metabolic regulation [71]. Similarly, in Eruca sativa, Jeotgalicoccus sp. led to higher unsaturated fatty acid content and improved plant biomass and nutritional quality [72]. Also, actinobacterial inoculants enhanced the fatty acid composition in oilseed crops such as Glycine max and Brassica napus [73, 74]. In Carthamus tinctorius (safflower), such treatments increased unsaturated while reducing saturated fatty acids, a shift linked to plant and health [75]. Conversely, our JTT04 strain resulted in reduced levels of certain fatty acids, including tetradecanoic (C14:0), pentadecanoic (C15:0), tetracosanoic (C21:0), and octadecanoic (C18:0). These reductions may be attributed to the reallocation of metabolic resources from primary pathways like fatty acid synthesis to the production of secondary metabolites involved in defense, such as phenolics and flavonoids [76]. Additionally, oxidative stress induced by beneficial microbes can cause lipid peroxidation, further depleting fatty acid pools [77]. Moreover, fatty acids like linolenic acid also serve as precursors for defense signaling molecules such as jasmonic acid, and their increased conversion during plant–microbe interactions may further reduce their availability [78].

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 improved secondary metabolites in Ashwagandha

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 impact on phenolics and flavonoids

Actinobacterial inoculations have been shown to significantly enhance the accumulation of phenolic acids and flavonoids, key components of plant defense responses [79, 80]. Phenols and flavonoids are also major factors in the actinobacterial potential as biocontrol agents contributing to disease resistance and growth promotion [37, 81]. Our results showed that, except for a non-significant reduction in gallic acid, the remaining phenolic acids were significantly improved after treating Ashwagandha with our JTT04 strain (Fig. 4). p-Coumaric acid, rosmarinic acid, and caffeic acids exhibited the highest increases by 2.4-, 1.9-, and 1.3-fold compared to uninoculated controls. Also, JTT04-treated Ashwagandha showed increases in all eight investigated flavonoids, with rutin, luteolin, and kaempferol showing the highest fold-increases, with 5-, 2.16-, and 1.4-fold, respectively. The PCA analysis (Fig. 4) showed that the enhanced individual components of the phenolic-flavonoid profile were separated towards the JTT04-treated Ashwagandha along the PCA vertical axis, with a total variance of 98.8%. Our results align with previous studies reporting increased phenolic acid and flavonoid accumulation in actinobacteria-treated maize and shallot plants [82, 83]. Moreover, the observed increase in phenolic compounds may be linked to elevated phenylalanine levels, as phenylalanine serves as the direct substrate for phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), a pivotal enzyme in the phenolic biosynthetic pathway. Similar positive associations were reported between polyphenolic accumulation and PAL activity under actinobacterial influence [84, 85]. The enhanced accumulation of phenolic acids and flavonoids following actinobacterial treatment may, in part, result from increased photosynthetic activity that supplies essential precursors and energy for their biosynthesis [86]. Specifically, sugars derived from glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway supply carbon skeletons and NADPH required for phenolic acid production [59]. Additionally, actinobacteria may directly secrete these secondary metabolites or stimulate their accumulation within plant tissues [86]. In this study, the accumulation of phenolic compounds following JTT04 inoculation may contribute to improved defense responses, antioxidant activity, hypocholesterolemic potential, and intercellular signaling in W. somnifera [87].

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 impact on withanolides

According to the results of withanolides, Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 significantly enhanced withanolides levels in W. somnifera shoots (Table 2). Compared to the control group, inoculation with JTT04 resulted in a 53.7% increase in withaferin A content, while withanolide A and withanolide D showed remarkable increases of 85.7% and 87.9%, respectively, nearly doubling their concentrations even at the juvenile seedling stage. From a biological perspective, this enrichment is highly significant as these compounds collectively contribute to the plant’s nutritive and medical properties [1–4]. Specifically, the 53.7% surge in Withaferin A directly underpins the potent inhibition of COX-2 and LOX enzymes observed in functional assays, while the near-doubling of Withanolide A and D—markers for neuroregeneration and shoot quality, suggests that JTT04 acts as a high-efficiency biological elicitor [1–4]. By redirecting host metabolic flux toward the isoprenoid and phenylpropanoid pathways, JTT04 fundamentally reprograms the host’s internal ‘chemical factory,’ transforming young seedlings into potent bio-factories of therapeutic compounds and significantly increasing their pharmaceutical grade.

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 impact on antioxidant capacity

The biological activity of medicinal plants is largely linked to their secondary metabolites, particularly phenolics and flavonoids. Endophytic actinobacteria have been shown to enhance these compounds, improving antioxidant capacity, stress tolerance, and associated health benefits [88, 89]. In this study, Ashwagandha treated with our JTT04 actinobacterial strain showed improved antioxidant activity. FRAP and ORAC assays revealed 1.51- and 1.13-fold increases, respectively (Fig. 7). Although not statistically significant, lipid peroxidation (TBARS) and hemolysis inhibition also increased by 1.21- and 1.22-fold. Anti-inflammatory activity was evident as JTT04 treatment reduced IC₅₀ values of cyclooxygenase-2 and lipoxygenase by 2.12- and 1.78-fold, respectively.

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 impact on antimicrobial activity

The ability of actinobacteria to produce bioactive secondary metabolites with potent antimicrobial properties against a wide range of pathogenic microorganisms underscores their potential role in suppressing plant pathogens and enhancing overall plant health and quality [97–99]. The antimicrobial effects of these secondary metabolites involve a multi-targeted mechanism, including the disruption of bacterial cell membranes, leading to the leakage of essential intracellular contents, and the inhibition of key enzymatic processes vital for growth and replication. Additionally, these actinobacteria-derived compounds interfere with quorum sensing pathways, which suppresses biofilm formation and increases bacterial susceptibility to treatments [100, 101]. In the present study, we assessed the antibacterial activity of control and JTT04-treated Ashwagandha plant extracts against eleven pathogenic bacterial strains using the agar well diffusion method, and the mean inhibition zone diameters (in mm) were measured (Table 3). The results demonstrated that inoculation with the JTT04 strain generally enhanced the antibacterial efficacy of Ashwagandha extracts. Notably, the most significant increases in inhibition zones were observed against Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Streptococcus salivarius, Enterobacter aerogenes, and Serratia marcescens. These findings are consistent with previous reports. For instance, Abdelgawad et al. [27] showed that extracts of Brassica oleracea sprouts treated with Nocardiopsis species exhibited enhanced antimicrobial activity against a range of pathogens, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Aspergillus flavus, Escherichia coli, S. saprophyticus, S. salivarius, and Candida glabrata. Similarly, actinobacterial endophytes have been shown to bolster chickpea plant resistance against Botrytis cinerea through enhanced plant defense responses [102]. In our study, the increased antibacterial activity observed in JTT04-treated Ashwagandha extracts may be linked to elevated levels of phenolic compounds following bacterial inoculation. This is supported by previous findings where inoculation with Streptomyces sp. in chickpea plants led to increased phenolic content and activation of antioxidant enzymes, contributing to improved plant defense and pathogen resistance [102].

Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 impact on inflammatory activity

Inoculation with Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 significantly enhanced the medicinal quality of Withania somnifera by suppressing key pro-inflammatory enzymes. The bacterial treatment reduced COX-2 activity by approximately 50% (4.0 to 1.9 µg/mL; p < 0.01) and LOX activity from 9.2 to 5.1 µg/mL (p < 0.001). This downregulation suggests that the bioinoculant acts as a metabolic elicitor, likely triggering the accumulation of withanolides that inhibit the arachidonic acid cascade. Ultimately, these results demonstrate that Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 does not just promote growth but actively “supercharges” the plant’s pharmaceutical potency by reducing the baseline levels of inflammatory markers.

Conclusion

This study highlights the biofortification potential of Jidongwangia sp. JTT04, an endophytic actinobacterium isolated from Tribulus terrestris, in enhancing the growth, biochemical composition, and biological activity of Ashwagandha plants. Inoculation with JTT04 significantly improved plant biomass, sugar and amino acid profiles, organic and fatty acid accumulation, and secondary metabolite content, including phenolic acids and flavonoids. These enhancements were associated with improved antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, as well as increased antibacterial efficacy against several pathogenic strains. The observed metabolic and functional improvements suggest that JTT04 inoculation modulates key physiological pathways in Ashwagandha, promoting plant development and boosting its medicinal value. The ability of this strain to produce phytohormones, solubilize nutrients, and trigger systemic responses in the host plant underscores its potential as a multifunctional bioinoculant. Overall, our findings demonstrate that endophytic actinobacteria such as Jidongwangia sp. JTT04 can serve as sustainable biotechnological tools for improving the yield and phytochemical quality of medicinal plants. Further field-based studies and molecular analyses are warranted to validate these effects under diverse environmental conditions and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of plant–microbe interactions.
