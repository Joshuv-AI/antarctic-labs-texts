The First Report on Cat Flea (Ctenocephalides felis), a Zoonotic Haematophagous Insect Infestation in Humans in Bangladesh: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Nusrat Nowrin Shohana, Md Shahadat Hossain, Sharmin Shahid Labony, Md Haydar Ali, Md Abdul Alim, Ashim Kumar Nandi, Anisuzzaman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe cat flea (Ctenocephalides felis) is a zoonotic insect that commonly infests cats. Unlike lice, they have a wide host range and can infest a variety of mammals, including humans. They can be vectors of many bacterial, viral and rickettsial diseases. Here, we report and describe a case of cat flea infestation in a human, which led to severe pruritus, urticaria and papulomatous lesions. An 18‐year‐old girl who had no pets but was infested with C. felis. The patient had typical flea‐bite dermatitis (FBD) on the neck and arm. The flea was removed and identified as C. felis. The patient was advised to take levocetirizine once daily for five days, which led to satisfactory recovery from FBD.Keywords: Ctenocephalides felis, flea‐bite dermatitis, human infestation

Introduction

Fleas are small, wingless, obligatory haematophagous insects of the order Siphonaptera. Their bodies are uniquely bilaterally compressed and oval‐shaped, with long hind legs adapted for jumping. The order Siphonaptera includes 16 families, 238 genera and approximately 2574 species that infest all vertebrates (Lewis 1999). The cat flea (Ctenocephalides felis) is assumed to be the most widely distributed species on earth. C. felis was first recorded in Africa, but they are now distributed worldwide, including in Austria, the USA, Germany and the United Kingdom (Rust 2017; Duscher et al. 2018). The cat flea has a euryxenous host preference, including cats, dogs, goats, cattle and sheep (Clark et al. 2018). In addition, cat fleas (C. felis) occasionally infest humans (Lawrence et al. 2019; Youssefi and Rahimi 2014). Their bites are painful and cause flea‐bite dermatitis (FBD) (Blagburn and Dryden 2009; Youssefi and Rahimi 2014). In addition to the direct skin injury, they have been reported to act as potential vectors of Rickettsia typhi causing endemic typhus, Bartonella spp., murine typhus, Coxiella burnetii, Dipylidium caninum and Rickettsia felis causing cat‐flea typhus/flea‐borne spotted fever in humans (Pe´rez‐Osorio et al. 2008; Duscher et al. 2018). The diseases transmitted by the cat flea infect both humans and animals. The flea has been reported from cats in Bangladesh, but human infestation is yet to be detected in the country. Here, we report a case of cat flea infestation in a human with its clinical manifestation and management.

Case History

An 18‐year‐old girl visited my clinic on 5 April 2025, with complaints of having insect bites on the neck and arm. She described a painful bite and severe itching. On close inspection, wide, pink to reddish lesions (0.3‐0.5 cm diameter) similar to urticaria were found on the affected elbow and neck region. She also complained about severe itching for 2 days, which became worse at night. Notably, other family members of the patient were not affected. The patient did not have any pet dogs or cats. However, one resident on the fourth floor of the same building had several cats. The patient lived on the third floor, and the cat owner frequently visited the patient's house.

Diagnosis of the Case

The patient successfully caught two arthropods. The arthropods were sent to the Diagnostic Parasitology Laboratory, Department of Parasitology, Faculty of Veterinary Science (FVS), Bangladesh Agricultural University (BAU), Mymensingh, Bangladesh. The parasites were initially examined under a dissecting microscope at low power magnification (4× objective). The initial examination showed their body was divided into three major divisions: head, thorax and abdomen, and three pairs of legs arose from the thorax, indicating they were insects. The insects were laterally compressed, wingless, and the last pair of legs were the longest and strongest. They had an abdomen with 10 segments, and the ninth segment bears a sensilium or pygidium (Figure 1A), which suggests they are fleas (Insecta: Siphonaptera). Then one of the fleas was treated with 10% KOH for 2 hours and dehydrated using a series of gradient concentrations of ethanol (50%–100%) (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Then the sample was treated with aniline oil, washed with xylene and mounted with Canada balsam. The slide was examined by a board of five parasitologists from the Department of Parasitology, FVS and BAU.

During examination, the flea was measured to be 2 mm long. The head of the flea was elongated with the presence of both genal and pronotal combs, indicating that it belonged to the genus Ctenocephalides (Siphonaptera: Ctenocephalidae). The genal comb consisted of seven spines, and the pronotal comb consisted of 16 spines. The length of the first and second spines of the genal comb was almost equal in length. Besides, the dorsal margin of the hind tibia had only six notches, bearing setae (Figure 1B,C). All the morphological features were exactly conformatory to the morphological characteristics of C. felis as described previously by Wall and Shearer (1997), indicating the arthropod as a cat flea. Therefore, the case was diagnosed as a ‘cat FBD’ (Figure 1D).

Treatment and Follow‐Up of the Case and Control Measures

The patient was advised to take levocetirizine once daily for five days. After treatment, the lesion of FBD subsided gradually and was fully cured on Day 5 of initial treatment. The veterinary team advised the patient's guardian (father) to use ‘aerosol insecticide spray’, containing 0.20% (w/w) prallethrin and 0.15% (w/w) D‐Phenothrin on the floor, walls, furniture and beds. He was also advised to repeat spraying once again after 2 weeks.

Discussion

The cat flea is an obligatory haematophagous insect that lives on a blood meal from warm‐blooded animals, most preferably from cats (Rust 2017). Besides, several reports suggest that the cat flea also infests feral animals such as opossums, foxes, rats, mongooses and hedgehogs (Hernández‐Camacho et al. 2016). Although fleas have host preferences, they are not strictly host‐specific. The cat flea infestation in humans has usually been reported among people who keep cats and/or share the same living space. Humans or other unnatural hosts are infested when natural hosts are unavailable. Infested humans can carry the fleas from one place to another, even to a house free from cats (Chin et al. 2010; Leelavathi et al. 2012; Youssefi and Rahimi 2014). From the circumstantial evidence of this study, we concluded that the cat owner living in the same building possibly carried the fleas during her visit to the patient's house. Through an in‐depth history of the case, we also learnt that the cat owner frequently sat on the patient's bed and spent 1–3 h by gossiping for several days. Possibly the fleas left the cat owner's clothing or body and entered the patient's bedding, and eventually caused infestation. Cat fleas can survive for 2–3 days off the host in the environment, such as in clothes, on the floor and on furniture. Human‐mediated dispersal of the C. felis has been reported even from one country to another country (Lawrence et al. 2019). There are a few reports available on ‘flea‐bite‐dermatitis’ in humans in different countries, such as in India (Fular et al. 2020), Malaysia (Leelavathi et al. 2012), Iran (Youssefi, Ebrahimpour, et al. 2014), the USA (Ferreira et al. 2020) and Australia (Lawrence et al. 2019). Even a mass flea outbreak had been reported at a childcare facility in northeast Mississippi, USA (Corpus and Corpus 1991).

In the present case, we found pink to reddish urticaria‐like lesions at the flea‐biting sites. During feeding on a blood meal from the hosts, fleas pierce the hosts’ skin with highly specialized mouthparts and cause mechanical damage to the skin. During feeding, they secrete various biochemical substances like proteins (e.g., platelet‐activating factor acetylhydrolase) and salivary allergens (e.g., Ctef1), and inject them into the biting site with their saliva. The sialotranscriptome analysis of the cat flea showed that the saliva also contains phosphatase, esterase, apyrase and defensin, which are believed to hamper host haemostasis. Along with biting insults, flea salivary allergens are believed to have roles in the development of clinical features of FBD (Ribeiro et al. 2012). Papular urticaria developed due to an allergic reaction to a flea bite, which can last for several weeks.

Conclusions

The authors concluded that the cat flea infestation is independent of cat rearing. If pet owners remain reluctant about routine cleanliness and insecticide treatment of their pets, it imposes a probable public health risk. This is the first report of identifying C. felis infestation in humans in Bangladesh with apparent signs of FBD and its management. Awareness must be built up among pet owners, One Health workers, and field veterinarians about the zoonotic impacts of the cat flea.
