Automated high-resolution CT analysis outperforms visual assessment in predicting interstitial lung disease progression in SSc
Authors: Francesca Motta, Antonio Tonutti, Federica Catapano, Gianluca Sellaro, Francesco Amati, Anna Stainer, Calogero Messana, Stefano Aliberti, Marco Francone, Carlo Selmi, Maria De Santis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesProgressive interstitial lung disease (ILD) is the leading cause of mortality in SSc. Early recognition of progression is crucial to timely treatment, but reliable early markers are lacking. Automated quantitative analysis of chest high-resolution CT (HRCT) may provide an objective tool to detect early progression. This study aims to compare automated and visual assessment in identifying early SSc-ILD progression and evaluate their predictive value for subsequent functional decline.MethodsIn this retrospective longitudinal study, 33 patients with SSc-ILD underwent HRCT and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) at baseline (T1) and after 1 year (T2; IQR 0.75–2.5 years). PFTs were repeated 12 months after T2 (T3; IQR 9–14.5 months, T3). Two radiologists performed blinded semi-quantitative visual scoring of fibrosis and ground-glass opacity (GGO), while automated analysis was conducted using Thoracic VCAR (GE Healthcare). Associations with PFTs and prediction of functional progression (Erice/INBUILD criteria) and treatment escalation were analysed.ResultsBoth visual and automated analyses detected fibrosis progression (visual: 1.5 → 1.6, P = 0.0005; software: 0.34% → 0.4%, P = 0.01). Only automated analyses identified increases in GGO (15.5% → 15.8%, P = 0.04) and reduced normal lung tissue (75.6% → 73%, P = 0.009). Software-derived parameters correlated strongly with PFT changes, whereas visual scores showed weaker correlations. Early (T1–T2) automatically-detected GGO increases predicted subsequent functional decline at T3 (P = 0.04), with changes >50 ml (8.2%) yielding 81% sensitivity and 77% specificity.ConclusionAutomated quantitative HRCT analysis, but not visual evaluation, predicts long-term functional progression in SSc-ILD.

Introduction

Interstitial lung disease (ILD) accounts for ∼35% of SSc related deaths [1, 2], and high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest reveals abnormalities in up to 65% of patients [1]. In some cases, SSc-ILD remains stable even without treatment, while in others it progresses with different rates over time [1, 3, 4].

As fibrotic changes are predominantly irreversible, monitoring for early ILD progression is essential to escalate treatment or adapt the management strategy [2], but reliable methods or biomarkers for predicting the progression of SSc-ILD are lacking, despite ongoing research efforts [2, 5–7]. Nevertheless, evidence of ILD progression is recommended as the major driver in deciding treatment changes [8], as it significantly impacts patient prognosis, affecting both morbidity and mortality [3]. Different sets of criteria to establish progression of SSc-ILD have been proposed, encompassing the OMERACT initiative [9], the minimal clinical important worsening (MCIW) of forced vital capacity (FVC) [10], the EUSTAR criteria [11], the Erice or INBUILD criteria [12], and the ATS/ERS/JRS/ALAT definition of progressive pulmonary fibrosis [13]. Although partly overlapping, these criteria are not interchangeable; real-life data indicate that most patients meet more than one definition of progression simultaneously. Notably, Erice and MCIW FVC criteria encompass the progression definitions proposed by the other criteria, while also identifying patients not captured by any alternative definition [14].

Furthermore, technical issues, diurnal or seasonal variations, patient-related factors like fatigue or cough, and extra-parenchymal complications including muscle weakness may affect the results of pulmonary function tests (PFTs) in a non-specific manner [5]. Last, the threshold for functional progression adopted in the majority of available scores is derived from idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a disease model with very different aetiology, pathogenesis, progression rate and mortality compared with SSc-ILD [14]. Therefore, there is an unmet need to identify tools capable of predicting the progression in patients with SSc-ILD and to stratify them according to the need for treatment escalation.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to aid in the identification and quantification of different morphological patterns on medical images, although its use is still limited due to the lack of consistent evidence [15]. However, automated quantification of affected lung parenchyma proved as an independent predictor of clinical deterioration in COVID-19 pneumonia [16], outperforming radiologist assessments [17]. Furthermore, AI-assisted quantification of lung alterations showed good correlations with PFT [18]. In SSc-ILD, automated quantification can support the interpretation of radiological images [19], along with quantifying ILD extent, avoiding the variability linked to visual scoring systems currently used [20, 21]. These methods, such as Kazerooni scoring [22] may overlook the presence of inflammatory and fibrotic patterns. These limitations highlight the need for more reproducible approaches, and automated analysis holds promise for overcoming these challenges.

This study aims to determine whether the software-assisted automated analysis of HRCT is more sensitive than visual scoring in detecting early signs of SSc-ILD progression, along with predicting functional deterioration.

Materials and methods

Patients

We retrospectively enrolled 33 consecutive SSc-ILD patients, followed at IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital (Rozzano, Milan) between 2015 and 2025. Patients aged ≥18 years were included if they fulfilled 2013 SSc classification criteria [23] and had SSc-ILD defined on HRCT scan [24]. Patients were excluded if they had concomitant pulmonary diseases potentially impacting HRCT scan and PFT evaluation.

The study was conducted in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration and the local Ethic Committee ‘Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 5’ approved the study (protocol n° ICH 831); all participants gave written informed consent for the study.

A comprehensive disease assessment including demographic, disease-related and therapeutic history was available for all patients at baseline (T1); information on PFT, chest HRCT and therapeutic history was collected at T1 and after 12 ± 3 months (T2). PFTs were also evaluated at a third timepoint (T3) after at least 6 months from T2, whenever available.

Disease duration was calculated from the first non-Raynaud’s manifestation to the first performed CT scan; patients with a disease duration lower than 3 years were considered to have early disease. Disease subsets were defined as ‘limited’ or ‘diffuse’ according to LeRoy classification [25]. Organ involvement assessment was evaluated per clinical practice.

Pulmonary function tests

PFTs were locally performed by trained pulmonologists with flow-sensing computer-connected spirometers (CareFusion) and provided measurements including predicted values of FVC (in litres and as percentage of the predicted), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1, in litres and percentage of predicted), diffusion lung capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO; in ml/min/mmHg and percentage of predicted) and DLCO divided by the alveolar volume (DLCO/VA, or KCO, in ml/min/mmHg/l and percentage of predicted), according to the ATS/ERS standards [26].

Chest HRCT—image acquisition and analyses

Chest HRCT was performed using either a 256-slice scanner (GE Revolution) or a 128-slice (GE Revolution Evo) scanner, with tube voltages of 100–120 kV and thin-section images reconstructed with a 0.6–1.0 mm increment. All scans were acquired in full inspiration, without intravenous contrast and with the patient in the supine position.

Two experienced pulmonary radiologists independently performed semi-quantitative visual scoring of ground-glass opacities (GGOs) and fibrotic changes in each of the five pulmonary lobes. Scoring was conducted according to the algorithm described by Kazerooni et al. [22], using a standardized 6-point scale. Briefly, the fibrosis score was based on the extent of interlobular septal thickening and honeycombing, defined as follows: 0 if no fibrosis; 1 if septal thickening without honeycombing; 2 if honeycombing involving <25% of the lobe; 3 if 25–49%; 4 if 50–74% and 5 if ≥75%. Discrepancies between the two radiologists were resolved by consensus. The interobserver agreement before consensus was moderate (Cohen’s κ = 0.59), in line with previously reported variability in visual interpretation of ILD extent. Visual scores for GGO and fibrosis were assigned per lobe, converted to the midpoint of the corresponding percentage range and then averaged across all lobes to estimate total lung involvement for each pattern. The final score was the average of the scores obtained in all lung lobes.

Quantitative analysis was performed using the Thoracic VCAR software (AW SERVER v.3.2, GE Healthcare, USA), which automatically segments lung parenchyma and quantifies the volume and percentage of five predefined components: hyperinflated lung (‘emphysema’, including also bronchiectasis and honeycombing alterations), GGO, reticulation (‘other’, encompassing reticular and reticulo-nodular alterations suggestive of fibrosis, and hereafter referred to as ‘fibrotic’), consolidation and normal lung. Among the 66 HRCT scans analysed (33 patients, two timepoints each), 12 scans required manual corrections of the automated segmentation mask due to suboptimal delineation.

Segmentation is based on predefined Hounsfield Unit (HU) thresholds (Fig. 1) as follows: hyperinflated lung: −1024 to −977 HU; GGO: −703 to −368 HU; fibrotic reticulation (‘other’): −367 to −250 HU; consolidation: −249 to +100 HU; normal lung: −950 to −701 HU. These data were provided in the software output file, and the original HRCT scan was overlaid with colour-coded enhancement according to the identified lung pattern in each zone, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Each lung segment corresponded to a pulmonary lobe, and each radiological pattern (emphysema, GGO, ‘other’, consolidation and normal lung) was automatically quantified as both percentage and absolute volume. Final scores for each pattern were calculated as the average of the corresponding scores across all lung lobes.

The long inclusion period and the use of two different multidetector CT scanners, together with potential evolution of acquisition and reconstruction protocols over time, may introduce a degree of variability in quantitative CT measures and may affect threshold-based classification approaches, especially for categories such as GGO and fibrotic patterns. To mitigate this issue, all scans were acquired following institutional HRCT protocols, with efforts to maintain consistent acquisition and reconstruction parameters over the study period, and follow-up examinations for each patient were preferentially acquired using the same scanner when feasible.

Outcomes

Patients were classified as having progressive or non-progressive ILD based on two separate functional criteria, achieving identical results. The EUSTAR criteria [11] define progression over a 12-month period, as either: (a) an absolute FVC decline ≥10% or (b) an absolute FVC decline of 5–9% with a concomitant reduction of DLCO ≥15%. When considering a 2-year range, we referred to the functional domains of the Erice/INBUILD criteria, which define progression as either (a) a relative FVC decline of ≥10%, or (b) a relative FVC decline of 5–9% accompanied by a concomitant DLCO reduction of ≥15% [12, 27]. One major advantage is that these two definitions identified the same subgroup of patients in our cohort as ‘progressors’ at every timepoint analysed, whom we refer to as the ‘EUSTAR/Erice progressors’.

The MCIW of FVC was also adopted, mirroring an absolute FVC decline ≥3% [10], enabling the identification of a larger number of patients as ‘progressors’ at each timepoint, thus offering greater sensitivity.

Primary end point of our study was to compare automated analysis with traditional visual scoring in detecting signs of SSc-ILD progression, defined according to functional criteria, and to assess whether such signs are predictive of subsequent functional deterioration. Secondary endpoints were the correlations between PFT and chest HRCT visual and automated analysis and the consistency between software and visual quantification of GGO and fibrosis.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as number (%), while quantitative variables are reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-parametric data, and repetitive measurements were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To detect significant correlations between variables, Spearman’s correlation coefficient was calculated, with r = 1 denoting a perfect positive correlation and r = −1 a perfect negative correlation.

The concordance between visual score and software-assisted analysis of chest HRCT was assessed using the Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (ρ), with values of ρ < 0.2 indicating poor concordance, while values >0.8 indicate excellent concordance.

Multivariable analysis was performed with stepwise logistic regression, as appropriate according to the outcomes (Erice/MCIW progression and treatment escalation at T2 or T3). Covariates included disease characteristics, visual scores and software-assisted score showing a P-value ≤0.1 at univariable regression. Odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% CIs were calculated. Analysis of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding areas under the curve (AUC) was performed to assess the performance of software-assisted score variation of HRCT in predicting functional progression. An AUC value >0.70 was deemed indicative for accuracy.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Software 9.5.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA) and StataSE/18.0 for Windows (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Correlation between PFT and chest HRCT

At both timepoints, software-derived HRCT analysis correlated with PFTs, particularly with FVC and DLCO, whereas visual scores showed only minor correlations (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2).

T1–T2 changes in PFT and chest HRCT

All 33 patients underwent chest HRCT at T1 and T2, median time between the two timepoints was 1 year (IQR 0.75–2.5). Among PFT parameters, only FVC (% predicted) showed a statistically significant reduction from T1 [88% (IQR 71–101)] to T2 [81% (IQR 69–93); P = 0.04].

Significant changes in visual scores and software-assisted analysis were observed comparing HRCT scans. According to visual scores, both GGO (1.35–1.57, P = 0.06) and fibrotic alterations increased (1.5–1.6, P = 0.0005). Software analysis revealed GGO increase (15.5–15.8%, P = 0.04, volume 0.51–0.54 l, P not significant), reticulation (fibrosis) increase (0.34–0.4%, P = 0.01, volume 0.07–0.08 l, P not significant), as well as consolidation (0.56–0.61%, P = 0.0007, volume 0.02–0.03 l, P = 0.03) and emphysema (1.54–2.56%, P = 0.05, volume 0.02–0.04 l, P = 0.06) increase; a reduction of normal lung (75.6–73%, P = 0.009, volume 2.89–2.52 l, P = 0.006) was detected.

Progression and change of treatment from T1 to T2

For nine patients, PFT data were unavailable at T2 for the definition of progression and were excluded from the longitudinal analysis of progression but, since data on treatment changes were available, they were considered in the analysis of treatments. Demographic and disease features did not differ among progressors and non-progressors, either when defined according to EUSTAR/Erice criteria (n = 16 vs 8, Table 2) or MCIW FVC (n = 17 vs 7, Supplementary Table S3). Both sets of criteria were concordant, as shown by the Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.33 (P = 0.04). The only significant differences were noted in FVC (both % and volume), as expected per the established definitions of progression.

Visual scores and software-assisted scores did not differ between progressors and non-progressors at both T1 and T2. Consistently, the intra-individual variation of visual and software-assisted scores from T1 to T2 was not different between progressors and non-progressors, independently from the adopted definition (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S3). No difference was found also when comparing patients who changed and did not change treatment from T1 to T2. Notably, patients changing treatment from T1 to T2 were younger, had a lower disease duration and more commonly diffuse disease. At multivariable analysis, only disease duration significantly predicted treatment change (P = 0.03, OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.412–0.955).

T1–T2–T3 outcomes

Twenty-eight/33 patients (85%) had available data at T3, representing a median period of 12 months from T2 (IQR 9–14.5) and 27 months from T1 (IQR 20.5–41). Nineteen/28 (68%) had available PFT at T3. One patient had died from T2 to T3 due to SSc-ILD. Patients’ data at T3 were analysed and compared with those at T1 and T2.

Between T2 and T3, clinical and demographic features remained similar in progressors compared with non-progressors defined according to both EUSTAR/Erice criteria (n = 6 vs 13, Table 3) and MCIW FVC (n = 10, Supplementary Table S4). Agreement between the two sets of criteria is shown by a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.57 (P = 0.003). Similarly, no differences were noted in patients changing vs not changing treatment from T2 to T3 (n = 8 vs 17). Regarding HRCT, the change (delta) in the visual GGO score from T1 to T2 was greater in patients who progressed according to EUSTAR/Erice criteria from T2 to T3. Similarly, an increase of fibrotic reticulations according to software-assisted interpretation predicted later functional progression of SSc-ILD. These significances were lost at multivariable analysis when adjusted for sex, age and disease duration.

The change in fibrosis visual score from T1 to T2 was greater in patients who subsequently changed treatment from T2 to T3, although not reaching statistical significance. Conversely, normal lung (both volume and %) significantly decreased, while GGO and fibrotic reticulations significantly increased from T1 to T2 in patients who subsequently changed treatment (Table 3). However, no significance was retained after adjusting for sex, age and disease duration.

Similar observations were made when assessing progression from T2 to T3 according to MCIW FVC (Supplementary Table S4).

When we analysed changes between T1 and T3 (median 27 months, IQR 20.5–41), patients progressing according to Erice criteria were less frequently on MMF, at lower doses (although not significantly different), and had shown a significantly greater increase in GGO from T1 to T2 according to software-assisted analysis of HRCT (P = 0.005, Table 4). Similar results were obtained when progression was assessed according to MCIW FVC (Supplementary Table S5), agreement with EUSTAR/Erice criteria, as indicated by a Cohen’s kappa coefficient (0.69, P = 0.0009). These results were confirmed after adjusting for age, sex and disease duration, with software-detected increases in GGO from T1 to T2 predicting functional ILD progression at T3 (P = 0.04, OR = 9 × 107 (95% CI 1.37–5.9 × 1015)). Serum anti-Scl70 antibodies and treatment with MMF were associated with an OR of 0 for progression, corresponding to a 100% negative predictive value in this cohort. Of note, when adjusting for ongoing treatments (any, ever in the timeframe T1–T3, n = 22/33), multivariable analysis was still statistically significant (P = 0.02), with software-detected GGO volume increase from T1 to T2 almost reaching significance for predicting ILD progression at T3 (P = 0.051, OR 7.46 × 107 (95% CI: 0.93–5.95 × 1015)). Similarly, in a model considering adjustment for treatment changes at T2 (n = 13/33 patients), multivariable analysis was significant (P = 0.02), with GGO volume increase still reaching significance for predicting ILD progression at T3 (P = 0.053, OR 8.31 × 107 (95% CI: 0.81–8.42 × 1015)), suggesting that patients who had a functional progression from T1 to T3, but escalated treatment at T2 (n = 3/11), had some benefit on progression. Multivariable analyses considering MCIW FVC showed similar results (not shown).

When considering the whole cohort of patients, variation in GGO volume from T1 to T2 calculated by Thoracic VCAR software accurately predicted ILD functional progression from T1 to T3. In detail, an increase in GGO volume >50 ml (corresponding to 8.2%) from T1 to T2 predicted ILD functional progression from T1 to T3 with a sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 77%, reaching an AUC-ROC of 0.85, 95% CI 0.68–1.00 (P = 0.03) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that software-assisted but not visual-only analysis of serial HRCT can predict PFT deterioration in SSc-ILD. Further, automated HRCT analysis showed better correlations with PFT, compared with traditional visual scoring.

Given the current lack of reliable predictors for functional decline [28], decision-making process for management of SSc-ILD might benefit from software-assisted evaluation, rather than waiting until functional decline becomes evident.

To date, only few studies have investigated automated analysis of SSc-ILD, whereas most research has focused on the umbrella of ILD. Some studies have explored the application of deep learning methods for the recognition of lung features [19], while others have examined the extent of SSc pulmonary fibrosis [29]. SSc-ILD is characterized by the co-existence of both inflammatory and fibrotic changes, mirroring the presence of a reversible and irreversible counterparts [30, 31]. Therefore, serial HRCT monitoring through automated analysis could help determine whether immunosuppressive and antifibrotic treatments can reverse or stabilize specific radiological patterns. Conflicting results have been reported regarding the correlation between AI-assisted analysis and visual scoring, with some studies showing agreement [29], while others, including our cohort, did not [21]. Nonetheless, different tools have been used across studies, further complicating direct comparisons.

We acknowledge some limitations of our study. First, the preliminary nature of the evidence, limited by small sample size and study design. The retrospective, real-word, longitudinal design of the study within over a 10-year time period might have introduced selection bias and sources of heterogeneity. In particular, variations in CT acquisition protocols and treatment strategies over the observation period may have influenced the clinical outcomes and the quantitative imaging outputs. Although efforts were made to maintain consistency in imaging protocols, some degree of variability is unavoidable, impairing the interpretation of quantitative thresholds and their predictive performance. Another limitation is the lack of a control group, which would have strengthened the scientific value of the study, improving the generalizability of the automated analyses. Therefore, our findings should be considered promising proof-of-concept evidence that requires validation. A second limitation concerns the software used, which was not a learning-based model and was not specifically designed for ILD. Dedicated investigations using machine learning tools tailored to ILD assessment are warranted to draw more definitive conclusions. There remains also uncertainty for the interpretation of certain parameters, particularly ‘consolidation’, which can include different parenchymal abnormalities. Since this pattern did not show statistically significant associations in our analysis, its impact can be considered minor. Moreover, we did not evaluate the vascular component, which may be prominent in SSc patients with pulmonary hypertension and has been successfully evaluated with other software [21]. Furthermore, the thoracic VCAR software used in this study is a vendor-specific post-processing tool; it is designed to analyse standard DICOM images and can be applied to CT scans acquired from different scanners. However, we acknowledge that variability related to scanner type and acquisition settings may influence quantitative results, limitating the extension to multicentre settings. Another relevant point concerns the independence of the threshold used. HRCT metrics cannot be considered fully independent of vendor-specific implementations. Different vendors may adopt distinct approaches to identify and quantify lung features, potentially leading to systematic variability. As a consequence, our cut-offs cannot be assumed to be directly transferable to other software platforms without external validation. Therefore, future studies in larger, prospective cohorts are needed to specifically address inter-scanner variability, software-related differences and to implement harmonization strategies essential to validate and standardize automated analysis across platforms before use in clinical practice.

In conclusion, similar to what is being developed in other areas of clinical research [32–34], AI-driven tools might offer the potential to improve monitoring accuracy and reduce interobserver variability in patients with SSc. In clinical practice, the most promising approach is likely a synergistic ‘human-in-the-loop’ model [35], in which clinicians and radiologists integrate their expertise with the software’s outputs. Such collaboration could potentially yield even higher accuracy, enhance clinical decision-making, and ultimately reduce mortality in patients with SSc-ILD.
