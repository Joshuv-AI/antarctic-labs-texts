Background

Uterine rupture (UR) during pregnancy poses a significant risk of perinatal and maternal morbidity and mortality [1]. It involves a complete tear in the uterine wall, including the outer layer (serosa). The median occurrence rate of this condition is estimated to be 5.3 per 10,000 total births, and is strongly linked to the presence of uterine scars, with approximately 90% of ruptures occurring in women who have had scarred uterus, with cesarean section being the primary cause of scarred uterus, whereas UR of an unscarred uterus remains an exceedingly rare condition. According to a systematic review by the World Health Organization, the estimated incidence of UR among women without a history of cesarean section is 0.02%. In developed countries, this figure is even lower, estimated at 0.006%. In contrast, the rate is approximately 1% for women who have had a prior cesarean section. Furthermore, ruptures in unscarred uterus tend to be more severe than those in scarred uterus, leading to higher rates of maternal and neonatal complications [2–4]. The predisposing factors for unscarred uterus rupture typically include conditions that weaken the uterine wall such as Loeys–Dietz and Ehlers–Danlos, trauma, aggressive labor induction, excessive uterine distension, uterine abnormalities, multiple pregnancy, and multiparity [1, 5]. While the definitive diagnosis is certainly established intraoperatively, laboratory tests and radiological imaging modalities such as ultrasound (US) may support the preoperative diagnosis [1]. UR is associated with elevated rates of severe bleeding that necessitate blood transfusions, as well as potential injuries to the bladder and other organs, often resulting in the need for a hysterectomy. Timely diagnosis and intervention, which may include proactive fluid resuscitation, blood transfusions, and surgical procedures, greatly influence the patient’s outcome [1–3]. In this article, we present a case of a 44-year-old female patient who had spontaneous non-laboring UR of unscarred uterus while she had no remarkable history of surgical interventions or any predisposing factor.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old gravida 3, para 2 Syrian woman at 36 weeks of pregnancy presented to the emergency department at our hospital with complaints of generalized fatigue, and mild lower abdominal pain. The patient reported a sudden onset of severe abdominal pain approximately 2 hours prior to arrival, which resolved spontaneously. She had no history of prior cesarean deliveries or abdominal surgeries and had been receiving regular antenatal care with her obstetrician. Furthermore, she denied taking any medications or herbal supplements during pregnancy. On initial physical examination, the patient was anxious and appeared pale. Vital signs revealed tachycardia of 112 beats per minute, hypotension of 90/40 mmHg, and a respiratory rate of 24 breaths per minute, with shock index of 1.2. The patient received a single prophylactic dose of 2 g intravenous cefazolin and was immediately transferred for exploratory laparotomy. Intraoperatively, hemoperitoneum was noted, and a deceased fetus with an intact placenta were found freely floating in the peritoneal cavity; the fetus weighed approximately 2600 g and Apgar score was 0 in the first minute and after 5 minutes. The uterus was longitudinally ruptured along the left lateral aspect involving the cervix and the body, and reaching the fundus, accompanied by a sizable paracorporeal hematoma. It is hypothesized that the hematoma may have exerted tamponade on the rupture site, thereby preventing massive hemorrhage and explaining the patient’s hemodynamically stable condition at initial presentation (this is an unusual finding in such critical UR cases, which typically present in shock and may not survive to hospital admission). Discussion

Uterine rupture (UR) is an uncommon but serious complication that occurs when the uterine wall is torn through all three layers. While UR is typically encountered in pregnant women, it had been documented in non-pregnant women following trauma, malignancy, or infection [1, 2, 6]. Scarred uterus remains the most predominant risk factor, with cesarean section being the most common cause of scarred uterus [2]. However, rupture in unscarred uterus remains an exceedingly rare phenomenon, with an incidence of nearly 1 in 10,000–25,000 births, though the incidence rates may be higher in developing countries due to the lack of advanced antenatal care [1, 2]. Predisposing factors for unscarred uterus rupture include conditions that weaken the uterine wall such as Loeys–Dietz and Ehlers–Danlos, trauma, overly prolonged or aggressive labor induction with oxytocin, especially in obstructed labor, as well as excessive uterine distension, such as in cases of polyhydraminos, uterine abnormalities, fetal macrosomia associated with gestational diabetes, and multiple pregnancy. Uterine torison and abnormally adherent placenta on unscarred uterus are also recognized as potential risk factors, since Vuong et al. reported severe spontaneous hemoperitoneum in pregnancy due to unscarred uterus rupture, which was caused by placenta accreta spectrum. Additionally, multiparity is identified as a significant risk factor due to the repeated stretching of the uterine wall [1, 5, 7–9]. Remarkably, our patient was gravida 3, para 2 and had none of these predisposing factors and did not undergo labor induction. Generally, symptoms such as vaginal bleeding along with severe and acute abdominal pain, as well as elevated heart rate and low blood pressure, must all prompt the clinical suspicion [1]. Nevertheless, in many cases of UR, the diagnosis is complicated, as symptoms such as acute abdominal pain are usually nonspecific and may be encountered in a variety of conditions such as pancreatitis, gallstones, and appendicitis [5]. Furthermore, acute abdominal pain accompanied by abnormal vaginal bleeding in the second and the third trimesters of gestation could be noted in several differential diagnoses such as UR, placenta previa, placental abruption, and miscarriage, though the hemorrhage resulting from UR is usually intraabdominal and the vaginal bleeding may not be detected [1]. Consequently, any abdominal pain or vaginal bleeding in the last two trimesters of pregnancy should be rigorously investigated to avoid further complications associated with delayed diagnosis [5]. Our patient presented with mild abdominal pain and generalized fatigue, and the gynecological examination identified vaginal bleeding. If UR is suspected, hemodynamic status, heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate must all be assessed to identify any signs of hypovolemic shock such as tachycardia and hypotension [1]. Laboratory tests and radiological imaging modalities may support the diagnosis, particularly in simple UR. However, in severe ruptures, these investigations may not be appropriate due to the urgent need for intervention to achieve hemorrhage control [1]. On US, a hematoma near a uterine scar, abnormal uterine wall, anhydramnios, or the presence of free intraperitoneal fluid are all supporting findings of UR. Although the extramural existence of a fetus on US substantially corroborates the clinical suspicion, the diagnosis is certainly established intraoperatively during laparotomy [1, 10]. Blood tests of our patient upon presentation revealed deranged values compatible with moderate blood loss, raising suspicion of an internal hemorrhage, while US examination supported the clinical suspicion. The first-line intervention is the delivery of the fetus through an emergency cesarean section if the fetus is still in the uterus, or exploratory laparotomy in cases of extramural existence of the fetus [1]. Post-delivery, hysterectomy is the typically preferred therapeutic management for UR, especially in cases of severe ruptures, though hysterorrhaphy may be considered when appropriate, especially in younger patients desiring to have future pregnancies [11]. Our patient underwent exploratory laparotomy and hysterectomy, followed by plasma and blood transfusions and adequate antibiotic therapy. Postoperative follow-up was uneventful. To the best of our knowledge, less than 100 cases of spontaneous UR of unscarred uterus were reported in literature. In contrast to these cases, our case is noteworthy as our patient had no predisposing factors such as multiparty or underlying conditions. Her stable condition at presentation after a relatively long period of 2 hours of uterine rupture further emphasize the uniqueness of our case, as in such severe and critical uterine ruptures, the hemorrhage is massive and the patient may not survive to hospital admission.

Conclusion

Spontaneous non-laboring UR, although extremely rare, may unexpectedly occur in the absence of any predisposing factor. Further inspection into the clinical symptoms is necessary to avoid delayed diagnosis and management of UR. Clinics must remain vigilant about the atypical presentations of UR to minimize the mortality rate associated with this condition. Imaging modalities such as emergent US may substantially corroborate the preoperative diagnosis. Hysterectomy should be considered in cases of severe UR, particularly if hysterorrhaphy is unable to be performed.
