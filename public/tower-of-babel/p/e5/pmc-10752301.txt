Equitable distribution in ROP care: The need of the hour
Authors: Rajvardhan Azad
Published: 2023

India has the highest number of preterm births, estimated at 30,16,700 in 2020.[1] Approximately one lakh of these babies survive and thus need screening for retinopathy of prematurity (ROP).[2] The number of pediatric ophthalmologists in India is around a thousand, and there are only around 2000 vitreoretinal specialists in the country.[34] The majority of these are centered around urban areas; however, most preterm births occur in rural areas. There is significant medicolegal liability in ROP diagnosis, with awards to the tune of 1.8 crores allotted for the significant number of blind years experienced by a child affected by ROP; yet, there is a gap in the referral of these patients to ophthalmologists for screening.[5] Thus, presumably, only 1% of the 20,000 ophthalmologists in the country are actually involved with ROP care.[4]

The implementation of the Facility-based Integrated Management of Neonatal and Childhood Illnesses program has led to the establishment of newborn stabilization units, special newborn care units, and neonatal intensive care units throughout the country. This has resulted in the survival of many preterm infants who would otherwise have died. However, the quality of care provided in terms of the regulation of the percentage of oxygen provided to these babies to levels where ROP would be prevented from occurring is still doubtful. There is also a high probability of the survivors being discharged from these facilities without being screened for ROP due to a lack of coordination between neonatologists, pediatricians, and ophthalmologists.[5]

ROP detection and management is one of the priority areas of the Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) under the National Health Mission of India.[6] However, the situation where these provisions are actually utilized will arise only if the training and equipment to detect and treat all stages of the disease are available in an equitable manner throughout the country and not confined to a select few centers of tertiary care.[7] In this regard, it is prudent to train all ophthalmologists of the specialist cadre at secondary level hospitals (community health centers and first referral units) throughout the country for screening and prompt referral of ROP cases as an initial measure, as well as to equitably provide and maintain retinal laser, intravitreal injection, and vitreoretinal surgical facilities at more centers in a hub-and-spoke model where vitreoretinal specialists and those trained in ROP interventions are posted.[6]

Currently, the involvement of parents and caregivers in the identification and management of ROP in infants appears to be suboptimal. This stems in part from the fact that there is no standardized protocol of communication to the parents about the need for timely fundus screening, and even if such communication occurs, the understanding of the disease process and appropriate steps they need to take in this regard remains unclear.[58] There is a need to establish effective communication channels to explain to the caregivers and parents the need for ongoing screening till these babies are pronounced to be out of danger of developing the disease, as well as proper tracking to mitigate the long-term sequelae of the disease in those treated.[9] The requirement for follow-up examinations should also be reinforced during home visits by auxiliary health personnel such as accredited social health activists.[68] Simplified fundus screening through the use of portable fundus cameras to capture pictures of the state of the retina can become a great mediator in promoting understanding of the disease for many caregivers.[10]

It is understood that the management of ROP is also difficult due to the inability of parents to focus on the disease in the face of more overwhelming issues related to the survival of the babies across various medical specialties which may require more than 30 visits per year in case of multiple comorbidities and expenditure often beyond their means.[5] A central registry of premature babies can be maintained with RBSK nodal officers, with digitally communicated reminders to parents or caregivers, reassurance regarding free-of-cost treatment under the program, and maps showing nearby ROP care centers bearing in mind the mobility of the population in our country. An ROP coordinator needs to be appointed in each district to coordinate the activities of these functionaries to ensure that no child is lost in the process of referral and treatment. In addition, social support for parental mental health will lead to diligent health-seeking behavior in the face of the disease.[9]

ROP management requires an integrated team of ophthalmologists, neonatologists, pediatricians, anesthesiologists, social workers, and counselors.[7] Hence, support for ROP prevention, diagnosis, and management needs to be expanded from on-paper referral and individualized reimbursement to awareness and cooperation among treating clinicians, active involvement of caregivers, and resource provision to strengthen appropriate centers for ROP screening and interventions throughout India.

ROP is an enigma as it is very unpredictable about its disastrous outcome as well as its course. The window period for treatment is very small, and early detection is the key to prevention. Although in recent years lots of ROP centers have opened due to the initiative of Govt of India and WHO, which has conducted many awareness workshops for detecting cases early. Despite increased awareness, there is a big gap between care needed and caregivers. We need to have more manpower, that is, experts, as ROP is a very specialized area that needs precision and commitment.

Although MOH established a national task force on ROP, later, it was merged with the RBSK program and hence it has gotten diluted. The need of the hour is to reactivate the national task force to prepare a wholesome approach considering the training of manpower, provision of equipment, and mapping of resource center India after assessing the load of ROP care and blindness.

A welcome step in this direction is the establishment of the Indian ROP Society IROP, which is involved in creating awareness among ophthalmologists, neonatologists, obstetricians, and paramedical personnel through zonal workshops all over the country and through its ROP ambassador program.[11] At the global level, very recently, the World ROP Council has been formed to tackle this issue at the global level.

About the author

Rajvardhan Azad

Prof.(Dr.) Rajvardhan Azad, a distinguished clinician, academician, educator, and researcher, is an eminent retinal surgeon renowned for his extraordinary contributions to Ophthalmology. While serving at Dr. R.P. Centre, AIIMS New Delhi, he made significant strides in preventing childhood blindness and enhancing quality of care for ROP cases. He established a cutting-edge ROP management unit in collaboration with Neonatology Department, AIIMS New Delhi, overseeing ROP screening, laser and surgical management, and training numerous young ophthalmologists.

Prof. Azad's dedication culminated in the formation of the Indian ROP Society (IROP) in 2016, which he presides over as the President, uniting ROP specialists, neonatologists, pediatricians, obstetricians, nurses, and other physicians. At Global level Prof Azad has been elected as President of World ROP Council. He is rightfully celebrated as "Father of ROP movement" in India and on global stage. His relentless efforts have earned him numerous orations and accolades, including the prestigious APAO 2023-Hose Rizal Award.
