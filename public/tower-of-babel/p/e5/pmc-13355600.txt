Unique presentation of immune thrombocytopenic purpura in adult osteopetrosis: A case report
Authors: Shane Johns, Joanna Fair, Austin R Pantel, Thomas M Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteopetrosis is a genetic disorder characterized by impaired osteoclast function, leading to diffuse osteosclerosis, brittle bones, and hematologic abnormalities. Herein, we report the case of a 55-year-old woman diagnosed with adult-onset osteopetrosis at age 33 after imaging demonstrated dense and marbled long bones, sclerotic medullary canals, vertebral endplate thickening, and hepatosplenomegaly. Bone marrow biopsy revealed preserved megakaryocytes, yet she experienced recurrent episodes of thrombocytopenia that responded to oral steroids and ultimately resolved following splenectomy, supporting an immune-mediated etiology of thrombocytopenia clinically managed as immune thrombocytopenic purpura. The clinical course was further complicated by multiple periprosthetic fractures, highlighting both the diagnostic and management challenges of this condition.Keywords: Adult-onset osteopetrosis, Osteopetrosis, Immune thrombocytopenic purpura, Bone scintigraphy, Periprosthetic fractures, Bone marrow failure

Introduction

Osteopetrosis is a rare genetic disorder characterized by the dysfunction or absence of osteoclast activity. The disruption of bone breakdown mechanisms results in unopposed osteoblast activity, leading to the development of brittle bones that are prone to fractures, although the severity of the disease varies widely. Osteopetrosis has been linked to up to 10 gene mutations, with inheritance patterns ranging from autosomal dominant to autosomal recessive and X-linked [1]. Osteopetrosis is classically classified into 4 distinct types: (1) malignant osteopetrosis, (2) intermediate osteopetrosis, (3) adult onset osteopetrosis I (ADO I), and (4) adult onset osteopetrosis II (ADO II), which is also known as Albers-Schönberg disease [2]. The age of onset of osteopetrosis can vary, from infancy (malignant osteopetrosis) through adulthood (intermediate and adult onset) [2]. Most commonly, ADO follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 20,000 live births [3]. While ADO is primarily managed with supportive care, malignant osteopetrosis in infants is often treated with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation due to the severity of the disease progression and low survival rates [4]. Clinical manifestations of ADO include pathologic fractures, cranial nerve palsies, and hematologic abnormalities, as well as increased risk of osteomyelitis and infections [5].

Evaluation of osteopetrosis severity and progression relies heavily on careful skeletal imaging. Most commonly, radiographs and computed tomography (CT) scans are used to evaluate for pathologic fractures and sclerotic changes that lead to clinical symptoms. Occasionally, bone scintigraphy is obtained to assess for infection, pathologic fractures, and metabolic activity in the bone [6,7].

Anemia and cytopenia are common findings in adults with osteopetrosis and may be the initial sign of the genetic disease. Often, patients are asymptomatic, and routine blood work detects the hematologic abnormalities. Cell line abnormalities occur due to the replacement of the medullary bone space with sclerotic bone.

This case details the presentation and clinical course of a patient with adult-onset osteopetrosis who was treated for immune thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), without a known family history. A literature search performed by the authors did not reveal any documented relationship between osteopetrosis and ITP in adult patients who have never received a hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

Case

A 55-year-old Native American woman initially presented at age 33 to an outside emergency department for abdominal pain. CT imaging at that time demonstrated mild hepatosplenomegaly and diffuse bony sclerosis (images not available, noting these images were obtained over 2 decades ago). Blood work revealed thrombocytopenia. The patient was referred to our facility for further evaluation.

At our center, the patient had a reported history of osteoporosis and degenerative joint disease of the hips, status post bilateral total hip arthroplasties (THA), the first performed as a teenager and the second as a young adult. No surgical pathology reports are available from these THA procedures performed at an outside hospital over 3 decades ago.

At our institution, the diagnosis of adult-onset osteopetrosis was established based on clinical history, imaging findings, bone marrow core biopsy results, and peripheral blood smear results. CT and radiographs demonstrated dense marbled appearance of the long bones and pelvis, sclerotic changes of the medullary space, and vertebral body deformities (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Analysis of the bone marrow core biopsy was difficult due to poor sampling secondary to sclerotic bone; however, pathologic evaluation reported foci of hematopoiesis consisting of erythroid precursors, maturing myeloid elements, scattered megakaryocytes, and plasma cells. Peripheral blood smear was more consistent with a myelophthisic process, demonstrating leukoerythroblastosis, occasional teardrop and nucleated red blood cells, mild granulocytic left shift, and normal platelet count with occasional large forms which can be seen in states of increased peripheral platelet turnover. Although the peripheral smear supported an underlying myelophthisic process related to osteopetrosis, the episodic isolated thrombocytopenia with preservation of other cell lines raised concern for a concomitant immune-mediated thrombocytopenia.

The patient’s platelet counts dropped as low as 3-4 × 10^3/µL multiple times, but each time, these counts responded to periodic treatment with a 4-day course of dexamethasone. Additional testing and consideration at this time included negative viral testing (including hepatitis C, HIV, and Epstein-Barr Virus), liver function testing within reference ranges, absence of thrombocytopenia inducing medications, and negative autoimmune markers (limited to antinuclear antibody or ANA). The patient was also relatively healthy with oral medications limited to calcitriol and losartan. Unavailable from our records include bleeding time results, antibody platelet testing, and nuclear platelet tracing to further evaluate for locations of platelet sequestering. These tests would have been desirable to establish a diagnosis of exclusion but were not performed. Given the information and data collected, a splenectomy was ultimately performed at an outside hospital at the age of 37 for presumed ITP. In the nearly 2 decades since the splenectomy, the patient has had platelet counts within reference range, further supporting the etiology of her isolated severe thrombocytopenia as ITP as opposed to a complication of osteopetrosis or hypersplenism. The patient does, however, continue to have mild anemia with evidence of a left shift consistent with progression of osteopetrosis.

Over the subsequent two decades, the patient experienced additional osteopetrosis-related complications, such as periprosthetic hip fractures and two right-sided periprosthetic joint infections, all requiring revisions and further surgical intervention. The patient is now 55 years old and has undergone a total of four right and two left hip surgeries to address infections and periprosthetic fractures. This complex fracture management related to osteopetrosis can be visualized in Fig. 5, Fig. 6, noting the differences in prosthetic hardware compared to prior imaging (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). The patient has also undergone bone scintigraphy to evaluate for periprosthetic fractures, as radiographs were inconclusive for new fractures. Bone scans demonstrated diffusely increased uptake of technetium-99m (Tc-99m) methylene diphosphonate throughout the bones, particularly the lower extremities, with minimal soft tissue uptake and renal excretion (Fig. 7A and B). The increased uptake at the end of the long bones of the lower extremities corresponds to the metaphyseal flaring seen on radiographs; both are imaging manifestations of dysregulated bone formation/resorption. Bone scans also demonstrated a possible left greater trochanteric fracture; however, the patient was not experiencing any clinical symptoms that correlated with this finding. Scintigraphy, although not required for the diagnosis of osteopetrosis, demonstrates a unique but characteristic uptake pattern of osteopetrosis. The patient is currently undergoing a formal genetic evaluation with buccal mucosal testing for osteopetrosis, which was not previously performed.

Discussion

Authors present a case of osteopetrosis diagnosed at age 33 with severe steroid-responsive thrombocytopenia clinically managed as ITP. This case underscores the management challenges of adult-onset osteopetrosis and the broad spectrum of clinical severity associated with this genetic condition.

Characteristic radiologic findings for this condition include osteosclerosis, marbled-appearing areas of bone, dense bone appearing in the medullary canals of long bones (“endobone” or “bone-in-bone”), vertebral body endplate sclerosis (often coined "sandwich vertebrae”), and an "Erlenmeyer flask” shape to long bones caused by metaphyseal flaring; many of these can be seen in the presented case [2,8,9]. Additional imaging considerations for patients with osteopetrosis include nuclear medicine studies such as Tc-99m bone scintigraphy to assess areas of bone turnover. In this case, the combination of minimal soft tissue and renal uptake with symmetric uptake at the ends of long bones supported the diagnosis of osteopetrosis, a finding previously described in the literature [10]. Additional differential diagnoses for this “superscan” appearance include diffuse metastatic disease, hyperparathyroidism, and renal osteodystrophy, which were ruled out by labs, imaging, and biopsies. While bone scintigraphy can serve as a helpful adjunct imaging modality, results should always be interpreted in the context of the full clinical picture.

Additional considerations for patients with osteopetrosis include monitoring for hematologic abnormalities identified through routine laboratory tests. Specifically, given the encroachment of disorganized bone into the marrow space, patients are susceptible to marrow failure, particularly pancytopenia, given this mechanism [5,11,12]. This patient’s presentation of isolated thrombocytopenia that responded to oral steroids was not typical of what would be expected in osteopetrosis. Of note, megakaryocyte count may be increased, decreased, or normal in ITP [13], and bone marrow biopsy is not necessary to establish this diagnosis [14]. Ultimately, thrombocytopenia episodes halted following a splenectomy, a documented treatment option for ITP [15], eliminating a major site of antibody production and the dominant site of platelet destruction [16]. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the simultaneous presentation of ITP and osteopetrosis has not been previously documented. PubMed search terms included “primary osteopetrosis,” “adult-onset osteopetrosis,” “Albers-Schönberg disease,” “immune thrombocytopenic purpura,” and “immune thrombocytopenia.” Following splenectomy, the patient’s platelet counts normalized and remained stable long-term. This clinical course supports an immune-mediated component to the thrombocytopenia and makes marrow space compromise from osteopetrosis less likely to fully explain the patient’s episodic severe thrombocytopenia.

The patient was noted to have mild hepatosplenomegaly on outside hospital imaging which, alongside severe episodes of thrombocytopenia, prompted referral to our center. Although the patient’s thrombocytopenia was clinically managed as ITP, the presence of splenomegaly introduces diagnostic uncertainty, as splenic enlargement is not a characteristic feature of classic primary ITP [17]. However, 1 study performed by Rabinovich et. al found in a cohort of 40 patients, 12 people with ITP had mild to moderate splenomegaly. Treating physicians considered additional causes of splenomegaly including hypersplenism, which is well documented in patients with severe osteopetrosis [18]. However, due to the patient’s severe episodes of thrombocytopenia with cell counts dropping to 3-4 × 10^3/µL, rapid improvement following a course of oral steroids, preservation of other cell lines, absence of liver disease, and peripheral blood smear results, treating physicians considered hypersplenism less likely to explain the severity and episodic nature of the thrombocytopenia. Authors acknowledge the limited evaluation of hypersplenism and recommend consideration of additional testing in the form of dedicated spleen imaging, platelet antibody testing, and nuclear medicine platelet sequestering studies. At the time of treatment, platelet sequestration studies and platelet antibody testing were not performed and are unavailable retrospectively. Although splenectomy may also improve thrombocytopenia related to splenic sequestration, the episodic severe thrombocytopenia, preservation of additional cell lines, and steroid responsiveness favored an immune-mediated component. The hepatosplenomegaly may have reflected extramedullary hematopoiesis related to osteopetrosis, although this was not definitively established.

At the time of presentation to our facility, the patient did not have a formal diagnosis of osteopetrosis but had already undergone bilateral total hip arthroplasties (right at age 16 and left at age 20) for osteoporosis and early-onset degenerative joint disease. Interestingly, at presentation to our facility, the patient's degenerative changes were instead attributable to osteosclerosis and the haphazard bone formation occurring due to unopposed osteoblast activity. Fortunately, arthroplasty procedures continue to be recommended for patients with end-stage osteoarthritis secondary to osteopetrosis [8,19,20]. Long-term management of patients with osteopetrosis is challenging from an orthopedic standpoint due to poor bone quality and failure under tensile stress, leading to cortical fractures in long bones, perioperative iatrogenic fractures, and periprosthetic fractures [8]. Careful planning and implant consideration should be performed, as the risk for periprosthetic fractures can result in the need for revisions and additional hardware, which in turn increases the risk for further complications such as infection.

Conclusion

Radiographic evaluation remains a mainstay diagnostic tool for adult osteopetrosis. This case documents the diagnosis and extended follow-up in a 33-year-old woman who presented to our facility with radiographic evidence of osteopetrosis and highlights the characteristic multimodality imaging findings and long-term orthopedic complications of adult osteopetrosis. The case also demonstrates an unusual presentation of severe steroid-responsive thrombocytopenia. While a causal link between these two conditions was not definitively established, the clinical course supports a concomitant immune-mediated process.
