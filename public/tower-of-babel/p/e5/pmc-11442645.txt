Physical inactivity and breakfast skipping caused visceral fat accumulation in rats
Authors: Syunsuke Nakajima, Fumiaki Hanzawa, Saiko Ikeda, Hiroaki Oda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhysical inactivity as well as breakfast skipping is known as risk factor for various metabolic diseases, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. We have previously reported that a breakfast skipping model, in which the timing of feeding is delayed, induces abnormal lipid metabolism by altering the circadian rhythm of lipid metabolism-related genes in rats. The purpose of this study was to elucidate the synergistic effect of physical inactivity and breakfast skipping on lipid metabolism. We adopted sciatic neurectomized rats as physically inactive models, because we confirmed that the rats mildly decreased their spontaneous locomotor activity compared to sham-operated rats. And then the physically inactive model rats were fed a mild high-fat diet during zeitgeber time (ZT) 12–0 in the control group and ZT16–0 in the breakfast skipping group for 11 days. Body weight gain and total food intake were similar in both groups. Breakfast skipping induced a significant visceral fat accumulation, which was not observed in our previous breakfast skipping or physically inactive studies. The mRNA levels of clock and lipogenesis-related genes were altered by breakfast skipping in the liver and epididymal adipose tissue, and serum insulin level was altered by breakfast skipping. These results suggest that physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically induces drastic visceral fat accumulation due to the alteration of circadian clock and lipid metabolism in the liver and adipose tissue. Therefore, regular feeding timing plays an important role in the health of a sedentary modern society.Keywords: Physical inactivity, Breakfast skipping, Visceral fat accumulation, Circadian rhythm, Lipid metabolismSubject terms: Molecular biology, Physiology, Risk factors

Introduction

Physical inactivity is a known risk factor for various metabolic diseases, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease1–4. The World Health Organization (WHO) showed that physical inactivity is the fourth leading risk factor for non-communicable diseases5. One in 4 adults and 4 in 5 adolescents worldwide do not get enough exercise5. The WHO estimated that if the global population were to engage in more physical activity, approximately 4–5 million deaths per year could be averted5. Recently, physical inactivity has received increased attention. Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has increased the number of physically inactive people due to telecommunications and self-isolation6. Physical inactivity is a global public health concern and a known risk factor for various metabolic diseases.

Several studies have shown that the timing of feeding is an important cue for health implications7,8. Time-restricted feeding protected against abnormal lipid metabolism in mice fed a high-fat diet7. We have also shown that time-restricted feeding ameliorated high-sucrose diet induced dyslipidemia in rats8. In humans, time-restricted feeding improved obesity and glucose levels9,10. In contrast, we reported that suppressed feeding rhythm developed hypercholesterolemia in rats fed a high-cholesterol diet11. Shift workers with irregular feeding timing frequently suffer from metabolic syndrome and abnormal blood lipids12,13. We need to pay attention not only to diet composition but also to feeding timing to maintain our health because abnormal feeding timing cause metabolic abnormalities.

Breakfast skipping is a major irregular feeding timing behavior worldwide. It has been reported that almost 10–30% of American and European children and adolescents skip breakfast14–16. In Japanese population aged 20–29 years, 30.6% men and 23.6% women skip breakfast17. Human epidemiological studies have shown that breakfast skipping is a risk factor for metabolic dysfunction, including metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes18–20. In our previous study, to investigate whether breakfast skipping affects lipid metabolism, we developed a breakfast skipping regimen for rats, which was similar to the characterize of the timing of feeding in human who skip breakfast. Under this regimen, rats fed a high-fat diet, or a high-sucrose diet showed increased body weight gain21,22, and rats fed a high-cholesterol diet accumulated liver lipids23. These rats showed delayed circadian oscillations in hepatic lipid metabolism-related genes21–23. Therefore, we concluded breakfast plays an important role in normal lipid metabolism via the hepatic circadian clock.

Obesity is a known cause of several metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease24,25. Waist circumstance reflects excess visceral fat accumulation is diagnosis of a metabolic syndrome and also has been known risk factor for several metabolic diseases, including abnormal glucose metabolism, dyslipidemia, and high blood pressure26,27. The mechanism of excess visceral fat accumulation induce metabolic abnormalities was thought to insulin resistant and higher level of adipocytokines27. On the other hand, in human study, visceral obesity is more common in men than in pre-menopausal women28. It has been known that fat accumulation is shift from subcutaneous to visceral fat accumulation due to aging29. For these reasons, visceral fat accumulation is more common in middle-aged men and is responsible for their metabolic abnormalities. Several studies showed that prevalence of breakfast skipping habits was higher in men than women30,31. A decline in physical activity with age has been reported, and this decline is more pronounced in men32,33. Although unhealthy lifestyle habits, which are more prevalent in men, could be identified as a potential contributing factor to visceral fat accumulation, we still don’t know the reason why visceral fat accumulation is induced by aging or gender differences.

The combination of physical inactivity and breakfast skipping may serve to exacerbate metabolic abnormalities, as each of these factors represents an independent risk factor for metabolic abnormalities. In fact, several studies showed that those who habitually breakfast skipping have lower physical activity than those who habitually consume breakfast34,35. However, how effect of physical inactivity with breakfast skipping on lipid metabolism remain unknown. Therefore, in this study, we developed a rat model with moderate physical inactivity induced by sciatic neurectomy. Physically inactive rats were exposed to a breakfast skipping regimen to investigate whether physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically induced abnormal lipid metabolism.

Results

Sciatic neurectomy induced mildly lowering of spontaneous locomotor activity in rats

Firstly, to confirm whether the sciatic neurectomy induce a decreased in spontaneous locomotor activity, we conducted sham operation (n = 5) as a sham-operated control or sciatic neurectomy (n = 4) in Wistar male rats. Activity counts in the sham-operated rats and sciatic neurectomized rats were 1944 ± 259/day versus 1177 ± 80 counts/day, respectively (p = 0.039) (Fig. S1A). This result showed that spontaneous locomotor activity of sciatic neurectomized rats were significantly decreased to 60% compared with that of sham-operated rats (Fig. S1A). The decreases seemed to be observed particularly in dark phase (ZT 12–0), which is their active period (Fig. S1B and C). These data indicated that sciatic neurectomized rats exhibited a mild but significant attenuation in spontaneous locomotor activity. Therefore, we adopted sciatic neurectomized rats as model for mild physical inactivity.

Physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically induced visceral fat accumulation

We conducted sciatic neurectomy for rats (n = 29) to investigate whether physical inactivity and breakfast skipping affect lipid metabolism. The sciatic neurectomized rats fed a mild high-fat diet (Table 1) during ZT12–0 in the control group (n = 14) and ZT16–0 in the breakfast skipping group (n = 15) for 11 days. After the regimen, body weight gain and total food intake were not significantly different between the groups, and liver weight was affected by the time of sacrifice but not by feeding timing (Table 2). The gastrocnemius muscle weights (g/100 g BW) calculated by adding ZT2 and ZT14 were similar in both groups (0.38 ± 0.006 in the control group vs 0.37 ± 0.006 in the breakfast skipping group, p = 0.162); however, only ZT2 was decreased in significance by breakfast skipping (Table 2). The plantaris, soleus, tibialis anterior, and extensor digitorum longus muscle weights were similar in both groups (Table 2). Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a significant feeding timing-effect on epididymal and retroperitoneal adipose tissue weights and a tendency feeding timing-effect on perirenal and mesenteric adipose tissue weights (Fig. 1A–D). Total visceral adipose tissue weight was calculated by summing epididymal, perirenal, retroperitoneal, and mesenteric adipose tissue weights showed a significant feeding timing-effect (Fig. 1E). These results showed that breakfast skipping increased visceral adipose tissue weight than control group. In our previous study, breakfast skipping in rats fed a high-fat diet did not increase adipose tissue weight, except for epididymal adipose tissue weight21. This physically inactive model rats did not increase epididymal adipose tissue weight (unpublished data). Thus, physical inactivity with breakfast skipping induced visceral fat accumulation.

Breakfast skipping suppressed surge of body temperature during ZT12–14 in physically inactive rats

It well known that body temperature is controlled by the hypothalamic thermoregulatory center and shows a circadian rhythm21–23. When the light was turned off (ZT12), an increase in body temperature was observed in both groups because the rats were nocturnal (Fig. 1F). Assuming that sphericity holds by Mauchly's sphericity test because the number of measured samples is smaller than ZT, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant time effect and interaction, indicating that circadian oscillations in body temperature differed between the two groups (Fig. 1F). The body temperature during ZT12–14 in the breakfast skipping rats seems to lower than that in the control rats (Fig. 1F). To estimate and compare the energy expenditure difference between the two groups, we calculated the average of body temperature during dark phase, light phase, and total in either group. This is because core body temperature is an indicator of energy expenditure36. The average of body temperature during dark phase, light phase, and total in the control group was 37.33 ± 0.06, 38.11 ± 0.07, and 37.72 ± 0.06, and that in the breakfast skipping group was 37.26 ± 0.04, 38.08 ± 0.03, and 37.67 ± 0.03, respectively. Comparing these results for each, no statistically significant differences were found between the two groups. Subsequently, we calculated area of body temperature during ZT12–16 and ZT0–4 in either group, because it seemed the length of time that body temperature is high differs between the two groups (Fig. 1F). The area of ZT12–16 was about 2.2-fold greater than that of ZT0–4. This value is similar to that reported in our previous breakfast skipping studies21–23. Even in the condition of physical inactivity, breakfast skipping may have altered energy expenditure.

Breakfast skipping altered level of serum glucose, triglyceride, bile acid, and insulin at ZT14 in physically inactive rats

A significant of feeding timing-effect was found for serum glucose, triglycerides, NEFA, and insulin levels (Fig. 2A,C,D,F) and that of sacrifice timing-effect was found for serum total cholesterol, triglyceride, NEFA, and bile acid levels (Fig. 2B–E). A significant interaction was found for serum glucose, NEFA and insulin levels, but not serum total cholesterol, triglyceride, and bile acid levels (Fig. 2A–F). Although serum glucose, total cholesterol, triglyceride, NEFA, and insulin levels did not change at ZT 2 in either group, serum glucose and insulin levels were significantly decreased, and NEFA levels were significantly increased at ZT14 by breakfast skipping (Fig. 2A,D,F). This change in serum NEFA level was similar to those observed in our previous study21. However, a dropping in serum glucose and triglyceride levels at ZT14 was not observed in our previous study, even though the control group showed similar results21. The levels of serum insulin at ZT2 in the breakfast skipping group were higher than those in the control group in our previous study21, but not in the present study (Fig. 2F); thus, it was speculated that these changes, which differed from our previous study21 observed in not the control group but in the breakfast skipping group, were induced by physical inactivity and breakfast skipping.

Breakfast skipping altered mRNA level of hepatic clock genes at ZT2 or ZT14 in physically inactive rats

In our previous studies, we showed that breakfast skipping regimen delayed the circadian oscillation of hepatic clock genes21,22. A significant of feeding timing-effect was found for the mRNA level of Per2 (Table S1) (Fig, 3D) and that of sacrifice timing-effect was found for the mRNA level of Bmal1, Clock, Per1, Per2, Cry1, Rev-erba, Rev-erbb, Rora, E4bp4, Dbp, Tef, and Hlf (Fig. 3A–E,H–O). A significant interaction was found for the mRNA level of Clock, Per2, Cry1, Cry2, Dec1, Rora, and Hlf (Fig. 3B,D–G,J,N).

In this study, the mRNA level of Clock was increased by breakfast skipping at ZT2 (Fig. 3B). The mRNA levels of Per2 and Cry1 were decreased after breakfast skipping at ZT14 (Fig. 3D,E). The mRNA levels of Bmal1, Per1, Cry2, Dec1, Rev-erba, Rev-erbb, Rora, E4bp4, Dbp, Tef, and Hlf did not change in either group at ZT2 or ZT14, respectively (Fig. 3A,C,F–N). The changes in almost all clock genes between the control and breakfast skipping groups at ZT2 and ZT14 were similar to those reported in our previous study21. In addition, some clock genes showed significant interactions, indicating that mRNA expression patterns differed between the two groups. Therefore, breakfast skipping regimen in this study would have delayed circadian oscillation of hepatic clock genes. However, alterations in Clock mRNA level after breakfast skipping was not observed in our previous study21. The mRNA level of Rev-erba did not change in either group at ZT14 in this study (Fig. 3H); however, it was decreased by approximately one-third by breakfast skipping in our previous study21. These several differences between the present study and previous study21 indicated that physically inactive slightly affected circadian oscillation of hepatic clock.

A significant interaction was found for the mRNA level of Srebp1, Acly, Fas, Elovl6, Me1, Hmgcr, and Cyp7a1 (Fig. 4A,B,D–F,M,N).

The mRNA levels of lipogenesis-related genes, such as Srebp1, Acly, Fas, Elovl6, and Me1, were increased by breakfast skipping at ZT2 (Fig. 4A,B,D–F). The mRNA level of Acaca did not show statistical significance at ZT2 and ZT14 (Fig. 4C). These data are relatively similar to those of our previous study21–23, suggesting that the circadian oscillation of lipogenesis-related genes was delayed by breakfast skipping in physically inactive rats. Although the mRNA levels of Srebp1, Acly, and Fas at ZT2 were about 1.2–1.6-fold higher in the breakfast skipping group than those in the control group in our previous study21–23, they were about 2.0–3.5-fold higher in this study (Fig. 4A,B,D). In contrast, the mRNA levels of lipolysis-related genes, such as Ppara and Cpt1a, were not altered by breakfast skipping at ZT2 and ZT14 (Fig. 4G,I). Although the mRNA level of Cpt1a at ZT14 was 1.5-fold higher in the breakfast skipping group than that in the control group21, this study showed similar values between the two groups. Thus, it was indicated that physical inactivity and breakfast skipping altered circadian oscillation of hepatic lipid metabolism-related genes. Compared to our previous study21, the breakfast skipping group showed a different pattern of mRNA levels of lipid metabolism-related genes at ZT2 or ZT14.

The mRNA levels of the cholesterol synthesis-related Srebp2 and Hmgcs1 were not altered by breakfast skipping (Fig. 4K,L). The mRNA level of Hmgcr decreased after breakfast skipping at ZT14, but did not change at ZT2 (Fig. 4M). The mRNA level of Cyp7a1, a rate-limiting enzyme in bile acid synthesis, was decreased at ZT14 after breakfast skipping (Fig. 4N). Thus, breakfast skipping altered the mRNA levels of a part of cholesterol metabolism-related genes at ZT2 and ZT14 in physically inactive rats. In our previous study, the mRNA level of Hmgcs1 at ZT2 was approximately one-half lower, while that at ZT14 was higher after breakfast skipping21. The mRNA levels of Hmgcs1 at ZT2 and ZT14 were similar in both group in this study (Fig. 4I), and those of Hmgcr at ZT14 showed a change different from those reported in our previous study21.

Breakfast skipping did not alter mRNA level of hepatic carbohydrate metabolism-related gene in physically inactive rats

A significant of sacrifice timing-effect was found for the mRNA level of Gckr, Gck, Pfkl, G6pc, and Pepck (Fig. S2A–C,E,G). However, a significant feeding timing-effect and interaction was not found in the mRNA level of hepatic carbohydrate metabolism-related genes.

The mRNA levels of Gckr, Gck, Pfkl, and Pklr were not altered by breakfast skipping (Fig. S2A–D). The mRNA levels of gluconeogenesis-related genes, such as G6pc, Fbp1, and Pepck were similar in both groups (Fig. S2E–G). These data indicated that breakfast skipping did not affect the mRNA levels of carbohydrate metabolism-related genes at ZT2 and ZT14 in physically inactive rats.

The mRNA levels of muscle clock genes, including Bmal1, Clock, Per2, Cry1, Rev-erba, and E4bp4 were not altered by breakfast skipping at ZT2 or ZT14 (Fig. 5A–F). Thus, breakfast skipping did not alter the mRNA levels of muscle clock genes at ZT2 and ZT14 in physically inactive rats. In our previous study, the mRNA levels of Bmal1 and Cry1 at ZT2 was dramatically decreased by breakfast skipping in mice37. However, in this study, the mRNA level of Bmal1 and Cry1 did not decrease significantly (Fig. 5A,D). The mRNA level of Rev-erba at ZT2 was high due to breakfast skipping37, but not in this study (Fig. 5E). Therefore, it is indicated that physical inactivity and breakfast skipping affected the mRNA levels of some clock genes at ZT2.

The mRNA level of myogenesis-related Myf6, Myog, and Myod1 was not altered by breakfast skipping at ZT2 and ZT14. The mRNA levels of muscle degradation-related genes, such as Foxo1, Foxo3, Atrogin-1, Murf1, Ctsl, and Casp3 also did not change in response to breakfast skipping at ZT2 or ZT14 (Fig. 5J–O). In our previous study, the mRNA level of Myog at ZT2 was increased by breakfast skipping37; however, the different result was observed in the present study (Fig. 5H). Other genes, such as Myod1, Atrogin-1, and Murf1 showed results similar to those of our previous study37. Therefore, the mRNA levels of some muscle metabolism-related genes were slightly altered by physical inactivity.

In this study, physical inactivity and breakfast skipping drastic induced visceral fat accumulation, we measured gene expression in epidydimal adipose tissue (Fig. 1A). The mRNA levels of the clock genes, such as Bmal1, Clock, Per2, and Cry2, were not altered by breakfast skipping (Fig. 6A,B,D,F). The mRNA level of Per1 increased in response to breakfast skipping at ZT14 (Fig. 6C). Thus, breakfast skipping slightly altered the mRNA levels of clock genes at ZT14 in physically inactive rat. In our previous study, the mRNA levels of Bmal1, Clock, and Per1 at ZT2 in the breakfast skipping group were markedly higher than those in the control group21. Additionally, the mRNA level of Cry2 at ZT14 in the breakfast skipping group showed dramatically lower value than that in the control group21. However, the mRNA levels of Bmal1, Clock, and Per1 at ZT2 and Cry2 at ZT14 were similar in both groups (Fig. 6A–C,F). The control group showed relatively similar results from our previous study21 but not the breakfast skipping group, indicating that physical inactivity and breakfast skipping altered the mRNA levels of clock genes at ZT2 and ZT14.

The mRNA levels of lipogenesis-related Acly significantly increased in response to breakfast skipping at ZT2 (Fig. 6G). The mRNA level of Fas was not altered by breakfast skipping (Fig. 6I). Although the mRNA level of the lipolysis-related Acox1 did not change, that of Cpt1a was significantly increased by breakfast skipping at ZT14 (Fig. 6J,K). The mRNA level of the fatty acid transport-related Ldlr decreased at ZT14 after breakfast skipping (Fig. 6L). However, Fatp1, Atgl, Lpl, and Hsl levels did not change in response to breakfast skipping at ZT2 or ZT14 (Fig. 6M–P). In our previous studies, we did not measure lipid metabolism-related genes in the adipose tissue. Hence, we could not compare our results with those of a previous study that used a breakfast skipping regimen. Therefore, it remains unclear whether this alteration was induced by breakfast skipping or by physical inactivity. However, these data suggest that breakfast skipping affected the mRNA levels of lipid metabolism-related genes at ZT2 or ZT14 in the adipose tissue in physically inactive rats.

Discussion

Physical inactivity is a leading risk factor for metabolic diseases1–4. Breakfast skipping increases the risk of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes19–21. Recently, the number of people who are physically inactive and skip breakfast has increased due to the COVID-19 pandemic38,39. Therefore, we investigated whether physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically induced abnormal lipid metabolism. In this study, we used sciatic neurectomized rats as models of mild physical inactivity (Fig. S1), which were fed a mild high-fat diet ZT12–0 in the control group and ZT16–0 in the breakfast skipping group. Physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically increased the accumulation of visceral fat (Fig. 1E). The surge in body temperature during ZT12–14 was suppressed by breakfast skipping (Fig. 1F). On the other hand, the level of serum insulin, which regulates lipogenesis, was altered by breakfast skipping at ZT14 (Fig. 2F). Moreover, the mRNA levels of clock and lipid metabolism-related genes in the liver and epididymal adipose tissue were altered by breakfast skipping at ZT2 and ZT14 (Figs. 3, 4 and 5). Some of the results differed from our previous study21 only in the breakfast skipping group, while the control group showed similar results. Thus, it is suggested that physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically altered serum parameters and gene expression in the liver and adipose tissue, inducing the accumulation of visceral fat.

Although physical inactivity and breakfast skipping did not affect body weight gain or food intake (Table 2), it significantly increased visceral fat accumulation in the epididymal and retroperitoneal adipose tissues (Fig. 1A,C,E). In our previous breakfast skipping study, a high-fat diet resulted in only perirenal adipose tissue fat accumulation21. Recently, we studied the effect of breakfast skipping on lipid metabolism in mice fed a normal diet using the same protocol as used in this study37. Mice that skipped breakfast also show an accumulation of perirenal adipose tissue37. The present study was the first time that significant visceral fat accumulation not only in perirenal but also in epididymal, and retroperitoneal adipose tissues was observed in our breakfast skipping regimen (Fig. 1A–E). We had previously found that sciatic neurectomy decreased epididymal adipose tissue weight in rats (unpublished data). In hindlimb suspension mouse, a physical inactivity model, fed a normal diet or high-fat diet feeding did not increase white adipose tissue weight40,41. These data indicated that physical inactive itself did not affect white adipose tissue accumulation in animal model for physical inactivity. Therefore, the visceral fat accumulation observed in this study was thought to be a synergistic effect of physical inactivity and irregular feeding habits, such as breakfast skipping. In modern society, adult men tend to be inactive and have irregular feeding timings; therefore, increased attention should be paid to feeding timing for health implications.

In the rats that skipped breakfast, the body temperature between ZT12 and ZT14 was lower than that in the control rats (Fig. 1F). Our previous breakfast skipping studies showed that breakfast skipping clearly delayed the surge in body temperature, but not the drop21–23,37. In addition, a human study also showed that breakfast skipping delayed body temperature42. These suggests that the period of high body temperature was shortened by breakfast skipping. Core body temperature is a determinant of energy expenditure36. Thus, one potential reason for the accumulation of visceral fat due to breakfast skipping is a reduction in energy expenditure. However, we did not measure energy expenditure using by metabolic cage in the present study. The further research is required to reveal effect of physical inactivity and breakfast skipping on energy expenditure. On the other hand, it is well known that thermic effect of food determined body temperature. It has been known that a high-fat diet intake increases thermogenic effect through sympathetic regulation of brown adipose tissue in rats43,44. It is speculated that delayed circadian oscillation of body temperature was induced by not only changes in circadian oscillation of innate metabolism but thermic effect of food. Furthermore, the surge in body temperature was thought to be regulated by both light and feeding (Fig. 1F).

In this study, physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically induced visceral fat accumulation owing to alterations in the levels of clock and lipid metabolism-related genes. The timing of meals is also important for metabolic health45,46. We previously reported that insulin, whose secretion is affected by feeding and regulates lipogenesis, is a synchronizer of the hepatic circadian clock47 and that abnormal feeding timing induces hypercholesterolemia due to an abnormal hepatic circadian clock11. Furthermore, clock genes regulate lipid metabolism, as Clock gene-mutated mice were induced abnormal feeding and obesity48, Per2 controls lipid metabolism by regulating Ppara49, and Cry1 knockdown decreases adipogenesis in vitro50. Rev-erba regulates the circadian oscillation of Srebp1, a master regulator of lipogenesis51. Although in this study, we only measured two time points, the results indicated that the circadian oscillation of clock and lipogenesis-related genes was delayed by breakfast skipping. In our previous breakfast skipping study, the breakfast skipping regimen affected the circadian oscillation of clock genes and clearly delayed that of insulin and lipogenesis-related genes21–23,37. Furthermore, the serum insulin and mRNA levels of Clock, Per2, Cry1, Srebp1, Acly, Fas, Elovl6, Me1, Hmgcr, and Cyp7a1 in the liver and those of Per1, Acly, Cpt1a, and Ldlr in the adipose tissue at ZT2 or ZT14 were altered by breakfast skipping in physically inactive rats (Figs 2F, 3B,D,E, 4A,B,D,E,M,N, 6C,G,K,L). In some of these results, only the breakfast skipping group showed differences from our previous study21. Thus, physical inactivity and breakfast skipping synergistically led to more abnormal lipid metabolism than breakfast skipping alone21–23 and induced visceral fat accumulation in rats.

Clock genes in skeletal muscles show a circadian pattern52. Both exercise and feeding timing are regulators for muscle clock53. Clock genes are thought to be important for maintaining muscle mass54–56. The Bmal1 knockout and Clock mutation mice were decreased muscle strength and mitochondrial volume54, and the Rev-erba deficient mice were decreased muscle mass with the enhancement of muscle atrophy-related genes55. Lean body mass decreases in Cry1/2-knockout mice56. Furthermore, a human study showed that shift work, which leads to disturbances in circadian oscillations, increases the risk of sarcopenia57. Recently, protein intake during breakfast was shown to be important for muscle mass maintenance via the muscle clock58. In fact, our previous breakfast skipping study in mice showed that breakfast skipping decreases plantar muscle weight, delays the circadian oscillation of muscle clock genes, and enhances the expression of muscle atrophy-related genes, such as Atrogin-1 and Murf137. However, in the present study, breakfast skipping did not accelerate muscle atrophy (Table 2). We used sciatic neurectomized rats as a physically inactive model, with dramatically decreased muscle mass due to the inability to move the lower legs59. Therefore, it is possible that the breakfast skipping could not affect muscle mass due to sciatic neurectomy-induced muscle atrophy. Moreover, the species differences between mice and rats could be a possible reason for these differences. Mice are more sensitive to dietary conditions than rats. In addition to the above reasons and the short experimental period (11 days), muscle weight only decreased at ZT2 by breakfast skipping in the present study (Table 2). In contrast, the mRNA levels of muscle metabolism-related genes were altered by breakfast skipping at ZT2 and ZT14, suggesting that prolonged exposure to this regimen may affect muscle weight. Furthermore, visceral fat accumulation without body weight change (Table 2 and Fig. 1E) indicated that body composition may be in a state of increased fat and decreased muscle mass, similar to sarcopenic obesity. Thus, avoiding breakfast skipping would be necessary to maintain muscle mass in modern people.

The purpose of this study was to elucidate the synergistic effect of physical inactivity and breakfast skipping on lipid metabolism abnormalities, such as fat accumulation, circadian oscillation of lipid metabolism-related genes, and muscle metabolism. This is because epidemiological studies have shown that people who skip breakfast are associated with physical inactivity. Visceral fat, including epididymal, perirenal, retroperitoneal, and mesenteric adipose tissue weights, was accumulated by both physical inactivity and breakfast skipping. This visceral fat accumulation was observed for the first time in our breakfast skipping study. Furthermore, only the breakfast skipping group showed differences in serum insulin levels, mRNA levels of clock genes, and lipid metabolism-related genes in the liver and epididymal adipose tissue at ZT2 or ZT14 compared to our previous study21. Therefore, we believe that there is a synergistic effect of physical inactivity and breakfast skipping. Moreover, visceral fat accumulation without changes in body weight may indicate the phenomenon observed in middle-aged men. The underlying mechanism is thought to be reduced energy expenditure and abnormal circadian oscillation of clock genes and lipogenesis-related genes in the liver and adipose tissue. In addition, physical inactivity and breakfast skipping altered the mRNA levels of muscle metabolism-related genes at ZT2 and ZT14, suggesting that prolonging this regimen may induce muscle atrophy. These results suggest that physical activity and timely breakfast are important for the prevention of metabolic syndrome and sarcopenia. On the other hand, we did not conduct 2 × 2 design (control-physical active; control-physical inactivity; breakfast skipping-physical active; breakfast skipping-physical inactivity) in the present study. Since this study could not mention the physical active control, it should be exercised in interpreting these results. Further research is needed to further clarify the relationship between physical inactivity and breakfast skipping. In conclusion, normal feeding timing is required to prevent health implications, especially in inactive people.

Materials and methods

Animals

The animal study was approved by the Animal Care Committee of Nagoya University (approval no. AGR2020076), and all the procedures were conducted in accordance with the Rules and Regulations of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, Nagoya University. This study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (https://arriveguidelines.org/arrive-guidelines). All surgical procedures were performed under isoflurane anesthesia, and efforts were made to minimize suffering. The animal room was maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C and kept under a 12 h light cycle (zeitgeber time (ZT) 0–ZT12). The rats were housed in wire-bottomed cages with free access to water throughout the experimental period. Five-week-old 29 Wistar male rats were purchased from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan). On the day the rats arrived, 16 rats of 29 rats were implanted with temperature data loggers (KN Laboratories Inc., Osaka, Japan) into their intraperitoneal cavity to measure body temperature. After the operation, the data logger implanted rats and non-operated rats were fed a basal diet (Table 1) for 7 days to adapt to the laboratory environment. After 29 rats were divided into two groups matched for body weight gain, both groups underwent sciatic neurectomy by a 1 cm-long transection on both sides. Postoperatively, the rats were fed a mild high-fat diet (Table 1) during the experimental period (11 days). The control (n = 14) and breakfast skipping groups (n = 15) were allowed access to a mild high-fat diet from ZT12 to ZT0 and ZT16 to ZT0, respectively. In this study, we placed in the rat’s cage with a feeding box containing enough diet before starting their feeding period and freely access to diet during the feeding period (12 h in the control group and 8 h in the breakfast skipping group). Seven or eight rats from each group were sacrificed at an interval of 12 h from ZT2 to ZT14 on day 11. The rats were euthanized by decapitation without anesthesia to minimize stress from anesthesia and blood sample was collected. Weight of liver, muscles, and adipose tissue sample was measured using by precision balance after collected. Subsequently, serum, liver, gastrocnemius muscle, and epididymal adipose tissue were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The frozen samples were stored at − 80 °C until assayed.

Measurement of spontaneous locomotor activity

Spontaneous locomotor activity was measured in separate individual transparent-plastic cages using a LOCOMO LS-8 system (Melquest, Toyama, Japan). A transparent-plastic cage was placed inside the sensor panel (28.0 × 44.0 cm) and the photobeam interruptions were counted by the interface when the rat moving in the cage. After the rats were acclimated to the measurement environment by placing them in the cages for 90 min, activity was counted every 10 min for a 24-h period.

Measurement of body temperature

The body temperatures were measured using a temperature data logger as described above21–23. During the experimental period, body temperature was continuously recorded every 10 min with a resolution of 0.1 °C. The data obtained were analyzed using the Rh Manager program (KN Laboratories Inc., Osaka, Japan) at an interval of 60 min.

Biochemical analysis

Serum glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), and bile acids were determined enzymatically using commercial kits (Glucose CII-test, Triglyceride E-test, Cholesterol C-test, NEFA C-test, TBA-test; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan). Insulin level was measured using rat-specific enzyme immunoassay kits (Rat insulin ELISA kit (#M1101); Morinaga Institute of Biological Science, Yokohama, Japan).

Total RNA extraction and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the liver, gastrocnemius muscle, and epididymal adipose tissue using the method described by Chomczynski and Sacchi60. After the RNA quality was determined by microchip electrophoresis with MultiNA (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan), cDNA was synthesized using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using the Thunderbird SYBR qPCR Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) and analyzed using a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The gene names and primer sequences are listed in Table S1. The relative levels of each mRNA were normalized to those of Apo E in the liver and 18S rRNA in the gastrocnemius muscle and epididymal adipose tissue.

Statistical analysis

The results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All data without spontaneous locomotor activity and body temperature were analyzed using the two-way ANOVA and then Tukey’s post hoc test was performed when the p value of interaction showed less than 0.05. Total count of spontaneous locomotor activity was analyzed by one-way ANOVA and then by Student’s t-test. Spontaneous locomotor activity in light phase, dark phase and body temperature were analyzed by two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 (IBM Inc., Tokyo, Japan).
