Background

Enterolithiasis, the presence of stone-like foreign bodies in the gastrointestinal tract, is an uncommon condition that can be hard to identify its underlying cause. Jejunal diverticula are also rare (with an incidence of less than 5%) and may be associated with enterolithiasis. The treatment of enterolithiasis typically involves surgical or endoscopic removal of the stones. However, it is essential to identify and address the cause of enterolithiasis to prevent recurrence. Herein, we report a case of true primary enterolithiasis associated with a jejunal diverticulum and describe its clinical presentation and management to provide additional insights for clinicians.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old female presented with a one-year history of intermittent abdominal pain and distension which often spontaneously resolved after one or two days. Her bowel movements remained normal and she experienced no nausea or vomiting. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no previous abdominal surgery, congenital malformations, or long-term medications. She maintained a normal diet and denied any psychiatric conditions such as trichotillomania or pica.

Laboratory investigations revealed anemia (hemoglobin 78 g/L). CT imaging showed multiple round, dense-rimmed lesions with radiolucent cores within a severely dilated pelvic intestinal segment (Figure 1A). As for patency evaluation, we recognized from CT imaging a sign of dilatation, but no exact sign of intestinal obstruction, thus we performed capsule endoscopy. Capsule endoscopy identified multiple round foreign bodies but got incarcerated 20 min after passing the pylorus (Figure 1B). Double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) reached 120 cm beyond the pylorus, revealing a dilated jejunum with multiple cavities containing several smooth, hard yellow stone-like foreign bodies (2–3 cm in size), the entrapped capsule. While distal openings were visible, they were inaccessible due to obstructing intestinal contents (Figure 1C). DBE successfully removed one foreign body (Figure 1D). X-ray Fluorescence Spectrometer(XRF) and infrared spectra analysis(IRA) analysis of the foreign body showed a composition primarily of cholate with traces of cholesterol and calcium (< 1%) (Figure 1E).

In this case, the recurring abdominal pain with spontaneous remission in this young female is likely due to the presence of foreign bodies in the upper jejunum, as no other abnormalities were detected via radiography or endoscopy. The primary consideration is determining the origin of these foreign objects.

The hard, smooth, cholate-enriched stones suggest enterolithiasis, consistent with the clinical presentation of intermittent abdominal pain. Only one site of abnormality was identified, making infectious and inflammatory conditions like tuberculosis or Crohn’s disease unlikely. The patient’s unremarkable history, negative imaging findings, and stone composition also make secondary enterolithiasis less likely, as gallstones and nephroliths are typically composed of bile acids, cholesterol, or uric acid. False primary enteroliths were ruled out due to the absence of exotic materials and a negative history of pica.

DBE revealed multiple stones trapped in a significantly dilated multi-cavity jejunum, which remained bypassed and isolated from the routine intestinal tract, as normal bowel preparation was achieved. The stones’ composition, primarily of choleic acid and low calcium, suggests their formation occurred in the proximal small bowel. While diverticula may be difficult to detect on CT or ultrasound, they should be strongly suspected given the severely dilated, non-obstructed multi-chamber intestinal cavity observed during endoscopy. Thus, the final diagnosis is true primary enterolithiasis associated with a jejunal diverticulum.

A curative surgery was performed, confirming an abnormally dilated segment of intestine. In addition to marked dilation compared with the adjacent normal bowel, an irregular interruption of the serosal surface was observed (Figure 1F). Gross examination of the specimen revealed a large jejunal diverticulum measuring 7 cm in diameter. Within the diverticulum, oval-shaped foreign bodies were identified, and the mucosal folds appeared diminished and flattened. Pathological examination of tissue from the dilated area revealed shortened villi and discontinuous or absent muscularis propria. It is noteworthy that the entire 7-cm diverticular wall exhibited interruption of the muscularis propria, as opposed to the pattern of distinct, short-segment interruptions distributed within a longer segment of otherwise normal muscle layer, further supporting the diagnosis of solitary jejunal diverticulum.

After the surgery, the patient’s wound recovered healthily and she was discharged after one week. She gradually resumed a normal diet one month after surgery. We have maintained contact with the patient since her discharge. During more than one year of follow-up, she reported no further abdominal discomfort and has regular bowel movements.

Discussion and Conclusions

The etiology of the enteroliths and the jejunal diverticulum in this case is of particular interest. Jejunal diverticula and enteroliths may mutually influence one another. In this patient, no history of long-term gastrointestinal dysmotility was identified. Given her relatively young age, a congenital jejunal diverticulum resulting in stasis of intestinal contents appears to be the most plausible explanation. Prolonged stasis may have facilitated the gradual concretion of bile and other digestive secretions, leading to enterolith formation, which in turn contributed to progressive dilation of the diverticulum. The patient’s abdominal discomfort likely resulted in decreased oral intake, subsequently causing malnutrition and moderate anemia.

Enterolithiasis, the presence of stone-like foreign bodies in the gastrointestinal tract, is an uncommon condition that can cause symptoms such as tumbling abdominal pain, distension, nausea, vomiting, and even obstruction. True primary enterolithiasis typically arises from anatomical or microenvironmental factors that predispose to stone formation, such as altered intestinal integrity (e.g., diverticular disease, blind pouches, strictures), radiation exposure, eosinophilic enteritis, mucosal diaphragmatic disease, intestinal duplication, fistulas, gut kinking, or malignancy.

False primary enteroliths result from the accumulation of indigestible materials, such as barium sulfate, chalk, lime, magnesium, or hair (in cases of trichotillomania). Secondary enterolithiasis involves stones originating from other organs, such as the gallbladder or kidney, that enter the gastrointestinal tract through abnormal tracts like a biliary-enteric fistula.

Although jejunal diverticula are rare (with an incidence of less than 5%), they account for over 80% of non-duodenal cases and can lead to the deposition of enteric contents that form stones [1]. Diverticulum-associated enterolithiasis is even less common. These stones may exacerbate diverticula through mechanical stress.

The optimal treatment for enterolithiasis typically involves surgical or endoscopic removal of the stones. However, to prevent recurrence, it is essential to address the underlying cause, whether by surgical correction of anatomical abnormalities or psychotherapy for conditions like pica [2].

This case demonstrating typical endoscopic images about enterolithiasis and a jejunal diverticulum highlights the importance of early endoscopic investigation for imaging-suspected foreign bodies, even if clinical symptoms resolve.
