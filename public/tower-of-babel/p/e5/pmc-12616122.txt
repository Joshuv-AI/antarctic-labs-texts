Distinct genetic responses to fluoxetine sertraline and citalopram in mouse cortical neurons
Authors: Hajime Yamamoto, Kentaro Abe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAntidepressants are widely prescribed for treating major depressive disorder (MDD); however, their precise therapeutic mechanisms remain incompletely understood. A notable conundrum is the substantial heterogeneity in clinical efficacy among drugs targeting the same molecular pathway, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). We performed comparative transcriptome analyses of mouse cortical neuron cultures in vitro, treated with fluoxetine, sertraline, or citalopram, revealing distinct drug-specific gene expression patterns. Utilizing self-organizing map (SOM) clustering, we identified gene sets uniquely responsive to each SSRI treatment. Corresponding in vivo analyses also demonstrated drug-specific transcriptomic changes within mouse brains. Integrating both in vitro and in vivo datasets allowed identification of genes consistently and selectively regulated by each SSRI. These findings highlight distinct molecular actions among SSRIs despite their common target. These insights underscore the importance of exploring SSRI actions beyond serotonin pathway and may facilitate the development of more effective and personalized treatment strategies for MDD.Subject areas: Pharmacology, Natural sciences, Biological sciences, Neuroscience, Molecular neuroscience

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is characterized by persistent behavioral dysfunctions and mental distress, affecting an estimated 300 million individuals worldwide.1,2 Antidepressants, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), are widely prescribed to treat depression; however, their therapeutic effect is notoriously variable, as a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve therapeutic benefit.3,4,5 The primary pharmacological mechanism of SSRIs is generally attributed to their capacity to modulate serotonergic neurotransmission through the inhibition of serotonin reuptake at synapses. Nonetheless, this mechanism alone does not fully explain the heterogeneity observed in clinical outcomes.6 Furthermore, clinical meta-analyses of antidepressant efficacy have indicated notable differences in therapeutic effectiveness and patient tolerability, even among drugs within the SSRI class.7 Consequently, despite their extensive clinical application, the precise pharmacological action underlying SSRIs remains incompletely understood. Given the heterogeneous therapeutic outcomes among SSRIs, it is crucial to evaluate and compare molecular consequences induced by each drug. Previous studies on SSRI mechanisms have often focused on individual reagents, thereby overlooking the drug-specific effects that may contribute to their distinct therapeutic profiles.

Emerging evidence has recently indicated that several SSRIs exert effects on additional molecular targets beyond their primary pharmacological action. For instance, fluoxetine has been implicated in the modulation of tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) neurotrophin receptors,8 sertraline has been suggested to influence the function of a transcription factor RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST),9 and citalopram has been shown to interact with glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β) signaling.10 The divergent effects on intracellular signaling pathways, beyond their shared modulation of serotonergic transmission, culminate in SSRI-specific gene expression profiles, potentially leading to distinct physiological outcomes. Accordingly, comparative transcriptomic analysis provides a powerful framework for elucidating the pharmacological mechanisms of SSRIs and uncovering the molecular determinants underlying their heterogeneity in therapeutic responses.

In transcriptome analysis, clustering algorithms are often utilized for the visualization and comparison of gene expression patterns. Hierarchical clustering is one of the major methods for exploring genes that exhibit specific characteristics shared among the groups. However, this approach is inherently suited for capturing relationships in gene expression differences caused by hierarchical mechanisms such as observed during cell differentiation or the evolution process, but is less effective in analyzing gene expression differences arising from non-hierarchical mechanisms.11,12 Instead, a self-organizing map (SOM), an unsupervised neural network algorithm designed for high-dimensional data analysis, provides an efficient alternative to informative and intuitive visualization. The algorithm of SOM was originally developed by Kohonen to model self-organizing principles in the visual cortex,13 and has since been widely adopted in transcriptome research to analyze gradual transitions in gene expression and gene co-expression networks.14,15,16,17 An important feature of SOM is its ability to preserve topological relationships when projecting high-dimensional data onto a two-dimensional space,13 thereby enabling the exploration of the structured expression patterns. If specific groups of genes respond distinctively to a particular stimulus, the SOM algorithm generates clusters representing this character. By treating genes as multidimensional data based on their expression changes, we applied SOM analysis to investigate the distinct transcriptomic effects of individual SSRIs. This approach allows us to reveal genes that form unique transcriptome signatures associated with each SSRI.

In this study, we hypothesized that individual SSRIs may induce distinct effects on the transcriptome through mechanisms beyond their shared serotonin-mediated actions. To test it, we implemented a comparative analysis of transcriptional changes induced by three representative SSRIs—fluoxetine, sertraline, and citalopram—using two distinct models: mouse (Mus musculus) cultured cortical neurons in vitro, which allowed us to isolate SSRI-induced effect independent of serotonin signal pathways, and the prefrontal cortex (PFC) of mice in vivo, a region consistently impaired in MDD.18 To identify gene sets uniquely modulated by the individual SSRIs, we applied SOM analysis to the transcriptional profiles in the cultures following treatment with these SSRIs. Subsequently, we compared the gene lists identified through SOM analysis with genes exhibiting consistent expression changes between in vitro and in vivo conditions. This approach revealed multiple genes specifically regulated by each individual SSRI in the mouse brains. These findings provide fundamental insights that may facilitate novel antidepressant development and support the advancement of personalized medicine in psychiatric care.

Result

SSRIs affect gene expression profiles of cultured cortical neurons

In order to assess transcriptome signatures induced by treatment of the individual SSRIs, we performed RNA-seq analysis on SSRI-treated cultured neurons prepared from the mouse embryos. However, including an intact serotonergic system would make it difficult to determine whether the gene expression differences among the SSRIs reflect intrinsic, drug-specific effects or simply differences in their efficacy at inhibiting serotonin reuptake. To address this, we used cortical cultures, which lack neurons from raphe nuclei, the primary source of serotonergic neurons. Thus, alterations in their transcriptome were not attributed to an enhancement in serotonin signals caused by synaptic reuptake inhibition. At 16 days in vitro, the cultures were treated with either vehicle, fluoxetine, sertraline, or citalopram for 24 h, after which their mRNAs were collected for RNA-seq analysis (Figure 1A). The final concentrations of the reagents in the medium were calibrated to correspond to the concentrations observed in the blood or plasma of patients taking clinical antidepressant treatment.19

Transcriptome analysis revealed different gene expression patterns in response to individual SSRI treatments compared to the vehicle control (Figure 1B; Tables S1, S2, and S3). Using a threshold of p value <0.05 and a fold change (FC) cutoff of >1.5 or <0.66, we identified 421 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for fluoxetine, 795 for sertraline, and 450 for citalopram (Figure 1C). The ratio of DEGs that were unique to each SSRI—not overlapping with DEGs from other treatments—was 50.1% for fluoxetine, 68.4% for sertraline, and 35.6% for citalopram (Figure 1D). To assess the biological relevance of the shared and distinct DEGs, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. We found that the significantly enriched pathways differed substantially across the DEGs for the three SSRIs (Figure S1). To further examine these differences in gene expression changes, we analyzed the biological features of the upregulated and downregulated DEGs induced by each SSRI. The significantly enriched pathways in the overlapped DEGs across two or more comparisons included several related to the nervous system such as “glutamatergic synapse (mmu04724)” and “GABAergic synapse (mmu04727)” in those of downregulated, and several related to immune function and cell death among those in upregulated (Figure 1E; Table S4). Analysis of unique DEGs revealed distinct enrichment patterns, with multiple pathways associated with inflammatory and cell-signal transduction differentially affected by each reagent (Figure 1E; Table S4). These findings underscore an SSRI-induced mechanism that causes transcriptomic alterations independent of serotonin signaling, with distinct gene expression changes specific to each antidepressant.

SOM analysis identifies genes that exhibit stimulus-specific expression variation

To further characterize the unique transcriptomic alterations caused by the individual SSRIs, we employed SOM analysis to systematically examine gene expression changes relative to vehicle treatment across the entire dataset. In SOM analysis, clusters—referred to as nodes—are initially arranged in a grid configuration that is randomly positioned within a multidimensional space. These nodes iteratively adjust their distribution in response to the input dataset while preserving topological relationships, ultimately assigning each gene to a specific node (Figure 2). Since the final distribution of SOM nodes preserves the topological structure of the input data, selecting nodes positioned closest to the axes and localized at the periphery allows us to identify groups of genes that uniquely respond to specific stimuli.

First, to validate the efficacy of SOM in identifying groups of genes selectively regulated by a given stimulus, we conducted a comparative analysis between a published dataset of bicuculline treatment of cortical cultures20 and our fluoxetine treatment result. We classified genes into a SOM map consisting of a 3 × 3 grid of nodes, based on the log2 transformed expression changes induced by bicuculine or fluoxetine treatment relative to their respective vehicle controls. This procedure successfully extracted gene sets that were uniquely regulated by each treatment. Specifically, nodes 1 and 9 contained genes whose expression was decreased or increased, respectively, in response to bicuculline treatment, whereas nodes 3 and 7 contained genes specifically regulated by fluoxetine treatment (Figure 2B). Notably, multiple immediate-early genes (IEGs), including Arc, Egr1, Egr2, Fos, Fosb, Fosl1, Fosl2, Jun, and Junb—all known to be upregulated by bicuculine—were included in node 9 (Figure 2C). These IEGs consistently appeared in nodes that were particularly prominent in bicuculline treatment across various SOM configurations with different node numbers (Figures 2D and 2E). Similar gene sets were consistently identified for bicuculline treatment when compared to culture treated with different SSRIs in SOM analysis (Figure S2). Moreover, the same results were also observed in the 4-dimensional data obtained by mixing bicuculline and all of the SSRI treatment datasets (Figure S3). These observations highlight the robustness of SOM analysis in identifying gene sets that are specifically regulated in response to a given stimulus, regardless of the number of stimuli being analyzed. Furthermore, since the transcriptomic changes induced by SSRIs were largely different from those observed following bicuculline treatment, the results also indicate that the observed gene changes induced by SSRIs are not directly attributable to modulation of neural activity.

Individual SSRIs exert distinct effects on transcriptome signatures in vitro

Having established that SOM analysis effectively captures gene sets specifically responsive to a certain treatment, we next applied this approach to analyze gene expression profiles in cortical cultures treated with three different SSRIs (Figure 1B). Using a 95% confidence interval as a criterion (see STAR Methods), we identified nodes that contained genes uniquely regulated by each SSRI. Specifically, fluoxetine-associated genes were clustered in nodes 1 and 2, sertraline-associated genes in nodes 7, 8, 29, 30, and 36, and citalopram-associated genes in nodes 18, 22, and 35 (Figure 3A). The total number of genes in these nodes was 125 for fluoxetine (0 upregulated, 125 downregulated), 627 for sertraline (279 upregulated, 348 downregulated), and 230 for citalopram (77 upregulated, 153 downregulated). The alterations in the expression levels of the genes assigned to these nodes were statistically different under the target SSRIs and others (Figure 3B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis following SOM-based clustering further revealed the biological significance of these grouped gene sets (Tables S5 and S6). Notably, gene lists from all clusters uniquely regulated by individual SSRIs exhibited significant enrichment with p values <0.05 and q values <0.1 in “Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction (mmu04080)” (Figure 3C). This pathway contains genes encoding plasma membrane receptors and ligands, including neurotransmitters and their receptors, potentially affecting neural functions through changes in intracellular signal transduction. These findings suggest that despite their distinct transcriptional effects, these three SSRIs converge on a common regulatory influence on receptor-mediated signaling pathways.

Individual SSRI triggered specific gene expression responses in the brain in vivo

Since the cortical culture lacks serotonergic cells, the expression of DEGs identified in cortical culture may be modulated by these SSRIs beyond their influence on serotonin signaling. Theoretically, these transcriptional alterations should also occur in vivo; however, they may be masked by the broader gene expression changes induced by SSRIs through their direct effect on serotonin reuptake inhibition. To assess whether distinct changes in gene expression among the SSRIs can be observed in vivo, we performed RNA-seq analyses on the PFC tissue samples from rodents treated with one of three antidepressants or vehicle via drinking water for 2 weeks before mRNA extraction (Figure 4A). The final concentrations of the reagents were calibrated to correspond to the concentrations observed in clinical antidepressant treatment.19,21 Comparative gene expression profiling of SSRI-treated samples versus vehicle controls revealed distinct signatures (Figure 4B; Tables S7, S8, and S9). The number of DEGs identified in each comparison was 807 for fluoxetine, 875 for sertraline, and 745 for citalopram. Among these, the proportion of unique DEGs specific to each SSRI was 51.9% for fluoxetine, 38.1% for sertraline, and 40.4% for citalopram, with observations of several significantly enriched KEGG pathways (Figure 4C; Figure S4). The enrichment analysis of these DEGs identified several significant enriched pathways (Table S10), including “Neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction,” which was also enriched in DEG analysis from in vitro experiment (Figure 3C).

Comparison of the transcriptional patterns induced by SSRI treatment in cortical neurons in vitro and brain tissues in vivo revealed distinct sets of DEGs (Figures 4D and 4E), with the in vivo data yielding larger numbers of DEGs. Principal-component analysis (PCA) further highlighted a clear divergence between in vitro and in vivo samples, with gene expression variability being more pronounced in brain tissues than in cultured neurons (Figure 4F). This difference can likely be attributed to experimental differences, including the duration of drug administration—2 weeks in vivo versus 24 h in vitro—as well as the absence of serotonin-mediated effects in vitro. Nevertheless, several genes exhibited similar expression changes in both conditions following SSRI administration. To systemically identify such genes, we performed rank-rank hypergeometric overlap (RRHO) analysis, a robust, threshold-free method for detecting shared gene expression changes between two independent datasets.22 By comparing transcriptomic changes between in vitro and in vivo conditions for each SSRI, this analysis identified 6,569, 7,106, and 5,723 genes as co-regulated in fluoxetine, sertraline, and citalopram treatment, respectively (Figure S5). Among these, 61, 265, and 68 genes were found to overlap with the genes identified by SOM analysis in the culture experiment (Figures 4G and 4H; Table S11). This result suggests the presence of genes that exhibit a conserved, SSRI-specific transcriptional response across both in vitro and in vivo conditions.

Discussion

The importance of transcriptome analysis in understanding the pharmacology of antidepressants has been recognized in recent studies, which have shown that antidepressant treatment induces transcriptome changes across various brain regions in rodents.23,24 While it is well established that chronic administration of SSRIs induces changes in brain state via serotonin signaling, limited evidence exists regarding their direct effects on intracellular signaling pathways independent of serotonin downstream. Furthermore, although research on antidepressant pharmacology across brain areas, cell types, and types of antidepressants has advanced,25,26,27,28 studies specifically comparing the molecular effects within the same pharmacological category, SSRI, remain scarce. By demonstrating drug-specific gene expression changes both in vitro and in vivo, our results provided a molecular framework for understanding how fluoxetine, sertraline, and citalopram modulate gene expression profiles. These unique transcriptional alterations among SSRIs may occur independently of serotonin signaling and elicit distinct responses in the brain specific to each SSRI. Therefore, our findings offer a potential explanation for the heterogeneous therapeutic responses observed among patients prescribed with antidepressants that are presumed to act through the same molecular mechanisms.7

The expansion of transcriptomic databases has facilitated comparative analyses to identify genes exhibiting consistent alterations across multiple datasets. This approach promotes a deeper understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms in the biological system of interest. Conventionally, heatmap visualization combined with hierarchical clustering has been widely employed to explore shared patterns of gene expression among samples. However, the extraction of genes with specific characteristics requires arbitrary thresholds to define clusters within the dendrogram, a process that is prone to potential bias. Similarly, although DEGs are commonly used to identify gene sets across different datasets, their identification relies on an arbitrary threshold for statistical significance (such as p value, false discovery rate [FDR]), and fold change criteria, raising the risk of overlooking biologically meaningful information. To mitigate these limitations, rank-rank hypergeometric overlap (RRHO) analysis,22 a threshold-free approach for identifying genes with co-regulation between independent datasets, has often been utilized.29,30 However, RRHO is inherently limited to pairwise comparisons due to its reliance on hypergeometric distribution-based calculations. Additionally, it is primarily designed to explore genes with concordant or discordant expression changes between two datasets, limiting its capacity to identify genes uniquely responsive to a specific stimulus. This constraint makes it insufficient for a comprehensive evaluation of distinct transcriptome signatures across multiple conditions. To circumvent these constraints, we employed SOM analysis, which projects high-dimensional data onto a two-dimensional space while preserving its topological structure.13 This approach enabled the identification of gene sets with specific transcriptional characteristics. By selecting nodes that exhibit distinctive expression features, SOM minimizes the biases associated with arbitrary thresholding in DEG selection, thereby enabling a more comprehensive and unbiased exploration of transcriptomic alterations.

This study identified genes uniquely regulated by a specific SSRI and compared their expression changes between in vitro and in vivo conditions, highlighting multiple genes with potential biological significance (Table S11). As the serotonin transporter is not commonly regarded as a causal factor in depression, therapeutic agents may act through mechanisms distinct from those involved in the onset of the disorder. In contrast to gene sets identified in studies focusing on transcriptomic alterations or genomic mutations associated with the etiology of depression, the gene sets identified in this study potentially reflect therapeutic responses. Further attempts at analyzing the gene expressions during the recovery process may offer new insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of depression from different perspectives.31 Although further studies are necessary, we propose the possibility that genes uniquely regulated by each SSRI may influence recovery from depression and offer insights into the mechanistic basis of therapeutic heterogeneity among these treatments. If similar changes occur in human subjects, and one of the corresponding gene products is detectable peripherally with dose-dependent expression, it may serve as a biomarker for assessing drug responsiveness. For example, Lrg1, a gene upregulated in sertraline treatment mice, encodes a secreted glycoprotein (leucine-rich alpha-2-glycoprotein 1), which has been used for serum biomarkers for cardioembolic stroke or lymphoproliferative disorder.32,33 Additionally, given that these transcriptomic changes occur independently of serotonin and considering the systemic nature of drug anti-depressant administration, it is plausible that similar changes may also occur in peripheral cells. Thus, detecting SSRI-induced molecular changes in peripheral tissues through non-invasive approaches, such as blood transcriptomics or metabolome analysis, could facilitate progress toward the clinical translation of our findings.34,35 Advancing these validation studies could significantly contribute to the development of personalized treatment strategies for MDD, including the selection of drugs with enhanced therapeutic efficacy and reduced side effects.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we identified genes differentially regulated among the three commonly prescribed SSRIs: fluoxetine, sertraline, and citalopram. Our analysis focused specifically on cortical neurons in vitro and in vivo; therefore, it remains unclear whether similar differential genetic responses occur in other brain regions or peripheral tissues. This study assessed gene expression changes in response to a single dose of each SSRI. How gene expression responds to different doses should be addressed in future studies. Since we used only male mice for in vivo analysis, it remains unknown whether the same gene sets exhibit altered expression in females, thereby limiting the generalizability of our findings. Previous studies have suggested mechanistic actions that may contribute to the specific gene expression changes induced by each SSRI. For example, fluoxetine has been shown to interact and modulate TrkB receptors,8 sertraline has been reported to bind mSin3A9 and influence the function of the transcription factor REST,36 and citalopram has been associated with modulation of GSK3β signaling, which can affect gene expression via Tcf/Lef1-mediated transcription.10 However, the direct molecular targets relevant to our in vitro or in vivo context remain undetermined in the present study. We also do not yet know whether these gene expression changes contribute to the heterogeneity of therapeutic responses among patients. Further investigation is required to determine whether these gene sets have any physiological function related to antidepressant action.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals and animal care

Mice were obtained from SLC Japan. Male C57BL/6J mice at 7 weeks of age were used for in vivo analyses, and pregnant ICR mice (Slc:ICR) were used to prepare cortical neuron cultures in vitro. Male mice were group-housed in clear plastic cages within a temperature- and humidity-controlled room under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to standard food and water. Mice were acclimated to these conditions for at least one week before experimentation. For experiments, mice were individually housed under the same environmentally controlled conditions. We used only male mice to evaluate SSRI-induced gene expression changes in order to determine whether the reagent-specific transcriptional change observed in vitro also occurs in vivo. Thus, it remains to be determined whether the same gene sets exhibit altered expression patterns in female mice. The care and experimental manipulation of animals used in this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tohoku University. All experiments and maintenance were performed following relevant guidelines and regulations.

Cell cultures

Cortical neurons were obtained from the cerebral cortex of mouse embryos of embryonic day 15.37 Their cortical regions were dissected and collected in an ice-cold buffer and treated with 0.05% trypsin at 37°C to yield single cells. Embryos of both sexes are mixed without sex determination during the tissue processing. The cells were then plated onto culture plates (IWAKI, #3820-024) coated by poly-L-lysine (SIGMA, #P2336). The cultured neurons were maintained in Neurobasal Plus Medium (Thermo Fisher, #A3582901) supplemented with B27 Plus supplement (1:50, Thermo Fisher, #A3582801), Gultamax-I (1:100, Thermo Fisher, #35050061), and penicillin-streptomycin (1:100, FUJIFILM-Wako, #168–23191) at 37°C under 5% CO2. The expression of Tph2 gene was checked by quantitative RT-PCR using a primer set of 5′-ACACCCCGGAACCAGATACAT-3′ and 5′-TTCCTGCAAGCAGGTCGTCT-3′ to verify that no serotonergic neurons were included in the culture.36

Method details

Chemicals and surgical procedures

Fluoxetine (#F0750), sertraline (#S0507), and citalopram (#C2370) were purchased from TCI; Tokyo Chemical Industry. For in vitro experiments, these compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted in a culture medium, with the final DMSO concentration being <0.01%. Cultured neurons at 16 days in vitro were exposed to the compounds (10 μM fluoxetine, 2.5 μM sertraline, and 4 μM citalopram) for 24 h. These concentrations were established based on serum levels in patients treated with these drugs and our previous experience with neuronal cultures. The duration of drug treatments was chosen to assess the direct gene responses induced by each drug while minimizing secondary or downstream effects. For in vivo analysis, these antidepressants were dissolved in DMSO and diluted in water containing 1% saccharin to mask their taste. The final concentrations were 18 mg/kg/day for fluoxetine, 10 mg/kg/day for sertraline, and 15 mg/kg/day for citalopram. Mice received diluted solutions by drinking ad libitum for two weeks. The administered doses were determined based on clinical use and the dosage guide for rodents.21 After 2 weeks of the drug administration, their brain samples were roughly dissected using a stainless brain slicer (RWD, #68707), and then the entire medial prefrontal cortex was manually dissected. These processes were performed on ice. The duration of drug treatment was chosen to reflect the clinically relevant changes that occur during recovery from depression, which typically takes several weeks.6

Transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues by the phenol-chloroform method, as previously described.38 The purified total RNA samples were processed for poly-A enriched library preparation using NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and then sequenced as paired-end reads. The raw data underwent quality control with fastp (v0.23.4), followed by alignment to the mouse genome (mm39) with Salmon (v1.8.0). Gene count matrices were generated using the tximport and TxDb.Mmusculus.UCSC.mm39.knownGene library in R (v1.30.0 and v3.18.0), and differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 library in R (v1.42.1) with Wald test and Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. The conversion from Entrez ID to gene symbol was performed using org.Mm.e.g.,.db library in R (v3.18.0). A complete linkage method was used for hierarchical clustering in R. Enrichment analysis for KEGG pathways was conducted using clusterProfiler library in R (v4.10.1), with significant terms defined as those with p-value <0.05 and q-value <0.1. RRHO analysis was performed with RRHO2 in R (v 1.0). In the transcriptome analysis of bicuculine treated cultures, we utilized published RNA-seq data (SRR5084307, SRR5084308, SRR5084312, and SRR5084313 in Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database; GSE90988).20

Self-organizing maps (SOM) analysis

We treated each gene as a multi-dimensional data point, with each dimension representing the expression change in drug-treated cultures relative to vehicle control. In the comparative analysis of fluoxetine, sertraline, and citalopram datasets, genes were clustered into a 6 × 6 hexagonal grid with the kohonen package in R (v3.0.12). Training iterations were repeated 500 times with the batch algorithm. Consequently, we explore clusters where the mean of log2FC under the target drug treatment exceeds that of the mean ± 2SD range (indicating that approximately 95% of those data are included) under the other drugs and where the mean ± 2SD of expression change under other drug treatments extend across zero while that in the condition with the target drug stays within positive or negative values. Gene sets categorized as such clusters were defined as characteristic genes that responded to a specific SSRI. The same package and parameters were used for the SOM analysis using the bicuculline treated dataset.

Quantification and statistical analysis

We performed all analyses and visualizations using R (v4.3.2). Statistical tests employed Dunnett’s test in the analysis shown in Figure 3, the significances were determined with a two-tailed p-value. This information is also provided in the corresponding figure legend. All bars with error bars represent mean ± standard deviation (SD).
