Mediterranean diet adherence and epilepsy: the mediating role of stroke based on a cross-sectional study
Authors: Mingjing Zhou, Xuyan Zhao, Peinan Liu, Xiangwei Hou, Yiyang Cao, Maode Wang, Jia Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpilepsy is a neurological disorder that can affect individuals of all genders and ages. Recent studies have indicated that the Mediterranean diet exerts a neuroprotective effect through its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties in non-Mediterranean regions. However, the association between the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy requires further elucidation.MethodsA total of 14,259 participants were enrolled in this study from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database, spanning the period from 2013 to 2018. Weighted logistic regression analysis assessed the association between Mediterranean diet adherence and epilepsy. Random forest analysis was conducted to evaluate the relative importance of diet components. Furthermore, mediation analysis with bootstrapping was employed to explore the mediating role of epilepsy.ResultsAfter adjusting for all potential covariates, higher Mediterranean diet adherence was associated with a lower risk of epilepsy (OR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.27–0.95, P = 0.033). Moreover, whole grain was identified as a pivotal component. Mediation analysis revealed that stroke functioned as a potential mediating factor in the association between Mediterranean diet adherence and epilepsy (mediation proportion = 6.49%, P = 0.034). In addition, interaction analysis suggested an interaction between the Mediterranean diet and diabetes (P = 0.029).ConclusionsThese novel findings suggested that higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet was linked with a lower risk of epilepsy, with stroke having a potential mediating role in the association. Promoting the Mediterranean diet may be a strategy for epilepsy management, supporting the need for further longitudinal studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03065-6.

Introduction

Epilepsy, characterized by a persistent tendency to trigger spontaneous epileptic seizures, is a neurological condition that affects over 50 million individuals worldwide [1]. Recent studies indicate an annual incidence of approximately 50 per 100,000 people in high-income countries, with even higher rates in low-income countries [1]. Individuals with epilepsy are susceptible to recurrent seizures, posing significant physical and mental health challenges and increasing the risk of sudden unexpected death [2, 3]. In the United States, epilepsy contributes to approximately 5,000 deaths annually [4]. Stroke is considered one of the leading causes of epilepsy and is associated with poorer clinical outcomes [5]. Despite the established pharmaceutical interventions and surgical treatments for epilepsy, modifiable risk factors for epilepsy prevention and management require further investigation.

Diet plays a vital role in the management of epilepsy. Substantial evidence demonstrates a robust correlation between nutritional intake and epilepsy [6]. It has been reported that patients suffering from epilepsy have a reduced consumption of nuts, grains, and fruits while simultaneously having a higher intake of sugar and fat [7]. The Mediterranean diet has been ranked as the most favorable diet for improving public health by US News and World Report for six consecutive years [8]. Originating in Mediterranean regions, this dietary pattern emphasizes fruits, nuts, whole grains, vegetables, fish, legumes, olive oil, and moderate alcohol consumption while limiting red meat [9]. This dietary structure is notable for its high fiber, monounsaturated fat, and antioxidant contents [10]. Emerging evidence suggests the Mediterranean diet may confer neuroprotective benefits against disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and epilepsy in non-Mediterranean regions [11–15]. Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties of the Mediterranean diet contribute to a protective effect against acute cardiovascular events, including stroke, particularly ischemic stroke [16, 17].

However, extant studies investigating the association between the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy have predominantly focused on children with epilepsy [15, 18]. Herein, this study not only explored the association in adults using large-scale population data but also, for the first time, investigated the mediating role of stroke, which was considered a major cause of epilepsy in this correlation.

In summary, this study aimed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the association between epilepsy and the Mediterranean diet, specifically exploring the potential mediating role of stroke. The present study hypothesized that there would be a correlation between higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet and a lower prevalence of epilepsy, with this correlation being potentially mediated by a smaller risk of stroke. These findings could offer novel insights for epilepsy management and expand dietary options for individuals with epilepsy in non-Mediterranean regions.

Methods

Study population and design

Data were obtained from the NHANES database (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). As a renowned database, NHANES employed a continuous multistage probability sampling method to record comprehensive and representative health and nutrition information of the American population. Following the NHANES protocol, we selected data from 2013 to 2018 to include reliable clinical information regarding epilepsy diagnosis, dietary intake, and other relevant covariates. To obtain more reliable information, participants below 20 years of age were excluded, as previously recommended in previous research [19, 20]. Moreover, participants lacking complete data on the essential research variables were also excluded. The diagnosis of epilepsy and stroke

To identify individuals with epilepsy, this study obtained their utilization of prescription medications and screened the international classification of diseases-tenth revision-clinical modification (ICD-10-CM) code of G40 (Epilepsy and recurrent seizures) for the use of these medications. The diagnosis of epilepsy was then determined based on the medication utilization and ICD-10-CM codes, as the previous research reported [19]. The detailed medications can be found in Table S1 in Supplementary file 1. Stroke diagnosis was determined by responses to the medical condition questionnaires, which asked if the subject had previously experienced a stroke. This was in accordance with previous studies [21, 22].

The assessment of Mediterranean diet score

The collection of all dietary data was conducted through the 24-h recall method among study participants enrolled in the NHANES. Subsequent to the initial dietary recall, which was conducted in a mobile examination center, participants were contacted via telephone for a second dietary recall after 3–10 days. To ensure the credibility of the results, the average food intake of two dietary recalls was used to assess diet behaviors. In addition, the nutritional content of the food consumed was ascertained using the United States Department of Agriculture food composition database. As previously validated in studies [23, 24], the alternate Mediterranean diet (aMED) score was utilized to assess adherence to the Mediterranean diet. The components of this diet model included fruit, vegetables, whole grains, nuts, fish, legumes, red meat, alcohol, and the ratio of monounsaturated fatty acids to saturated fatty acids (MUFAs/SFAs). For the components of fruit, vegetables, whole grain, nuts, fish, legumes, and MUFAs/SFAs, the score recorded 1 point if intake of these components was higher than the cohort-specific median. Conversely, 0 points were recorded for intake levels below the cohort-specific median. It was further noted that red meat was regarded as an unbeneficial dietary component, and consequently, its scoring system was established in a reverse way. Furthermore, alcohol intake ranging from 5 to 15 g per day for women and 10–25 g per day for men was assigned 1 point. Consequently, the scores of aMED ranged from 0 to 9, which suggested the adherence to the Mediterranean diet in non-Mediterranean regions. Participants were then divided into low and high adherence groups according to their aMED scores and median values.

Other covariates

The study incorporated a range of demographic variables, including age, gender, race, education, marital status, and poverty–income ratio (PIR) level. The racial categories encompassed Mexican–American, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Other. Education was categorized as below high school and high school or above. Marital status was classified as not married and married. The PIR (poverty–income ratio) level was stratified into low (< 1.3), middle (1.3–3.5), and high (> 3.5). The health-related variables comprised smoking, drinking, body mass index (BMI), calories, white blood counts, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) level, hypertension, coronary heart disease and diabetes. Drinking status was categorized as follows: never drinker, past drinker, and current drinker. SII was defined as the result of neutrophil/lymphocyte counts multiplied by platelets [25] and divided into low and high levels based on the cohort median. Hypertension was determined by a medical diagnosis and recorded blood pressure measurements, with systolic pressure defined as ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic pressure defined as ≥ 90 mmHg. Three separate blood pressure measurements were taken and the average result was used for analysis. Diabetes was determined by a medical diagnosis or physician examination data of hemoglobin A1C (≥ 6.5%) and fasting blood glucose (≥ 126 mg/dL) [19]. Coronary heart disease was determined by a response to the question of whether the participants had suffered from coronary heart disease.

Statistical analysis

To ascertain a credible association between the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy, the study data were adjusted according to the weight of the diet. In the study, three cycles (2013–2014, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018) were incorporated, and the combined sampling weights were calculated as “combined weight = (1/3) * single cycle weight”. These approaches were in accordance with the database guidelines (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/weighting.aspx). Analyses incorporated sampling weights to ensure nationally representative estimates. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard error) and compared using t tests. Categorical variables were presented as weighted frequencies (percentages) and compared using chi-square tests. Weighted logistic regression models assessed the association between Mediterranean diet adherence (score or different levels), stroke (yes vs. no), and epilepsy. Three models were constructed: unadjusted, adjusted for age, gender, and race; and fully adjusted for all covariates listed above. The relative importance of Mediterranean diet components was evaluated using random forest analysis. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 3 knots (at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) were used to visualize the linear association between the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy more clearly. What’s more, mediation analysis was performed to quantify the potential proportion of the total effect of Mediterranean diet adherence on epilepsy mediated by stroke. In instances where the sample size was limited and the design was intricate, the bootstrap was utilized in accordance with the methodologies reported in previous studies [26–28]. Here, bootstrapping with 1,000 replications was employed to estimate the confidence interval (CI) for the mediation effect and direct effect. Moreover, stratified analyses and interaction analyses were employed to reveal the association between epilepsy and other covariates. Results are presented as odds ratio (OR) with 95% CI. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

1. Demographic characteristics of epilepsy and non-epilepsy participants

After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1), 14,259 participants were finally enrolled in this study. Based on antiepileptic medication usage and ICD-10-CM coding, 121 participants were classified with epilepsy. This equated to 1,724,876 individuals in the US adult population, following the application of NHANES sample weights. Compared to controls, participants with epilepsy had significantly lower Mediterranean diet adherence (P = 0.005) and higher stroke prevalence (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Significant differences were also observed in age (P = 0.029) and marital status (P = 0.017), suggesting associations between epilepsy and patients’ health and social circumstances. Notably, these findings indicated a potential relationship between Mediterranean diet adherence, stroke, and epilepsy. MED Mediterranean diet

Participants were also stratified by Mediterranean diet adherence level (high vs. low). Those with high adherence had significantly lower prevalences of stroke (P = 0.026) and epilepsy (P = 0.005) (Table S2, Supplementary file 1). No significant differences were observed for hypertension (P = 0.126), coronary heart disease (P = 0.295), or diabetes (P = 0.382) between the diet groups, suggesting a more pronounced protective role of the diet against neurological disorders and acute cardiovascular events like stroke.

2. Association between Mediterranean diet, stroke, and epilepsy

Subsequently, multivariate logistic regression analyses were utilized to investigate the comprehensive association between the Mediterranean diet, stroke, and epilepsy in Table 2. Following the adjustment of all relevant confounding variables, a significant association was observed between epilepsy and Mediterranean diet level (OR = 0.50, 95% CI 0.27–0.95, P = 0.033), as well as between epilepsy and stroke (OR = 8.71, 95% CI 4.01–18.90, P < 0.001). In addition, statistical analysis also demonstrated the negative association between the prevalence of epilepsy and Mediterranean diet scores in Table S3. Moreover, a three-spot RCS based on the 10th, 50th, and 90th was selected by its superior Akaike information criterion (Fig. 2). The findings indicated the clear linear relationship between the scores of the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy. To further explore the contributions of specific Mediterranean diet components to epilepsy risk, a random forest algorithm model was established. This machine learning assay revealed that intake of whole grain and fruit is of greater importance (Fig. 3), which may be associated with increased dietary fiber and vitamins. 3. Stroke was a potential mediator in the association between the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy

As was indicated in the previous studies, stroke has been reported as a risk factor for epilepsy, with 2–14% of stroke survivors suffering post-stroke epilepsy [29, 30]. To investigate the possible intermediary role of stroke between the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy, mediation analysis was carried out in the present study. As illustrated in Table 3 and Fig. 4, following the adjustment of pertinent covariates, the potential mediation role of stroke remained statistically significant according to the results of the bootstrapping analysis (proportion mediated = 6.49%, P = 0.034). This finding suggested that a higher adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a lower risk of epilepsy, with a corresponding smaller risk of stroke. 4. Subgroup and interaction analysis

To further investigate the heterogeneity of the aforementioned association, stratified analyses and interaction analyses were employed. As illustrated in Fig. 5, statistical heterogeneity was observed among subgroups categorized by age, gender, race, education, marital status, PIR level, smoking, drinking, BMI level, SII level, hypertension, coronary heart disease, and diabetes (all P < 0.05). Furthermore, a significant difference was observed among participants aged between 40 and 60 years, those of other races, those with a BMI over 25 kg/m2, and those with histories of coronary heart disease and diabetes. Discussion

Epilepsy is a highly prevalent neurological disorder affecting individuals across all demographics, including genders, ages, geographical locations and ethnicities [31]. Characterized by recurrent seizures, it is frequently associated with physical and psychological comorbidities, such as migraine, depression, and anxiety [32], underscoring the global importance of effective management strategies.

Dietary patterns play a crucial role in epilepsy prevention and management. The ketogenic diet (KD), characterized by high fat, moderate protein, and very low carbohydrate intake, is an established therapeutic option, particularly for drug-resistant epilepsy [33]. The KD is characterized by elevated fat intake, moderate protein intake, and low carbohydrate intake, resulting in increased ketone body production. These ketone bodies have been demonstrated to contribute to neurological protection, antioxidant activity, and inflammatory response [34]. However, the KD is also associated with adverse effects, including nausea, constipation and dyslipidemia, which can pose a significant challenge to adherence, particularly among children and adolescents [35, 36].

In contrast, the Mediterranean diet, consistently ranked as the top dietary pattern [8], features moderate fat intake (~ 37%, predominantly monounsaturated), high fiber (~ 33 g/day) [37], and diverse components that enhance palatability and adherence while minimizing gastrointestinal side effects [18, 34]. Although seemingly divergent from the high-fat KD, the Mediterranean diet shares protective elements; notably, its emphasis on whole grains provides dietary fiber [38], which has been reported as a protective food component in epilepsy patients [39]. Herein, based on the NHANES database, this study found significantly lower Mediterranean diet adherence among adults with epilepsy compared to controls. Within this diet pattern, higher intake of whole grains and fruits, coupled with lower red meat consumption, were identified as key protective components, highlighting the importance of dietary fiber and vitamins.

Stroke is a leading cause of epilepsy and is often associated with elevated mortality and increased disability. Previous studies have indicated a correlation between stroke and both the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy. Large-scale randomized controlled trials have demonstrated the protective influence of the Mediterranean diet on stroke [16], while Lin R et al. have established a prediction model of post-stroke epilepsy [40]. These findings provide a novel perspective on the diet strategies for both cranial vascular diseases and neurological disorders. However, further research is required to explore this novel insight. In the present study, we sought to investigate a more comprehensive correlation between the Mediterranean diet, epilepsy, and stroke. Following the establishment of the mediation models, we presented the possible intermediary role of stroke after adjusting for other covariates. The present findings also revealed the potential dietary effects on neurological disorders through the management of cerebrovascular diseases, which may provide a novel perspective on the dietary management of epilepsy.

In addition, following the analysis of subgroup and interaction, diabetes had a significant interaction with the relationship between the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy. For those individuals with diabetes, following the Mediterranean diet surprisingly decreased more risk of suffering epilepsy. This result may be caused by the neuroprotective effect of glucose-lowering drugs [41].

This study leveraged the NHANES database, which provided extensive demographic, health, and detailed dietary data that is difficult to obtain from single institutions. However, limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small number of epilepsy cases limited subgroup analyses. Lack of data on seizure types, frequency, or other relevant disorders prevented more nuanced investigations. Therefore, combining data from multiple databases and clinical centers in future studies would be valuable. In addition, the use of medication may have interfered with the observed correlations, necessitating further in-depth investigations. Second, while neuroinflammation is implicated in epilepsy [42], we found no difference in systemic inflammatory markers (WBC, SII) between groups. This discrepancy might reflect the blood–brain barrier’s role; future studies incorporating cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers could elucidate neuroinflammation’s potential mediating role. Third, the cross-sectional design precluded causal inference. Despite the findings of mediation analysis suggesting plausible pathways involving stroke, the assumptions regarding the temporal sequence of the mediator remained unconfirmed. Consequently, the observed associations remain hypothetical due to unresolved time-related ambiguity and potential residual confounding (e.g., genetic factors). Nevertheless, the significant correlations provided a strong rationale for future longitudinal studies assessing dietary patterns and incident epilepsy, along with stroke, especially in high-risk populations.

Conclusion

This study provided novel insights into the complex interplay between dietary patterns, cerebrovascular disease, and neurological disorders. We identified a significant inverse association between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and epilepsy risk in US adults, with stroke acting as a potential mediator in this relationship. If the correlations are confirmed by prospective studies, promoting the Mediterranean diet could emerge as a valuable strategy for epilepsy prevention and management in non-Mediterranean regions.
