Molecular genetic influences on sustained attention and executive processes and their links with psychopathology in the AFFECT study
Authors: Justin D Tubbs, Travis T Mallard, Maria Dalby, Yunxuan Jiang, Younga H Lee, Karmel W Choi, Tian Ge, Niels Plath, Lene Hammer-Helmich, Julie M Granka, 23andMe Research Group, Andrew D Grotzinger, David Hinds, Jordan W Smoller, Joshua W Buckholtz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoth sustained attention (SA) and executive functioning (EF) are critical for adaptive behavior and compromised across a range of psychiatric disorders, yet their etiological relationships remain underexplored. Here, we report the first multivariate characterization of molecular genetic influences on SA (vigilance, lapse recovery cost, lapse propensity) and EF (processing speed, response selection, working memory). In collaboration with 23andMe, we report previously unpublished genome-wide association studies of these phenotypes in a single cohort of more than 20,000 individuals enriched for diagnoses of major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder. We used Genomic Structural Equation Modeling to formally model patterns of genetic covariance among these task-based measures of cognition, and their relationships with other cognitive, clinical, and imaging-derived phenotypes. We identified two distinct latent genetic factors: one influencing EF and one influencing SA. Both the EF and SA factors were genetically correlated with cognitive and clinical phenotypes, with each latent factor uniquely linked to liability for psychiatric disorders, including attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. However, genetic correlations with imaging-derived neuroanatomical phenotypes were modest and non-significant after correction for multiple comparisons. Collectively, these results provide an initial multivariate genetic characterization of SA and EF, though replication in larger and more diverse samples will be important. They suggest that genetic influences on sustained attention are generally distinct from those that influence executive function. The EF and SA factors show distinct patterns of genetic overlap with multiple cognitive and psychiatric outcomes, underscoring the need for more granular cognitive phenotyping to generate new insights into the genetic architecture of cognition and the etiology of psychopathology.Subject terms: Genetics, Psychology

Introduction

Sustained attention, defined as the ability to maintain a stable on-task attentional state over extended periods and recover that state following transient disengagement, is a fundamental aspect of higher-order cognition [1, 2]. Sustained attention is prominently implicated in numerous forms of psychiatric and neurological dysfunction. Consequently, sustained attention has long been of interest to scientists and clinicians, as difficulties in this domain are prevalent across diverse patient populations, including those with psychiatric, neurodevelopmental, and neurodegenerative disorders, as well as acquired brain injuries [1, 3–5]. Underscoring its relevance to psychopathology, the National Institute of Mental Health has recognized multiple indices of sustained attention (e.g. attentional lapses, response time variability) as critical elements of the Cognitive Systems domain within the Research Domain Criteria framework [6].

An important conceptual question is how sustained attention relates to executive function. While sustained attention is closely related to executive cognition, dominant cognitive neuroscience models typically distinguish attentional stability (or vigilance) from executive control operations such as working memory updating, inhibitory control and set shifting [7–10]. This is consistent with work suggesting that sustained attention modulates the ability to implement executive control, while remaining partly separable from the executive operations typically emphasized in classic executive function taxonomies [11–13]. These findings raise the possibility that sustained attention and executive function may reflect partially distinct etiological influences despite their phenotypic interdependence.

Despite its clear importance, molecular genetic influences on sustained attention have not been well characterized and its genetic covariance with executive function have not been explored. Decades of cognitive science and neuroscience have advanced our understanding of sustained attention [1, 2], but genetically informative work has largely been limited to twin heritability studies [14–16] and relatively small genome-wide association studies (GWAS) [17–20]. This slow progress is even more apparent when compared to GWAS of other complex traits related to cognition, such as general intelligence [21] and educational attainment [22], which have received considerably more focus and resources. Prior multivariate genomic studies of executive function have advanced our understanding of shared etiology among cognitive processes [23]. Given the substantial progress in understanding other aspects of cognition, joint characterization of the etiological influences that drive individual differences in executive function and sustained attention could provide critical insights into the heritable components of these important phenotypes, and inform our understanding of the latent genetic structure of cognition.

In the present study, we address this gap in the literature by describing the largest GWAS of the gradual-onset continuous performance task (gradCPT), which measures the ability to maintain consistent responding to frequently presented stimuli for extended epochs and inhibit responding to infrequent, unpredictably presented stimuli. Specifically, we report GWAS results for multiple indices of sustained attention (vigilance, lapse propensity, and lapse recovery cost) measured by this task, as well as GWAS results for several measures of executive function in the AFFECT study [24], a large cohort enriched for individuals with diagnoses of major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder (maximum N = 23,318 individuals with European-like genomes). We then use Genomic Structural Equation Modeling (GenomicSEM) [25] to formally characterize patterns of genetic sharing and differentiation among these cognitive phenotypes in an exploratory, data-driven approach. Finally, to determine the validity of our observed factors and better understand the common and unique impacts of sustained attention and executive function on development and psychopathology, we evaluate genetic relationships between the identified latent factors and a variety of cognitive, neuropsychiatric, and imaging-derived phenotypes. Collectively, our results provide new insights into the genetic architectures of higher-order cognitive processes and clarify their broader etiological structure.

Methods

Sample recruitment and phenotyping procedures

Participants were recruited as part of the Affective disorders, Environment, and Cognitive Trait (AFFECT) study in collaboration with 23andMe, Inc [24]. Study design, participant recruitment, and data collection are described in a prior publication [24]. Briefly, participants were invited to participate in an online study involving genetic data collection and a series of surveys including task-based cognitive assessments over the course of 9 months. Recruitment was conducted using a combination of the 23andMe research participant pool and social media advertisements. The overall sample was designed to be enriched for participants with a self-reported diagnosis of bipolar disorder or major depressive disorder with pharmacological treatment, while control participants were restricted to those reporting no history of major psychiatric diagnoses or prior psychiatric prescription. A total of 23,835 non-psychiatric controls, 14,768 major depressive disorder cases, and 9864 bipolar disorder cases completed consent procedures, provided a DNA sample, and answered the baseline set of questionnaires. Participants provided informed consent and volunteered to participate in the research online, under a protocol approved by the external AAHRPP-accredited IRB, Ethical & Independent (E&I) Review Services. As of 2022, E&I Review Services is part of Salus IRB (https://www.versiticlinicaltrials.org/salusirb). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

An extensive cognitive battery was administered to each participant across nine online data collection sessions over the course of a nine-month study period [24]. In the current study, we focus a subset of tasks from the full battery that yield measures of distinct executive processes: the Digit-Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) [26], the gradCPT [27, 28], and the THINC-Integrated Tool (THINC-it) [29]. The DSST was administered at the first session, gradCPT was assessed at sessions 3 and 9, and the THINC-it was administered at sessions 2 and 7. The DSST takes approximately 2 min to administer, the gradCPT is a 12-min paradigm, and the THINC-it takes 10–15 min to complete in total. From these tasks, a set of seven variables indexing conceptually distinct executive processes were selected for GWAS analysis. Processing speed was measured using the number of correct trials from the DSST. Measures of vigilance (sustained attention variability), lapse recovery cost, and lapse propensity, were derived from the gradCPT, using the coefficient of variation of response times for correct responses to target trials, post-error slowing (the difference in response time for target trials following correct vs. incorrect responses to targets), and false-alarm rate for no-go trials, respectively. Measures of response selection, working memory, and cognitive flexibility derived from three of the THINC-it tasks: log-transformed mean reaction time for correct symbols in the “Spotter” (choice reaction time) task, number of correct responses in the “Symbol Check” (1-back test) task, and the time to complete the “Trails” (trails making test B) task. If multiple observations were available for a participant, the median value was used. All cognitive phenotypes were oriented such that positive values indicate better performance.

Preprocessing, quality control, and GWAS procedures

Complete SNP genotyping and imputation procedures are described in a prior publication [24]. Briefly, saliva samples underwent DNA extraction and genotyping at the National Genetics Institute, a CLIA-licensed clinical laboratory under the Laboratory Corporation of America. Genotyping, phasing, and imputation followed the standardized 23andMe pipeline [24]. Following preprocessing and quality control, approximately 9.2 million high-quality genotyped and imputed SNPs were available for analysis.

All GWAS analyses were restricted to individuals with European-like genomes, as identified using a local ancestry-based algorithm [30] in a set of maximally unrelated individuals [31]. GWAS were performed using linear regression on imputed and genotyped SNPs. Each cognitive phenotype was regressed on genotype dosage with sex, age, five genetic principal components, and genotyping platform as covariates. For each GWAS, SNPs with fewer than 20% of the available sample size for a given trait were removed prior to subsequent analyses. As noted in Table 1, final analytic sample sizes for each GWAS ranged from 10,129 participants with data on lapse recovery cost to 23,318 participants with data on processing speed. GWAS were performed by members of the 23andMe research team, with summary statistics provided to the MGH study team for subsequent modeling.

GC genomic control.

GWAS summary statistics were prepared for downstream analysis using GenomicSEM R package version 0.0.5 [25]. In line with best practices, variants were only retained if they had imputation quality scores ≥0.9 (when available), minor allele frequency ≥0.01, and were present in the HapMap3 reference panel. For external psychiatric phenotypes, the sum of effective sample sizes were calculated for use in GenomicSEM [32].

Heritability, genetic correlation, and factor analyses

SNP-based heritability (hSNP2) was estimated for each cognitive trait by applying LD score regression (LDSC) [33], as implemented in the GenomicSEM R package [25]. Similarly, GenomicSEM was used to estimate the genome-wide covariance and sampling variance matrices via multivariable LDSC.

Having no a priori hypothesis as to the multivariate genetic factor structure of our cognitive measures, we applied two non-graphical scree tests to determine the optimal number of latent factors required to describe the observed genetic covariance matrix: the acceleration factor and optimal coordinates [34]. Parallel analysis [35] was also performed as a complementary metric for determining the optimal number of factors. These findings (see Results section) led us to perform exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with promax rotation for one, two, and three latent factors (i.e., k ± 1) using the GenomicSEM R package. Model selection was guided by assessing incremental variance explained and parsimony of the implied latent factor structure.

Based on EFA model results (see Results section), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the fit of the EFA-implied optimal factor model using unit variance identification. Model fit was evaluated using the model χ2 statistic, the comparative fit index (CFI), and the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR).

Genetic correlations with other complex traits

Genetic correlations (rg) were estimated between the identified latent factors and other complex traits measured in external samples (Supplementary Table 1) using GenomicSEM. These phenotypes were organized into three broad categories: cognition and education, psychopathology, and cortical morphology (see Supplementary Table 1 for a complete list). A χ2 difference test was used to assess whether the latent factors differed in their genetic correlations with other traits. Essentially, this involved testing whether model fit significantly worsened when the rg between each factor and the external trait were constrained to be equivalent. Additionally, QTrait heterogeneity tests were used to evaluate the extent to which genetic relationships between the factors and an external trait were adequately explained by the common pathway implied by the factor structure [36]. To account for multiple testing, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was used to control the false discovery rate (FDR) at α = 0.05 within each family of tests for each factor. Although raw p-values are reported in the text, statistical significance was determined by an FDR-corrected p-value (q-value) of less than 0.05.

Conditional genetic associations between cognitive factors and externally measured traits

Being interested in the unique contributions of the model-identified latent genetic factors, we used GenomicSEM to fit multiple genetic regression models, quantifying the degree to which genetic associations between the factors and other traits were unique (i.e., the conditional genetic association). Specifically, each external trait Y was regressed onto the two identified latent cognitive factors simultaneously, taking the form Y~F1 + F2. This provides estimates of the independent (or unique) association of each factor with Y, conditional on its association with the other factor.

Results

Sustained attention and executive processes are influenced by common variants

Univariate LDSC results for each of the seven executive processes are reported in Table 1, with Manhattan and QQ plots shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. All phenotypes exhibited statistically significant hSNP2, ranging from 0.08 for cognitive flexibility (SE = 0.03) to 0.18 for both processing speed (SE = 0.03) and lapse propensity (SE = 0.04). Despite exhibiting a nominally significant hSNP2, the THINC-it cognitive flexibility measure (time to completion on the “Trails” task) was excluded from subsequent analyses. Its inclusion required extensive smoothing to ensure a positive-definite genetic covariance matrix, suggesting that this GWAS had low statistical power and was unsuitable for genetic correlation and factor analysis.

Genetic correlations among the six cognitive processes selected for analysis are presented in Fig. 1a (Supplementary Table 2). Briefly, we observed two clusters of phenotypes. One cluster consisted of vigilance, lapse propensity, and lapse recovery cost, which had modest to strong genetic correlations with one another, ranging from 0.21 to 0.77. The second cluster consisted of processing speed, working memory, and response selection, which showed relatively high positive genetic correlations with one another, ranging from 0.58 to 0.83. These three measures exhibited strong negative genetic correlation with lapse recovery cost (−0.64, −0.35, −0.6, respectively).

Genomic factor analysis reveals complex patterns of pleiotropy among cognitive processes

Further examination of the standardized genetic covariance matrix revealed that the first two eigenvectors explained the majority (81.3%) of the total genetic variance across cognitive measures, accounting for 49.2 and 32.1% of the variance, respectively (Fig. 1b). All three factor retention methods suggested that a two-factor solution would provide the best balance of fit and parsimony.

The results of our EFA confirmed that a two-factor solution provided a balance of parsimony, variance explained, and interpretability. While a single factor explained 46.6% of the total genetic variance, two correlated factors explained 78.3% of the variance. The addition of a third factor yielded marginal improvement, bringing the total variance explained to 84.1%, but contributed little in terms of interpretability, as the third factor largely explained variance in a single phenotype (lapse recovery cost). The results of our two-factor EFA are visualized in Fig. 1c (Supplementary Table 3). Informed by these results, we used GenomicSEM to fit a confirmatory factor model with two latent genetic factors that were allowed to correlate (Fig. 1c). Fit indices indicated that this model provided a reasonably good fit to the observed data (χ2[7] = 17.07, CFI = 0.96, SRMR = 0.11). Although the residuals between observed and model-implied correlations were slightly above optimal thresholds, deviations were minor and not specific to any particular measure or combination of measures. Given its divergent cross-loading on the two factors, we also assessed a post-hoc model excluding lapse recovery cost. This reduced model resulted in worse fit than the full model across all metrics (χ2[4] = 18.49, CFI = 0.93, SRMR = 0.11). Likewise, constraining lapse recovery cost to load exclusively onto the first factor (χ2[8] = 20.64, CFI = 0.94, SRMR = 0.14) or the second factor (model failed to converge) did not improve model fit.

The first factor, which we refer to as Executive Function, exhibited strong positive relationships with processing speed (λ = 1, SE = 0.12, p = 4.42e-18), response selection (λ = 0.72, SE = 0.12, p = 1.84e-9), and working memory (λ = 0.80, SE = 0.12, p = 1.68e-10), as well as a strong negative relationship with lapse recovery cost (λ = −0.78, SE = 0.19, p = 4.58e-5). This pattern of factor loadings suggests that the Executive Function factor represents a broad dimension of executive control ability. The second factor, which we refer to as Sustained Attention, exhibited a strong positive relationship with vigilance (λ = 1, SE = 0.33, p = 2.45e-3) and a moderate positive relationship with lapse propensity (λ = 0.58, SE = 0.23, p = 1.36e-2). Although lapse recovery cost was also allowed to load onto this factor per the EFA results, there was no statistically significant relationship (λ = 0.47, SE = 0.34, p = 0.17). Thus, this latent factor appears to index processes more specifically related to the stability of sustained attention rather than executive ability. Interestingly, a modest positive correlation between the two latent genetic factors was observed, though it was not statistically significant (rg = 0.25, SE = 0.15, p = 0.10). This may be a function of statistical power rather than a true null genetic correlation between the factors.

Sustained attention has nuanced relationships with other aspects of cognition

To evaluate the validity of our latent factors, we first estimated genetic correlations between the modeled factors and cognitive traits measured in external samples. These results generally support our characterizations of the latent factors (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table 4). For example, Executive Function showed the strongest genetic correlations with a previously published GWAS of the g factor (rg = 0.80, SE = 0.07, p = 1.4e-31) and an executive function factor (rg = 0.80, SE = 0.06, p = 1.9e-42). In general, Executive Function exhibited stronger positive genetic correlations with each of the external cognitive traits than Sustained Attention. Formal statistical tests indicated that these differences were statistically significant (ps ≤ 8.8e-6, Supplementary Table 5). One notable exception to this pattern was educational attainment, where the moderately positive genetic correlations observed with Executive Function (rg = 0.30, SE = 0.04, p = 4.7e-14) and with Sustained Attention (rg = 0.26, SE = 0.08, p = 2.1e-3) did not significantly differ from one another (p = 0.07, Supplementary Table 5).

To estimate the unique contributions of each latent factor to the observed associations with external cognitive traits, we jointly regressed each trait onto the Executive Function and Sustained Attention factors (Supplementary Table 6). Despite most traits exhibiting at least a nominally significant genetic correlation with Sustained Attention, these associations were notably attenuated when jointly regressed onto both factors simultaneously (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Only educational attainment (p = 0.037) and non-cognitive skills (p = 0.048) were significantly associated with Sustained Attention when jointly regressed on Executive Function. Conversely, genetic associations between external cognitive traits and Executive Function did not exhibit significant attenuation. These results suggest that the modest, non-significant positive genetic correlation between the two latent factors may at least in part explain why external cognitive traits exhibit significant zero-order positive genetic correlations with Sustained Attention that do not hold in conditional analyses.

Next, we performed QTrait analyses to evaluate heterogeneity in the patterns of association between each of the factor indicators and external cognitive phenotypes (Supplementary Table 4). When the relationship between an external trait and a latent factor has a significant QTrait p-value, there is evidence that the factor does not adequately explain the covariance patterns between the external trait and the factor’s indicators. Here, we found relatively low QTrait signal for the Executive Function factor, with only one indicator (episodic memory) exhibiting significant heterogeneity (QTrait p = 1.4e-3). However, statistically significant heterogeneity was observed for most of the significant genetic correlations between external cognitive phenotypes and the Sustained Attention factor.

Patterns of heterogeneity are visualized in Fig. 2b for the most significant QTrait signal (p = 6.76e-10; g factor, Sustained Attention), least significant QTrait signal (p = 1; educational attainment, Sustained Attention), and episodic memory, which showed significant heterogeneity for both factors (ps = 1.14e-4). QTrait results for the remaining external cognitive traits are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 4. In general, we found that genetic correlations between external traits and Executive Function indicators varied as a function of the indicators’ unstandardized factor loadings (λ), suggesting that genetic relationships are consistent with the factor structure. However, this was often not the case for Sustained Attention indicators, as lapse recovery cost often deviated from this pattern. Notably, lapse recovery cost was often estimated as having a negative genetic correlation with the external cognitive traits, but a positive loading on Sustained Attention.

Sustained attention and executive function have distinct links to psychopathology

To assess whether genetic influences on Sustained Attention and Executive Function were related to risk for psychopathology, we estimated the genetic correlation between each latent factor and 13 psychiatric disorders measured in external samples (Supplementary Table 1). We found that most psychiatric disorders showed significant negative genetic correlations with Executive Function, while genetic correlations with Sustained Attention tended to be significantly attenuated or not significantly different from zero (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

After accounting for multiple testing using an FDR correction, we found that Executive Function was significantly genetically correlated with ADHD (rg = −0.28, SE = 0.05, p = 6.00e-8), alcohol use disorder (rg = −0.31, SE = 0.10, p = 2.21e-3), anxiety disorder (rg = −0.36, SE = 0.07, p = 1.74e-6), bipolar disorder (rg = −0.27, SE = 0.05, p = 1.85e-7), cannabis use disorder (rg = −0.34, SE = 0.07, p = 2.19e-6), major depressive disorder (rg = −0.40, SE = 0.07, p = 8.95e-10), obsessive-compulsive disorder (rg = −0.45, SE = 0.07, p = 8.71e-10), opioid use disorder (rg = −0.53, SE = 0.09, p = 2.01e-9), and schizophrenia (rg = −0.38, SE = 0.04, p = 1.82e-17). Sustained Attention, on the other hand, was only significantly genetically correlated with ADHD (rg = −0.39, SE = 0.14, p = 4.75e-3) and alcohol use disorder (rg = −0.65, SE = 0.20, p = 9.08e-4). While the significant ADHD genetic correlations were consistent with the factor structure, alcohol use disorder exhibited a significant QTrait signal for Sustained Attention (Fig. 3b).

The two latent factors exhibited significant differences in genetic overlap for several psychiatric disorders, suggesting that the reduced number of significant links between Sustained Attention and psychopathology was not solely a consequence of lower statistical power (Supplementary Table 5). However, the two disorders that were significantly genetically correlated with both factors (ADHD and alcohol use disorder) showed non-significant between-factor differences (ps ≥ 0.20).

Given that both factors were significantly genetically correlated with these disorders, and those genetic correlations were statistically similar in magnitude, we next asked whether the genetic relationships were independent of one another. To test this, we fit genetic regression models that estimated the conditional genetic association between those psychiatric disorders and Sustained Attention and Executive Function (Supplementary Table 6). Importantly, we found that both factors were uniquely associated with ADHD (Sustained Attention: βg = −0.34, SE = 0.15, p = 0.02; Executive Function: βg = −0.20, SE = 0.08, p = 6.72e-3), but only Sustained Attention was uniquely associated with alcohol use disorder (Sustained Attention: βg = −0.61, SE = 0.21, p = 3.47e-3; Executive Function: βg = −0.15, SE = 0.13, p = 0.24). These results suggest that the genetic overlap between ADHD and cognition involves partially independent contributions from multiple cognitive processes related to executive function and sustained attention, while the genetic overlap between alcohol use disorder and cognition appears to be more specific to sustained attention-related processes.

Genetic relationships between both factors and cortical structure are modest

To investigate potential genetic relationships between the observed latent cognitive factors and neuroanatomy, we estimated genetic correlations between each latent factor and 34 regional measures of cortical thickness and surface area, unadjusted for intracranial volume (68 phenotypes total) [37]. After correcting for multiple comparisons, no statistically significant genetic correlations were found between either of the latent factors and cortical thickness or surface area measures (Supplementary Table 4). The patterning of these genetic relationships across the cortex are presented in Fig. 4a. Although no robust genetic correlations were identified, we did observe nominally significant genetic correlations between the thickness of the inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis) and Executive Function (rg = 0.22, SE = 0.07, p = 0.003), as well as thickness in the precentral region (motor and premotor cortex) and Sustained Attention (rg = 0.34, SE = 0.15, p = 0.03). Likewise, five regions showed nominally significant genetic correlation between surface area and Executive Function: the inferior frontal gyrus (pars triangularis) (rg = 0.20, SE = 0.07, p = 0.005), transverse temporal cortex (rg = 0.17, SE = 0.07, p = 0.02), lateral orbitofrontal cortex (rg = 0.14, SE = 0.06, p = 0.03), medial orbitofrontal cortex (rg = 0.16, SE = 0.07, p = 0.03), and inferior frontal gyrus (pars orbitalis) (rg = 0.14, SE = 0.07, p = 0.04). QTrait results indicated there was relatively little indicator-level heterogeneity for these relationships (Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

Sustained attention and executive control are fundamental aspects of higher-order cognition that are integral to adaptive human behavior. Deficits in both are evident across the spectrum of psychiatric illness. Using data from the AFFECT study, we report the first multivariate examination of molecular genetic influences on multiple sustained attention and executive processes, providing novel insights into the complex genetic architecture of cognition. Our findings reveal that sustained attention is significantly heritable and influenced by common genetic variants with complex patterns of pleiotropy. These influences exhibit modest patterns of genetic overlap with other cognitive phenotypes and unique patterns of genetic association with psychopathology independent of executive functioning. Notably, genetic influences on sustained attention stability appear dissociable from those driving executive ability.

The results of our study provide the first large-scale empirical evidence that individual differences in sustained attention processes are influenced by a shared polygenic common variant architecture. Consistent with findings from prior GWAS of cognitive traits, the SNP-based heritability estimates considered here were generally modest for task-based measures of executive function and sustained attention, ranging from 0.08 for cognitive flexibility to 0.18 for processing speed and lapse propensity. Moreover, the common genetic variants influencing these phenotypes tended to be quite pleiotropic, but with distinct patterns of genetic sharing observed across the six phenotypes. We found that patterns of genetic sharing tended to segregate across distinct cognitive domains, with processing speed, working memory, and response selection being strongly genetically correlated with each other, while vigilance, lapse propensity, and lapse recovery cost were more genetically interrelated. Our exploratory results are generally in line with prior phenotypic factor analyses, which found that measures derived from the gradCPT tend to be distinct from those derived from measures of executive function; further, they are distinct even from a latent factor indexing general attentional capacity [38]. Moreover, prior work suggests that vigilance (i.e., response time variability), which loaded most strongly onto the Sustained Attention factor, also appears to exhibit life-course trajectories distinct from those related to fluid intelligence components of executive functioning [39].

Our genomic factor analysis provided deeper insights into the observed patterns of complex genetic sharing and differentiation, underscoring the segregation of genetic covariance into two domains. We found that the observed patterns were best explained by two latent genetic factors: one that influences “classic” executive processes, and one that influences sustained attention. Interestingly, the Executive Function and Sustained Attention factors exhibited only a modest, nonsignificant genetic correlation with one another. However, this estimate is imprecise at current sample sizes, with a 95% confidence interval that extends from −0.04 to 0.54. Notably, lapse recovery cost appeared to have particularly complex relationships with other phenotypes. In contrast to traditional performance monitoring tasks where explicit feedback is provided, post-error slowing in the gradCPT may reflect the cognitive demand associated with re-engaging an on-task state (i.e., a lapse recovery cost) rather than an adaptive adjustment of executive control. This is consistent with the genetic overlap between greater recovery cost and poorer executive performance across internal and external measures.

Our genetic correlation analyses substantiated the convergent and discriminant validity of these factors, as both were significantly related to a wide variety of cognitive traits, with Executive Function exhibiting stronger and broader patterns of associations. This is further evidenced by significant indicator-level heterogeneity observed for genetic correlations between Sustained Attention and externally measured cognitive traits, in contrast to largely homogeneous associations between these traits and indicators for Executive Function. Although associations between Sustained Attention and other cognitive phenotypes could largely be attributed to its modest genetic overlap with Executive Function, both factors exhibited significant independent associations with educational attainment.

These independent genetic contributions are consistent with experimental work dissociating executive ability from attentional stability [1, 2, 11, 12], and in line with neuroimaging studies linking these cognitive domains to distinct, but interacting, neural circuitry. Whereas executive function is most strongly associated with the engagement of dorsal attention and frontoparietal control systems [40–44], sustained attention appears to uniquely rely on arousal- and engagement-related circuits that may help orchestrate the large-scale network dynamics associated with on-task performance (e.g. dorsal attention network engagement and default-mode network suppression) [1, 45–52]. Notably, a connectomic profile that robustly predicts individual differences in performance for multiple facets of attention (including sustained attention) [53] failed to predict performance on executive function tasks, further emphasizing differences in the neural mechanisms that support executive function ability versus sustained attention stability [44].

When we examined genomic relationships between the Executive Function and Sustained Attention factors and psychiatric outcomes, we found that both factors were generally negatively correlated with clinical phenotypes, with stronger relationships observed for the Executive Function factor. In contrast to external cognitive traits, we observed higher concordance of indicator-level genetic correlations between external psychiatric disorders and the Sustained Attention factor. After correcting for multiple tests, we found that Sustained Attention was only significantly genetically correlated with ADHD and alcohol use disorder, while Executive Function was significantly genetically correlated with ADHD, anxiety disorder, alcohol use disorder, bipolar disorder, cannabis use disorder, major depressive disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and schizophrenia. This difference is potentially attributable, in part, to the narrower focus of sustained attention as a cognitive construct and the notably lower statistical power of the Sustained Attention factor. Interestingly, follow-up genetic regression models revealed that Sustained Attention and Executive Function are each uniquely genetically related to ADHD, indicative of a multifaceted etiological relationship between cognition and ADHD. These results highlight the importance of considering a more comprehensive range of cognitive domains beyond executive function when studying inter-individual variation in cognition across disorders [54].

Finally, we found that the Executive Function and Sustained Attention factors were not robustly genetically correlated with measures of cortical thickness or surface area. While nominally significant associations were observed, no genetic correlations survived FDR correction. Given the modest effect sizes of brain-behavior relationships at both phenotypic and genetic levels of analysis [37, 55, 56], it is likely that the cognitive process GWAS reported here are not sufficiently well-powered to characterize nuanced etiological relationships in relatively small imaging genetic samples. Prior GWAS of general cognitive function [21] with a much larger sample size (N = 269,867) exhibited only modest genetic correlation with total cortical surface area (rg = 0.27) and non-significant genetic correlation with average cortical thickness [37]. Meanwhile, genetic correlations with region-specific surface area and thickness ranged from −0.15 to 0.27, with the vast majority failing to reach statistical significance. Thus, dramatically larger sample sizes will be necessary for GWAS of attention-related cognitive phenotypes to uncover potential genetic sharing with region-specific variation in cortical morphology.

At the level of performance, sustained attention is closely intertwined with executive function, but cognitive neuroscience models typically distinguish vigilance or attentional stability-related processes from executive control operations such as goal maintenance, working memory updating, and response selection [7–10, 47–50]. Here, the relevant question is not whether sustained attention is phenotypically important for executive performance, but whether the common variant influences on these domains are sufficiently overlapping to be explained by a single latent genetic factor. Our results do not support such a unidimensional architecture. In fact, our results suggest that common genetic influences on executive control and sustained attentional stability are partially separable. The estimated genetic correlation between Sustained Attention and Executive Function was modestly positive, but statistically non-significant (rg = 0.25, SE = 0.15, 95% CI = −0.04 to 0.54). Thus, while there may be some degree of genetic overlap between these two factors, our current level of precision cannot distinguish modest overlap from near-independence with confidence. However, the SNP-based heritabilities and covariances estimated here reflect only additive common variant influences, rather than the full set of factors contributing to phenotypic variance and covariance (e.g., environmental factors). Accordingly, our results are more informative about the organization of common variant liability across these domains than about the precise neurobiological mechanisms that underlie their phenotypic expression.

Several additional limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. Most notably, although our GWAS of task-based attentional processes are among the largest to date, the AFFECT study sample is still considerably smaller than recently published molecular genetic studies of other executive functions (for example, those in the UK Biobank) [57, 58]. While the present study is generally well-powered for characterizing the genome-wide correlational structure of executive processes and investigating their links to other complex traits, it is not well-powered for variant discovery. This is especially true given the modest SNP heritability estimates of investigated traits. Furthermore, to reduce confounding related to population stratification, the current analyses were restricted to those of European ancestry. As AFFECT study samples were collected in collaboration with a consumer genetics company, it is also possible that there are unique ascertainment biases present. Additionally, this cohort is enriched for patients with diagnoses of major depressive disorder or bipolar disorder, which may limit the generalizability of its findings to non-psychiatric populations. Furthermore, although task-based measures of cognitive function are often more engaging for participants, all cognitive phenotyping was performed through multiple online assessments over an extended period, which may introduce participation and attrition biases or reduce the precision and reliability of our cognitive measures [59, 60]. Moreover, administering cognitive tasks at separate sessions could potentially inflate their within-task correlations and bias factor solutions. However, this may be partially mitigated by the fact that both the gradCPT and THINC-it were measured twice. Additionally, patterns of correlations between each factor and cognitive traits measured in independent samples provided external validation for our identified factor solution. This is the first attempt to characterize the molecular genetic architecture of sustained attention and its links with other cognitive processes. Future work will be necessary to replicate and build on these findings, ideally by incorporating data from in-person assessments while expanding the ancestral and contextual diversity of participants. Further, incorporating multiple sustained-attention task variants, richer executive batteries, and more mechanistically proximal neurobiological phenotypes may further refine the structure of these liabilities.

This study describes the first multivariate genomic examination of sustained attention and executive function, and their links to other cognitive and psychiatric phenotypes. We found that sustained attention processes are influenced by genetic factors that are relatively distinct from those that influence executive function. Critically, we identified several instances of unique or differential genetic overlap with psychiatric disorders like ADHD, validating the notion that the dimensions of cognitive variation that drive psychiatric symptoms are multifactorial in nature [54]. We also found evidence of unique genetic relationships with educational attainment, where Sustained Attention and Executive Function were jointly related to this complex life outcome. Our results suggest that expanding the scope of cognitive phenotypes studied may lead to additional insights into the latent genetic architecture of cognition and clarify how distinct cognitive liabilities may relate to psychiatric risk.
