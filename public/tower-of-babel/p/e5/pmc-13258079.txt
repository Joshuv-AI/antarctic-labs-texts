Multisensory virtual reality reduces postoperative delirium in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery
Authors: Wenjun Luo, Yi Duan, Tingting Ni, Zhuqun Mao, Zhifeng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDEffective interventions for postoperative delirium (POD) remain unclear. Research indicates that light and sound stimulation may enhance the functional connectivity of the medial visual network, while olfactory stimulation improves cognitive function. This study aims to address POD by integrating auditory, olfactory and visual stimuli to develop a multisensory virtual reality device.OBJECTIVETo assess the effect of multisensory virtual reality on reducing POD in patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery.DESIGNRandomised controlled trial.SETTINGSingle-centre tertiary hospital in China between September 2023 and July 2024.PARTICIPANTSA total of 342 patients aged 60 years or older, undergoing elective laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery.INTERVENTIONParticipants were randomly assigned to either a multisensory virtual reality group, who received standard care along with a 20-min virtual reality session featuring a biophilic environment infused with juniper essential oil, or a standard care group, who received standard care alone for 20 min pre-operatively.MAIN OUTCOME MEASURESThe primary outcome was the incidence of POD, assessed twice daily using the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) for the three postoperative days. Introduction

Postoperative delirium (POD) is a transient neurocognitive disorder characterised by disturbances in attention, consciousness and cognition, often with apparent fluctuating symptoms.1 The incidence of POD in patients undergoing noncardiac surgery ranges from 13 to 50%.2 POD is associated with adverse outcomes, including increased length of hospital stay, medical complications, mortality and persistent cognitive impairment.3

The pathophysiology of POD remains incompletely understood,4 hindering the development of effective preventive strategies. Preclinical studies suggest that sensory stimulation may mitigate neurodegenerative changes and improve behavioural performance in Alzheimer's disease models, possibly by enhancing functional connectivity in the medial visual network.5 Virtual reality technology, which uses head-mounted displays to create immersive audio-visual environments,6 has shown promise in improving cognitive function in patients with neurological diseases.7

The biophilia hypothesis proposes an innate human connection to nature.8 Biophilic virtual reality environments have been shown to enhance cognitive performance,9 but access to natural settings is often limited for surgical patients. Our research team previously developed a biophilic virtual reality device that successfully reduced intra-operative anxiety and pain in children undergoing circumcision under local anaesthesia.10

Most virtual reality devices primarily engage visual and tactile senses, with limited olfactory stimulation. Recent research indicates that olfactory stimulation may attenuate anaesthesia- or surgery-induced cognitive deficits in mice11 and improve cognitive function in humans.12 Juniper essential oil, known for its light fragrance, has demonstrated memory-enhancing properties and may promote immersion in biophilic virtual reality experiences.13

On the basis of this evidence, we designed a multisensory virtual reality device incorporating visual, auditory and olfactory stimulation with juniper essential oil. This study investigates the hypothesis that pre-operative use of multisensory virtual reality may reduce POD in elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design

Ethical approval for this study (KY20221211) was provided by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University in China (Chairperson Prof Haijiao Mao) on 22 December 2022, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (https://www.chictr.org.cn/index.html) prior to patient enrolment (ChiCTR2300075624, Principal investigator: Luo Wenjun, Date of registration: 11 September 2023).

This open-label, blinded-endpoint, randomised controlled trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Ningbo University, a three-campus hospital in Ningbo, China. Participants were recruited from 15 September 2023 to 31 July 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent. The day before their operation, participants signed a consent form to enrol in the study and provide personal information.

Study population

Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients undergoing elective laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery, estimated duration of surgery at least 2 h, age at least 60 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I to III, estimated length of hospital stay after surgery at least 72 h and the pre-operative cognitive function was evaluated using the 30-point Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (secondary education >24, primary education MMSE >20, illiterate education MMSE >17).

Exclusion criteria were as follows: inability to provide informed consent, emergency surgery, history of psychiatric disease, dementia or cognitive impairment, known hearing, olfactory or visual impairment, allergies to essential oils and use of psychotropic medication (e.g. hypnotics, antipsychotics, antidepressants, mood stabilisers, anxiolytics, psychostimulants and anticonvulsants) within the week before surgery.14,15 The Questionnaire of Olfactory Disorders (QOD) was used to assess olfactory disorders.

Randomisation and blinding

Patients meeting the inclusion criteria were approached by a researcher the day before surgery for initial screening. The researcher introduced the trial details to eligible patients and obtained written informed consent. There was no fee to participate in this study.

After providing consent, patients were randomly assigned in a 1 : 1 ratio to one of the two groups by a research assistant not involved in the trial using a computer-generated randomisation table. The assistant concealed group allocation in identically sized, opaque envelopes and sequentially provided the envelopes to the head researcher. Both groups remained indistinguishable to the anaesthesiologists, surgeons and outcome evaluators to preserve blinding integrity.

Trial procedure

The study methodology is depicted in Fig. 1. All diagnostic tests were performed by researchers who received 1 month of specialised training from neurologists. The researchers were blinded to the treatment assignments. Upon admission to the pre-operative holding area, all enrolled patients underwent baseline assessments, including mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR) and anxiety levels (via the Amsterdam Preoperative Anxiety and Information Scale [APAIS] and 6-item State-Trait Anxiety Inventory [STAI-6], pain intensity (by the Visual Analogue Scale [VAS]) and serum interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels. Patients were then randomly assigned either the multisensory virtual reality group or the standard care group. In the intervention arm, alongside standard care from a nurse, patients also received multisensory virtual reality therapy for 20 min. Patients in the standard care group received standard care only from a nurse for 20 min. Afterwards, MAP, HR, APAIS and STAI-6 were measured again. Patients’ serum IL-6 levels were measured 1 h after incision.16 During the first three postoperative days, assessments included the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM), Confusion Assessment Method for ICU (CAM&ICU), the Delirium Rating Scale-Revised 98 (DRS-R-98), the Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS), the Amsterdam pre-operative anxiety and information scale (APAIS), and the Spielburger state-trait anxiety inventory (STAI-6) and VAS.

Interventions

Setting and timing

The intervention was conducted on the day of surgery in the pre-operative holding area. To allow for the full 20-min session as well as a brief rest period afterward, the intervention was initiated approximately 35 to 45 min before the patient's scheduled transfer to the operating room. To ensure a consistent and private environment, the intervention was delivered in a bay with curtains drawn. The patient was first assisted into a comfortable semi-recumbent position on the bed.

Apparatus

The multisensory system comprised the following components: a visual system, consisting of a virtual reality headset (Shu Rui Medical, China) with a 5.5-inch FAST LCD screen (resolution: 2560 × 1440), which displayed a continuous video of a tranquil natural landscape (featuring blue skies, white clouds, swaying trees, a sapphire lake and flying birds); an auditory system, consisting of a pair of noise-isolating headphones that played soft, instrumental music at a preset, uniform volume for all patients; and an olfactory system, consisting of an oxygen catheter connected to a humidification bottle. The bottle contained a 1 : 20 dilution of juniper essential oil in distilled water, prepared fresh daily. A constant oxygen flow rate of 2 l min−1 was used to deliver the aroma (Fig. 2).

Standardised procedure

The intervention lasted exactly 20 min and was performed by a trained researcher following a standardised protocol: briefing, during which the researcher provided standardised instructions, explaining the purpose of the relaxation session and encouraging the patient to immerse themselves in the virtual environment; setup, during which the researcher fitted the participant with the virtual reality headset and headphones, and then gently positioned the oxygen catheter under the nostrils to deliver the olfactory stimulus; monitoring, during which the researcher remained present to ensure safety and monitor the timing but maintained silence to avoid disrupting the patient's immersion; and completion, at which point the researcher gently removed all equipment and informed the patient that the session had concluded, after which the patient proceeded with routine pre-operative preparations.

Anaesthesia procedures

Intra-operative monitoring included pulse oximetry, electrocardiography, end-tidal gas analysis, invasive arterial blood pressure and bispectral index (BIS). General anaesthesia was induced with intravenous midazolam (0.02 to 0.03 mg kg−1), propofol (2 mg kg−1), sufentanil (0.3 μg kg−1) and rocuronium (0.3 mg kg−1) to achieve anxiolysis, analgesia, hypnosis and neuromuscular blockade, respectively. Intra-operative sedation was maintained with intravenous propofol and remifentanil titrated to maintain BIS levels of 40 to 60. Intra-operative MAP and HR were maintained within 20% of baseline values using vasoactive medications to minimise haemodynamic changes and preserve organ perfusion. During the peri-operative period, neither nerve blocks nor other forms of regional anaesthesia were administered, and lidocaine was not used. Postoperative pain was managed with patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) with sufentanil (1.5 μg kg−1), ondansetron (0.2 mg kg−1) and saline. PCIA settings included a background infusion rate of 2 ml h−1, a demand dose of 0.5 ml and a lockout interval of 15 min.

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes

The diagnosis of POD was based on the CAM, a validated screening instrument derived from DSM-III-R guidelines designed for non-psychiatric clinicians.17 It has demonstrated high diagnostic validity with sensitivity of 94 to 100% and specificity of 90 to 95%.17 The CAM-ICU was used for patients admitted to the ICU who were mechanically ventilated or non-verbal.18 All assessments were performed by a trained investigator who was strictly blinded to the group assignments. To capture the fluctuating nature of delirium, the investigator evaluated patients twice daily (08 : 00 to 10 : 00 and 16 : 00 to 20 : 00) for the first three postoperative days. In addition, we incorporated input from nurses and caregivers regarding any symptoms of confusion or agitation. A diagnosis of POD was established if a patient tested positive on the CAM or CAM-ICU on any single day during this period.

Secondary outcomes

Sedation was assessed before delirium assessment using the Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS). Delirium subtypes were differentiated based on RASS scores: hyperactive delirium (RASS scores consistently +1 to +4), hypoactive delirium (RASS scores consistently -3 to 0) and mixed delirium (RASS scores positive, 0, or negative at different times). Other secondary outcomes included delirium severity and delirium duration. Delirium severity was assessed using the Delirium Rating Scale-Revised 98 (DRS-R-98). Delirium duration was defined as the time between the first positive assessment for delirium and the first negative assessment.

During the first three postoperative days, pain intensity at rest and during movement was assessed using the VAS. The requirement for postoperative rescue opioids was recorded as a binary variable (use versus non-use). Pre-operative anxiety levels were assessed using the APAIS19 and STAI-6. Postoperative anxiety levels were measured using the STAI-6 during the first three postoperative days. The reliability and validity of the Chinese version of STAI-6 has been confirmed in the general Chinese population diagnosed with anxiety disorders.20 Total scores on the STAI-6 questionnaire range from 6 to 36. The STAI-6 is commonly used to measure anxiety related to personality traits or brief emotional states triggered by stimuli. Total scores on the APAIS range from 6 to 30 for pre-operative anxiety. The APAIS has better construct validity for the specific situations faced by surgical patients. Therefore, this study used two scales to assess pre-operative anxiety. Postoperative anxiety was assessed using only the STAI-6.

Haemodynamic parameters, specifically MAP and HR, were recorded in the pre-operative holding area before and after the intervention. Pre-operative and postoperative inflammatory markers, including white blood cell (WBC), C-reactive protein (CRP) level, neutrophils and lymphocytes were also recorded. Li et al.16 conducted a study that included 544 elderly patients undergoing laparoscopic abdominal surgery and found that only serum IL-6 levels within 1 h after incision were an independent risk factor for delayed recovery of neurocognitive functions. Therefore, IL-6 levels were also recorded pre-operatively and 1 h after incision. Length of hospital stay and unplanned transfer to the ICU were also recorded.

Adverse events

The exclusion criteria considered the possible adverse effects of virtual reality headsets, such as seizures, impaired vision, light-headedness, nausea and other related ailments. All interventions were closely monitored by researchers to ensure the well being of the patients. If any patient exhibited symptoms that may be attributed to the intervention, the session was promptly terminated. Any negative events or unintended consequences resulting from the intervention were recorded.

Data collection and management

A research assistant independent of the trial compiled baseline data, demographic information, medical history and pre-operative evaluations. All individual data were kept confidential and used exclusively for research purposes. Trained research evaluators, blinded to group assignments, assessed primary and secondary outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Sample size estimation

According to a study by Xiang et al., 21 the prevalence of POD in patients undergoing laparoscopic surgery is approximately 25%. On the basis of the results of our pilot study, which included 100 patients in each group, 21 patients in the standard care group developed POD, and 10 patients in the experimental group developed POD. Therefore, this study assumed that the incidence of POD in the experimental group was 10%, and that the incidence of POD in the standard care group would be 21%. The two-sided test level (α) = 0.05 and the test power (1-β) = 80% were set. Considering the estimated 5% loss to follow-up during the trial, a sample size calculation using PASS 15.0 indicated that 356 individuals were required.

Data analysis

Standardised differences were calculated, and variables with an absolute standardised difference at least 0.15 were considered imbalanced. The normality of continuous variables was assessed. As none of the continuous variables followed a normal distribution, they were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and were examined using Pearson's χ2 test or, if not applicable, adjusted χ2 test.

Subgroup analyses of the incidence of POD were performed for the following factors: sex, age (≥ 70 years or < 70 years), ASA physical status, age-adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (< 7 or ≥7), peri-operative blood transfusion, year of education (> 9 years or ≤9 years), anaesthesia duration (>4 h or ≤ 4 h) and type of surgery. In the subgroup analysis, P value less than 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance for the interaction between treatment and covariates. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 25.0, IBM Co., Armonk, New York, USA. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all hypotheses.

Results

For the study, 502 patients were screened for eligibility, of whom 146 were excluded, and 356 were randomly assigned to the multisensory virtual reality group or the standard care group (Fig. 3). Six patients did not complete the full multisensory virtual reality intervention. Eight patients required intra-operative conversion to open surgery. The final numbers recruited were 170 patients in the multisensory virtual reality group and 172 patients in the standard care group.

Primary outcome

The overall incidence of POD was 14.3% (49 of 342 patients). The cumulative incidence of POD at the end of the third postoperative day was 9.4% (16/170) in the multisensory virtual reality group and 19.2% (33 \ 172) in the standard care group (P = 0.01, Table 3). The cumulative incidence of POD was significantly lower in the multisensory virtual reality group: hazard ratio 0.48, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.88; P = 0.02, Fig. 4. There were no significant interactions between the relative rates of delirium after multisensory virtual reality versus standard care (Fig. 5).

Secondary outcomes

There were no significant differences between the multisensory virtual reality group and the standard care group in the worst delirium severity score (P = 0.83), delirium duration (P = 0.26) or subtype of delirium (P = 0.90).

Compared with the standard care group, the multisensory virtual reality group had significantly lower postintervention anxiety, APAIS scores and STAI-6 scores (P = 0.001 for all). On postoperative day 3, STAI-6 scores in the multisensory virtual reality group were significantly lower than those in the standard care group (P = 0.001 for both).

Compared with the standard care group, the multisensory virtual reality group had significantly lower postintervention MAP and hazard ratio (P = 0.001 for both). There were no significant differences between the multisensory virtual reality group and the standard care group in pain scores at rest on POD 1 (P = 0.25), POD 2 (P = 0.35) or POD 3 (P = 0.94), nor during movement on POD 1 (P = 0.42), POD 2 (P = 0.45) or POD 3 (P = 0.50). Similarly, no significant difference was observed in the requirement for postoperative rescue opioids between the two groups (P = 0.83). There were no significant differences in the length of hospital stay (P = 0.29), or hospitalisation costs (P = 0.74).

In addition, no statistical differences were found in the pre-operative and postoperative inflammatory markers between the two groups (Table 4).

Adverse events

No serious adverse events occurred in either group. Five patients (2.9%) in the multisensory virtual reality group reported mild dizziness. No patients discontinued the study because of adverse events associated with the use of multisensory virtual reality.

Discussion

In this study, multisensory virtual reality significantly reduced the incidence of POD during the first three postoperative days following laparoscopic abdominal surgery. This reduction was consistent across subgroup analyses and was accompanied by a decrease in anxiety throughout the first three on the postoperative days.

In line with our findings, a previous study used tablet-based interactive distractions to significantly reduce the incidence of emergence delirium.22 Another study found that pre-operative video distraction reduced the incidence and severity of emergence delirium during anaesthesia for strabismus surgery.22 Tang et al.23 found that watching cartoon videos reduced peri-operative anxiety and emergence delirium. However, in contrast with our study, a virtual reality intervention by Ryu et al.24 did not reduce the incidence of emergence delirium in paediatric patients. This discrepancy may be due to differences in the duration of the virtual reality intervention and the monitoring period for delirium. In the study by Ryu et al.25, the virtual reality intervention lasted only 4 min, whereas ours used a 20-min virtual reality intervention that incorporated visual, auditory and olfactory stimuli to enhance immersion. In addition, our study monitored patients throughout the first three postoperative days, while Ryu et al.25 focused only on the occurrence of delirium in the recovery room, potentially missing delirium episodes that occurred later and so biased their results.

The mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects of virtual reality are likely multifaceted. Auditory stimulation in virtual reality devices may also play a role. Listening to music can trigger cognitive and emotional responses involving limbic and paralimbic regions.26,27 A randomised clinical trial found that intra-operative auditory stimulation reduced emergence delirium in children undergoing ENT surgery.28 In addition, this study used virtual reality technology with biophilic content. Natural environments can restore directed attention and enhance cognitive performance.29 Contact with nature also reduces stress indicators, such as cortisol levels and blood pressure.30

Notably, our study incorporated olfactory stimulation into the virtual reality experience. The olfactory system, which detects and processes odour signals, is a potential biomarker for neurodegenerative diseases.31 It has extensive brain connections, including areas involved in learning, memory and emotional processing.32 Olfactory impairment is associated with POD,33 and animal experiments show that olfactory stimulation improves olfactory and cognitive deficits in mice.11

Crucially, our study observed a concurrent reduction in both anxiety and POD, suggesting a potential mechanistic link. Pre-operative anxiety is increasingly recognised as a predictor of delirium across various surgical populations, including orthopaedic, oncological, gastrointestinal and postanaesthesia care unit settings.34–37 Anxiety triggers the stress response, activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and elevating glucocorticoid levels.38–40 Concurrently, anxiety can stimulate the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6,39 which is a known biomarker for delirium.41 Both excessive glucocorticoids and neuroinflammation are established pathogenic drivers that impair neuronal survival and precipitate delirium.38,42,43 Therefore, by providing an immersive and relaxing environment, virtual reality intervention may disrupt these stress cascades, thereby reducing the incidence of POD.

This single-centre study has several limitations. First, blinding was not possible, which may have led to bias in patient-reported outcomes because patients may have had preconceived notions about treatment effects. Future studies should develop a multisensory virtual reality application for POD that allows for blinding. Second, this study lacked a separate olfactory control. Future study should include four groups: a multisensory virtual reality group, a virtual reality only group, an olfactory-only group and a control group. Third, the short postoperative follow-up period and lack of long-term outcome data pose additional limitations. In addition, the patient population was limited to adults aged 60 years or older undergoing laparoscopic abdominal surgery. This limitation, along with the single-centre design, may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations. Furthermore, a limitation of our study is the lack of evaluation regarding patients’ quality of life and peri-operative experience. As these factors are important for understanding overall patient outcomes, future research should consider including validated instruments such as the EVAN scale to comprehensively assess these dimensions.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that a pre-operative, noninvasive multisensory virtual reality intervention effectively and safely reduces the incidence of POD after laparoscopic abdominal surgery. This therapy was well tolerated, suggesting confirmatory research would be justified.
