Pickering Syndrome: An Overlooked Renovascular Cause of Recurrent Heart Failure
Authors: Viola Sanga, Eleonora Bertoli, Filippo Crimì, Giulio Barbiero, Michele Battistel, Teresa Maria Seccia, Gian Paolo Rossi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTRecurring and rapidly developing (flash) pulmonary edema is the hallmark of Pickering syndrome, affecting patients with hypertension and atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis (either bilateral or unilateral) in a solitary functioning kidney, and impaired renal function. We herein report on a series of consecutive patients with recurrent hospital admissions for pulmonary edema, impaired renal function (chronic kidney disease class 4–5), and atherosclerotic bilateral renal artery stenosis, in whom Pickering syndrome had been long neglected. We also describe a streamlined diagnostic strategy entailing little or no need for contrast medium, thus carrying no risks of further worsening of renal function. This allowed us to make the correct diagnosis and opened the way to revascularization by percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty with stent, which provided swift recovery of kidney function with resolution of pulmonary congestion and long‐term pulmonary edema‐ and dialysis‐free survival in all cases. In summary, these findings support the following key messages: (1) considering the diagnosis of Pickering syndrome, followed by searching atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis, is an essential step toward a life‐saving revascularization that avoids dialysis and an otherwise poor outcome; and (2) a simplified strategy entailing little or no need for contrast medium, carrying no associated risks of deteriorating renal function, permits the diagnosis of Pickering syndrome.Keywords: atherosclerosis; chronic kidney disease; pulmonary edema; renal artery stenosis; revascularization, angioplasty, stentingSubject Categories: Hypertension, Heart Failure, Atherosclerosis, Peripheral Vascular Disease, Nephrology and Kidney

In 1988, Pickering et al first described recurrent episodes of flash pulmonary edema and reduced kidney function in patients with arterial hypertension and bilateral renal artery stenosis or unilateral renal artery stenosis in a solitary functioning kidney, 1 a condition thereafter named Pickering syndrome. 2 The term flash was used by Pickering to describe pulmonary edemas that occurred rapidly and repeatedly in these patients, like the flash of a camera. Activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS), in the setting of an impaired glomerular filtration rate caused by the renal artery stenosis and multi‐infarct cortical nephropathy, was suggested to set the stage for sodium and water retention, progressive worsening of renal function, uncontrolled hypertension, and altered lung vascular permeability. 3 In the presence of left ventricular hypertrophy attributable to hypertensive cardiomyopathy, which impairs diastolic left ventricular filling, these alterations lead to lung congestion with recurrent pulmonary edema (Figure 1).

Pickering syndrome.

In patients with arterial hypertension and bilateral renal artery stenosis or unilateral renal artery stenosis in a solitary functioning kidney, the activation of the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) and other hormonal axes, in the setting of an impaired glomerular filtration rate caused by renal ischemia and multi‐infarct cortical nephropathy, sets the stage for sodium and water retention, progressive worsening of renal function, uncontrolled hypertension, worsening New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, and altered lung vascular permeability. In the presence of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) attributable to hypertensive cardiomyopathy, with ensuing diastolic dysfunction, these alterations predispose to lung congestion with recurrent flash pulmonary edema, a condition named Pickering syndrome. As atherosclerotic renal artery stenosis (ARAS) accounts for the vast majority of Pickering syndrome cases, this syndrome is encountered with increasing frequency in an aging population with multisite atherosclerotic disease. Nonetheless, these patients are regularly overlooked and treated with RAAS blockers following heart failure guidelines. 4 These agents, by further decreasing renal perfusion pressure and the compensatory postglomerular arteriole constriction, can acutely worsen renal function, especially in case of hemodynamically relevant renal artery stenosis involving both renal arteries or a single renal artery to a solitary functioning kidney, 5 , 6 thus making mandatory the monitoring of serum creatinine in such patients receiving RAAS blockers.

Currently, most affected patients remain exposed to an avoidable fatal course because Pickering syndrome is undersuspected, underdetected, and, therefore, undiagnosed.

The publication of exploratory randomized clinical trials 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 that reported no benefit of revascularization in patients with ARAS, but recruited patients without hemodynamically relevant ARAS, has contributed to further worsen this ominous scenario by decreasing markedly the yearly detection rate of ARAS in all major hospitals, both in Europe and the United States. 15 According to a published survey of the nationwide Medicare Part B fee‐for‐service databases, submitted services per year for percutaneous renal artery arteriography decreased by 70% from 2010 to 2018, 16 as shown in Figure 2. Therefore, as ARAS is rarely sought for, most patients with Pickering syndrome are being treated like any other patients with pulmonary congestion, renal failure of uncertain origin, or both, and thus remain exposed to poor outcomes, such as recurrent hospitalizations for heart failure, progression to end‐stage chronic kidney disease (CKD), and, ultimately, progression to chronic dialysis, with a death‐free 5‐year survival of <25%. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20

We hypothesized that these ominous events could be effectively prevented by a timely renal artery revascularization. 21 , 22 , 23 To investigate this hypothesis, we examined a consecutive series of patients with reduced kidney function and recurrent hospitalizations for acute pulmonary edema, uncontrolled hypertension, or both, in whom the diagnosis of Pickering syndrome was neglected for a long time. Once Pickering syndrome was diagnosed, revascularization by means of percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty with stent (PTRAS) resolved the symptoms, avoided the need for long‐term dialysis, and provided long‐term survival. We herein also describe a simplified diagnostic workup that avoids the risks associated with use of contrast medium in these patients with impaired glomerular filtration rate.

Case 1

A 79‐year‐old White man presented to the emergency department because of shortness of breath, dry cough, and recurrent episodes of chest pain in the previous week. He had a long‐standing history of cigarette smoking and multidistrict vasculopathy that required bilateral carotid thromboendarterectomy, aortofemoral bypass, and 2‐vessel coronary artery bypass grafting.

His ECG showed atrial fibrillation with a high ventricular rate, but no signs of ischemia; blood test results showed an increase in serum creatinine levels. Amiodarone and anticoagulants were started, and he was discharged with minimal residual lung congestion.

Two months later, he was readmitted for recurrence of shortness of breath, high‐rate atrial fibrillation, and amiodarone‐induced hypothyroidism. Increasing the dose of enalapril doubled his serum creatinine levels (from 169 to 335 μmol/L); therefore, the drug was stopped, with ensuing reduction of serum creatinine (226 μmol/L). He was discharged after a successful direct current cardioversion, and amiodarone was stopped. A 99TcDTPA (diethylene‐triamine‐pentaacetate) renal scintigraphy showed a small hypofunctioning left kidney. Two weeks later, he was readmitted for recurrence of overt pulmonary edema. A renal arteriography performed with a minimal dose of contrast medium (15 mL) showed total occlusion of the left renal artery and a tight right renal artery stenosis, which was resolved with PTRAS. His New York Heart Association functional class rapidly improved from IV to II; moreover, an angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitor could be reintroduced with no worsening of renal function.

The patient remained in New York Heart Association class I/II with a stable renal function for the next 5 years, after which he developed angina abdominis that required mesenteric artery bypass grafting. He died of metastatic colon cancer 8 years later, at the age of 87 years.

Case 2

A 71‐year‐old White man with a 50‐year history of heavy cigarette smoking (>2 packs/day for 50 years) presented to the emergency department with shortness of breath, lower limb edema, and uncontrolled blood pressure (BP) (200/100 mm Hg), despite therapy with canrenone, furosemide, carvedilol, and amlodipine. Over the past 5 years, we could track 4 accesses to the emergency department for pulmonary edema.

Despite continuous furosemide infusion, he developed oliguria and increased serum creatinine levels (262 μmol/L), after which he became anuric and was started on 3 hemodialysis sessions per week. A direct abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan showed a small right kidney and diffuse calcified plaques in the abdominal aorta involving the ostial portion of both renal arteries, causing a tight stenosis on the left side and total occlusion on the right side. Plaques were noted in practically every arterial district (Figure 3). Using a minimal amount of contrast medium (15 mL), the left ARAS was imaged and treated with PTRAS. Immediately after the procedure, the patient recovered spontaneous diuresis and showed a prominent increase of the renogram on angiography, so that hemodialysis was no longer necessary (see Video S1 before PTRAS and Video S2 after PTRAS). At regular follow‐up, the patient showed good BP values with 4 antihypertensive drugs, cardioaspirin, and statin, with stable renal function. He survived for the following 5 years, after which he died of lung carcinoma.

The unenhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan of a patient with Pickering syndrome.

The unenhanced abdominal CT scan of a patient with Pickering syndrome (case 2) showed calcified plaques all along the abdominal aorta involving the ostial portion of both renal arteries (stenosis at the left side, and occlusion at the right side), a small left kidney with small cortical defects, indicating cortical multi‐infarct nephropathy, and a shrunken right kidney. The left renal artery stenosis was treated with percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty with stent.

Case 3

A 75‐year‐old non‐smoking White man with type 2 diabetes complicated by multidistrict vasculopathy presented to the emergency department because of pulmonary edema and uncontrolled BP. He had a history of hypertension, postinfarction dilatated cardiomyopathy, for which he received a bicameral implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator in primary prevention, and aortic valve replacement with a bioprothesis. He had experienced ischemic stroke 6 years before, and he was treated with systemic thrombolysis and left carotid thromboendarterectomy. He was hospitalized 7 times in the previous 15 years for heart failure.

He was initially treated with continuous positive airway pressure, furosemide, and nitroglycerin intravenously with some benefit, and then referred to our semi‐intensive unit, where he was switched to amlodipine, spironolactone, sacubitril/valsartan, carvedilol, and furosemide. Treatment provided resolution of congestion but worsening of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), with serum creatinine increasing from 150 to 224 μmol/L. Because renal artery Doppler ultrasonography examination was not feasible because of poor acoustic window, a direct CT scan was performed, which showed bilaterally small kidneys and diffuse calcified plaques in the aorta and in all the main abdominal arteries. Angiography with minimal amount of contrast medium (15 mL) confirmed a 70% ostial ARAS of the left renal artery and patency of the right renal artery. A PTRAS restored flow in the left renal artery. In the following days, BP normalized and serum creatinine rapidly decreased to 160 μmol/L (eGFR, 39 mL/min per 1.73 m2). He was discharged with clopidogrel, rosuvastatin/ezetimibe, and antihypertensive therapy, entailing amlodipine, spironolactone, carvedilol, and furosemide. At 6‐month follow‐up, eGFR increased to 37 mL/min per 1.73 m2, and BP was on target with amlodipine, carvedilol, and furosemide. He had no further hospitalizations for heart failure.

Case 4

A 78‐year‐old White man, who was a long‐standing smoker, with a 40‐year history of stage 3 hypertension, CKD class IV (eGFR, 28 mL/min per 1.73 m2), right carotid endarterectomy, severe aortic stenosis (aortic valve area, 0.61 cm2/m2) with moderate aortic regurgitation, mitral valve regurgitation, and obstructive sleep apnea syndrome regularly treated with continuous positive airway pressure, was admitted to our semi‐intensive unit because of heart failure. Pickering syndrome was suspected and confirmed at a direct CT scan that showed diffuse calcified plaques of abdominal aorta, carrefour, and the ostial portion of both renal arteries and furthermore a left adrenal adenoma. Plasma aldosterone was markedly increased (47.7 ng/dL; normal values <12 ng/dL on a 100–300 daily Na+ intake) while active renin was markedly elevated (597 mIU/L; normal values 10–40 mIU/L on a 100–300 daily Na+ intake) likely because of treatment with an angiotensin receptor blocker and a K+ losing diuretic. After relief of congestion with loop diuretics, a PTRAS of the left renal artery with minimal amount of contrast medium (15 mL) allowed the correction of the stenosis, with resolution of lung congestion and symptoms, normalization of BP, and no worsening of renal function (eGFR, 29 mL/min per 1.73 m2). As adrenal venous sampling showed a left lateralized hypersecretion of aldosterone (selectivity index, >2.0 bilaterally; left lateralization index, 3.4), he was discharged on a 5‐drug antihypertensive therapy with the indication to undergo left adrenalectomy in case of resistance of high BP values to treatment, because the anesthesiologist judged that his general conditions would render elective video laparoscopic adrenalectomy a high‐risk procedure. After 8 months of follow‐up, his general health was good, with BP values around 150 to 155/80 mm Hg. At 1 year follow‐up, he had no further hospitalizations for heart failure, but several hospital admissions for recurrent episodes of severe gastrointestinal bleeding attributable to intestinal angiodysplasia. Therefore, with a diagnosis of Heide syndrome, he has been scheduled for transcatheter aortic valve implantation.

Case 5

A 76‐year‐old non‐smoking White woman presented to the emergency department because of shortness of breath, worsened renal function (eGFR, 22 mL/min per 1.73 m2), and uncontrolled BP (250/110 mm Hg), while on 4 antihypertensive agents. She was discharged, but 4 days later, she presented again with shortness of breath and high BP values (185/80 mm Hg), and was referred to an internal medicine ward. Over the following days, despite improved BP control with an intensified antihypertensive treatment, she developed respiratory wheezing, she became oliguric, and her renal function decreased to an eGFR of 7 mL/min per 1.73 m2. Abdominal ultrasonography showed lung congestion (B lines), a right pleural, abdominal effusion, and a 7.1‐cm small left kidney with irregularly decreased cortical thickness. Pulmonary embolism was ruled out at ventilation‐perfusion lung scintigraphy, and microbiological test results for pneumonia were negative. A 4‐L/min flow O2 administration and a continuous infusion of furosemide were started, but the clinical conditions rapidly worsened. Two hemodialysis sessions were performed, and she was put on continuous positive airway pressure, methylprednisolone, metolazone, and ceftriaxone with scant benefit.

She was transferred to our semi‐intensive unit because of progression to end‐stage renal disease. Although no flow could be detected at the origins of the renal arteries with Doppler ultrasonographic examination, an increased intrarenal arterial acceleration time on both the right (142 ms) and left (168 ms) sides (normal value, <70 ms 24 ) was observed. Renal arterial resistivity indexes were reduced (0.64) on the right side and undetectable on the left side. Because an angiographic CT scan was believed to be risky because of the severely reduced renal function, a direct unenhanced abdominal CT scan was ordered. Besides the smaller left kidney, it demonstrated diffuse calcified plaques in the aorta and the ostial portion of both renal arteries (Figure 4A and 4B).

Using a minimal amount of contrast medium (15 mL), a PTRAS (Blue‐Palmaz, Cordis 7–15 mm) allowed correction of the tight right ARAS (Figure 4C and 4D) and BP normalization with an increase of eGFR to 39 mL/min per 1.73 m2. At 24‐month follow‐up, she has had no further hospitalization, her renal function is stable (eGFR, 42 mL/min per 1.73 m2), and her BP is optimally controlled with perindopril/indapamide/amlodipine and bisoprolol.

Discussion

The publication of several studies 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 that reported little or no benefit of revascularization in patients with ARAS was followed by a marked decrease of the yearly number of renal arteriographies and, therefore, the detection rate of ARAS in all major hospitals, in both Europe and the United States, as shown in Figure 2. 15 , 16 In line with this, in our University Hospital, the largest hub of northeastern Italy, the search of ARAS by angiographic CT, digital subtraction angiography, or both has decreased by >50% from 2007 to 2017 and, with the exception of the few cases herein reported, was never undertaken in patients with heart failure. Therefore, these cases testify what happened in real‐life practice after the publication of the aforementioned studies: even when the clinical picture is highly suggestive of Pickering syndrome, the diagnosis of ARAS is no longer considered.

In contrast, the available data and several objective considerations do not support, in our view, this change in practice. The DRASTIC (Dutch Renal Artery Stenosis Intervention Cooperative) study enrolled patients with normal or mildly impaired renal function, who had no resistant hypertension and, in a relevant sample of patients, no hemodynamically significant ARAS 7 ; moreover, the active arm received revascularization with PTRA without stenting, a procedure no longer accepted for ARAS because of the high restenosis rate. In addition, the chances of detecting any advantages of revascularization over medical therapy were minimized by the 44% crossover of the patients in the medical therapy arm to PTRA.

Ten years later, the STAR (Benefit of Stent Placement and Blood Pressure and Lipid‐Lowering for the Prevention of Progession of Renal Dysfunction Caused by Atherosclerotic Ostial Stenosis of the Renal Artery) study also recruited many patients with no hemodynamically significant RAS, as the cutoff for inclusion was an RAS ≥50%, even if stenoses are well known to become hemodynamically significant when ≥70%. Moreover, patients were required to have stable hypertension and a normal or only mildly impaired renal function, suggesting that many patients were unlikely to gain benefit from PTRAS. 10 Besides, ≈30% of those originally assigned to the interventional arm did not receive PTRAS because of an ARAS <50%.

The inclusion criteria of the ASTRAL were similar: it recruited patients without hemodynamically relevant ARAS. 8 Moreover, 65% of the participating centers randomized <1 patient/year, which likely accounts for a PTRAS complication rate >4‐fold higher than reported in literature (9% versus 2% 25 ). Dr White commented, “The ASTRAL (Angioplasty and Stent for Renal Artery Lesions) criteria created an obvious selection bias toward enrolling … patients unlikely to benefit from revascularization.” 26

The CORAL (Cardiovascular Outcomes in Renal Atherosclerotic Lesions) study recruited patients with mild CKD (defined as eGFR <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2) but without hypertension, or with systolic BP ≥155 mm Hg but without considering diastolic BP. 9 The mean diastolic BP in CORAL patients was 79±13 mm Hg, indicating that half of them had isolated systolic hypertension, and only half had systolic‐diastolic hypertension. 27 Although elderly patients with isolated systolic hypertension can also have concurrent ARAS, it is likely that most CORAL patients had no renovascular hypertension caused by hemodynamically relevant ARAS. In fact, the latter are held to activate the RAAS and, thereby, increase both preload and afterload with ensuing systolic‐diastolic hypertension.

A small sample size, with ensuing low statistical power, and analogous limitations affected also the EMMA (Blood Pressure Outcome of Angioplasty in Atherosclerotic Renal Artery Stenosis: A Randomized Trial. Essai Multicentrique Medicaments vs Angioplastie Study), 11 SNRASCG (Scottish) (Randomised Comparison of Percutaneous Angioplasty vs Continued Medical Therapy for Hypertensive Patients With Atheromatous Renal Artery Stenosis), 12 RADAR (A Randomised, Multi‐Centre, Prospective Study Comparing Best Medical Treatment Versus Best Medical Treatment Plus Renal Artery Stenting in Patients With Haemodynamically Relevant Atherosclerotic Renal Artery Stenosis, 13 and NITER (The Nephropathy Ischemic Therapy) studies. 14

On the whole, it is altogether evident that none of the exploratory randomized clinical trials on renal revascularization recruited patients with recurrent flash pulmonary edema and, therefore, their results are not relevant for patients with Pickering syndrome, for whom all the American Heart Association guidelines and statements unambiguously stated that the indication for revascularization is class of recommendation I a, level of evidence B since 2006. 15 , 28 , 29

Strikingly, in contrast with these recommendations, the present series, entailing consecutive patients with Pickering syndrome, testifies that this diagnosis is no longer considered and, therefore, regularly missed, resulting in lack of appropriate treatment for a long time.

An additional factor contributing to overlooking Pickering syndrome could be concern on use of a contrast medium for imaging the renal arteries, which can worsen CKD in patients, who already have an impaired eGFR attributable to the ARAS, cortical multi‐infarctual nephropathy, or nephroangiosclerosis.

In this regard, our results showed that the suspicion of Pickering syndrome can be confirmed with the following streamlined strategy comprising renal ultrasonographic measurement of kidney size, Doppler assessment of renal artery flow velocity and intrarenal resistivity indexes (RIs), and direct (unenhanced) CT scan (Figure 5). In patients with recurrent pulmonary edema and increased serum creatinine, the finding of ostial calcifications of the renal artery on direct CT indicates atherosclerotic involvement. Moreover, the detection at renal ultrasonography of a unilateral small (<6–7 cm in longitudinal diameter) kidney usually indicates total renal artery occlusion. In this situation, the intrarenal RIs are not measurable, as in our case 5. If the renal artery is not totally occluded, the side affected with a hemodynamically relevant stenosis shows RIs generally <0.55. The side with a severe stenosis can usually be identified by the lower RI when the difference from the mean RI of the contralateral kidney is >0.05. When there are multiple renal arteries, which can be differently stenosed, a difference of >0.05 among the upper, mid, or lower third of the kidney usually denotes a severe stenosis. 30 , 31 This simplified diagnostic strategy can strongly support the suspicion of Pickering syndrome and, thus, reinforce the indication to proceed with revascularization using a contrast medium amount as tiny as possible.

More important, in all cases of ours, who were on 4 or more antihypertensive agents and thus met the AHA criteria for resistant hypertension, 32 PTRAS was life saving, resolved signs and symptoms of lung congestion, preserved patients from long‐term dialysis, allowed us to achieve control of high BP values even when resistant to treatment, 33 and ultimately provided long‐term event‐free survival. Thus, although anecdotal, this case series provides compelling evidences that a high degree of suspicion followed by a simplified diagnostic approach can change the disease course and the ominous outcomes of these patients.

We would like to underline that these achievements were made possible by multidisciplinary team work that involved a tight cooperation between clinicians and skilled interventional radiologists. This collaboration involved a careful clinical assessment of the patient's general conditions, comorbidities, renal function, ARAS characteristics, and death risk stratification. After a renal Doppler ultrasound and a direct unenhanced CT scan supported the diagnosis, a successful percutaneous revascularization was safely performed using a minimal dose of iodine contrast medium. Severe CKD, or allergy to contrast medium, could lead us to consider CO2‐enhanced angiography, 34 , 35 which, in the present series, was not used because the interventional radiologists believed that the lower quality of the images would have rendered more difficult undertaking the revascularization. Nonetheless, it is worth emphasizing that eGFR did not decrease in our patients, despite the use of the contrast medium, but impressively improved.

As guidelines state with a class of recommendation I to II a, level of evidence B, 15 , 28 , 33 , 36 revascularization is the treatment of choice (PTRA + stent placement) for adult patients with ARAS in whom medical management fails to control hypertension, improve worsening renal function, or resolve heart failure. Pulmonary edema occurring swiftly and repeatedly (flash pulmonary edema), the key presentation of the Pickering syndrome, should be explicitly added to this list, as mentioned in the last Canadian Hypertension Guidelines, 37 along with predialytic/dialytic CKD, especially if renal function decline has occurred in a short time after exposing the patients to an RAAS blocker (Table 2).

The anecdotal nature of our cases might be perceived as a limitation of this study. However, this is compensated by the amazing results obtained, which speak for themselves: revascularization saved our patients from hemodialysis and from an otherwise certain poor outcome in the short‐term; in some, it provided a long event‐free survival, which was even more impressive considering their age and clinical condition at presentation.

Conclusions

In summary, our results highlight the need to keep on high guard regarding the potential diagnosis of Pickering syndrome in patients with recurrent pulmonary edema and impaired renal function, particularly if they have signs/history of atherosclerosis. The diagnosis is feasible even when administration of iodine contrast medium quantity would seem to be contraindicated because of severely reduced renal function. Revascularization with PTRAS with little use of contrast medium can help these patients avoid dialysis and recurrent episodes of life‐threatening pulmonary edema.
