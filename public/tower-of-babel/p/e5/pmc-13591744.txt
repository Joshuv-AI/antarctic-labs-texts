Effects of different aligner thicknesses and expansion step sizes on maxillary arch expansion with Invisalign Palatal Expander: a three-dimensional finite element study
Authors: Luhua Ding, Jifang Hui, Hu Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the biomechanical effects of different aligner thicknesses and expansion step sizes with the Invisalign Palatal Expander (IPE) on the midpalatal suture, teeth, and periodontal tissues during maxillary arch expansion using the finite element method (FEM), thereby facilitating its more precise and effective clinical application.MethodsThree-dimensional (3D) finite element models, including teeth, bilateral maxilla, midpalatal suture, periodontal ligament (PDL), aligner, and attachments, were constructed. Stress distribution and initial displacement of the midpalatal suture, teeth, and PDL were evaluated under three aligner thicknesses (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mm) and different step sizes (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mm).ResultsDuring expansion with the IPE, increased aligner thickness markedly enhanced the buccal displacement of posterior teeth, increased midpalatal suture initial displacement, and promoted bodily movement rather than tipping, albeit with increased PDL stress. A greater step size increased buccal displacement of the first molar but reduced that of the primary molars. Thicker aligners (≥ 1.0 mm) combined with a larger step size directed mechanical effects posteriorly to a greater degree, promoting greater suture initial displacement in the posterior region.ConclusionThe thickness of the IPE aligner is the dominant factor regulating tooth movement patterns and midpalatal suture initial displacement, while step size modulates the magnitude and distribution of the mechanical response in the posterior maxillary arch. To promote greater posterior midpalatal suture displacement, we recommend using aligners with a thickness of at least 1.0 mm in combination with an appropriately increased step size.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09033-8.Keywords: Arch expansion, Invisalign palatal expander, Finite element method, Aligner thickness, Expansion step size

Background

Maxillary transverse deficiency (MTD) is a common malocclusion in orthodontic practice, with a reported prevalence of 8% to 23% in the deciduous and mixed dentitions and approximately 10% in the permanent dentition [1]. Clinically, it manifests as narrow arches, interarch discrepancy, posterior crossbite, arch crowding with protrusion, excessive buccal corridors during smiling, and may contribute to sagittal Class II/III malocclusions, impairing stomatognathic function and aesthetics [2]. Since Angle proposed in 1860 that maxillary expansion could correct MTD [3], available approaches have included slow and rapid maxillary expansion (SME and RME) for growing patients, and miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) and surgically assisted rapid maxillary expansion (SARME) for non-growing individuals [4]. Conventional expanders are classified by anchorage (tooth-borne, tooth-tissue-borne, and bone-borne); however, they are often bulky and visible, and because they are cemented onto the teeth, they can impair oral function, complicate hygiene, and increase periodontal and mucosal risks as well as the risk of debonding.

In recent years, clear aligner therapy (CAT) has gained widespread use due to its aesthetics and comfort, offering a viable approach for arch expansion in cases of mild maxillary transverse deficiency or crowding [5]. It creates space to resolve crowding and posterior crossbite, improving occlusal coordination. However, CAT has limitations for arch expansion. Expansion with clear aligners is achieved primarily through buccal tipping, and the expansion magnitude is positively correlated with the degree of tipping [5–7], which reduces expansion efficiency. Levrini et al. reported that in early mixed dentition, clear aligners produced an expansion effect comparable to conventional slow maxillary expansion [5], gaining about 2.8–3.72 mm, involving a 4.62° change in molar inclination and rotation around palatal roots, primarily increasing mesio-buccal intercusp width, transforming the arch from ovoid to parabolic and creating space for permanent tooth eruption [8]. In the permanent dentition, however, the reported efficiency varies. Houle et al. reported that Invisalign achieved an average efficiency of 72.8%, predominantly through tipping (82.9% at the cusp tip vs. 62.7% at the gingival margin) [7], whereas Zhou et al. found the efficiency for bodily movement was lower (36.35%) [9]. Similarly, Morales-Burruezo et al. demonstrated that the Invisalign system with SmartTrack material was effective for arch expansion, highest at the second premolar (81.0%) and lowest at the second molar (65.2%) [10].

Align Technology Inc. recently introduced the Invisalign Palatal Expander (IPE), which mimics conventional acrylic‑based designs. The device covers the clinical crowns of the upper first permanent molars and the first/second primary molars, increasing palatal wall contact without engaging the vault. Each expander produces approximately 0.25 mm of transverse expansion per stage and is typically replaced daily. Unlike conventional clear aligners, which are highly flexible and exhibit shape memory [11, 12], the IPE consists of removable, direct-printed Nylon-12 (polyamide-12) units with high rigidity and dimensional stability [13, 14], making it specifically suited to skeletal expansion. As a relatively new device, published clinical studies on the IPE remain limited. A prospective pilot study [15] reported that the IPE effectively achieved maxillary transverse expansion and suture opening, with skeletal and occlusal outcomes comparable to those of the Hyrax expander (suture opening: 2.12 ± 0.79 mm vs. 2.67 ± 1.20 mm, p = 0.178). The IPE also showed more controlled and predictable results (a narrower 95% confidence interval), supporting its use as a valid alternative for maxillary expansion in the mixed dentition. Nevertheless, its underlying biomechanical mechanisms remain unclear, and a systematic investigation is needed to enhance expansion efficiency and achieve precise control.

The finite element method (FEM) is a computational technique widely used in orthodontic research to analyze stress and deformation in complex structures [16]. By constructing detailed three-dimensional (3D) models of the teeth, periodontal ligament (PDL), and jawbones, FEM simulates initial displacement and stress distribution under loading, providing visualized tissue responses that inform clinical treatment design. Owing to its non-invasive nature and high accuracy, FEM has become an indispensable tool for analyzing clear aligner biomechanics [17].

Therefore, this study constructed 3D models comprising the dentition, bilateral maxillary bones, midpalatal suture, PDL, IPE, and attachments to evaluate the stress distribution, displacement, and expansion efficiency of the dentition and craniofacial structures under IPE loading with different expansion step sizes and aligner thicknesses.

Materials and methods

Construction of the 3D finite element model

An 8.5-year-old mixed dentition patient with maxillary constriction underwent CBCT imaging (LARGEV, Beijing, China) using a 360° full field of view rotation at 100 kV and 4 mA. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of the participant for the acquisition and use of CBCT data. The patient had no history of orthodontic treatment, and the bilateral primary molars showed no mobility or obvious root resorption on CBCT. Reconstruction used a slice thickness of 0.15 mm and a pixel size of 0.3 mm. DICOM images were imported into Mimics 21.0 (Materialise, Belgium) for initial 3D reconstruction of the maxilla and dentition via threshold segmentation (Fig. 1A). Resulting STL models were processed in Geomagic Wrap 2021 (3D Systems, North Carolina, USA) using the “Relax” command for surface smoothing. Cortical and cancellous bone compartments were generated by a 2.0-mm inward offset of the maxillary model, while tooth roots were offset outward by 0.25 mm to create the PDL models (Fig. 1B).

The 3D models were exported in STEP format and imported into SolidWorks 2021 (Dassault Systèmes, France). The maxilla was sectioned midsagittally to create left and right halves with a 0.5-mm-wide suture. Subsequently, horizontal rectangular attachments (3 mm × 2 mm × 1 mm) were positioned at the buccal center of bilateral first and second primary molars and first permanent molars. Finally, Boolean operations were employed to fabricate smooth and integrated alveolar sockets and attachments (Fig. 1B).

The IPE model was constructed based on the crowns and maxillary cortical bone. First, the bilateral posterior teeth with attachments were moved buccally to target positions, and the palatal vault was offset by 1 mm. These components were assembled and offset outward by 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mm to create the initial models. The initial models were then trimmed anteriorly at the mesial marginal ridges of the first primary molars and posteriorly at the distal marginal ridges of the first molars, with a buccal extension of 3–4 mm over the gingiva. A final Boolean operation generated the final IPE models. All components were imported into ANSYS Workbench 2021 (Ansys, Pennsylvania, USA) to generate 3D finite element models meshed with 10‑node tetrahedral elements (Fig. 1C).

Experiment design

We applied step sizes of 0.1 mm (Group A), 0.2 mm (Group B), and 0.3 mm (Group C) to simultaneously expand the bilateral posterior teeth (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, corresponding assembled models were expanded outward by 0.5 mm (A1, B1, C1), 1.0 mm (A2, B2, C2), and 1.5 mm (A3, B3, C3) following dentition movement (Fig. 1E). Finally, the IPE model was overlaid onto the initial dentition model to generate the orthodontic forces.

Material properties and meshing

Material properties were taken from previous literature [18, 19]. All components (maxillary cortical/cancellous bone, midpalatal suture, teeth, attachments, periodontal ligament, and IPE) were defined as homogeneous, isotropic, linear elastic materials. Specifically, IPE properties were derived from 3D-printed Nylon-12 parameters [20]. All material properties are summarized in Table 1. Models were meshed with C3D10M tetrahedral elements; element and node counts are listed in Table 2.

Boundary constraints and contact conditions

The orbital base of the maxilla was defined as a fixed constraint. Bonded contacts were defined between teeth and PDL, PDL and alveolar bone, cortical bone and the midpalatal suture, and between cortical and cancellous bone. A nonlinear surface‑to‑surface contact (µ = 0.2) was defined between the outer crown surfaces and the inner IPE surface [21]. Frictionless sliding was assumed between tooth crowns. No additional constraints or loads were applied.

Coordinate system setting

In the global coordinate system, the X‑axis was coronal (positive left), Y‑axis sagittal (positive posterior), and Z‑axis vertical (positive superior). For each tooth, a local coordinate system was defined at its geometric center: X‑axis buccolingual (positive buccal), Y‑axis mesiodistal (positive mesial), Z‑axis vertical (positive toward root apex). For the midpalatal suture, a local coordinate system was centered at the midpoint corresponding to the second primary molar: X-axis (coronal, positive left), Y-axis (sagittal, positive anterior), Z-axis (vertical, positive superior) (Fig. 1F).

Calculation and analysis

Due to bilateral symmetry, analysis focused on the midpalatal suture and left posterior teeth. Landmarks included three crown and three root points per posterior tooth, plus two bilateral midpalatal suture points at the primary canine, first primary molar, second primary molar, and first permanent molar levels.

The analysis included the following: (1) total displacement of each tooth and the average X-axis displacement of the crown and root landmarks to derive the crown-to-root displacement ratio to assess inclination during movement; (2) the X-axis displacement of landmarks in the midpalatal suture at four tooth levels; (3) von Mises stress distribution in PDL and midpalatal suture. Convergence was verified in all load steps; representative solver output for the 0.3 mm step size is included in the Supplementary File 1.

Results

Effects of different aligner thicknesses

Initial displacement and movement trends of posterior teeth

The IPE aligner thickness markedly affected the initial displacement and movement pattern of the posterior teeth during expansion. Finite element analysis revealed that in groups A and B, total and buccal displacement of the first primary molar crown increased with greater aligner thickness, whereas the crown-to-root displacement ratio decreased progressively (Figs. 3 and 4). For instance, in group A, the buccal crown displacements for aligner thicknesses of 0.5 mm, 1.0 mm, and 1.5 mm were 2.345 × 10⁻² mm, 3.104 × 10⁻² mm, and 3.185 × 10⁻² mm, respectively, with corresponding crown-to-root ratios of 3.1427, 1.8453, and 1.3549 (Fig. 4), indicating a shift from buccal tipping toward bodily movement. The second primary molar and first molar also showed notable pattern changes, with the crowns transitioning from palatal to buccal displacement (Figs. 5 and 2). For instance, in group A, the first molar crown exhibited palatal displacement of approximately 2.785 × 10⁻² mm with the 0.5-mm aligner but moved buccally by 1.425 × 10⁻² mm (while retaining a palatal inclination) at 1.5 mm (Fig. 2). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that greater IPE aligner thickness improves root control, promoting bodily tooth movement over buccal tipping.

Von Mises Stress Distribution in the Posterior Tooth PDL

Increasing aligner thickness notably elevated PDL stress in posterior teeth (Fig. 6). Finite element analysis showed that von Mises stress in posterior PDL was slightly elevated with the 1.0 mm aligner compared with the 0.5-mm aligner, with substantial increases at 1.5 mm thickness. Furthermore, the maximum principal stress contour map of the first primary molar showed that as the aligner thickness increased, the stress distribution on the palatal root gradually transitioned from cervical concentration to a more uniform pattern, which was consistent with the corresponding change in its crown-to-root displacement ratio (Supplementary Fig. 1). Thus, increasing aligner thickness was associated with a more uniform stress distribution along the palatal root.

Initial X-axis displacement of the midpalatal suture

Aligner thickness notably affected the initial displacement of the midpalatal suture during expansion. Finite element analysis revealed that X-axis displacement of the midpalatal suture increased markedly with greater thickness, with the maximum displacement consistently occurring in the region corresponding to the first primary molar, followed by the primary canine region (Fig. 7).

Effects of different expansion step sizes

Initial displacement and movement trends of posterior teeth

Expansion step size is another key mechanical parameter governing both the initial displacement and its distribution of posterior teeth. Results showed that as step size increased, the overall buccal displacement of posterior teeth rose (Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 2), while the crown-to-root ratio exhibited no consistent pattern, presumably because it is dominated by appliance thickness. Furthermore, in groups A and B, the first primary molar crown consistently displaced buccally, while the first molar crown shifted from palatal to buccal displacement with palatal inclination (Fig. 2). In group C, however, the first primary molar crown displaced palatally (Fig. 4), and the second primary molar showed reduced buccal displacement (Fig. 5). Meanwhile, the first molar crown exhibited markedly increased buccal displacement (except at 0.5 mm thickness), leading to an overall buccal inclination (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that greater step sizes direct the mechanical response toward the posterior dental arch, enhancing expansion in that region at the cost of increased buccal inclination of the first molar and reduced expansion efficiency of the first primary molar. Notably, with 0.5-mm aligner thickness, the first molar crown consistently exhibited palatal movement, regardless of the step size (Fig. 2B).

Von mises stress distribution in the posterior tooth PDL

Increasing expansion step size markedly influenced both the magnitude and distribution of PDL stress in the posterior teeth (Fig. 6). In group B, the von Mises stress in the target teeth increased slightly compared with group A, while the overall stress distribution and the location of peak stress remained unchanged, with stress concentrated within the palatal PDL of the second primary molar. However, in group C, the peak stress shifted to the palatal PDL of the first molar, corresponding to the greatest observed tooth displacement (with the exception of group C1). Meanwhile, the maximum principal stress contour map of the first molar showed tensile stress concentration at the palatal cervical region in groups C2 and C3, which corresponded to the buccal tipping displacement (Supplementary Fig. 3). Thus, a larger step size (e.g., 0.3 mm) increased PDL stress and shifted its peak location posteriorly, particularly toward the first molar.

Initial x-axis displacement of the midpalatal suture

Expansion step size was a key factor influencing the initial displacement of the midpalatal suture, particularly in the posterior region. Finite element analysis revealed that the X-axis displacement of the suture increased with step size in all groups, with maximum displacement consistently in the area corresponding to the first primary molar, followed by the primary canine region (Fig. 7). Notably, the X-axis displacement of the midpalatal suture in the first molar region was slightly elevated with the 1.0 mm aligner thickness compared to the 0.5 mm, and increased substantially at 1.5 mm (Fig. 7 D), although it remained lower than in the first primary molar region. Thus, the posterior suture displacement was greatest when a larger step size was combined with a thicker aligner.

Von mises stress distribution in the midpalatal suture

A larger expansion step size markedly increased the von Mises stress within the midpalatal suture without altering its distribution pattern (Fig. 8). Finite element analysis showed that the maximum von Mises stress increased with step size and was concentrated in the suture region corresponding to the first primary molar (coinciding with the area of greatest displacement), followed by the primary canine region.

Discussion

Clear aligners are widely used clinically for their aesthetics and comfort. However, their application in maxillary expansion is limited by the inadequate strength of plastic materials [22], often resulting in dental compensation with limited skeletal effect [7, 23]. Recently, Align Technology Inc. introduced the Invisalign Palatal Expander (IPE); however, its underlying mechanism and the influence of key design parameters remain unclear. In this finite element study, we systematically evaluated how aligner thickness (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mm) and expansion step size (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mm) affected the biomechanical response of the teeth, periodontal tissues, and midpalatal suture during IPE expansion. The main findings indicated that aligner thickness primarily governs the pattern of posterior tooth movement, whereas step size mainly modulates its distribution.

In this study, aligners with uniform thicknesses of 0.5 mm, 1.0 mm, and 1.5 mm were designed to analyze their influence on expansion using the IPE system, whereas the commercial IPE has a non-uniform, transpalatal design (0.75 mm at the buccal surfaces, 1.5 mm at the occlusal surfaces, and 2.5–3 mm in the palatal region), contacting the palatal walls without engaging the vault. The results indicate that aligner thickness is a key parameter controlling posterior tooth movement type during expansion. Increasing aligner thickness enhanced buccal displacement of posterior teeth while reducing the crown-to-root displacement ratio, reflecting improved root control and transition from tipping to bodily movement. This contrasts with earlier studies on conventional clear aligners, where 0.75-mm aligners increased tooth displacement and PDL stress compared with 0.5-mm aligners but failed to promote bodily movement, with tipping remaining dominant [18]. Liadi et al. reported in a meta-analysis that thickness within 0.5–0.75 mm did not significantly affect the M/F ratio [24]. We attribute the IPE’s tendency for bodily movement to its 3–4 mm extension over the buccal gingiva of posterior teeth. Supporting this, Lyu et al. reported that a higher gingival margin position (+ 2 mm) of clear aligners significantly decreased the crown-to-root displacement ratio compared with a lower margin (-2 mm) [25].

Our findings demonstrate that greater aligner thickness markedly increases PDL stress in posterior teeth. Lee et al. noted maximum tolerable physiological PDL stress of 26 kPa [26]. Hohmann et al. observed that root resorption may occur when pressure in the PDL exceeds approximately 4.7 kPa [27], a threshold exceeded by maximum PDL stress in our groups. However, these simulations represent initial stress states, whereas orthodontic movement is dynamic. Research on conventional clear aligners demonstrated that high transient stresses decay exponentially over time and are unlikely to cause substantial periodontal damage [22]. Therefore, selecting an appropriate thickness requires balancing expansion efficiency with periodontal load [18], and clinicians should monitor stress concentration areas to prevent periodontal damage and root resorption.

To assess the effect of step size on IPE maxillary expansion, we applied step sizes of 0.1, 0.2, and 0.3 mm to the posterior teeth. On a per-side basis, the 0.1 mm step is slightly below the clinical increment of the IPE (≈ 0.125 mm; 0.25 mm bilaterally), whereas the 0.3 mm step is slightly above that of conventional rapid maxillary expansion (≈ 0.25 mm; 0.5 mm bilaterally). Increasing step size enhanced posterior buccal displacement, but the crown-to-root ratio showed no consistent trend. Notably, as step size increased, the first primary molar crown shifted from buccal to palatal movement, with reduced expansion efficiency. This aligns with conventional clear aligner mechanics. Preset molar expansion ≥ 2 mm significantly reduces premolar displacement and effectiveness [18]. In contrast, our results showed that increased step size generally shifted the first molar crown from palatal to buccal displacement (except at 0.5 mm thickness). This contrasts with conventional systems, where buccal displacement decreases from first premolar to second molar [18], and longer strides primarily increase buccal inclination [28] with limited terminal expansion [18, 29]. Potential reasons include: (1) molars feature multiple roots and a proportionally larger PDL surface area; (2) alveolar bone thickness increases posteriorly; and (3) the posterior segment experiences greater masticatory loads and cheek tissue resistance [7, 23]. Furthermore, the conventional U‑shaped aligner offers limited terminal control. In contrast, the palatal expander has an anteriorly narrow, posteriorly wide trapezoidal shape, which provides larger palatal contact. Thus, a higher step size can generate increased transient loading to overcome posterior anatomical resistance and promote more efficient expansion. However, this is accompanied by increased buccal inclination of the first molar and reduced expansion efficiency of the first primary molar.

Vertical side effects are clinically important during maxillary expansion in the mixed dentition. Critically, excessive buccal tipping can induce adverse effects, including inferior displacement of the palatal cusps, a reverse transverse occlusal curve, localized open bites, and an increase in vertical dimension [6]. We therefore provide the vertical displacement data in Supplementary Data 6. Overall, maxillary expansion with the IPE was accompanied by extrusive movement of the posterior cusps, and the maximum cuspal extrusion increased with both step size and aligner thickness. The vertical displacement of individual cusps was associated with the direction of tooth tipping: palatal tipping was accompanied mainly by downward (extrusive) movement of the buccal cusp, and buccal tipping mainly by downward movement of the palatal cusp. Among the posterior teeth, vertical changes of the first molar have the greatest clinical impact on occlusion and are therefore presented as an example. The first molar showed the largest vertical extrusion in the C2 and C3 groups, where buccal tipping was accompanied by extrusion of both the buccal and palatal cusps, with the palatal cusp showing the larger change. Such posterior extrusion could, in principle, increase the vertical dimension and contribute to clockwise mandibular rotation, especially in hyperdivergent patients.

A simplified midpalatal suture model was established in this study by separating the maxilla to analyze the expansion performance of the IPE. Based on patient age and literature, the suture was modeled as a uniform 0.5-mm elastic sheet (elastic modulus 0.68 MPa, Poisson’s ratio 0.45) [19, 30]. Results indicated that total and X‑axis suture displacement increased markedly with greater thickness and step size, with maximum displacement consistently at the first primary molar region, followed by primary canine. Notably, with 0.5-mm aligner thickness, the first molar crown consistently exhibited palatal movement, regardless of step size, yet the displacement of the corresponding suture region remained minimal. This suggests that insufficient aligner stiffness tends to produce palatal crown movement with limited transmission of force to the suture. The maximum principal stress contour map of the first molar showed that at an aligner thickness of 0.5 mm, tensile stress concentration occurred at the palatal apical region and the buccal cervical region, consistent with the palatal crown displacement (Supplementary Fig. 3). In the 1.5-mm thickness group, however, buccal displacement of the first molar crown increased substantially with longer step sizes, accompanied by markedly greater suture displacement. Thus, thickness is the dominant factor in IPE expansion. A thicker aligner combined with a greater step size directs mechanical forces toward the posterior region to a greater degree, thereby promoting greater midpalatal suture displacement in the posterior region.

This finding contrasts with previously reported skeletal effects of conventional clear aligner expansion. For example, Zhou et al. reported a significant increase in dental arch width measured by CBCT following conventional clear aligner treatment, with no significant change in maxillary basal bone width, indicating that expansion was achieved mainly through buccal tipping [9]. In a simulation of rapid expansion, Priyadarshini et al. applied a transverse load to the premolars and first molars and reported a maximum suture von Mises stress of approximately 299.40 MPa [31]. Bocklet et al. [32] applied a symmetrical 5-mm transverse displacement to a conventional Hyrax expander, recording a midpalatal suture stress of 24.35 MPa. Liu et al. simulated micro-implant-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE) with a staged protocol (0.2 mm per side in stage 1 and 2 mm per side in stage 2), estimating a midpalatal suture stress of 30–50 MPa for a four-implant device [33]. The von Mises stress at the midpalatal suture in the present study is lower than these values, as our simulation captures the instantaneous mechanical response under a single static load (0.1–0.3 mm per side) rather than the cumulative stress that would accumulate under multi-step, long-term loading with bone remodeling, which forms the basis of stable skeletal expansion in vivo. In addition, the initial displacement of the midpalatal suture was several orders of magnitude smaller than that of the tooth crowns, indicating minimal immediate skeletal engagement relative to dental displacement at this initial stage.

Notably, a prospective pilot study [15] reported a clinical midpalatal suture opening of 2.12 ± 0.79 mm with the IPE, not significantly different from the Hyrax group (2.67 ± 1.20 mm, p = 0.178), indicating that the IPE can achieve clinically meaningful suture opening through tissue creep and bone remodeling under sustained loading. However, the posterior tooth and suture displacements quantified in the present model may reflect a combination of true sutural separation, alveolar bending, and dentoalveolar compensation, which the current model does not fully distinguish. The reported midpalatal suture displacement should therefore be interpreted as a relative indicator of the distribution of mechanical effects rather than a final quantification of skeletal expansion. Suture opening in vivo depends on multiple factors, including patient age, suture maturity, tissue remodeling capacity, and the expansion protocol. Future studies incorporating clinical three-dimensional imaging (e.g., CBCT) to quantify the relative skeletal and dentoalveolar contributions to expansion would help validate the conclusions drawn from the present analysis.

The FEM is widely used for simulating clear aligner biomechanics. This study, however, has several limitations: (1) the models were reconstructed solely from CBCT data, lacking the accuracy of combined intraoral scans; (2) simulated initial displacement and stress reflect transient static responses, not dynamic tissue remodeling; (3) material properties were simplified to isotropic behavior; (4) anatomical variability in bone and suture morphology, density, and maturity may limit generalizability; (5) extraoral factors such as aligner wear patterns and masticatory function were not evaluated; (6) aligner thickness was modeled as uniform, which does not replicate the clinically non-uniform thickness design of the commercial IPE; and (7) standardized rectangular attachments were used across all groups, whereas clinical attachments are often customized. Previous studies indicate that attachment position, shape, and number significantly affect aligner force delivery [34–36]. Specifically, occlusally beveled horizontal attachments combined with buccal root torque reduced unintended buccal tipping [34], while attachment position (buccal vs. palatal) markedly affected tooth displacement and suture stress distribution [35]. Palatal attachments improve expansion efficiency but increase root stress 4–7–fold relative to buccal attachments, whereas buccal attachments with palatal pressure ridges better balance efficiency and torque control [36]. Therefore, FEM conclusions should be interpreted cautiously in clinical context. Future research should investigate IPE biomechanics using non-uniform thickness aligners and varied attachment configurations, along with animal experiments and large-sample prospective clinical trials to validate its effectiveness.

Conclusion

This study employed the FEM to analyze the biomechanical effects of IPE with different aligner thicknesses and expansion step sizes on teeth, periodontal tissues, and the midpalatal suture during maxillary expansion. Based on the results, the following conclusions were drawn:

Aligner thickness is a key parameter controlling the tooth movement pattern. Increasing thickness not only enhances the buccal displacement of posterior teeth but also improves root control, promoting bodily movement over buccal tipping, albeit at the cost of a marked increase in overall periodontal ligament stress.

Thinner aligners (0.5 mm), due to insufficient stiffness, tend to induce palatal displacement of the first molar with limited force transmission to the suture, whereas thicker aligners (≥ 1.0 mm) combined with a larger step size direct mechanical effects toward the posterior region to a greater degree, promoting greater midpalatal suture displacement in the posterior area.

Clinical recommendations

The primary clinical strategy should be to use thicker aligners (≥ 1.0 mm) to ensure sufficient stiffness for controlled bodily movement and effective expansion. On this basis, the step size can be moderately increased (e.g., 0.2–0.3 mm), accompanied by differentiated control of posterior teeth in terms of step size, sequencing, torque, and vertical anchorage, thereby enhancing expansion. As these step sizes exceed the current clinical increment, this recommendation is based on the present finite element analysis and warrants further clinical validation.
