Total Immunoglobulin G Variation Through the Phenological Cycle of Migrant and Resident Lesser Long‐Nosed Bats (Leptonycteris yerbabuenae) in the Drylands of Mexico and Its Relationship With Bacterial Killing‐Ability of Plasma
Authors: David Alfonso Rivera‐Ruiz, José Juan Flores‐Martínez, Carlos Rosales, Luisa I Falcón, Marco Tulio Solano de la Cruz, Rodrigo A Medellín, L Gerardo Herrera M
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBats have unique immunological characteristics among vertebrates. Understanding how the immune system responds during the life cycle of bat populations is crucial for grasping how their defenses against infection vary during critical life events. We used the lesser long‐nosed bat (Leptonycteris yerbabuenae) as a model to assess adaptive humoral immunity (total immunoglobulin G concentration, tIgG) at different periods during the year involving different ages, reproductive conditions and migratory habits (females migrate seasonally to give birth while males are resident). We also determined the relationship of tIgG with previously published values of plasma bacterial killing ability against Escherichia coli (BKA). tIgG did not vary among reproductive periods in males and females, although higher values were measured in non‐reproductive males in 2019 than in 2020. In contrast, tIgG was lower in young individuals than in non‐reproductive and lactating females. The tIgG had a positive relationship with BKA in non‐reproductive females, but no relationship was observed in non‐reproductive males and reproductive females. These results suggest that tIgG is resilient to physiological and ecological changes experienced by adults throughout their reproductive and migratory cycle. The low tIgG during early life suggests that young individuals have not developed an IgG pool comparable to that of adults. The positive relationship between tIgG and BKA in non‐reproductive females suggests that E. coli clearance is enhanced by the presence of antibodies that facilitate its elimination.Keywords: bats, BKA, early life, IgG, immunity, migration, reproduction

Introduction

The vertebrate immune system comprises innate and adaptive elements that work together to maintain homeostasis (Iwasaki and Medzhitov 2015). Some of these elements adjust their response depending on the energetic, hormonal, and environmental conditions of the individual (Adamo 2012; Demas et al. 2012; L. B. Martin et al. 2008; Tieleman 2018). This immune plasticity can be seen in the effect that demographic factors such as age (Simon et al. 2015), reproduction (Klein and Flanagan 2016), and migration (Hegemann et al. 2019) have on the immune system. Studies in birds, humans, laboratory animals and wild rodents show that immunological changes that occur throughout the life cycle are key to animal health (Beldomenico et al. 2008b, 2008a; Klein and Flanagan 2016; Moshkin et al. 1998; Sinclair and Lochmiller 2000; Tieleman 2018; Valdebenito et al. 2021).

The relationship between immunity and demography has been scarcely studied in wild mammals, particularly the immunological changes that occur from early life to adulthood in migratory animals. Bats are ideal organisms to address this issue, given that this group of mammals exhibits a great array of developmental and reproductive strategies, some of which are linked to migratory movements (Barclay and Harder 2003; Fleming and Eby 2003; Martínez‐Coronel et al. 2014; Popa‐Lisseanu and Voigt 2009). Additionally, bats have evolved immune adaptations that may enable them to coexist with some pathogenic microorganisms (Banerjee et al. 2020; Letko et al. 2020; Roffler et al. 2024; Szentivanyi et al. 2023). This coexistence is not always harmless, as bats are susceptible to multiple infectious agents (Hajkova and Pikula 2007; Helmick et al. 2004; Mühldorfer et al. 2014; Mühldorfer et al. 2011; Mühldorfer et al. 2011; Mühldorfer et al. 2011; Simpson 2000) whose biological cycles are closely linked to host phenology (Bergner et al. 2020; Dietrich et al. 2018; Plowright et al. 2008). Therefore, the immunological changes that bats experience throughout their life cycle are linked to their susceptibility to infection and potential outbreaks.

Although migration is widespread among mammals (Avgar et al. 2014), information on the impact of these movements on immunity is limited. Migration is a versatile strategy that, in most species, is associated with periodic movements that seek to exploit the most energetically favorable conditions (Fudickar et al. 2021; Gnanadesikan et al. 2017). Migration might affect immunity by either increasing (de Angeli Dutra et al. 2021; Hall et al. 2022; Poulin and de Angeli Dutra 2021) or reducing the risk of infection (Altizer et al. 2011; Shaw and Binning 2016, 2020), and by the energy costs (Owen and Moore 2008) and oxidative stress (Eikenaar et al. 2018) associated with this behavior. The effect of migration on the immune system of bats has only been studied in a handful of species. Migration affects the acute phase response (haptoglobin levels) of Pipistrellus nathusii, as well as the basal number of peripheral lymphocytes and neutrophils (Voigt et al. 2020). Males of Lasionycteris noctivagants that migrate long distances show no immunological changes with respect to resident males, but short‐migratory males have reduced total immunoglobulin G concentration (tIgG) and higher neutrophil counts (Rogers et al. 2022). Flow cytometry analysis in the Mexican free‐tailed bat (Tadarida brasiliensis) also reveals that migration alters systemic cellular immunity: innate immune cells increase during the migratory period, whereas lymphocyte numbers decrease (Allira et al. 2026). These results suggest that migration affects specific arms of bat immunity, and that this effect depends on the species' particular characteristics.

Reproduction affects many aspects of mammalian biology that can influence immune function (Josefson et al. 2020; S. M. Martin et al. 1995; Speakman 2008). In bats, the immune system may be altered by pathogen transmission (Christe et al. 2000; Dietrich et al. 2015; Drexler et al. 2011; Plowright et al. 2008) as well as by the hormonal (Otálora‐Ardila et al. 2022), microbial (Gaona et al. 2019; J. Li et al. 2021; Phillips et al. 2012) and redox changes experienced by reproductive individuals (Hernández‐Arciga et al. 2018). There is limited evidence of how reproduction affects the immune system of bats. For instance, bacterial‐killing ability against Escherichia coli (BKA) and other immunological parameters (i.e tIgG) remain unaffected by reproduction in males of Myotis daubentonii (Ruoss et al. 2019). While tIgG increased in pregnant M. daubentonii females compared to non‐reproductive (NR) females, BKA did not change between these two cohorts (Ruoss et al. 2019). Otherwise, pregnant M. vivesi exhibit decreased BKA compared to NR females (Otálora‐Ardila et al. 2022). Based on this limited evidence, there are no clear patterns in the way bat's immune response is related to reproductive activity.

The immunological changes that occur between early life and adulthood are one of the least explored topics in wild mammals. During early life, most innate immunity mechanisms are fully developed (Georgountzou and Nikolaos 2017), whereas several adaptive immunity components are not (Adkins et al. 2004; Cardoso et al. 2021; Pereira et al. 2019; Simon et al. 2015). Similarly, some innate immunological parameters are fully developed in young bats (Friedrichs et al. 2022; Rivera‐Ruiz et al. 2023), while humoral adaptive immunity still depends on maternal passive immunity (Baker et al. 2014; Epstein et al. 2013; Sohayati et al. 2011; Storm et al. 2018). Young bats may have a greater susceptibility to infection (Amman et al. 2012; Plowright et al. 2008), but the studies quantifying their immune system are scarce.

We have previously tested the relationship between immunity and demography on innate immunity in the lesser long‐nosed bat (Leptonycteris yerbabuenae, Phyllostomidae). This bat is mainly distributed in the arid and semi‐arid regions of Mexico (Medellín 2016). Most females on the Pacific coast mate in winter in central‐western Mexico (Ceballos et al. 1997; Stoner et al. 2003) and migrate to higher latitudes to form maternity colonies in spring‐summer (Cockrum 1991; Horner et al. 1998; Peñalba et al. 2006). In contrast, males are resident through the year (Arteaga et al. 2018; Menchaca et al. 2020; Morales‐Garza et al. 2007; Trejo‐Salazar et al. 2021; Wilkinson and Fleming 1996). We found that migratory females that have just arrived in the central‐western region of Mexico have lower BKA compared to non‐reproductive (NR) and pregnant females, that the BKA of young does not differ from that of lactating females, and that reproductive males exhibit a higher BKA than NR males (Rivera‐Ruiz et al. 2023).

Here, we extended our work with L. yerbabuenae to examine the relationship between adaptive humoral immunity (tIgG) and age, migration, and reproduction. As immunological experience accrues with age, young bats (having encountered fewer antigens and infections that mature their adaptive system) would have lower tIgG than adults (Bayram et al. 2019; Erhard et al. 1999; King et al. 1994). Regarding male reproduction, we tested whether male tIgG remains stable during reproduction, as observed in other bat species (Ruoss et al. 2019), or whether it decreases due to suppression of antibody production by androgens (Derting and Virk 2005; Hoffmann et al. 2023). We expected that females in west‐central Mexico in winter would have lower tIgG than pregnant females arriving in northern Mexico in spring, and that females returning to west‐central Mexico in fall. These predictions are based on evidence that pregnancy can increase the specific and total IgG in bats (Plowright et al. 2008; Ruoss et al. 2019) and that migration may further increase tIgG due to exposure to pathogens (Abolins et al. 2017; de Angeli Dutra et al. 2021; Poulin and de Angeli Dutra 2021). The impact of lactation on tIgG is not uniform among mammals (Czyżewska‐Dors et al. 2022; Flies et al. 2016; King et al. 1994; Xu et al. 2012) and the only study in bats suggests that lactation has a minimal impact on maternal tIgG (Ruoss et al. 2019). Therefore, we expected no significant differences between lactating females in summer and NR females sampled in this same period. Finally, we predicted that females returning to west‐central Mexico might exhibit higher tIgG than those captured in December (mating females) if they were exposed to a broader array of infections during their journey (Abolins et al. 2017; Poulin and de Angeli Dutra 2021). We also examined the relationship between tIgG and BKA in adults and expected a positive correlation because some IgG isotypes can activate the complement system (Goldberg and Ackerman 2020), which is the main immunological component associated with BKA against E. coli (Moore et al. 2011).

Materials and Methods

Study Sites and Sample Collection

We collected samples of L. yerbabuenae in west‐central Mexico in a marine cave in Don Panchito Island (also known as San Agustin), Jalisco (19°32′08.4″N, 105°05′18.8″W) and in the Fábrica cave in Coquimatlán, Colima (19°09′05.8″N, 103°50′06.9″O), and in northwestern Mexico in the Mariana cave near Carbó, Sonora (29°35′25.9″N, 110°48′8.9″W; Figure 1). Adul females and reproductive and NR adult males were captured in Don Panchito and Coquimatlán in March, May, October and December of 2019, and in Don Panchito in March 2020. In October–January, the colony in the cave in Don Panchito reaches its maximum size due to the arrival of migratory females in synchrony with the reproductive activity of males (Ceballos et al. 1997; Rincón‐Vargas et al. 2013; Stoner et al. 2003). After this period, many adult females progressively disappear from the colony, which is predominantly composed of NR adult males from February to August (Ceballos et al. 1997; Stoner et al. 2003). Although the population dynamics in Coquimatlán have not been described (Víquez‐R et al. 2021), it is probable that it follows the same pattern as in Don Panchito. It has been suggested that females migrate towards higher latitudes in spring and summer to give birth, following the blooming of columnar cacti and agaves (Burke et al. 2019; Cockrum 1991; Fleming et al. 1993). This hypothetical migratory route is supported by the fact that young individuals and lactating and pregnant females appear during May–August in northwestern Mexico and southwestern USA (Cockrum 1991). Accordingly, we collected samples of young individuals and pregnant and lactating females in Carbó in May and July–August 2019.

The Ethics Committee in Research and Teaching of the Institute of Biology, National Autonomous University of Mexico, approved the protocol of the study (CoETICA‐2025‐02), and we followed the guidelines established in the research permits authorized by the Dirección General de Vida Silvestre (#13222/18 y 8053/19). The capture and sampling of bats were carried out in accordance with the guidelines of the American Society of Mammalogists (Sikes et al. 2011). Bats were captured between 22:00 and 06:00 h with mist nets placed near the entrance of the Mariana cave, and with a hand net inside the caves in Don Panchito and Coquimatlán. Each individual was placed in a clean cotton bag until processing. Blood samples (ca. 100 µl, less than 1% of total blood volume) were collected within 30 min after capture by antebrachial vein puncture with a needle (30G × 1/2″) using a micropipette and sterile tips previously treated with heparin (Inhepar®, 5000 Ul/mL). Blood was placed in sterile heparin‐impregnated Eppendorf tubes and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 4 min to separate plasma from blood cells. Blood plasma was collected in cryotubes and placed in liquid nitrogen for transport and final disposal at −70°C in the laboratory. Finally, we recorded the sex, age category (adult or young), reproductive condition, length of forearm (Mitutoyo CD‐6, Mexico; ± 0.01 mm) and body mass (Ohaus, Nueva Jersey, USA, ± 0.1 g) of each individual. Young should be approximately 2 to 3 months old as they were captured in July, and pregnant migratory females arrive in the Sonora region around May to rear their young (Cockrum 1991). We distinguished adults from young by examining multiple phenotypical traits: the classical epiphyseal–diaphyseal fusion of the 4th metacarpal–phalangeal joint (Anthony 1988; Brunet‐Rossinni and Wilkinson 2009), the hair, and the body mass index (BMI; see below). In contrast to adult females in Sonora, young individuals had short grayish hair, wide epiphalangeal sutures (in almost all phalanges), and lower BMI. Reproductively active males of L. yerbabuenae are characterized by scrotal testicles and a prominent patch on the scapular region, which is involved in attracting females (Muñoz‐Romo et al. 2012; Nassar et al. 2008). Although there are males with scrotal testicles during April‐July in Don Panchito, only the September‐December males with scrotal testicles and the dorsal patch produce semen (Rincón‐Vargas et al. 2013). For females, we classified them as pregnant by palpation of their abdomen and lactating if they presented hairless nipples that released milk after being pressed. Some females captured in Carbó did not present external morphological characteristics indicating that they were pregnant or lactating and were arbitrarily classified as NR, and we followed the same criteria for females captured between October and December in Don Panchito that were probably mating.

Body Mass Index (BMI)

Previous studies have determined that fat stores of L. yerbabuenae in Don Panchito vary throughout the year (Ceballos et al. 1997). Therefore, we calculated the BMI (body mass/ forearm length) as a measure of body condition across different demographic cohorts (Durán 2022; Lacki et al. 2015; Park et al. 2000; Pearce et al. 2008; Speakman and Racey 1986). This ratio is intended to determine the magnitude of fat reserves as a function of individual size, as individuals with a larger lean mass should be heavier than smaller individuals regardless of accumulated fat reserves (McGuire et al. 2018).

Quantification of Total IgG Concentration

As a measure of adaptive humoral immunity, we quantified tIgG by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay in 96‐well plates (SARSTEDT “ELISA‐Plate, High Binding 82.1581.200). Because plasma tIgG had not been measured previously in L. yerbabuenae, we performed a pilot test to determine the ideal plasma dilution. We randomly selected two samples (2µL per sample) representing NR and reproductive males, NR and reproductive females, and young individuals. The plasma samples were subjected to double serial dilutions using sodium bicarbonate as solvent (NaHCO3, 0.1M, pH = 9.6), within a dilution range of 1/625 (2 µL of plasma in 1248 µL of NaHCO3) to a dilution of 1/80000. Each of the dilutions performed was worked in triplicate, where a volume of 50 µL per well was used.

The plate with the plasma dilutions was incubated for 18 h at 4°C to favor the binding of the plasma proteins to the bottom of the wells. Following this incubation, most of the diluted plasma volume was removed, and 2 washes were performed with 200 µL of 0.05% PBS‐Tween‐20 to remove excess sample. The next step consisted of blocking the bottom of the plate with 50 µl of bovine serum albumin (BSA) diluted 1% in PBS 1×. The plate was incubated for 2 h at room temperature and then washed 2 times with 200 µL of PBS‐Tween‐20 0.05%. To detect plasma tIgG, a volume of 50 µL of peroxidase‐coupled anti‐IgG antibody (Goat anti‐Bat IgG (H+L) Secondary Antibody [HRP], NB7238, NOVUS BIOLOGICAL) was added at a 1/10000 dilution and allowed to react for 2 h at room temperature. After incubation, two washes were performed with 200 µL of PBS‐Tween‐20 0.05% and 50 µL of SIGMA FAST‐OPD (p9187) developer solution was added to each well, and the colorimetric reaction was stopped after 5 min with 100 µL of H2SO4. Finally, the absorbance of the plate was recorded from 450 to 500 nm to detect the most appropriate wavelength.

The average absorbance of the triplicates of each of the 10 serially diluted samples was calculated to determine the most appropriate dilution. From the range of dilutions in which tIgG decreased as the dilution factor increased (Supplementary Figure S1 and Table S1), the 1/20000 dilution was determined to be the most appropriate as it has the steepest slope (highest rate of change per dilution factor). The absorbance was recorded at 490 nm because the highest absorbance was observed at this wavelength. Therefore, all remaining samples were worked in triplicate and diluted at a ratio of 1 part plasma (2 µL) to 20000 NaHCO3 using the same procedure used for assay standardization. Antibody concentration for each sample was determined from the average optical density (OD) of three replicates, as color intensity is directly proportional to antibody concentration.

Plasma Bacterial Killing‐Ability (BKA)

Some of the BKA data were extracted from Rivera‐Ruiz et al. (2023) and used exclusively to test their relationship with tIgG. We complemented the BKA dataset with additional samples that were not included in the original article. The BKA of these additional samples is presented here as supporting information (Supplementary Figures S2, S3). The experimental design of BKA was based on the protocols proposed by Liebl and Martin (Liebl and Martin II 2009) and French and Neuman‐Lee (French and Neuman‐lee 2012), in which the Escherichia coli strain ATCC 8739 (Microbiologics, St. Cloud, MN, USA) was used as a target microorganism to quantify the ability of bat plasma (natural antibodies, complement system and lysozyme) to inhibit its growth. The BKA assay is a common immunological measure in bat studies (Becker et al. 2017, 2018; Costantini et al. 2019; Fritze et al. 2021; Hernández‐Arciga et al. 2018; Ingala et al. 2019; Moore et al. 2011; Otálora‐Ardila et al. 2022; Ruoss et al. 2019; Schneeberger et al. 2013, 2014; Seltmann et al. 2022; Strobel and Becker 2015). To avoid contamination, experiments were conducted in a laminar flow hood using autoclaved equipment and sterile disposable materials. Lyophilized pellets of E. coli were reconstituted in a phosphate‐buffered solution (PBS) and diluted to a working concentration of 105 bacteria/mL. Plasma samples were thawed only once, and they were used immediately. We used 96‐well conical‐bottom cell culture microplates (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany, 83.3926.500) to plate 9 replicates of positive controls (18 µL of 0.01 M sterile PBS; Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 6 replicates of negative controls (18 µL of PBS), 9 replicates of an antibiotic control (2 µL of ampicillin at a concentration of 0.05 µg/mL in 16 µL of PBS), and 3 replicates of each sample (2 µL of plasma diluted in 16 µL of PBS). We then added 4 µL of a bacterial suspension with a concentration of 105 bacteria/mL to all wells except the negative controls. The plate was partially covered during all procedures that required removal from the cabinet to avoid contamination (incubation and absorbance measurement) and to allow oxygen flow. After the plate was covered, the plate was incubated at 200 rpm and 37°C for 30 min. Later, 128 µL of nutrient broth (BD 234,000) was added to each well, and the absorbance of the plate was recorded at a wavelength of 600 nm (Epoch Microplate Reader, Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA). Following this measurement, the plate was incubated for a second time at 200 rpm for 12 h at 37°C. Then, we measured the absorbance at 600 nm (Epoch Microplate Reader, Biotek). The negative control was used to ensure that there was no contamination, and the antibiotic control was used to verify that the bactericidal action could be evaluated with the assay, but they were not used in the final calculation of BKA. The calculation of BKA was determined as the percentage of bacteria eliminated by plasma in relation to the positive control, using the mean of the replicates for each parameter, with the following formula:

BKA (%) = percentage of bacterial‐killing ability, FA pla = final plasma absorbance, IA pla = initial plasma absorbance, FA cont = final absorbance of the positive control, and IA cont = initial absorbance of the positive control.

Statistical Analysis

We used R software version 4.3.2 for statistical analysis (R Core Team 2022). According to the results of the fitdistrplus package (Delignette‐Muller and Dutang 2015), the lognormal and normal probability distribution functions were the most appropriate for the analyses of BMI and tIgG, respectively. The analyses of both variables were performed with a general linear model, and the statistical significance was determined by means of a deviance analysis using the ANOVA function of the car package (Fox and Weisberg 2019). When an explanatory variable significantly affected the response variable, the multcomp package was used to perform a Tukey multiple comparisons test to identify cohorts that differed from each other (Hothorn et al. 2008) and for the case of interacting factors, we used the emmeans package (Lenth 2024). The statistical models used to assess demographic variation in tIgG did not include BMI as a covariate because body condition was not homogeneous within the demographic groups (Figure 2), which violates the principle of independence of explanatory variables (Miller and Chapman 2001). Outliers that could significantly affect the models for BMI (we removed 2 NR males of Don Panchito) and tIgG (we removed one NR female and one young) were identified by means of Cook's distances. Finally, all models were validated by residual analysis using the DHARMa package (Hartig 2024).

To enable coherent biological comparisons, the data were divided into four sets. The first set of data was used to test the effect of male reproductive activity on tIgG. For this model, we only considered adult males from Don Panchito (n = 82) because we did not collect reproductive males in other locations. The reproductive cohorts in December were compared with the NR cohorts in March–May and October–December 2019 and March 2020. A second set of data examined how location affects tIgG in NR adult males in west‐central Mexico (n = 82). NR adult males from Coquimatlán and Don Panchito were compared in March–May 2019 and October‐December 2019. Location and season were used as the main factors in the statistical model for this dataset, along with their interaction.

A third dataset evaluated the effect of the different reproductive phases of females on tIgG (n = 62). As female reproduction and migration occur concomitantly, it is not possible to discern the isolated effect of either factor. Pregnant and lactating migratory females from Carbó in May–July 2019 were compared with non‐reproductive migratory females from Don Panchito in October–December 2019 and from Carbó in March–May 2019. Finally, a fourth data set was used to examine the development of adaptive immunity in the early stages (n = 40). Young individuals from Carbó in May–July 2019 were compared with lactating and non‐reproductive females that were captured in the same region and time. Sex can affect bat immunity (Costantini et al. 2019; Hernández‐Arciga et al. 2018; Otálora‐Ardila et al. 2022; Ruoss et al. 2019), and although it is possible to assess the effect of this variable between NR cohorts of males and females of Don Panchito in October‐December 2019, we discarded this comparison because females had probably completed migration, which would obscure the physiological differences associated with sex. BMI analysis was performed across the same four data sets described for tIgG analysis.

We tested the relationship between BKA and tIgG with a Spearman correlation test (Puth et al. 2015) because BKA had a non‐parametric distribution with many tied data (many individuals have 100% and 0% values) for each of the following cohorts: NR females, reproductive females (we pooled pregnant and lactating females because there was no immunological difference between them), and NR males (we pooled NR males from Coquimatlán and Don Panchito because these cohorts did not exhibit immunological differences). To visualize the relationship between BKA and tIgG concentration in the group where a significant correlation was detected (non‐reproductive females), we fitted a generalized linear model (GLM) with a quasi‐binomial error distribution and a logit link function (Dunn and Smyth 2018). The model was used to predict the trend line and its 95% confidence interval. The relationships between immunological parameters of young and reproductive males were not tested due to low sample sizes.

Results

Body Mass Index Varied Seasonally and Demographically

For the subset 1 (reproductive cycle of males in Don Panchito), BMI differed between males in different reproductive stages (F3,76 = 3.99, p = 0.01; n = 80, Figure 2A). Reproductive males in December (−0.670 ± 0.110, mean ± SD, here and thereafter, n = 7) had significantly higher values than NR males in March–May 2019 (−0.775 ± 0.098, n = 37), October–December 2019 (−0.784 ± 0.111, n = 19) and March 2020 (−0.814 ± 0.546, n = 19; Table 1). In the subset data 2 (variation by locality and season in males), BMI was not affected by locality (F1,76 = 1.39, p = 0.24, n = 80, Figure 2B) and season (F1,76 = 2.19, p = 0.14, n = 80, Figure 2B) in NR males from Don Panchito and Coquimatlán. However, we detected an effect of the interaction between locality and season (F1,76 = 6.39, p = 0.01, n = 80, Figure 2B). This was driven by an increase in BMI (Table 1) in males from October‐December 2019 (0.747 ± 0.102, n = 14) in Coquimatlán compared to males from March–May 2019 (0.861 ± 0.080, n = 12). In contrast, NR males in Don Panchito did not exhibit seasonal variations in BMI (Table 1).

Finally, young in Carbó (n = 17), lactating (n = 17) and NR females (n = 8) captured in the same period exhibit differences in their BMI (F2,39 = 25.80, p < 0.001, n = 42; Figure 2D). Young in Carbó (−0.893 ± 0.070) had lower BMI than lactating females (−0.700 ± 0.075) and NR females (−0.753 ± 0.105; Table 1).

Discussion

The relationship between immunity and demography in bats is a controversial and understudied topic that is key to identifying periods of susceptibility when bat defenses may be lowered, and infection can proliferate (Becker et al. 2018; Plowright et al. 2008). We provide evidence that tIgG, an important immunological parameter to control systemic infection (Augustyniak et al. 2017; Leitao Filho et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2016), is stable throughout the adult reproductive and migratory cycle of L. yerbabuenae, but it is significantly reduced during early life.

Seasonal Variation of Total Immunoglobulin G Concentration in Adult Males

As in M. daubentonii, reproductive males showed no difference in plasma tIgG compared to NR males. We hypothesized that reproductive males of L. yerbabuenae would exhibit lower tIgG than NR males because increased androgenic hormones during reproduction could decrease antibody production (Derting and Virk 2005; Greives et al. 2006; Gubbels Bupp and Jorgensen 2018; Hoffmann et al. 2023; Kanda et al. 1996, 1997; Klein and Flanagan 2016). While it is plausible to conclude that IgG production is unaffected by androgenic hormones in L. yerbabuenae, measurement of plasma testosterone concentration is warranted to fully test this idea. This scenario would be consistent with Otálora‐Ardila et al. (2022), who found no relationship between plasma testosterone and elements of the innate cellular and humoral response of M. vivesi. Alternatively, we cannot discard the possibility that reproductive season may not be directly related to an increase in androgenic hormones, as is the case in some bat species (Krishna and Bhatnagar 2011; L. Martin and Bernard 2000). However, we consider this scenario unlikely given the anatomical (Rincón‐Vargas et al. 2013) and immunological (Rivera‐Ruiz et al. 2023) changes that L. yerbabuenae males undergo during the reproductive season. Finally, it is possible that we did not detect the immunosuppressive effect of androgenic hormones on antibody production in reproductive males. This might have occurred as a result of increase contagious rate during mating when population size doubles (Nunn et al. 2000; Stoner et al. 2003) and to the presence of potential pathogenic bacteria in the dorsal patch of reproductive males (Gaona et al. 2019) that could have stimulated IgG production (Abolins et al. 2011, 2017; Beuvon et al. 2021; Cave et al. 2012; Hunziker et al. 2003; Maden et al. 2013; Proverbio et al. 2014).

NR males had lower tIgG in March‐May 2019 than in March 2020, and there is no demographic factor that could explain these differences. It is possible that males in March–May 2019 were malnourished and had been recently exposed to pathogens, because the combination of these factors can decrease tIgG (Michael et al. 2020). However, the BMI of males was similar in both years, which suggests that this scenario is unlikely. Other possible scenarios that could explain the reduced tIgG in some NR males in March–May 2019 could be related to changes in gut microbial composition, as gut microbiota is a key determinant of tIgG (Kim et al. 2016; Vujkovic‐Cvijin et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2016).

Effect of Female Reproduction and Migration on Total Immunoglobulin G Concentration

Plasma tIgG in females of L. yerbabuenae was stable throughout their reproductive cycle. Although the energetic cost of lactation is high (Dewey 1997; Speakman 2008) and tIgG is lower during this period (Flies et al. 2016; King et al. 1994), tIgG is reported to increase in some lactating species (Xu et al. 2012). Lactating L. yerbabuenae exhibited similar tIgG than NR females in Carbó. This finding is consistent with the results reported for M. daubentonii (Ruoss et al. 2019) and with our hypothesis. Our results might be explained by the lack of an energetic trade‐off between lactation and tIgG due to the high availability of food resources for lactating L. yerbabuenae (Burke et al. 2019; Horner et al. 1998), the resilience of tIgG to the energetic cost of lactation (Brown et al. 2008; Brüssow et al. 1996; Rytter et al. 2014), and the rapid tIgG restoration to the levels found in NR females (Anugu et al. 2013; Czyżewska‐Dors et al. 2022; Devillers et al. 2004; Hernández‐Castellano et al. 2019; Herr et al. 2011).

Pregnancy in L. yerbabuenae did not affect tIgG, contradicting our hypothesis that the combined effect of gestation and migration would elevate tIgG. We anticipated this increase because migration might expose females to a wide array of pathogens (Abolins et al. 2017; Poulin and de Angeli Dutra 2021), and previous studies in bats reported elevated levels of specific (Plowright et al. 2008) and total IgG (Ruoss et al. 2019) during pregnancy. The tIgG dynamic varies throughout gestation (Feijo et al. 2026; Kustritz 2005; Zhang et al. 2021), and the discrepancy between our results and those for M. daubentonii might reflect distinct gestational stages. Pregnant females of M. daubentonii were sampled in early‐to‐mid gestation (Ruoss et al. 2019), whereas we obtained samples from females in late gestational stages (Peñalba et al. 2006; Stoner et al. 2003). Furthermore, L. yerbabuenae may exhibit hemochorial placentation, as this type of placentation is widespread among bats and it has only been recorded in Phyllostomidae species (Badwaik and Rasweiler 2000; Caldwell et al. 2025; Carter and Mess 2008). A hallmark of this placentation is the progressive reduction of maternal tIgG due to IgG transfer to the fetus (Palmeira et al. 2012; Sulila et al. 1988; Zhang et al. 2021), which is inconsistent with our results and future studies should address this issue.

We did not detect changes in tIgG associated with migration in female L. yerbabuenae. In contrast, Rogers et al. (2022) reported lower tIgG in male L. noctivagans that migrated up to 250 km compared to resident individuals. This apparent negative effect of migration is unlikely to stem solely from energy cost, as tIgG did not differ between short‐ and long‐migratory bats (Rogers et al. 2022). This is further supported by our findings in L. yerbabuenae, which migrates up to 2000 km per journey without significant shifts in tIgG, aligning with studies that demonstrate the stability of this immune parameter to energetic constraints (Brown et al. 2008; Brüssow et al. 1996; Rytter et al. 2014). Although we hypothesized that exposure to diverse pathogens across multiple caves during their return journey to west‐central Mexico would elevate tIgG (Banskar et al. 2016; L. Li et al. 2010; Poulin and de Angeli Dutra 2021), our data do not support this.

Concentration of Total Immunoglobulin gG During Early Life

According to our hypothesis, lactating and NR adult females from Carbó had higher tIgG than young individuals. This partially aligns with previous findings in Saccopteryx bilineata, where tIgG increased with age in 1–6‐year‐old individuals (Schneeberger et al. 2014). To our knowledge, our study is the first attempt to assess tIgG in bats during early life, specifically in bats that were two and 3 months of age. This age range was inferred from their forearm length (53.17 ± 1.27 mm) and weight (21.81 ± 1.64 g), which were slightly higher than individuals of L. yerbabuenae with a recorded age of 35 days (Martínez‐Coronel et al. 2014). Furthermore, these individuals were sampled 2–3 months after the establishment of maternity colonies in Sonora (Cockrum 1991; Horner et al. 1998; Peñalba et al. 2006), and they presented a lower BMI compared to lactating and NR adult females in Carbó, which further suggests that they were still in a growth phase.

The lower concentration of tIgG in young compared to adults is a conserved immunological feature across all mammals studied to date (Bayram et al. 2019; Chastant and Mila 2019; Erhard et al. 1999; King et al. 1994), regardless of the mode of placentation and maternal antibody transfer (Borghesi et al. 2014). It is therefore plausible that the reduced antibody production capacity during early life in humans (Clemens and Alexander‐Miller 2021; Hill et al. 2020; Labeur‐Iurman and Harker 2024) and model rodents (Pyle et al. 2021) also account for the lower tIgG in young L. yerbabuenae. Although certain B1 cell populations spontaneously secrete natural IgG antibodies independently of antigenic stimuli (Palma et al. 2018), the main source of circulating tIgG comes from plasma cells derived from antigen‐specific B2 lymphocytes (Bos et al. 1989; Cukrowska et al. 2001; Manz et al. 2005). Therefore, the reduced tIgG in young L. yerbabuenae likely reflects limited immunological experience. Consistent with patterns in other mammals, these results suggest that, although some components of innate defenses are fully developed in young bats (Friedrichs et al. 2022; Rivera‐Ruiz et al. 2023), adaptive humoral immunity requires a longer period to mature. This ontogenetic delay may increase the susceptibility of young bats to infection, as they have not yet built up a robust pool of IgG (Augustyniak et al. 2017; Leitao Filho et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2016).

Correlation Between BKA and tIgG in Adult Males and Females

BKA and innate immunity may have an antagonistic relationship with adaptive immunity when infection risk or the energy status of the individual prioritizes the activation of one component over the other (Becker et al. 2018; Ezenwa et al. 2012; Lee 2006; Mcdade et al. 2016). However, the synergy between both branches of the immune system is key to host health and homeostasis. BKA against E. coli is mainly dependent on complement activity (Becker et al. 2019; Matson et al. 2005; Merchant et al. 2003; Moore et al. 2011), which can be activated by some IgG isotypes via the classical pathway (Goldberg and Ackerman 2020; Merle et al. 2015; Nesargikar et al. 2012). Unlike males, NR females showed a strong positive correlation between BKA and tIgG. These results suggest that NR females have a pool of IgG that recognizes E. coli and favors their elimination via the complement system. The presence of these antibodies in plasma could be a consequence of a bacterial translocation process from the gut to the bloodstream (Vujkovic‐Cvijin et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2016), since E. coli inhabits the intestinal tract of bats (Federici et al. 2022; Nowakiewicz et al. 2020) and its translocation may stimulate the generation of antibodies (Liu et al. 2024). Alternatively, antibodies directed against other types of microorganisms may cross‐react with E. coli antigens (Augustyniak et al. 2017; Keasey et al. 2009). Thus, testing this hypothesis will require the use of specific E. coli antigens as targets. Beyond the presence of E. coli‐specific IgG, the correlation between BKA and tIgG in NR females might also reflect an IgG profile composed of allotypes, isotypes, and glycosylation patterns that promote complement activation (Damelang et al. 2023; Jennewein and Alter 2017).

Conclusions

Our results showed that plasma tIgG was reduced during early life in L. yerbabuenae, and although systemic concentration of this antibody may be compromised in some adult individuals, tIgG was not affected by the reproductive season in adults and, in the case of females, by migration. Despite low tIgG in young individuals and the stability of tIgG in adults, there is a large variability between individuals, which may be a consequence of the genes (Liao et al. 2012), microbiota (Kim et al. 2016; Vujkovic‐Cvijin et al. 2022), and age of the individual (Schneeberger et al. 2014). For instance, preliminary work with this species shows the existence of significant associations between tIgG and gut microbiota composition (D. A. Rivera‐Ruiz and L. G. Herrera M., in preparation). Therefore, future studies aimed at determining the contribution of these factors on tIgG in healthy individuals of L. yerbabuenae would allow the establishment of a reference range that would help to identify not only individuals who may be experiencing an infectious process (Abolins et al. 2011, 2017; Beuvon et al. 2021; Cave et al. 2012; Hunziker et al. 2003; Maden et al. 2013; Proverbio et al. 2014), but also individuals who may be more resistant to infection (Leitao Filho et al. 2020; Pollack et al. 1983). In line with the latter idea, NR females showed a positive relationship between tIgG and BKA, suggesting that females have a systemic antibody pool that enhances E. coli clearance. These results highlight the need for a thorough characterization of the IgG profile in L. yerbabuenae, as the subclasses present, their biological function, and the antigens that stimulated their development are unknown.
