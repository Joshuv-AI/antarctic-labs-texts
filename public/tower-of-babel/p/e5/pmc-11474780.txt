Efficacy of 100% autologous platelet-rich plasma and 100% autologous serum in dry eye disease: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Passara Jongkhajornpong, Kaevalin Lekhanont, Sasivimol Rattanasiri, Pawin Numthavaj, Gareth McKay, John Attia, Ammarin Thakkinstian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe 0bjective is to compare treatment effects between undiluted autologous platelet-rich plasma (APRP) and autologous serum (AS) in patients with moderate-to-severe dry eye disease (DED).Methods and analysisA single-centre, randomised, double-masked, non-inferiority clinical trial was conducted. 96 adult DED patients with an Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) Score of ≥23 and/or Oxford staining grade of ≥2 were randomised to receive either 100% APRP (n=48) or 100% AS (n=48) for 4 weeks. Primary outcomes included OSDI Score and ocular surface staining measured by Oxford grading scale at 4 weeks. Secondary outcomes included fluorescein tear break-up time, Schirmer’s test, meibum quality and expressibility, and adverse events. The 95% CI for the mean difference in OSDI scores between groups was estimated to assess non-inferiority of the OSDI score at a prespecified margin of 4.18 points.ResultsAt week 4, there was no significant difference in decreased OSDI scores between groups, with the mean difference (100% APRP−100% AS) of 1.41 (95% CI −1.26, 4.08, p=0.299). The upper limit was less than the prespecified margin, indicating non-inferiority of 100% APRP vs 100% AS. The probabilities of achieving an Oxford grade 0–1 after treatment were not significantly different between groups, with an OR of 0.61 (95% CI 0.25, 1.52, p=0.288). No significant differences in secondary outcomes were observed between groups.ConclusionIn the short-term, 100% APRP was not inferior to 100% AS in reducing dry eye symptoms and ocular surface staining in moderate-to-severe DED.Trial registration numberNCT04683796.Keywords: Clinical Trial, Ocular surface, Tears, Treatment Medical

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common and heterogenous entity, varying widely in clinical presentation, severity and response to treatment among patients.1 2 Biological tear substitutes have been recommended as an additional treatment option by the Tear Film and Ocular Surface Society Dry Eye Workshop (TFOS DEWS) II for patients who have not responded to conventional treatments.3 In addition to providing lubrication to the ocular surface, they offer significant advantages over artificial tears due to their similarity to natural tears in many biochemical properties, including osmolarity, pH, vitamins, enzymes, fibronectin and essential growth factors.3

Biological tear substitutes encompass a range of products distinguished by their preparation process. Autologous serum (AS) eye drops are considered the primary and most commonly used blood derivative to treat patients with DED.4 5 However, recent evidence has shown that platelet-based products might provide a superior healing effect in the treatment of ocular surface diseases compared with AS,6 7 possibly because of the higher levels of growth factors necessary for corneal epithelialisation present in platelet releasate compared with serum.8 A network meta-analysis has also demonstrated that platelet-based products outperformed AS in reducing Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) and corneal fluorescein staining scores in dry eye patients. Nonetheless, these findings remain uncertain due to sparse data and the low quality of the included studies.9 Additionally, there have been only three small prospective studies in the literature comparing the efficacy of 20%–100% autologous platelet-rich plasma (APRP) and AS eye drops in patients with either primary Sjögren’s syndrome (SS)-related DED or non-SS DED and their results are still controversial.10,12 Therefore, we conducted a randomised controlled trial (RCT) to compare the efficacy and safety of 100% APRP and 100% AS in patients with moderate-to-severe DED.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was a single-centre, randomised, double-masked, parallel, non-inferiority clinical trial. The trial protocol has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier, NCT04683796) and published elsewhere.13

Participants

Patients diagnosed with moderate-to-severe DED based on the dry eye severity grading scheme recommended by TFOS DEWS I,14 who attended the ophthalmology out-patient department at Ramathibodi Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand between February 2021 and November 2022 were screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were: (1) adults aged 18–75 years at the time of consent; (2) OSDI Score of ≥23 and/or Oxford staining grade of ≥2; (3) unresponsive to conventional treatments; (4) none of the following conditions: uncontrolled systemic diseases, active infection, advanced cancer, pregnant or lactation; received anticoagulants; topical blood products use within 3 months; punctal plug or contact lens use; prior ocular surgery within 6 months; active ocular infection or inflammation, abnormal eyelid function or severe meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD stage 4)15; positive for HIV, hepatitis B or C, syphilis and anaemia.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the development, design and conduct of the study, and also our plans to disseminate the study results to participants and relevant wider patient communities. However, at the end of the study, all patients were asked to assess the burden of the study interventions and time required to participate in the study.

Randomisation and masking

Block randomisation with varying block sizes of four to eight and an allocation ratio of 1:1 was generated by an independent statistician. The randomised assignments were concealed from the investigators in sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes until immediately before phlebotomy. This task was primarily the responsibility of an independent research nurse. All participants and research team members were masked to the randomisation sequence. One or both eyes of each participant were enrolled, depending on eligibility, and were treated identically.

Procedures

The study protocol was described in detail elsewhere.13 Briefly, eligible patients were asked to stop their current topical dry eye treatments including artificial tears at least 48 hours before the first visit. During this washout period, patients were allowed to use preservative-free 0.3% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose/0.1% dextran (Tears Naturale Free; Alcon, Fort Worth, Texas, USA) as needed. At the first visit, patient sociodemographic and clinical information such as age, sex, educational level, occupation, smoking status, ocular and systemic comorbidities, previous ocular surgery, and current topical and systemic medications was collected. Patients then underwent baseline ophthalmic examinations in the following order: best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), intraocular pressure (IOP) measured by an air-puff tonometer, OSDI Questionnaire, fluorescein tear breakup time (FBUT), ocular surface staining using the Oxford Scheme, Schirmer’s test (ST) without anaesthesia and meibomian quality and expressibility. Slit-lamp examination was performed by one of two masked ophthalmologists (PJ or KL).

After completing baseline assessment, patients were randomly assigned to receive either 100% APRP or 100% AS every 2 hours (eight times daily) for 4 weeks. The importance, possible side effects, application and storage of eye drops were explained to participants and accompanying helpers by a research assistant. Follow-up assessments were scheduled at 2 and 4 weeks. At each follow-up visit, a masked examiner (PJ or KL) evaluated OSDI Questionnaire, FBUT, ocular surface staining and ST in the same manner as was used during baseline assessment. BCVA, IOP and meibomian quality and expressibility were measured only at 4 weeks post-treatment. Adherence was monitored by asking participants to record the number of eye drops administered per day. Both treatment options were given free of charge to patients, and transport cost was subsidised to further increase adherence. Non-compliance was defined as missing eye drop treatment more than two times per daily (more than 30% of the expected number of eye drops administered to the eye per day). Adverse events were assessed and recorded at all follow-up visits.

Interventions

Of note, APRP was chosen rather than plasma rich in growth factors and platelet lysate because its preparation was less complicated and was easier to use in clinical practice.4 16 Both biological tear substitutes were prepared from peripheral blood of each patient under strict sterile conditions inside a laminar flow hood according to well-established protocols.17 18 Due to limited equipment availability in our laboratory, some preparation parameters were adjusted to achieve the optimal platelet enrichment recommended by the protocol.17 Briefly, venesection was performed at the antecubital fossa and 108 mL of whole blood were collected aseptically into non-heparinised sterile plastic syringes. For 100% APRP, the collected blood was equally divided into three 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing 4 mL of 3.2% sodium citrate anticoagulant solution to ensure a blood-to-citrate ratio of 9:1 and then centrifuged at 350×g for 10 min at 20°C in a Sorvall Legend Mach 1.6R benchtop centrifuge (Kendro Laboratory Products, North Carolina, USA). After centrifugation, the plasma was carefully collected and 1.5 mL aliquots were transferred into 5 mL sterile opaque plastic bottles with eye drop applicators.

For 100% AS, the collected blood was equally divided into three 50 mL sterile centrifuge tubes without anticoagulant. The tubes were left standing in an upright position for 2 hours at room temperature (18°C–25°C) for complete clotting before centrifugation at 3000×g for 15 minutes at 20°C. The serum supernatant was removed and filtered through a 0.2 µm pore size membrane filter. Volumes of 1.5 mL were aliquoted into sterile opaque plastic dropper bottles, identical to those used for 100% APRP. Bottles were sealed and labelled with the patient’s name, hospital number and the date of production. With our preparation techniques, 108 mL of whole blood approximately yielded 45 mL of final blood products (30 bottles/patient). Patients were instructed to keep the bottle in use in the refrigerator at 4°C for 24 hours. A new bottle was opened every day. The remaining bottles were stored frozen at −20°C.

Undiluted solutions were used in this study based on previous evidence from a Cochrane review, which found inconsistency in possible benefits of 20% AS eye drops over artificial tears for DED.19 Additionally, our prior studies demonstrated higher success rates and shorter corneal re-epithelialisation time for 100% serum eye drops in patients with persistent corneal epithelial defect compared with conventional treatments.20 21

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were OSDI Score and ocular surface staining, evaluated using the Oxford Scheme at 2 and 4 weeks post-treatment. The OSDI Score comprised 12 questions of 3 domains, that is, ocular symptoms (5 questions), vision-related function (4 questions) and environmental triggers (3 questions). The overall OSDI Score ranged from 0 to 100 and classified as normal (0–12 points) or as having mild (13–22 points), moderate (23–32 points) and severe (33–100 points) disease.22 The Oxford Scheme classifies corneal and conjunctival fluorescein staining into six grades from 0 to 5 based on the number, density and area of stained dots, with higher grades indicating more severe disease.23

Secondary outcomes included FBUT, ST, meibum quality and expressibility, and adverse events at 4 weeks. FBUT was the time period from complete opening of the eyelid to the first tear break up. ST was measured using a strip of filter paper 35 mm long and 5 mm wide without anaesthesia at 5 min.24 Meibum quality was evaluated in eight glands of the central one-third of the lower lid using a scale of 0 to 3 for each gland, in which 0, 1, 2 and 3 referred to clear, cloudy, cloudy with debris (granular) and thick like toothpaste with a total score of 24. Meibum expressibility was graded according to the secretion observed in five glands: 0, all glands; 1 (3–4 glands); 2 (1–2 glands); and 3 (no glands).15 Improvement in meibum expressibility was defined if the expressibility grade changed from 2 or 3 to ≤1 after 4 weeks of treatment.

Sample size

The 100% APRP was considered non-inferior to 100% AS if the difference in OSDI scores did not exceed 20% of the mean OSDI Score in the AS eye drop group, estimated as 4.18 points by pooling data from relevant published RCTs.25,28 With a one-sided alpha error of 0.025 and 90% power, 46 patients per group (92 patients in total) were required to detect the difference in OSDI scores between the two interventions.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics were described by intervention groups using mean (SD) or median (IQR) for continuous variables, and frequency along with percentage for categorical/ordinal variables. These baseline covariates were compared between the two groups using independent t-test and χ2 test for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

The main analysis was performed based on an intention-to-treat approach, given that there was no evidence of protocol violation from non-adherence and loss to follow-up. Comparisons of mean continuous outcomes (ie, OSDI Score, ST, FBUT and meibum quality) between groups were analysed using linear mixed effect models by fitting each of these outcomes on treatments considering subjects as a random effect. Relative treatment effect along with 95% CI were estimated accordingly. 100% APRP was considered non-inferior to 100% AS if the OSDI upper limit mean difference was 4.18 points or lower as a prespecified margin for the sample size estimation. For dichotomous and ordinal outcomes of Oxford grading and meibum improvement, mixed effect (ordinal) logit models were applied by regression of the outcomes on treatment groups. OR along with 95% CIs were estimated accordingly. Finally, a Poisson regression was used to compare adverse events between treatment groups and relative risks (RR) and 95% CIs were estimated.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by adjusting for important baseline covariates including presence of SS, use of systemic medications related to DED and Oxford grade. All analyses were performed using STATA V.16 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). A p value<0.025 using one-sided test for primary outcomes and a p value<0.05 using two-sided test for baseline characteristics and secondary outcomes were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1418 DED patients visited the out-patient clinic and were screened for eligibility (figure 2). Of these, 118 patients were eligible for inclusion; 22 declined to participate, and the remaining 96 patients were enrolled (48 patients per group). 4 eyes from the 100% APRP group were excluded from the analyses due to a previous history of punctal occlusion, therefore 92 eyes from 48 patients were included in the 100% APRP group. Although two patients from the 100% APRP group were lost to follow-up due to COVID-19 infection at 2-week follow-up, both of them completed the study protocol and attended the 4th week visit. Table 1 provides patient baseline characteristics by treatment groups. All variables were evenly distributed between groups. Mean age (SD) was similar in both groups, that is, 54.9 (10.1) and 54.7 (10.5) years in the 100% APRP and AS groups, respectively. Most patients were women (95.8% in 100% APRP group vs 91.7% in 100% AS group). Baseline mean OSDI scores (SD) were 32 (13) and 31 (13) points in the 100% APRP and 100% AS groups, respectively. Severe ocular surface staining (Oxford grade 3–5) was slightly higher in the 100% APRP group than the 100% AS group (43.5% vs 32.3%), but this was not significant.

OSDI scores were calculated at baseline, 2 and 4 weeks post-treatment (figure 3A). At 4 weeks after treatment, OSDI scores (95% CI) decreased to 9.73 (7.84, 11.62) and 8.31 (6.43, 10.20) in the 100% APRP and 100% AS groups, respectively (table 2). Applying a mixed effect linear regression model, the mean difference of OSDI score between groups was 1.41 points (95% CI −1.26, 4.08), that is, the OSDI score was 1.41 higher in the 100% APRP group, although this was not significant. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for SS, use of DED-related systemic medications and Oxford grade at baseline showed a slightly lower mean OSDI Score difference of 1.38 (−1.29, 4.06) which still met the non-inferiority margin (online supplemental table 1). Overall, mean OSDI Score changes from baseline were −22.22 (−24.90, −19.55) in the 100% APRP group and −22.47 (−25.13, −19.80) in the 100% AS group. FBUT, ST without anaesthesia and meibum quality improved from baseline to 4 weeks after treatment in either group, although not statistically significant. Changes in FBUT and ST are shown in table 2 and online supplemental figure 1A,B. The 100% APRP group had slightly higher mean FBUT (95% CI) than the 100% AS group, that is, 4.04 (3.75, 4.33) vs 3.90 (3.61, 4.18) with a mean difference of 0.14 s (−0.27, 0.55) and an adjusted mean difference of 0.24 (−0.16, 0.63), but these were not significant. Mean ST in the 100% APRP group was also higher than that in the 100% AS group, that is, 8.06 (6.16, 9.95) vs 6.83 (4.94, 8.71) with a mean difference of 1.23 (−1.44, 3.91), but this was also not significant. Non-significant improvement in meibum quality tended to be greater in the 100% AS group compared with the 100% APRP group; however, meibum quality and expressibility post-treatment did not differ significantly between the two groups (p=0.067 and 0.795, respectively) (table 2).

The risk of adverse events was slightly higher in the 100% APRP group compared with the 100% AS group (0.44 (0.25, 0.62) vs 0.25 (0.11, 0.39)) with an RR of 1.75 (0.86, 3.56), but this was also not significant. Common side effects included eye irritation (23.9% in 100% APRP; 6.6% in 100% AS) and sticky sensation (13% in 100% APRP; 10.8% in 100% AS). No eye infection was observed. All patients were able to use the eye drops at least six times per day indicating good compliance.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that 100% APRP was not inferior to 100% AS in terms of reducing OSDI and Oxford scores in patients with moderate-to-severe DED either caused by SS or non-SS aetiologies. We also found that 100% APRP was comparable to 100% AS on all secondary outcomes, including FBUT, ST, meibum quality and expressibility, without a significant increase in reported adverse events.

Although APRP and AS share some certain properties, they differ in a few important ways. First, APRP contains concentrated platelets, which are known as a reservoir of various growth factors and proteins involved in tissue regeneration and wound healing, while AS does not have platelets since they are eliminated in the preparation process. Second, due to the physiologic activation of leukocytes during blood coagulation, AS contains proinflammatory cytokines released by the leucocytes, such as interleukin (IL) 6, IL-1β and tumour necrosis factor-α, more than APRP. These cytokines might be harmful to the ocular surface and lead to further damage, especially in patients suffering from immune-mediated DED. Third, transforming growth factor-β concentrations in AS are five times higher than in natural tears.18 Conversely, APRP is supposed to have lower TGF-β concentrations because there is no activation of the coagulation until APRP eye drops are instilled and platelet aggregation takes place on the ocular surface. Thus, AS is typically diluted to a 20% concentration to reduce the cytokine load and to match the TGF-β concentration in natural tears.4 18 However, dilution, in turn, decreases the concentration of other epitheliotrophic factors. On the other hand, PRP is not usually diluted prior to use,17 theoretically having higher epitheliotrophic factor concentrations and better potency in restoring the ocular surface health than AS.

Nonetheless, higher concentrations or undiluted AS prepared using the optimised protocol have been reported to be associated with very high success rates in treating several ocular surface diseases.20 21 27 The optimised protocol differed from other protocols in clotting time and centrifugation parameters. A longer clotting time (≥120 min) and centrifugation at higher g force for a longer duration have been shown to increase concentrations of some epitheliotrophic factors such as epidermal growth factor and hepatocyte growth factor, while significantly decreasing TGF-β1 concentration in AS. Hence, dilution was unnecessary, resulting in preserved concentrations of epitheliotrophic factors.8

To the best of our knowledge, this is a large RCT to compare the efficacy and safety of 100% APRP and 100% AS in DED patients. We found that both biological tear substitutes significantly reduced OSDI scores and Oxford grades after 4-week treatments. The positive effects for both outcomes were slightly lower in patients treated with 100% APRP compared with those treated with 100% AS, but these were neither statistically significant nor clinically inferior according to the minimal clinically important difference for the OSDI Score of 7 points.22 The dramatic improvement was observed in the first 2 weeks of treatment and then slowed down towards the end of the 4-week treatment period as shown in figure 3. This supports previous clinical trial findings by García-Conca et al29 who reported greater reductions in dry eye symptoms and signs 15 days of PRP treatment. In addition, our findings are consistent with the results of two recent small RCTs, which reported no significant differences in beneficial effects between 20% APRP and 20% AS eye drops in patients with primary SS DED10 and non-SS DED.11 Nevertheless, both studies demonstrated that mean OSDI reductions for both 20% APRP and 20% AS were less than 10 points after a 4-week treatment, whereas our study observed mean changes in OSDI scores of 22.22 points in the 100% APRP group and 22.47 points in the 100% AS group. This suggested that more concentrated biological tear substitutes (100%) could provide better or faster improvements in dry eye symptoms given the same treatment duration.

Conversely, in another prospective, non-randomised trial, although there were no significant differences in the improvements in dry eye signs measured between the 100% APRP and 100% AS groups in primary SS DED patients, the mean changes in OSDI scores were significantly greater in the 100% APRP group compared with the 100% AS group.12 This might be explained by the different preparation protocols used in their study and ours. In our study, in addition to employing the optimised protocol for 100% AS production, 100% APRP was produced by centrifugation at 20°C, instead of 5°C which was the temperature suggested by the original protocol due to limited equipment availability at our laboratory.7 As lower temperatures appear to prolong platelet viability,30 centrifugation at 20°C probably reduced platelet viability and consequently decreased the amounts of growth factors in APRP. Therefore, both treatments were found to be equivalent at relieving symptoms in this study.

For secondary outcomes assessed, there was a slightly increased trend in FBUT and ST without anaesthesia post-treatment, although neither differed significantly between the two groups. Our findings were similar to those of the recent RCT, which showed slight improvements in FBUT and ST from baseline following treatment with 20% APRP and 20% AS.11 Additionally, although a vast majority of dry eye patients demonstrate MGD, there has been only one study in the literature, evaluating the effects of blood-derived eye drops for MGD. It was a retrospective case series, revealed that 25% of eyes experienced an improvement in meibomian gland dropout in meibography, all by one grade, although not significantly, after 4 weeks of 100% APRP treatment.31 Our study represents the first RCT investigating the effects of biological tears on meibomian glands in dry eye patients. After 4-week treatment, we observed similar non-significant improvements in meibum quality from baseline in the 100% APRP and 100% AS groups, but no significant differences in meibum expressibility were detected within each group and between the two groups. The lack of differences might be attributed to the mild degree of MGD in the participants in both groups reflected by the low meibum parameter values at baseline, limiting the potential for significant changes over the short treatment period.

For the adverse events, the incidence of eye irritation appeared to be higher in the 100% APRP group (23.9%) compared with 100% AS group (6.6%). The unexpected trend might be attributed to the sterilising filtration step added to the AS preparation protocol. This step generally aims at removing any corpuscular blood components, fibrin strands and bacteria or fungi that might accidentally be suspended in the serum. It may also help to decrease unwanted side effects of AS. Nevertheless, the reduction of side effects might be offset by increased time and cost required for the AS eye drops preparation (figure 1).

There were some limitations to our approach. First, this study evaluated short-term outcomes at 4 weeks. Long-term studies are needed to comprehensively assess the effects of both treatments in all aspects. Second, the standard 48-hour washout period might be inadequate to ensure that there were no residual treatment effects of previously administered medications when the patients entered the trial. However, because all participants had moderate-to-severe DED, stopping their ongoing medications for a longer period of time might not be practical. Third, 8.3% of patients had pre-existing glaucoma, requiring topical antiglaucoma therapy. This could be a substantial factor negatively influencing Oxford grading, compromising the reliability of the results to a certain degree. At last, a composition analysis was not performed. Thus, the differences in the concentrations of epitheliotrophic factors between both eye drops as well as the relationship between the concentrations of epitheliotrophic factors and clinical outcomes could not be elaborated and warrant further investigation.

In summary, the evidence from our RCT indicates that 100% APRP prepared from a single-spin centrifugation method is not inferior to 100% AS in reducing dry eye symptoms and ocular surface staining for patients with moderate-to-severe DED. Each biological tear substitute considerably reduced the OSDI scores, especially within the first 2 weeks of treatment, without notable adverse effects. As such, 100% APRP could be considered as an effective alternative to 100% AS because it entails a simpler and less time-consuming preparation process.
