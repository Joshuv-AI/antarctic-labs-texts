Artificial intelligence model based on the detection of key anatomical structures for identifying standard planes on ultrasound of the first-trimester fetal central nervous system: a multicenter study
Authors: Qingyu Zheng, Xiaoqian Zhang, Liya Li, Yanyun Du, Pan Zeng, Shaozheng He, Guorong Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrenatal ultrasound examination at 11+0 to 13+6 weeks’ gestation can detect approximately 33–50% of major fetal structural abnormalities. Despite recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) for fetal ultrasound, its application in examining the first-trimester fetal central nervous system (FCNS) remains limited. This study aimed to develop, validate, and determine the clinical value of an AI FCNS model (FCNS-Model) based on the detection of key anatomical structures with the purpose of identifying FCNS ultrasound standard planes (FCNS-USPs).MethodsThis study included 2,901 FCNS-USPs images from three hospitals, which were split into a training set (2,124 images), a test set (264 images), an internal validation set (254 images), and a clinical validation set (259 images). Additionally, an independent external validation set (300 images) was formed from images acquired at a fourth hospital. The performance of junior, intermediate, and senior physicians, as well as that of the FCNS-Model, was compared against the standard of an expert ultrasound team.ResultsIn the test set, the model achieved precision, recall, and F1 scores above 92%. For the internal validation set, classification accuracy for each plane was 99.3–100%, with kappa values >0.90, indicating strong agreement with the expert team. In the clinical validation set, FCNS-Model achieved significantly higher areas under the curve (AUCs) than did the senior physician for the anterior-posterior midsagittal plane (0.984 vs. 0.906; P<0.001), the transverse plane through the thalamus (0.982 vs. 0.938; P=0.003), and the coronal plane through the frontal lobe (0.915 vs. 0.852; P=0.03). For the transverse plane of the lateral ventricle, the FCNS-Model’s AUC of 0.979 was slightly higher than that of the junior physician (AUC =0.971; P=0.042). Furthermore, McNemar testing demonstrated that the FCNS-Model achieved significantly higher sensitivity than did the junior physician in identifying FCNS-USPs (P=0.02). In the external validation set, the model maintained stable performance despite a moderate decrease, supporting its generalizability.ConclusionsFCNS-Model accurately identifies FCNS-USPs and is an effective tool for assisting ultrasound physicians in obtaining standard planes and identifying key anatomical structures, particularly in settings with varying levels of operator experience.Keywords: Fetal ultrasound in the first trimester, central nervous system, deep learning, standard plane, critical anatomical structure

Introduction

Fetal cranial abnormalities are among the most common congenital malformations, and ultrasound is the primary method for diagnosing fetal central nervous system (FCNS) abnormalities (1,2). Although prenatal screening is typically performed during the second or third trimester, many cranial anomalies actually develop during the first trimester (2). Certain ultrasound findings are considered potential indicators of anatomical abnormalities in the fetus and suggestive of latent risks (3,4).

However, due to the small size and developmental immaturity of the FCNS in the first trimester, its comprehensive identification is challenging, making thorough ultrasound assessment difficult. Obtaining standard ultrasound planes is a prerequisite for accurate diagnosis. Research (5) has shown that the sensitivity of diagnosing lethal FCNS malformations in the first trimester through standard ultrasound planes is 85.7%, with a specificity of 100%, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 100%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 99.9%. Accurate identification of standard planes and key anatomical structures is the foundation of fetal malformation diagnosis. However, acquiring standard planes is not only time-consuming and labor-intensive, but it also heavily depends on the clinical experience of the ultrasound operator. The application of artificial intelligence (AI) models in medical imaging is becoming increasingly widespread, particularly in the recognition of standard ultrasound planes (6-11). Through deep learning algorithms, AI models can achieve high recognition accuracy, thereby reducing interobserver variability and minimizing reliance on clinical experience. This benefit is particularly valuable for novice operators, as AI can serve as an auxiliary tool for the accurate acquisition of standard planes and the identification of key anatomical structures, thereby improving diagnostic effectiveness (12,13).

Although AI has shown considerable potential for automated plane recognition, image quality assessment, and malformation detection in the second and third trimesters (14-17), its application to first-trimester screening remains largely unexplored. Therefore, in this study, we developed a deep learning AI FCNS model (FCNS-Model) based on the detection of key anatomical structures. We evaluated its accuracy and efficiency in recognizing FCNS ultrasound standard planes (FCNS-USPs). Furthermore, we compared the model’s performance against physicians with varying levels of expertise to assess its potential for clinical application. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-1-2830/rc).

Methods

Research object

A core group of eight ultrasound experts from prenatal diagnosis and screening centers, university-affiliated hospitals, and regional hospitals developed a draft of the Chinese Expert Consensus on Fetal Ultrasound Anatomy for 11+0 to 13+6 weeks of gestation. Following the Delphi method (18,19), this draft was distributed to 122 prenatal ultrasound experts across China to gather multicenter feedback. After the experts’ suggestions were incorporated, the consensus was revised and finalized through three rounds of voting. The final consensus included 11 key ultrasound views for targeted examination of the FCNS. Among these, the transverse plane of the lateral ventricle (T-LV), the transverse plane through the thalamus (T-Th), the anterior-posterior midsagittal plane (AP-MSP), and the coronal plane through the frontal lobe (C-FL) were recommended as standard planes, while the other seven planes were considered optional (20-22).

Based on the Delphi survey results, this study selected four recommended standard planes (T-LV, T-Th, AP-MSP, and C-FL) as the standard ultrasound planes to be recognized by FCNS-Model. The collected images were randomly partitioned into a model construction set (for training and testing) and a clinical validation set. The validation set was used to evaluate and compare the FCNS-Model with physicians of varying levels of experience in their ability to identify first-trimester FCNS-USPs. All images were obtained from the ultrasound workstations of three hospitals: The Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, Anxi County Maternal and Child Health Hospital, and Quanzhou Children’s Hospital. Six types of color Doppler ultrasound diagnostic instruments, including the Resona 7, Resona 8, Resona I9S (Mindray, Shenzhen, China), Voluson E6, Voluson E8, and Voluson E10 (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) (with convex array probes at a frequency of 3.5–5.0 MHz), were used to perform the first-trimester fetal ultrasound scans and store the images.

The inclusion criteria for images were as follows: (I) a clear image, with the target structure occupying one-half to two-thirds of the image’s center, and a clean background without artifacts; (II) no superimposed color Doppler images, measurement calipers, or text annotations; and (III) postpartum confirmation of the fetus showing no facial or other structural abnormalities. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria for images were as follows: (I) images with blurring or trailing due to maternal obesity, image shaking, or unclear display of the target structure; and (II) ultrasound or postpartum confirmation of fetal abnormalities.

The image standards for the FCNS standard planes were as follows: (I) T-LV, display of the complete skull, with a continuous midline of the brain, symmetrical lateral ventricles on both sides, and the choroid plexus; (II) AP-MSP, display of a round anechoic diencephalon, an “S”-shaped brainstem, an anechoic fourth ventricle posterior to the brainstem, the anechoic posterior cranial fossa, and the nuchal translucency layer; (III) T-Th, display of the anterior third of the brainstem midline, symmetrical thalami on both sides, and a complete tectum structure (including the aqueduct of Sylvius and the anechoic midbrain tectum on both sides); (IV) C-FL, display of the midline of the brain, symmetrical lateral ventricles and choroid plexus on both sides, and symmetrical orbits (Figure 1).

To ensure the high quality of the training data, three ultrasound physicians with 5 years of experience in prenatal ultrasound diagnosis (Annotators A, B, and C) manually annotated the selected FCNS-USP images. Subsequently, two ultrasound physicians with 15 years of experience (Reviewers D and E) conducted the first round of review and correction for all annotated results. For samples with remaining discrepancies, a second round of re-evaluation and validation was performed. The labels that were unanimously agreed upon were used to train the first iteration of FCNS-Model.

For the external validation set, 300 images were independently collected from an additional institution (Nan’an Hospital Affiliated to Quanzhou Medical College) that was not involved in model development or internal validation. To promote data heterogeneity, ultrasound examinations were conducted with six different types of color Doppler ultrasound systems: the HERA W10, RS6 Pro (Samsung, Suwon, Korea), ACUSON Redwood, Sequoia 512 (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), Nuewa A20 (Mindray), and LOGIQ Fortis (GE HealthCare) (equipped with convex array probes at a frequency of 1.0–9.0 MHz). The same inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied, and image evaluation was conducted with the same standardized protocol as those of the internal dataset to ensure consistency and comparability. All images were annotated and reviewed according to the same multistep consensus process to minimize labeling bias. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (No. 2024394). Informed consent was obtained from all pregnant women or their families. Anxi County Maternal and Child Health Hospital, Quanzhou Children’s Hospital, and Nan’an Hospital Affiliated to Quanzhou Medical College also agreed to and approved the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Instruments

In this study, we developed an offline, two-stage deep learning model, FCNS-Model, specifically tailored for accurate recognition of first-trimester FCNS-USPs. FCNS-Model builds upon and significantly enhances the baseline You Only Look Once (YOLO) detection framework by integrating advanced attention mechanisms and sequential feature-encoding techniques. Specifically, the model incorporates the convolutional block attention module (CBAM) (23) and a gated recurrent unit (GRU) (24,25), markedly improving clinical performance and reliability in identifying anatomical structures within ultrasound images.

The CBAM attention mechanism consists of two sequential submodules: the channel attention module, which captures the relationships between different feature channels through global pooling operations and fully connected layers; and the spatial attention module, which emphasizes informative regions by generating a spatial attention map using convolutional layers based on pooled channel descriptors. By integrating CBAM into the YOLO detection framework, FCNS-Model effectively highlights relevant anatomical structures, allowing the model to better focus on critical features and suppress irrelevant background information and noise. This significantly enhances FCNS-Model’s capability to accurately detect and identify anatomical structures in challenging ultrasound imaging conditions.

Following the initial precise anatomical structure detection by the YOLO framework, the GRU encodes these detection results into a comprehensive and unified feature vector. The GRU effectively captures sequential and spatial relationships between detected anatomical structures, ensuring a deeper understanding of anatomical context within each ultrasound image. This encoded vector is then combined with visual features extracted by a vision transformer (ViT)-based classification model (26). This integration allows FCNS-Model to simultaneously leverage both detailed structural information and overall image characteristics, thereby significantly boosting interpretative accuracy.

In summary, the core approach of the model design consists of a YOLOv8 framework enhanced with CBAM for the accurate detection of key anatomical structures. These detected structures are subsequently encoded with a GRU to generate a representative feature vector. This vector is then integrated into a ViT-based classification model, which thus forms a multitask framework capable of both precise anatomical structure detection and accurate classification of FCNS-USPs. Consequently, our method significantly enhances performance and accuracy in clinical FCNS ultrasound assessments.

To achieve the recognition of key anatomical structures in the four ultrasound planes, the expert ultrasound team marked the key structures in each plane with high precision. Based on the annotated key anatomical structures, a scoring system was applied to automatically determine whether the plane met the criteria for a standard plane, thereby enabling classification and recognition of the first-trimester FCNS-USPs. After model construction, images from the clinical validation set were independently analyzed by three types of physicians: a junior physician (JP) who had completed standardized residency training, an intermediate physician (IP) with more than 6 months of specialized training in first-trimester fetal ultrasound, and a senior physician (SP) with over 15 years of experience in prenatal ultrasound diagnosis and a certified qualification in prenatal diagnosis. Additionally, FCNS-Model was used to independently identify the images. The expert team consisted of three physicians, each with more than 25 years of experience in prenatal ultrasound and specialized training in the screening of first-trimester fetal anatomical structures.

To assess the effectiveness of FCNS-Model in identifying key anatomical structures, the JP, IP, SP, and the model were each compared to the expert team in terms of their ability to recognize the standard planes.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of this study was performed with SPSS 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.1.2 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) software. Continuous data are expressed as means and were compared with t tests. Categorical data are expressed as frequencies or percentages and were compared with the χ2 test. The Cohen kappa coefficient was used to determine the consistency of the results from the JP, IP, SP, and FCNS-Model with those of the expert team. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted with the expert team’s identification serving as the reference standard to assess the performance of the JP, IP, SP, and FCNS-Model in recognizing standard planes and key anatomical structures. The area under the curve (AUC) was compared via the DeLong test, with a P value <0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. In addition, the McNemar test was used to evaluate the paired differences in classification performance between FCNS-Model and the JP; in particular, it was used to assess discrepancies in correctly identifying standard and nonstandard planes.

This study used several evaluation metrics to assess the model’s performance in plane recognition and anatomical structure detection tasks based on first-trimester fetal ultrasound, with the expert team’s results serving as the reference standard. These metrics included precision, recall, F1 score, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, PPV, NPV, kappa value, AUC and mean average precision at an intersection over union threshold of 0.5 (mAP@0.5).

Precision or PPV measures the proportion of correctly identified positive samples among all samples predicted as positive (i.e., the accuracy of the model in recognizing positive samples). A higher precision indicates fewer false positives. Recall or sensitivity measures the proportion of actual positive samples that are correctly identified by a model (i.e., the ability of a model to recognize all positive samples). A higher recall indicates fewer false negatives. Combining precision and recall allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of FCNS-Model’s performance in different tasks, with precision reflecting the number of false positives (incorrect prediction of negative samples as positive) and recall reflecting the number of false negatives (the missing of actual positive samples). F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, reflecting a balanced measure between them. Its use can help indicate if the model is ignoring true-positive samples (if precision is too high) or overpredicting positive samples (if recall is too high). Specificity assesses a model’s ability to correctly identify negative samples (nontarget planes). It measures the proportion of true negative samples correctly identified. In our study, higher specificity indicated better differentiation between target and nontarget planes and a reduced risk of misidentification. Accuracy is an important metric for evaluating the overall performance of a model. It reflects the proportion of correctly classified positive and negative samples out of all samples. NPV measures the proportion of samples predicted as negative that are truly negative. It reflects the reliability of negative predictions. A higher NPV indicates that when the model classifies a sample as a nontarget plane or the absence of a target anatomical structure, the prediction is more likely to be accurate, reducing the risk of overlooking incorrect classifications. However, in cases of imbalanced datasets, accuracy alone may not fully capture a model’s performance. In this study, the kappa value was used to measure the consistency between the model’s predictions and the expert team’s assessments. Adjusted for random agreement, it provided a quantitative indicator of actual agreement. A higher Kappa value signified a stronger alignment between the model’s results and the expert’s judgments. The AUC of the ROC curve is a comprehensive indicator of a model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative samples. In our study, the closer the AUC value was to 1, the stronger the model’s ability to distinguish between target and nontarget planes. AUC is particularly useful for evaluating the overall performance of a model across different thresholds.

mAP@0.5 is a comprehensive metric for evaluating object detection performance. It reflects the model’s overall ability to correctly identify and localize target anatomical structures across different categories. In our study, a higher mAP@0.5 value indicated superior detection performance and more accurate localization of FCNS anatomical structures.

The independent external validation dataset included 300 images collected from Nan’an Hospital Affiliated to Quanzhou Medical College, which was not involved in model development. Except for the T-LV, the FCNS-Model achieved significantly higher AUCs than did the SP for the AP-MSP (0.984 vs. 0.906; P<0.001), T-Th (0.982 vs. 0.938; P=0.003), and C-FL (0.915 vs. 0.852; P=0.03). For the T-LV, the FCNS-Model’s AUC of 0.979 was slightly higher than that of the JP (AUC =0.971; P=0.042). Therefore, we further evaluated paired classification outcomes using the McNemar test to better understand the practical implications. Importantly, in correctly identifying positive outcomes for FCNS-USPs, FCNS-Model demonstrated a significantly higher sensitivity than did the JP (McNemar’s exact test, P=0.02), indicating a reduced rate of misrecognizing standard planes. In contrast, no significant difference was observed for negative outcomes (McNemar exact test, P=0.63), suggesting comparable specificity.

In standard plane recognition, AUC values generally increased progressively from JP to the IP and then to the SP, except for the C-FL plane, for which the JP achieved a higher AUC than the IP. Most pairwise comparisons of AUC values between the JP, IP, and SP demonstrated statistically significant differences (P<0.05).

External validation

To further evaluate the generalizability of FCNS-Model, an independent external validation dataset was introduced (Table S1). Compared with its performance in the internal and clinical validation results, the model performed moderately worse in the external validation dataset, which is expected when a model is applied to data from a different institution. Nonetheless, FCNS-Model maintained good diagnostic performance across all four standard planes, with AUCs of 0.958, 0.920, 0.924, and 0.865 for the T-LV, AP-MSP, T-Th, and C-FL, respectively. The model consistently outperformed the JP and showed performance comparable to or better than that of the IP across the four planes. Compared with the SP, FCNS-Model achieved higher AUCs for the AP-MSP and T-Th but lower AUCs for the T-LV and C-FL. Notably, for the more challenging planes (AP-MSP and T-Th), FCNS-Model demonstrated higher sensitivity than both the JP and IP, indicating a reduced risk of missing standard planes.

Overall, although a decline in performance was observed, FCNS-Model maintained a stable and clinically acceptable performance, which supports its generalizability and suggests a low risk of overfitting.

In detecting anatomical structures within first-trimester FCNS-USPs, FCNS-Model demonstrated superior performance, achieving a precision of 94.3%, a recall of 93.9%, and a mAP@0.5 of 93.8%, consistently outperforming all comparative YOLO variants. Notably, the differences between FCNS-Model and the best-performing YOLO variants were statistically significant, specifically for precision (P=0.02 vs. YOLOv9-m), recall (P=0.02 vs. YOLOv8-m), and mAP@0.5 (P=0.044 vs. YOLOv8-m). These findings highlight the robustness of FCNS-Model in detecting key anatomical structures in first-trimester FCNS-USPs.

Computational cost and inference efficiency

We evaluated the computational efficiency of the proposed FCNS-Model on a workstation equipped with a Core i7-14700KF CPU (Intel Corp., Santa Clara, CA, USA) with 64 GB of RAM and a GeForce RTX 3090 GPU (Nvidia, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with 24 GB of VRAM. The inference time was measured using 197 valid ultrasound images, with the first 10 images excluded as warm-up samples. Therefore, the final results were based on 187 images.

Under the final model configuration, the average end-to-end processing time of the complete pipeline was 41.39 ms per image, corresponding to approximately 24.16 frames per second (FPS). Specifically, the classification branch required 7.06 ms per image on average, including image preprocessing, model inference, and postprocessing. The YOLO-based anatomical structure recognition branch required 32.17 ms per image on average. The rule-based scoring module introduced negligible computational overhead, with an average processing time of only 0.0095 ms per image.

These results indicate that the proposed pipeline can achieve near real-time inference on a standard GPU workstation. Although the pipeline integrates multiple modules, including image classification, YOLO-based anatomical structure recognition, and rule-based scoring, its computational cost remains acceptable for practical use. The bulk of computational demand is from the YOLO-based anatomical structure recognition branch. In future clinical deployment, further acceleration strategies, such as TensorRT optimization, model pruning, lightweight backbone replacement, and deployment-specific inference scheduling, may further improve the efficiency and real-time applicability of the system.

Discussion

Ultrasound is a radiation-free, simple, and convenient imaging method that is commonly used for prenatal examinations during pregnancy (27,28). Prenatal screening for fetal anatomical structures mainly focuses on the midpregnancy stage, but first-trimester fetal anatomical screening offers significant advantages for clinical management. The early detection of abnormalities provides more time for genetic testing, further screening, and the development of treatment plans for the fetus (20). With the advancements in ultrasound equipment and scanning technology, along with a deeper clinical understanding of the first-trimester fetal anatomy on ultrasound, first-trimester fetal anatomical screening has garnered heightened research interest (29-31). However, despite its advantages, challenges remain in clinical practice. In their review, Karim et al. (32) recommended that a comprehensive protocol for standard anatomical planes be developed and adopted to optimize the detection rate in first-trimester ultrasound screening. However, in clinical practice, challenges remain for physicians in acquiring standard planes and identifying key anatomical structures due to factors such as maternal abdominal fat, fetal positioning, and the resolution of ultrasound machines.

Deep learning has shown considerable potential in fetal ultrasound imaging; however, most of the related studies have focused on the second or third trimester. Baumgartner et al. (33) used a convolutional neural network (CNN) to localize 13 standard fetal planes in ultrasound videos, while Qu et al. (34) proposed a differential CNN to identify six fetal cranial planes between 16 and 34 weeks of gestation, achieving an accuracy of 92.93%. In contrast, first-trimester screening remains more challenging due to the less distinct anatomical features and greater interexamination variability (35). Similar to our work, Lin et al. (36) developed a framework for identifying the standard midsagittal plane in early pregnancy based on expert annotations.

Building upon our previous experience in AI across related domains (37), we developed an innovative deep learning model (FCNS-Model) for accurate identification of first-trimester FCNS-USPs. By incorporating a CBAM-enhanced YOLOv8 detection framework, GRU-based anatomical feature encoding, and ViT-based image classification, FCNS-Model achieved improved anatomical structure detection and standard-plane recognition.

In this study, we further extended this framework to a more challenging task: the evaluation of the FCNS in the first trimester. Despite the increased difficulty of fetal cranial imaging at this stage, FCNS-Model achieved a performance comparable to that of models developed for the second or third trimester. The model demonstrated robust overall performance, with recall, precision, and F1 scores all exceeding 92%. In both the internal and clinical validation sets, it achieved an accuracy above 92% and kappa values above 0.85. Overall, FCNS-Model performed comparably to experienced physicians, although its relative performance varied across standard planes; notably, it outperformed the SP for some planes. In addition, it consistently outperformed other comparison models across key metrics.

Although the difference in AUC between FCNS-Model (0.979) and the JP (0.971) was statistically significant, the clinical relevance of the model may appear limited if this metric is considered in isolation. Therefore, we further evaluated paired classification outcomes using the McNemar test to better ascertain the practical implications. Importantly, for positive outcomes identified correctly on standard fetal cranial ultrasound planes, FCNS-Model demonstrated significantly higher sensitivity than did the JP (McNemar exact test, P=0.02), indicating a reduced rate of missed standard planes. In contrast, no significant difference was observed for negative outcomes (McNemar exact test, P=0.63), suggesting comparable specificity. This finding is clinically meaningful, as the primary goal of fetal ultrasound screening is to ensure accurate acquisition and identification of standard planes (38). Missing a standard plane may lead to incomplete or inadequate evaluation of the fetal anatomy. Therefore, even modest improvements in sensitivity can translate into meaningful clinical benefit.

Beyond these performance metrics, the value of FCNS-Model also lies in its consistency. Unlike human operators, whose performance may vary with experience, fatigue, or training background, the model provides stable and reproducible results. This consistency can help standardize ultrasound examinations across different clinical settings and operators, which would especially benefit less experienced physicians. Overall, FCNS-Model offers clinically relevant advantages by reducing the number of missed standard planes and improving consistency, thereby enhancing the reliability and quality of fetal ultrasound screening.

To assess the interpretability of FCNS-Model, we employed the gradient-weighted class activation mapping (Grad-CAM) technique to generate activation maps of the ViT classifier on the four FCNS-USPs (39). Overall, these activation maps clearly reveal the key anatomical regions the model focuses on, which closely align with the landmarks clinicians rely on for plane identification, indicating that the model’s decisions are highly interpretable.

As shown in Figure 3, the Grad-CAM heatmaps on the four standard planes highlight the model’s focus on diagnostically relevant regions. In the T-LV, activations concentrate on the bilateral lateral ventricles, the brain midline, and bilateral choroid plexus. In the T-Th, the thalamus, intact midbrain tectum, and bilateral choroid plexus are emphasized. The AP-MSP heatmap indicates a focus on the nasal bone, diencephalon, fourth ventricle, posterior cranial fossa, and nuchal translucency. Finally, in the C-FL, strong activations appear around the bilateral orbits, brain midline, bilateral choroid plexus, and lateral ventricles. These focused patterns confirm that our CBAM-enhanced YOLO detection and GRU-encoded ViT classification stages consistently leverage the same anatomical landmarks used by clinicians, thus validating the clinical interpretability of the two-stage design.

The clinical importance of the four standard planes investigated in this study is well-established by expert consensus and previous research, as they serve as the gateway to detecting 33–50% of major structural anomalies during early pregnancy (20). Targeted FCNS examination allows for the detection of specific abnormalities in each plane. These specifically include the following: anencephaly, encephalocele, hydrocephalus, and choroid plexus cysts in the lateral ventricle transverse plane; open spina bifida, Chiari II malformation, and Dandy-Walker malformation in the median sagittal plane; holoprosencephaly, hydrocephalus, and midbrain malformations in the transverse plane at the thalamic level; and holoprosencephaly and ocular and lens malformations in the coronal plane (40-47). These abnormalities cover almost all major severe central nervous system malformations in the first trimester.

When internal validation metrics such as accuracy, precision, and recall are high, this may indicate overfitting. Overfitting occurs when a model performs exceedingly well on the training and validation sets but fails to generalize to unseen data.

In this study, FCNS-Model achieved exceptional performance, with the internal validation accuracy reaching 99.3–100% and kappa values being over 0.90. This high level of performance may suggest that the model is very well-suited to the specific patterns within the internal validation dataset, which may represent overfitting to those exact patterns and not the learning of more generalized features. The model’s strong performance on the internal set could lead to a situation in which it might not perform as well on external, unseen data or under real-world clinical conditions, where variations in imaging data (e.g., different ultrasound machines and maternal factors) might occur. This concern is further supported by the inherent limitations of the dataset. The dataset in this study was derived from three centers and involved a limited range of ultrasound devices, which may constrain the generalizability of the model in other clinical settings. Ultrasound imaging is inherently operator-dependent, and image acquisition is influenced by multiple factors, including operator experience and scanning technique, leading to considerable variability (48). In addition, differences in patient populations and clinical practices across regions may further exacerbate inconsistencies in data distribution (49). From a technical perspective, variations among ultrasound systems, such as probe parameters, image resolution, and postprocessing algorithms can significantly affect image quality (50). This intrinsic variability, together with interdevice differences, represents a major challenge for the stable application of AI models in ultrasound imaging. To address these issues, we incorporated an independent external validation cohort and expanded the diversity of imaging data by including six additional ultrasound devices. The results demonstrated that the model maintained strong performance across this external dataset, although a slight decline compared with internal validation was observed, supporting its potential applicability in broader clinical environments.

Despite the promising findings, this study involved certain limitations that should be acknowledged. First, FCNS-Model operates in an offline manner and has not yet been integrated into real-time ultrasound scanning workflows, which may limit its immediate clinical applicability. Second, although an independent external validation cohort and multiple ultrasound devices were included, the regional distribution and range of equipment remained limited. Therefore, caution is warranted in deploying the model in clinical settings with substantially different imaging protocols or equipment conditions (51). Third, the model focuses primarily on standard-plane recognition and is not yet capable of structural abnormality detection. In future work, we plan to develop an end-to-end real-time model incorporating abnormality detection and to prospectively conduct large-scale, multicenter testing in order to improve applicability in routine clinical practice.

Finally, several ethical considerations should be noted in the implementation of AI in prenatal screening. First, AI predictions are intended to function as decision-support tools, not standalone diagnostic instruments. Second, strict compliance with data privacy and security standards is essential given the sensitivity of maternal and fetal data. Third, transparent communication of results accompanied by genetic counseling and psychological support is recommended to mitigate potential distress among expectant parents.

Conclusions

The proposed FCNS-Model demonstrated robust performance in recognizing first-trimester FCNS-USPs and key anatomical structures across multicenter datasets. It has the potential to serve as a practical decision-support tool for improving the standardization and consistency of ultrasound examination of the first-trimester FCNS, particularly for less experienced operators.
