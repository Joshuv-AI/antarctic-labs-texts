Status and correlates of body dysmorphic disorder symptoms among school-aged female adolescents in Jordan
Authors: Ruba S Hejjeh, Shaher H Hamaideh, Lobna Harazneh, Ahmad M Rayani, Ayman M Hamdan-Mansour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescence is a developmental stage characterized by significant biological, psychological, and social changes. Evidence suggests that nearly half of all mental disorders, including body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), emerge around the age of 14. However, little is known about BDD among Jordanian adolescent girls. Understanding its status and correlates in this population may inform earlyThe authors of this study extend their detection and intervention strategies.MethodsA cross-sectional, descriptive–correlational design was used. Convenience sampling recruited 402 school-aged adolescent girls aged 12–18 years from the northern, central, and southern regions of Jordan. BDD symptoms were assessed using the validated Body Dysmorphic Disorder–Symptoms Scale. Descriptive and inferential statistics were employed to identify symptom prevalence and associated factors.ResultsParticipants reported overall low BDD symptom levels. Introduction

Adolescence is a critical developmental period characterized by profound biological, psychological, and social transitions. The World Health Organization emphasizes that emotional, physical, and social changes during adolescence, combined with exposure to stressors such as abuse and peer victimization, may increase vulnerability to mental health disorders [1]. Epidemiological evidence suggests that nearly half of all mental disorders begin by the age of 14 and are often preceded by nonspecific psychosocial difficulties [2, 3]. Among the psychiatric conditions that may emerge during this stage is Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD).

BDD is defined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR) as a preoccupation with one or more perceived defects in physical appearance that are either unobservable or appear slight to others, accompanied by repetitive behaviors or mental acts and significant functional impairment [4, 5]. These preoccupations are typically time-consuming, difficult to control, and associated with marked distress. Adolescents, particularly females, may be especially vulnerable to BDD symptoms because physical appearance plays a central role in identity formation and social acceptance during this developmental stage [4].

From a psychological perspective, several theoretical frameworks help explain the development and persistence of BDD symptoms. Cognitive-behavioral models emphasize maladaptive beliefs and cognitive distortions, such as selective attention to perceived flaws and magnification of minor imperfections [6, 7]. Perfectionism has also been identified as a key vulnerability factor, characterized by unrealistically high appearance standards and excessive self-criticism [8]. Sociocultural and social comparison theories further highlight the influence of peer evaluation, internalization of cultural beauty ideals, and exposure to appearance-focused media [9]. Neurobiological perspectives suggest that BDD shares features with anxiety and obsessive-compulsive spectrum disorders, including intrusive thoughts and compulsive behaviors, indicating overlapping psychopathological mechanisms [10, 11]. Emerging constructs, such as charismaphobia—the fear of being or becoming unattractive—have also been proposed to explain contemporary manifestations of appearance-related anxiety in increasingly image-driven digital environments [12].

Puberty introduces rapid hormonal changes, including increases in estrogen among females, which may interact with psychosocial stressors to heighten vulnerability to emotional disturbances [1, 6]. Beauty is an important pillar for women, and physical charms are considered their primary social responsibility. In many societies, particularly collectivist cultures, physical appearance is closely linked to social identity, perceived femininity, and interpersonal value. Cultural norms strongly influence beauty standards, and in Middle Eastern contexts, idealized facial features—such as symmetrical faces, large eyes, and clear skin—are often emphasized [7]. For many Muslim adolescent girls, the wearing of the hijab may further shift attention toward facial appearance, potentially intensifying concerns related to visible features [7]. However, whether these cultural and religious norms increase or mitigate vulnerability to BDD symptoms remains insufficiently explored. Global variations in female beauty standards are strongly influenced by cultural, religious, and family norms, including those prevalent in many Muslim communities. This study enables comparisons of how such differences may shape the prevalence of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) and the factors associated with it. In many Muslim contexts, the body is viewed as a trust from God, a belief that can provide comfort but may also discourage open discussion of severe appearance-related distress due to feelings of shame. At the same time, increasing exposure to idealized images through social media and global beauty standards may create internal conflict, particularly among women who wear the hijab (head and body covering). Globally, BDD prevalence has been reported to range from 5 to 10% in cosmetic dentistry patients, approximately 20% among individuals seeking rhinoplasty, and 13–15% among general cosmetic surgery patients [8]. Among non-clinical populations, prevalence estimates vary considerably: 1.7% among university students in the United States [13], 4.4% among female medical students in Saudi Arabia [8], 14.1% among Arab dermatology patients [9], and 20.3% among university students in Jordan [14]. Despite growing recognition of BDD in adult and clinical samples, data on school-aged adolescents—particularly within Middle Eastern societies—remain limited.

Adolescent BDD symptoms have been associated with significant functional impairment, including academic difficulties, poor concentration, school absenteeism, and social withdrawal [10, 11]. Many adolescents conceal appearance-related distress due to embarrassment or fear of stigma, which may contribute to underdiagnosis or misclassification as social anxiety or depressive disorders [12, 13]. Poor insight, excessive reassurance seeking, and a desire for cosmetic interventions are commonly reported among adolescents with BDD and may increase the risk of psychological harm [14, 15].

In Jordan, research on BDD symptoms among adolescents is scarce. Existing studies have primarily focused on adults or university students, leaving a critical gap in understanding BDD during early and middle adolescence. The Jordanian cultural context—characterized by modest dress norms, strong family influence, and increasing exposure to social media—may uniquely shape how body image concerns and BDD symptoms are experienced by adolescent girls. Furthermore, the rapid proliferation of social media platforms has intensified appearance-based comparison across cultures, potentially amplifying vulnerability to BDD symptoms among young users.

Given these gaps, the present study was designed as an exploratory investigation to examine the status and correlates of body dysmorphic disorder symptoms among school-aged adolescent girls in Jordan. Specifically, the study aimed to [1] describe the prevalence and severity of BDD symptoms and [2] explore differences in symptom patterns according to selected sociodemographic, health-related, and psychosocial factors. By focusing on a non-Western, school-based adolescent population, this study seeks to contribute culturally relevant evidence to the limited regional literature and inform early detection and prevention efforts within school and community settings.

Methods

Design

A cross-sectional, descriptive design was employed to examine the prevalence of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) symptoms among adolescent girls in Jordan.

Setting

Data were collected from both governmental and private schools across the three main regions of Jordan (North, Center, and South). From each region, one private and four governmental schools were randomly selected to ensure geographic and institutional diversity.

Sample and sampling

A convenience stratified random sampling technique was used to recruit adolescent girls aged 12–18 years. This age range was selected because BDD symptoms typically emerge around ages 12–13, with an average onset at 16–17 years. Inclusion criteria were [1] enrollment in grades 7–12 and [2] ability to read and write Arabic. Exclusion criteria included a prior diagnosis of a mental disorder, based on school records or parental report.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.7 for a cross-sectional design, assuming a medium effect size (d = 0.30), α = 0.05, and power (1–β) = 0.80. The minimum estimated sample was 350 participants. To account for potential non-response or incomplete data, 700 students were approached, 409 agreed to participate, and 402 completed the survey and were included in the analysis.

Data collection

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at [XYZ University] and the Jordanian Ministry of Education. After obtaining approval, selected schools were contacted, and principals provided permission for data collection. The questionnaire link, including the study purpose, consent form, and researcher contact information, was distributed electronically through teachers to students via existing school communication channels. Parental and participant consent were both required before participation. All responses were anonymous and securely stored on a password-protected device accessible only to the research team. Several steps were taken to minimize response bias. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and participants were informed that there were no right or wrong answers to encourage honest responses. Attention-check items were embedded to identify inattentive responding, and incomplete or inconsistent responses were excluded from the analysis.

Instruments

The Arabic version of the scales has been used. The original language of BDD-SS was English. Therefore, it had to be translated into Arabic to provide an Arabic version suitable for our cultures. WHO guidelines on the translation and adaptation of instruments (2022) were followed to translate the instrument into Arabic. The translated instrument should be acceptable and function in the same manner when used in both forward and backward translations. Implementation of this procedure included four steps: forward translation, expert panel, back translation, and final version. The instrument was translated forward from the original language (English) to the target language by one bilingual translator, a health professional who was familiar with the terminology of the topic related to the topic covered by the instrument and the content area of the instrument construction in the target language (Arabic). The instrument was then translated into English by an independent translator who did not know the questionnaire and whose first language was English. It was validated as being equivalent to the original. Cultural and conceptual equivalence, rather than linguistic equivalence, was emphasized in the back-translation. Later, the back-translation version was sent to the original author, who approved it. The Body Dysmorphic Disorder Symptoms Scale (BDD-SS) was used to assess the presence and severity of BDD symptoms. The original instrument was developed in English and translated into Arabic following the World Health Organization guidelines for translation and cultural adaptation of instruments (2022), including forward translation, expert panel review, back translation, and author approval.

In the current study, the internal consistency reliability of the Arabic version of the BDD-SS was assessed and demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.83. This value exceeded the reliability reported in the original validation study.

The Body Dysmorphic Disorder Symptoms Scale (BDD-SS) [16, 17]

was used to examine the presence and severity of symptoms. The BDD-SS consists of 54 symptoms distributed into seven groups, with each group containing 2–19 symptoms. The groups of symptoms, respectively, are checking, grooming, shape/weight, hair pulling/skin picking, surgery/dermatology seeking, avoidance, and BDD-related cognition. Individuals endorse (yes or no) the symptoms they suffered in the last week. Therefore, opposite to all symptoms in each group, there is a rating scale from 0 to 10 (0 = no problem, 10 = very severe). Individuals were asked to rate the overall severity of the symptoms, in which at least one symptom was endorsed in the group. Individuals endorse (yes or no) the symptoms they experienced in the last week. Therefore, opposite to all symptoms in each group, there is a rating scale from 0 to 10 (0 = no problem, 10 = very severe). Individuals were asked to rate the overall severity of the symptoms for which at least one symptom was endorsed in the group. The BDD-SS provides two critical scores: BDD-SS Symptom (representing the number of symptoms experienced, with a score range from 0 to 54, based on the number of symptoms endorsed) and BDD-SS Severity (representing the total of all severity ratings, ranging from 0 to 70, as the sum of the severity scores in the seven groups). Any symptom group with a severity level of 5 (moderate) required medical attention, whereas symptom groups with a severity level of 0 indicated no need for medical attention. To verify the content validity of this instrument, a panel of expert physicians from the Massachusetts General Hospital Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (MGH OCD) program and other related disorders programs conducting studies in the BDD field reviewed all items. A study conducted to determine the internal consistency of the BDD-SS showed that the reliability of the BDD-SS (symptoms) was measured using Kuder-Richardson Formula-20 (KR-20 = 0.81). In addition, the reliability of the BDD-SS (severity) scales was Cronbach’s α = 0.75 (Wilhelm et al., 2016). In this study, the internal consistency was much higher than the original scale, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83.

Sociodemographic characteristics were assessed using a researcher-developed questionnaire and included age, grade level, school type, academic average, height, weight, health conditions, and social media use. Bullying related to physical appearance was assessed using a single self-reported item (yes/no). While this item captured participants’ subjective experience of bullying, it was not derived from a standardized bullying scale and was therefore interpreted cautiously.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM SPSS), version 26, was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics, including measures of central tendency and dispersion, were employed to summarize the study variables, while categorical variables were reported using frequencies and percentages. Inferential statistical tests (independent-samples t-tests, Pearson correlation coefficients, and analysis of variance [ANOVA]) were applied to examine group differences, comparisons, and associations among variables. Owing to the exploratory nature of the study, no a priori hypotheses were tested. Before conducting inferential analyses, the assumptions underlying each statistical test were systematically evaluated, and no violations were detected.

Ethical approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for conducting the research was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of Jordan (Approval Number: 34-2023), and the Jordanian Ministry of Education granted permission to access school settings and recruit participants. Participation in the study was voluntary. Informed consent was also obtained from the parents of all participating adolescents, and assent was also secured from the adolescents themselves before data collection. Confidentiality and anonymity of the participants were maintained throughout the study. No personal identifiers were collected, and all data were stored securely on a password-protected device that was accessible only to the research team.

The analysis showed that participants’ ages ranged between 12.0 and 18.0, with a mean of 15.0 (SD = 2.0) years. Regarding school information, 85.6% (n = 344) of them were at governmental schools, 35.9% (n = 144) of them were at the high school level, and 35.3% (n = 142) of them had an academic average ranging from 81.0 to 90.0. Concerning social media, 81.8% (n = 329) of them use it regularly, and 14.7% (n = 59) reported experiencing bullying (Table 1).

Discussion

Female adolescents are struggling to adapt to the accelerated flow of information and media. Such widespread media effects are not allied with their skills and acquired experience, which interferes with their abilities to manage their psychosocial needs. Several studies have been conducted recently and found that adolescent girls in Arab culture are experiencing various forms of psychosocial disturbances, such as eating problems [18], anxiety and depression ([19, 20]), substance use [21], internet addiction [22], and general pain and emotional expression dysfunction ([23, 24]). This study emphasizes one significant component related to the identity of females, in general, and adolescent girls, in particular, which relates to their perceptions of appearance. We assessed the prevalence and severity of body dysmorphic symptoms and their association with media and bullying effects. In addition, the study assessed the predictive power of selected socio-demographic factors among adolescent girls. We found that the overall severity of BDD symptoms in this study was low; however, low mean severity scores do not preclude the presence of clinically meaningful distress among a subset of participants. Several symptom domains—particularly grooming, checking, and avoidance—were commonly endorsed, suggesting that even subthreshold BDD symptoms may interfere with psychosocial functioning during adolescence, a critical period for identity development.

BDD symptoms are strongly influenced by sociocultural factors and gender role expectations, which may differ across societies. In Arab cultures, appearance often reflects a complex interplay of cultural heritage, religious values, and evolving social norms. Previous studies among adolescents and young adults in Jordan and neighboring countries have reported high levels of body dissatisfaction, eating-related concerns, and appearance anxiety, particularly among females [18]. These findings support the notion that appearance-related distress is a salient mental health issue in the region, even when formal psychiatric diagnoses are uncommon.

Sex roles in Arab culture are shaped by the cultural heritage and religious beliefs. It has been noted that many Arab societies often emphasize that the visual presentation traditionally communicates social identity, modesty, and gender norms. Females have historically been expected to embody modesty through clothing styles that vary widely by region, class, and personal belief. As a result, appearance in Arab societies reflects a dynamic blend of cultural heritage, religious values, and modern expressions of gender [25]. It is also noted that adolescents are notoriously known to go through many hormonal changes during puberty [26], which may contribute to several explanations for the perception of appearance among adolescent girls. Comparing current results with previous studies might be challenging due to the lack of standardized screening tools and the lack of similar studies within the Jordanian/Arab population, acknowledging that such a topic has been addressed by the literature in other cultures, which might not enable comparison or highlight similarities. Eating disorder symptoms, which are related to the topic of BBD symptoms, have been reported with alarming proportions among female adolescents in Jordan [18].

In terms of obsessive behaviors related to BDD, it has been found that the prevalence of BDD symptoms among female adolescents was 4.5% [16]. The most frequent obsessive behavior was checking in front of a mirror, and the least frequent symptom was excessive cleaning; however, excessive grooming among participants was low. Such reports were lower than other similar studies in other societies [16, 26] that could be related to the use of Hijab, which might minimize the need for excessive grooming compared to other cultures and societies. While Summers et al. [26] found that the most frequent symptom is related to bodily checks, our study revealed that changing clothes is the most frequent one, supporting the notion that the hijab influences which behaviors are most prevalent and which behaviors might be most prevalent in terms of BDD symptoms. The analysis indicated that sociodemographic characteristics of school-aged female adolescents, such as age, were not significantly associated with BDD symptoms and severity. Such an issue was controversial in the literature. While previous reports assured that there is no association between BDD symptoms and age and grade among female students [6], others found that BDD was associated with age and was significantly higher among young people [26]. Concerning academic performance, we found that there was a significant difference in the severity of BDD symptoms, while others found that females were significantly different in their BDD from their academic average [27]. Regarding academic performance, the present study identified a significant association with BDD symptom severity but not symptom frequency. This suggests that the intensity of appearance-related distress, rather than the mere presence of symptoms may be more relevant to academic functioning, consistent with previous findings linking body dissatisfaction to poorer academic outcomes among Jordanian adolescents [17].

Regarding school type, we found significant differences between participants who were from private and governmental schools, while Alam et al. [28] found that the students in private universities had a higher probability of having BDD symptoms than the students in public universities. Moreover, regarding the use of social media, this study revealed significant differences between participants who used social media and those who did not. The findings further confirm the role of social media exposure in the development of BDD symptoms. Adolescents are increasingly exposed to idealized and unrealistic appearance standards through digital platforms, which may mediate the relationship between sociocultural pressures and body image dissatisfaction. Increased reliance on appearance-focused social media has been consistently associated with greater BDD symptom severity in adolescent and young adult populations [1]. There have been abundant studies about the effect of using social media on BDD symptoms, but little is known about what negates these differences. Adolescents were influenced by universal features of appearance that were conveyed via social media and compared their appearance to these features, resulting in body dissatisfaction [19].

In the current study, the analysis showed significant differences between the participants who suffered from bullying and those who did not. Adverse childhood experiences, such as bullying at school, have been reported to be a significant contributor to the stability of their mental health [24–26]. Participants who reported experiencing bullying related to appearance demonstrated significantly higher BDD symptom frequency and severity, with large effect sizes. Although bullying was assessed using a single self-reported item rather than a standardized scale, the strength and consistency of the association suggest a robust relationship. This finding aligns with previous research identifying bullying and appearance-related teasing as key adverse childhood experiences contributing to the development and maintenance of BDD symptoms [17, 26, 27].

Concerning health-related factors, the analysis revealed that BDD symptoms were significantly different from congenital problems/deformities, dermatological problems, and the use of regular medications. In addition, there was a significant difference in health problems regarding the severity of BDD symptoms. Regarding congenital problems/deformities, previous studies assessed the negative effects of keloids on body image and found that 40% of the participants had negative parts of their body image (BDSS scores ≥ 6) [28–30]. Regarding health problems, the literature revealed that there was a significant relationship between BDD and obesity, while others found that there was an insignificant difference in obesity between non-BDD and BDD groups [30]. In terms of dermatological problems, the results indicated a high prevalence of BDD among adolescent girls with acne [31], and those with skin and nose dermatological problems [31]. This was relatively in agreement with the concerns of female college students, where skin represented the most frequent concern related to their bodies [30]. The high rates of skin concerns are related to acne vulgaris, which is common among adolescents [31]. These findings underscore the importance of integrating psychological screening into routine medical and dermatological care for adolescents.

This study has some limitations that include using a structured format of data collection, in which further investigation and clinical evaluation for participants might reveal informative outcomes.

Limitations of the study

This study has some limitations. Its cross-sectional design limits the establishment of causal relationships between BDD symptoms and related factors. The reliance on self-reported data may have introduced response bias. Moreover, cultural sensitivity and social stigma surrounding mental health and body image in Arab societies may have led to underreporting, particularly of symptoms perceived as shameful or socially unacceptable. The use of convenience sampling and online questionnaires also limits the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the absence of clinical assessment may have affected the accuracy of identifying actual BDD cases. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal designs, clinical interviews, and qualitative approaches to validate and deepen these findings. In addition, the study was not preregistered, and bullying was assessed using a single self-reported item rather than a validated bullying instrument, which may limit the precision of this variable.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on an important, yet often overlooked, issue affecting adolescent girls in Arab societies—their struggles with body image and the symptoms of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD). While the overall severity of BDD symptoms among the participants was low, the results still point to a deeper concern that could affect their mental health and day-to-day life. The study highlights the role of social media, bullying combined with health-related factors and sociodemographic characteristics of the adolescent girls, such as skin conditions, chronic health issues, being older, being in a private school rather than a public one, and having a congenital deformity. The study has implications for mental and psychological counselors at the schools and in the community. There is a need to organize a period screening for such psychological disturbances and initiate an early detection protocol. Using the general psychological assessment tool that incorporates BDD symptoms would enable early detection of such a problem. In addition, adolescent girls need to be educated about BDD symptoms and the sociocultural influence and encouraged to seek psychological counseling and help if they may suffer from such a problem. School counselors play a critical role in educating adolescent girls about expressing dissatisfaction with their bodies to support early detection and management of body-related concerns. In Muslim cultural contexts, where discussing personal or psychological distress may be discouraged due to stigma, modesty norms, or fear of social judgment, counselors are particularly important in creating safe, culturally sensitive spaces that encourage help-seeking and normalize mental health support. Although comparing these results with other research was difficult due to cultural differences and varying tools of measurement, it’s clear that more attention needs to be given to how adolescent girls perceive their bodies within their culture, and to emphasize the influence of social media that allows adolescent girls to globally compare themselves to their counterparts in other parts of the world. Future research should incorporate more personal and clinical assessments to better understand and support the needs of these young women.
