Optimizing microgrid operations with consideration of energy conservation and emission reduction benefits in spatial econometrics
Authors: Beibei Zhao, Xin Guan, Xingshuo Tao, Zhili Bai, Shiya Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study combines spatial econometric models with intelligent optimization algorithms to explore the spatial distribution characteristics of China’s carbon emissions and their optimization and regulation mechanisms. It aims to improve the operational efficiency of regional multi-microgrid systems under the constraints of energy conservation and emission reduction. The Moran index is used to analyze spatial autocorrelation based on China’s carbon emission data from 2013 to 2023. The results show that the spatial agglomeration of carbon emissions is strengthening year by year. In addition, the study constructs a three-layer multi-microgrid control system and adopts an improved whale optimization algorithm for scheduling optimization. On 10 standard test functions, the average error value of the improved algorithm is less than 0.0023, which is about 31.4% lower than that of the original algorithm. Meanwhile, the improved algorithm’s performance is better than other similar algorithms on most functions. In the actual scheduling simulation, during daytime hours with abundant renewable energy, Microgrid 1 achieves a minimum operating cost of 214.9 yuan, which is 1.3% lower than that of Microgrid 2 (217.65 yuan). Moreover, the environmental emission cost is reduced to 54.47 yuan. This study enhances the low-carbon scheduling capability of multi-microgrid systems; it also provides theoretical support and policy references for realizing regional collaborative emission reduction and the national carbon neutrality strategy.Keywords: Carbon emissions, Spatial distribution, Moran’s I, Multi-microgrid, Whale optimization algorithm, Adaptive inertia weight mechanismSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Information technology

Introduction

Global climate change and environmental issues are becoming increasingly severe, and promoting the transformation of energy structures to clean and low-carbon has become an international consensus1,2. Against this background, China has put forward the national strategic goal of “carbon peak and carbon neutrality”, which poses an urgent demand for building an efficient, low-carbon, and intelligent regional energy system3–5. Microgrid is an important carrier for integrating distributed renewable energy, improving energy utilization efficiency, and enhancing system resilience. Thus, microgrid rapidly develops from the traditional single independent operation mode to the direction of multi-microgrid collaborative operation and regional interconnection6,7. However, carbon emission control has significant spatial heterogeneity characteristics; effectively integrating the spatial coordination mechanism of regional carbon emissions into the microgrid scheduling optimization process is one of the key challenges in achieving regional collaborative emission reduction. At the same time, the advancement of policies such as smart city construction provides new opportunities for the structural optimization and intelligent management of energy systems.

To address the above challenges and serve the national “Dual Carbon” strategy, this study is committed to establishing a regional multi-microgrid collaborative operation framework; it integrates regional carbon emission spatial characteristics with intelligent optimization scheduling algorithms. The core goal is to bridge the gap in current research where there is a lack of systematic integration between “analysis of regional carbon emission spatial evolution” and “intelligent scheduling optimization of multi-microgrids”. Traditional scheduling methods often face problems such as insufficient algorithm convergence accuracy and poor robustness when dealing with complex energy scenarios with high dimensions, nonlinearity, and multiple constraints. These problems are difficult to simultaneously achieving the dual goals of economic cost control and minimum environmental emissions. For this reason, this study proposes an improved intelligent optimization algorithm, aiming to enhance the solution efficiency and adaptability of the scheduling model in complex operating environments.

The content framework of this study includes the following aspects. (1) The study establishes a regional carbon emission analysis model based on spatial econometrics; this model aims to quantify the spatial agglomeration effect and spillover effect among different cities or regions, and identify microgrid cluster structures with potential for collaborative emission reduction; (2) A multi-objective optimization model incorporating environmental costs, operating costs, and energy supply–demand constraints is constructed to guide the distributed scheduling of multi-microgrid systems in complex energy scenarios; (3) An improved whale optimization algorithm (WOA) is designed to enhance the convergence accuracy and robustness of scheduling schemes in operating environments with high nonlinearity and multi-variable constraints; (4) A risk-driven model expansion mechanism is introduced to strengthen the scheduling stability of the model in scenarios with uncertainty and volatility. Through these contents, this study offers a theoretical basis and decision-making reference for regional energy systems to achieve low-carbon and efficient operation.

Theoretical framework and methods

Carbon emission spatial distribution characteristics

Figure 1 presents the actual measured values of the global Moran’s I for carbon emissions in China from 2013 to 2023, reflecting the development trend of spatial autocorrelation in carbon emissions. The following is an analysis of the main changes observed. From 2013 to 2016, Moran’s I coefficients were relatively low and showed minor fluctuations, ranging from 0.138853 to 0.144400. This indicated weak spatial autocorrelation in carbon emissions, with a more dispersed distribution across regions. In 2017, the Moran’s I coefficient surged to 0.252991, demonstrating a significant increase in spatial clustering. This suggested that the geographical distribution of carbon emissions became more concentrated in that year. The coefficients remained relatively high in subsequent years. Since 2018, this coefficient exhibited a steady upward trend, reaching its highest value of 0.351300 in 2023. This trend revealed that the spatial clustering of carbon emissions was strengthened year by year, illustrating an increasing consistency in carbon emission levels among neighboring regions. The expected value (e(I)) remained at − 0.0027 across all years, indicating that the theoretical expectation for Moran’s I was negative. It showed that in the absence of spatial autocorrelation, carbon emissions should tend to be randomly distributed spatially. In summary, from 2013 to 2023, China’s Moran’s I coefficient for carbon emissions exhibited a significant upward trend. This demonstrates an increasing concentration in the spatial distribution of carbon emissions and a growing similarity in emissions between regions. This trend may suggest a gradual improvement in regional coordination in carbon emission control.

Multi-microgrid

The concept of “agent” was first defined by Minsky in The Society of Mind at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, referring to social entities capable of independently solving problems and negotiating with one another17. Figure 2 illustrates the basic structure of an agent.

Basic structure of an agent.

These intelligent entities can perceive their environment, influence it, and independently solve specific problems through computation. Agents can also exchange information and collaborate with other agents18.

A centralized control system decomposes the overall system into multiple units, each managed by independent agents, while all are under the unified control and coordination of a single central processor. The schematic diagram of the centralized control structure of the microgrid system is presented in Fig. 3.

Centralized control structure of microgrid systems.

The pyramid structure is generally considered cost-effective due to its superior synchronous control capability and scheduling convenience. However, this structure exhibits limited stability. In large-scale systems, as problem complexity increases, the central processing unit (CPU) must handle significantly larger data volumes, potentially triggering the “Curse of Dimensionality”19. The “Curse of Dimensionality” refers to the phenomenon where the volume of data space grows exponentially as dimensions (i.e., feature numbers) increase. It causes drastic performance declines in traditional algorithms within high-dimensional spaces. The specific manifestations are as follows. (1) The increase of data sparsity: Data points become extremely sparse in high-dimensional spaces, making it difficult to identify valid statistical patterns. (2) The improvement of computational complexity: Processing demands and time requirements grow exponentially with dimensionality, remarkably reducing algorithmic efficiency. (3) The decline of model generalization: High-dimensional data is prone to overfitting, impairing prediction accuracy for new samples. (4) The failure of distance measurement: Traditional measures (e.g., Euclidean distance) lose discriminative power in high-dimensional spaces, adversely affecting clustering and classification algorithms.

The distributed control structure of the microgrid system is revealed in Fig. 4.

The schematic diagram of the distributed control structure of the microgrid system.

Unlike centralized control structures, the distributed control structure maintains equal status among all agents without hierarchical differentiation. Each agent independently acquires information and collaboratively reaches optimization solutions through information exchange with other agents. This structure demonstrates high flexibility and scalability, allowing new agents to be added as needed. However, it requires frequent communication for information exchange at higher costs, and optimization results may be affected by local optimization biases. Specifically, local optimization bias refers to situations where agents, lacking global information, make optimal decisions based solely on their local perspectives. These decisions may fail to achieve the global optimum for the overall system. Thus, it leads to deviations in scheduling performance or even trapping in local optima, ultimately affecting system scheduling efficiency and stability.

The microgrid system’s hybrid control structure is demonstrated in Fig. 5.

The hybrid control structure of the microgrid system.

Centralized control is challenging to scale and has high communication demands, while global optimization in distributed control may be influenced by local results. Hybrid control combines the advantages of both approaches, mitigating their respective shortcomings and typically adopting a hierarchical structure that provides greater stability and scalability. In this structure, multiple central management agents coordinate the unit agents to achieve overall goals. Agents at each level maintain equal status and the ability to independently solve subproblems. This control method is more commonly applied in practice.

To enhance the optimization model’s capability in addressing future energy system uncertainties, this study adopts the multi-scenario simulation approach proposed by Cano et al.20. It compares performance variations of energy storage combinations with different scales and structures under future demand growth and resource intermittency conditions. Compared with single microgrid systems, multi-microgrid systems primarily differ in their significantly increased number of independent microgrids and distributed energy resources. This increase leads to substantially higher control complexity in multi-microgrid systems. As complexity grows, scheduling difficulty and communication requirements escalate accordingly. Improper scheduling may cause adverse system effects, potentially leading to system disintegration under extreme conditions. Therefore, this study proposes a multi-microgrid system’s topological structure with three-layer hybrid control, as suggested in Fig. 6.

The topological structure of multi-microgrid systems with three-layer hybrid control.

The designed multi-microgrid control system adopts a three-layer architecture, with the multi-microgrid agent at the top layer, the microgrid agent at the middle layer, and the distributed energy source agent at the bottom layer.

Multi-Microgrid Agent As the top layer of the three-tier control structure, it is responsible for coordinating the overall system’s scheduling plan. Its core functions include receiving upper-level electricity price and scheduling information; collecting load and generation forecasts, netload, and equipment status from sub-microgrids; summarizing information, calculating optimization objectives, formulating and issuing scheduling plans, and facilitating energy exchange and system optimization.

Microgrid Agent Positioned in the middle layer, it responds quickly to fluctuations in distributed energy sources, utilizing centralized control for optimization. Its main tasks are to gather information from lower layers, calculate optimization objectives, and report the results. Simultaneously, it receives upper-level directives to coordinate generation operations, reduce computational costs, and enhance control efficiency.

Distributed Energy Source Agent It is responsible for generation and load forecasting, controlling and monitoring equipment status, and executing upper-level scheduling directives to ensure the stable operation of distributed energy sources. It employs methods such as Power/Reactive Power (P/Q) control, Voltage/Frequency (P/Q) control, and droop control.

WOA

WOA is a novel intelligent algorithm proposed by Australian researcher Seyedali Mirjalili in 2016. WOA simulates the hunting behavior of humpback whales, making it a type of meta-heuristic optimization algorithm. Due to its simple search structure and reduced parameter settings, it has garnered widespread attention. Its uniqueness compared to other algorithms lies in its use of random searches or the simulation of the predatory behavior of the best individual in the population21. If not considered, the optimal scheduling results may deviate from expectations in practical operation, increasing operational risks and economic losses. Analysis of microgrid operational optimization results considering energy conservation and emission reduction benefits

This section focuses on the operational optimization of interconnected regional microgrids, aiming to minimize operational costs while considering environmental emission costs. The improved WOA is employed to optimize the scheduling model of two interconnected microgrids (Microgrids 1 and 2). The controllable unit for Microgrid 1 is an FC, while the controllable unit for Microgrid 2 is a DE. The optimization addresses the power output of Battery Technology (BT), WT, PV, and controllable units over a 24-hour operational schedule for each microgrid. Figures 8a, b demonstrate the power output patterns of major equipment (WT, PV, BT, FC) in Microgrids 1 and 2 across different periods. This reveals their dynamic scheduling response capabilities under load fluctuations and renewable energy output variations. For WT, Microgrid 2 achieves 86.25 kW output during high-wind daytime hours (12:00-16:00). It represents an 8.1% increase compared to Microgrid 1’s 79.8 kW, indicating stronger support during resource-abundant periods. Regarding PV, Microgrid 1 reaches a maximum output of 29.76 kW between 08:00 and 12:00, slightly higher than Microgrid 2’s 27.48 kW during the same period, though with greater relative volatility. Battery output significantly increases during off-peak hours, with Microgrid 2 delivering 23.14 kW between 00:00 and 04:00 before decreasing to 19.23 kW during daytime, demonstrating peak-shaving and valley-filling functions. Microgrid 1 shows its highest battery power output (31.42 kW) during evening peak hours (16:00–20:00), reflecting its load response capability. Furthermore, FC in Microgrid 2 consistently outperforms Microgrid 1 throughout the day, particularly reaching 37.98 kW during early morning (04:00-08:00) versus 36.45 kW in Microgrid 1. This confirms Microgrid 2’s greater reliance on stable, controllable energy sources for system balance. Both microgrids exhibit excellent multi-source complementarity, with scheduling strategies dynamically optimizing power allocation to enhance overall system stability and operational efficiency.

Beyond conventional lithium battery storage systems, this study simulates the effects of supercapacitors and hydrogen storage units. Results indicate supercapacitors significantly improve dynamic response in high-frequency power fluctuation scenarios. In contrast, hydrogen storage demonstrates greater economic and environmental advantages in regions with pronounced peak-valley load differences or long-duration storage requirements. Future research could explore flywheel energy storage and its coordination optimization with existing units to develop more comprehensive hybrid energy storage models.

However, as the carbon price increases, emission constraints begin to play a more significant inhibitory role. It is noteworthy that when the carbon credit price is 100 yuan/ton, the environmental costs of both microgrids decrease significantly. This demonstrates that the carbon price policy, as an external constraint mechanism, has a sensitive regulatory effect on the optimization model and can serve as a green guiding tool at the policy-making level.

The impact of changes in battery replacement costs: When the battery replacement cost decreases to 0.8 times, the operating cost of Microgrid 1 drops to 234.20 yuan (a reduction of approximately 2.8%); Microgrid 2 drops to 243.40 yuan (a reduction of 0.9%). When the battery cost increases by 1.5 times, the operating cost of Microgrid 1 increases to 252.94 yuan (an increase of approximately 5%). This is far higher than the increase in Microgrid 2 (248.70 yuan, approximately 1.3%). It illustrates that the scheduling of Microgrid 1 relies more on the energy storage system, so its operational economy is more affected by the life cycle cost of the battery. Since Microgrid 2 is dominated by controllable units, it is less sensitive to battery costs. The battery replacement cost has little impact on the environmental cost. This suggests that the energy storage system mainly plays the role of peak shaving, frequency modulation, and reducing economic costs rather than directly affecting carbon emissions.

In summary, Microgrid 1 as a whole exhibits strong economic and environmental dual stability. It has small fluctuations in operating costs under multiple scenarios, making it suitable as a priority object for promoting regional low-carbon microgrids. Microgrid 2 shows high sensitivity to carbon credits and fuel prices. This suggests that more economic regulation space or subsidy support should be given to such microgrids in policy design to enhance their green transformation capabilities. Considering the three types of factors, fuel price is the main cause affecting economic benefits; carbon credit price is the key variable for environmental regulation; energy storage cost determines the economic sustainability of long-term system operation.

Discussion

This study aims to improve the multi-microgrids’ collaborative scheduling efficiency by integrating carbon emission spatial characteristics with intelligent algorithms. Empirical results show that: (1) The carbon emission agglomeration revealed by the spatial econometric model (Moran’s I index rising to 0.351300) provides a geographical basis for microgrid cluster division, supporting the “regional low-carbon collaboration” hypothesis; (2) The error reduction of the improved WOA and the optimization of Microgrid 1’s operating cost verify the supporting role of algorithm innovation in the dual objectives (economic-environmental); (3) In the sensitivity analysis, the regulatory effect of carbon prices on emission costs corresponds to the design goal of linking policy tools with scheduling models. These findings support the initial assumptions while expanding the boundaries of traditional scheduling theory through the spatial-algorithm coupling mechanism. Based on this, from the following five aspects, this study conducts extended discussions to clarify the innovation and practical adaptability.

This section conducts an extended discussion of five aspects to clarify the innovation and practical adaptability. They include model method comparison, interaction mechanism modeling, environmental significance of artificial intelligence (AI), result comparison, and model resilience, to enhance theoretical contribution and empirical value.

First, regarding the innovation in microgrid transaction control mechanisms, this study establishes a distributed scheduling framework based on a three-layer agent structure. It also incorporates spatial spillover effects of regional carbon emissions as scheduling constraints. This approach significantly differs from existing literature that predominantly employs cooperative game-based scheduling models. For instance, Salyani et al.30 reported that the flexible scheduling framework based on cooperative game performed well in post-disaster recovery scenarios. However, its economic-environmental dual optimization mechanism was relatively weak. Especially by quantifying emission externalities through spatial econometric models, the “regional low-carbon coordination” principle can be reflected in transaction path design. The integration of spatial mechanisms constitutes an important supplement to conventional transaction control research. In terms of algorithm performance, the average error of the improved WOA proposed in this study is less than 0.0023 on 10 test functions. Besides, the convergence accuracy is better than the multi-objective non-dominated ranking WOA used by Wang et al.15. At the practical application level, the proposed model-driven microgrid scheduling reduces operating and environmental emission costs. This breaks through the single-goal focus of community microgrid optimization in cost reduction and adaptation to load uncertainty in Malik et al.12. It indicates the comprehensive value of the spatial carbon emission constraint and the multi-objective coordinated optimization of economy and environment. The ecological impact model proposed by Jodeiri-Seyedian et al.6 achieves the quantitative assessment of “energy conversion-environmental benefits”. However, it does not consider the constraints of spatial heterogeneity of carbon emissions on scheduling strategies. In contrast, this study makes up for this limitation by introducing Moran index analysis. Nourollahi et al.31 used information gap decision theory to deal with demand response risk. However, the convergence accuracy of its algorithm in complex energy scenarios is significantly lower than that of the improved WOA in this study. These comparisons highlight the dual innovative value of this study, integrating spatial econometric analysis and an adaptive inertial weight mechanism. Second, concerning microgrid interaction modeling, the current version does not directly incorporate game theory frameworks. Given the autonomous nature of microgrid operations and bilateral transaction characteristics, future research could integrate game-theoretic approaches into transaction modeling. The multi-agent game mechanism proposed by Ma et al.32 may inspire the subsequent development of interaction models that consider information gaming and power exchange strategy selection. This approach enables more dynamic and strategy-driven multi-microgrid coordination. Third, regarding the environmental implications of AI methods, the improved WOA adopted here for solving high-dimensional nonlinear scheduling problems enhances computational efficiency and solution quality. Meanwhile, it demonstrates AI’s supportive role in green scheduling objectives. However, as revealed by Li & Li et al.33, AI serves not merely as an optimization tool but also as a potential catalyst for emission reduction pathways in energy systems. Future work could expand AI applications to carbon emission forecasting, equipment failure prediction, and energy-saving strategy identification, ultimately constructing an integrated “perception-assessment-decision” green intelligent scheduling system. Fourth, concerning result validity, this study demonstrates the proposed method’s superior performance in average error and convergence speed through comparative testing against traditional algorithms like PSO, GA, and DE. Meanwhile, it achieves lower operational and emission costs in practical simulations. To strengthen methodological persuasiveness, subsequent research could adopt publicly available benchmark systems (e.g., IEEE 33-node microgrid cases) for broader comparative experiments with existing scheduling models. Thus, the proposed method’s applicability boundaries and limitations can be precisely defined. Finally, regarding system resilience design, the current model does not systematically address operational strategies under extreme scenarios (e.g., main grid disconnection, peak load surges). Future frameworks could incorporate resilient scheduling mechanisms inspired by Nourollahi et al.34 to enhance comprehensiveness. These frameworks establish hybrid uncertainty optimization with predefined “backup capacity” and response rules, maintaining a continuous power supply and basic environmental safeguards during contingencies. In summary, the results obtained in this study have certain guiding significance for different stakeholders. For grid operators, the identified “economic-environmental optimal” scheduling configuration can provide a reference for the cooperative operation of regional multi-microgrids. Especially during periods of high renewable energy penetration, giving priority to calling on low-carbon microgrid resources can further reduce the overall system emissions and operating costs. Meanwhile, this scheduling configuration offers a universal framework for managing microgrid clusters with different resource endowments (such as areas rich in wind and solar resources or high-load density areas); its core algorithm can flexibly adjust parameters to adapt to local differences. For policymakers, the study’s findings on the differentiated response characteristics of different microgrids to economic and environmental parameters provide a quantitative basis for formulating differentiated and precise regional incentive policies. For example, special subsidies can be granted for the deployment of microgrids in areas with abundant wind and solar resources but weak power grids. At the same time, carbon credit rewards can be introduced through the carbon market mechanism to guide more operators to adopt clean scheduling strategies. For equipment manufacturers and microgrid investors, specific technology combinations (such as PV + FC + lithium battery in Microgrid 1 have advantages in cost and emission reduction, as verified by the optimized model. These advantages can guide them to prioritize investment in the research, development, and deployment of related technologies. Future research can explore multi-energy complementary scheduling, extreme climate adaptation strategies, or apply the proposed model to special scenarios such as islands and remote rural areas. These methods verify this model’s robustness and promotional value in a wider range of regions and complex energy structures. These efforts jointly promote the low-carbon and efficient transformation of regional energy systems, thereby comprehensively supporting the technical implementation and continuous advancement of the national “Dual Carbon” strategy.

The proposed AI-optimized multi-microgrid operational scheduling framework integrates spatial econometric analysis with an improved WOA. It comprehensively addresses the triple objectives of carbon emission reduction, cost control, and energy reliability. This strategy achieves theoretical maximization of energy system efficiency and minimization of environmental impact. It also provides actionable technical support for policy formulation under China’s “Carbon Peak and Carbon Neutrality” strategic goals. Accordingly, this study proposes the following policy recommendation. First, the optimization strategy reveals operational response characteristics of different microgrid types (e.g., those dominated by controllable units versus energy storage) under varying economic and environmental parameters, offering quantitative scheduling references. The government should implement regionally differentiated measures when formulating distributed energy incentive policies. For example, special financial subsidies can be provided for microgrid deployment in areas with abundant wind/solar resources but weak grid access capacity. Simultaneously, carbon credit rewards should be introduced through carbon market mechanisms to incentivize low-emission operational strategies and guide more multi-microgrid operators toward clean scheduling pathways. Second, regarding renewable energy deployment, the identified “economically and environmentally optimal” scheduling configurations can serve as reference templates for local governments when establishing guidance standards for renewable energy capacity allocation (wind/PV/storage). This facilitates a transition from “quota-based construction” to “optimized configuration.” For microgrid expansion, a “tiered incentive mechanism” is recommended. Interconnected microgrids participating in regional scheduling should receive prioritized electricity price compensation and revenue sharing. Independently operated microgrids could qualify for green credit support or equipment depreciation tax deductions based on their annual carbon reduction benefits. Finally, the government should accelerate intelligent upgrades of microgrid operation platforms through top-level design. Power grid companies are encouraged to collaborate with universities and enterprises in developing “AI + low-carbon scheduling” demonstration projects. Incorporating intelligent optimization algorithms as core modules of new power systems helps achieve the systemic goals of “renewable energy consumption priority” and “source-grid-load-storage coordination”. Consequently, these measures comprehensively support the technological implementation and sustained advancement of the national “Dual Carbon” strategy.

Conclusion

This study conducts an in-depth investigation into regional multi-microgrid operational optimization using an improved WOA. It aims to achieve efficient coordinated scheduling of wind power, PV, energy storage, and controllable generation units with the dual objectives of minimizing operational costs and environmental emissions. First, regarding the analysis of spatial distribution characteristics of carbon emissions, the calculation of Moran’s I index reveals a significant enhancement in spatial autocorrelation of China’s carbon emissions between 2013 and 2023. Second, across all tests from Functions 1 to 10, the improved WOA maintains an average error value below 0.0023, achieving the lowest value of 0.0014 on Function 9. This demonstrates strong adaptability and stability across diverse optimization problems. Finally, operational analysis of Microgrids 1 and 2 shows that Microgrid 1 maintains relatively stable operational costs and environmental emissions across all periods, while Microgrid 2 exhibits slightly higher costs. This difference becomes particularly evident during periods of insufficient renewable energy output, when Microgrid 2 relies more heavily on controllable generation units, leading to increased overall costs. To enhance the framework’s applicability for long-term planning, this study considers the impacts of renewable energy output fluctuations and system degradation on scheduling strategies. Annual attenuation coefficients are incorporated to simulate the efficiency decline of the energy storage system, and fluctuation factors are introduced to model the seasonal and long-term variations of renewable energy. As a result, the proposed framework demonstrates improved stability and robustness under future operational conditions. Although this study has achieved certain results in the low-carbon scheduling optimization of regional multi-microgrids, there are still several limitations. 1) The model insufficiently considers the fluctuations in renewable energy output under extreme weather conditions; in the future, more refined prediction models can be constructed by combining meteorological big data. 2) The computational efficiency of the current algorithm in ultra-large-scale microgrid clusters needs to be verified; subsequent research can explore the application of distributed parallel computing frameworks. 3) The real-time linkage mechanism between the carbon emission rights trading market and the scheduling model has not been fully discussed, which is an important direction for realizing market-technology collaboration under the “Dual Carbon” goals. These limitations also provide clear technical breakthrough paths for subsequent research.
