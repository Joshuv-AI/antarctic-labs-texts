Dual impact of interleukin-17 A blockade on inflammatory and stromal pathways in peripheral spondyloarthritis
Authors: Ihsan Hammoura, Renée H Fiechter, Leonieke J J van Mens, Henriëtte M Y de Jong, Inka A Fluri, Sander W Tas, Dominique LP Baeten, Marleen GH van de Sande, Nataliya G Yeremenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIL-17 A plays a role in the pathology of spondyloarthritis (SpA), as evidenced by the clinical efficacy of IL-17 A inhibitors (IL-17Ai). Nevertheless, the exact role of IL-17 A in driving synovial inflammation and pathological remodeling remains unknown. In this study, we investigated the molecular pathways affected by IL-17Ai in SpA synovitis to determine whether this response is tissue- and/or treatment-specific.MethodsSynovial biopsies from 12 peripheral SpA (pSpA) patients before and after 12 weeks of IL-17Ai treatment were analyzed by RNA-sequencing and qPCRs. The synovial tissue response to IL-17Ai in 7 psoriatic arthritis (PsA) patients was compared to open-source gene expression data of skin biopsies from 15 psoriasis (PsO) patients treated with IL-17Ai, and synovial biopsies from 7 PsA patients receiving IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade.ResultsCompared to baseline, IL-17Ai significantly modulated the expression of 1255 genes (549 upregulated and 706 downregulated, FDR < 0.1) in the synovium at week 12. The downregulated genes were predominantly associated with inflammatory processes and stromal functions. Comprehensive analysis revealed that IL-17Ai-mediated suppression of bone remodeling is independent of its inhibition of inflammatory pathways. While IL-17Ai consistently attenuated inflammation-related pathways in both psoriatic joints and skin, its effect on bone remodeling pathways was specific to the synovium. This distinguishes IL-17Ai from IL-12p40/IL-23p40 inhibitors, which, while mitigating synovial inflammation, did not affect tissue remodeling.ConclusionsOur findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of IL-17 A blockade in pSpA, underscoring its unique role in selectively targeting bone-remodeling processes specific to the joints of individuals with SpA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-025-03638-0.Keywords: Peripheral spondyloarthritis, Stromal pathways, Synovium, Psoriatic skin, IL-17A inhibitors

Background

Spondyloarthritis (SpA) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disease characterized by inflammation of axial and peripheral joints, excessive new bone formation, and extra-musculoskeletal manifestations such as skin psoriasis, anterior uveitis and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). SpA can be classified into axial SpA (axSpA) and peripheral SpA (pSpA) [1, 2] depending on the predominant clinical feature. Within the pSpA spectrum, psoriatic arthritis is the most common form. The pro-inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin-17 A (IL-17 A) are key drivers of chronic inflammation and structural damage in SpA. The vital role of both cytokines in SpA pathogenesis is emphasized by the clinical efficacy of TNF- and IL-17 A inhibitors as therapeutics for SpA [3–8]. IL-17 A, a member of the IL-17 superfamily, can synergistically act with TNF to induce a robust pro-inflammatory response. Blockade of IL-17 A has been shown to reduce inflammation and significantly improve axial symptoms, peripheral arthritis, dactylitis, enthesitis and psoriasis [3, 4, 9–12]. In animal models, a direct effect of IL-17 A inhibition on structural damage, including new bone formation, was suggested [13]. Also, in clinical trials, low rates of radiographic progression are observed after an inhibition of IL-17 A [14, 15]. However, whether this is due to a direct effect on new bone formation, or a result of decreased inflammation remains to be elucidated. Although IL-17 A inhibition improves SpA in multiple domains, not all patients achieve remission, and treatment responses can vary between the clinically affected sites [12]. Our understanding of how IL-17 A operates in target tissues is still limited. Using selective therapeutic interventions in patients, combined with the ex vivo analysis of primary human tissues, we aimed to investigate how IL-17 A contributes to pSpA pathogenesis by unravelling the mechanisms that mediate IL-17 A-induced tissue damage. To investigate the mechanism of action of IL-17 A blockade with secukinumab in pSpA synovium, we conducted a clinical trial, which showed that the clear clinical improvement was accompanied by a significant decrease in CD15-positive neutrophils and CD68-positive sublining macrophages, without impacting systemic immune responses. In addition, significant reductions were observed in the expression levels of MMP3, CCL20, IL-6, and IL-17 A itself, but not of TNF [16]. In this study, we applied transcriptional profiling of paired pre- and post-treatment synovial biopsy samples to reveal IL-17 A-driven mechanisms of pathology in SpA synovitis and decipher molecular pathways affected by its targeting with secukinumab.

Methods

Study design

The study design of the mechanism-of-action study of anti-IL17A in pSpA and synovial tissue sampling have been described previously [16]. In short, 20 patients with a clinical diagnosis of pSpA fulfilling the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis International Society criteria were included in a 12-week open-label, investigator-initiated, mechanism-of-action study in which they received 300 mg secukinumab according to standard care: once weekly from baseline to week 4 and then every 4 weeks thereafter. Clinical efficacy parameters and safety were assessed every 4 weeks. Synovial tissue samples were obtained by needle arthroscopy from an inflamed knee or ankle joint at baseline and repeated in the same joint at week 12. The synovial tissue samples were either snap-frozen for RNA extraction or embedded in Tissue-Tek (Sakura Finetek, USA) or paraffin for histological evaluation. All patients have provided a written informed consent, and the study has received approval from the ethics board of the Academic Medical Center Amsterdam.

Patients

To investigate the molecular pathways affected by IL-17 A blockade using secukinumab in pSpA synovitis, we selected 12 pSpA patients from the original 20 study participants who had paired baseline and week 12 synovial tissue samples, which passed quality control for RNA sequence analysis. This cohort included patients with PsA (n = 7) and non-PsA disease (n = 5; including 3 undifferentiated SpA, 1 ankylosing spondylitis with peripheral arthritis, and 1 inflammatory bowel-disease-related SpA without active IBD at screening and baseline). The mean age of these patients was 44.4 years (± 9.9 SD), and 9 were male. Their median disease duration was 8 (IQR 3–12) years. Stable doses of concomitant medications were allowed: 3 patients used non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), 3 patients used NSAIDs in combination with conventional disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (leflunomide, or methotrexate), and 1 patient used NSAIDs in combination with prednisone. Six patients (50%) had previously been treated with no more than one TNF inhibitor. The clinical improvement in these 12 patients was similar to the total cohort (data not shown). Specifically, the primary clinical efficacy outcome, an ACR20 response at week 12, was achieved by 8 out of 12 patients (5/7 PsA and 3/5 non-PsA patients), comparable to the response of the total cohort (13 out of 20, respectively).

cDNA library generation and next-generation sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the snap-frozen whole-tissue synovial biopsies using RNA Stat-60 (Tel-Test Inc), treated with DNase I (Invitrogen) and cleaned using RNeasy columns (79254, Qiagen) according to manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and integrity were measured using the Qubit RNA BR Assay kit (Life Technologies) and the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), correspondingly. cDNA libraries were constructed with Illumina TruSeq™ RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina) using 1 µg of total RNA with RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7. Briefly, the protocol consisted of polyA-RNA enrichment, RNA fragmentation, reverse transcription of fragmented RNA into cDNA, adapters ligation onto both ends of the cDNA fragments, and amplification of cDNA fragments by PCR. The resulting cDNA libraries were paired end sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea) to obtain around 60 million reads per sample. Quality control of raw sequence reads (FastQC v0.11.7), low-quality reads removal (Trimmomatic 0.38), mapping of the reads to the reference genome (USCS hg19 assembly) with HISAT2 v2.1.0, transcript assembly with Stringtie v1.3.4d, and calculation of raw transcription profile as a read counts for each gene and each sample was performed.

Differential gene expression and pathway enrichment analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) read counts, pipeline edgeR (v3.40.2; [17]), R (v4.2.2), and RStudio (v2022.12.0) were used. Briefly, DEGs data analysis included filtering out low-expressed genes, normalizing the samples according to their effective library sizes and generating generalized linear models to assess differential expressions. DEGs were identified using the likelihood ratio test, and P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini‒Hochberg method. Genes with adjusted P < 0.1 and without restrictions on the fold changes (FC) were identified as significantly different. Visualizations were conducted with edgeR, ggplot2 (v3.4.1), pheatmap (v1.0.12), EnhancedVolcano (v1.16.0), and Venn Diagram (v1.7.3) packages. Based on up- and downregulated DEGs, the clusterProfiler package in R was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) pathway enrichment analysis with a cut-off of P < 0.05 and count ≥ 2.

Comparative transcriptomics analysis

Transcription changes in the lesional skin of psoriatic patients in response to secukinumab treatment were assessed using publicly available RNA-seq data set GSE171012 [18] and Affymetrix GeneChip data set [19]; DEGs table kindly provided by the authors were analyzed. Open-access RNA-seq data set (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA693312/; [20]) was used to evaluate transcription changes in synovial biopsies after 12 weeks of ustekinumab treatment. For the compatible comparisons, GSE171012 and PRJNA693312 were reanalyzed according to our edgeR RNA-seq analysis pipeline with the same parameters as in the current research project. Shared and unique DEGs among the comparisons between secukinumab-treated joint vs. secukinumab-treated skin and secukinumab-treated joint vs. ustekinumab-treated joint were then identified with a subsequent estimation of overrepresented biological processes by pathway enrichment analysis. To determine differentially enriched pathways at baseline in our dataset compared to the PRJNA693312 dataset, a Single-Sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was performed. This analysis calculates an enrichment score for a specific gene set (pathway) within each individual sample, providing a single score per sample per pathway. Then a statistical test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test with multiple testing correction) was applied to compare the distribution of ssGSEA scores for each pathway between the two datasets. Pathways with a statistically significant difference in their enrichment scores are identified as being differentially active, highlighting key biological pathways that are significantly up- or down-regulated in one dataset compared to the other.

Data availability

The raw and processed RNA-sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus database under accession number GSE304063.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Of ten paired samples with substantial RNA left after RNA sequencing, total RNA was reverse transcribed using a RevertAid H Minus First-Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific) and analyzed by real-time qPCR with TaqMan gene expression assays for IL1B (Hs01555410_m1), IL-26 (Hs00218189_m1), CXCL6 (Hs00605742_g1), CXCL11 (Hs00171138_m1), CXCL13 (Hs00757930_m1), CCL20 (Hs00355476_m1), HAMP (Hs00221783_m1), SOCS3 (Hs02330328_s1), TNC (Hs01115665_m1), ALPL (Hs01029144_m1), RUNX1 (Hs02558380_s1), BMP1 (Hs00241807_m1), ECM1 (Hs00189435_m1), COL17A1 (Hs00990036_m1), COL3A1 (Hs01103890_m1), SNAI1 (Hs00195591_m1), SNAI2 (Hs00161904_m1), FHL2 (Hs00991866_m1), GJA1 (Hs00748445_s1), P4HA1 (Hs00914594_m1), CHI3L1 (Hs01072228_m1), IBSP (Hs00173780_m1), ADAMTS4 (Hs01548440_m1), CHRDL2 (Hs00248808_m1), ADAMTS14 (Hs01548440_m1), MMP1 (HS00899688_m1), MMP3 (Hs00968305_m1), IGFBP5 (Hs00181213_m1), SMAD3 (Hs00969210_m1), RORB (Hs00199445_m1) (all Thermofisher) using Real-Time PCR QuantStudio3 PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Expressions of all genes were normalized to the expression of GAPDH (Hs02786624_g1) as a housekeeping gene and presented as fold change relative to the reference sample.

Statistical analysis

Real-time qPCR and immunohistochemistry were analyzed using non-parametric statistics with the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test, and values under 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

IL-17 A blockade modulates inflammatory and stromal molecular pathways in pSpA synovitis

To identify molecular pathways underlying the previously observed clinical and histological improvement in pSpA joints in response to IL-17 A blockade [16], we performed RNA sequencing analysis on paired whole synovial tissue biopsies obtained before and after 12 weeks of secukinumab treatment. Differential gene expression analysis yielded 1255 genes, including 549 upregulated and 706 downregulated (False Discovery Rate: FDR 10%) (Fig. 1A). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis, using all significant DEGs, largely separates pre- and post-treatment synovial tissue, identifying 9 clusters (Fig. 1B). Further analysis of these clusters for GO enrichment revealed that genes in cluster 6 were enriched in stromal/bone remodeling, while genes in cluster 1 were enriched in inflammatory GO terms.

Detailed analysis of inflammatory pathways modulated in pSpA synovitis upon IL-17 A blockade identified multiple inflammatory GO terms such as granulocyte migration (GO:0097530), regulation of inflammatory response (GO:0050727), neutrophil migration (GO:1990266), cell chemotaxis (GO:0060326), leukocyte cell-cell adhesion (GO:007159), and leukocyte mediated immunity (GO:0002443) among the downregulated DEGs (Fig. 1C).

Moreover, detailed analysis of stromal cell-mediated pathways modulated in pSpA synovitis upon IL-17 A blockade identified multiple GO terms such as extracellular matrix organization (GO:003018), collagen metabolic process (GO:0032963), collagen fibril organization (GO:0030199), collagen catabolic process (GO:0030574), ossification (GO:0001503), osteoclast differentiation (GO:0030316), and extracellular matrix disassembly (GO:0022617) among downregulated DEGs; negative regulation of osteoblast differentiation (GO:0045668), muscle cell differentiation (GO:0042692) and detection of mechanical stimulus (GO:0050982) among upregulated DEGs (Fig. 1C).

To confirm the RNA-sequencing data, we selected several genes from different GO terms associated with inflammatory pathways for RT-qPCR analysis (Fig. 2A), which demonstrated a significant decrease in the expression after 12 weeks of secukinumab for most genes, including HAMP (p = 0.027), CXCL13 (p = 0.030), CXCL6 (p = 0.020), CCL20 (p = 0.020), IL1B (p = 0.027), and IL-26 (p = 0.020). Thus, these data show that 12 weeks of IL-17 A blockade suppressed multiple inflammatory processes in the inflamed peripheral SpA synovium.

Blocking of IL-17 A downmodulates DEGs associated with inflammatory and stromal responses in the synovium of patients with pSpA. (A) The expression patterns of 8 genes associated with inflammatory responses, encoding interleukins, chemokines, suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) and hepcidin antimicrobial peptide (HAMP) obtained from the RNA-seq were validated by RT-qPCR. (B) The expression patterns of selected genes, associated with stromal responses, obtained from the RNA-seq were validated by RT-qPCR

When evaluating the gene expression of several genes selected from the modulated stromal and extracellular-matrix-related pathways, we could confirm this observed modulation with significant differences present for half of the genes (Fig. 2B). These genes included: CHI3L1 (p = 0.039); IBSP (p = 0.011); ADAMTS4 (p = 0.002); CHRDL2 (p = 0.039); ADAMTS14 (p = 0.0098); MMP1 (p = 0.014); MMP3 (p = 0.02); IGFBP5 (p = 0.02); SMAD3 (p = 0.0195). Together, these data demonstrate that 12 weeks of IL-17 A blockade resulted in the modulation of processes associated with bone/tissue remodeling and angiogenesis in the inflamed pSpA synovium.

Target tissue-specificity: comparison of gene expression data of psoriatic arthritis synovium versus psoriasis skin after IL-17 A blockade

Since we have observed that IL-17Ai resulted in the modulation of inflammatory and bone remodeling-associated pathways in affected pSpA joints, we set out to identify whether this response is target-tissue specific. To this aim, we compared the changes in gene expression in response to secukinumab in PsA synovium in our cohort to that of the lesional skin biopsies obtained from psoriasis patients 12 weeks of post-treatment with secukinumab (300 mg subcutaneously every week up to week 4, and every 4 weeks thereafter, identical to our cohort) [18]. Reanalyzing this publicly available dataset (GSE171012) with our RNA-seq analysis pipeline (adjusted p-value < 0.1), we identified 9248 DEGs, including 4953 up- and 4295 down-regulated, respectively. The subsequent comparative analysis revealed that over half of genes significantly modulated in response to IL-17Ai in the PsA synovium (210 up- and 206 down-regulated out of 811 genes) were also modulated in the same direction in the PsO skin (Fig. 3A). We also observed that 93 DEGs, including 34 upregulated and 59 downregulated, exhibited opposite regulation in the joint compared to the skin (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, GO enrichment analysis yielded no significant results for the genes that were upregulated in the joint but downregulated in the skin. However, a contrasting scenario was observed for the 59 genes downregulated in the joint but upregulated in the skin upon IL-17 A targeting. These genes were significantly associated with processes related to bone and tissue remodeling, including collagen fibril organization (GO:0030199), extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198), collagen metabolic process (GO:0032963), endochondral bone morphogenesis (GO:0060350), connective tissue development (GO:0061448), and bone morphogenesis (GO:0060349) (Fig. 3C).

Detailed enrichment analysis revealed that genes downregulated in response to IL-17 A blockade in both pSpA joint and psoriatic skin were enriched in GO terms related to the inflammatory responses, such as positive regulation of cytokine production (GO:0001819), leukocyte-mediated cytotoxicity (GO:0001909), T cell-mediated cytotoxicity (GO:0001913), and cell activation involved in immune response (GO:0002263) (Fig. 3D). The blockade of IL-17 A targeted specific inflammatory responses in the skin, such as regulation of innate immune response (GO:0045088), type II interferon production (GO:0032606), type I interferon production (GO:0032606), and canonical NF-κB signal transduction (GO:0007249) (Fig. 3D). In the joint tissue specifically, the genes that were down-regulated as a result of IL-17 A blockade were associated with dendritic cell antigen processing and presentation (GO:0002468), regulation of antigen processing and presentation (GO:0002577), and macrophage activation (GO:0042116). Remarkably, GO enrichment analysis showed that IL-17 A blockade had a distinct impact on tissue remodeling and bone development processes in the joint and skin. Of note, particularly in the joint, these processes were suppressed following anti-IL-17 A treatment. In contrast, the skin tissue exhibited an opposite response, as the same processes of tissue remodeling and bone development were induced by the IL-17 A blockade.

To further substantiate these tissue-specific effects of IL-17 A blockade in inflamed psoriatic synovial tissue and skin, we utilized Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). This method allows us to identify if a set of genes linked to a specific GO term shows significant differences between two biological states. Unlike GO analysis, which focuses on DEGs, GSEA considers all genes by ranking and weighing them using statistics like fold change or log(p-value), adjusting an enrichment score based on each gene’s association with a pathway. The GSEA largely confirmed GO data demonstrating that genes related to inflammatory responses, T-helper 17 type immune response, and T-helper 1 type immune response were downregulated in both joint and skin (Fig. 3E). In contrast, a reverse expression pattern was observed for genes involved in tissue and bone remodeling, such as those related to ossification, collagen fibril organization, bone development, and extracellular matrix organization genes, which were relatively downregulated in the joint but upregulated in the skin following IL-17 A blockade (Fig. 3E), underscoring the tissue-specific impacts of IL-17 A inhibition in psoriatic conditions.

To mitigate potential bias and validate the contrasting effects of IL-17 A blockade on tissue remodeling in psoriatic joint and skin, we compared gene expression changes in synovial tissues from our cohort to those in another PsO skin cohort kindly provided by James G. Krueger and colleagues. This skin cohort’s data was derived from lesional skin biopsies obtained from PsO patients before and after 12 weeks of secukinumab treatment.

Further analysis of this dataset revealed that anti-IL-17 A treatment suppressed genes involved in inflammatory responses in both skin and joints. These genes included those in the JAK-STAT cascade and TNF signaling pathway. However, genes involved in ossification, collagen fibril organization, and extracellular matrix organization were modulated in opposite directions in these tissues: IL-17 A blockade suppressed these genes in the joint but induced them in the skin (Supplementary Fig. 1). This observation, which is in line with our findings in the (GSE171012) dataset, further highlights the complex and tissue-specific impact of IL-17 A blockade, with distinct effects on bone remodeling pathways in the skin and synovium.

In summary, IL-17 A inhibition differentially affects bone remodeling pathways in the skin and synovium, demonstrating a tissue-specific remodeling response, which is in contrast with the largely overlapping modulation of inflammatory pathways observed across both tissues.

Treatment-specificity: comparing IL-17 A blockade with IL-12p40/IL-23p40 Blockade in the pSpA synovium

Since IL-17 A production can occur independently of interleukin-23, in addition to the canonical IL-23/IL-17 A pathway, we aimed to distinguish the pathways modulated in pSpA synovitis by IL-17 inhibition versus IL-23 inhibition. To investigate this, we compared gene expression modulation after IL-17 A inhibition with that observed after treatment with IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade. For this, we used open-access gene expression data from another mechanism-of-action study in which RNA-sequencing was performed on paired synovial tissue samples from 7 PsA patients before and after 12 weeks of IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade [20]. We compared the changes in gene expression in whole synovial tissue after IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade with the changes observed after IL-17 A blockade in our cohort (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, we observed minimal overlap in the DEGs modulated after each treatment. Further analysis of these DEGs revealed that IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade primarily down regulated pathways associated with the positive regulation of mast cell chemotaxis (GO:0060754), cell-cell signaling (GO:007267) and positive regulation of angiogenesis (GO:0045766) (Fig. 4B). However, as indicated previously the blockade of IL17A resulted in the modulation of DEGs associated with both stromal and immune pathways. Moreover, upon performing a ssGSEA we identified differentially enriched pathways in the baseline sets of IL-17 A blockade and IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade samples. Although pathways were differentially enriched between these groups at baseline, only two immune- and stromal-related pathways—identified among the downregulated DEGs after IL-17 A blockade—were significantly upregulated in the IL-17Ai group compared with the IL-12p40/IL-23p40i group at baseline (Supplementary Excel file S1).

Comparison of gene expression data in the pSpA synovium after IL-17 A blockade or IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade. (A) A Venn Diagram represents the number of DEGs that were significantly upregulated and downregulated in the level of the joint upon the blockade of IL-17 A or IL-12p40/IL-23p40. (B) Dot plot of enriched GO terms downregulated in the joint of pSpA patients after the treatment of secukinumab or ustekinumab

In summary, although both treatments down-modulated inflammatory pathways, targeting IL-17 A suppressed multiple bone-remodeling-related pathways in the synovium, while IL-12p40/IL-23p40 blockade did not.

Discussion

In this study, we identified the molecular impact of IL-17 A blockade on various SpA tissues. We demonstrated that IL-17 A blockade using secukinumab down-modulated disease-relevant inflammatory- and stromal-related pathways, including multiple bone remodeling pathways in the inflamed SpA synovium. These effects on bone remodeling pathways upon secukinumab treatment are specific to the joint, as these pathways were modulated in the opposite direction in psoriatic skin. Interestingly, although ustekinumab treatment suppressed some inflammatory pathways in SpA synovium, no effect on bone remodeling pathways was observed.

In addition to the proven efficacy of secukinumab in reducing clinical inflammation, our mechanism of action study showed that secukinumab downregulated various inflammatory immune-mediated molecular pathways in the SpA synovium. The blockade of IL-17 A resulted in the downregulation of genes involved in the various inflammatory responses, such as neutrophil chemotaxis, neutrophil migration and leukocyte-mediated immunity. These results confirm previous findings showing significant decreases in IL-6, CCL20 mRNA levels and CD15 positive neutrophils after 12 weeks of IL-17 A blockade [16]. Similarly, in the HLAB27 transgenic (tg) animal model of spondyloarthritis, an experimental animal model with a disease phenotype resembling human SpA, we have previously demonstrated that the inhibition of IL-17 A resulted in the downregulation of genes involved in neutrophil chemotaxis and the regulation of chemokine secretion [13]. Neutrophils are increasingly recognized as pivotal cells in the immunopathology of SpA, as they are recruited to target disease sites and secrete cytokines and chemokines that amplify inflammation [21].

Clinically, it has been shown that around 79% of axSpA patients on secukinumab show a sustained absence of radiographic progression over four years of follow-up [22]. It is, however, still debated whether this is due to a direct effect on bone remodeling or whether it results from the downregulation of inflammation. In the HLA-B27 tg rat model of SpA, the inhibition of IL-17 A resulted in a significant reduction in axial and peripheral structural damage at the clinical, histological and molecular levels. Moreover, the demonstrated effects of IL-17 on new bone formation in osteogenic differentiation cultures of human fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) in vitro suggest a direct link [13]. Here, we demonstrate that the blockade of IL-17 A altered the expression of various genes involved in bone remodeling and stromal-related molecular pathways in synovial tissue of SpA patients, which was not observed after successful suppression of synovial inflammation by IL-12p40/Il-23p40 inhibition. In addition, we showed that the blockade of IL-17 A resulted in the suppression of the expression of MMP3 and MMP1, important players in the remodeling of the extracellular matrix, which is in line with previously demonstrated in vitro in the co-cultures of PsA FLS with CD4 + T cells treated with IL-17 A inhibitors [23].

Because the mechanism of action of IL-17 A in distinct target tissues remains poorly understood, we set out to further investigate the impact of IL-17 A blockade in two key sites: the joint and the skin. We observed that IL-17 A blockade regulated genes involved in inflammatory pathways in both the skin and the synovium. However, certain pathways were differentially modulated in the skin versus the joint, which might explain the known divergence in clinical response in skin and joints in clinical trials [24, 25]. This aligns with recent findings suggesting that the heterogeneity of specific inflammatory pathways, such as the IL-23 axis, in the synovium versus skin contributes to these variable clinical outcomes, even among clinically indistinguishable patients. For instance, Nerviani et al. demonstrated distinct gene expression clustering between synovium and paired skin, with the IL-23 axis showing highly heterogeneous expression in synovial tissues compared to the more homogeneous expression in lesional skin [26]. This mechanistic difference may underpin the varied therapeutic responses seen across different anatomical sites. Interestingly, the blockade of IL-17 A resulted in the downregulation of genes involved in collagen fibril organization, cartilage development and extracellular matrix organization in the synovium, while an upregulation of these genes was observed at the level of the skin. It has been previously shown that the pathological mechanisms involved in skin and synovial tissue inflammation in PsA are different [27]. Moreover, the tissue responses of epithelial and stromal cells are quite divergent and dependent on the surrounding environment. The location and nature of resident and inflammatory cells, as well as the expression patterns of cytokine receptors and cytokines, are essential factors contributing to tissue-specific responses [28]. In addition to that, the expression of IL-17 A receptors on various stromal cells is quite divergent [29].

In our study, we also set out to further distinguish the pathways that are modulated in pSpA synovitis by IL-17i versus IL-23i, given that the IL-23/IL-17 axis plays a vital role in the disease pathogenesis of SpA [30] and thus has evolved as a target of various therapeutic antibodies. Evidence suggests that the IL-23/IL-17 axis is not always involved in SpA pathogenesis in a linear way and IL-23-independent expression of IL-17 has been observed [31]. Also, clinically, there is a divergence in the response to IL-17Ai and IL-23i depending on the involved site in SpA. Our study demonstrates that targeting IL-17 A and IL-12p40/IL-23p40 both modulate inflammatory pathways, but blockade of IL-17 A specifically modulated stromal pathways whereas p40 blockade did not. Thus, further molecular studies are eagerly awaited and the outcomes of the ongoing head-to-head trial of secukinumab versus ustekinumab in PsA (ID NCT04632927) may provide a better insight into the differences between these two distinct treatment modalities.

Therefore, our study demonstrated that IL-17Ai and IL-12p40/IL-23p40i lead to distinct transcriptional changes, highlighting a potential divergence in their mechanisms of action. A key insight from our baseline enrichment analysis is the finding that there was no significant difference in the enrichment of immune and stromal pathways downregulated in response to IL-17 A blockade between the IL-17Ai and IL-12p40/IL-23p40i groups at baseline. This is crucial, as it suggests that the divergent post-treatment outcomes are not a result of pre-existing differences in the activity of these specific pathways. Instead, the distinct transcriptional responses likely reflect the unique and independent roles of the IL-17 and p40 axes in regulating these biological processes. The differences observed after blockade, therefore, directly highlight the specific downstream effects of targeting each pathway, even when the initial activity of the pathways appears similar.

A key limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size of the cohorts. This may limit the statistical power of our analysis and the generalizability of our results. We acknowledge that while our findings reveal important transcriptional differences, future studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate them and to more comprehensively explore the distinct mechanisms of these therapeutic blockades. Furthermore, due to the limited sample size of our cohort, performing a robust differential gene expression analysis comparing clinical responders versus non-responders was not feasible in this study. Additionally, because we performed bulk RNA sequencing analysis, we cannot identify the specific cells responsible for the observed changes in the synovium after treatment. Therefore, studies at the single-cell level, such as single-cell RNA seq or spatial transcriptomics, would be valuable to further delineate the contribution of specific cell types. When determining the effects of secukinumab on the skin versus the joint, we compared our dataset with publicly available data. Due to this approach, we could not match the patient groups for psoriasis severity, representing an inherent limitation when integrating data from independently generated studies. More severe skin involvement in the psoriasis cohort may have affected the results. In future research, studying paired skin and joint samples from the same PsA patients would be important to overcome this bias when investigating tissue-specific responses.

Conclusions

In summary, we have generated novel insights into the impact of IL-17 A blockade on the molecular pathways associated with pSpA. Blocking IL-17 A in SpA patients reduced inflammation, bone remodeling and stromal activation in the synovial tissue. Furthermore, we observed a differential impact of IL-17 A blockade on various SpA tissues (i.e. synovial tissue vs. skin) and identified differences between the effects of IL-17Ai and IL-12p40/IL23p40i on the inflamed synovial tissue. Consequently, our findings shed new light on various aspects of the therapeutic potential of IL-17 A blockade in pSpA and provide a better understanding of its mechanism of action. Additional molecular and spatial transcriptomics studies on various types of disease tissues will be important to further dissect the underlying mechanisms of action. Moreover, the anticipated results of head-to-head trials will be specifically instrumental in identifying the differential effects of IL-23 versus IL-17 A blockade on specific aspects of disease pathology.
