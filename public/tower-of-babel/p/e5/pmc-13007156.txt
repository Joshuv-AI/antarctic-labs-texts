Management of malignant pleural effusions using an indwelling tunnelled pleural catheter and non-vacuum collection system
Authors: Max T Wayne, Jennifer Massoni, Alexander Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndwelling tunnelled pleural catheters (TPCs) are a minimally invasive way to palliate symptoms for patients with recurrent symptomatic malignant pleural effusions. Current data on TPCs have used vacuum-assisted drainage systems. Gravity-based drainage systems may represent an alternative approach with improvement in patient symptoms, but have a smaller overall footprint and a decreased cost. We therefore aimed to understand the efficacy, complication rate and spontaneous pleurodesis rate of a TPC that uses a non-vacuum-assisted drainage system.MethodsThis is a prospective, observational study of 40 patients who underwent placement of a TPC with a non-vacuum-assisted drainage system for management of recurrent symptomatic malignant pleural effusions. The primary outcome of interest was dyspnoea as assessed by a 100 mm Visual Analogue Scale for Dyspnoea (VAS-Dyspnoea) at 2 weeks following insertion. Secondary outcomes included catheter-related complications and spontaneous pleurodesis. Patients were seen in follow-up at 2 weeks post-catheter placement and then every 6 weeks.ResultsAmong 40 patients who underwent TPC placement, the median age was 64 years, 65% were female, 80% were white and 20% were black. At baseline, participants had a median VAS-Dyspnoea score of 43 mm and at 2 weeks, participants (n=24) had a median VAS-Dyspnoea score of 16 mm (IQR: 6, 50) (p=0.07). A total of 19 (47.5%) patients experienced some form of TPC-related complication. A total of 15 (37.5%) achieved pleurodesis at a median of 55 days following catheter placement.ConclusionsIn this single-centre, prospective cohort of patients with recurrent symptomatic malignant pleural effusions, TPCs with a non-vacuum-assisted drainage showed a trend towards improvement in dyspnoea with complications and pleurodesis similar to vacuum-based TPC systems.Keywords: Lung Cancer, Pleural Disease, Small Cell Lung Cancer

Introduction

As many as 15% of patients with malignancy develop a malignant pleural effusion (MPE).1 The presence of an MPE usually reflects advanced malignancy with limited survival, typically on the order of months.2 For many of these patients, MPE results in significant symptom burden, including dyspnoea and cough that impairs quality of life and results in unscheduled office visits and emergency department encounters.1 3 4 Consequently, management of MPE is focused on palliation of symptoms.1 3 4 Treatments have traditionally consisted of intermittent thoracentesis or pleurodesis to palliate symptoms.5,8 However, these interventions require either frequent trips to the hospital or an extended hospitalisation, which may not be ideal in patients nearing the end of life.1 4

More recently, tunnelled pleural catheters (TPCs) have been introduced as a means to palliate dyspnoea associated with MPE in a minimally invasive way that does not require repeated procedures or an inpatient hospitalisation.9,14 These systems consist of a 15.5 Fr flexible catheter that is placed into the affected pleural space with an external portion containing a one-way valve that prevents leakage of pleural fluid when the catheter is not connected to a drainage system. Palliation of dyspnoea using indwelling TPCs has been reported in greater than 90% of patients undergoing procedures. Additionally, spontaneous pleurodesis has been reported to occur in 30%–50% of patients undergoing TPC placement and infectious complications are rare.9,14 Yet, all data on palliation of dyspnoea as well as on rates of spontaneous pleurodesis have been performed using indwelling TPCs with vacuum-assisted drainage systems.9,14 Non-vacuum collection systems, relying on gravity drainage as well as a hand-pump mechanism, are now available and may have the added benefits of improved patient comfort, smaller overall footprint and decreased cost to patients.15 16 However, it is currently unclear on the efficacy, complications or rate of spontaneous pleurodesis when using indwelling TPCs with non-vacuum-assisted drainage systems.

We therefore performed a prospective, observational study of patients who underwent placement of an indwelling TPC with a non-vacuum-assisted drainage system for management of symptomatic MPEs. We aimed to understand the efficacy, complications and rate of spontaneous pleurodesis in patients using this system as part of their clinical care.

Methods

Population

Patients presenting to the Interventional Pulmonology service at Washington University in St. Louis School of Medicine with symptomatic, recurrent MPE were screened for this study.

Inclusion criteria consisted of: (1) ≥18 years of age; (2) MPE as confirmed by positive cytology or exudative (per Light’s criteria: pleural fluid protein/serum protein >0.5, pleural fluid lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)/serum LDH >0.6 or pleural fluid LDH >2/3 the upper limit of normal) in the context of histocytologically proven malignancy elsewhere, with no other clear cause for fluid identified; (3) symptomatic relief with prior thoracentesis; (4) able to provide informed consent; (5) sufficient fluid to allow for safe insertion as determined by primary investigator (PI) based on ultrasound evaluation; (6) patient able to perform home drainage or has sufficient resources (family member, caregiver, home health) to care for drain. Patients with the following criteria were excluded: (1) active pregnancy; (2) evidence of ongoing systemic or pleural infection as determined by PI; (3) uncorrected coagulopathy.

A total of 40 patients were targeted for enrolment in this pilot study. Written informed consent was obtained prior to enrolment in a private environment (eg, patient examination room) free from distractions. The PI approached the subject at their standard of care clinic appointment or prior to their scheduled pleural drainage procedure. Interviews to obtain consent did not follow any stressful situation (eg, patient being informed he/she may have cancer) and were not conducted if the patient had received any mind-altering medications or anaesthesia. Patients were assessed for their capacity to consent by the ability to show comprehension of the procedure, ask appropriate questions and appear properly oriented. Participants were enrolled between 1 April 2019 and 30 September 2023. It was not possible to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Catheter insertion

Prior to catheter insertion, baseline dyspnoea was assessed using the 100 mm Visual Analogue Scale for Dyspnoea (VAS-Dyspnoea), where a score of 0 indicates no dyspnoea and a score of 100 indicates the maximal dyspnoea imaginable. The patients then underwent insertion of the 15.5 Fr Aspira TPC (Aspira, Merit Medical, South Jordan, Utah, USA).16 Catheter placement was performed in a dedicated endoscopy unit by an experienced provider. The procedure was performed in a sterile fashion. Patients did not receive perioperative antibiotics. Moderate sedation was available for administration at the discretion of the provider. The effusion was drained immediately following catheter insertion and then a routine chest X-ray was obtained per standard of care. Patients were discharged home the same day if the catheter was placed as an outpatient or returned to the hospital ward for treatment of any other medical issues if placed as an inpatient. Caregivers received catheter teaching and instructions at the time of the procedure.

Patients were instructed to drain catheters three times weekly up to 1 L per session and as needed for palliation of dyspnoea. Patients were instructed to record drainage amounts and corresponding drainage dates in the patient guide booklet, included in information sent home with the patient supplies on catheter insertion day.

Outcomes and follow-up

The primary outcome was dyspnoea as assessed by the VAS-Dyspnoea score at 2 weeks following catheter insertion. This was chosen as it is a validated measure to assess patient symptoms and the catheter is intended only for palliation of symptoms, with a score of 100 indicating the worst breathlessness imaginable and a score of 0 indicating no breathlessness.911 17,19 VAS-Dyspnoea score at 2 weeks was selected as the primary outcome because by nature of the underlying disease, the targeted patient population will have many other competing problems that may influence their VAS score (eg, chemotherapy, lymphangitic spread of malignancy, progression of disease) the further they get from catheter placement.

Secondary outcomes were procedural complications, catheter-related complications, catheter-related infections and spontaneous pleurodesis. Procedural complications included unsuccessful catheter placement, haemothorax or respiratory failure. Catheter-related complication was defined as any catheter migration requiring intervention, catheter occlusion requiring intervention, valve malfunction requiring intervention and retained cuff requiring dissection. Catheter-related infections were defined as either a TPC-related cellulitis (presence of infection at the catheter site or tunnel tract) or a TPC-related pleural space infection (defined as purulent fluid from the pleural space or Gram stain or culture-positive pleural fluid with clinical signs of infection).20 Spontaneous pleurodesis was defined as less than 75 mL of output from the catheter on three consecutive drainages, with ultrasound evaluation of the thorax confirming no effusion.

Patients were seen in follow-up 2 weeks following catheter insertion and then every 6 weeks until catheter removal or death.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe baseline demographics, type of malignancy, procedural details and complications. Discrete variables were described by number and percentage, while continuous variables were described by median and IQR. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA/MP V.18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). We used a Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test to compare VAS-Dyspnoea scores prior to TPC insertion and at 2 weeks, given the non-normal distribution of values. We considered p<0.05 (two-sided) to be significant.

Results

A total of 41 patients were consented and 40 proceeded with catheter placement (figure 1). The median age was 64 years (IQR: 54, 73), 26 (65%) of patients were female, 32 (80%) were white and 8 (20%) were black (table 1). The most common malignancies were lung cancer in 19 patients (48.7%), breast cancer in 9 patients (23.1%), other malignancies (which included oesophageal cancer, leiomyosarcoma, lymphoma, ovarian cancer, prostate cancer, T cell leukaemia and thymic cancer) in 9 patients (23.1%) and melanoma in 3 patients (7.7%). Among patients, 22 (55.0%) catheters were placed on the left side and 18 (45.0%) were placed on the right side, with a median of 1130 mL (IQR: 787, 1666) of fluid drained at the time of catheter insertion. 13 patients (32.5%) had the catheter placed during an inpatient hospitalisation. 18 (45.0%) had the catheter placed with local anaesthetic only, 9 (22.5%) received analgesia in addition to local anaesthetic and 13 (32.5%) received moderate sedation in addition to local anaesthetic for catheter placement. Among patients who received moderate sedation, the median patient received 75 µg (IQR: 50, 100) of fentanyl and 3 mg (IQR: 2, 4) of midazolam.

At baseline, participants (n=40) had a median VAS-Dyspnoea score of 43 mm (IQR: 24, 58) and at 2 weeks, participants (n=24) had a median VAS-Dyspnoea score of 16 mm (IQR: 6, 50) (table 2, figure 2). Among the 16 patients who did not have a VAS-Dyspnoea score at 2 weeks, 6 patients were hospitalised at the 2-week time-point, all for reasons not related to their catheter and an additional 2 patients had died prior to the 2-week mark. While there was a trend towards improved VAS-Dyspnoea scores at 2 weeks, the difference between the two time-points was not statistically significant (p=0.07). Among patients who completed a 2-week VAS-Dyspnoea score compared with patients who did not have this obtained, there was no difference in patient age (61 vs 66, p=0.39), baseline VAS-score (40 vs 43, p=0.41) but a greater proportion of patients missing the 2 week VAS-Dyspnoea score died with the catheter in place (62.5% vs 20.8%) (online supplemental table 1).

No complications occurred at the time of catheter insertion (including no episodes of unsuccessful catheter placement, haemothorax or immediate post-procedural respiratory failure) (table 2). All patients receiving the catheter as an outpatient were discharged home the same day.

Among all patients, a total of 19 (47.5%) experienced some form of catheter-related complication (table 2). No patient experienced a catheter migration. Nine (22.5%) patients required the catheter valve to be replaced; one of whom self-removed the valve to drain the catheter, six patients had the catheter accessed with a different brand drainage system by healthcare providers, resulting in catheter valve malfunction, and for two patients, the reason for malfunction is missing. A total of 8 patients (20.0%) experienced catheter occlusion requiring instillation of TPA, 3 (7.5%) of the catheter occlusions occurred within 90 days of catheter placement. Among all patients who developed catheter occlusion, TPA was successful in 7 out of 8 (87.5%) to restore catheter patency. 3 (7.5%) patients had the cuff fracture at the time of catheter removal, for which additional incision/dissection was performed to fully remove the cuff. A total of 6 (15.0%) patients experienced a catheter-related infection. Among these patients, 3 (7.5%) patients received antibiotics for TPC-related cellulitis and 3 (7.5%) patients experienced a pleural space infection. All cases of local TPC-related infection were successfully treated with antibiotics alone, as determined on follow-up, showing resolution. Among the three patients with pleural space infection, one patient was successfully treated with antibiotics, with repeat culture confirming resolution of infection, one patient had catheter removal with placement of a new chest tube to clear the infection, and the third patient was treated with antibiotics and the catheter was ultimately removed (without need for an additional chest tube). No patient died from a catheter-related infection.

Among all participants, 15 (37.5%) achieved pleurodesis, 5 (12.5%) patients were lost to follow-up and 22 (55.0%) of patients did not achieve pleurodesis (figure 3). Among patients who achieved pleurodesis, this occurred at a median of 55 days following catheter insertion (IQR: 24, 103). A total of 15 patients (37.5%) died with the catheter in place. Among the 35 patients not lost to follow-up, the TPC remained in place (either catheter removed or patient passed away) for a median of 89 days (IQR: 21, 153).

Discussion

In this single-centre prospective, observational cohort study of patients with recurrent symptomatic MPEs who underwent placement of an indwelling TPC with a non-vacuum-assisted drainage system, there was a trend towards symptomatic improvement (as assessed by the VAS-Dyspnoea score). While catheter-related complications occurred in over 40%, the vast majority of these were mild and did not require hospitalisation.

While our primary outcome of change in VAS-Dyspnoea score was not statistically significant, there was a clinically meaningful trend toward improvement.17 The VAS-Dyspnoea scores in our cohort and the improvement seen following catheter placement are both consistent with prior clinical studies comparing TPC to chemical pleurodesis, where patients had a 14 point improvement at 1 month following catheter insertion and a 12 point improvement in VAS-Dyspnoea score at 6 weeks following catheter placement.9 11 Likewise, the improvement in our cohort is consistent with changes described following draining of symptomatic MPEs with simple thoracentesis.21

While nearly half of the patients in our cohort experienced some form of catheter-related complication, this is also in line with what has been described in the literature. For example, in the The Second Therapeutic Intervention in Malignant Effusion Trial (TIME2) comparing TPC to talc pleurodesis, 40% of patients with a TPC had a catheter-related complication, 13% developed a pleural space infection and 12% developed cellulitis (compared with 48%, 7.5% and 7.5%, respectively, in our cohort).9 In the The Australasian Malignant Pleural Effusion (AMPLE) trial comparing TPC to talc pleurodesis, the overall complication rate was 30%. While overall and infectious complications were lower in this study with TPC than what we found (3% pleural space infection, 5% cellulitis), patients were followed until catheter removal or 1 year, which may explain the lower rates (in our cohort, multiple patients had complications after 1 year).11 In a similar vein, we found an overall higher rate of catheter-related occlusion than has been previously reported. However, when we assess our outcome at a similar time point to that in the Outpatient Talc Administration by Indwelling Pleural Catheter for Malignant Effusion (IPC-Plus) or Randomized Trial of Pleural Fluid Drainage Frequency in Patients with Malignant Pleural Effusions (ASAP) trials, our rate of catheter occlusion was quite similar (7.5% vs 4%–5%).22 23 Likewise, our rate of infectious complications is in line with other reports.911 23,28

In our cohort, just under 40% of patients achieved pleurodesis. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report pleurodesis from non-vacuum drainage TPC. The rate of pleurodesis with the non-vacuum drainage system in our study is consistent with vacuum drainage systems, with estimates ranging from 24% to 51%.9 11 13 23 While our cohort had a slightly lower incidence of autopleurodesis compared with a daily drainage schedule, it was almost double that of a three times per week drainage schedule using a vacuum-based platform.23 Additionally, pleurodesis in our cohort occurred at a similar time frame to patients treated with an aggressive drainage schedule (median time to pleurodesis of 54 days vs 55 days in our cohort).23

An unexpected finding in our cohort was the occurrence of a cuff fracture at the time of catheter removal. This occurred in 3 (7.5%) patients in our cohort, for whom an additional incision was performed to remove the retained cuff. This has previously been described in a cohort of patients who underwent TPC removal at Swedish Medical Center, where removal of 6 out of 122 (4.9%) TPCs was complicated by a retained granulation cuff.29 All of these occurred with a vacuum-assisted pleural drainage catheter—PleurX Pleural Catheters (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA). In that cohort, the retained cuffs were not removed and no long-term adverse outcomes were identified. A separate group reported catheter fracture occurring at the time of catheter removal in just under 10% of patients who had a different vacuum assisted pleural drainage catheter system, Rocket TPC (Rocket Medical, Watford, UK).30 In these patients, the retained catheter was left behind without adverse event, although several were treated with prophylactic antibiotics. Given concern for a retained foreign body, we elected to proceed with an additional incision to remove this retained cuff. However, the optimal management for this complication is unclear.

While our study had a number of strengths, including the prospective design with prespecified and defined outcomes, there are also several limitations. First, this was a single-centre study with a relatively small sample size. We tried to enrol a diverse cohort of patients, including a variety of malignancies, to mitigate this. Second, a sizeable portion of patients did not complete the VAS-Dyspnoea score at 2 weeks. This may explain why our primary outcome was not statistically significant in spite of the large numeric difference. This lack of 2-week follow-up may not be surprising given the patient population with numerous competing comorbidities, but it is consistent with other literature in this patient population, where follow-up is challenging due to competing comorbidities.23 31 However, we were able to obtain long-term follow-up for 88% of patients in this cohort. Third, the procedures were performed at a high-volume centre with experienced proceduralists and extensive experience with TPCs, which may limit generalisability. Fourth, we do not have data on patient discomfort with drainage or time spent on drainage. Prospective randomised trials directly comparing vacuum-assisted drainage systems to non-vacuum-assisted drainage systems would be performed to assess these endpoints.32 33

In this single-centre, prospective cohort of patients with recurrent symptomatic MPEs, an indwelling TPC with a non-vacuum-assisted drainage system showed clinical outcomes comparable to those of TPCs with a vacuum-assisted drainage system.
