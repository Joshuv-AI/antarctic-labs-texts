Neurosyphilis with simultaneous brain and spinal cord involvement mimicking intracranial tumors: a case report
Authors: Dong Bai, Zhiqun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeurosyphilis is a heterogeneous manifestation of Treponema pallidum infection with diverse clinical and radiological presentations. Concurrent involvement of the brain and spinal cord is rare and may mimic intracranial neoplasms, leading to diagnostic uncertainty.Case presentationA 48-year-old woman presented with acute-onset limb numbness and diplopia. Brain and cervical spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed multiple nodular and ring-enhancing lesions involving the brain parenchyma and cervical spinal cord, initially raising suspicion of lymphoma or metastatic tumors. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) showed atypical hypointensity without diffusion restriction. Histopathological examination demonstrated perivascular inflammatory infiltration without evidence of malignancy. Serological testing confirmed a reactive serum rapid plasma reagin (RPR) titer with treponemal confirmation, and metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) detected Treponema pallidum deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), supporting the diagnosis of neurosyphilis. HIV serology was negative. Due to a documented penicillin allergy (positive penicillin skin test), the patient received alternative antimicrobial therapy with fosfomycin (8 g/day intravenously) and minocycline (100 mg orally twice daily), resulting in marked clinical improvement and regression of lesions on follow-up imaging.ConclusionThis report describes a patient in whom neurosyphilis presented with concurrent brain and spinal cord lesions that radiologically resembled malignant central nervous system (CNS) tumors. Multifocal nodular and ring-enhancing lesions without diffusion restriction, combined with clinical improvement following antimicrobial therapy, were features observed in this case that contributed to the diagnostic re-evaluation. This case highlights the importance of including neurosyphilis in the differential diagnosis of tumor-like CNS lesions when imaging findings are atypical, while acknowledging that definitive diagnostic conclusions are limited by the single-case nature of this report.

Introduction

Neurosyphilis, caused by central nervous system (CNS) invasion by Treponema pallidum, can manifest in a wide variety of neurological syndromes, including meningitis, meningovascular disease, and parenchymal involvement [1, 2]. The incidence of syphilis has been rising globally in recent decades, and atypical presentations of neurosyphilis — particularly those mimicking space-occupying neoplasms — represent an underrecognized diagnostic challenge [1, 3]. Most reported cases of neurosyphilis involve the brain parenchyma alone, and simultaneous involvement of both the brain and spinal cord presenting as tumor-like enhancing lesions is infrequently described in the literature [1, 3–5].

When CNS lesions demonstrate nodular or ring-like enhancement on MRI, the differential diagnosis typically prioritizes neoplastic conditions such as primary CNS lymphoma, brain metastases, or glial tumors. In such cases, neurosyphilis may not be immediately considered, leading to diagnostic delays and potentially unnecessary invasive procedures [4, 5]. The present case illustrates this diagnostic dilemma and demonstrates how integration of clinical, serological, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), histopathological, and molecular findings — including mNGS — can support a definitive diagnosis in atypical presentations. This report also highlights management considerations in the setting of penicillin allergy, where evidence-based alternatives are limited.

Case presentation

Demographics and presenting complaint

A 48-year-old Han Chinese woman with no prior neurological history was admitted on February 9, 2021, following a one-week history of sudden-onset bilateral limb numbness and binocular diplopia. She had no known malignancy, no history of immunosuppressive therapy, and no prior diagnosis of syphilis or sexually transmitted infection. Epidemiological history including sexual contacts and risk exposures was documented per institutional protocol; details are withheld to protect patient confidentiality. A prior penicillin allergy had been documented in her medical records, identified by a weakly positive penicillin skin test performed prior to treatment planning.

Clinical examination

Neurological examination revealed binocular diplopia. Muscle strength was normal in all four limbs (Medical Research Council grade 5/5 bilaterally), and no pathological reflexes were elicited. General physical examination was unremarkable.

Imaging

Brain MRI demonstrated multiple lesions affecting the pons, basal ganglia, and frontotemporal lobes. These lesions appeared hypointense on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), with nodular hypointense foci. Post-contrast images demonstrated nodular and ring-like enhancement, while DWI showed hypointensity without diffusion restriction. Cervical MRI revealed similar lesions involving the spinal cord and meninges (Fig. 1). Based on these imaging findings, lymphoma and metastatic disease were initially considered.

Laboratory and CSF results

On initial pre-operative screening (February 10, 2021), serum treponemal antibody was reactive (8.97 COI). Following discharge and readmission on March 3, 2021, lumbar puncture was performed on March 4, 2021. CSF analysis demonstrated mild pleocytosis (white blood cell count 39/µL, predominantly lymphocytes at 98%; reference range < 5/µL), elevated protein at 98.5 mg/dL (reference range 15–45 mg/dL), and glucose 2.88 mmol/L. CSF Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) test was reactive. Serum rapid plasma reagin (RPR) was positive at a titer of 1:32, confirmed by treponemal-specific antibody (TP-Ab) of 34.99 S/CO. CSF Treponema pallidum particle agglutination (TPPA) assay was reactive, supporting intrathecal infection. HIV-1/2 antibody/antigen serology was non-reactive. Routine blood counts, renal and hepatic function panels, and tumor markers (including CEA, AFP, CA-125, and CA19-9) were within normal limits.

Histopathology

To exclude malignancy, a stereotactic brain biopsy was performed on day 2. Histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed glial cell hyperplasia with prominent perivascular lymphocytic infiltration, hypertrophy of small arterial endothelial cells, intimal fibrosis, vessel wall thickening, and luminal stenosis — changes consistent with syphilitic vasculitis. No evidence of neoplastic transformation was identified (Fig. 2). At that time, infectious etiologies had not been strongly suspected based on imaging alone.

mNGS results and confirmatory testing

CSF mNGS was performed following lumbar puncture on March 4, 2021 (during the second admission) at a certified clinical laboratory using the BGISEQ-500 sequencing platform (BGI Genomics, Shenzhen, China). Library preparation targeted both DNA and RNA. Bioinformatic analysis identified Treponema pallidum with 38 specific sequence reads, exceeding the laboratory reporting threshold (≥ 3 unique reads for bacterial pathogens). Additionally, varicella-zoster virus was detected, consistent with concurrent herpes zoster. Confirmatory syphilis testing included positive rapid plasma reagin (RPR) test on CSF and treponemal-specific antibody (TP-Ab 34.99 S/CO), with results reported on March 8, 2021. Targeted PCR for T. pallidum was not separately performed, as CSF mNGS detection combined with reactive serology and CSF-VDRL was considered sufficient for diagnosis per institutional protocol. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for T. pallidum on biopsy tissue was not performed. Taken together, these findings — reactive serological testing, reactive CSF-VDRL, positive mNGS, and histopathology consistent with syphilitic vasculitis — supported the diagnosis of neurosyphilis.

Treatment and rationale

The patient had a documented penicillin allergy, identified by a weakly positive penicillin skin test conducted prior to treatment. Aqueous crystalline penicillin G (24 million units/day IV), the guideline-recommended first-line agent for neurosyphilis, was therefore contraindicated. Penicillin desensitization was discussed with the patient and the dermatology specialist; however, the patient declined this option. Given the strong pre-operative serological evidence of syphilis (reactive treponemal antibody 8.97 COI on February 10, 2021) and the urgent clinical picture, empirical antimicrobial therapy was initiated on Day 2 of the first admission, prior to completion of the full CSF and mNGS workup. Per the 2021 CDC guidelines, ceftriaxone (1–2 g IV daily for 10–14 days) is the only currently cited alternative to penicillin for neurosyphilis [6, 7]; however, given the patient’s documented allergy to penicillin and the theoretical risk of beta-lactam cross-reactivity, ceftriaxone was also considered unsafe by the treating team without formal allergy evaluation, which was not available in time. The clinical team therefore selected a combination regimen of fosfomycin and minocycline based on available case-level evidence and the patient’s clinical profile [6, 8]. The patient received fosfomycin sodium 8 g/day intravenously and minocycline 100 mg orally twice daily, initiated on February 10, 2021 (day 2 of first admission) and continued for the inpatient course. Fosfomycin was selected on the basis of in vitro activity against T. pallidum and limited published reports of its use in penicillin-allergic neurosyphilis patients [6, 8]. Minocycline was selected for its documented CSF penetration and reported use as an alternative agent in neurosyphilis [8, 9]. Supportive therapy included dexamethasone intravenously (tapered over 14 days) and mannitol 250 mL IV every 8 h for cerebral edema management. During the second admission (March 3–18, 2021), acyclovir 0.5 g every 8 h IV was added for concurrent herpes zoster, and pantoprazole sodium was given for gastric protection.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient’s neurological symptoms gradually improved; diplopia had resolved and limb numbness was significantly reduced by day 15. Follow-up MRI at day 12 demonstrated partial regression of lesions. Repeat MRI at day 40 showed marked regression of both intracranial and spinal lesions (Fig. 3). Serum RPR titer declined to 1:8 at the 3-month follow-up, indicating a serological treatment response. Follow-up MRI and clinical assessment at 6 months confirmed sustained remission with no evidence of clinical or radiological recurrence; serum RPR titer at 6 months was 1:4, consistent with ongoing serological improvement(Table 1).

Discussion

Uniqueness of multi-neuroanatomical involvement

Neurosyphilis is known for its heterogeneous clinical and radiological manifestations, which can complicate diagnosis, particularly when imaging findings are atypical. Most reported cases primarily involve the brain parenchyma, whereas spinal cord involvement is uncommon, and simultaneous involvement of the brain, meninges, and spinal cord presenting as tumor-like nodular and ring-enhancing lesions is infrequently reported in the literature [1, 3, 4]. In this patient, the simultaneous involvement of the brain parenchyma, meninges, and spinal cord, together with tumor-like enhancement patterns, led to significant diagnostic uncertainty. A systematic review of 7,486 Chinese neurosyphilis cases found that cerebral parenchymal syphilis accounted for 57.3% of presentations, initial diagnoses were incorrect in over 53% of cases, and spinal manifestations remained rare [1]. Syphilitic myelitis is estimated to represent fewer than 3% of all neurosyphilis cases, and published reports of syphilitic meningomyelitis masquerading as spinal neoplasm are limited to isolated case reports and small series [4, 5]. While individual reports of spinal cord involvement or intracranial tumor-mimicking lesions exist [4, 5], the concurrent presentation of both patterns in a single HIV-negative patient, as observed here, constitutes an exceptionally challenging and uncommonly described clinical scenario with a high risk of diagnostic delay and unnecessary surgical intervention.

The underlying mechanisms of such multi-neuroanatomical involvement remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have suggested that Treponema pallidum may disrupt endothelial integrity and facilitate CNS invasion [2]. Recent molecular studies have identified specific treponemal adhesins (including TP0751 and TP0136) that interact with endothelial receptors, alter tight junction proteins, and promote blood-brain barrier permeability, providing mechanistic insight into why multifocal CNS involvement can occur [2]. Spinal lesions may arise secondary to syphilitic vasculitis, ischemic injury, or granulomatous inflammation, similar to pathological processes described in intracranial neurosyphilis [2, 3]. Specific MRI patterns in syphilitic myelitis — including subpial “candle guttering” enhancement and the “flip-flop sign” of T2/T1 enhancement inversion — suggest that pathological invasion may occur from the cord surface inward, consistent with meningeal seeding preceding parenchymal involvement [5]. These mechanisms may partially explain the extensive distribution of lesions observed in this patient, although definitive mechanistic conclusions cannot be drawn from a single case.

Imaging features and differential diagnosis

The radiological presentation posed a significant diagnostic challenge. Multiple nodular and ring-enhancing lesions involving both intracranial and spinal regions initially raised concern for lymphoma or metastatic disease. In this case, DWI demonstrated hypointensity without diffusion restriction, a pattern less typical of CNS lymphoma, which characteristically shows restricted diffusion due to high nuclear-to-cytoplasm ratios and densely packed tumor cells [10]. Corrêa et al. reviewed the full spectrum of MRI features in neurosyphilis and noted that enhancing nodular or gummatous lesions can closely simulate high-grade neoplasms on conventional sequences, and that the absence of diffusion restriction may represent a useful though non-specific discriminating feature in this context [10]. While this finding supported a non-neoplastic process in the context of this case, it must be interpreted as one contributing observation rather than a definitive exclusionary criterion, particularly given the documented variability of neurosyphilis imaging appearances.

Syphilitic vasculitis may result in ischemic or inflammatory changes, which can present as nodular or ring-enhancing lesions, including involvement of the spinal cord [4]. The combination of ring-like enhancement and DWI hypointensity may reflect inflammatory or granulomatous processes rather than neoplastic disease. Shang et al. observed in a cohort of 102 neurosyphilis patients that neuroimaging features were highly variable and did not reliably distinguish symptomatic from asymptomatic cases, and that some patients demonstrated radiological progression despite treatment, underscoring the difficulty of using imaging alone for prognostication [11]. The exact pathological correlates of these imaging features are not fully understood, and interpretation in isolation should be approached cautiously. Awareness of this pattern may nonetheless prompt consideration of infectious etiologies in the differential diagnosis of atypical CNS lesions, particularly in regions with rising syphilis incidence.

Adjunctive diagnostic role of metagenomic next-generation sequencing

The diagnosis of neurosyphilis traditionally relies on clinical features, serological testing (including serum nontreponemal titers confirmed by treponemal assays), and CSF parameters (cell count, protein, and CSF-VDRL), although these methods may yield inconclusive results in atypical cases [6, 7]. In the present case, mNGS detected Treponema pallidum DNA in CSF and served as an adjunctive diagnostic tool when interpreted in conjunction with serological, imaging, and histopathological findings. The diagnostic sensitivity of conventional CSF tests for neurosyphilis is limited: CSF-VDRL, while highly specific, has a sensitivity of only approximately 30–70%, and treponemal tests are sensitive but non-specific [12]. The TPPA assay, evaluated by Marra et al. in CSF, demonstrated higher sensitivity than CSF-VDRL but cannot reliably distinguish intrathecal from serum antibody transfer [12]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of mNGS for CNS infections reported a pooled sensitivity of 57.1% and specificity of 97.7%, with mNGS providing pathogen identification in cases where conventional tests were negative or inconclusive [13]. However, false-negative mNGS results for Treponema pallidum have been documented, potentially due to the organism’s low DNA burden in CSF and susceptibility to nucleic acid degradation [14]. Accordingly, mNGS is best regarded as a complementary rather than replacement tool for conventional serology and CSF analysis in neurosyphilis. Rather than serving as a standalone diagnostic test, mNGS may be particularly useful in selected cases with non-specific clinical or radiological manifestations [14]. This case supports the potential role of mNGS as a complementary method to increase diagnostic confidence in challenging presentations of CNS infection. Importantly, mNGS also identified concurrent varicella-zoster virus (VZV), leading to targeted antiviral therapy with acyclovir. It must be acknowledged that VZV co-infection could theoretically contribute to CNS inflammation and imaging abnormalities; however, the combination of reactive treponemal serology, CSF-VDRL reactivity, mNGS detection of T. pallidum, and histopathology demonstrating syphilitic vasculitis collectively supports neurosyphilis as the primary pathological process. The relative contributions of VZV and T. pallidum to the overall clinical and radiological picture cannot be definitively disentangled from a single case, and this represents an additional interpretive limitation.

Treatment considerations

Penicillin remains the first-line therapy for neurosyphilis; however, management can be challenging in patients with penicillin allergy. The 2021 CDC Sexually Transmitted Infections Treatment Guidelines recommend aqueous crystalline penicillin G (18–24 million units/day IV for 10–14 days) as the standard regimen, with ceftriaxone (1–2 g IV/IM daily for 10–14 days) as the only currently cited alternative; no other regimen has been adequately evaluated for neurosyphilis [6, 7]. Penicillin desensitization is specifically recommended when alternative agents are insufficient or cross-allergy concerns preclude ceftriaxone use [7]. A large retrospective multicentre study by Bettuzzi et al. comparing ceftriaxone with benzylpenicillin in 208 neurosyphilis patients found similar clinical response rates after propensity weighting, suggesting ceftriaxone may represent a viable alternative in selected patients without cross-allergy; however, the authors themselves emphasized that randomised controlled trial data remain lacking [6].

In this case, penicillin was contraindicated due to a positive penicillin skin test, and desensitization was declined by the patient. Per the 2021 CDC guidelines, ceftriaxone (1–2 g IV daily for 10–14 days) is the only currently cited alternative to penicillin for neurosyphilis [6, 7]; ceftriaxone was also deemed potentially unsafe in this case due to theoretical beta-lactam cross-reactivity in the setting of a documented penicillin allergy, in the absence of available formal allergy evaluation. The clinical team therefore selected a combination regimen of fosfomycin and minocycline based on available case-level evidence and the patient’s clinical profile. Fosfomycin has demonstrated in vitro activity against Treponema pallidum and has been used in combination regimens for neurosyphilis in penicillin-allergic patients, though evidence from large clinical trials is limited [6, 8]. Shao et al. reported a randomized study demonstrating minocycline efficacy in early syphilis comparable to tetracycline, with minocycline’s favourable CNS penetration making it a candidate for neurosyphilis in non-penicillin settings [8]. A network meta-analysis of syphilis treatment regimens similarly identified tetracycline-class agents as alternatives with modest supporting evidence [9]. It must be emphasized that none of these data specifically validate fosfomycin or minocycline for neurosyphilis, and this report should not be interpreted as endorsing this regimen as a standard alternative to guideline-recommended therapy. The concurrent use of dexamethasone may have contributed to early symptomatic improvement; however, the subsequent radiological regression observed at follow-up MRI is consistent with a response to targeted antimicrobial therapy rather than immunosuppression alone. No conclusions regarding comparative efficacy can be drawn from a single case.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a single-patient case report, no generalizable conclusions can be drawn regarding diagnosis, imaging characteristics, or treatment outcomes. Second, the follow-up period of 6 months may be insufficient to exclude late recurrence or treatment failure. Third, direct pathological confirmation of Treponema pallidum in tissue specimens was not available; IHC and targeted PCR were not performed on biopsy tissue. The diagnosis rested on serological, CSF, mNGS, and histopathological data, the latter showing changes consistent with but not exclusive to syphilitic infection. Fourth, the concurrent use of corticosteroids limits attribution of early clinical improvement solely to antimicrobial therapy. Fifth, concurrent VZV co-infection detected by mNGS introduces a potential diagnostic confounder; the independent contributions of VZV and T. pallidum to CNS inflammation and the imaging phenotype cannot be separated in this report. Sixth, the stereotactic brain biopsy was performed prior to lumbar puncture, as infectious etiologies had not been strongly suspected based on imaging and initial serology at that stage; retrospectively, earlier CSF sampling before surgical intervention would have been preferable and may have expedited the diagnosis.

Conclusion

This case describes a patient with neurosyphilis who presented with simultaneous brain and spinal cord involvement producing radiological features resembling intracranial tumors. The diagnostic process, which ultimately integrated clinical, serological, CSF, histopathological, and mNGS findings, illustrates the importance of considering infectious etiologies — including neurosyphilis — in the evaluation of atypical tumor-like CNS lesions. These observations are limited to a single case and should not be generalized. Future multi-case studies are needed to better characterize the imaging spectrum, optimal diagnostic workup, and treatment strategies for this unusual presentation of neurosyphilis.
