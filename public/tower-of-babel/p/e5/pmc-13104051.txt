Options for retrieval of a fractured catheter include balloon traction, snare, and surgery. Few studies have reported the successful removal of a fractured catheter with balloon traction.2,3 However, we note that it is difficult to pass the guidewire through the floating broken catheter accurately, which significantly decreases the overall retrieval success rate with balloon traction. The present study describes a novel double-guidewire-assisted balloon traction approach for retrieving a fractured catheter in a small arterial branch, providing a feasible alternative approach.

Case Presentation

A 36-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital due to cervical pregnancy and underwent uterine artery embolization. First, right femoral arterial access was obtained using a 5-F, 10-cm-long sheath (Terumo Corporation). Then a 5-F Slip-Cath Beacon Tip catheter (Cook Medical) was sequentially passed through the right femoral artery, right common iliac artery, and located in the right internal iliac artery. Subsequent angiography showed the uterine artery. Then, the FINECROSS Micro-Guide catheter (Terumo Corporation) was advanced into the right uterine artery, through which gelatin sponge particles were injected. Angiography confirmed the successful embolization of the right uterine artery. Then the 5-F Slip-Cath Beacon Tip catheter (Cook Medical) was passed through the right femoral artery, right common iliac artery, abdominal aorta, left common iliac artery, and located in the left internal iliac artery. Unexpectedly, the catheter tip fractured and migrated to the left internal iliac artery branch (Figures 1A and 1B). We observed that the arterial branch was too thin to be removed with a snare. Moreover, surgical removal could cause more trauma to the patient. We observed that the longitudinal axis of the catheter was parallel to the artery, facilitating the insertion of the guidewire. Therefore, we decided to perform balloon-assisted traction to remove the fractured catheter. A 0.014-inch floppy guidewire (Abbott Vascular) was advanced through the 5-F Vistabrite guiding catheter (Cordis Corporation), and then we tried to pass it through the lumen of the broken catheter fragment. But it was difficult to advance the soft guidewire through the free-floating fractured catheter accurately (Figure 1C). Therefore, we proposed double-guidewire-assisted balloon traction. First, under fluoroscopy, we advanced a 0.014-inch floppy guidewire (Abbott Vascular) to pass through the gap between the fractured catheter and the vessel wall to induce vasospasm, thereby stabilizing the free-floating broken catheter. Then, the other 0.014-inch floppy guidewire (Abbott Vascular) was advanced through the fractured catheter to reach its distal end. Subsequently, the first guidewire was retrieved, and a 2.5-mm coronary artery balloon (Boston Scientific) was advanced over the second guidewire, reached the midsection of the fractured catheter, and was inflated to 6 atm. Then, the fractured catheter was kept at the distal end of the guiding catheter and retrieved (Figure 1D). Ultimately, the fractured catheter was successfully removed from the right femoral artery. Postoperative angiography showed no vascular stenosis or injury (Figure 1E). Discussion

Complete fracture of the catheter is a rare complication. Among the limited available literature, fractures occur more frequently via the transradial than the transfemoral approach, which is likely attributable to the smaller vessel caliber and higher tendency of catheter self-twisting.3,4 Potential factors contributing to catheter fracture include torquing, forceful withdrawal of catheters entrapped in arterial spasm, reuse, manufacturing defects, inadvertent passage of a large catheter through a smaller-sized access sheath, polymer aging, or a combination of these factors.1 In the present study, the catheter fracture occurred via the transfemoral approach and was attributed to polymer aging resulting from prolonged catheter storage.

Fractured catheter fragments can cause vascular injury if not promptly addressed. While retrieving fractured catheters from relatively superficial or larger vessels is feasible using a snare or surgical approach, removal from deeper or smaller vessels remains a significant challenge. Previous studies have reported the use of balloon-assisted traction for retrieving fractured catheters in arteries.2,3 Different from our study, as shown in Figure 3A, one study described a different balloon location for fractured catheter removal using balloon traction, noting that if the proximal end of the fractured catheter is curved, the proximal half or one-third of the balloon should remain outside the catheter fragment during inflation. This ensures proper alignment with the guiding catheter or sheath, enabling the balloon to be retracted into the guiding catheter.2 In contrast, as shown in Figures 3B and 3C, the proximal end of the fractured catheter was not curved in both the case reported by Wang et al3 and our case. We inflated the balloon at the midsection of the fractured catheter, with the distal end supported by a guidewire. Another difference is that, during the retrieval process, we kept the balloon and fractured catheter at the distal end of the guiding catheter rather than retracting them into it, which is attributed the small guiding catheter. To retract a fractured catheter into the guiding catheter, a larger-caliber guiding catheter is required. Furthermore, we observed that in clinicians with limited interventional experience, accurately passing the guidewire through the fractured floating catheter is often challenging. Such difficulty inevitably leads to multiple attempts, which not only prolongs procedural time but also increases the risk of vascular injury. Therefore, we innovatively proposed a novel double-guidewire-assisted balloon traction approach. As illustrated in Figure 3C, initially, the first guidewire was advanced through the gap between the fractured catheter and vessel wall, inducing spasm of the internal iliac artery branch and thereby fixing the fractured catheter. Subsequently, the second guidewire was advanced through the fractured catheter, and a balloon was advanced along this guidewire to the fractured catheter's midsection. This novel approach increases the likelihood of successfully passing a guidewire through a free-floating broken catheter and removing the fractured fragment.

Catheter-induced vasospasm is frequently observed in small-caliber arteries, such as coronary arteries.5 Underlying mechanisms of vasospasm primarily involve hyperreactivity of vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial dysfunction.5 In our case, guidewire-induced vasospasm most likely resulted from a myogenic reflex of the vascular smooth muscle caused by the mechanical stimulation of the guidewire and from endothelial denudation caused by the fractured catheter, which can promote the release of the potent vasoconstrictor endothelin-1.6 Given the potential that guidewire-induced vasospasm may be insufficient to stabilize the fractured catheter, this approach is more suitable for small-caliber vessels. If balloon-assisted traction fails to remove the broken fragments, snare or surgical removal should be considered.

In order to prevent catheter fracture, some measures should be implemented. First, high-quality, appropriately sized, undamaged, and nonaged catheters should be selected. Second, catheters should be advanced or retrieved gently and gradually and avoid violent manipulation and excessive stretching. Third, guidewire guidance and angiography should be used as auxiliary techniques. In case of catheter fracture, removal can be achieved via surgery, balloon-assisted traction, or snare. For fractured catheters located in deeper or smaller vessels, balloon-assisted traction is recommended. During this procedure, operators must pass the guidewire through the catheter carefully, gently, and precisely and avoid repeated and forceful manipulation. If the fractured catheter is free-floating within the vessel, making guidewire passage difficult, the double-guidewire-assisted balloon traction technique proposed herein could be attempted. In addition, the balloon should be inflated to an appropriate pressure to securely anchor the fractured catheter and prevent dislodgement. If the proximal end of the broken catheter is curved, the proximal half or one-third of the balloon should be kept outside the fractured catheter. In contrast, if the proximal end is not curved, the balloon could be positioned at the catheter's midsection. Finally, regardless of whether the balloon and fractured catheter are retracted into the guiding catheter, their retrieval should be performed with caution and care under fluoroscopy to prevent vascular injury.

Conclusions

Overall, double-guidewire-assisted balloon traction can be considered an effective approach for retrieving fractured catheters in small-caliber vessels, particularly when the guidewire is unable to traverse the broken floating fragment accurately.
