Treatment of Brain Abscess in Patient with Eisenmenger Syndrome
Authors: Su Jeong Sung, Dae Lim Koo, Songyi Park, Dong Hoon Shin, Haechan Cho, You Jung Ok, Jin-Young Hwang, Hack-Lyoung Kim, Yoon Hwan Byun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eisenmenger syndrome (ES) is a complex multisystem disorder caused by untreated congenital heart disease. Management of brain abscess in ES is challenging, as treatment decisions must balance infection control with altered cardiopulmonary physiology, necessitating a multidisciplinary approach. We present a case of a 45-year-old male with ES and symptomatic brain abscess treated successfully with abscess aspiration under monitored anesthesia care and appropriate antibiotic therapy. We share our experience, outline key perioperative considerations, and discuss relevant literature.

INTRODUCTION

Eisenmenger syndrome (ES) is a life-threatening multisystem disorder characterized by increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and right-to-left (R-to-L) shunt through intracardiac defects [4,16]. It represents the most advanced form of pulmonary vascular disease associated with congenital heart defects and carries a high risk of morbidity and premature mortality from complications such as progressive heart failure [4,16,17].

Cerebral abscess is a rare but devastating complication of ES, attributed to the R-to-L shunt, which allows venous bacteria to bypass pulmonary filtration and seed the central nervous system [19,22]. Surgical intervention is a key treatment for symptomatic brain abscess but places patients with ES at risk of acute cardiopulmonary decompensation because of their vulnerable hemodynamic status [23]. Anesthetic management is particularly challenging in this population, as general anesthesia carries substantial risk. Mechanical ventilation with positive pressure and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) may aggravate R-to-L shunting, worsen hypoxemia, and precipitate right ventricular (RV) failure [8]. Therefore, a multidisciplinary approach involving cardiologists, anesthesiologists, neurologists, and neurosurgeons is essential to ensure safe perioperative management. In this report, we describe the successful treatment of a brain abscess in a patient with ES and discuss the associated clinical challenges and therapeutic strategies.

CASE REPORT

The current study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul Metropolitan Government - Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center (IRB No. 30-2025-68). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards set forth in the Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the respective IRB due to the retrospective nature of the analysis.

A 45-year-old man with a long-standing ventricular septal defect complicated by ES presented with acute dysarthria and right-sided weakness that had begun the previous day. On examination, he was drowsy with a Glasgow coma scale (GCS) score of E4V3M5, unable to follow commands, and exhibited motor dysphasia, dysarthria, and right hemiparesis (grade 4). His blood pressure was 147/94 mmHg, and peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO₂) was 71%. Laboratory evaluation revealed polycythemia, with a hemoglobin level of 17.7 g/dL and a hematocrit of 62.1%. Non-contrast brain computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a left temporoparietal cystic lesion suspicious for a brain abscess. During contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), he developed a generalized tonic-clonic seizure with abrupt desaturation to the 30% range, requiring emergent intubation. MRI subsequently confirmed a 3-cm abscess in the left temporoparietal region (Fig. 1A).

Emergent craniotomy and abscess removal were initially considered for the symptomatic brain abscess. However, general anesthesia was ultimately considered too high risk because of limited cardiopulmonary reserve and concern for RV failure. The patient was transferred to the medical intensive care unit and started on antibiotics (vancomycin 1 g q12h, ceftazidime 2 g q8h, and metronidazole 500 mg q8h), an antiepileptic drug (levetiracetam 1 g q12h), and a steroid (dexamethasone 4 mg q6h). Vancomycin dosing was subsequently adjusted using repeated therapeutic drug monitoring. The dose was titrated to 900 mg every 8 hours and later to 1100 mg every 8 hours, achieving predicted trough levels of 13.5 to 16 μg/mL, consistent with the recommended target range of 15 to 20 μg/mL [31].

By hospital day (HD) 2, the patient’s mental status had improved, allowing extubation and transfer to the general ward. SpO₂ remained at 85% on 6 L/min of supplemental oxygen, with stable systolic blood pressure. Diagnostic evaluation, including blood and urine cultures, chest radiography, transthoracic echocardiography, paranasal sinus CT, and dental assessment, did not identify an infectious source. Cerebrospinal fluid examination was deferred because of suspected increased intracranial pressure. In discussion with the infectious disease team, the most likely causative organisms were considered to be oral flora, including Streptococcus species and anaerobes, given the patient’s chronic alcohol consumption, smoking history, and poor oral hygiene. Antibiotics were subsequently changed to ceftriaxone 2 g q12h and metronidazole 500 mg q8h on HD 12. A peripherally inserted central catheter was placed for prolonged intravenous (IV) antibiotic administration. With continued antibiotic therapy, the patient’s neurologic function gradually improved.

On HD 15, the patient developed new focal aware seizures with right orofacial twitching, accompanied by acute worsening of dysarthria and motor dysphasia. Fosphenytoin 300 mg was added. MRI demonstrated enlargement of the abscess to 3.8 cm with progression of surrounding edema (Fig. 1B), indicating failure of medical therapy. After multidisciplinary evaluation, navigation-guided aspiration under monitored anesthesia care (MAC) was planned, with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) on standby for potential pulmonary hypertensive crisis or RV failure, in which case ECMO would be the only viable rescue therapy. However, given the anticipated difficulty of successful weaning once ECMO support is initiated, ECMO was prepared strictly as a last-resort measure.

Sedation was induced with midazolam 2 mg IV and maintained with remifentanil infusion and dexmedetomidine. An additional 1 mg of midazolam was administered after 30 minutes. With facial oxygen at 6 L/min, SpO₂ remained at 91%. A scalp block was performed using ropivacaine with epinephrine. A linear incision and burr hole were made, and aspiration was attempted. However, the abscess capsule was too thick to permit needle penetration. The burr hole was therefore enlarged, and the abscess capsule was directly incised and drained under microscopic guidance. Multiple swabs and abscess wall specimens were obtained (Fig. 2). The 90-minute procedure was completed without seizures or hemodynamic instability. Postoperative MRI demonstrated reduction of the abscess (Fig. 1C). All neurologic deficits improved immediately, and the patient returned to the general ward on postoperative day 1.

Intraoperative cultures yielded no growth. IV antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone and metronidazole was continued to complete an 8-week course. Follow-up MRI at 6 weeks postoperatively (HD 57) demonstrated only minimal residual wall enhancement without abscess contents (Fig. 1D). Before discharge, secondary erythrocytosis was managed with therapeutic phlebotomy, and aspirin 100 mg was started. The patient was discharged without neurologic deficits or recurrent seizures. At 3-month follow-up, he remained neurologically intact and seizure-free, and MRI confirmed resolution of the abscess. DISCUSSION

ES is a late consequence of untreated congenital heart defects. Increased pulmonary blood flow from an initial left-to-right (L-to-R) shunt leads to endothelial shear stress, progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling, and elevation of PVR. When PVR exceeds systemic vascular resistance (SVR), R-to-L shunting develops, allowing blood to bypass the pulmonary circulation and resulting in systemic hypoxemia and related symptoms [5]. Secondary erythrocytosis may cause hyperviscosity syndrome, and platelet dysfunction has been reported to increase the risk of coagulopathy [4]. Long-term outcomes remain poor, with premature mortality rates reported to reach 42% at 25 years [32]. Brain abscess is an uncommon but well-recognized complication of ES. A proposed mechanism is that R-to-L shunting permits bacteria to bypass pulmonary filtration and gain access to the cerebral circulation [30].

Surgical intervention for brain abscess is generally indicated when the lesion exceeds 2.5 cm in diameter or when patients present with decreased mental status or focal neurological deficits [3]. IV antibiotics are typically administered for 6–8 weeks [7]. However, management of brain abscess in patients with ES is particularly challenging, as treatment decisions must balance effective infection control against altered cardiopulmonary physiology. To the best of our knowledge, only twelve cases of brain abscess associated with cyanotic heart disease have been reported in the literature (Table 1) [1,2,8-12,14,15,24,27,33].

The initial clinical question was whether antibiotic therapy alone would be sufficient. Although surgical criteria were met, empirical medical therapy was initiated because of the patient’s hemodynamic instability. Hall et al. [15] reported successful medical management of a moderate-sized abscess, whereas other studies have described higher failure rates and mortality with conservative treatment [13]. In the present case, the patient initially improved but subsequently deteriorated on HD 15, necessitating surgical intervention for decompression and microbiological evaluation despite appropriate antibiotic therapy. Perioperative planning therefore required multidisciplinary discussion among multiple specialties. Clinical priorities included maintaining hemodynamic stability, preventing RV dysfunction, minimizing operative duration, and reducing perioperative seizure risk.

Anesthetic management in patients with ES presents significant challenges. General anesthesia, particularly through exposure to positive-pressure ventilation and PEEP, can exacerbate R-to-L shunting, worsen hypoxemia, and precipitate acute RV failure. Maintaining adequate intravascular volume and SVR is essential, as a reduction in SVR increases R-to-L shunting, whereas excessive vasoconstriction may overload the RV. Venous access also requires particular caution because of the risk of paradoxical air embolism [6,8]. Additional perioperative considerations included maintaining adequate intravascular volume, addressing platelet dysfunction, and providing postoperative deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis in the setting of hyperviscosity.

To mitigate these risks, MAC with continuous remifentanil and dexmedetomidine was used to maintain hemodynamic stability while preserving airway reflexes, spontaneous ventilation, and adequate sedation and analgesia [24]. As bolus administration of dexmedetomidine may increase vascular resistance with reflex bradycardia, continuous infusion was preferred to avoid abrupt hemodynamic fluctuations while maintaining sufficient blood pressure [34]. However, it should be noted that MAC carries risks including airway obstruction due to excessive sedation, inadequate sedation, and potential conversion to general anesthesia [20,29]. Therefore, careful patient selection is essential.

Intraoperative seizure was another major concern. The patient had experienced abrupt desaturation during a seizure in the emergency department and continued to have focal aware seizures preoperatively despite antiepileptic therapy, placing him at high risk for intraoperative seizure [18]. If a seizure were to occur under MAC, emergent airway control and conversion to general anesthesia with mechanical ventilation could be required, potentially precipitating acute RV failure. In such a scenario, ECMO would represent the most viable rescue option. However, given the anticipated difficulty of successful weaning, ECMO was reserved strictly as a last resort. Nevertheless, the ECMO team remained on standby for rapid initiation should acute RV failure occur, thereby minimizing the risk of prolonged hypoxemia and secondary brain injury. We do not suggest that ECMO preparation is warranted for all patients with ES undergoing neurosurgical procedures. In this case, however, ECMO standby was considered necessary given the patient’s clinical condition. Such decisions should be made through careful multidisciplinary discussion.

Craniotomy with abscess excision has been reported to be associated with a low risk of recurrence [21,25], but it requires general anesthesia, longer operative duration, and is more prone to hemodynamic fluctuation. Therefore, navigation-guided aspiration was initially planned to shorten operative time and reduce physiologic stress. However, needle aspiration was unsuccessful due to a thick abscess wall, and drainage was completed under microscopic guidance. This prolonged the total operative time to 90 minutes, although no major hemodynamic events occurred. This case suggests that preparing the operative microscope in advance is beneficial, as navigation-guided aspiration may require intraoperative conversion, and that direct drainage through a limited craniotomy can be feasible under MAC.

On retrospective review, several measures might have further improved safety. The use of in-line air filters during venous access may reduce the risk of paradoxical air embolism. In addition, although preventive phlebotomy was performed before discharge, earlier intervention might have reduced hyperviscosity and thrombotic risk, although this must be balanced against the risks of dehydration and hemodynamic compromise. Furthermore, molecular diagnostic methods such as 16S rRNA or 16S rDNA gene sequencing [26,28] may improve pathogen detection in brain abscess and help guide more tailored antibiotic therapy. However, this diagnostic method was not available at our institution.

CONCLUSION

This case suggests that abscess aspiration under MAC combined with appropriate antibiotic therapy can be a safe and effective strategy for brain abscess in ES when supported by comprehensive multidisciplinary planning. Careful intraoperative hemodynamic management under MAC, ECMO standby, and seizure-risk mitigation were key elements contributing to successful care.
