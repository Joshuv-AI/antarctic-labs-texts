A framework for comparative analysis of human and mouse cortical neuron dendrites in corresponding brain regions
Authors: Zhixi Yun, Wen Ye, Nan Ji, Yujin Wang, Jing Rong, Tian Li, Yingxin Li, Qi Shen, Ruilong Wang, Hairuo Zhang, Hao Xu, Nan Mo, Binghua Song, Xin Chen, Yi Wang, Yiying Mai, Ming Ding, Song-Lin Ding, Lijuan Liu, Liwei Zhang, Hanchuan Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cross-species comparisons of neuronal morphology can reveal evolutionary diversification of cortical organization, but they remain limited by scarce human reconstructions, uncertain regional correspondence and scale-dependent morphometrics. Here we introduce a region correspondence framework to compare single-neuron dendritic morphology between mouse and human cortex. Corresponding cortical regions were rigorously defined by integrating anatomical annotations, functional evidence and registration stability and then evaluated using cross-species transcriptomic cell composition profiles. We analyzed dendritic reconstructions of 2,363 human and 16,011 mouse neurons from frontal, parietal and temporal lobes, comparing size-normalized local morphometric features within matched regions. Human cortical neurons showed higher branching frequency and shorter branching intervals, indicating compact local dendritic architecture beyond proportional size enlargement. Local morphology also showed stronger lobe-level separability in humans, associated mainly with spatial dendritic organization, whereas mouse separability reflected branching topology. Additional molecular, physiological, metabolic and geometric analyses showed partial concordance with morphology-based lobe separability, supporting divergent organization of cortical dendrites across species.

Main

Given the noteworthy upsurge in the number of studies that utilize mammalian neurons’ digitized morphology for brain-wide, region-specific exploration of cell typing and brain anatomy1–6, a natural question arises: Can the patterns primarily observed in the mouse brain be reexamined in other mammalian brains, particularly the human brain? Clarifying how neuronal morphology and its organizational principles differ across species is crucial for understanding functional specialization and the potential augmentation of particular functional capabilities. Previous efforts have been made to compare neuronal morphology across species7–19. However, three major challenges have persisted: the scarcity of reliable three-dimensional (3D) reconstructed neuron morphologies, limitations in brain mapping and neuron standardization techniques necessary for objective comparisons and the lack of appropriate neuronal features to quantify differences across species. Here we introduce an extensible framework to address these challenges by analyzing the morphological features and anatomical implications of single-neuron reconstructions from both mouse and human brains.

Digital atlases of mammalian brains, particularly human brains, have been recognized as essential resources in neuroscience over the past three decades20–30. At the single-neuron level, these atlases are invaluable for comparing the overall distribution, projection patterns and feature-based categorization of individual neurons across multiple spatial levels and organizational frameworks2,5. However, numerous challenges remain in performing cross-species comparisons, particularly between mouse and human brains. Most macroscale brain atlases are constructed primarily using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data at millimeter or submillimeter resolution. For example, the Human Connectome Project31,32 has mapped both the structural and functional connectivity of the human brain. By contrast, mesoscale brain atlases, such as the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework27 (CCFv3) and the Allen projectome of mouse neuron populations33, provide considerably higher spatial resolution than macroscale atlases. However, there is currently no direct comparative framework that enables single-neuron resolution comparisons between mouse and human brains at the lobe or anatomical levels.

Although previous studies, as cited above, have compared neuronal morphologies across species using dendritic or even axonal reconstructions, many cross-species analyses lack rigorous selection of robustly registered brain regions and carefully defined protocols for establishing correspondence between regions in different species. Moreover, morphological features are often not normalized prior to comparison, potentially introducing variability. By contrast, a central idea of this study is that cross-species comparisons must be grounded in brain regions that are both functionally and molecularly associated, as determined by expert annotation and validated with minimal variability in anatomical mapping. Crucially, morphological features must also be normalized to control for size-related differences. Equally important is our focus on quantitative analysis of local morphological features, specifically those near the neuronal soma, before extending the framework to include more global aspects of neuron morphology. To this end, we compiled large-scale datasets of local dendritic morphologies from single neurons in both human and mouse brains and developed an analytical framework that aligns neurons based on clearly defined, functionally corresponding brain regions. This framework enables precise, side-by-side comparisons across species, with a focus on systematically capturing local, intrinsic morphological features within a standardized neuronal space.

This study introduces our approach to address the challenge of cross-species comparison of individual neurons in a coordinated manner. The importance of these anatomically, functionally and molecularly corresponding mouse−human region pairs is highlighted by examining the intrinsic local features of their respective cortical regions. Our findings reveal that human cortical neurons exhibit substantially different branching organization that cannot be explained by simple linear scaling of neuronal size compared to mouse brains, particularly in terms of normalized dendritic branching frequency and dendritic branching intervals. Across the broader morphological feature space, neuronal populations also display stronger separability between cortical lobes in humans than in mice. This is mainly driven by the topological characteristics of mouse neurons and the spatial characteristics of human neurons. We also found that this neuronal separability can be partially recapitulated when examining the global functional states of these brain lobes as well as their global metabolic states, molecular profiles and cortical folding/geometry. Together, these results demonstrate that comparative analysis of intrinsic neuronal features can provide a valuable foundation for investigating conserved and divergent principles of cortical organization across species, with the potential for broad applicability.

Results

A framework for joint mouse−human cortical analysis of single neurons in corresponding mouse and human brain regions

We used 100 whole mouse brains to generate dendritic reconstructions of 16,011 cortical neurons (Fig. 1a and Methods). We also used ex vivo surgical tissues from clinically normal cortical regions of 23 human patients (Supplementary Table 1) to generate dendritic reconstructions of 2,363 neurons of three cortical lobes (frontal lobe (FL), temporal lobe (TL) and parietal lobe (PL)) (Fig. 1a and Methods). To achieve these results, we leveraged our recent neuromorphometry and neuroinformatics platforms5,6 for the routine generation of high-quality neuron reconstructions in both mouse and human brains. The mouse brains, which contained sparsely labeled cortical neurons, were imaged using the fMOST method34. We then employed the All-Path-Pruning-2 (APP2) algorithm35, an automatic neuron-tracing technique, to generate initial reconstructions of the 3D neuron morphologies from these fMOST images. These neuron models were subsequently curated using a Collaborative Augmented Reconstruction (CAR) system based on crowdsourcing36. In addition, for the human samples, which were obtained from patients undergoing surgeries to remove brain tumors, we utilized the Adaptive Cell Tomography (ACTomography) method6. Previous work6 comparing ACT-imaged and fMOST-imaged neurons in mice (Supplementary Fig. 6 in that study) suggests that morphological measurements are consistent across these imaging modalities. ACTomography enabled us to produce 200-μm-thick, even 600-μm-thick, brain sections from human ex vivo tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1b), followed by injecting Lucifer Yellow into the cortical neurons and acquiring both two-photon imaging data of local dendrites and MRI data of whole brains. Notably, we approximated spatial homogeneity by massively sampling neurons in all regions to ensure that there was no selection bias in the cortical areas studied in mouse brains (Fig. 1b). For the human tissues, we focused on broad anatomical subdivisions in the frontal, temporal and parietal lobes corresponding to various functional regions, which were chosen as a post hoc observation of brain-wide coverage rather than a pre-specified sampling design (Fig. 1c). These human neurons are mostly large pyramidal neurons from cortical layers 3 and 5, consistent with the previous study6. We also determined the layers of neurons through DAPI pre-staining (Supplementary Fig. 1a). With all neurons reconstructed, we next built a framework for the joint mouse−human neuron analysis. We caution that establishing a cross-species comparison of neuron morphologies requires careful attention to avoid overfitting the geometry during the alignment of brain anatomy. Specifically, because mouse brains lack the complex sulci and gyri found in the human cortex, forcing an alignment of these features onto a mouse brain template would be meaningless. Therefore, we initially aligned mouse brain images to a mouse brain template and similarly for human brains. This was followed by a comparison of the neuron morphologies that had been mapped within potential corresponding regions of their respective canonical spaces. Specifically, we employed mBrainAligner29, a landmark-based cross-modality registration tool, to map all individual brains to their respective template brains (Fig. 1a, step S1). For mouse brain images, we used the CCFv3 as the template. For human brains, we utilized the ICBM 2009c nonlinear template37, which led to enhanced alignments compared to previous alignments6 based on the MNI152 linear template. Subsequently, mBrainAligner was used again to map individual neuron reconstructions or ex vivo tissue segmentations to the two respective atlases (Fig. 1a, step S2, and Methods). Layer information for the reconstruction of neurons in mice was determined based on their cell body location registered in the CCFv3 atlas. Given that the human ex vivo tissues were extracted from a limited number of cortical regions (Fig. 1c), we identified the corresponding mouse brain regions for these human samples based on expert neuroanatomists’ annotations of brain regions corresponding to similar brain anatomies and functions as well as a data-driven approach using gene expression analysis developed below (Fig. 1a, step S3, and Methods). Within the canonical spaces of these atlases, standardized neuron morphologies could be quantitatively compared based on various features (Fig. 1a, step S4).

We examined the global morphological features of the standardized mouse and human neurons to ensure that they could be compared fairly and quantitatively. We found that the dendritic morphologies of mouse neurons in this study statistically matched those produced in a previous study2 in terms of their global morphometry (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Our human neurons exhibit less overall reconstructed neurite length than mouse neurons (Extended Data Fig. 1c), attributable mostly to the common situation of truncated neurites in brain tissue sections6. The detailed comparison of neuron morphology will be discussed later in the paper.

For SEU-M16011, we registered the respective fMOST images to CCFv3 (Methods). The overall average map of the registration was anatomically consistent with the mouse CCFv3 atlas. Indeed, the variance is less than 0.05 in most brain regions, especially the cortical regions studied here (Fig. 2a). Indeed, although the mouse neurons and their neurites span the entire cortical regions of the frontal, temporal and parietal lobes, their somas and neurite arbors exhibit the greatest density in the middle of these lobes. This distribution is not influenced by the slightly higher registration variation found around the borders of some brain areas (Fig. 2a). Of the 16 mouse cortical regions in the three lobes, supplemental somatosensory area (SSs), visceral area (VISC) and postrhinal area (PoR) contained the largest overall density of mouse neurons and the greatest arbor density (Fig. 2b). The normalized variation of registration for all 16 brain regions is approximately 0.02, indicating that the morphometry of cortical neurons in SEU-M16011 is less likely to be impacted by potential registration inaccuracies and anatomical variability of original brain images (Fig. 2c).

For SEU-H2363, we mapped the MRI data of all 23 patients to the ICBM 2009c template37 (Methods). Samples were sequentially labeled H001 through H023 as anonymous donor identifiers. Before brain image registration, surgeons annotated the names of the ex vivo brain regions and also labeled their actual 3D segmentations in the sample extraction areas. After the registration, these standardized locations revealed the overall distribution of sampled neurons in the human atlas (Fig. 3a). We found that the surgeon-generated annotations of brain regions matched with annotations based on the CerebrA atlas38 (Fig. 3a,e and Extended Data Fig. 2a), a refined version of Mindboggle-101 atlas39. The registered cortical regions of interest also display overall small anatomical variations of less than 0.06 (Fig. 3b). The intensity variances of these cortical regions peaked near 0.02, similar to those of the registered mouse brains (Figs. 2c and 3c). For all three cortical lobes, we also sampled neurons at a rate of approximately 100 neurons per subject per lobe (TL: 104.8, FL: 94.9 and PL: 116.7) (Fig. 3d). This implies that the neurons randomly sampled from these regions are not expected to display a systematic skewness attributable to brain registration or sampling ratios in subsequent analyses. Finally, mouse and human neurons were processed under strictly matched conditions, including neuron type classification, cortical layer alignment, spherical cropping to minimize slice artifacts and standardized proofreading (Supplementary Table 2), thus ensuring that they are suitable for reliable cross-species comparisons. Although human reconstructions may underestimate total neurite length because of tissue sectioning and dye perfusion limits, key local structures, including bifurcation points and fully traced branch segments, remained suitable for subsequent morphological analyses. Comparisons between registered and non-registered mouse neurons further suggested that registration-related effects on these morphological features were limited (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To utilize the data more effectively and achieve a deeper analysis, we determined corresponding regions based on the anatomical and functional correspondences between mouse and human brain regions. Initially, we identified the lobes to which human brain regions belonged and matched these lobes to anatomically and functionally corresponding regions in the mouse brain. We then further refined this correspondence by matching human and mouse brain regions at a finer level according to well-established anatomical or functional knowledge, which allowed for more detailed and targeted cross-species comparisons of neuronal morphology (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 3).

Cellular RNA sequencing validates key corresponding mouse−human regions in brain lobes

Although comparing anatomical and functional properties provided insights into the correspondence of cross-species brain regions, we further explored genetic correspondence by utilizing transcriptomic data. Many cell taxonomic studies provide a good basis for recognizing mouse and human cell types40–43. Several studies have demonstrated both gene expression differences and homologous relationships of cell types and brain regions between mouse and human10,12,13,44,45; our goal was to validate this genetic correspondence by examining regional genetic profiles across the entire cortex. Despite potential differences in gene expression profiles, most cell types were conserved across species. By comparing the cellular composition and proportions of shared cell types in the human and mouse brain regions, we constructed a cellular composition similarity map and inter-regional genetic similarity rankings to assess and validate regional correspondence further.

We considered three state-of-the-art whole-brain transcriptomic datasets: single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from mice46, multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH) data from mice47 and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data from humans48. We prioritized the analysis of the MERFISH dataset given its granularity of anatomical source regions of cells. Referring to the genes that were included in the mouse data, we identified 15,506 homologous genes (Supplementary Table 4) common to both species queried from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene database49. After filtering for cortical neuronal cells and homologous genes, we integrated each mouse dataset with the human snRNA-seq dataset, generating two integrated groups (group 1: mouse MERFISH and human snRNA-seq, Fig. 4a; group 2: mouse scRNA-seq and human snRNA-seq, Extended Data Fig. 3a and Methods). The cells were well mixed across species (Fig. 4a) and across shared well-annotated cell types reported in the original datasets (Fig. 4b, left panel, Extended Data Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4b). This integration allowed for further unsupervised clustering (Fig. 4b, right panel) and cell counting in each cluster. Using the normalized cell type proportions of each lobe and region, we constructed correlation maps to approximate pairwise genetic correspondence (Fig. 4c, Extended Data Fig. 3c and Methods). Due to the potentially distinct proportions of cortical cell types in mice and humans12,45,50 (Supplementary Fig. 4a), normalization of cell type proportions was performed within each species (Methods), which assumed that the relative changes of the same cell type were consistent between different brain regions within a species. To improve robustness, the correlation map also provided a confidence value to indicate the stability of the correlation scores computed with different numbers of principal components (Fig. 4c and Methods). In this kind of map, region pairs with high correlation and high confidence indicate possible genetic correspondence. When cells from different brain regions were pooled at the lobe level, the correlation map consistently showed genetic correspondence between the same lobes in both mice and humans (Fig. 4c).

Regarding regional correspondence, the correlation map provides a reference for the relative genetic similarity among cortical regions, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3. For instance, the cuneus (Cun) and visual (VIS) areas show a strong correlation in both species (group 1: mean ± s.d.: 0.68 ± 0.05; group 2: mean ± s.d.: 0.77 ± 0.08), suggesting that vision-related cellular mechanisms may be conserved between species. Similarly, the superior parietal lobule (SPL) and posterior parietal association (PTLp) areas show high correlation (group 1: mean ± s.d.: 0.43 ± 0.03; group 2: mean ± s.d.: 0.65 ± 0.06), as do the transverse temporal gyrus (TTG) and auditory area (AUD) (group 1: mean ± s.d.: 0.64 ± 0.1). In group 1, the genetic correspondence between precentral gyrus (PrCG) and primary motor (MOp) areas was consistent, as was that between parahippocampal gyrus (PHG) and perirhinal (PERI) areas. The clearer correspondence observed at the lobe level likely results from data averaging across multiple regions, which smooths out local variations to reveal broader cross-species conservation. However, the regional-level maps also indicate molecular divergence within individual cortical regions (Fig. 4e and Extended Data Fig. 3c).

To provide a more robust delineation of genetic correspondence, we adopted three metrics (Methods) and used an integrated rank instead of individual metric values to avoid situations where the values of multiple metrics cannot be simply averaged (Extended Data Fig. 4). Relevant regional correspondences (Supplementary Table 3) were examined more comprehensively based on the available transcriptomic data. The AUD in the mouse brain best matched the TTG in human brain regions. The SPL in the human brain matched well with the PTLp in mouse brain regions (Fig. 4d). It was possible that VIS and PTLp had similar genetic correspondence due to spatial adjacency and that PTLp received dense projections from VIS51. Other anatomically and functionally corresponding regions also showed the highest or very high ranks (Extended Data Fig. 4). These approaches enabled us to determine the relative similarity of cell type proportions across all compared regions, strengthening the anatomical and functional correspondences and making them more convincing.

Human and mouse cortical neurons differ in branching frequency and intervals

To make the most of the limited data and explore the morphological similarities and differences between human and mouse cortical neurons, even at finer regional correspondences, we developed an analytical framework that includes multi-level morphological characterization and soft-labeled neuron analysis based on robust regional correspondences.

We initiated our morphological analysis on the species level to gain a holistic understanding of neuronal characterizations. Typically, our knowledge of complex structures begins with the examination of the basic constituent structures. Therefore, we first investigated the characteristics of single branches. In consideration of the completeness of neuron reconstructions, we focused on the non-terminal branching patterns of lower branch order (BO; Extended Data Fig. 1f) branches, ensuring that these branches were fully reconstructed from available imaging signals. We found that, when pooling single branches from all neurons, the peaks in the branch length distributions differed minimally between the two species in low BO branches (basal: BO2 and BO3; apical: BO2−BO5; Fig. 5a, upper panel). This might indicate similarity in signal transmission between human and mouse at lower-order single branches. As the branching order increased, the branch length of human basal dendrites appeared to be greater than that of mice. Previous studies established that soma volume correlates with dendrite volume in the mouse cortex52 and that the total length of basal dendrites in human temporal lobe neurons was approximately twice that of mice7. Considering that soma sizes of human neurons were, on average, 1.68 times larger than those of mouse neurons in our study (Extended Data Fig. 1d), we normalized mouse neurons by scaling them using the average soma diameter ratio between species in order to eliminate the potential effect of overall dendrite size on comparison between the two species. Under these circumstances, the distributions of branching lengths are much more distinguishable (Fig. 5a, upper panel). In other words, considering the overall scale in the human brain, neurons tend to use relatively short, low-order branches to bifurcate rapidly. We also examined sibling branches, defined as pairs of branches connected to the same parent branch, by calculating the angle between them. The peaks in the two angle distributions are nearly identical (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Most of these features at the level of the single-level branch show minor differences between lobes within the species. Building upon our analysis of the single-branch structures, we further examined the overall structure of local neurons. Noteworthy differences emerge when examining the ‘bifurcation numbers’ and ‘total length’ within a local spherical range near the soma (radius = 50 μm, with an ‘R50_’ prefix before the morphological feature name; Extended Data Fig. 1e). This range is moderate for encompassing adequate branches while minimizing the inclusion of incomplete branches resulting from insufficient slice thickness (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 1a). Based on the same normalization approach (Methods), an intuitive discrepancy emerges in bifurcation numbers and total length between the two species that human neurons exhibit the higher structural complexity (Fig. 5a, lower panel). Further examination of branch numbers of the first five BOs indicates that the branch abundance of human neurons is primarily attributed to richer primary branches (BO1), shorter branch intervals and rapid branching resulting from these shorter intervals (Fig. 5a). These trends can still be observed on individual cross-species pairs of lobes (Fig. 5b). This analysis reveals a distinct organizational principle in human dendritic architecture that becomes apparent after controlling for neuronal size. Although low-order, non-terminal dendrites show conserved or minimally different structural patterns at the raw scale, normalization uncovers that human dendrites are not linearly elongated versions of mouse dendrites. Specifically, human neurons exhibit shorter branch intervals and a higher bifurcation frequency, leading to a more compact and densely branched dendritic arborization.

The same morphometric analyses were repeated on high-confidence layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons to ensure that these results are not an artifact of pooling across layers (Methods). Results well recapitulate the principal patterns reported above (Supplementary Fig. 5). We next performed unsupervised clustering of layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons based on their morphometric features derived from their basal and apical dendrites in 100-μm range (Methods). In both species, this analysis discretized the continuous variation of dendritic structures and consistently identified five major morphological types (M-types). By comparing key morphological parameters, we manually established one-to-one correspondences among the five M-types between species, enabling direct cross-species comparisons (Extended Data Fig. 6). We then quantified the relative abundance of each M-type across cortical lobes within each species. In both the frontal and parietal lobes, the overall distributions of M-types were broadly similar between mouse and human, suggesting a conserved organizational pattern of layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons in these regions. Within the parietal lobe, we observed a relative enrichment of the H_m2−M_m2 pair, characterized by larger and denser apical and basal dendritic arbors. However, the temporal lobe of mice showed a marked predominance of the H_m3−M_m4 pair (intermediate-sized apical and basal dendrites), which accounted for more than 50% of all sampled cells (Extended Data Fig. 6c). These results indicate that while the frontal and parietal lobes exhibit broadly conserved cellular composition, the temporal cortex displays species-specific divergence in dendritic local morphology, potentially reflecting a region-specific local circuit organization between species.

To further understand morphological relationships at a finer anatomical level, we conducted a more targeted comparison between the cross-species corresponding regions (Fig. 3e). Given that some human brain sampling regions spanned multiple areas that complicated the accurate annotation by the expert anatomist, we used a probabilistic region assignment method to allocate soft labels to neurons based on the volume proportion of sampled tissue in different brain regions (Fig. 3 and Extended Data Fig. 2a). Soft labels maintain consistency with manual labels and also provide detailed information on regional localization.

Considering the correspondence between brain regions inspected at the genetic level in the previous section and the abundance of our dataset in specific brain regions, we focus on the group of brain regions SPL and PTLp (Fig. 5c, yellow background), but, for the other groups of brain regions where there is an anatomical and functional correspondence (Fig. 5c, gray background), we similarly show the morphological features between them if they have sufficient neuron numbers and adequate cumulative probabilities (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2b). Combining probabilistic labels with our corresponding region analysis, we calculated the weighted mean and cumulative probability quartiles of neuronal morphological features in each region (Fig. 5c and Methods). We examined bifurcation numbers, the first five BO branch numbers and the maximum BO across corresponding regions. For the regions of SPL and PTLp, they exhibit a similar number of BO1 and BO2 of basal dendrites and start to differ at higher-order branches. Compared to other brain region pairs, SPL and PTLp show stronger similarity in the number of primary branches, although all still share the commonality that the number of branches is much larger in humans than in mice. Notably, secondary motor area (MOs) and superior frontal gyrus (SFG) exhibit the greatest discrepancies in branch abundance among these four region pairs, with human SFG neurons displaying richer and more complex branching structures. This difference is likely due to more first-order branches, indicating a more complex signal integration, possibly underscoring advanced executive functions and higher-order motor planning capabilities in humans. This approach reduces the potential inaccuracies and absoluteness of manual region labeling, enabling more precise and reliable comparisons of neuronal morphologies.

To investigate molecular programs that might underlie the regional differences in dendritic morphology between human and mouse cortex, we constructed species-specific co-expression networks using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and tested module-level functional enrichment (Extended Data Fig. 7 and Methods). We limited the scope of research to excitatory neurons in SPL and PTLp. WGCNA identified six species-specific modules of interest—four human-specific and two mouse-specific—several of which showed significant or near-significant enrichment for dendrite-related processes, including dendrite morphogenesis and positive regulation of neurogenesis. The conservative WGCNA specificity metric (Z) supports the species bias of these modules (Extended Data Fig. 7b). From these modules, we selected four representative homologous genes based on the Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis: human EPHA4, SERPINE2, SLIT2 and CUX2 and their mouse homologs Epha4, Serpine2, Slit2 and Cux2. Each gene displays species-biased expression or intramodular connectivity in the integrated single-cell uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) embeddings (Extended Data Fig. 7c). Upregulation of EPHA4/Epha4 expression is expected to inhibit dendritic extension by promoting growth cone collapse through SPAR-mediated inactivation of Rap1 (ref. 53). Consistent with this, Epha4 expression was higher in mice than EPHA4 expression in humans. By contrast, elevated SERPINE2/Serpine2 expression is predicted to facilitate dendritic extension by inhibiting thrombin activity, as blockade of thrombin permits neurite elongation and network formation54. SERPINE2/Serpine2-expressing cells showed a broader expression range in mouse than in human. The SLIT2/Slit2 genes encode SLIT2/Slit2, a secreted guidance cue that acts through Robo receptors and supports polarized neurite outgrowth55. Higher SLIT2 expression in human excitatory neurons may, therefore, contribute to organized dendritic extension. Finally, the CUX2/Cux2 genes encode CUX2/Cux2, transcription factors involved in dendritic elongation and branching56. Although CUX2 expression was lower in human excitatory neurons than in the corresponding mouse regions, its higher module membership, indicated by an intramodular connectivity measurement of kME = 0.72 in human versus kME = 0.52 in mouse, suggests stronger co-regulatory connectivity within the human neurogenesis module. Although these co-expression and enrichment results provide plausible mechanistic candidates, direct multimodal integration and experimental perturbation will be required to establish causal relationships.

Human dendrites are far more distinct across major cortical lobes compared to those in mice

Neurons from the cortical lobes of both human and mouse brains show marked differences in local branching morphometrics (Fig. 5). However, the distinctions between neurons from different lobes within the same species remain unclear. To explore this, we used a quantitative approach, measuring basal and apical morphological discrepancies of neurons across different lobes within each species using balanced classification accuracy (Methods). We reduced data variability and minimized the risk of overfitting by using five-fold cross-validation and optimized classifier parameters through grid search, resulting in balanced accuracy scores and other categorical evaluation metrics (Methods and Supplementary Fig. 6).

As more features are incorporated into the training, the local morphologies of the three human lobes (FL, PL and TL) show stronger discriminatory power between the pairwise combination of ‘FL−PL’ and ‘PL−TL’ compared to those in mice (Fig. 6a–c, upper panels). For the ‘FL−PL’ pair, human neurons achieve approximately 70% balanced classification accuracy, whereas mouse neurons reach only around 60% (Fig. 6a, upper panel). We used both classification accuracy and multivariate Jensen−Shannon divergence (MJSD) to quantify the regionwise separability, which reflects the statistical diversity of neuronal morphologies (Methods). The difference in this separability could reach approximately 2.7 times (Supplementary Table 5). Although the score for ‘PL−TL’ is lower, human neurons still exhibit higher classification scores than mice. These results suggest that the spatial organization and structural complexity of local morphologies vary more significantly across human cortical lobes compared to those in mice. When comparing the separability of neuronal morphology in the FL and TL neurons, human neurons exhibit lower separability in a localized range. This suggests that, at finer spatial scales, neurons in the FL and TL exhibit more similar morphology. Higher classification performance generally indicates more separable features. We thus analyzed the normalized distribution discrepancy (NDD) of features between neurons from every two lobes successively (Fig. 6a–c, middle panels). We counted the number of features where the discrepancy in humans was greater than that in mice. For the ‘FL−PL’ pair, the NDDs of 37 of 41 morphological features in human lobes are higher than in mouse lobes, indicating greater differences between FL and PL in humans for nearly every morphological characteristic. In the other two pairs of lobes, the NDD scores for more than half of the human features are higher than the NDD scores for the corresponding features in mice.

To facilitate a fair comparison, we also investigated the morphological separability using representative feature subsets for both species. With the minimum redundancy maximum relevance (mRMR) feature selection algorithm61, we identified the top five most characterizing features for each lobe pair in both species. In most cases, humans have higher NDD scores for these top-ranked features. Even when using the top-ranked mouse features as references, few mouse features have higher NDD scores than humans. For the ‘FL−PL’ and ‘PL−TL’ pairs, only one of five top-ranking features has higher NDD scores in mice, respectively (Fig. 6a–c, lower panels, scatter plots). In addition, we noted that top-ranking features within an individual species rarely overlapped between species. Only ‘average straightness’ frequently appeared among the top-ranked features in both basal and apical dendrites. In mice, these features were more associated with topological depth, whereas, in humans, they were more associated with spatial organization (Fig. 6a–c, lower panels, texts in bold).

Human cortical lobes exhibit varying separability in multimodal space

Given the quantitative evidence that human cortical lobes are more separable than mouse brain lobes in the neuron morphology space, we next asked whether this lobe separability is reflected in other cortical modalities. We examined multiple complementary data types that characterize cortical organization at different biological scales, including gene expression profiles across cell populations, physiological signals, cortical anatomical geometry and metabolic measurements (Methods).

Overall, different modalities reflected varying degrees of lobe separability. Based on MJSD measurements, gene expression exhibits the highest degree of separability (Fig. 7a and Methods). We then examined the relative separability of cortical lobes across different modalities. In this analysis, the single-neuron morphology of humans provides a more balanced classification of cortical lobes (Fig. 7b,c). Interestingly, both gene expression profiles and single-neuron morphologies show a stronger ability to distinguish between FL and TL when excitatory neurons are considered (Fig. 7c). We further calculated correlation coefficients between pairs of brain lobes using the proportional coding of cell types in each lobe, targeting the superclusters that are shared between each species (Methods). These analyses indicate that the differences between brain lobes are mainly introduced by excitatory neurons, consistent with the MJSD-based separability analysis (Extended Data Fig. 9b,c). These results suggest that excitatory neuron composition contributes to lobe-level differentiation.

We next focused on the human brain and examined whether lobe separability could also be detected from cortical anatomical geometry information, including cortical thickness and curvature (Supplementary Table 6), physiological signals such as electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) data of resting state and visual tasks (Methods) and global metabolic states62 (Supplementary Table 7).

For physiological signals, we examined the signal characteristics of different sensors corresponding to specific visual tasks or resting state (Extended Data Fig. 10 and Methods). These task-evoked signals demonstrate greater state specificity or task specificity compared to the resting-state data, exhibiting task-dependent differences in lobe separability. EEG data, although effective at distinguishing FL−PL pairs, show lower overall lobe separability (Extended Data Fig. 8a). MEG data also show task-dependent separability patterns (Extended Data Fig. 8a). For metabolism62, we analyzed both cerebral blood flow (CBF) and the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen consumption (CMRO2) (Supplementary Table 7). These metabolic features showed relatively weak lobe differentiation, with broadly consistent patterns across age groups (Extended Data Fig. 8b). As a global reference, we also examined the cortical geometry in characterizing these three lobes. We used four metrics, including average thickness, thickness standard deviation, integrated rectified mean curvature and integrated rectified Gaussian curvature, to assess geometric differences. Our analysis shows that the parietal lobe has a relatively smaller average thickness, and the temporal lobe displays reduced curvature compared to the other lobes (Extended Data Fig. 8c).

Discussion

This work provides an atlas-based framework for cross-species comparison of cortical neurons, addressing key challenges in neuron mapping, regional correspondence identification and morphology analysis. By combining large-scale mouse and human dendritic reconstructions with species-specific atlas registration, this framework enables quantitative localization and comparison of neurons across matched cortical regions. By integrating anatomical, functional and molecular information, we identified corresponding brain regions between mice and humans, facilitating a meaningful and targeted comparison of their intrinsic morphological features at single-neuron resolution.

A primary finding of our study is that human cortical neurons have significantly higher complexity compared to mouse neurons under the condition of size normalization, particularly in terms of dendritic branching frequency and dendritic branching intervals. This difference cannot be explained by simple linear scaling with neuron size. The higher branching frequency and denser branching patterns of human neurons compared to mouse neurons are directly linked to the enhanced dendritic computation and storage capacity that were revealed by the previous study63. These differences are pronounced in the MOs and SFG, which may be related to evolutionary divergence manifested in enhanced cognitive and motor abilities in humans. From the perspective of dendritic cable theory, such architectures may have important consequences for signal propagation and integration. Shorter dendritic segments can reduce electrotonic attenuation, allowing synaptic inputs to propagate toward the soma with higher fidelity. Moreover, from the standpoint of dendritic gating, these additional branching sites may support more frequent nonlinear integration events. Although previous studies have noted differences in dendrite length and complexity on limited neurons and brain regions between species7–9,15, our analyses expand on this by systematically quantifying these differences across multiple and finer cortical regions, utilizing a large-scale neuron reconstruction dataset and a carefully designed analytics approach to explore the feature space. Although tissue thickness, dye perfusion and imaging modality may affect the recovery of terminal branches, our focus on fully reconstructed non-terminal branches reduces this concern. In addition, the normalization based on soma diameter provides a useful framework for comparing intrinsic dendritic architecture.

Our classification analysis shows that human neurons exhibit higher separability between cortical lobes compared to mouse neurons, suggesting a higher degree of morphological specialization in the human cortex. This is particularly evident in the frontal and parietal lobes, where human neurons are classified with close to 70% accuracy, compared to approximately 60% accuracy for mouse neurons. This cross-species difference suggests that local dendritic morphology varies more strongly across major cortical subdivisions in humans than in mice. The higher separability in humans supports the notion that regions of the cerebral cortex are more morphologically differentiated. These differences may reflect evolutionary, developmental and functional diversification. Greater morphological separability may reflect increased regional differentiation of cortical circuitry, although direct links to functional specialization remain to be established. Moreover, local dendritic branching densities could be a feature contributing to lobe separability, indicating that branching complexity varies across cortical regions in a species-dependent manner.

From a methodological standpoint, previous efforts to annotate corresponding brain regions across species often relied on empirical methods, lacking the rigorous quantification needed for robust validation. By contrast, our analytic approach integrates quantified neuron sampling, minimal alignment error, evidence-based regional correspondence and probabilistic neuron localization. Our approach also uses molecular profiles to validate genetic similarities between cross-species brain regions. This framework demonstrates generalizability, allowing for the creation of a morphological atlas of the human brain with an increasing number of neurons. It may also be extended to explore conserved and divergent features in other mammalian species and other biological modalities.

Our study utilizes cell type ratios to investigate genetic associations between brain regions across species based on their anatomical, functional or homologous relationships. Our analysis depends on the quality of the respective scRNA-seq and MERFISH datasets. For instance, this correspondence is consistent in group 1 (mouse MERFISH and human snRNA-seq) but less correlated in group 2 (mouse scRNA-seq and human snRNA-seq). This discrepancy can likely be attributed to the low spatial resolution of the mouse scRNA-seq data, which leads to the mixing of multiple regions within the same dataset. By contrast, although MERFISH offers high spatial resolution, it may be limited by the size of the gene set, which could also contribute to the observed differences in regional correspondence. These factors should be considered, particularly for motor-related regions such as the PrCG and MOp. Because the correspondence analysis is based on cell type composition, regional differences in excitatory and inhibitory neuron proportions may influence these cross-species matches (Supplementary Table 8). These discrepancies constitute non-negligible influence on the regional correspondence, which may reflect genuine biological differentiation between species but could also stem from sampling, microdissection, annotation or other technical factors within the original datasets47,48. We also note that the differences in neuron composition between VIS subregions (for example, V1 and V2) could be significant40,64. It will be important to explore these regional correspondences further when higher spatial resolution and more complete whole-brain transcriptomic data become available.

We acknowledge that there is ongoing debate about the extent to which meaningful cross-species comparisons can be made and whether such comparisons should occur at the brain region level, the cellular level or even the subcellular level65–68. Future work incorporating molecular signatures, cell-type-specific markers and physiological properties will help refine correspondences among brain regions, cell types and subcellular features across species.

Building upon the established progressive increase in complexity across species7,18,58, a key future question is to quantify how much of this increased morphological regionalization specifically co-varies with distinct anatomical, functional or molecular properties of cortical regions. Data from intermediate species such as non-human primates will be essential to construct evolutionary gradients linking neuronal morphology with other cortical specializations.

In addition to cross-species comparisons, we examined whether the lobe separability observed in human single-neuron morphology could be reflected in other data modalities, such as gene expression, EEG, MEG, cortical geometry and metabolism. Our quantitative analysis indicates that gene expression shows the highest degree of lobe separability, highlighting the importance of molecular differences in distinguishing brain regions. By contrast, separability based on physiological signals such as EEG and MEG tends to be task dependent, which may not generalize across different cognitive tasks or resting-state conditions. Metabolic and cortical geometric features showed weaker or more limited lobe differentiation in the present analysis. Future studies with larger multimodal datasets may clarify how neuronal morphology, molecular identity, physiology, metabolism and cortical geometry jointly define cortical regions. Our results suggest that a comparative analysis of single-neuron intrinsic features could serve as a valuable framework for future comprehensive studies of cross-species brain structures and functions.

Reconstruction of mouse local morphology

The sparsely labeled fMOST mouse brains were previously introduced by Peng et al.2, where the raw brain images are publicly available through the Brain Image Library (http://www.brainimagelibrary.org). The brain samples used in this study are identified by their respective image IDs. Species, strain background, sex, age, animal number and other relevant metadata are detailed in Supplementary Table 9.

The preprocessing and reconstruction followed the similar procedures described in Liu et al.5. However, there were several modifications. We used 16-bit highest-resolution images with a size of 1,024 × 1,024 × 256 voxels. We applied the NIEND (Zhao et al.69) image enhancement algorithm to the brain images. We only used the APP2 algorithm for neuron tracing.

Human specimens and reconstruction of human local morphology

A total of 23 human cases were included in this study. Cases H001−H019 were obtained in our previous study6, and four additional surgical cases (H020, H021, H022 and H023) were acquired from Beijing Tiantan Hospital for the present study. Overall, the cohort included 22 surgical cases from Beijing Tiantan Hospital and one case of donated brain tissue from the Human Brain and Tissue Bank of the China National Clinical Research Center for Neurological Disorders. These cases included 11 males and 12 females, aged 18−71 years. Informed consent was obtained in all cases, and all research procedures involving human tissues were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital.

The procedures, including human specimen acquisition, brain tissue preparation, sectioning, neuron labeling, neuron imaging and neuron tracing, were performed as follows. Surgically resected human cortical tissues were immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer solution (0.1 M, pH 7.4) within 5−10 minutes after surgery or within 20 minutes for large tissues. Tissue blocks larger than 1 cm3 were trimmed before fixation and stored at 4 °C for approximately 24 hours. Fixed tissues were rinsed, embedded in 3% agarose and sectioned into 200−300-μm slices using a vibratome in 1× PBS. The slices were further fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 3−5 days. Single neurons were labeled by intracellular injection of Lucifer Yellow CH lithium salt under an upright differential interference contrast microscope. Borosilicate glass pipettes with 1−2-μm tips were filled with 4% Lucifer Yellow in PBS, and neuronal somata located 30−50 μm below the slice surface were injected using a negative current of 10 nA for 15−20 minutes. Labeled neurons were imaged using a Bruker Ultima Investigator two-photon fluorescence microscope with a ×25 water immersion objective. Lucifer Yellow was excited at 900 nm, and fluorescence was collected using a 565-nm long-pass dichroic beam splitter and an ET525/70 m-2p emission filter. z-stack images were acquired after defining the x, y and z imaging ranges for each neuron. Neuronal reconstructions were generated from the image stacks and subsequently proofread using CAR as described below. All 23 samples were obtained from cortical regions classified as normal based entirely on the neurosurgeonʼs clinical judgment, which integrated preoperative MRI with intraoperative visual and tactile assessment. Detailed information on extracted brain tissues from each patient is summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Crowdsourcing-based reconstruction proofreading using CAR

Reconstructed neurons of mice and humans were organized and managed using the CAR platform36. Proofreading was performed by a team comprising one expert annotator and four additional trained annotators, to ensure accuracy and consistency of the neuronal reconstructions.

Mouse brain registration and neuron mapping

Brain images were aligned to 25-μm CCFv3 templates using the cross-modal alignment tool mBrainAligner29,70. We used a similar approach described in these papers. Registered channels were utilized whenever possible. Prior to alignment, the resolution of the brain was reduced to approximately 25 μm. Non-brain tissue was semiautomatically removed using Vaa3D71–73. Neuronal local morphologies were inversely mapped into CCFv3 space using the inverse deformation field.

Human brain registration and sampling region mapping

All patients involved in this study had complete MRI data. On the basis of the operation records and the preoperation and postoperation MRI data, experienced surgeons provided the annotated segmentation indicating the neuron sampling regions. Preference was given to using preoperative T1 magnetic resonance images of the patient’s brain for alignment and sampling region outlining.

For most images, the neck region was first cropped out while ensuring that the complete brain tissue was retained. Then, we automatically stripped skulls using the Brain Surface Extractor (BSE) tool in BrainSuite (version 23a). The results of skull stripping were refined by BrainSuite if needed. Next, the brain images were registered onto the ICBM 2009c Nonlinear Asymmetric template (a template of ICBM 152 nonlinear atlases) using mBrainAligner. Segmentations of neuronal sampling regions were inversely mapped into ICBM 152 space using the inverse deformation field.

Evaluation of mouse neuron sampling

To assess the spatial homogeneity of neuronal sampling within each mouse cortical region, we first extracted the 3D somite coordinates of the reconstructed neurons in the CCFv3 reference space. We then computed all pairwise Euclidean distances between these coordinates to determine the spatial distribution of the sampled mouse brain data. As a theoretical benchmark for fully homogeneous sampling, we generated a 3D grid of points filling the same region volume, equally spaced along the x, y and z axes, and calculated the pairwise distances between these grid points. Finally, for visual comparison, we smoothed the histogram of ideal distances by fitting a 20th-order polynomial to obtain a continuous reference curve.

Neuron morphology standardization

The optimal neuron cropping radius was set to 50 μm based on the average extension range of human neuronal z axis reconstruction (Extended Data Fig. 1a). Spherical cropping was performed following a depth-first search traversal initiated from the soma. During traversal, each node was evaluated for its spatial distance relative to the soma. When the traversal first encountered a node with the distance greater than the cropping radius (located outside the sphere), that node and all of its successor nodes were excluded from further processing (Extended Data Fig. 1e).

We calculated the number of primary branches after assembling branches within a spherical range (1−20 μm). Using the number of primary branches from neurons in the same brain regions in SEU-ALLEN1741 as a reference, we calculated the Jensen−Shannon divergence (JSD) between the distribution of primary branches in the processed data and that in the reference data. Based on the smallest JSD value, we determined the optimal radius to be 8 μm for correcting the soma topology (Extended Data Fig. 1b).

The reconstructions in SWC format were first processed by the multi_bifurcations_processing function from https://github.com/Vaa3D/vaa3d_tools/blob/master/hackathon/shengdian/NeuroMorphoLib/neuro_morpho_lib.h and resampled to 5-μm spacing between continuous nodes.

Data integration

The overall integration was conducted by Seurat version 5 (ref. 76) (R package, version 5.0.3). For the mouse scRNA-seq and human snRNA-seq data, the integration follows the procedure described below. Gene counts of each cell were normalized to a total of 10,000, and then a natural logarithm was applied. The process was implemented using the NormalizeData function with default parameters. The top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) were identified using the FindVariableFeatures function with default parameters. After scaling the data and running principal component analysis (PCA), 50 principal components were generated. The data were split by their batch keys (mouse data: regarded as a whole; human data: donor ID) into several layers. To manage computational load, we sketched the merged dataset to 15% of total cells using the LeverageScore method implemented in the SketchData function. The sketched data were integrated using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) through the IntegrateLayers function. Finally, CCA dimensional reductions learned on the sketched cells were extended to the full dataset using the ProjectIntegration function.

For the mouse MERFISH and human snRNA-seq data, there are slight variations in the parameters. The total gene counts of each cell were set to 1,000. All genes were set as HVGs. Mouse data also split by different donors. Ten percent of cells were used for the construction of sketch data.

Integrated ranking of genetic similarity

Besides the average Pearsonʼs correlation coefficient described in the last section, we also calculated the cosine similarity based on each PCA representation. Additionally, according to the cell-type-count coding of each brain region, we constructed the cell-type-count matrices for each species. We used χ2 tests to calculate the cell types enriched in each brain region. We calculated the ratio of the number of observed cells to the expected number of cells (Ro/e) for each cluster in each species78. We recorded the first M cell types with the highest Ro/e values in each brain region and calculated the Jaccard index between different brain regions. We sorted all three similarity metrics (correlation coefficient, cosine similarity and Jaccard index) in descending order, respectively, and recorded the average rankings between a particular region and every other region of another species.

Classification of layer 2/3 neurons

We used the H01 dataset79 and manually selected 1,600 high-quality pyramidal neurons as reference data to train a three-class layer classifier (L2/3, L5 and other). Morphometric features were computed on 100-µm cropped reconstructions, and all features were z-score standardized with a StandardScaler fitted only on each training partition. Dimensionality was reduced by PCA fitted on the training data and retaining 99% of explained variance. Model selection and evaluation employed stratified five-fold cross-validation. An XGBoost classifier configured for multiclass probability outputs was used as the supervised model; hyperparameters for tree number, learning rate, depth, regularization and sampling fractions were optimized by randomized search using balanced accuracy as the objective; and class imbalance during training was mitigated by supplying per-sample weights computed to balance class frequencies. The resulting tuned parameter set was fixed for the final classifier, and then the classifier was fit on all data. For the final prediction on our dataset, the same scaler and PCA objects from training were applied, and a linear domain alignment based on correlation alignment (CORAL) was used to reduce domain mismatch. Prediction uncertainty was quantified using the maximum predicted class probability and the normalized Shannon entropy of the probability vector. High-confidence classifications were defined by thresholding these statistics at their empirical medians in the predicted data, and predictions failing either threshold were labeled uncertain and excluded from further analyses.

Statistical test and effect size calculation

A linear mixed-effects model was fitted using the MixedLM function from the statsmodels (version 0.14.0) package. We assessed the morphological feature comparison based on the fixed effect of the ‘species’ group, setting human ‘subject’ as random effects. We employed maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) with the reml = False option in the MixedLM model fitting process. The optimization used the Broyden−Fletcher−Goldfarb−Shanno (BFGS) method to estimate the model parameters, and convergence was monitored using default tolerances. The maximum number of iterations was set to 5,000, with a tolerance of 1 × 10−6. All reported Pvalues were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction.

Cohenʼs d was reported as the standardized effect size for mean differences between species, with positive values indicating larger feature values in the mouse group relative to humans.

We performed statistical comparisons between groups using a weighted Mann−Whitney U-test. The analysis was conducted in the R environment using the Ecume package (version 0.19.0) for each test80. Reported P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction.

Morphology type clustering

Unsupervised clustering of dendritic morphology features was performed to identify distinct morphological neuron types on layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons. Both basal and apical dendritic features were used. All features were z-score normalized using the StandardScaler implementation in scikit-learn. PCA was used to reduce dimensionality while preserving at least 99% of the total variance. The preprocessed data matrix was then subjected to hierarchical agglomerative clustering using Wardʼs minimum variance linkage criterion on Euclidean pairwise distances.

To determine the optimal number of clusters, multiple complementary metrics were evaluated, including the average Silhouette coefficient, Calinski−Harabasz index and Davies−Bouldin index. The final cluster number was selected by the aggregation of metric-specific rankings.

The resulting clusters were visualized using UMAP (n_neighbors = 15, min_dist = 0.1, metric = ‘euclidean’) applied to the principal components. Two-dimensional UMAP embeddings were used to display the spatial separation between clusters. For visualization of individual neuron morphologies, SWC skeletons were re-projected into the UMAP plane by translationally aligning the soma to their embedding coordinates.

Quantification of feature separability

The features including both basal and apical dendrites sorted by mRMR (pymrmr, Python package, version 0.1.11) were sequentially used in classification. We standardized the morphological features using z-score normalization. Dimensionality reduction was then performed using PCA, selecting principal components that collectively explained 95% of the variance. We employed a five-fold cross-validation technique and made folds retain the percentage of samples in each category when splitting the dataset. For classification, we used a random forest classifier (ensemble.RandomForestClassifier function in the scikit-learn Python package, version 1.2.2) with fixed parameters: max_features = ‘sqrt’ and class_weight = ‘balanced’. A grid search was conducted to optimize the hyperparameters, specifically examining the following ranges: n_estimators = [100, 200, 300, 400, 500], min_samples_split = [2, 3, 4] and max_depth = [4, 5]. The modelʼs performance was evaluated using the balanced accuracy score, and the configuration yielding the highest average test score was selected as the optimal model.

Multimodal lobe separability

Cortical geometry

We applied FreeSurfer81 (version 7.4.1) software on the ICBM 2009c template to measure the anatomical geometry of brain structures by running the recon-all command. The region-level statistics were derived from the rh.aparc.stats file. We also ran the mri_annotation2label command with the –lobesStrict parameter to get a lobe annotation. Then, we re-called the mris_anatomical_stats command to generate the geometric statistics at the lobe level. We verified the structural segmentation using Freeview, a visualization tool in FreeSurfer. In total, 24 brain regions were measured using surface area, average thickness, average curvature and Gaussian curvature. The concrete values were recorded in Supplementary Table 6.

Physiological signals

Data acquisition

We recorded synchronized EEG and MEG data (collectively known as MEEG) from nine participants, aged 22−28 years, during resting and task states. The group comprised three females and six males. Each participant was placed in a dark room and equipped with a 61-sensor MEG recording helmet and a 22-sensor EEG system. The MEG sensors were mounted on a helmet fixed to an operation bed in a magnetically shielded environment, where the participants were instructed to lie down. The EEG sensors were applied using the 10-20 system under medical supervision, with an additional sensor placed on the chest to monitor the electrocardiogram (ECG). The ground electrode was attached to the left arm while the reference electrode was placed on the prefrontal cortex. Both EEG and MEG signals were sampled at a rate of 1,000 Hz. Initially, participants were asked to close their eyes, relax and maintain a resting state for approximately 2 minutes while the MEEG data were recorded. Each 2.8-second interval was treated as a separate trial. In the task state, participants were instructed to perceive and imagine a random shape, with each trial consisting of 1 second of preparation, 0.2 seconds of perception and 1.6 seconds of post-imagery. The sizes of shapes were equal. There were 540 trials for each shape.

Data preprocessing

The MEEG data were preprocessed using the MNE-Python package (version 1.7.1). We automatically processed all trials, starting with filtering the data using a passband of 2–80 Hz, applying a 50-Hz notch filter for EEG data and a 22-Hz notch filter for MEG data. Baseline correction was performed based on the preparation period, and channels with relative amplitudes exceeding 0.0004 V for EEG and 40 fT for MEG were excluded. Next, the data were decomposed into 15 independent components using independent component analysis (ICA), and artifact components representing heartbeats and eye blinks were manually identified and removed.

Feature extraction

For each trial, we extracted 13 features used for the calculation of MJSD from both EEG and MEG signals. These features included latency of maximum value, latency of minimum value, latency of maximum amplitude, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, differential entropy, zero-crossing ratio, outlier ratio and four band power ratios. The power ratios corresponded to the following frequency bands: delta (0.1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–14 Hz), beta (14–30 Hz) and gamma (30–100 Hz).

MEEG sensor labeling

The 21 EEG sensors are of the international 10-20 sensor placement system, and the 61 MEG sensors were designed by Beijing X-MAGTECH Technologies. All sensor coordinates were registered to ICBM 2009a Nonlinear Symmetric 1 × 1 × 1-mm template approximately. Then, we performed edge detection on the brain mask of the ICBM template and identified a total of 198,128 edge voxels as the sources. To evaluate the electric or magnetic field contribution, we calculated the Euclidean distance between each sensor coordinate and each voxel. We selected the nearest 100 voxels, and the CerebrA label of the brain region with most enrichment was noted as the label of one sensor.

Metabolism

Data on global metabolic status were taken from Table 2 of Aanerud et al.62. Both mean and standard deviation were averaged by the left and right brain hemispheres.

Molecular

Molecular data were collected using mouse MERFISH data47 and human snRNA-seq data48. The genes on the X and Y chromosomes were excluded. A series of genes associated with mitochondria, heat shock proteins, ribosomes and dissociation were also excluded. The latter gene list was taken from Supplementary Table 1 of Xue et al.83. Excitatory neurons were classified under the following categories in the ‘supercluster_term’ column: ‘Upper-layer intratelencephalic’, ‘Deep-layer intratelencephalic’, ‘Deep-layer corticothalamic and 6b’ and ‘Deep-layer near-projecting’. Inhibitory neurons were categorized as ‘MGE interneuron’, ‘CGE interneuron’ and ‘LAMP5-LHX6 and Chandelier’ in the same column. We processed the data using the Scanpy (version 1.9.8) Python package. The gene count matrix was sequentially applied with normalize_total, log1p (scale.factor = 1,000) and pca (50 principal components) functions. We categorized cells into three lobes (FL, PL and TL) and used top 50 principal components as their feature vectors to calculate the pairwise MJSD.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were determined by the availability of human specimens, publicly available mouse brain imaging datasets, publicly available transcriptomic datasets and samples passing predefined quality control criteria. Data were excluded only according to the filtering and quality control procedures described above. No additional data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. During neuron tracing and proofreading, investigators did not use information on species, brain region or group allocation, as these steps were based on neuronal morphology in the image stacks. Blinding was not applicable to downstream registration and statistical analyses because species, anatomical region and dataset labels were required for these analyses. Quantitative feature extraction and statistical analyses were performed using standardized procedures and computational pipelines.
