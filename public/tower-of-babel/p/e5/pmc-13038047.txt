Increased Xanthine Oxidoreductase Activity in Patients With Systemic Sclerosis-Associated Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Julie C Coursen, Tijana Tuhy, Adrianne Woods, Laura K Hummers, Ami A Shah, Paul M Hassoun, Rachel L Damico, Stephen C Mathai, Catherine E Simpson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreclinical studies have demonstrated the importance of xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) in the pathobiology of pulmonary hypertension (PH) through generation of uric acid, reactive oxygen species, and vascular endothelial dysfunction.Research QuestionIn 2 well-characterized cohorts of patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc), what is the relationship of systemic XOR enzymatic activity to PH diagnosis, development, and severity?Study Design and MethodsSerum samples from 48 patients with systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary hypertension (SSc-PH) were assayed for XOR activity using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in a discovery cohort from the Johns Hopkins Pulmonary Hypertension Program, including 12 index treatment-naive patients with samples collected before and after 36 weeks of PH-specific therapy. Sixty-nine patients with SSc-PH were compared with patients with SSc without PH in a second cohort from the Johns Hopkins Scleroderma Center using a nested case-control design. Patients in the Johns Hopkins Scleroderma Center cohort were under longitudinal surveillance for development of right heart catheterization-confirmed PH, which allowed for serum collection at proximate and distant time points before PH diagnosis (1 proximate sample within 12 months of PH diagnosis and 1 distant sample within 5 years of PH diagnosis, with a minimum 6-month separation between samples).ResultsXOR activity temporally correlated with SSc with pulmonary arterial hypertension disease development, and increasing XOR activity over the time preceding PH diagnosis was associated with worse disease severity by invasive hemodynamics. In a small subset of patients, XOR activity increased after initiation of PH-specific therapy.InterpretationTaken together, these results suggest increased XOR activity and hyperuricemia may represent modifiable risk factors in SSc-PH.Key Words: pulmonary arterial hypertension, scleroderma

Take-Home Points

Study Question: What is the relationship of xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR) activity to diagnosis, development, and severity of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) in patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc)?

Results: XOR activity and its byproduct uric acid increased in the time preceding diagnosis of PAH on right heart catheterization in patients with SSc, and higher rates of XOR increase were associated with worse hemodynamic severity at time of diagnosis.

Interpretation: XOR activity and hyperuricemia may represent modifiable risk factors in early PAH.

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a syndrome of progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling that culminates in right-sided heart failure if left untreated.1 PH is a frequent complication of systemic sclerosis (SSc), representing an inflection point in the disease, because patients with systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary hypertension (SSc-PH) experience increased mortality compared with patients with SSc without PH.2 Patients with SSc with pulmonary arterial hypertension (SSc-PAH), a form of precapillary PH, demonstrate poorer responses to PH-specific therapies compared with other pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) phenotypes.3,4 Given these disparities in clinical outcomes, identification of traits associated with PH risk and prognosis is of particular interest in SSc.

We and others have previously demonstrated that circulating uric acid (UA) levels are higher in SSc-PAH compared with SSc without PH, are associated with disease severity, and predict mortality in SSc-PAH.5 In light of this, UA is incorporated into the DETECT algorithm for predicting risk of PAH in SSc.6 Others have shown higher UA levels correlate with worse pulmonary hemodynamics7,8 and predict mortality in idiopathic PAH.9,10 UA is a metabolic byproduct of the enzyme xanthine oxidoreductase (XOR), which catalyzes the final step of purine metabolism in mammals, in which xanthine is converted to UA in a reaction that also generates reactive oxygen species.11 Preclinical studies have shown circulating XOR binds directly to vascular endothelium, induces endothelial cell death, and inhibits nitric oxide production.12,13 XOR is a source of reactive oxygen species in pulmonary arterial (PA) endothelial cells.14 Purine metabolites downstream of XOR, including UA, are associated with right ventricular (RV) oxidative injury and remodeling in animal models of PH,15 and lung UA worsens occlusive vasculopathy in PH.16 Abnormal UA metabolism has been found in PA smooth muscle cells in both experimental and human PH.7 Provocatively, XOR inhibition with allopurinol improved hemodynamics and reversed RV remodeling in a mouse model of PH.17

Despite the breadth of preclinical findings in experimental PH, little is known about XOR activity in human PH. Given the importance of UA in predicting PAH in SSc, and the role of XOR upstream of UA, we hypothesized that measured XOR would itself be a robust PH biomarker in SSc. Because currently available drugs lower UA via inhibition of XOR enzymatic activity (eg, allopurinol, febuxostat), we considered that significant XOR associations with PH risk and progression could represent a potentially treatable trait. To investigate XOR in SSc-PH, we leveraged 2 distinct but complementary SSc cohorts (1) to compare XOR activity among patients with SSc with and without PH cross sectionally, and (2) to evaluate the kinetics of XOR activity longitudinally as patients with SSc develop PH.

Study Design and Methods

Patient Cohorts

Two cohorts were investigated for this study (Fig 1). The first cohort was from the Johns Hopkins Pulmonary Hypertension Program (JHPHP), which maintains an institutional review board-approved registry of patients referred to our clinic. Registry patients contribute to a biorepository that accrues blood specimens via convenience sampling. Reasons for referral of patients with SSc to the JHPHP have been previously published.5 Forty-eight patients with SSc with PH and 23 patients with SSc without PH with both clinical data and banked serum were identified. A subset of 12 patients with PAH from the JHPHP had serum samples available at time of PAH diagnosis and again after 36 weeks of PH-specific therapy.

Overall study schematic. PH = pulmonary hypertension; SSc = systemic sclerosis; SSc-PAH = systemic sclerosis with pulmonary arterial hypertension; SSc-PH = systemic sclerosis-associated pulmonary hypertension.

The second cohort was from the Johns Hopkins Scleroderma Center (JHSC), which maintains a large research registry and companion biorepository.18 Samples are collected from consenting patients with SSc who contribute serum longitudinally to the biorepository during routine care. Patients are regularly monitored for PH development with annual pulmonary function testing and echocardiography and are referred to the JHPHP if there is concern for probable PH.5 Within this larger observational cohort, we identified case patients who developed SSc-PAH while under surveillance and contributed at least 2 serial samples to the JHSC biorepository (including 1 proximate sample within 12 months of PAH diagnosis and 1 distant sample within 5 years preceding PAH diagnosis, with minimum 6-month separation between samples). Cases were matched 1:1 to control patients with SSc who did not develop PH based on age, sex, and duration of SSc, as previously described.19 SSc was diagnosed by expert opinion in accordance with American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism classification criteria.20

For this study, PH was defined as any mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) ≥ 25 mm Hg, and PAH was defined as mPAP ≥ 25 mm Hg with pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ≤ 15 mm Hg and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) ≥ 3 Wood units, in the absence of significant left-sided heart disease, chronic lung disease, or thromboembolic disease, because this was the definition at time of cohort enrollment.21 We enrolled adult patients only; otherwise, aside from sample availability requirements, there were no specific inclusion or exclusion criteria.

XOR enzymatic activity was quantified by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay of peripheral serum samples using the commercially available Amplex Red Xanthine/Xanthine Oxidase Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific).22 Two JHSC patients with SSc-PAH were excluded from downstream analyses due to an insufficient sample for XOR assay.

Statistical Analysis

The data were summarized with descriptive statistics, and continuous variables are reported as means ± SDs (for normally distributed data) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) (for nonnormally distributed data). Untransformed XOR activity measures were compared in case (SSc with PH) vs control (SSc without PH) patients using t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests, as appropriate. For regression modeling, XOR activity levels were rescaled and standardized such that the mean was set to 0 and the SD set to 1 within each cohort, which yielded Gaussian distributions. Linear and logistic regression with adjustment for age and sex was performed to examine relationships between XOR activity levels, clinical characteristics, and the odds of PAH diagnosis at right heart catheterization (RHC). Missing data were minimal, and complete-case analyses were performed. Model assumptions, including linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity, were assessed and met. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to characterize XOR’s ability to discriminate SSc-PH from at-risk patients with SSc. In the subset of patients who had pretreatment and posttreatment XOR activity levels measured, Wilcoxon sign rank tests of paired samples were performed. P < .05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp).

Results

Among those with PH, most had PAH (n = 40), whereas 6 had PH related to interstitial lung disease and 2 had PH associated with left-sided heart disease. Patients with PH had a mean mPAP ± SD of 37 ± 12 mm Hg and a mean PVR ± SD of 7 ± 5 Wood units. XOR Activity Kinetics With Evolution of SSc-PAH in the JHSC Cohort

We turned to the JHSC surveillance cohort for assessment of XOR activity kinetics with disease evolution. Demographics and clinical characteristics of the JHSC cohort are listed in Table 1. Similar to the JHPHP cohort, patients were mostly female (88%) and White (75%). The mean mPAP ± SD was 39 ± 13 mm Hg, and the mean PVR ± SD was 9 ± 6 Wood units. In contrast to the JHPHP cohort, which was drawn from patients referred for RHC for suspected PH and therefore captured some patients with mildly elevated pulmonary pressures, the patients without PH in the JHSC cohort did not have clinical findings suggestive of PH to prompt RHC referral. All patients with PH in the JHSC cohort had PAH.

With unadjusted comparison, absolute XOR activity was significantly increased in patients with SSc-PAH (median, 1.71; IQR, 1.30-2.11 mU/mL) compared with patients with SSc without PH (median, 1.11; IQR, 0.95-1.32 mU/mL) (P < .001) (Fig 4A). Age- and sex-adjusted logistic regression showed patients with SSc with higher XOR activity were significantly more likely to have PAH: each SD higher XOR activity level was associated with 4-fold increased odds of PAH at RHC (OR, 4.07; 95% CI, 2.22-7.45; P < .001). ROC analysis demonstrated good discrimination of PAH from no PH, with an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI, 0.75-0.89) (Fig 3B). Adding XOR to UA alone for prediction of PAH increased the AUC from 0.73 (95% CI, 0.64-0.85; for UA alone) to 0.83 (95% CI, 0.73-0.92) (Fig 3B), but this improvement was of borderline significance (P = .07). In the JHSC cohort, XOR activity and UA modestly correlated at time of PAH diagnosis (correlation coefficient, 0.31; P < .001).

Two years before diagnosis, there was no difference in XOR activity in patients who would go on to develop PAH compared with those who would not develop PH. Pretreatment vs Posttreatment Differences in XOR Activity

A subset of 12 patients within the JHPHP cohort were index, treatment-naïve cases with paired pretreatment and posttreatment samples. Eleven of the 12 patients were female and White (91%). The mean PA pressure ± SD at diagnosis was 41 ± 11 mm Hg and the mean PVR ± SD was 7 ± 4 Wood units. For each patient, the initial sample was drawn at diagnosis, and the posttreatment sample was drawn after 36 weeks of combination therapy with ambrisentan and tadalafil. In this subset of patients, XOR activity was significantly higher posttreatment than pretreatment (P = 3 × 10-3) (Fig 6).

Discussion

Serum XOR activity levels rose over the time preceding PAH diagnosis in patients with SSc under surveillance, and steeper rates of XOR rise were associated with higher PVR and lower cardiac output at diagnostic RHC. Although the sample size was small, our pretreatment/posttreatment analysis suggests XOR activity levels continue to rise despite PH-specific therapies. XOR activity was modestly correlated with UA in the JHSC cohort, but not significantly correlated with UA in the JHPHP cohort. Although XOR activity levels provided good discrimination of SSc-PAH from SSc, XOR did not significantly improve the predictive capacity of UA as a disease biomarker. To our knowledge, ours is the first clinical study to demonstrate that increasing XOR activity is an early event in PAH development that correlates with disease severity at diagnosis.

Our results complement preclinical data that collectively suggest XOR-derived oxidative injury contributes to PAH development and progression.15,26, 27, 28 Oxidative stress markers correlate with mortality in PAH,29 and markers of nucleotide turnover and metabolism are associated with PAH hemodynamic severity.30 However, although XOR measurements might reflect relevant pathobiologic information, our findings show XOR does not add significant value as a biomarker beyond discrimination offered by UA. This may be because XOR and UA are biologically linked in a common metabolic pathway, and the effects of XOR activity are sufficiently captured by UA levels. Unlike XOR measurements, UA levels reflect upstream purine metabolism and downstream renal handling, potentially capturing a more integrated measure of redox stress and metabolism. Practically, UA is chemically stable and reliably measured in serum, whereas XOR activity can be labile and sensitive to sample handling. In weighing these practical considerations and our statistical comparisons, we do not plan additional efforts to develop XOR as a complementary disease-specific biomarker.

Whether pharmacologic XOR inhibition has therapeutic potential in PH remains unclear. The XOR inhibitors allopurinol and febuxostat have demonstrated mixed results thus far as therapeutic adjuncts in cardiopulmonary diseases. UA is associated with risk of coronary artery disease (CAD),31 but allopurinol failed to provide benefit in older adults with ischemic heart disease.32 Febuxostat has had uneven performance, resulting in decreased UA level and improved composite event rate (driven by nephroprotective effect) among patients with hyperuricemia in the Febuxostat for Cerebral and Cardiorenovascular Events Prevention (FREED) study,33 yet worsened all-cause and cardiovascular mortality compared with allopurinol in the Cardiovascular Safety of Febuxostat or Allopurinol in Patients with Gout (CARES) study.34 In heart failure, urate-lowering therapy was associated with improved all-cause mortality in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction,35 but in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, allopurinol failed to improve exercise capacity, quality of life, or left ventricular ejection fraction.36 Urate-lowering therapy in PH has also shown variable results, albeit has been less frequently studied. In a small cohort of patients with PH with chronic lung disease, allopurinol reduced RV mass in a subgroup analysis of patients with COPD-associated PH, but did not improve 6-minute walk distance, quality of life, or spirometry.37

The factors underlying discrepant results in studies of XOR inhibition are uncertain. One possibility is genetic polymorphisms affect the efficacy of XOR inhibition, as a Mendelian randomization study showed differing effect of allopurinol on patients with a genetic variant in XDH, the gene that encodes XOR.38 Additionally, effects of XOR inhibition may vary with disease duration. Our study demonstrates XOR activity rises early in SSc-PAH, near time of clinical diagnosis. This raises the possibility that increased XOR activity and hyperuricemia may represent modifiable risk factors, particularly in subclinical or early disease. A prospective study in patients with CAD found elevated UA levels were associated with cardiovascular events for patients with 0 or 1 standard modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, even more so than for patients with ≥ 2.39 Together with our findings in early SSc-PAH, these results suggest a potential role for urate-lowering therapy to modify disease early in the clinical course.

Although hyperuricemia is an established risk factor for multiple cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, heart failure, and CAD,31 its role in primary risk reduction remains unclear. The UA threshold at which hyperuricemia increases cardiovascular risk is undefined, yet one study suggests serum UA levels as low as 5.6 mg/dL (within normal range in reference assays) may be required for optimal CVD risk reduction.40 If hyperuricemia is indeed a modifiable risk factor for PH development, as our study posits, there may be opportunities for metabolic regulation via pharmacologic (eg, allopurinol, febuxostat) and nonpharmacologic means. Nonpharmacologic approaches to UA reduction include lifestyle modification, such as changes in diet, alcohol intake, or body weight. If further studies support our findings, patients with SSc may be considered a novel at-risk group for urate-lowering therapies to prevent or modify PAH development.

Our observation that XOR activity continued to rise in patients on therapy warrants replication and cautious interpretation. One possible explanation is oxidative stress in PH is unmitigated by current therapies, and the XOR increases we observed in surveilled patients with SSc before PH diagnosis continue unchecked after diagnosis. Other possible explanations involve the potential for phosphodiesterase-5 inhibition to intersect with redox balance and purine metabolism via several biologically plausible mechanisms, including enhanced nitric oxide signaling, increased guanosine nucleotide turnover in cyclic guanosine monophosphate salvage or degradation pathways, and increased adenosine triphosphate turnover from treatment-induced metabolic remodeling. Although merely speculative at present, if compensatory or rebound processes such as these are elucidated in experimental studies, preventative XOR inhibition during PH treatment initiation may be worth dedicated study. In this study, none of the patients analyzed pretreatment/posttreatment were prescribed XOR inhibitors.

This study has some limitations. Given the rarity of SSc and PH, the cohorts were modest in size. Some of our results are of borderline significance or not consistently replicated across cohorts, and these findings should be interpreted with caution. The JHPHP and JHSC rely on different protocols for sample processing and storage, which could introduce variability; however, all assays were performed using standardized materials, protocols, and instrumentation with consistent personnel to minimize technical variation. We measured XOR activity in peripheral circulation because serum is most feasible for biospecimen collection in the clinical setting. Although our findings are consistent with studies in other biological compartments (increased XOR activity has been demonstrated in the pulmonary vasculature of experimental and human PH and the RV in rodent PH7), we cannot be certain that our serum measurements reflect these compartments. Our study focuses on SSc-associated PH, and generalizability is uncertain in application to other forms of PH; however, increased serum XOR activity has been described in idiopathic PAH.41

The complementary nature of the cohorts and our ability to sample subsets of patients at multiple time points are strengths. There were more substantial differences in XOR activity in patients with PAH vs patients without PH in the JHSC cohort, possibly related to differences in cohort composition. The JHPHP cohort was developed through referral of patients with SSc to RHC for suspected PH. Therefore, patients without PH may not be completely free of cardiopulmonary disease because they met referral triggers for RHC. This contrasts with the JHSC cohort, in which patients without PH never displayed features concerning for PH development. These differences may explain the borderline statistical significance in the JHPHP cohort, vs clear differences in the JHSC cohort. Multiple comparisons raise the potential for inflated type I error. However, a Bonferroni correction for 15 hypothesis-driven tests, yielding an adjusted P < 3 × 10-3, does not affect the significance of key results in the JHSC cohort.

Interpretation

Our results show that XOR activity measurements are associated with PH presence and severity, but as biomarkers, they do not add significant discriminative value to UA measurements available clinically. Aberrant XOR activity was shown to temporally coincide with SSc-PAH development; however, additional mechanistic studies are required to better understand underlying pathobiology. Increased XOR activity and hyperuricemia may represent treatable traits, particularly in early disease or within a risk reduction framework. Our findings suggest that future studies examining XOR inhibition as a therapeutic adjunct may be best undertaken in subclinical or early cardiopulmonary disease.
