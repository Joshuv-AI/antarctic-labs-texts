Addressing the overlooked psychological and social impact of fecal incontinence in inflammatory bowel disease patients
Authors: Xinli Chen, Wenjun Wang, Jianyu Lv
Published: 2024

Editors,

We read with great interest the study by Wespi et al. on fecal urgency and incontinence among patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). 1 The authors provided a detailed analysis of the prevalence of these symptoms and their impact on quality of life, emphasizing their clinical significance. This research offers valuable insights into the care and management of IBD patients, particularly by highlighting these common but often overlooked symptoms to clinicians. However, we believe it is necessary to further discuss the psychological and social impacts of these conditions on patients.

Clinical guidelines indicate that fecal incontinence significantly affects patients' psychological health and social well‐being. 2 Due to the embarrassment and shame associated with these symptoms, many patients often experience anxiety and depression, avoid social activities, and become isolated. The private nature of these symptoms also makes patients reluctant to openly discuss them with healthcare providers. The guidelines emphasize that physicians should proactively inquire about these issues to prevent further deterioration of patients' mental health. While the article mentions a decline in the quality of life, it does not sufficiently explore the psychological and social dimensions.

The impact of fecal urgency and incontinence on mental health and social life is profound. These symptoms cause emotional distress, exacerbating anxiety, shame, and depression, especially due to the unpredictability of incontinence episodes. Patients often avoid going out or participating in social activities for fear of accidents, further intensifying feelings of isolation. Additionally, long‐term anxiety and depression may negatively affect disease management. 3 , 4 Unfortunately, the article lacks an in‐depth discussion of psychological interventions such as counseling, cognitive behavioral therapy, or patient support groups, which are crucial for alleviating emotional burdens and improving the quality of life. Future research should explore the effectiveness of these psychological interventions in IBD patients and include longitudinal studies to better understand the relationship between mental health and symptom management.

As healthcare professionals, we must pay attention not only to patients' physical symptoms but also to their psychological and social needs. Actively and sensitively inquiring about fecal incontinence and urgency during consultations can help reduce the stigma associated with these issues. Moreover, providing psychological support services, such as counseling or patient support groups, can help patients manage the anxiety and isolation resulting from their symptoms. Building trust and encouraging patients to express their emotional concerns can offer holistic care that goes beyond symptom management.
