Primary age-related tauopathy
Authors: Timothy E Richardson, Jamie M Walker, Kurt Farrell, Tiago Gil Oliveira, Charles L White, John F Crary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrimary age-related tauopathy (PART) was proposed in 2014 as a neuropathological term to describe patients with Alzheimer’s-type medial temporal lobe neurofibrillary degeneration in the absence of significant β-amyloid pathology. Over the past decade, this designation has gained widespread adoption, helping to clarify the interpretation of biomarker profiles, delineate early-stage tauopathy in aging, and differentiate non-Alzheimer tauopathies from aging and classical Alzheimer disease. This review revisits PART ten years following its conception, critically evaluating its neuropathological features, clinical correlates, molecular underpinnings, and current limitations. We synthesize recent advances in neuroimaging, biomarkers, genetics, and epidemiology, explore the relationship between PART and other age-associated neurodegenerative processes, and propose revisions to the original PART criteria. While PART has served as a valuable framework for studying tau pathology in aging, key questions remain regarding its pathogenesis, clinical significance, and relationship to the broader spectrum of tauopathies. We highlight major gaps in knowledge and outline priorities for future research aimed at defining the mechanisms, biomarkers, and clinical criteria that will determine whether PART represents a distinct disease or a universal feature of human brain aging.Keywords: Primary age-related tauopathy (PART), Alzheimer’s disease neuropathologic change (ADNC), Limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy neuropathologic change (LATE-NC), Cornu ammonis 2 (CA2) hippocampal subfield, CA1 hippocampal subfield, Aging, Cognitive reserve, Resilience

Introduction

Since Alzheimer disease (AD) was first described in the early twentieth century [10, 45] it has been recognized as the most common neuropathologic finding associated with cognitive decline and dementia. AD neuropathologic change (ADNC) is based on the presence, extent, and distribution of abnormally hyperphosphorylated tau (p-tau), primarily in the form of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), and diffuse and neuritic β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques, and current diagnostic criteria requires the presence of both of these protein deposits [178]. Accumulation of Aβ and p-tau occurs in interrelated hierarchical spreading patterns [31, 238], proceeding in concert through stereotypical stages, although there are a number of subtypes of ADNC with unconventional p-tau and Aβ distribution patterns, including Aβ-predominant, posterior cortical atrophy, hippocampal-sparing, and limbic-predominant variants [4, 40, 117, 144, 181]. These defining lesions have shaped how we conceptualize age-related cognitive decline. However, there remains a disconnect between the clinical and pathologic findings, which may be explained to a degree by evolving disease definitions, the historical inclusion of other tauopathies under the umbrella of “AD”, and the presence of co-pathologies and their interactions with AD [92, 109, 142, 159, 204, 212, 222, 233].

The development of NFTs without significant Aβ deposition was first described as a distinct pattern in 1992 [268], and originally given designations such as “tangle-only dementia”, “neurofibrillary tangle-predominant dementia”, and “tangle-predominant senile dementia”, among others [13, 14, 90, 98, 99, 103, 119, 120, 301, 302]. This pathologic presentation has been found in many independent cohorts, and prompted reexamination of the conventional AD framework. Ultimately, this led to the proposal of a new neurodegenerative entity, distinct from ADNC, termed “primary age-related tauopathy” (PART) by Crary and colleagues in 2014 [54], which introduced a new nomenclature and working diagnostic criteria. The goal was not just nosological, it was also to facilitate clearer communication across disciplines, provide a framework to distinguish p-tau pathology without Aβ from classic ADNC, spur mechanistic research into tauopathy and cognitive aging, and address diagnostic ambiguity in antemortem vs. post-mortem assessments.

In the past decade, the designation of PART has achieved widespread adoption with a growing body of evidence to suggest that PART is a distinct process from ADNC (and other Aβ-independent tauopathies) with divergent histopathology, genetics, biochemistry, biomarkers, and clinical correlates. However, the “PART hypothesis” (i.e., that such cases represent a distinct entity) has been controversial, and debate continues as to whether PART should be considered part of the ADNC spectrum [26, 30, 54, 58, 63, 117, 118, 187]. PART has served as a useful heuristic for explaining amyloid negative/tau positive (A-T +) biomarker cases, expanded understanding of normal brain aging, and indirectly stimulated refinement of related diagnoses. Its utility lies not only in delineating a tauopathy subtype but in serving as an approach to studying aging. This review critically revisits the PART concept considering the expanding research corpus of the past decade and proposes recommendations for revised PART criteria. We evaluate its strengths and limitations, synthesize new data across multiple domains (i.e., neuropathology, clinical correlates, genetics/epigenetics, imaging, and biomarkers) and highlight remaining gaps and opportunities for future discovery.

Histopathologic definitions and features of PART in the context of ADNC

The current neuropathologic consensus criteria for ADNC were created in 2012, and define and stage ADNC based on the presence, density, and extent of p-tau and Aβ deposits as they progress throughout the brain. P-tau aggregates are thought to originate in the brainstem early in life [28, 29, 33] before they progress to the transentorhinal and entorhinal cortex (EC), hippocampus and other limbic structures, and finally neocortex and striate cortex in six described hierarchical Braak stages [5, 25]. Within the hippocampus and its adjacent structures, p-tau accumulates in the pyramidal neurons of transentorhinal cortex (stage I) before progressing to layer 2 pre-α clusters in the entorhinal cortex (stage II). In stage III, the p-tau pathology of stages I-II worsen, and the pyramidal cells in CA1 and/or CA2 are involved, with subsequent involvement of the CA3 and CA4 subregions in stage IV and granular cells of the dentate gyrus in stage V [27]. Aβ is thought to begin to accumulate in the neocortex and progress to the EC and CA1 hippocampal subfield, deep gray nuclei and other regions of the hippocampus, brainstem, and cerebellum in five Thal phases [263], initially co-occurring spatially with p-tau at the level of the hippocampus [280]. The density of neuritic plaques is assessed in the neocortex according to Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD) criteria [173]. These three separate but interrelated measures are combined to determine an overall level of ADNC (Fig. 1) [96, 178], which generally corresponds to the level of cognitive impairment, although cognitive impairment has been shown to correlate best with the Braak stage and CERAD neuritic plaque (NP) score components [93, 159, 186, 239]. This progression is thought to be relatively consistent for “typical AD”, although there are a number of subtypes which may have variations on this pattern [4, 40, 117, 144, 181].

PART is, by definition, a neuropathological diagnosis, currently lacking validated antemortem clinical or biomarker criteria, which is thought to differ significantly from defined subtypes of AD [113]. Macroscopically, brains with PART are typically unremarkable or may show a range of atrophy of medial temporal lobe (MTL) structures. Microscopically, PART is an Aβ-independent tauopathy, which is largely restricted to the MTL, corresponding roughly to Braak stage I-IV [54], although in rare cases there may be sparse p-tau immunoreactive NFTs in the superior temporal lobe or middle frontal lobe [18, 287]. PART is separated into two categories (Table 1): definite PART, which is defined by MTL p-tau deposition in the complete absence of Aβ (Thal phase 0 and CERAD NP score “absent”) and possible PART, which consists of a similar level of MTL p-tau pathology with mild Aβ deposition (restricted to Thal phase ≤ 2 and CERAD NP score of “absent” or “sparse”) [54]. Notably, while definite PART always corresponds to “not” ADNC by National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) guidelines, possible PART overlaps entirely with low-level ADNC (Fig. 1) [178]. PART exhibits a spectrum of p-tau severity, from mild pre-tangle pathology to fully formed NFTs, however staging and reproducibility remain a challenge. Braak staging is still the dominant framework, but it lacks granularity in early stages where PART may be most distinct. Additionally, there is high inter-rater variability in distinguishing early Braak stages, and many cases fall near the threshold between PART and low-level ADNC.

There is debate as to whether there truly exists a "PART-specific" progression pattern in MTL structures, separate from the progression of AD [32, 58, 280, 285]. A notable feature of PART in many cohorts is a predilection for early and severe neurofibrillary degeneration in the cornu ammonis 2 (CA2) hippocampal subfield (often preceding involvement of the CA1 subfield and preceding or concurrent with involvement of the entorhinal cortex), assessed by both semi-quantitative assessments and computer-assisted quantitative positive pixel counts of AT8-stained sections [90, 101, 115, 179, 216, 231, 259, 278–280, 283, 285]. This is in contrast to ADNC, in which the CA2 subfield is often spared from severe p-tau pathology until later in the disease process (Fig. 2), although there can be significant variation between individuals with ADNC in this subregion in particular [25, 33, 58]. Like ADNC, PART frequently has NFTs in locus coeruleus (LC) neurons (Fig. 3a, b), but early cases may have extensive CA2 NFT pathology with relative preservation of the transentorhinal cortex, layer 2 pre-α clusters of the entorhinal cortex, and CA1 subregion (Fig. 3c–f), suggesting that there may be a different pattern of progression from the classic Braak stages of ADNC. There may also be significant neuronal loss, sclerosis, and extracellular “ghost” tangle deposition as well as tangle-associated neuritic clusters (TANCs) [176, 180] in the CA2 subregion of PART cases (Fig. 3g, h), and this subregion may be affected by Lewy body disease (LBD) and other tauopathies, including argyrophilic grain disease (AGD) and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) [2, 21, 51, 66, 102, 165].

Some studies have suggested that PART may have more extracellular tangles compared with ADNC [119], but p-tau pathology is generally higher in ADNC than definite or possible PART in all hippocampal subregions except for CA2. The CA2/CA1 ratio of p-tau deposition is significantly higher in PART compared to ADNC, and this progressively decreases with increasing Aβ progression and hippocampal Aβ burden [231, 280]. At Thal phase 3, the point at which currently accepted definitions change from classifying cases as PART to definitively being on the ADNC spectrum, the CERAD NP score significantly predicts the CA2/CA1 p-tau ratio [285], which suggests that PART (in particular possible PART) and ADNC may form a continuum, and that the presence, location, quantity, and form of Aβ significantly affects the deposition patterns of p-tau.

Additional studies have suggested that CA1 and the EC may be spared in some subgroups of the oldest old [274, 275], and non-demented elderly subjects without senile plaques can be separated into those with prominent CA2 p-tau pathology and those with relative sparing of the CA2 subregion in comparison to the EC and CA1 subfield [101, 259, 275, 280]. Interestingly, an artificial intelligence (AI)-based study constructing a histopathologic brain age estimation model based on 689 PART subjects demonstrated a robust ability to predict chronologic age and showed significant preferential attention weights in the CA2 subfield, as well as white matter adjacent to the hippocampal formation [162]. Other research found relative preservation of CA1 neurons in normal aging [293] and preferential loss of synapses in the CA2 subfield in PART subjects (with relatively preserved CA1 and EC synapses), corresponding to increased local p-tau pathology patterns [179, 283, 289]. Synapse and dendritic spine loss are one of the best anatomical correlates of cognitive impairment in neurodegenerative disease [258], and this has been correlated with p-tau pathology in humans and animal models [47, 111, 168, 170, 277].

PART is somewhat more likely to feature asymmetry in p-tau pathology and Braak stage compared with ADNC (and this asymmetry is often most pronounced in the CA2 subregion), which may form a source of cognitive reserve, although early stages of both disease processes (and other neurodegenerative disorders) may have significant asymmetry, with variable effects on clinical presentation [134, 171, 223, 253, 279]. Moderate to severe cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is present in 25–60% of ADNC cases (and has been shown to be associated with cognitive impairment, independently of ADNC), but is more rarely found in definite PART (3–13%) and is found with an intermediate frequency in possible PART cases (16–38%) [3, 24, 64, 230, 231, 247, 252, 286]. This suggests that the parenchymal and intravascular Aβ deposition processes are linked, but it is notable that CAA may occur in brains completely devoid of parenchymal Aβ [286].

On the other hand, the NFTs present in PART are morphologically and immunohistochemically indistinguishable from those of ADNC. Although CA2 is also specifically affected by some 4-repeat (4R) tauopathies [102], the NFTs of both PART and ADNC are composed of paired helical filaments and are positive for 3-repeat (3R) and 4R p-tau (Fig. 3i, j) [54, 228, 285]. These paired helical filaments in ADNC and PART also have identical ultrastructure with electron cryo-microscopy (cryo-EM), which is also identical to p-tau in Down syndrome and the posterior cortical atrophy (PCA) variant of ADNC, indicating that the presence of Aβ plaques is not necessary to induce the “Alzheimer fold” [76, 234, 241, 243]. This is not the case with other Aβ-independent tauopathies; 3R tauopathies (Pick disease [PiD]) and 4R tauopathies (progressive supranuclear palsy [PSP], corticobasal degeneration [CBD], AGD, and globular glial tauopathy [GGT]) have distinct cryo-EM structures/folding patterns, and CTE (another 3R/4R tauopathy) has a folding pattern which separates it from ADNC and PART [65, 76, 80, 206, 240, 241, 243].

CA2 hippocampal subfield structure, function, and pathology

The CA2 hippocampal subfield represents a distinct anatomical and functional region with unique molecular characteristics and extensive connectivity patterns that distinguish it from neighboring areas. CA2 is a relatively straight hippocampal region containing the most densely packed pyramidal cell layer, anatomically located between the CA1 and CA3 subfields [66, 100, 183, 224, 255, 280], although clear separation between CA2 and CA3 has long been controversial [100]. CA2 may be distinguished from CA3 by its lack of mossy fiber projection from the granular cell layer of the dentate gyrus and its absence of thick dendritic spines, but may also be defined from CA1 and CA3 by differing immunohistochemical features and unique molecular expression [62, 100, 149, 150, 195]. Mouse, non-human primate, and human studies have demonstrated various projections to CA2 from diverse brain regions, including the amygdala (basolateral subnucleus), hypothalamus (supraoptic nucleus [SON], paraventricular nucleus [PVN], and supramamillary nucleus [SuM]), nucleus basalis of Meynert, medial septal nucleus, dorsal Raphe nucleus, locus coeruleus, and collateral branches of the perforant pathway from pre-α clusters of EC [56, 61, 100, 140, 184, 201, 218, 244, 272]. CA2 forms reciprocal projections with the EC, as well as projecting to and modulating the output of both CA1 (where the dorsal CA2 projects to ventral CA1 in rodents, equivalent to posterior CA2 projecting to anterior CA1 in humans) and CA3, the medial and lateral septum, and cortical areas associated with social cognition [56, 100, 140, 169, 184, 201, 218, 225].

The precise function of CA2 in humans is not fully understood. Studies suggest it is involved with modulating complex pre-existing circuitry in CA1 and CA3 [169, 201], recognizing novelty in social stimuli, and may be important for discrimination between safety-associated and threat-associated conspecifics in mice [7, 128, 195, 218], encoding social memory [42, 60, 91, 169, 201, 248, 256], social recognition and learning/encoding face name pairs (anterior CA2 may be involved in learning while posterior may be involved with recall) [62, 257], and may be involved with temporal encoding and episodic memory [160]. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies of blood oxygen level-dependent response, reflecting neural activity of medial temporal lobe substructures, showed significant uptake in CA2 (as well as CA3 and dentate gyrus) during encoding of face-name association pairs, but not during retrieval [308].

CA2 demonstrates distinct patterns of vulnerability and resistance across neurodegenerative and neurological conditions that may relate to specific cognitive functions. In models of temporal lobe epilepsy, CA2 pyramidal neuron excitability is increased and CA2 inhibition was shown to reduce the frequency of spontaneous seizures [295]. The CA2 subfield itself is relatively resistant to hypoxia and excitotoxicity compared to CA1, however it is significantly affected by Lewy body pathology and CTE [2, 21, 44, 62, 66, 165, 195]. It has also been suggested that CA2 is a subregion with frequent NFT degeneration in 4R tauopathies, particularly in the presence of AGD [102], but this has not been a consistent finding in other studies [11, 70, 145, 172, 227, 265]. In LBD, CA2 had a higher burden of α-synuclein pathology compared with any other hippocampal region [2], consisting mostly of α-synuclein-immunoreactive neurites and axon terminals, while the EC had more Lewy bodies, suggesting that the reciprocal pathways between EC and CA2 identified in mice and non-human primates [2, 42, 61, 225, 299] may be involved with this pathology, particularly since the α-synuclein burden in CA2 was strongly correlated with that in EC [2]. Facial recognition has been shown to be impaired in LBD [52] and aging [55], and may be preserved with damage that selectively spares the CA2 region (including hypoxic-ischemic injury and epilepsy) [62, 232], suggesting that CA2 may play a role in facial recognition. Global cognition and most other selective memory deficits are correlated more with accumulating α-synuclein pathology in CA1 in both neuropathologic and imaging studies [2, 59, 158], which is consistent with the known amnestic effects of ADNC with high CA1 burden as well as amnesia caused by selective CA1 damage [316], so CA1 may be more functionally relevant despite being affected later with LBD.

The CA2 subregion undoubtably has numerous functions that are interrelated with CA1, CA3, the EC, and the hypothalamus, but assigning specific functions to this region has proven challenging. Pathology in CA2 pyramidal neurons likely has minimal effects on global cognition, but may have numerous subtle effects, particularly with aspects of social memory and as a mediator of hippocampal excitability. It is possible that standard neurocognitive and neuropsychological testing does not fully capture potential understated deficits associated with progressive pathology developing somewhat specifically in the CA2 hippocampal subregion, and thus clinical profiles of large cohort studies may miss or underestimate symptoms of PART.

Epidemiology of PART

A study of 2,332 brains from 1 to 100 year old subjects, irrespective of cognitive status, demonstrated that p-tau continued to progress with age but Aβ appeared to plateau after 90 [33]. By age 70, virtually all individuals had some degree of p-tau pathology in the MTL, however approximately 20–25% of these same individuals never developed Aβ up to 100 years of age, suggesting that in the general population approximately 25% of subjects may never develop Aβ. In subjects with normal cognition (global CDR = 0), median Braak stage is between I-II at age 65 and II-III at age 85, Thal phase between 1–2 at age 65 and around 2 at age 85, and CERAD NP score “absent” or “sparse” at both ages 65 and 85 (corresponding to an overall ADNC level of “low”). This suggests that the average subject with intact cognition at these ages has pathology on the PART spectrum, and many of these individuals could be classified as definite PART. By comparison, the entire National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC) dataset, irrespective of global CDR, has a median ADNC level of “intermediate” for subjects aged 65–85, with Braak stage III-IV, Thal phase 3–4, and CERAD NP scores between “sparse” and “moderate” [284, 288].

PART is a nearly ubiquitous pathology in aging cohorts, but its prevalence varies depending on the neuropathological criteria used. Early studies prior to the introduction of the term PART estimated it to occur in < 10% of demented subjects, but more recent studies that do not require the presence of cognitive impairment have identified PART in approximately 20–30% of subjects over 80 years of age [3, 6, 16, 18, 19, 33, 54, 97, 116, 133, 167, 188, 212, 230, 261, 262, 279, 281, 285, 304], although the prevalence was approximately 41% in The 90 + Study [219]. Definite PART (cases completely devoid of Aβ) is less common, generally found in 2–10% of subjects, although it may be found as frequently as 18–20% in some cohorts [3, 14, 33, 54, 146, 159, 185, 188, 189, 191, 219, 230, 281]. Definite and possible PART are also common in centenarians with relatively intact cognition [189, 273, 310, 311]. These findings are in keeping with the observation that a degree of neurofibrillary degeneration is virtually ubiquitous with aging, irrespective of cognitive status [23, 43, 54, 189, 202, 203, 230, 250, 273, 284, 288]

While early studies suggested a female predominance in subjects with tangle-only dementia, there is no consensus on a gender predisposition in PART subjects among more recent work. The age of symptom onset/cognitive decline and age of death is generally reported to be in the 9th to 10th decades, older than that of ADNC subjects [3, 8, 9, 14, 18, 19, 53, 54, 92, 116, 119, 120, 125, 133, 147, 185, 220, 221, 228, 230, 261, 262, 281, 285], although a few studies have found a younger mean age of PART (or otherwise Aβ-negative) subjects [32, 58, 94, 210, 305]. PART pathology has also been documented in younger individuals [6, 94], although it is unclear if these younger subjects would have eventually developed ADNC or if they would have continued to be Aβ-negative later in life. The age at death in PART subjects tends to increase along with increasing Braak stage, which suggests that p-tau pathology is progressing in the absence of Aβ development in a subset of people, and the probability of having PART rises with age from 80–100, suggesting that PART does not necessarily progress to ADNC in many cases, although this observation may be affected by survivorship bias [33, 188, 189].

Genetics of PART

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) is the most consistent genetic factor associated with late-onset AD (LOAD). The worldwide allele frequency is estimated to be 77.9% for ε3, 13.7% for ε4, and 8.4% for ε2, although this varies by ethnic group [68, 153, 214]. The presence of at least one APOE ε2 allele reduces the risk of sporadic AD and increases the age at which cognitive symptoms occur [48, 68, 153, 214]. Conversely, the presence of at least one APOE ε4 allele is associated with a 2–15 fold increase in the risk of sporadic AD and decreased age of symptom onset [41, 49, 68, 153, 207, 214], although the ε4/ε4 genotype may raise the risk of AD by as much as 33 fold in Asian cohorts [68, 174]. This strong association has led some to propose that homozygosity for APOE ε4 essentially represents a genetic form of AD [72]. The presence of ε4 may also increase the risk of LBD [196, 267] and limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy neuropathologic change (LATE-NC) [292, 303], as well as increase the risk for developing poly-proteinopathy, although this link is less clear and may be driven primarily by ADNC progression [92, 159, 284].

PART has the opposite association with APOE. At the population level, the overwhelming majority of studies demonstrate significant enrichment for ε2 and decreased frequency of ε4 alleles. Overall, APOE ε2 was found in approximately 5–10% of subjects with ADNC compared with 10–25% of subjects with PART, while ε4 was found in 35–55% of subjects with ADNC compared to approximately 5–20% of subjects with PART [1, 3, 8, 13, 16, 18, 19, 54, 92, 99, 113, 119, 120, 125, 159, 167, 220, 230, 231, 261, 262, 266, 281, 304]. Some data also shows that possible PART also has lower rates of ε4 compared with ADNC and variable rates compared with definite PART, with no differences in ε2 frequency, suggesting that some cases of possible PART may be part of the ADNC spectrum [3, 53, 54, 220]. These associations are not surprising, given the putative role of ApoE protein in cholesterol metabolism and Aβ production and clearance, with ε4 resulting in increased plaque formation [157]. This may partially explain the difference in Aβ between the two disorders, as well as the difference in p-tau distribution [280], although some studies have also suggested that APOE ε4 may promote p-tau proteinopathy independently of Aβ [242, 300]. Notably, a recent study examining the spread of p-tau and Aβ demonstrated that APOE ε4 carriers were more likely to have earlier and more extensive Aβ deposition, prior to p-tau extending beyond the medial temporal lobe (“amyloid-first”), while those without APOE ε4 were more likely to have a degree of p-tau spread beyond the medial temporal lobe prior to interacting with amyloid (“tau-first”) [4]. This study also demonstrated that “tau-first” APOE ε4-negative individuals had PART-like features, while “tau-first” APOE ε4 carriers may constitute a part of the AD spectrum.

A recent autopsy-based genome-wide association study (GWAS) using Braak NFT stage as a quantitative endophenotypic trait demonstrated associations between PART and several known AD-loci (SLC24A4, MS4A6A, and HS3ST1) and PSP (MAPT and EIF2AK3), as well as a novel association on chromosome 4q28.2 near JADE1, SCLT1, and C4orf33 [67]. JADE1 protein co-localizes with both 3R/4R- and 4R-tau aggregates, binds 0N4R p-tau, and attenuates neurofibrillary degeneration in a Drosophila melanogaster model. However other work has demonstrated the lead variant (rs56405341) may be more significantly associated with C4orf33 mRNA expression which is significantly downregulated in both AD and PSP [313]. The MAPT H1 haplotype was associated with PART as well as other Aβ-independent tauopathies and subtypes of ADNC in several studies, although this finding may be inconsistent between studies/cohorts and nonspecific to PART [75, 113, 181, 198, 215, 228, 230]. Other studies have revealed that certain BIN1 and PTK2B genotypes in PART may be associated with reduced AD risk [144, 167], and additional work in centenarians has suggested single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in APOE, ANKH, GRN, TMEM106B, SORT1, PLCG2, and RIN3 as potential resilience factors against AD [260], but more studies are needed to delineate the genetic risk for PART.

Spatial, single cell, and multi-omics studies

Few studies focusing on single cell and spatial -omics have focused on PART thus far. A recent manuscript examined single cell transcriptomics comparing PART and ADNC [254], although it should be noted that transcriptomics does not always correlate well with proteomics in AD [121]. The authors found similar overall gene expression patterns between ADNC and PART in CA1. Synaptic genes were downregulated in both PART and ADNC compared with control subjects, which is similar to previous work in ADNC and aging [17]. Paradoxically, synaptic genes involved with calcium regulation and pre-synaptic vesicle exocytosis were upregulated in NFT-bearing neurons compared with non-NFT-bearing neurons in both PART and ADNC. Both of these findings were more pronounced in ADNC than PART. The discrepancy may reflect a compensatory mechanism [193], although some studies suggest that p-tau-positive neurons are not necessarily the most susceptible to death in ADNC [82, 317].

Other recent work investigating epigenetic differences between PART and ADNC [81] has identified specific methylome associations with p-tau severity and variation (independent of Aβ). Machine learning classifiers stratified cases that fell between PART and ADNC by neuropathologic criteria, which translated into significant differences in cognitive performance and cognitive trajectory between cases predicted to be PART and ADNC. This study also suggested that synaptic signaling pathways correlate with early p-tau severity in the hippocampus in both PART and ADNC, while neuroinflammatory genes were associated with frontal cortex p-tau severity in ADNC only.

Studies on the quantitative proteomics of NFT-bearing and non-NFT-bearing neurons in specific hippocampal subregions using a panel-based approach demonstrated that unbiased clustering of cells by protein composition can reliably distinguish between ADNC, definite PART, possible PART, and CTE (another Aβ-independent tauopathy) [216]. Many of these proteins also segregated into groups of definite PART/CTE and possible PART/ADNC, suggesting that the proteomic composition of NFT-bearing and non-NFT-bearing neurons is either driven by the presence/absence of Aβ or that the protein milieu is significantly affecting the degree and distribution of Aβ deposition [280, 285]. A number of differences in the pathways driving Aβ production, processing, and regulation were different between definite PART and possible PART/ADNC, suggesting that possible PART may be more similar to (and part of the spectrum of) ADNC than it is to definite PART [216, 283]. Other differences between these disorders included specific p-tau epitopes, proteins involved with inflammation, gliosis, microglial regulation, oxidative stress, autophagy, and general proteostasis. Lower regional Aβ density and progression were associated with increased quantities of proteins associated with protein degradation and autophagy in addition to specific Aβ regulation and processing, indicating that these proteins are regulating local Aβ through either more effective degradation or prevention of accumulation.

Proteins related to oxidative stress response were also increased in definite PART [216, 283], suggesting a different environment for regulating and mitigating oxidative stress compared with ADNC [192]. There were also differences in proteins related to neuronal health and synaptic function that inversely correlated with the local p-tau burden in both NFT-bearing and non-NFT-bearing neurons [283]. These findings are similar to results from resilient individuals (those with significant ADNC pathology for their age but with retained cognition [177, 284, 288]), where there was a maintenance of neuronal integrity (healthier axons and synapses), higher levels of proteins associated with degradation of misfolded proteins, lower inflammatory markers, and less oxidative stress compared with cognitively impaired individuals with ADNC [282].

There is also evidence of p-tau seeding into the neocortex in both ADNC and PART using real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) assays, which demonstrate that p-tau seeding is much more widespread throughout the brain at earlier time points than is found with standard clinical neuropathologic workups [36, 74, 130]. This could suggest that categorizing PART by CERAD neuritic plaque density and not Thal phase may capture a more biologically meaningful patient population [36]. Region-specific tau seeding is present before the development of identifiable neuropathology and occurs in both ADNC and PART, is significantly higher in ADNC compared with PART, and the severity is correlated with both Thal phase and CERAD NP plaque score in the frontal cortex (but not hippocampus). This indicates that Aβ pathology is involved in the progression of p-tau beyond the hippocampal structure and into the neocortex and/or that Aβ is required for p-tau seeds to form neocortical NFTs [37]. This was partially hampered by the inclusion of both definite and possible PART in this study (and the inclusion of a case with “moderate” CERAD NP plaque score as PART), but this finding may partially explain why neocortical p-tau is only rarely observed in PART [287].

Fluid and imaging biomarkers

ADNC and PART are first and foremost autopsy-based neuropathologic diagnoses, and there are currently no validated biomarkers that reliably distinguish PART from early AD or other tauopathies. Yet, the past decade has seen substantial developments in fluid and imaging biomarkers that may eventually allow PART to be reliably identified in living individuals. Recent development of in vivo antemortem biomarkers for p-tau and Aβ have led to the creation of a category termed suspected non-Alzheimer disease pathology (SNAP) [57, 108] and the development of A/T/N (Aβ/p-tau/neurodegeneration) criteria [106, 107, 137, 269]. SNAP emerged as a catch-all for individuals with neurodegeneration biomarkers (N +) in the absence of Aβ (A-), and many of these cases are now hypothesized to represent PART or another Aβ-independent disease category.

Aβ and p-tau positron emission tomography (PET) has become a cornerstone in AD research, but its application to PART remains limited. [11C]-Pittsburgh compound-B (PiB)-PET [135, 182] can reliably demonstrate the presence of Aβ and [18F]-flortaucipir (FTP)-PET [71, 122, 226, 235, 249, 264] identifies p-tau in vivo, and these may topographically recapitulate Thal phase and Braak stage, respectively [112, 122, 156, 235, 237]. While some studies suggest that FTP-PET can differentiate between ADNC levels and PART, it has been shown that it has relatively non-specific uptake with limited ability to detect PART [126, 148]. Most p-tau PET ligands are most effective at detecting high-density neocortical tau pathology, typical of AD. PART, by contrast, involves lower levels of p-tau, largely restricted to MTL structures, and often below the threshold of PET detectability. MTL retention may be observed in cognitively normal older adults with A-T + profiles, sometimes interpreted as PART, but specificity is unclear. Additionally, the spatial resolution of current tau PET may limit its ability to discriminate signal in subfields (e.g., CA2 and subiculum) that are more selectively involved in PART. Additionally, structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and [18F]-2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (FDG)-PET may give information on the overall level of neurodegeneration, although issues remain around the thresholds for this modality [20, 108]. Studies combining these modalities demonstrate a temporal sequence in individuals with ADNC in which detectable Aβ develops early, followed by p-tau and neurodegeneration markers [86].

The application of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and plasma biomarkers within the AT(N) framework also includes an A-T + category, which many consider a fluid biomarker signature of PART. CSF Aβ1-42, Aβ1-40, p-tau, and total tau and plasma p-tau181 and p-tau217 biomarkers may have the potential to discriminate between PART and ADNC, as CSF p-tau181 and p-tau217 are elevated in AD, but not substantially in PART [88, 307]. Using A/T/N criteria, recently revised for ADNC diagnosis, prognosis, and staging by the Alzheimer’s Association Workgroup [105], A + /T + /N + likely represents typical ADNC (with or without comorbidities), A + T-N ± may be “preclinical” ADNC, A-T + N ± may be PART or “preclinical” AD (or another tauopathy such as CTE, PSP, CBD, etc.), and A-T-N + could represent an unrelated disorder, such as LBD, LATE-NC, or cerebrovascular disease (CVD), in the absence of ADNC [106–108]. Unlike postmortem neuropathologic diagnosis, these modalities can be used to follow changes in pathology and changes in disease over extended periods of time, and provide invaluable longitudinal information on the ways in which these diseases progress and interact with other neurodegenerative processes to cause neurodegeneration [151]. Plasma tau assays are improving, but signal from early-stage MTL pathology may be too subtle for reliable detection. Total tau (t-tau) and neurofilament light (NfL) are nonspecific and may reflect comorbidities or general neurodegeneration. Thus, although PART is used to explain A-T + biomarker profiles, this remains an inference, not a validated in vivo diagnosis. Nevertheless, imaging techniques are generally superior to fluid-based biomarkers, as they have the ability to provide information about the regional vulnerability and stage alongside the presence or absence of a particular protein accumulation, and may be able to discern unusual disease subtypes [69].

Despite these limitations, biomarker studies have enabled an approach to estimating the prevalence of PART beyond autopsy series. Approximately 25% of MCI patients and 23% of cognitively normal individuals over the age of 65 have SNAP [108], and individuals over the age of 85 are almost all positive for p-tau with FTP-PET [315], while 13–30% of subjects are classified as A-T + [291, 304]. Importantly, a recent study indicated that patients with negative Aβ and p-tau limited to the MTL by PET imaging generally had slow progression of p-tau without development of Aβ signal, and p-tau remained largely confined in the MTL, suggesting that they were not progressing towards ADNC, and demonstrating a longitudinal signal that may represent PART [50]. Some cohorts of A-T + individuals had a relatively low frequency of APOE ε4 and intermediate levels of cognitive dysfunction between A-T- and A + T + subjects, but this group was heterogeneous; 29% had p-tau restricted to MTL while 32% had a more ADNC-like pattern, so some of these subjects likely were truly PART while others may eventually develop Aβ [304]. Radiologic and CSF biomarkers of neurodegeneration are also present in the absence of Aβ, indicating that a measurable degree of neurodegeneration is occurring in PART [136, 138, 139, 188, 276, 296]. FTP-PET studies also demonstrate that there may be some age-related p-tau deposition in the frontal and parietal regions, independent of Aβ, giving evidence that p-tau may have limited spread outside of the MTL in PART, but PET studies also suggest that concomitant Aβ and p-tau have a synergistic effect with much further p-tau progression, more severe atrophy, and worse cognition than either process in isolation [110, 290].

Structural neuroimaging studies also enable assessment of atrophy, another marker with relevance to disease progression and stage (Fig. 4a). There is increased atrophy of the medial temporal lobe with increasing Braak stage, and when controlling for Braak stage there was a trend toward increased atrophy of anterior and medial temporal lobe with age in definite PART [125, 209], although medial temporal lobe and hippocampal atrophy are also very common in ADNC [199]. There was also an association between Braak stage and atrophy of the left anterior hippocampal head with sparing of the posterior aspect, which may relate to preservation of episodic memory [125]. In comparison to a cohort with ADNC, PART subjects had better maintenance of right hippocampus, bilateral amygdala, temporal neocortex (superior, middle, and inferior temporal gyri), parietal neocortex (left superior/inferior gyri, right inferior gyrus), frontal (bilateral rostral middle gyri, bilateral medial orbitofrontal gyri, right superior gyrus), and left posterior and anterior caudal cingulate volume (Fig. 4b), and cognitive deficits correlate more directly with regional atrophy in ADNC [9, 210].

CVD burden correlates with T2 MRI white matter hyperintensities (WMH), which have been associated with axon loss and myelin rarefaction, ischemia, and small vessel disease in antemortem and postmortem imaging studies [87, 152, 175, 190, 205]. WMH and CVD can be assessed with Fazekas grading scores, which is a predictor of AD progression and independent predictor of cognitive impairment with ADNC [84, 97, 194, 208]. There is a significant correlation between Fazekas grading and regional atrophy in both PART and ADNC, but PART appears to have a stronger correlation than ADNC (particularly in the fronto-insular region), even though the overall level of CVD as assessed by the Fazekas grading scores is not different, suggesting that CVD may have a larger overall contribution to regional brain atrophy in PART than AD, and atrophy is less dependent on proteinopathy in these cases [208]. More work is needed to fully distinguish PART and ADNC by imaging and fluid biomarker modalities, and to investigate the impact of other common comorbidities (including LATE-NC) on the radiographic and cognitive progression of both PART and ADNC [200].

Clinical aspects of PART

Numerous studies have been conducted over the past decade focused on the clinical correlates of PART. While many have uncovered deficits in various cognitive domains, the majority of these studies have found that the clinical effects of isolated/pure PART (particularly isolated definite PART) are mild in comparison with ADNC. ADNC independently produces profound amnestic dementia with progressive global and impairment across numerous cognitive domains, including memory, attention, language, judgement, visuospatial, orientation, processing speed, and executive function that all progress with increasing ADNC level and duration of the disease process [8, 9, 92, 93, 96, 131, 159, 178, 186, 233]. The clinical symptoms of ADNC correlate best with Braak stage and CERAD NP score, but not independently with Thal phase, suggesting that there may be more biological value in defining and separating ADNC and PART based on p-tau progression and neuritic plaque density as opposed to Thal phase [12, 159, 186, 239]. While the hierarchical progression of Aβ appears not to independently cause significant cognitive decline, there is evidence that Aβ is fundamental to the pathophysiology of AD [37, 53]. Many of the genetic alterations associated with LOAD and early onset Alzheimer disease (EOAD) affect genes that are known to be associated in some way with Aβ production, processing, and deposition, including APOE [1, 68, 153], amyloid precursor protein (APP) [38, 78, 141, 297, 298], and presenilin 1 and 2 [83, 95]. Despite seeding of p-tau into the neocortex early in both ADNC and PART (which may correlate with domain-specific impairment), hippocampal and neocortical Aβ appears to help the formation of explicit p-tau pathology in regions beyond the hippocampus [1, 36], and it is possible that protein–protein interactions between Aβ and p-tau may be a driver in ADNC and/or that Aβ and p-tau are interlinked processes with a common upstream driver [37, 127, 246]. Recent studies on centenarians have produced similar findings, suggesting that Aβ pathology interacts with tau and is not simply a benign consequence of the aging process, although the density of regional Aβ deposition may be more impactful than simple Thal phasing, and this may also depend on the presence of p-tau [37, 224].

There have been conflicting reports on the clinical manifestations of PART. Symptomatic PART is frequently diagnosed clinically as probable AD, with some reports finding this in more than 50% of cases [261]. However, when examining isolated/pure definite PART, the majority of subjects (~ 55%) are unimpaired, there is no definitive correlation with amnestic dementia, and these subjects are only diagnosed as probable AD in ~ 16% of cases (by contrast isolated/pure ADNC and isolated/pure LATE-NC were diagnosed as probable AD in ~ 77% and ~ 58% of cases, respectively) [92]. Studies have suggested that PART may affect measures of processing speed and executive function, among others [125, 262, 281], and others studies have shown PART to have relative sparing of semantic memory, language, visuospatial performance compared with ADNC [16, 19, 209].

Most groups agree that PART presents with less overt dementia and less severe global cognitive impairment compared with ADNC, and the cognitive impairment is generally of shorter duration with slower rates of decline [3, 8, 9, 14, 16, 19, 54, 101, 119, 147, 159, 186, 210, 221, 230, 261, 262, 281, 285]. Some studies have suggested that possible PART has an intermediate clinical phenotype, although there may be differences in the specific domains affected in the early stages of ADNC and PART [18, 19, 221, 230, 281]. Subjects with very slow rates of progression were also more likely to have minimal or no Aβ by PiB-PET imaging (particularly in the hippocampal regions) and CSF Aβ1-42 [39], and ~ 25% of subjects with normal cognition or MCI had SNAP based on neuroimaging [108]. Longitudinal measures of global cognition demonstrate that subjects with PART have statistically similar outcomes with control subjects lacking significant pathology and significantly better cognitive trajectories than ADNC (Fig. 5a) [8, 9, 16, 262].

Some authors have suggested that cognitive impairment in PART is related to Braak stage [18, 114, 125, 230, 247], while other studies have not found this correlation or have found that the burden of MTL/hippocampal and inferior temporal lobe NFTs and p-tau pathology more significantly affects cognition in PART [3, 97, 114, 125, 163, 280, 285], which has also been demonstrated with FTP-PET imaging [85]. AI algorithms have also been able to identify and quantify NFTs, and AI-detected NFT counts in the MTL and EC were able to predict cognitive status after adjusting for age [163, 245]. In these models, Braak stage may just be a reflection of p-tau burden (i.e., higher Braak stage correlates with higher hippocampal p-tau burden, which in turn correlates with cognitive status), and previous stereological quantification studies have found similar results in the entorhinal cortex, CA1 subregion, and frontal/temporal neocortex of ADNC subjects [22, 35, 77]. Higher NFT burden and Braak stage have also been associated with more rapid decline of language/semantic memory, attention, working memory, visuospatial reasoning [114, 125]. Atrophy of anterior and medial temporal regions has also been specifically correlated with semantic memory/fluency tests and language composite neuropsychology tests [209].

Comorbid neuropathologic processes in PART

A substantial portion of PART subjects have other comorbid neuropathologic processes that influence the frequency and severity of symptomatology in PART, most commonly including CVD, LATE-NC, and LBD, although rates of these co-pathologies vary significantly between some studies [9, 43, 124, 189, 208, 210, 230, 247, 281]. Other pathologies such as frontotemporal lobar degeneration with TDP-43 (FTLD-TDP) and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) may rarely co-occur with PART, but are thought to represent coincidental processes and generally occur at younger ages [132, 261]. Aging-related tau astrogliopathy (ARTAG), composed of p-tau immunoreactive “thorn-shaped" astrocytes, has also been identified in individuals with PART, however this concurrence may be incidental as both are common in brain aging and positively correlated with LATE-NC and CVD, without a direct correlation between ARTAG and PART [129, 143]. Additionally, some recent studies have suggested that TDP-43 and p-tau accumulation in PART and ADNC may be mechanistically linked rather than being separate processes with incidental convergence [123, 154, 217, 247, 312].

It has also become clear in recent years that a significant portion of the cognitive effects that have historically been attributed to ADNC may be the result of comorbid neuropathologies (primarily LATE-NC, LBD, and CVD) acting in concert with ADNC to impair various aspects of cognition [8, 9, 15, 92, 93, 159, 211, 222, 251, 284, 294]. Multivariate logistic regression models which account for these co-pathologies demonstrate that PART has minimal effects on global cognition and specific neuropsychological domains [92, 159]. Many studies have suggested that the overall cognitive effect on specific cognitive domains (including language and memory), and psychological/psychiatric features which may be attributed to PART may be variably affected by the presence of other neurodegenerative disease processes, including LATE-NC (with or without hippocampal sclerosis [HS]), LBD, CVD, white matter myelin loss, AGD, ARTAG, and the degree of hippocampal atrophy, as well as genetic factors [3, 8, 9, 18, 19, 97, 124, 161, 166, 200, 229, 230, 247, 281, 305, 306]. Comorbid neurodegenerative pathologies strongly affect global cognition of PART subjects in a progressive manner, impacting both the magnitude and rate of decline (Fig. 5b), whereas in ADNC comorbid neurodegenerative pathologies progressively impair overall cognition (as well as logical memory and language domains), but may not affect the speed of cognitive decline [3, 8].

Controversy surrounding the PART hypothesis

There remains debate as to whether PART should be differentiated from ADNC [16, 19, 26, 30, 53, 54, 58, 63, 118, 187, 262, 281, 283, 285, 289]. The NFTs are identical between the two pathologies by immunohistochemical, biochemical, and ultrastructural analysis [54, 63, 118, 241, 243, 285], and although PART tends to have a distinct p-tau distribution pattern in the hippocampus [280, 285], p-tau distribution has a spatiotemporal relationship to Aβ and appears to exist on a continuum [236, 280]. It is also possible that a subset of PART cases evolve into ADNC or alternatively ADNC may develop as a secondary process on top of PART (effectively “overwhelming” the hippocampal PART pathology). PART pathology could also develop concurrently with ADNC, a scenario that is indistinguishable from simply ADNC with current neuropathology protocols [54, 63]. Determining the temporal sequence of events in autopsy studies has historically been complicated by the fact that there is only a single neuropathologic datapoint from which to draw conclusions for the entire pathologic sequence. With the incorporation of structural MRI, fMRI, FTP-PET, and PiB-PET, it is clear that some cases progress from an A-T + N + state with presumed PART or PART-like pathology to ADNC. This recognition may evolve with the increased deployment of antemortem biomarkers to evaluate patients with PART and ADNC [4, 164]. Recent neuroimaging work has indicated that there may be divergent subtypes of p-tau and Aβ progression and interaction in ADNC and PART which are driven to some degree by genetics and individuals with ADNC may have either “amyloid-first” or “tau-first” pathologic patterns [4]. Similarly, the recent approval and incorporation of therapies targeting Aβ into clinical practice may shed light on the interplay between p-tau and Aβ in the coming years [89, 197, 213, 270, 309], although co-pathologies in these subjects may complicate this picture [92, 159, 271].

The majority of current data indicates that PART is an independent non-amyloid dependent pathological process. PART cohorts generally have a very old median age, often in the 9th and 10th decades at symptom onset and patient death, while the incidence of ADNC tends to peak in the 7th and 8th decade and decline afterward (alongside the APOE ε4 allele frequency), suggesting that many of these subjects would never progress to ADNC [23, 54, 118, 189, 202, 230, 250, 284, 288]. Neuropathologic and antemortem biomarker studies also indicate that 20–25% of those over 90 do not develop Aβ, while p-tau pathology is almost ubiquitous [33, 189]. While some studies have shown that p-tau frequently precedes Aβ deposition, and PART is a frequent finding in younger subjects in large autopsy studies, the post-mortem identification of PART cases likely includes individuals that would have eventually progressed to ADNC, as well as those that would have maintained a more PART-like trajectory. While the spread of p-tau does not necessarily require the presence of Aβ, there is a significant trend of thought leaning toward the concept that Aβ plaque deposition boosts NFT propagation (albeit occurring along different trajectories) in ADNC, and some have suggested neuron-to-neuron, prion-like mechanisms of spread [34, 73, 79]. NFT pathology in PART does not progress beyond the MTL (with limited exceptions), and these cases lack significant neuritic plaques in the neocortex, despite their presence in early AD [14, 54, 119, 120, 185, 287]. The presence of neuritic plaques appears to be associated with an inflammatory milieu that accelerates tau spread throughout the neocortex [36, 37, 110].

Clinically, there is evidence that those who survive to very old ages with PART pathology maintain better cognition than those with ADNC, and subjects with “early AD” (those with low Braak stage and significant Aβ deposition) do not have the same pattern of selective deficits as PART subjects [281]. Moreover, there are significant genetic differences between PART and ADNC. ADNC cohorts are highly enriched for APOE ε4 alleles with only rare cases found to have APOE ε2, a pattern which is reversed in PART [1, 13, 16, 19, 54, 92, 99, 119, 120, 125, 167, 220, 230, 231, 261, 262, 266, 281, 304]. There is also a predilection for early involvement of the CA2 hippocampal subregion in some PART subjects, as well as more severe CA1 and EC involvement in others, which may suggest two separate pathologic groups, one which tends more towards ADNC-like pathology and one which tends toward PART-like pathology [101, 115, 231, 259, 280, 285].

A recent study by Del Tredici and colleagues [58] has called the early involvement of CA2 in PART into question, as the authors found that only a minority of the included 325 Aβ-negative subjects had p-tau pathology that was more severe in CA2 compared with CA1 and EC, suggesting that the majority of PART cases may simply be early or prodromal AD instead of a separate entity. It is notable, however that the average age of the cohort used in this study was only ~ 66 years old, and included 91 cases (28%) ≤ 60 years of age and only 49 cases (~ 15%) > 80 years of age, which is significantly below the average age of PART cohorts in the vast majority of studies reviewed here [9, 14, 18, 19, 54, 92, 119, 120, 125, 133, 147, 185, 220, 221, 230, 231, 261, 262, 281]. In addition, the majority of their cases (69.5%) were Braak stage I-II and 44% had at least one APOE ε4 allele, well above the average allele frequency for PART cohorts and more consistent with a cohort of early ADNC subjects. Based on the demographic, genetic, and histological characteristics of these subjects, the population studied was very likely enriched for early/prodromal AD. The majority of these may be cases that have not yet developed Aβ pathology but very likely would have if they had been sampled at older ages, a much different population from true PART cases as 80% of people in their 50 s and 60 s do not have Aβ plaques, but this number shrinks (and then plateaus) to around 20% of people over 80 [33], although Aβ may continue to develop at extreme ages in some individuals [224].

The existence of PART as a separate entity has a number of implications in the understanding of clinical and pathologic nature of AD, as well as the underlying biology of these pathologic processes. PART both supports the amyloid hypothesis generally and calls some aspects into question [53]. NFTs, which are indistinguishable between ADNC and PART [54, 243, 285], correlate with local synapse loss and neuronal death in the absence of Aβ [179, 283], indicating that neurofibrillary degeneration does not require the presence of Aβ to impair cellular function. On the other hand, the specific pattern of p-tau progression in the hippocampus appears to be largely dependent on the presence, amount, and distribution of Aβ [280]. Neurofibrillary degeneration in PART is also largely limited to the medial temporal lobe in the absence of Aβ and appears to require Aβ to progress substantially in the neocortex, even though neocortical seeding of p-tau species (beyond what is detectable on routine neuropathology evaluation) is clearly present early on in both ADNC and PART [36, 74, 280]. Conversely, CTE and 4R tauopathies readily form mature tau aggregates in the neocortex without the presence of Aβ, indicating that Aβ is not strictly necessary for this process to occur. PART also has implications for grading CTE in elderly individuals. In the presence of neuropathologically-confirmed pathognomonic CTE lesions (perivascular accumulation of p-tau in neurons at the depths of cortical sulci [21, 165]), aging-related and PART-like p-tau pathology, particularly in the CA2 subfield, may be used to grade CTE, despite the likelihood that these are distinct processes [104].

The tau prion hypothesis posits that misfolded tau can seed and propagate through the brain in a self-templating, trans-synaptic manner [46]. This framework predicts a stereotyped, progressive pattern of pathology, as is observed in AD and some frontotemporal lobar degenerations (FTLD-tau). However, in PART, tau pathology is largely confined to limbic regions with no consistent evidence of progressive neocortical spread, and the tempo and extent of spread are minimal, if present at all. In this context, amyloid might serve as an enabler: Aβ may facilitate tau spread by destabilizing networks, impairing clearance, or priming glia, and distinct microenvironmental factors, such as local glial or vascular factors, may shape p-tau’s propagation capacity. Since cryo-EM studies and biochemical profiling do not support the existence of a structurally distinct PART tau strain, this molecular similarity supports the idea that the disease divergence might not lie in the tau protein aggregate structure itself, but in the context, including age-related changes in immunity, synaptic function, glial response, and presence/absence of Aβ. For this reason, PART acts as a natural human model for p-tau and Aβ progression and provides a unique lens through which to study ADNC, including very early ADNC and the progression of p-tau to the neocortex, which appears to correspond to the most severe symptomatology.

Knowledge gaps and future directions

Although the term PART has been widely adopted, many critical gaps remain in our understanding of its biology, pathogenesis, clinical implications, and relationship to other neurodegenerative processes. Addressing these gaps will be essential to move beyond postmortem classification and toward a mechanistic and clinically actionable framework. Currently, there are no clinical diagnostic criteria for PART, making it challenging to estimate its prevalence, study its natural history, or stratify individuals in clinical trials. Developing such criteria will require longitudinal data, integration of biomarker findings, and further clinicopathologic correlation. Biomarker development for PART also remains in its early stages. PART is increasingly invoked to explain A-T + profiles, but no fluid or imaging markers can currently definitively diagnose PART, and development of tau PET ligands that are sensitive to low-grade, MTL-predominant pathology is needed. Highly sensitive liquid biomarker based approaches (e.g. mass spectrometry-based) may help distinguish PART from other tauopathies.

The most fundamental remaining question is why do some individuals with PART pathology display cognitive decline, while others with similar Braak stages remain cognitively normal? Possible contributing factors include cognitive reserve, individual resilience, co-existing pathologies, neuroinflammatory or glial responses, subtle differences in p-tau burden, subregional distribution, or cytoarchitectural changes (e.g. atrophy). Braak staging provides a coarse metric, but early-stage PART cases are difficult to classify reproducibly, particularly as p-tau burden is highly variable even within the same Braak stage [97]. Digital pathology, brain clearing, and high-resolution multiplexing may improve staging and reveal distinct PART subtypes. In addition, it is now clear that PART rarely occurs in isolation; concurrent TDP-43, α-synuclein, and vascular pathology are common and understanding how PART interacts with these pathologies is key to defining its independent clinical impact. PART also has implications for healthy brain aging; a better understanding of PART could yield insights into resilience and may also help identify protective factors that delay or prevent the transition from PART to symptomatic disease.

Finally, the cause of PART remains unknown; it may reflect a universal consequence of aging, selective neuronal vulnerability, environmental stressors, or other injury [155]. One intriguing hypothesis is that bio-mechanical stress may impinge the entorhinal cortex in aging and drive tauopathy [314]. The genetics of PART is also still unclear; unlike AD, we have a minimal understanding of the risk genes for PART, and whether certain p-tau haplotypes predispose to PART remains under investigation. More single-cell and spatial genetic, transcriptomic, and proteomic studies focused on tauopathy brains may help resolve these knowledge gaps.

Considerations for revisions to the diagnostic and staging criteria of PART

Given the wealth of recent research on PART since the original diagnostic criteria were first published, we highlight a number of issues, propose minor revisions, and provide guidance in applying the PART criteria (Table 2). Definite PART should be reserved for cases with a complete absence of Aβ pathology (CERAD NP score “absent” and Thal phase 0) and p-tau-positive neurofibrillary degeneration limited to the brainstem and medial temporal lobe structures. While PART demonstrates a predilection for presence of NFT pathology in the CA2 hippocampal subregion, this is not strictly required for the diagnosis and is a non-specific finding that can also be seen in AGD, CTE, and some cases of ADNC.

Although p-tau pathology in PART roughly correlates with Braak stage I-IV (B1-B2) [5, 25, 54, 178], we discourage the use of the term “Braak stage” in these definite PART cases. Braak stage does not generally correlate well with cognition in PART, and the distribution pattern of p-tau lesions throughout the medial temporal lobe may differ from that of ADNC, particularly early in the disease course (Figs. 2, 3) [27, 32, 280, 285]. Further autopsy and biomarker-based studies are needed to characterize the progression of PART pathology though the medial temporal lobe in the absence of Aβ, and additional clinical correlation will be necessary to determine if this progression (or other measures, such as hippocampal p-tau density or inferior temporal lobe NFTs) correlate with aging-related cognitive decline [3, 97]. We also discourage the inclusion of subjects who would qualify as “Braak NFT stage 0” in definite PART, as all individuals are Braak stage 0, Thal phase 0, and CERAD NP score “absent” at birth, in the complete absence of any tauopathy. The removal of Braak staging from the diagnostic criteria of PART leaves a gap in the ability to predict clinical outcomes from pure PART pathology. Staging of PART may be better accomplished with measures of the relative p-tau burden in the hippocampus proper and entorhinal cortex, as this may better explain differences in cognition [35, 97, 280].

It is also necessary to define the methods used in detecting pathology in PART and ADNC cases. Immunohistochemical stains are more sensitive than silver stains and Thioflavin S for identifying NFTs and plaques, and different immunohistochemical stains may identify different subsets of NFTs [176]. It is also preferred (but not required) that definite PART subjects be ≥ 80 years of age and lack APOE ε4 alleles and/or have one or more APOE ε2 alleles, as younger subjects with an APOE ε4 genotype may represent individuals with early or “tau first” ADNC pathology who may develop Aβ with more time [58]. Neuropathologists should therefore exercise caution in diagnosing definite PART in younger individuals, particularly those with APOE ε4.

We also suggest combining “possible PART” and low-level ADNC to “low ADNC/possible PART” to acknowledge that these diagnoses are uniformly overlapping (Fig. 1). We also suggest that the Aβ pathology in low ADNC/possible PART should be restricted to CERAD NP score “absent” (instead of “absent” or “sparse”, as in the original criteria) and Thal phase 1–2, given the number of studies that have demonstrated cognitive correlations with CERAD score but not Thal phase. Finally, it is unclear where cases with neocortical extension of p-tau in the complete absence of Aβ pathology should fall in this classification system; this appears to be an extremely rare occurrence [287], but may represent an early ADNC stage or the “tau-first” progression of ADNC, so this scenario may best fit with possible PART as well [4].

Development of additional criteria will guide both the diagnosis and staging of PART, as well as the framing of future research studies in relation to Alzheimer disease, and will likely require revisions to both PART and ADNC consensus criteria. The distinction between PART and ADNC has implications not only for neuropathological classification but also for biomarker development, clinical trial design, and patient care. Whether PART is a component of the ADNC spectrum has critical mechanistic considerations; if PART is part of the ADNC spectrum, then Aβ may act as a “trigger” or “amplifier” of tau pathology, allowing it to spread beyond the limbic system. In PART, tau remains localized, possibly due to absence of this permissive Aβ-rich environment. On the other hand, if PART is distinct, then it may have a separate initiating mechanism and its clinical trajectory, molecular drivers, and therapeutic targets may diverge significantly from AD.

The distinction between PART and AD also has important implications for clinical trials and biomarker frameworks. Many clinical trials now use AT(N) classification to stratify patients; however, A-T + individuals are often excluded, despite representing a sizable subset of the elderly. If PART represents a meaningful pathology with clinical consequences, excluding A-T + individuals may limit therapeutic discovery in age-related tauopathies. Conversely, treating PART as “benign” may lead to a missed opportunity to identify patients at risk for subtle cognitive decline. Ultimately, the relationship between PART and AD reflects a broader debate about neurodegenerative disease taxonomy: should we define diseases by their biomarkers, their clinical course, or their underlying mechanisms?

Conclusions

Ten years after its introduction, PART has emerged as a powerful conceptual and diagnostic framework to understand tau pathology in the aging brain. Its introduction clarified a once-ambiguous subset of neuropathological cases, provided language to describe A-T + biomarker profiles, and inspired a wave of research across neuropathology, clinical neuroscience, biomarker development, and molecular biology. Importantly, PART has served as more than a static definition; it has raised key questions about tau propagation, disease thresholds, co-pathology, and the boundaries of normal aging and has helped the field grapple with the complexity of the aging brain, where mixed pathologies are common and clear lines between disease and resilience are difficult to draw. Yet, critical challenges remain. We still lack a validated clinical criteria and staging systems, biomarkers that reliably identify PART in living individuals, a clear understanding of what causes PART, why it progresses in some and not others, and whether it is an inevitable feature of aging or a distinct disease process. As the field moves forward, PART offers a unique vantage point for studying tau biology, a bridge to understanding Alzheimer heterogeneity, and perhaps a model for resilience in the face of neurodegenerative change. Continued study of PART will not only deepen our understanding of tauopathies but may illuminate fundamental principles of aging and cognitive integrity and may provide an opportunity to better understand the mechanisms and underlying biological processes that may be targeted to help prevent or modulate neurodegeneration and cognitive dysfunction, and ultimately guide patients onto a more benign, “normal” aging trajectory.
