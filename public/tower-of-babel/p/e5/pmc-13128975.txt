Diagnostic Performance and Clinical Outcomes of Pertussis in Hospitalized Children in Relation to Available Vaccination Status: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Dominika Lachowicz, Barbara Jastrzębska, Ewa Bukowska, Magdalena Grzeszczuk, Małgorzata Pieścik-Lech, Waleria Hryniewicz, Edyta Podsiadły
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBordetella pertussis infections have resurged globally, posing notable public health challenges, particularly in pediatric populations. Poland remains the only EU country that continues to use the whole-cell pertussis vaccine (wP) in routine immunization. This study evaluated the vaccination status and diagnostic value of available testing modalities in relation to the clinical presentation and management of pertussis in pediatric patients.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of 269 children was conducted between January and December 2024. Patients with B. pertussis infection confirmed by real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR; PCR-Fluorescence Probing Kit, Sansure, China) and/or serological testing (NovaLisa B. pertussis ELISA, Gold Standard Diagnostic, Germany) were included. Clinical characteristics and vaccination histories of confirmed cases were obtained from electronic medical records and telephone interviews with parents.ResultsPertussis was laboratory-confirmed in 76 of the 269 tested children (28%). PCR accounted for the majority of laboratory-confirmed cases and remained positive in some children during the fifth and sixth week of coughing. The highest proportion of cases was observed in infants under 1 year of age (n = 25, 32.9%). In older age groups, the proportion of cases reached 17% in children aged 1–5 years, 19% in those aged 6–11 years, and 10% in children aged 12–15 years. Adolescents over 15 years of age accounted for only two cases (2.6%). Twelve patients met criteria for severe pertussis (PSS > 5), which occurred in both vaccinated and unvaccinated children. The interval between disease onset and the last vaccine dose ranged from 1 month to 12 years, and a trend toward more pronounced clinical manifestations with longer time since the last booster was observed. No differences in clinical symptoms were found between children vaccinated with wP and acellular (aP) vaccines.ConclusionsThe occurrence of cases several years after vaccination highlights the importance of continued surveillance to strengthen pertussis prevention strategies. Introduction

Pertussis, also known as whooping cough, is a highly contagious respiratory disease caused mainly by Bordetella pertussis. Despite widespread vaccination efforts, the disease remains a significant global health concern, particularly in pediatric populations. In recent years, the incidence of pertussis has increased both in Europe and worldwide [1–4].

In 2023–2024, several European countries reported record pertussis outbreaks, including Denmark, France, Spain, and the Czech Republic, with incidence rates exceeding 200–300 cases per 100,000 people [5–8]. Similar increases were also observed in China, and the USA, where the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reported a fivefold increase in 2024 compared to 2023 [9, 10]. The most affected groups varied by country with adolescents in Denmark and Czech Republic, and children aged 1–6 years in France [5–7].

According to national surveillance data, the epidemiology of pertussis in Poland has followed a pronounced cyclical pattern over the past decade, with major incidence peaks recorded in 2012 and 2016. A substantial decline occurred during the COVID19 pandemic years (2020–2021), likely reflecting a combination of reduced transmission, widespread nonpharmaceutical interventions, and limited access to diagnostic services. This downward trend continued through 2023. However, in 2024 Poland experienced a marked resurgence of pertussis, with 32,656 reported cases (incidence 86.94 per 100,000 population) compared with only 922 cases (2.45 per 100,000) in the preceding year. The 2024 figures represent the highest burden observed in recent years and highlight a rapid re-emergence of transmission following the relaxation of pandemic-related restrictions [11].

While adolescents and adults often experience mild or atypical symptoms, they can act as reservoirs of infection for infants, who are particularly vulnerable to severe disease. The severity of pertussis depends on age, vaccination status, and underlying health conditions. The most common complication across all age groups is secondary bacterial superinfection, with pneumonia being the leading cause of death in infants under 6 months of age. In unvaccinated infants, pertussis encephalopathy is more frequent and may lead to long-term consequences such as abnormal psychomotor development, epilepsy, and hearing loss [12, 13].

Pertussis is a notifiable disease requiring surveillance. Diagnosis relies on clinical, epidemiological, and laboratory criteria. According to the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing has the highest sensitivity (70–99%) within the first 3 weeks of symptoms but declines sharply afterward. Serology is more reliable in later stages [14].

Vaccination remains the cornerstone of pertussis prevention. In Poland, the diphtheria, tetanus, and whole-cell pertussis vaccine (DTwP) is administered at 2, 4, 6, and 16–18 months, followed by aP boosters doses at 6 and 14 years. Poland remains the only EU country that continues to use the wP vaccine for primary immunization and the first booster in infancy, without transitioning to aP formulations. Although parents may choose which vaccine their child receives, aP are not reimbursed, limiting their uptake. Nevertheless, some parents obtain aP vaccines through the private market, which may influence the distribution of vaccine types observed in clinical cohorts.

Pregnant women are advised to receive the Tdap vaccine between the 27th and 36th week of pregnancy, or after the 20th week in cases of preterm birth risk. In Poland, this vaccination has been funded through the national immunization program since October 2024. Placental antibody transfer provides newborns with temporary protection, particularly during the first 2 months of life, before vaccination begins [15, 16].

National surveillance data show that completion of the primary pertussis vaccination series in Poland remains consistently high (> 95%), with booster coverage in childhood generally > 90%, though uptake gradually decreases in older age groups. Lower values reported in the most recent cohorts mainly reflect delayed administration, not reduced acceptance. In school-age children (5–14 years), coverage after early childhood doses remains high: following the first childhood booster, national coverage typically stays in the approximate range of 90–95% at 7–11 years, while later school-age boosters show more variability and a modest dropoff, often settling in the high 80s to low 90s depending on cohort and region [17].

Despite high vaccination coverage in children, vaccination coverage among pregnant women remains low. In a recent regional study, only 23.4% of pregnant women reported receiving pertussis vaccination during pregnancy [18].

Understanding pertussis epidemiology and vaccine effectiveness is crucial for optimizing disease prevention and management strategies. This study aims to examine the clinical presentation, diagnosis, and management of B. pertussis infections in pediatric patients in relation to vaccination status.

Methods

Study Population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted between 1 January and 31 December 2024 at the Józef Polikarp Brudziński Public Pediatric Clinical Hospital, University Clinical Centre, Medical University of Warsaw. The hospital is the largest pediatric clinical center in central Poland, with 527 beds. The hospital treats patients from all over Poland who require multispecialty care and provides 24-h on-call services for general pediatric patients. In 2024, the hospital admitted a total of 96,218 patients and 147,364 inpatient-days recorded during the year. All pediatric patients aged 0–17 years old (inclusive) with B. pertussis infection confirmed by either PCR and/or serological testing were included. They were recruited into the study when hospitalized or when seen as outpatients but not admitted.

Laboratory Confirmation of B. pertussis

PCR Testing

Nasopharyngeal swab samples were collected from patients with clinical suspicion of pertussis. DNA of B. pertussis was detected using a real-time PCR-Fluorescence Probing Kit (Sansure, China), targeting the IS481 insertion sequence, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A cycle threshold (Ct) value ≤ 40 was considered positive, according to the manufacturer’s recommended and validated threshold for the routine clinical diagnostics. Negative and positive controls for a series of tests and internal control in each amplification run were included to ensure assay reliability.

Serological Testing

Serum PT-specific IgG (PT-IgG) and PT-specific IgA (PT-IgA) antibodies against B. pertussis were measured using the NovaLisa B. pertussis ELISA (Gold Standard Diagnostic, Germany) with pertussis toxin (PT) as the antigen. Tests were performed on a ThunderBolt analyzer, and results were reported quantitatively in IU/ml. Calibrators and internal controls supplied with the kit were used to verify assay performance and to ensure the validity of each test run.

Laboratory-Confirmed Case Adopted Definition for the Study

A case of pertussis was considered laboratory-confirmed if at least one of the following criteria occurred [19–21]:

Positive B. pertussis PCR test, regardless of serological results.

Positive B. pertussis PCR test, regardless of serological results.

PT-IgG ≥ 100 IU/ml when the last pertussis vaccine had been administered more than 1 year earlier. When reliable information on the timing of the last vaccine dose was not available, the child’s age, together with the parents’ report of immunization according to the national vaccination schedule, was used to establish that the last dose had been given more than 1 year prior.

PT-IgG ≥ 100 IU/ml when the last pertussis vaccine had been administered more than 1 year earlier. When reliable information on the timing of the last vaccine dose was not available, the child’s age, together with the parents’ report of immunization according to the national vaccination schedule, was used to establish that the last dose had been given more than 1 year prior.

Clinical Data Collection of Patients Diagnosed with Pertussis

Clinical features, vaccination status, imaging, and treatment were collected throughout the entire hospitalization from the electronic medical records of the Hospital Information System. We assessed the severity of pertussis through the pertussis severity score (PSS). This is calculated by summing scores (0–3) from clinical parameters (duration of hospitalization, level of hospitalization, hydration requirement, respiratory support, presence of complications), with a total score of 15 being the maximum, and a threshold of PSS > 5 used to classify cases as severe [22]. Information regarding the pertussis vaccination status (dates and type of vaccines) was available for a cohort of 61 patients via telephone interviews with their parents.

This study was performed in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Ethical approval was not required, as the study was based on a retrospective analysis of anonymized clinical data collected during standard care. No identifiable patient data are included, and informed consent for participation and publication was therefore not necessary.

Results

Laboratory Confirmation of Pertussis in the Study Population

During the study period, a total of 269 children suspected of B. pertussis infection were tested. Diagnostic methods were not applied uniformly across all patients. PCR served as the sole diagnostic approach in 164 cases, serology in 69, while both modalities were used in 36 children. Among the 269 children who underwent testing, 76 cases were laboratory-confirmed. PCR accounted for 47 confirmations (61.84%), serology for 26 (34.21%), while both diagnostic methods identified 3 cases (3.95%). The highest incidence of pertussis was observed in children under 1 year of age, accounting for 25 of the 76 confirmed cases (32.9%). In older age groups, the positivity rate was 17% among children aged 1–5 years, 19% in those aged 6–11 years, and 10% in children aged 12–15 years. Adolescents over 15 years of age constituted the smallest group, with 2 confirmed cases (2.6%).

Detailed individual laboratory and clinical data, including serology and PCR results, duration of cough prior to hospitalization, and vaccination status, are provided in Supplementary Material (Table S1).

Usefulness of the PCR Method Depending on the Time of Infection

Of all 50 PCR-confirmed pertussis cases, the majority (n = 37; 74%) were identified in children with a cough duration of ≤ 3 weeks, while seven cases (14%) were confirmed between weeks 4 and 6. These findings reflect the distribution of PCR-confirmed cases according to duration of cough within this cohort. In cases where serology results were equivocal, PCR allowed confirmation of pertussis in four out of five cases, supporting the complementary use of both methods.

Clinical Characteristics of the Study Population

Of the 76 children with laboratory-confirmed pertussis cases, 73 had been hospitalized, and 3 had been seen at the outpatient clinic. The predominant clinical manifestations among children with confirmed pertussis included paroxysmal cough with whooping in 98.5% of cases, post-tussive vomiting in 33%, and rhinorrhea in 33%. Vaccination and Clinical Course of Pertussis by Age Group

Among 61 hospitalized patients with known vaccination status, 41 were vaccinated with at least one dose of vaccine. The number of vaccinated children varied in different age groups; details are presented in Table 1. Eight patients had received the wP vaccine, the remaining 33 patients had received the aP vaccine.

The mean interval between the last pertussis vaccine dose and disease onset varied across age groups: 1 month in infants under 12 months of age, 10 months in children aged 1–5 years, 5.3 years in those aged 6–11 years, 12 years in adolescents aged 12–15 years, and 3 years in patients older than 15 years.

All 12 severe cases (PSS > 5) occurred in children younger than 12 years, with four patients in each age group (< 12 months, 1–5 years, and 6–11 years). Severe disease was observed among both vaccinated and unvaccinated children.

Infants younger than 12 months constituted the largest subgroup of children with confirmed pertussis. Eight of them (32%) were vaccinated according to the recommended schedule, three (12%) were partially vaccinated, and 14 (56%) were completely unvaccinated. Among the 14 unvaccinated infants, 7 were too young to receive the first vaccine dose, 1 had vaccination temporarily postponed due to symptoms of an acute infection, and 6 were eligible for vaccination but had not been vaccinated due to parental decision. Within this age group, two children had received the wP vaccine, whereas nine (36%) had been immunized with the aP formulation. No differences in disease severity were observed between vaccinated and unvaccinated infants; however, one unvaccinated child required admission to the ICU. One infant (6 weeks old), who had not yet received pertussis vaccination, had a mother vaccinated during pregnancy and experienced a mild disease course without the need for oxygen therapy, intravenous fluids, or hospitalization due to apneas. Details of pertussis symptoms in each age group are presented in Table 1.

Overall, children who had received the wP vaccine more than 5 years earlier exhibited a more heterogeneous clinical presentation compared to those vaccinated within the previous 5 years. Their symptoms included dyspnea and the need for intravenous fluids and/or oxygen therapy, whereas the < 5-year group mainly experienced vomiting after cough and required IV fluids. However, the number of cases in each subgroup was limited.

In the aP ≤ 5-year subgroup, post-tussive vomiting occurred in 24% of patients (n = 6), and the need for oxygen therapy or intravenous fluids was observed in 8% (n = 2). Notably, the aP > 5-year subgroup demonstrated a higher proportion of patients with post-tussive vomiting (n = 3; 37.5%) and intravenous fluid requirement (n = 2; 25%). These findings may indicate a potential trend toward more pronounced clinical manifestations with increasing time since the last pertussis vaccine dose.

Discussion

Although routine vaccination has significantly reduced pertussis incidence in young children, infections remain most frequently reported in adolescents and infants [2, 23]. Among our hospitalized cohort, infants accounted for 25/76 (32.8%) and children older than 12 years of age 13/76 (17.1%) of confirmed infections. These distributions reflect the severe nature of the disease in infants and the role of adolescents as asymptomatic transmitters. Symptoms in vaccinated children are often nonspecific, potentially delaying diagnosis and treatment [24]. Most hospitalized children under 5 years had household or closed contact with symptomatic older siblings or parents, or unvaccinated children who had delayed booster doses. This highlights the role of adolescents and adults as reservoirs of infection.

Among adolescents aged 12–15 years with pertussis, most children were either unvaccinated or had delayed vaccinations, with a median time since the last dose of 12 years. These findings emphasize the need for improved vaccination strategies and also booster coverage in older age groups to reduce pertussis transmission to vulnerable infants.

In Poland, there is an 8-year gap in the vaccination schedule between the booster doses given at 5 and 13 years of age. This is particularly important because studies indicate that aP vaccines provide shorter-lasting immunity compared to whole-cell wP vaccines—approximately 5 years versus 10 years [25]. In our cohort of 6–11-year-olds, the median time since last dose was 5.3 years, and most received the aP vaccine. Among children with severe pertussis in this age group, children were predominantly vaccinated properly and with aP vaccines, supporting concerns about immunity waning. The data also suggest that the time since the last dose may influence the clinical course of pertussis, with some indication of more severe symptoms among patients vaccinated more than 5 years prior.

The occurrence of full-blown pertussis in children vaccinated with the wP vaccine less than 5 years earlier may suggest possible antigenic divergence between circulating strains and those included in the vaccine. Recent studies have shown that circulating B. pertussis strains increasingly differ from vaccine strains, particularly due to mutations in genes encoding pertactin Prn and PT promoter ptxP. Pertactin-deficient strains, which may evade immune responses induced by aP vaccines, are now widespread in many countries. This antigenic divergence may contribute to reduced vaccine effectiveness and ongoing pertussis circulation. These findings underscore the need for vaccine updates and ongoing pathogen surveillance [26–29]. However, as a result of the small sample size and lack of genotyping data, this hypothesis remains speculative and requires further investigation.

Studies by Paradowska-Stankiewicz et al. demonstrated that post-vaccination immunity significantly wanes after 8 years; however, our findings suggest that this period may now be shorter, approximately 5 years [26]. This aligns with data reported by Schwartz et al., indicating that vaccine effectiveness drops below 40% after 5 years [27]. Locht emphasizes the need for new vaccines that not only prevent disease but also block infection and transmission. One promising candidate is the live attenuated BPZE1 vaccine, which is designed to mimic natural infection without causing illness [28].

Current ECDC and World Health Organization (WHO) guidance indicates that PCR sensitivity is highest during the first 2 to 3 weeks of symptom onset and recommends PCR testing in young children. In our cohort, PCR-confirmed cases were also identified in weeks 5 and 6 of cough, consistent with previous reports by Palmer et al. and Stone et al. These observations suggest that molecular testing may still detect cases beyond the early phase of illness [2, 7, 30, 31].

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this study is the comprehensive clinical and laboratory evaluation of a relatively large pediatric cohort with confirmed pertussis. However, the study is limited by its retrospective design and restriction to hospitalized patients, which may overrepresent severe cases and limit generalizability to the community-based population. Another limitation is that we did not analyze the population with negative PCR with regard to vaccination status, clinical symptoms, and specific anti-PT antibody levels.

Conclusions

The occurrence of cases several years after vaccination highlights the importance of continued surveillance to strengthen pertussis prevention strategies. Pertussis cases occurring within 5 years of wP vaccination warrant further population-based and molecular studies to evaluate potential factors such as waning immunity or antigenic changes in circulating B. pertussis strains.
