Excessive intraoperative hemorrhage during orthognathic surgery in a patient with neurofibromatosis type 1: a case report and literature review
Authors: Nobuyuki Sasahara, Kazuyuki Yusa, Mao Nishikawa, Satoshi Kasuya, Masaru Satomi, Shigeo Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by NF1 gene mutations, resulting in neurofibromin deficiency and vascular fragility. Although excessive hemorrhage during surgery has been reported in patients with NF1, such complications during orthognathic surgery are extremely rare.Case reportA 26-year-old woman with NF1 presented with skeletal Class II malocclusion. Preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed no neurofibromas along the airway or near the surgical field. Le Fort I osteotomy and bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomies were planned following orthodontic treatment. Considering the bleeding risk associated with NF1, 600 mL of autologous blood was collected preoperatively. During surgery, persistent diffuse microvascular bleeding occured without identifiable vessel injury. The total intraoperative estimated blood loss was 1,715 mL, approximately five times higher than the average value reported for combined Le Fort I osteotomy and bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomies. Hemostasis was achieved using oxidized cellulose compression. The patient received autologous blood transfusion only and recovered without allogeneic transfusion. Postoperative hemoglobin improved gradually with oral iron therapy, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 16 without further complications.ConclusionThis case highlights the potential for excessive hemorrhage during orthognathic surgery in a patient with NF1, even in the absence of apparent neurofibromas. Comprehensive preoperative vascular assessment and autologous blood preparation are essential. Surgeons should be prepared for prompt hemostatic management to ensure safe surgical outcomes in patients with NF1.Keywords: Autologous blood, Excessive hemorrhage, Neurofibromatosis type 1, Orthognathic surgery

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the NF1 gene, leading to a loss of neurofibromin, a negative regulator of rat sarcoma viral oncogene (RAS) activity [1]. Neurofibromin deficiency leads to a wide range of clinical manifestations, including the development of neurofibromas, which can occur not only on the skin but also on mucosal surfaces, including the nasal cavity and pharyngeal region [2, 3]. Blood vessels within neurofibromas are fragile owing to structural changes caused by the infiltration of tumor cells [1]. Few cases of hemorrhage from neurofibromas following trauma have been reported [4]; therefore, careful preoperative evaluation for the presence of neurofibromas near the surgical field is recommended. However, some patients with NF1 may exhibit vascular fragility even in areas without any apparent mass, potentially leading to hemorrhagic complications [5]. Here, we present the case of a patient with NF1 who experienced massive hemorrhage during orthognathic surgery despite the absence of identifiable neurofibromas in the surgical field on preoperative examination.

Case report

On June 15, 2022, a 26-year-old woman visited a local orthodontic clinic with a chief complaint of protruding maxillary anterior teeth and mandibular retraction. She was diagnosed with skeletal Class II malocclusion and was referred to our department for orthognathic surgery on August 15, 2022. The patient reported a history of NF1, and her mother and sister had also been diagnosed with NF1.

At the time of her first visit to our department, the patient had a normal build (height, 159 cm; weight, 49 kg; BMI, 19.3). Café-au-lait spots were observed on the neck, anterior chest, and forearms. No cutaneous neurofibromas were noted on the body surface. Extraoral facial photographs revealed a symmetrical frontal appearance and a convex profile with mild upper-lip protrusion and mandibular retrusion (Fig. 1). Intraoral examination revealed Angle Class II malocclusion with crowding of the maxillary anterior teeth. In addition, an anterior open bite was observed, with overbite and overjet of − 0.8 mm and 7.8 mm, respectively (Fig. 2).

Panoramic radiography revealed impaction of the bilateral maxillary and mandibular third molars. Cephalometric radiographs showed that both the maxilla and mandible were nearly symmetrical. The maxillary incisors were positioned anteriorly and the mandible posteriorly, consistent with skeletal mandibular retraction (Fig. 3).

Based on clinical and radiographic findings, a diagnosis of skeletal mandibular retrognathia was made. Preoperative orthodontic treatment was initiated after extraction of the bilateral maxillary first and mandibular second premolars and all third molars. All extractions were performed under local anesthesia, and no abnormal bleeding was observed during the procedure. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed preoperatively to assess for the presence of neurofibromas along the airway and near the surgical field, which could pose risks during the procedure. No neurofibromas were identified using either imaging modality. Results of the preoperative blood tests showed that the hemoglobin (Hb) level, platelet (Plt) count, prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) were within the respective normal ranges, with no evidence of anemia or bleeding tendency. Owing to the potential risk of bleeding associated with NF1, a total of 600 mL of autologous blood was collected on two separate occasions as part of preoperative preparation.

After preoperative orthodontic treatment, a gummy smile was noticeable in the maxillary anterior region. In addition, the patient was concerned about persistent upper lip protrusion after orthodontic alignment (Fig. 4). After consultation with the patient’s orthodontist, Le Fort I osteotomy (LF1) to move the maxilla 2 mm posteriorly and 2 mm superiorly and bilateral sagittal split ramus osteotomy (SSRO) to advance the mandible were planned. Orthognathic surgery was performed under general anesthesia on June 3, 2024. During the maxillary gingivobuccal incision and mucoperiosteal flap elevation, persistent microvascular bleeding was observed. An osteotomy line was created from the piriform aperture to the maxillary tuberosity along the subzygomatic ridge using an ultrasonic cutting instrument. The maxillary tuberosity, nasal septum, and lateral wall of the maxillary sinus were separated and down fractured using an osteotome. At this point, the operative time was 40 min, the estimated blood loss (EBL) was 420 mL, and no major vessel injury was observed. After removal of the bone fragments around the descending palatine artery, an intermediate splint was placed and intermaxillary fixation was performed using wire. Fixation was achieved with titanium miniplates, and the incision was sutured. At this point, 150 min had elapsed and EBL was 951 mL. Subsequently, bilateral SSRO was performed as per the standard procedure. Upon completion of mandibular splitting, the operative time was 220 min, and EBL was 1,361 mL. A final splint was placed, intermaxillary fixation was performed using wire, and titanium miniplates were used for stabilization. Closed suction drains were placed to ensure wound closure and hemostasis. The total operative time was 271 min, with a total EBL of 1,715 mL (Fig. 5). The Hb level decreased from 13.7 g/dL preoperatively to 7.3 g/dL intraoperatively. After transfusion of 600 mL of autologous blood, the postoperative Hb level improved to 10.1 g/dL.

The patient was admitted to the high care unit. Four hours later, the Hb level decreased to 8.7 g/dL, and by the following morning it was approximately 8.0 g/dL (Fig. 6A). Owing to the potential for irregular antibody formation due to allogeneic transfusion, red blood cell (RBC) transfusion was avoided in favor of oral iron therapy. On postoperative day 1, the Hb level further declined to 7.3 g/dL. However, because no signs of respiratory compromise were observed, the patient was transferred to the general ward. On the same day, the total drain output from the bilateral mandibular sites was 247 mL. By postoperative day 2, total drainage decreased to 22 mL, and the drains were removed. On postoperative day 5, the Hb level improved to 7.9 g/dL. On postoperative day 7, owing to the onset of menstruation, the Hb level again reduced to 7.3 g/dL; however, it improved to 8.4 g/dL on postoperative day 14 (Fig. 6B). Mild gait instability was noted until postoperative day 7, which resolved completely by day 12. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 16 after confirming stable occlusion, adequate oral intake, and improvement of anemia symptoms.

Discussion

The vasculature within neurofibromas in patients with NF1 is often fragile owing to infiltration by Schwann cells, which constitute the tumor, and a reduction in vascular smooth muscle [1]. Neurofibromas can occasionally develop within the airway, including the nasal cavity and pharynx [2, 3], causing difficulties with intubation during general anesthesia. Furthermore, the presence of neurofibromas not only within the airway but also near the surgical field may increase the risk of intractable bleeding due to tumor injury. In the report by Saito et al., a neurofibroma was detected posterior to the right mandibular ramus on preoperative contrast-enhanced CT in a patient with NF1 undergoing orthognathic surgery [6]. In that patient, intraoral vertical ramus osteotomy (IVRO) was performed to avoid damaging the neurofibroma. Therefore, when performing surgery on patients with NF1, preoperative assessment to detect neurofibromas along the intubation route and around the surgical field and collaboration with other medical specialists as needed are important. However, in some patients with NF1, vascular fragility and subsequent bleeding may occur even in areas where no apparent tumor mass is observed. In our patient, preoperative contrast-enhanced CT and MRI revealed no neurofibromas along the intubation pathway or near the surgical site.

Vascular fragility and vascular lesions such as aneurysms, stenoses, and arteriovenous malformations observed in patients with NF1 are collectively referred to as NF1 vasculopathy [5]. NF1 vasculopathy likely develops through two mechanisms [7]. The first involves a mutation in the NF1 gene, leading to loss of neurofibromin expression in the vascular endothelial and smooth muscle cells. This impairs the regulation of cell proliferation and repair, thereby resulting in structural abnormalities in the vessel wall. The second mechanism is analogous to the vascular changes observed in neurofibromas, where infiltration of neurofibroma cells into the vessels results in vascular fragility. When neurofibroma cells infiltrate arteries, they can cause fibromuscular dysplasia with neointimal hyperplasia and loss of smooth muscle cells in the tunica media. In contrast, infiltration into veins leads to significant loss of vascular smooth muscle and destruction of elastic fibers, which increase the risk of vascular rupture. These vascular changes associated with NF1 vasculopathy arise at the histological level of the vessel wall; therefore, vascular fragility may not be detected by imaging modalities in the absence of overt macroscopic lesions, such as aneurysms or tumors [7]. Consequently, routine contrast-enhanced CT, contrast-enhanced MRI, and even CT angiography may be inadequate for identifying subclinical NF1-related vasculopathy without apparent morphological abnormalities. Although CT angiography was considered, it was not expected to provide additional information beyond routine imaging in this context and was therefore not performed. Accordingly, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of preoperative imaging in detecting asymptomatic NF1-associated vascular lesions.

In our patient, preoperative testing revealed no abnormalities in the Plt count, PT, and APTT, and daily life assessments did not indicate a bleeding tendency. However, intraoperative blood loss was substantial, with a total EBL of 1,715 mL. Ichikawa et al. reported that the mean operative time and intraoperative blood loss for combined Le Fort I osteotomy and SSRO were 223.1 ± 42.1 min and 328.1 ± 252.6 mL, respectively [8]. Although the operative time in our case (271 min) was comparable to that reported previously, the intraoperative blood loss was approximately five times greater than the reported mean value. Furthermore, the operative time in this case did not deviate from the average operative time at our institution over the previous 3 years (255.1 ± 45.6 min). Therefore, prolonged operative time alone cannot explain the excessive hemorrhage observed in this patient. In addition, no obvious vascular bleeding was identified intraoperatively, and no vascular abnormalities were detected on preoperative contrast-enhanced CT or MRI. Taken together, although these findings suggest that the excessive hemorrhage was presumably associated with NF1-related microvascular fragility within the surgical field, a definitive causal relationship cannot be established.

Lin et al. reported three key strategies to minimize bleeding in the treatment of neurofibromas: (1) hypotensive anesthesia, (2) preliminary suturing around the lesion, and (3) ligation of a limited number of nutrient vessels associated with the vascular malformation of neurofibromas [9]. Choi et al. reported that vessels affected by NF1 vasculopathy had weak or absent contraction of the muscular layer, and that the appropriate method of hemostasis is physical vessel ligation or external compression [1]. However, in orthognathic surgery, the vessels in the surgical field that can be ligated are generally limited to arteries, such as the descending palatine artery, and venous structures like the pterygoid venous plexus are nearly impossible to ligate. Therefore, in cases of bleeding during orthognathic surgery in patients with NF1, compression using gauze or hemostatic agents should be considered the first-line approach, except when arterial ligation is possible. In our patient, persistent bleeding was managed by compression using an oxidized cellulose preparation. Although significant bleeding was observed, hemostasis was confirmed at the end of the surgery.

One response to blood loss is blood transfusion. However, allogeneic transfusions are associated with the risk of infection with agents such as hepatitis viruses and human immunodeficiency virus [10]. Moreover, RBC transfusions may induce alloimmunization, i.e., the production of irregular antibodies against antigens on the RBC surface. These antibodies can trigger immune reactions during transfusion and may make future allogeneic transfusions impossible. To avoid these risks, autologous blood transfusion is often used in orthognathic surgery [11]. Autologous transfusion is suitable for elective surgeries in patients with good general condition and predictable blood loss. In our patient, a total of 600 mL of autologous blood was collected preoperatively in two sessions. Despite reinfusion of the entire autologous blood volume, the estimated blood loss amounted to approximately 29% of the circulating blood volume, indicating substantial hemorrhage. Furthermore, the hemoglobin level decreased to 7.3 g/dL on the day after surgery. According to the Japanese guidelines, the decision to transfuse during the perioperative (postoperative) period is generally based on anemia defined as hemoglobin levels of 7–8 g/dL [12, 13]; therefore, this case met the threshold at which additional transfusion could be considered. However, the patient’s postoperative hemodynamics remained stable, with no clinical signs of hemorrhagic shock or ongoing bleeding. Based on the patient’s clinical stability, young age, and the potential risk of alloimmunization, allogeneic red blood cell transfusion was avoided. In the absence of institution-specific transfusion protocols, this decision was made in accordance with generally accepted transfusion thresholds and clinical judgment. Instead, fresh frozen plasma and oral iron supplement were administered, and the patient was discharged without RBC transfusion. Owing to the risk of bleeding, preoperative autologous blood preparation is essential in patients with NF1. However, if blood loss exceeds expectations, indications for RBC transfusion should be carefully evaluated, balancing the risks of alloimmunization and clinical findings.

SSRO, which is commonly selected for mandibular osteoplasty, offers several advantages, including a wide range of possible movement directions and distances, and postoperative stability owing to rigid fixation of the bone segments [14]. However, because a larger area of cancellous bone is exposed in SSRO, the intraoperative blood loss tends to be greater. In contrast, although IVRO is associated with a longer duration of postoperative intermaxillary fixation, it offers advantages such as shorter operative time compared to SSRO and reduced intraoperative bleeding [15]. Regarding mandibular osteoplasty for patients with NF1, successful outcomes have been reported using SSRO, including adaptations involving the mandibular body [16, 17]. Furthermore, favorable results have been reported with IVRO in patients with NF1 (Table 1) [6]. In the present case, the patient presented with maxillary protrusion and mandibular retrusion. Despite the known bleeding risk associated with NF1, surgical planning was based on the presence of a skeletal maxillary deformity and the patient’s strong esthetic demands. After careful discussion with the orthodontist, a combined Le Fort I osteotomy and SSRO was selected. Although direct comparison with previously reported cases is challenging, surgical manipulation of both the maxilla and mandible may have contributed to the increased intraoperative blood loss. Nevertheless, the combined procedure resulted in improvement of the skeletal discrepancies and achieved an esthetic outcome that was satisfactory to the patient, suggesting that the treatment plan was reasonable. On the other hand, prioritizing bleeding risk reduction—such as performing mandibular surgery alone at the expense of partial esthetic improvement—could also be considered an alternative approach. Therefore, in orthognathic surgery for patients with NF1, surgical procedures should be selected cautiously by comprehensively considering skeletal deformities, patient esthetic demands, and the potential risk of excessive bleeding.

Conclusion

We reported the case of a patient with NF1 who underwent orthognathic surgery for skeletal mandibular retraction, along with a review of the relevant literature regarding the risk of bleeding and perioperative management. The findings in this case suggest that massive hemorrhage can occur in patients with NF1 even in the absence of obvious vascular lesions; therefore, developing a surgical plan that considers the risk of bleeding is important.
