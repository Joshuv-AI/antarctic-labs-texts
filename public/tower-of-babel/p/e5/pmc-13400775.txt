Data set of neurocognitive markers for short-term risk for suicidal behavior in high-risk Veterans
Authors: Jake T. Smolinsky, Neha Gardilla, Alejandro Interian, Vibha Reddy, Kailyn Rodriguez, Catherine E. Myers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Data were collected prospectively from 60 Veterans at risk for suicide at multiple time points over a one year period to identify predictors of suicidal events in the next 90 days. This outcome included suicide attempts, interrupted/aborted attempts, preparatory behaviors, or suicide-related hospitalizations. Entry criteria included prior year suicidal behavior or prior week severe suicidal ideation. At each time point, participants completed neurocognitive testing and self-report measures of suicidality, and reported suicide-related events since the previous time point. Available data include suicide-related outcomes, question-level data for clinical interviews and self-report measures (including assessments of suicidal thoughts and behaviors, suicidal ideation severity, as well as the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS), Holmes-Rahe Stress Inventory, Automatic Thoughts Questionnaire (ATQ-30), and Acquired Capacity for Suicide Scale (ACSS)), and trial-level data for neurocognitive testing (including go-no/go task, a reward- and punishment-based learning task, death/suicide version of the Implicit Association Task, a memory recognition test), as well as latent cognitive variables extracted from computational modeling of the neurocognitive tests. This data may be used in secondary analyses for identification of predictors of multiple suicide-related outcomes over different time frames.

Background

This dataset enhances its associated research article [2], which focuses on negative automatic thoughts and acquired capability for suicide as predictors of upcoming suicide attempt, with additional interview, self-report, and neurocognitive measures. Some portions of the neurocognitive data were previously reported [3,4,5].

There is need for development of tools to more accurately and reliably identify those at risk for suicidal behavior. Among high risk individuals, an emerging priority is to identify short-term risk. Since long-term intensive prevention efforts are costly and burdensome, identifying short term risk can help prioritize deployment of resources. Existing tools largely rely on self-report which may be unreliable or inaccurate if individuals fear stigma or hospitalization, and may not capture risk factors not easily measured via self-report. Existing models for predicting suicidal behavior have good sensitivity but have poor specificity [6], and may result in inefficient allocation of resources [7].

This dataset was compiled to (1) identify neurocognitive domains predictive of suicidal behavior (SB) in high-risk Veterans; (2) use computational modeling to extract cognitive meta-variables associated with imminent SB; and (3) develop a statistical model from actuarial (e.g. demographic and clinical), neurocognitive, and modeling data to predict imminent SB.

Experimental Design, Materials, and Methods

Participants

Participants were n=60 Veterans receiving care at VA New Jersey Health Care System (VANJHCS), with a history of acute psychiatry admission due to suicide attempt (SA) or severe suicidal ideation (SI) during the prior year.

Recruitment

To identify potential participants the study staff conducted regular screening of VANJHCS acute psychiatry admissions records for patients with admission related to suicidal ideation of behavior, supplemented by review of Veterans Health Administration (VHA) electronic medical records to identify Veterans with a prior year admission to an acute psychiatry ward or emergency department following either SA or severe SI, and who appeared to meet other eligibility criteria.

For potential participants identified during an in-patient stay on the VANJHCS acute psychiatric ward or residential stay at VANJHCS, a study clinician (Masters- or Doctoral-level study personnel with formal mental health training) approached the individual (in-person or by telephone) to briefly describe the study and discuss whether the individual might be willing to participate. If the patient agreed, screening was conducted and, if inclusion/exclusion criteria were satisfied, the participant was enrolled and baseline testing either occurred, or else was scheduled for a convenient time, usually the next day.

Potential participants not on the VANJHCS campus (e.g. post-discharge) were contacted via IRB-approved recruitment letter, which briefly described the study and provided information about how to opt-out of further contact; if opt-out was not triggered, study staff contacted potential participants by telephone at least one week later to explain the study and answer any questions. If the patient agreed, screening was conducted (typically by telephone) and, if inclusion/exclusion criteria were satisfied, the (in-person) baseline session was scheduled.

Screening

Potential participants were screened for inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria were Veteran over 18 years of age with at least one of the following: (a) actual, interrupted, or aborted suicide attempt during the prior year, as measured by the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS); or (b) one or more instances of preparatory behavior in the prior year, defined as defined as actions directed toward a suicide attempt that go beyond verbalizations or suicidal ideation, such as assembling a specific method (e.g., buying pills, purchasing a gun) or preparing for one’s death by suicide (e.g., giving things away, writing a suicide note); or (c) severe SI within the prior week, defined as a score of 4+ on the Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (SSI).

Exclusion criteria were severe hallucinations or delusions, disorganized or disruptive behavior, medical instability (e.g., acute drug withdrawal), or lack of cognitive capacity to complete cognitive tests and provide informed consent. Screening related to medical/clinical exclusions was based on the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI), a brief structured interview to assess diagnostic criteria for DSM-IV Axis 1 and ICD-10 psychiatric diagnoses. Note that current or past medical or psychiatric diagnoses would not automatically trigger exclusion. For patients screened in person, cognitive competency was assessed via the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA); for participants screened by telephone, the MoCA-Blind v. 8.1 was used, which eliminates visual elements. Following prior studies with Veterans [10], we used a cut-off score of 20+ on the 30-point MoCA (or 15+ on the 22-point MoCA-Blind); potential participants scoring below this level were considered to have cognitive impairment and were excluded from participation.

Participants were required to have English fluency sufficient to provide informed consent and complete tasks and questionnaires delivered in English, or if they had severe sensorimotor impairments (e.g. full blindness, severe upper body paralysis) rendering them unable to view the computer screen or press keyboard keys; participants were not excluded if they had corrective technology (e.g. glasses/contacts) allowing them to complete the tasks.

Baseline and follow-up testing

Participants meeting all criteria at screening were enrolled in the study. Information obtained during screening (C-SSRS, SSI, MINI, MoCA or MoCA-Blind, and medical record information) became part of the study data for that participant.

Baseline testing (session 1) included clinical interview, self-report questionnaires and computer-based neurocognitive testing (see below and ‘CSRD_Overall_Methods.docx’).

Following baseline testing (session 1), follow-up testing sessions were scheduled at approximately 3 month intervals over one year, i.e. approximately 3, 6, 9, and 12 month after baseline testing (sessions 2-5). Follow-up sessions included updated clinical interview, self-report questionnaires, and computer-based neurocognitive testing (see below and ‘CSRD_Overall_Methods.docx’).

Telephone check-ins were scheduled at the halfway point between testing sessions, to maintain contact and check on participant welfare, including facilitating referral to mental health services if appropriate.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, a year-long administrative hold on face-to-face testing (Spring 2020-Spring 2021) at VANJHCS interrupted planned follow-up of the first cohort of 30 enrolled participants. Where possible, partial follow-up sessions were conducted by telephone and/or mail during this period (clinical interview and self-report questionnaires). Additionally, participants who had granted permission for re-contact at the time of informed consent were re-contacted after Spring 2021 and asked if they would be willing to re-enroll for up to another year of follow-up. Five participants agreed, and subsequently completed one or more additional testing sessions; these data are discussed more fully under Results.

The baseline session lasted approximately 2 hours and subsequent testing sessions lasted about 1.5-2 hours. Where possible each session was completed in a single sitting, but on occasion the interview and cognitive testing took place on subsequent days. If a participant could not complete one or more follow-up sessions, due to administrative hold on face-to-face testing during the COVID pandemic (Spring 2020-Spring 2021) or due to inability of the participant to travel to the testing site (e.g. having moved out-of-state), attempts were made to complete the clinical interview and self-report packet via telephone, particularly the C-SSRS; in a few cases, self-report packets were sent and returned by mail.

Participants were reimbursed for participation at the rate of $50 for baseline testing (session 1) rising to $70-$100 for sessions 2-5. Participants (excluding in-patients and those in VANJHCS residential program) were also reimbursed for travel to/from the testing site at the rate of $0.415/mile (rounded up to nearest $5).

Procedures

Baseline testing (session 1) included a clinical interview, self-report questionnaire packet, and computer-based neurocognitive testing. These are summarized below; a complete list of tests administered and methods for the neurocognitive tasks appear in “CSRD_Overall_Methods.docx”.

Clinical interview

The clinical interview was administered by a study clinician and included updated C-SSRS and Beck SSI (covering the period since screening, if this was more than 1 day previously), as well as brief medical history, including the Brief Traumatic Brain Injury Screen (BTBIS) modified to include injuries sustained outside of as well as during deployment; Brown-Goodwin Lifetime History of Aggression (LHA) Scale; current (prior 30 days) alcohol, tobacco, and substance use; and the Combat Exposure Scale (CES) modified to include an item asking whether the participant ever served in a combat zone.

Self-report questionnaires

The self-report questionnaires included Beck Depression Inventory (BDI-II), a well-validated inventory that measures severity of current depression symptoms; the Beck Hopelessness Scale (BHS), which assesses hopelessness and negative attitudes about the future; the Acquired Capability for Suicide Scale (ACSS), which assesses constructs such as fearlessness about death and pain tolerance; the Holmes-Rahe Stress Inventory (HRSI), which queries for stressful events such as divorce, illness, and loss of employment in the past 3 months; and the Automatic Thoughts Questionnaire (ATQ-30), which assesses frequency of automatic thoughts reflecting negative self-concept. A subset of participants also completed the Suicide-Related Coping Scale (SRSC), which assesses the ability to cope with suicidal thoughts, urges, and crises through means such as contacting a health care professional, confiding in a friend or family member, and other methods.

Neurocognitive testing

A short battery of neurocognitive tests was administered to assess cognitive domains including impulse control and inhibition via Go/No-Go (GNG) task, implicit association of self with concepts of death and suicide via death/suicide version of Implicit Association Test (IAT), short-term memory via Recognition Memory Test (RMT) and learning from negatively- vs. positively-valenced feedback via Reward- and Punishment-Learning Task (RPLT). A subset of patients also completed the emotional Stroop task (EST) as a test of executive attention under emotional distraction. See “CSRD_Overall_Methods.docx” for additional details of the tasks.

Each task took approximately 5-10 minutes to complete, and was programmed using PsychoPy [8,9] and presented on a Dell laptop or similar computer. For each task, for each trial, the stimuli, participant response, reaction time, and any on-screen feedback were recorded.

The behavioral output (trial-by-trial responses and reaction times) were also used as input for computational modeling (drift diffusion model (DDM) [11], linear ballistic accumulator (LBA) [12,13], or reinforcement learning (RL) model [14]) to extract latent cognitive variables. See word document “CSRD_Overall_Methods.docx” within the repository for details of DDM for EST, IAT, and RMT; LBA for GNG-RG and GNG-XY; RL model for RPLT.

A subset of participants also completed the Monetary Choice Questionnaire (MCQ), which queries preference for small immediate rewards vs. willingness-to-wait for larger delayed rewards. Participant responses can be used to infer a k-value describing the slope of that individual’s delay discounting function. See “CSRD_Overall_Methods.docx” for details of scoring MCQ.

Clinical outcomes

Primary outcome was presence of 1+ instance of suicidal behavior (SB) during the 90-day period following a testing sessions (sessions 1-4 only), with SB operationalized as (1) actual suicide attempt (SA), defined as deliberate self-harm with at least some intent of ending one’s life; (2) aborted, or interrupted suicide attempt, defined as initiating action to end one’s life that is stopped before self-harm occurs; or (3) preparatory behavior, defined as actions directed toward a suicide attempt that go beyond verbalizations or suicidal ideation, such as assembling a specific method (e.g., buying pills, purchasing a gun) or preparing for one’s death by suicide (e.g., giving things away, writing a suicide note).

Secondary outcomes assessed within the 90-day follow-up window after each testing session included both a narrower outcome definition of actual SA (excluding interrupted/aborted attempts), and broader outcome definition of suicidal event (SE) which includes either SB or severe suicidal ideation resulting in in-patient emergency room or mental health admission.

Participants were censored if they died or withdrew from the study during the 1-year follow-up period; data collected and outcomes occurring prior to censoring remained part of the study record. Participants who were lost-to-contact during the follow-up period were not considered censored as long as clinical outcomes could be tracked through VHA electronic medical records.

These clinical outcomes were determined based on information obtained during clinical interview (C-SSRS) at each follow-up session, supplemented by chart review for the one-year period following enrollment, to capture additional events as well as for information on patients who may have died or who were otherwise lost to follow-up. Specific sources of outcome events included: (1) C-SSRS at Screening and T1-T5, (2) medical chart review, and (3) intake notes (noted by experimenter during chart review for recruitment/screening).

Subject sample: screening and baseline

60 participants participated in baseline (session 1) testing. As expected from a Veteran sample, participants were disproportionately male (n=7 female, 11.7%), with mean age 45.3 years (SD 13.8; range 23-72 years). Queried for race and ethnicity, 36 self-identified as White, 15 as Black or African American, 1 as Asian, and 8 as Other/Mixed; 16 self-identified as Hispanic and 44 as non-Hispanic. Queried for highest educational attainment, 3 of 60 self-reported less than high school degree; 20 reported high school graduate or GED; 24 reported some college but no degree, and 13 reported 4-year college degree or higher. Queried for current employment status at baseline testing session, 11 self-reported working full time with an additional 3 reporting that they were full-time students and 1 reporting work in a sheltered workshop; 5 reported working part-time; the remaining 40 reported that they were unemployed (including 7 looking for work, 20 disabled, and 13 other/retired).

Based on the MINI, 27 (45.0%) met criteria for current major depressive disorder, 14 (23.3%) had bipolar disorder, and 34 (56.7%) had post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Twenty-seven participants (45%) self-reported history of combat exposure; overall, mean CES score was 5.7 (SD 7.0; range 0-26).

All participants reported use of at least one (prescription) psychoactive medication, usually antidepressant and/or antianxiety medication. However, due to extreme variability in medications reported, the fact that the same medication could be prescribed to different patients for different purposes (e.g. sleep aid vs. anti-anxiety), and the fact that blood/urine testing was not conducted to verify medication status at time of testing, further characterization was not attempted.

A majority of participants (n=39, 65%) received baseline testing while in-patient on the acute psychiatric ward following index suicide-related event (admission related to SA or severe SI); 8 were tested after discharge to a residential unit, and 13 were tested as outpatients after discharge to home.

Most participants (n=47, 78.3%) had at least one prior SA; mean number of lifetime SA was 1.7 (SD 1.6, range 0..7); mean SI severity assessed by Beck SSI at baseline was 12.7 (SD 9.8).

Study completion and censoring

Participants were enrolled over a four-year period from May 2019-June 2023, with recruitment/enrollment halted during a one-year administrative shutdown for face-to-face testing due to the COVID-19 pandemic (March 2020-April 2021).

Of the 60 participants who completed baseline testing, most completed at least one follow-up session (36 in session 2, 24 in session 3, 17 in session 4, 35 in session 5; average 2.4 sessions per participant). Note that, for 30 patients enrolled prior to March 2020, one or more in-person testing sessions were cancelled due to the COVID-19-related shutdown. During the COVID-19 shutdown, the study team attempted to at least contact each enrolled participant at scheduled follow-up times to administer C-SSRS to capture any SE-related events; SSI and other interviewer-administered and self-report measures were also captured by telephone where possible. Five of the 30 patients agreed to “re-enroll” after the lifting of the COVID-19 shutdown, and participated in one or more in-person sessions (including interviewer-administered and self-report measures, as well as neurocognitive testing).

Of the 60 participants, 35 of the 60 study participants were considered to have completed the study because they participated in session 5, either in-person (n=15) or as a partial session by telephone (n=20); this included the 5 individuals who “re-enrolled” after the COVID-19 pandemic allowing the study team to administer C-SSRS within the one-year follow-up period post original study enrollment.

Six individuals were censored, including two participants who died due to natural causes during the one-year follow-up post study enrollment; one additional participant was withdrawn by study staff due to the patient entering hospice care for terminal cancer. Three additional participants were withdrawn by the study team during the follow-up period due to criminal justice involvement; an additional participant “re-enrolled” after the COVID-19 pandemic, and so was not considered censored since >5 testing sessions had been completed before withdrawal. One participant completed session 1 and then when contacted for scheduling session 2 withdrew from the study, citing that they were in recovery and wished for no reminders of that period in their life. For these participants, study data obtained before death or withdrawal were retained, including any SE-related outcomes that occurred prior to censoring.

The remaining 19 participants who did not complete session 5 and could not be reached by telephone were classed as lost to contact, but medical chart review after the expiration of the one-year follow-up period was used to document any SE-related outcomes since last testing session.

Computational modeling

Raw neurocognitive data output from PsychoPy was prepared for computational modeling with Python scripts to strip extraneous data information (See repository folder /5_analysis_scripts/5a_python_strip_file_scripts/). Computational modelling was performed via R scripts. For the DDM and LBA models, the R-based Dynamic models of choice (DMC) software is used [15]. RL modelling was performed using custom code in R.

Limitations

Data collection was impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, including the interruption of in-person research testing. Most participants enrolled before the shut-down were able to provide follow-up outcome information, and data was removed for only a few participants due to withdrawal or death. A key limitation of this dataset is the low incidence of suicide attempt and related outcome events, even in this high-risk sample. The participant sample is relatively small and roughly mirrors the population of Veterans at large, consisting mostly of middle-aged or older, white males. As such, findings from this dataset may not generalize across age group, gender, or race.

Ethics Statement

Participants meeting all criteria at screening were enrolled in the study, and written informed consent was obtained. A copy of the consent form is available in the data repository. The study was approved and monitored by the VANJHCS Institutional Review Board and VA Central IRB Panel 3 (protocol #1577294), and conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and U.S. Federal policy for the protection of human subjects.
