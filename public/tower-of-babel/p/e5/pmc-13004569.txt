Fluoxetine inhibits severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 replication in vitro
Authors: Yethindra Vityala, Tugolbai Tagaev, Elmira Mainazarova, Ziiabidin Aidarov, Middela Karthik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
No drugs exist that effectively inhibit the cell entry or genomic replication of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARSCoV2). This study evaluated the effects of fluoxetine on SARSCoV2 replication in vitro. Cytotoxicity of both fluoxetine isomers, as well as their impact on the replication of a clinical SARSCoV2 isolate (strain IHUMI3), was assessed using the human nasal epithelial cells. The fluoxetine isomers exhibited similar activities, each significantly inhibiting SARSCoV2 at a concentration of 0.9 µg/mL. The halfmaximal effective concentration was 0.39 µg/mL for both isomers. Treatment with a concentration of 10 µM of either isomer resulted in a 63.9% reduction in the SARSCoV2 titer. Fluoxetine also decreased viral protein expression. The present study demonstrated that fluoxetine inhibited SARSCoV2 replication in vitro, thus providing initial evidence for its potential therapeutic use against SARSCoV2 infection.

Introduction

Currently available drugs are not effective against the cell entry or genomic replication of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). Until the newly authorized vaccines become more readily available, supportive care and nonspecific pharmacological interventions are the only strategies available to reduce COVID-19-associated morbidity and mortality. Drugs such as hydroxychloroquine[1] or lopinavir/ritonavir[2] have previously been considered promising but have unfortunately failed to exhibit significant clinical therapeutic efficacy or have demonstrated unfavorable adverse effect profiles. Therefore, identifying existing treatments or discovering novel pharmacological interventions with efficacy against SARS-CoV-2 is a priority.

Fluoxetine, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), is a well-known drug for which the patent has expired, making it easily accessible and affordable. Commercially available fluoxetine that is used in the clinic is a racemic mixture of the R(−)- and S(+)-enantiomers, but S-fluoxetine exhibits more potent SSRI activity.[3] Notably, fluoxetine decreases viral protein expression and inhibits the replication of human enteroviruses B and D via this mechanism.[4] This study evaluated the effect of fluoxetine on SARS-CoV-2 replication in vitro.

Materials and Methods

Human nasal epithelial cells (hNECs) are a known in vivo target of SARS-CoV-2.[5] hNECs were derived via in vitro differentiation of the human nasal epithelial stem/progenitor cells (hNESPCs) obtained from healthy donors. All donors provided written informed consent before tissue biopsy, and the confidentiality of collected data was maintained. This study was approved by the International Higher School of Medicine Ethics Committee (Protocol no. 4113).

Enriched, isolated hNESPCs were expanded in vitro and moved into 12-well plates with 0.4-μm Transwell inserts (Corning, NY, USA) for differentiation into hNECs, as previously described.[6] Derived hNECs (7.5 × 104 cells) were seeded into a 96-well conical-bottom plate (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in 100 μL of complete medium, which consisted of RPMI-1640 medium containing 4% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% glutamine. Cells were incubated at 37°C in the presence of 5% CO2 for 4 weeks, with the medium replaced every 48 h. A clinical SARS-CoV-2 isolate, strain IHUMI-3, was then inoculated into the wells at various multiplicities of infection (MOIs; 0.25, 0.5, and 0.1), and 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Ivesco, Iowa Falls, IA, USA) containing the R(−)- or S(+)-fluoxetine isomer (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was added to each well to produce a range of final fluoxetine concentrations of 0.01, 0.1, 1, and 10 μM. An equivalent DMSO concentration (100 μM) was added to the untreated cells as a vehicle control.

Incubation was continued for further 72 h, after which the cells were harvested into the 96-well conical-bottom plate (Sigma–Aldrich) via trituration. The cells were diluted 10-fold using complete medium and then pelleted by centrifugation (500× g, 10 min, 37°C). The supernatants (200 μL per condition) were removed and used to evaluate viral replication, whereas the cells were evaluated for viability using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Sigma-Aldrich).

To evaluate viral replication, RNA was purified using the MagNA Pure 24 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), followed by real-time quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction using the LightMix Modular SARS-CoV-2 RdRP gene kit (TIB MOLBIOL, Berlin, Germany) in conjunction with the RNA process control kit (Roche), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. To assess cell viability, 100 μL of MTT (0.5 mg/mL, diluted in RPMI-1640 medium without FBS) was added per well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Thereafter, the supernatants were replaced with 100 μL of DMSO (100 μM, diluted in RPMI-1640 medium without FBS) to dissolve the formazan crystals formed. The plates were centrifuged (500 × g, 10 min, 37°C), and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a Tecan Infinite F200 microplate reader (Tecan Group, Männedorf, Zurich, Switzerland), with DMSO used as a blank.

The mean half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) and 50% cytotoxic concentration (CC50) of fluoxetine were determined using an inhibitory sigmoid Emax model provided by ICEstimator v.1.2 website (http://www.antimalarial-icestimator.net).[7] All experiments were performed in sextuplicate. In addition, to elucidate the viral inhibitory mechanism of fluoxetine, patient-derived serum with or without fluoxetine exposure was subjected to immunofluorescence to visualize viral protein expression. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v9.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Both fluoxetine enantiomers demonstrated equivalent activity against SARS-CoV-2 in vitro, significantly inhibiting viral replication at a 0.9 μg/mL concentration [Figure 1a]. Furthermore, the mean EC50 value was 0.39 μg/mL for both fluoxetine enantiomers, which was significantly below the CC50 value of >30 μg/mL for both fluoxetine enantiomers. Fluoxetine-mediated inhibition of viral replication occurred concomitantly with a decrease in viral protein expression, as assessed using immunofluorescence. Finally, incubation with either fluoxetine enantiomer at a concentration of 10 μM showed a 63.9% reduction in the SARS-CoV-2 titer [Figure 1b].

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that both fluoxetine enantiomers exhibited similar activity against SARS-CoV-2 in vitro, suggesting an antiviral mechanism independent of their SSRI activity. As these results are similar to the reported effect of fluoxetine on enteroviruses,[8] direct inhibition of serotonin reuptake is not a reason for the inhibition of viral replication. Instead, fluoxetine may directly target the virus via an unknown mechanism, perhaps involving an interaction with a protein required for virion assembly.[8] The EC50 value of fluoxetine determined in the present study (0.39 μg/mL) is consistent with that estimated in a previous study, in which Vero E6 cells were cocultured with SARS-CoV-2 at an MOI of 0.5.[9] This study demonstrated that fluoxetine inhibited SARS-CoV-2 replication in vitro, thereby providing initial preclinical evidence to support the clinical potential of this well-known and accessible drug for the safe in treating patients with COVID-19. Importantly, the R(−)-enantiomer can be a better candidate than the S(+) for the treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Conclusions

In this study, both fluoxetine enantiomers demonstrated equivalent activity against SARS-CoV-2 in vitro, significantly inhibiting viral replication. Fluoxetine-mediated inhibition of viral replication occurred concomitantly with a decrease in viral protein expression. However, further in vivo studies are required to verify the effect of fluoxetine on SARS-CoV-2 replication before considering clinical trials. We suggest that early treatment with fluoxetine may be effective and should be assessed in patients with COVID-19 in the future.
