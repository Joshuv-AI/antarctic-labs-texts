Prognostic factors and treatment outcomes in female genital tract melanoma: a 10-year retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mengjie Mao, Chunyu Wang, Wenlong Yang, Zhuo Zuo, Bin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo investigate the clinical characteristics and prognostic factors influencing overall survival (OS) in patients with primary female genital tract malignant melanoma (PFGMM). Methods Clinical data of 60 patients with PFGMM treated at Sichuan Cancer Hospital between 2014 and 2024 were retrospectively analyzed. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Variables with a P value < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent prognostic factors. Results The 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates of patients with PFGMM were 73.3%, 53.3%, and 16.7%, respectively, with a median survival of 25 months. Univariate analysis indicated that menopausal status, surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and post-treatment recurrence were significantly associated with OS (all P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis identified age ≥ 55 years, presence of chronic comorbidities, tumor located in the lower or middle genital tract, menopausal status, and absence of chemotherapy or immunotherapy as independent adverse prognostic factors (all P < 0.05). Conclusion PFGMM is a rare and aggressive malignancy with poor long-term survival. Older age, menopausal status, tumor site, and lack of systemic therapy were independently associated with worse prognosis. Comprehensive treatment, including surgery combined with chemotherapy and immunotherapy, may improve patient outcomes.Keywords: Female genital tract melanoma, Vulva, Vagina, Cervix, Prognostic factors, Survival, Immunotherapy

Introductory

Malignant melanoma (MM) is a highly aggressive tumor arising from the malignant transformation of melanocytes [1]. Although melanoma most commonly occurs in the skin, it can also develop in mucosal sites, including the female genital tract, where it represents approximately 3% of all melanomas [2]. PFGMM includes vulvar, vaginal, cervical, uterine, and ovarian melanomas, with the vulva and vagina being the most frequent sites [3]. Due to its rarity and atypical clinical manifestations, the diagnosis of PFGMM is often delayed, and the prognosis remains poor. PFGMM is an extremely rare but aggressive malignancy. According to data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, mucosal melanomas account for a small fraction of all melanomas, with those arising in the female genital tract being particularly infrequent. Specifically, SEER database analyses indicate that the age-adjusted incidence rate for vulvar melanoma is approximately 0.1 to 0.15 per 100,000 women annually, while vaginal melanoma is even rarer, representing only about 0.02 to 0.03 per 100,000 women. Furthermore, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study highlights significant geographical and temporal variations in melanoma incidence, underscoring the necessity of regional retrospective studies like ours to complement these large-scale global registries [4–6]. Because of the low incidence, there are no established treatment guidelines. Surgery is generally recommended for early-stage disease, whereas patients with advanced or recurrent tumors usually receive radiotherapy or chemotherapy, though outcomes are unsatisfactory. Recently, the emergence of targeted therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors has offered new treatment opportunities for patients with mucosal melanoma [7]. A recent study demonstrated that Maprotiline prompts an antitumor effect by inhibiting PD-L1 expression in melanoma models, suggesting potential new avenues for immune checkpoint modulation in melanoma treatment [8]. For example, the SGSM2 study in uveal melanoma demonstrated that immune infiltration plays a significant role in survival, which supports our findings regarding the relevance of immune factors in PFGMM [9]. A global bibliometric analysis has demonstrated rapid growth in melanoma immunotherapy research [10], with increasing attention to immune-related complications, underscoring both the potential and challenges of these therapies in clinical practice. Despite growing interest in PFGMM, most available data are limited to single-center case reports or small retrospective studies, resulting in considerable heterogeneity and uncertainty regarding prognostic factors and optimal management. Complementarily, immune-related organ toxicities, such as acute kidney injury, have emerged as a significant clinical concern in cancer immunotherapy [11]. Reported five-year overall survival (OS) rates vary by site, ranging from 37–50% for vulvar melanoma, 13–32% for vaginal melanoma, and approximately 10% for cervical melanoma [5]. Given the rarity and clinical challenges of this malignancy, a better understanding of its clinicopathological features and prognostic determinants is urgently needed. Therefore, we retrospectively analyzed 60 cases of PFGMM treated at Sichuan Cancer Hospital between 2014 and 2024 to identify prognostic factors influencing survival and to provide evidence that may help guide individualized treatment strategies for this rare and aggressive tumor.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria:①diagnosed by preoperative biopsy pathology or postoperative pathology as female genital tract melanoma;②complete medical history data;③only one malignant tumor of PFGMM. Exclusion criteria:①Pathology of other melanoma such as skin;②having a second primary malignant tumor;③incomplete clinical data.④Patients with other serious medical comorbidities (e.g., organ transplantation, immune system diseases, etc.).

Statistical methods

SPSS 27.0 software was used for the statistical analysis of this study. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (range), and categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages (%). The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate survival rates and plot survival curves. The log-rank test was used to analyze factors associated with survival. The chi-square test was applied for univariate prognostic analysis, and the Cox proportional hazards model was used for multivariate prognostic analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Considering the small sample size, there may be a lack of statistical power for subgroup analyses. Future studies should incorporate post-hoc power analyses or larger sample sizes to further validate the observed findings and reduce the potential for type II errors.

Clinical data of 60 patients with primary female genital tract malignant melanoma were retrospectively analyzed. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was used to identify independent prognostic factors. Variables with a P value < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate Cox regression model to identify independent prognostic factors. In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, we included variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis. Moreover, clinically important variables (e.g.age and chronic comorbidity) were included even if they did not reach univariate significance, as guided by clinical relevance and prior evidence [12, 13]. Such an approach combining statistical criteria with clinical judgment for variable selection is recommended in the literature on clinical prediction modeling and regression strategies, where pre-specification of potential predictors based on subject matter knowledge is considered an essential step in building robust multivariable models. Additionally, variable selection strategies in clinical modeling emphasize that clinical importance should inform inclusion decisions to avoid omission of known confounders or prognostic factors [14].

Clinical data of 60 female patients with genital tract melanoma who were admitted to Sichuan Cancer Hospital between 01/01/2014 and 31/12/2024 were retrospectively analyzed. This study was approved by the Scientific Research Management Committee of Sichuan Cancer Hospital (Approval No. SCCSMC-01–2024-039). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Follow-up visits

Patient clinical data were collected from inpatient records, outpatient records, and telephone follow-ups, with follow-up survival beginning on the date of diagnosis and ending on December 31, 2024. The median follow-up time for the cohort was 24 months, with follow-up ranging from 12 to 36 months. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the point in time from diagnosis to death from any cause or final follow-up. During the follow-up period, 16.7% of patients were lost to follow-up, resulting in an attrition rate of 16.7%. Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 60 patients with primary female genital tract malignant melanoma (PFGMM) were included in this study, aged 22–78 years (mean, 55 years). Among them, 40 patients (66.7%) were postmenopausal and 20 (33.3%) were premenopausal. Nineteen patients (31.7%) had chronic comorbidities, including hypertension (n = 10), hepatitis (n = 6), diabetes mellitus (n = 5), hypothyroidism (n = 1), and cerebrovascular disease (n = 1). Seven patients (11.7%) reported a family history of malignancy. HPV testing was conducted in 23 patients, testing for several high-risk HPV types, including HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 59, and 68. However, due to the retrospective nature of this study, all patients were tested using a uniform testing approach, without performing independent analysis for individual HPV subtypes. A total of 6 patients (26.1%) were HPV-positive, but we were unable to analyze the association between specific high-risk HPV subtypes and PFGMM in detail.

The primary tumor sites were as follows: vagina (n = 35, 58.3%), vulva (n = 15, 25.0%), and cervix (n = 10, 16.7%). No cases of uterine or ovarian melanoma were identified. Among vaginal cases, 19 were located in the middle or lower third of the vagina, and 8 were located in the upper third. The most common presenting symptom was irregular vaginal bleeding or discharge (65.0%), followed by a vulvar mass or nodule (8.3%), vulvar hyperpigmentation or ulceration (11.7%), and pruritus or foul-smelling leukorrhea (3.3%).

Tumor size ranged from 0.5 to 7.0 cm (mean, 2.58 cm). Most tumors were unilateral (63.3%), and bilateral involvement was observed in 8 cases (13.3%). Lesions were predominantly black or dark brown (85%), with occasional violet or red pigmentation. Imaging prior to treatment indicated lymph node metastasis in 15 patients (25%), post-treatment nodal metastasis in 18 (30%), distant organ metastasis in 22 (36.7%), and disease recurrence in 19 (31.7%). Treatment

Most patients received multimodal therapy. Surgical resection was performed in 35 patients (58.3%), including 12 who underwent lymph node dissection. Postoperative adjuvant therapy was administered in 29 cases (48.3%). Chemotherapy was used in 46 patients (76.7%), immunotherapy in 23 (38.3%), targeted therapy in 18 (30.0%), radiotherapy in 17 (28.3%), and biotherapy in 5 (8.3%).Given the heterogeneity of treatment strategies in our cohort—including surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy—it is important to acknowledge that treatment selection may be influenced by disease stage, patient condition, and other clinical factors. These variables could lead to indication bias when interpreting survival differences between treatment groups. For example, patients with more advanced disease may have been more likely to receive chemotherapy or immunotherapy, while those with earlier-stage disease may have undergone surgical resection. As a result, the observed survival differences between treatment groups may be partially attributable to factors other than the treatment modality itself. Therefore, caution should be exercised when drawing conclusions regarding the efficacy of individual treatment strategies, and further prospective studies are needed to account for these potential biases.

For vaginal melanoma (n = 35), 18 patients underwent surgery (7 with lymph node dissection), 12 received adjuvant therapy only, and 3 received neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery.For vulvar melanoma (n = 15), 13 underwent surgery (3 with lymph node dissection), and 11 received adjuvant therapy.For cervical melanoma (n = 10), 4 patients underwent surgery (3 with lymph node dissection), and 6 received nonsurgical management.

Chemotherapy regimens primarily included dacarbazine + cisplatin, paclitaxel + carboplatin, and temozolomide-based combinations. Immunotherapy agents included sindilizumab, teraplizumab, and pembrolizumab, while targeted therapies such as bevacizumab and imatinib were also used.

Survival and prognostic analysis

All 60 patients were successfully followed up until December 31, 2024. The median overall survival (OS) was 25 months, and the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates were 73.3%, 53.3%, and 16.7%, respectively (Fig. 3).The median follow-up time was 24 months, with follow-up ranging from 12 to 36 months. During this period, 16.7% of patients were lost to follow-up, resulting in an attrition rate of 16.7%. These details provide context for the survival analyses presented in the study.

Univariate Cox analysis identified menopausal status, surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and post-treatment recurrence as significant prognostic factors for OS (all P < 0.05, Table 3).Multivariate analysis revealed that age ≥ 55 years (HR = 0.009, 95% CI: 0.000–0.205, P = 0.003), the hazard ratio (HR) for age ≥ 55 years is 0.009, which may appear counterintuitive at first glance, as older age is commonly associated with poorer prognosis. However, it is important to note that in Cox regression analysis, a **HR < 1** indicates a lower risk. Therefore, the HR of 0.009 for age ≥ 55 years suggests that patients in this group have a significantly lower risk of mortality compared to those aged < 55 years. This result may be influenced by confounding factors such as treatment modality, comorbidities, or disease stage, which could have affected the observed mortality risk for this group.

Discussion

Malignant melanoma (MM) is a highly aggressive tumor originating from melanocytes located in the basal layer of the epidermis or mucosa. While cutaneous melanoma is the most common type, primary female genital tract malignant melanoma (PFGMM) remains extremely rare,and their concealed anatomical locations and non-specific early symptoms, PFGMM are often diagnosed at advanced stages, showing high rates of local recurrence and distant metastasis.One limitation of this study is the wide confidence intervals observed for certain variables, such as menopausal status and tumor site. This may suggest model instability, likely due to the small sample size and potential collinearity between variables. These issues are expected in rare-disease cohorts, and caution should be taken when interpreting the effect sizes. Further validation with larger, multi-center studies is necessary to confirm the findings and reduce the potential bias introduced by small sample sizes. The small sample size (n = 60) in this study limits the power of subgroup analyses, which may affect the reliability of the prognostic factor associations. To strengthen the validity of these findings, future studies should consider conducting post-hoc power analyses or expanding the sample size. Moreover, as this is a retrospective, single-center study, the sample's heterogeneity and representativeness are limited. Therefore, further large-scale, multi-center prospective studies are needed to validate these results and minimize potential biases introduced by the small sample size. In this 10-year retrospective cohort, our data showed a mean age of 55 years and a predominance of vaginal cases (58.3%). This site distribution highlights an interesting contrast when compared to established Western cancer registries. Specifically, population-based studies from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database indicate that in Western populations, vulvar melanoma is typically the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 70–80% of female genital tract cases, while vaginal melanoma is significantly rarer [4, 5]. Our findings, showing a higher proportion of vaginal primaries, may reflect distinct ethnic or regional variations in tumor biology and referral patterns within the Chinese population.

Furthermore, while the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data reflects a general increasing trend in global melanoma incidence, the specific epidemiological trajectory of PFGMM remains less characterized due to its extreme rarity [6]. The age-adjusted incidence rate reported in SEER is approximately 0.12 per 100,000 women for vulvar melanoma and as low as 0.02 per 100,000 for vaginal melanoma. By comparing our single-center experience with these large-scale global registries, we underscore the clinical significance of regional datasets in supplementing global cancer statistics and identifying population-specific nuances in this aggressive malignancy.

Epidemiology and clinical features

Our cohort revealed a mean age of 55 years, with the majority of patients being postmenopausal—consistent with previous findings that PFGMM predominantly affects elderly women.This aligns with prior analyses showing postmenopausal predominance and late presentation in gynecologic melanomas [15]. The hazard ratio (HR) for age ≥ 55 years was 0.009, which is lower than expected, as older age is typically associated with a poorer prognosis in melanoma. However, this result suggests that older patients may have had access to more aggressive treatments or may have had a lower burden of comorbidities, which could have contributed to a more favorable survival outcome. These potential confounding factors should be carefully considered when interpreting these findings. Moreover, we recommend conducting additional analyses to account for these variables and refine the model in future studies, which will help improve our understanding of the complex relationship between age and melanoma prognosis.Vaginal melanoma was the most common subtype in our series (58.3%), followed by vulvar and cervical melanoma, which differs slightly from Western data where vulvar melanoma predominates.This site distribution differs from Western cohorts, where vulvar primaries are more common [16]. This discrepancy may reflect ethnic or regional differences in tumor biology and referral patterns. Tumour size ≥ 2.5 cm and amelanotic (or lightly pigmented) lesions were associated with worse survival in univariate analysis— echoing prior data indicating that amelanotic mucosal melanomas may harbour more aggressive biology and are prone to diagnostic delay.Similar findings were observed in recent European series [17]. More than 30% of patients also had chronic comorbidities, suggesting that host factors (such as immune senescence or metabolic dysfunction) may play a role in disease biology [18].

Pathogenesis and diagnostic considerations

Although UV exposure plays a major role in cutaneous melanoma, the etiology of mucosal melanoma remains unclear. Recent studies have implicated HPV infection, KIT mutations, and immune dysregulation as potential contributors.Whole-exome sequencing studies have identified recurrent KIT, SF3B1, and KRAS alterations in gynecologic mucosal melanomas [19]. Although our study found an HPV positivity rate of 26.1%, the lack of independent analysis for individual HPV subtypes limits our ability to definitively determine whether HPV plays a significant role in the occurrence and progression of PFGMM. The HPV types tested in our cohort included high-risk strains such as HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 59, and 68. However, due to the retrospective nature of this study, future research should consider conducting a more detailed analysis of specific high-risk HPV subtypes, particularly HPV 16 and 18, to further explore their potential pathogenic role in PFGMM. Such studies would provide deeper insights into the mechanisms through which HPV contributes to PFGMM and help inform more targeted, personalized treatment strategies. While our findings suggest that HPV may not be the primary factor in the development of PFGMM, the lack of independent subtype analysis means this conclusion is preliminary. Therefore, further studies focusing on the analysis of specific high-risk HPV subtypes are needed to better understand their pathogenic involvement in PFGMM and guide individualized therapeutic approaches.

The expanded molecular profiling in this study provides deeper insights into the genetic alterations associated with PFGMM. The presence of BRAF V600E, KIT, and NRAS mutations may influence the response to targeted therapies, while TP53 and CDKN2A mutations are linked to worse prognosis. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating genetic testing into clinical decision-making to optimize treatment strategies. Additionally, we plan to further investigate the correlation between these mutations and treatment outcomes, particularly in relation to immunotherapy. Given the potential predictive role of these mutations, future studies should aim to validate these findings in larger cohorts and explore the interactions between genotype and treatment response, especially for immunotherapies such as PD-1 inhibitors.

Our immunohistochemical findings are in broad agreement with literature: high positivity for S-100 (> 96%), HMB-45 (~ 91%) and Melan-A (~ 91%) in PFGMM, underscoring their diagnostic robustness in this rare setting. Recent molecular work in anorectal and gynecologic melanoma via whole-exome sequencing identified variants in KMT2D and TP53 among others, highlighting a distinct mutational landscape compared to cutaneous melanoma (low BRAF, higher TP53/KMT2D pathway involvement).These patterns further emphasize that PFGMM harbors a mutation spectrum distinct from cutaneous melanoma [20]. Additionally, emerging single-cell studies have begun to illuminate the tumor microenvironment (TME) in melanomas. For instance, pan-cancer single-cell analyses have revealed significant heterogeneity within cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) across tumor types, including skin tumors. While such granular TME characterization for PFGMM specifically is still lacking, these methodologies highlight the potential to dissect stromal-immune interactions that may influence tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic response in mucosal sites [21].

Treatment modalities

There is no established standard of care for PFGMM due to its rarity and heterogeneity. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for localized disease, as confirmed by our data showing longer OS in surgical patients (P < 0.05).However, surgical strategies continue to face significant limitations in aggressive or recurrent melanoma cases. Bibliometric analyses of recurrent solid tumors have emphasized the persistent challenges of surgical treatment, highlighting the need for novel therapeutic approaches [22]. The extent of surgery and the role of lymph node dissection are controversial. In our study, lymphadenectomy did not improve OS, supporting current recommendations that sentinel lymph node biopsy may be preferable to routine dissection.Comparable findings were noted in large registry and NCDB analyses [23, 24]. In fact, a recent study of gynecologic tract melanomas found that even in the contemporary era the 4-year melanoma-specific survival remained only ~ 49% and overall survival ~ 48%, with local and distant control rates still unsatisfactory as reported by recent population-based analyses [12]. As reported recently in a real-world gynecologic melanoma cohort [23].

Adjuvant and systemic therapies

In our study, most patients with PFGMM received multimodal therapy. Surgical resection was performed in 58.3% of cases, followed by chemotherapy with carboplatin, dacarbazine, and paclitaxel, in accordance with standard treatment protocols for mucosal melanoma. Our analysis confirmed that both chemotherapy and immunotherapy were independently associated with improved survival.Additionally, immunotherapy with pembrolizumab was administered in cases of advanced or recurrent disease. Our findings align with global treatment trends, where immune checkpoint inhibitors have been shown to improve survival in patients with mucosal melanomas, including vulvar and vaginal melanomas. We also observed a higher proportion of vaginal melanomas in our cohort, which differs from Western studies where vulvar melanoma is more common. This underscores the need for region-specific treatment strategies, considering the unique tumor biology and treatment patterns in different populations.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of PD-L1 expression as a biomarker for predicting the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly PD-1 inhibitors, in various cancer types, including melanoma. While our study included PD-1/PD-L1 testing in a subset of patients, the correlation between PD-L1 expression and treatment efficacy remains unexamined. Given the growing relevance of immune checkpoint inhibition in the treatment of PFGMM, further investigation into this association is critical. Future studies should aim to correlate PD-L1 expression with clinical outcomes to identify predictive biomarkers that could guide treatment decisions, optimizing the use of immunotherapy in this rare malignancy. Historically, dacarbazine-based regimens achieved limited efficacy (response rate < 20%). In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed the therapeutic landscape of melanoma.Multiple reviews and meta-analyses have documented the clinical benefit of PD-1 based regimens in mucosal melanoma [25, 26]. According to a multicenter analysis, PD-1/CTLA-4 combination therapy achieved an objective response rate of 37% in mucosal melanoma, and a 2024 Chinese multicenter study further reported that anti–PD-1 plus anti-angiogenic therapy improved median progression-free survival from 4.2 to 7.5 months. These advances are consistent with our findings that patients receiving immunotherapy exhibited significantly prolonged OS.This survival advantage is consistent with national database and cohort results [24, 27]. Nevertheless, immune-related adverse events and variable response rates highlight the need for predictive biomarkers to optimize patient selection.Systematic reviews underscore this variability, particularly in sinonasal and genital subtypes [28, 29].

Moreover recent cutting-edge studies add further nuance:A 2024 single-cell RNA-seq study of vulvar melanoma revealed cellular and immune-microenvironment heterogeneity, offering deeper insight into mechanisms of immune evasion and potential therapeutic targets (e.g., distinct T-cell subsets, myeloid populations).Recent single-cell and nomogram-based studies highlight immune and molecular heterogeneity within mucosal melanoma [30]. A phase 1/2 trial reported in 2025 (IBI363, a bispecific PD-1/IL-2Rα fusion protein) showed promising signals in acral and mucosal melanoma populations previously treated with immunotherapy—indicating that next-gen immunomodulation beyond PD-1 alone may benefit these resistant subtypes. An adjuvant therapy review emphasised that, even in the immunotherapy era, mucosal melanomas remain under-studied and require tailored strategies; baseline tumour thickness, anatomic subsite and immune-microenvironment metrics (e.g., TILs, PD-L1 expression) may modulate response. These insights underscore that PFGMM should no longer be treated as a mere extension of cutaneous melanoma—its distinct biology demands specialized consideration.

Recent molecular profiling studies have revealed that mucosal melanomas, including those of the female genital tract, exhibit distinct genetic landscapes compared with cutaneous melanomas, with higher frequencies of KIT, NRAS, and SF3B1 mutations but fewer BRAF alterations. Moreover, germline predisposition appears more frequent than previously thought in mucosal melanoma [31]. These molecular distinctions may explain the lower response rate to PD-1 inhibitors observed in mucosal subtypes. Furthermore, the tumor immune microenvironment of PFGMM tends to show reduced CD8 + T-cell infiltration and lower interferon-γsignaling, which may contribute to intrinsic resistance to immunotherapy. Recent immune-profiling studies demonstrate organ site-specific infiltration and IFN-y signature differences in mucosal melanoma [32]. Combination strategies that enhance T-cell recruitment—such as PD-1 blockade plus anti-angiogenic therapy or TIL (tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte) therapy—are currently under investigation and may represent promising future approaches.

Although PD-L1 testing was performed in only a small subset of patients in this study, observational data suggest that PD-L1-positive patients may derive greater benefit from immunotherapy, consistent with reports in cutaneous melanoma and other mucosal melanomas. However, due to the limited sample size and lack of standardized testing, no definitive conclusions can be drawn. Future studies should systematically evaluate PD-L1 expression (e.g., using combined positive score [CPS] or tumor proportion score [TPS]) in prospective cohorts, and incorporate tumor mutational burden, microsatellite instability, and tumor immune microenvironment features (e.g., CD8 + T-cell infiltration, interferon-γ signaling) to develop multidimensional predictive models. Additionally, exploring the dynamic changes in PD-L1 expression in relation to treatment response may provide more precise guidance for individualized immunotherapy strategies.

Prognostic factors

Multivariate analysis identified older age, menopausal status, presence of comorbidities, tumor site (middle/lower tract), and absence of systemic therapy as independent adverse prognostic factors.Comparable prognostic determinants have been confirmed in multi-institutional cohorts [33]. The poor prognosis associated with lower tract involvement may reflect deeper invasion, richer lymphatic drainage, and delayed symptom recognition.The fact that older/post-menopausal status emerged as a strong prognostic marker may reflect age-related immune decline and potential hormone-related tumour behaviour, echoing broader melanoma sex/age outcomes where female sex does not always predict improved survival in the setting of ICI therapy.This age-associated immune decline mirrors patterns in large melanoma immunotherapy datasets [34]. The 5-year OS rate in our cohort (16.7%) remains comparable to previously reported mucosal melanoma survival rates (10–20%), emphasizing the aggressive nature of this disease.

Notably, our cohort showed a predominance of vaginal melanoma, whereas Western studies report vulvar melanoma as the major subtype.This contrasts with Western series reporting a higher prevalence of vulvar and clitoral lesions [35]. This divergence may reflect ethnic and environmental differences or diagnostic bias. Several genomic studies have suggested that Asian mucosal melanoma tends to harbor more KIT and SF3B1 mutations, while Western populations show higher NRAS alterations. These genetic differences could influence therapeutic response and prognosis, highlighting the importance of population-specific treatment strategies and global collaborative databases for rare cancers.

Future perspectives

With the growing application of next-generation sequencing, molecularly driven therapy may become the cornerstone of PFGMM management.Emerging genomic analyses support such individualized approaches [36]. KIT inhibitors (imatinib, avapritinib) have demonstrated partial responses in KIT-mutant mucosal melanoma, while early-phase trials of NRAS-targeted MEK inhibitors and SF3B1 splicing modulators show encouraging signals. Additionally, integration of artificial intelligence–based radiomics and liquid biopsy–based minimal residual disease monitoring could improve early diagnosis and prognostication.Given the rarity of PFGMM, prospective multicenter studies are urgently needed to establish standardized diagnostic and therapeutic protocols. Incorporating genomic profiling and molecularly targeted agents (e.g., KIT or NRAS inhibitors) into treatment strategies may improve individualized management. Moreover, real-world data and registry-based studies will be invaluable for optimizing multimodal therapy combinations.Given the limited sample size in this study, it is essential that future research incorporates larger cohorts or multi-center designs to validate the findings. Expanding the sample size would improve the power of prognostic factor analyses and provide a more robust understanding of the associations between molecular markers and clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective single-center design, small sample size, and potential selection bias. Furthermore, missing data for certain clinicopathological variables (as indicated in Table 1) may introduce additional bias and limit the completeness of the analysis. Several variables, such as menopausal status and tumor site, demonstrated wide confidence intervals in the multivariate models, which may suggest potential instability in the model due to sample size limitations or collinearity. These issues are expected in rare-disease cohorts like PFGMM and may affect the robustness of the effect size estimates. Caution should be exercised when interpreting these results, and further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts is necessary to confirm the findings. Additionally, detailed molecular data were available for only one-third of the cohort, limiting the ability to correlate genetic variations with clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the 10-year follow-up and inclusion of multiple treatment modalities provide valuable real-world evidence. Future multicenter prospective studies with integrated genomic profiling are warranted to validate our findings.

In summary, PFGMM is an uncommon but highly aggressive malignancy with dismal long-term outcomes. Early detection, surgical management with clear margins, and integration of systemic therapies—particularly immunotherapy—represent the most promising approaches for improving survival. Our findings contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting multimodal, personalized management strategies for this rare disease.

Moving forward, several strategies merit emphasis:Prospective multicentre registries of PFGMM with harmonised data collection (clinical, pathological, molecular, treatment) to enable more robust subgroup analyses.Routine incorporation of advanced molecular profiling (e.g., WES, RNA-seq, immune-microenvironment mapping) to characterise PFGMM biology and identify predictive biomarkers for ICI/targeted therapy.Design of therapeutic trials specifically for mucosal/gynecologic melanoma rather than extrapolating from cutaneous melanoma datasets. For instance, next-generation immunomodulators (e.g., bi-specific antibodies, IL-2R modulators) or combinations of ICI + targeted/anti-angiogenic therapy (as seen in recent trials) may be critical.Integration of neoadjuvant strategies: early data suggest that neoadjuvant ICI may downstage disease and facilitate less morbid surgery in mucosal melanoma subtypes.Next-generation checkpoint and cytokine-fusion platforms are already in early clinical testing [37]. Furthermore, PD-L1 expression is too small a sample for meaningful statistical analysis and precludes reliable evaluation of its role as a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy. Future multicenter, prospective studies should incorporate standardized biomarker testing to clarify the predictive value of PD-L1 and other immune-related markers in PFGMM immunotherapy.

Beyond the specific genomic and immunotherapeutic strategies discussed herein, exploring novel pharmacological agents and their mechanisms of action represents another promising avenue for rare and aggressive cancers like PFGMM. The process of drug discovery and mechanistic validation—from natural compounds to synthetic molecules—often provides translatable insights across different cancer types. For instance, studies employing integrated pharmacological methodologies to deconvolute the mechanisms of plant-derived agents (e.g., p-hydroxylcinnamaldehyde in gastric cancer models) demonstrate a valuable framework for identifying antitumor effects, such as inducing apoptosis and inhibiting proliferation, through multi-omics and experimental validation. While the specific compound and cancer type differ, this pharmacological perspective and the approach of mechanistic exploration highlight a paradigm that could be applied to PFGMM [38]. Future research could benefit from screening bioactive compounds specifically for mucosal melanoma, utilizing similar integrative strategies to uncover new therapeutic targets and overcome the current limitations of systemic therapy.
