Recommended histopathologic features to evaluate on liver core biopsies for donor liver transplantation
Authors: Payam S Pahlavan
Published: 2025

Orthotopic liver transplantation is widely regarded as the treatment of choice for patients with end-stage liver disease. Routinely, following the confirmation of death in potential donors, the transplant surgeon submits a core liver biopsy to the pathologist as a frozen section sample for evaluation of the histopathologic criteria relevant to transplantation. This practice has become increasingly common as there is a continuous need to expand the donor pool, for example, by including older donors or those with viral hepatitis. The use of extended criteria is now generally accepted as a means to increase the availability of donor organs. Histopathological evaluation of a core liver biopsy prior to transplantation provides critical information regarding organ quality, suitability of the candidate, transplant outcomes, and survivability, as well as preventing unnecessary organ discard when the liver may actually be suitable for transplantation [1]. This approach can significantly benefit patients on the waiting list by increasing the number of available donor organs [2]. However, some centers do not routinely perform this evaluation due to various factors, including younger donor age (under 30 years), low body mass index (lean individuals), lack of risk factors for liver disease, absence of family consent, time constraints, or the unavailability of technicians and pathologists [3].

From a demographic perspective, there is no strict age limitation for the allograft [4]. The core biopsy should be submitted fresh and as soon as possible after sampling to the frozen section, in order to avoid drying artifacts (such as hole artifacts), which may negatively impact the assessment of steatosis. There is no universally accepted guideline for the adequacy of a core biopsy, but a commonly accepted standard is that the core should display at least six portal tracts. The initial step in evaluating the biopsy is to search for any potential malignant lesion that would render the specimen unsuitable for transplantation [5]. The next key parameter to evaluate is steatosis, which is subdivided into macrovesicular and microvesicular steatosis. Understanding the correct definitions and subclassifications of steatosis is essential. According to the Banff consensus recommendations for steatosis assessment [6,7], the following definitions are used: (1) large-droplet macrovesicular steatosis (LDMS) is defined as a single fat droplet distending the hepatocyte and displacing the nucleus to the cell periphery. (2) Small-droplet macrovesicular steatosis (SDMS) refers to fat droplets that do not meet the criteria for LDMS or microvesicular steatosis; these are typically small, variably sized droplets occupying the cytoplasm with the nucleus remaining centrally located. (3) Microvesicular steatosis consists of diffuse, small droplets within hepatocytes, usually less than 2 μm², producing a foamy appearance without nuclear displacement. In some centers, the term microvesicular steatosis is still used for what the Banff consensus now defines as SDMS, highlighting the need for gradual adoption of the new Banff terminology to ensure universal interpretation of steatosis assessments. According to the literature, the presence of SDMS is not a contraindication for transplantation [8]. SDMS is most likely an indicator of acute liver stress and has limited impact on long-term graft survival [6]. Pure microvesicular steatosis is rare and is typically encountered in conditions such as Reye’s syndrome, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, or alcoholic liver disease (ALD) [9]. Therefore, the focus should be on LDMS. LDMS content greater than 33% increases the risk of posttransplant complications and may decrease the suitability of the donor liver for transplantation, necessitating a case-by-case decision. The distribution pattern of macrovesicular steatosis can provide clues to underlying diseases; for example, in nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), steatosis typically begins in Zone 3 and may progress to panlobular distribution as the disease advances, whereas a nonzonal distribution may suggest regression of an underlying disease [9]. Hepatitis can be graded as none, mild, moderate, or severe based on the absence or presence of lymphocytes in some or all periportal areas, or extension to septa (Ishak grading).

Fibrosis should be staged as none, some portal fibrosis, all portal fibrosis with or without fibrous septa, portal fibrosis with occasional portal-to-portal bridging, portal fibrosis with marked portal-to-portal and portal-to-central bridging, occasional nodules (incomplete cirrhosis), and complete cirrhosis (Ishak staging). Studies indicate that an Ishak stage 2 or higher (all portal fibrosis with or without fibrous septa and above) may render a liver less suitable for transplantation, and such cases require individualized assessment [10].

Lobular inflammation is another important parameter to evaluate in a liver core biopsy, and its detection is best performed at higher magnifications (×200), focusing on foci of chronic inflammation rather than on neutrophils [11]. A predominance of neutrophils in the portal tracts or lobules may indicate ALD when considered in the appropriate clinical context [9]. Ballooned hepatocytes—enlarged cells with cytoplasm often containing Mallory-Denk bodies—are indicative of hepatocyte injury. These are typically observed in Zone 3 and are similar in size to large droplets of macrovesicular steatosis in adjacent hepatocytes [9].

Ischemic necrosis, particularly in livers from deceased donors (e.g., after cardiac or brain death), is another parameter that should be specifically addressed when encountered. The spectrum of ischemic injury may range from a few apoptotic bodies to more extensive, clear-cut necrosis. Although some studies suggest that diffuse necrosis of at least 10% renders a liver unsuitable for transplantation [12], there have been successful transplants with up to 50% necrosis, especially in livers from brain-dead donors [13,14].

Other findings in a core biopsy may include hemosiderosis, which can be difficult to appreciate on frozen section-prepared hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) slides. The search should begin in the periportal areas (Zone 1), with extension depending on the severity. Hemosiderosis usually appears as dark brown pigments. When it advances to involve Zone 3 (the central vein), it may indicate iron overload states, such as frequent blood transfusions or hemochromatosis, making the liver a less suitable candidate for transplantation. Lipofuscin, a granular brown pigment, is more commonly found in elderly donors. Unlike iron pigments, lipofuscin typically accumulates in Zone 3 hepatocytes. Bile pigments (indicative of cholestasis) present as green-to-golden brown granules, starting in Zone 3 (central vein and perivenules) and also seen within the canaliculi. Other potential findings in the core biopsy include ductular proliferation, sinusoidal dilatation, hepatocytes with glycogenated nuclei, granulomas, cysts, or bile duct hamartomas, none of which constitute a contraindication to transplantation. Fibrous obliteration of the central vein (phlebosclerosis) is another uncommon finding, often associated with microvesicular steatosis, particularly in patients with ALD [9]. Pitfalls in interpretation may include interobserver variability in steatosis estimation, misidentification of SDMS as microvesicular steatosis, as well as technical artifacts such as dryness, fragmentation, or tissue overlap during slide preparation. Some pitfalls can be avoided by standardizing steatosis assessment using the Banff consensus recommendations. Improving slide preparation quality can also be achieved by providing ongoing education and feedback to technicians. The use of telepathology in centers lacking on-site pathologists can be extremely helpful for delivering services to underserved locations.

In summary, generating a high-quality histopathology report from the biopsy can assist the transplant surgeon in making the final decision by integrating histopathologic findings with clinical information not always available to the pathologist, ultimately determining whether the donor liver is suitable for transplantation.
