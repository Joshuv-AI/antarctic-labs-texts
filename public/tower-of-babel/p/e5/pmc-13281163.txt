Effect of Intranasal Midazolam–Butorphanol Premedication on Sevoflurane Anaesthesia in Traumatised Buzzards (Buteo spp.)
Authors: Can Nacar, Hatice Ozlem Nisbet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundStress management is vital for safe avian anaesthesia. Intranasal (IN) midazolam–butorphanol may reduce handling stress and anaesthetic requirements in buzzards (Buteo spp.)ObjectivesTo evaluate the clinical efficacy and cardiopulmonary effects of IN midazolam–butorphanol premedication compared to mask induction with sevoflurane in buzzards.MethodsSixteen juvenile buzzards of undetermined sex were randomly allocated into two equal groups. The experimental group received IN midazolam (2 mg/kg) and butorphanol (1 mg/kg). The control group received sevoflurane mask induction without premedication. Heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (f R), ECG, SpO2, pe'co 2 , body temperature and sevoflurane vaporiser setting (SVS) were monitored at 0, 5, 15, 30 and 60 min. Aortic peak velocity (APV) was measured via echocardiography. Data (mean ± SD) were compared using Student's t‐test, and repeated‐measures ANOVA (p < 0.05).ResultsStatistical analysis revealed no significant differences between groups regarding peri‐anaesthetic, f R, body temperature, pe'co 2 and APV. However, SpO2 was significantly higher in the premedicated group at 15 min (p = 0.045). Notably, the premedicated group required significantly lower SVS at 0 and 5 min compared to the control group (p = 0.001). Repeated‐measures analysis indicated significant differences in SVS (p = 0.042) and HR (p = 0.033) with the control group showing higher SVS and lower HR; regarding ECG parameters, both QT (p = 0.001) and ST intervals (p = 0.011) were significantly prolonged in the control group. All physiological variables remained within acceptable limits, and all birds recovered uneventfully from anaesthesia.ConclusionsThe IN midazolam–butorphanol provided superior induction quality, significantly reduced inhalant anaesthetic requirements and maintained stable cardiopulmonary function. This protocol is a practical alternative for the anaesthetic management of traumatised buzzards.

Introduction

Birds have similar nociceptive pathways, neurotransmitter activity and stress responses to mammals, indicating that painful stimuli can provoke measurable physiological and behavioural changes. Consequently, the use of analgesic, sedative and anaesthetic agents during both minor and major procedures has become increasingly common in avian practice (Lierz and Korbel 2012).

The extensive taxonomic diversity and interspecies variation among birds necessitate a species‐specific approach to ensure safe anaesthesia and minimise complications. Differences in anatomical structures, cardiopulmonary physiology and metabolic rates mean that a protocol suitable for one species may not be appropriate for another. Sedation may therefore be required not only for routine procedures, such as physical examination, radiography, or sample collection, but also as premedication prior to more invasive surgical interventions (Schaffer et al. 2017; Doss et al. 2018).

It is well established that both domestic and wild bird species are highly sensitive to handling, manual restraint and sudden noises. Such stressors can significantly affect the sympathetic nervous system, altering vital parameters such as heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (f R) and body temperature (Siegel 1980; Greenacre and Lusby 2004; Paula et al. 2013; Bittencourt et al. 2015; Doss et al. 2018). The use of appropriate sedative or anaesthetic protocols alongside careful restraint is therefore strongly recommended to reduce stress‐related complications and improve procedural safety (Redig et al. 2014; Araghi et al. 2016; Doss et al. 2018).

Traditionally, intramuscular (IM) and intravenous (IV) routes have been the most commonly used methods for delivering sedatives and anaesthetics in birds (Sadegh 2013). The intranasal (IN) administration of sedatives and anaesthetic agents has gained popularity in veterinary and human medicine in recent years due to its minimally invasive nature, reliability, rapid absorption from highly vascularised nasal mucosa and reduced side effects compared with IV, IM or subcutaneous (SC) routes.

The present study aimed to evaluate the effects of minimally invasive IN midazolam–butorphanol premedication on vital parameters during sevoflurane anaesthesia in traumatised buzzards (Buteo spp.), with a particular focus on cardiopulmonary function. We hypothesised that IN premedication would provide more stable intraoperative cardiopulmonary parameters and a higher quality of anaesthetic induction compared to mask induction alone. This approach is expected to reduce stress, decrease anaesthetic requirements and facilitate safer surgical and rehabilitation procedures through more stable and predictable intraoperative vital parameters.

Materials and Methods

All procedures in this study were approved by the Ondokuz Mayıs University Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2022–30, Date: 23.06.2022), and the study was conducted with the permission of the Republic of Türkiye Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks (Ref. No. 21264211–288.04‐6121062, Date: 30.06.2022) in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for animal research. The study material consisted of sixteen traumatised buzzards presented to the Department of Wild Animal Diseases Clinic, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Ondokuz Mayıs University for clinical intervention. Following ethical guidelines, all birds received initial stabilisation and supportive care. Sixteen juvenile buzzards of undetermined sex, requiring anaesthesia for orthopaedic (e.g., fracture repairs) or soft tissue surgical procedures (e.g., wound management) following trauma, were enrolled in this prospective, randomised, controlled study. To minimise potential bias arising from clinical heterogeneity, birds were randomly assigned to experimental (n = 8) and control (n = 8) groups by drawing lots. Care was taken to ensure that surgical categories (orthopaedic vs. soft tissue) were distributed equally between the groups to balance the level of surgical invasiveness. To provide pre‐emptive analgesia, meloxicam 0.5 mg/kg PO q24h was administered to all subjects in both groups prior to surgical intervention. The selection of meloxicam was based on its common clinical application and reported safety profile in various raptor species (Heatley 2008). Birds were enrolled in the study only after becoming eligible for surgery. All surgical interventions were clinically necessary, and the duration of these procedures naturally met or exceeded the 60‐min study period. To further standardise the physiological assessments and exclude the cumulative effects of prolonged surgical manipulation, only the data recorded during the first 60 min of anaesthesia were included in the statistical analysis. A total of 21 buzzards were initially evaluated; however, five birds were excluded based on clinical assessment (dehydration > 9%–11% estimated by skin turgor and eye position, signs of systemic shock or the presence of open fractures with heavy contamination), leaving 16 birds for final enrolment. Exclusion was based on standard avian physical examination findings rather than invasive blood parameters due to the emergency nature of the cases. For sedation, midazolam and butorphanol tartrate were used. The required sample size was determined a priori using respiratory rate (f R) as the primary outcome variable, based on data from a previous study (Yaygingul 2020). A minimum of seven birds per group provided sufficient power (90%) to detect significant differences with an effect size of 2.0 (α = 0.05). While the study focused on a comprehensive cardiopulmonary profile, cardiovascular parameters and anaesthetic requirements were assessed as secondary outcomes. The study continued until this calculated minimum sample size was reached. The 16 traumatised birds were randomly allocated into two equal groups: experimental and control. Birds in the experimental group were premedicated intranasally with midazolam (2 mg/kg) and butorphanol tartrate (1 mg/kg) using a single syringe via a 22–24 G catheter with the guide needle removed, ensuring equal volumes administered to both nostrils (Figure 1). The catheter was inserted only 2–3 mm to avoid mucosal irritation. Administration was slow and controlled (30–40 s) to prevent aspiration and loss of medication due to sneezing or head movements, with the birds’ heads held stable. Birds in the control group were induced with 6% sevoflurane vaporiser setting (SVS) via appropriately sized face masks without any sedative premedication. This method was chosen to reflect the standard induction protocol for wild raptors in our clinic and to provide a baseline for comparison with the novel IN protocol.

Intranasal administration of the midazolam–butorphanol combination.

All birds were fasted 12 h prior to procedures. For circulatory support and maintenance of hydration, a 22–24 G catheter was placed in the medial metatarsal vein of each bird. Intraoperative fluid therapy was provided using a combination of Lactated Ringer's solution and 5% dextrose (administered at a constant rate of 10 mL/kg/h throughout the anaesthetic period via a volumetric infusion pump) to maintain glycaemic and hemodynamic stability. Baseline echocardiographic aortic peak velocity (APV) values were recorded before induction. After jaw tone relaxation, tracheal intubation was performed using a 2.0/3.5 mm Cole endotracheal tube in both groups, anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane in 100% oxygen at 2 L/min using a Jackson‐Rees non‐rebreathing circuit. The birds were primarily maintained under spontaneous ventilation; however, manual assisted ventilation was provided only when necessary to maintain respiratory rates and depth within acceptable clinical limits. The start of anaesthesia was recorded as 0 min.

During anaesthesia, HR, f R, oesophageal temperature, SVS (Vol%), SpO2 (%) and end‐tidal CO2 (pe'co 2 , mmHg) were monitored at 0, 5, 15, 30 and 60 min. Simultaneous HR and f R auscultation was performed using a modified oesophageal stethoscope, and cloacal temperature was measured with an electronic thermometer (Figure 2). Anaesthetic depth was assessed subjectively based on jaw tone and the presence and intensity of third eyelid, pupillary, palpebral, pedal and cloacal reflexes (see Appendix S1). Each bird was monitored continuously during the induction period to identify the exact moment of reflex loss. Time indices were recorded using a digital stopwatch with 1‐s precision, with the start time (T0) defined as the immediate completion of IN administration or face mask placement. The onset of sedation was marked by the first observation of third eyelid reflex sluggishness and signs of incoordination, characterised by ataxia or loss of balance. Subsequent time points were recorded based on the loss of specific motor functions and reflexes: sternal recumbency was noted when the bird was unable to maintain a standing posture, and the palpebral reflex was considered lost when the blink response to medial canthus stimulation was absent. The pedal and cloacal reflexes were deemed absent upon the lack of withdrawal response to a haemostat pinch and the lack of sphincter contraction to a thermometer probe, respectively. Finally, the time to loss of jaw tone was recorded when the beak could be freely opened by the clinician without resistance, permitting endotracheal intubation. Standardised aseptic procedures were completed within 15 min, and surgery commenced thereafter, with feathers retracted and remaining plumage protected with gauze. Hypochlorous acid was used for antisepsis.

Monitoring of some vital parameters during the anaesthetic period (A–C).

Electrocardiography

Initial electrocardiography (ECG) recordings were obtained at 0 min after intubation and commencement of sevoflurane maintenance, followed by recordings at 5, 15, 30, and 60 min. Alligator clip electrodes were blunted to reduce trauma, applied to depilated skin over the proximal cranial margin of each propatagium and both inguinal regions using ultrasound gel to improve conductivity. ECG signals were recorded using a VET ECG at 50 mm/s paper speed and 1 mV = 10 or 20 mm amplitude. Only lead II was recorded, and P wave, QRS complex, and T wave amplitudes and durations were measured according to standard ECG nomenclature.

Echocardiography

Birds were fasted for 12 h to minimise interference from regurgitation or gastric content. Echocardiography (ECHO) was performed using a doppler ultrasound device with a 7.5 MHz phased‐array probe (P8‐2s, bandwidth 2.3–8.0 MHz, 90° field of view) at 150 frames/s. Birds were placed in dorsal recumbency on a surgical drape at 45°, and acoustic gel was applied for ventromedian imaging. The probe was positioned caudal to the sternum to obtain a cardiac acoustic window. B‐mode horizontal four‐chamber images were obtained, followed by five‐chamber views with the aortic root clearly visible. Pulsed‐wave Doppler was applied with the cursor parallel to the interventricular septum along the aortic outflow tract, and maximum APV was measured (Figure 3). Measurements were repeated five times, and the mean values were calculated. Echocardiography and APV measurements were repeated at 60 min. Since all clinical and surgical tasks (including wound closure and bandaging) were completed by or after this final time point, the anaesthesia was discontinued immediately following the 60‐min data collection. The birds were then allowed to recover at room temperature.

Measurement of aortic peak velocity (APV) using pulsed‐wave Doppler echocardiography in the five‐chamber view.

Since the induction protocols (IN vs. mask) were visibly distinct, the anaesthetist could not be blinded to the study groups during the procedure. However, to minimise observer bias, all electrocardiographic and echocardiographic recordings were stored digitally and subsequently analysed by an independent specialist who was blinded to the group allocations.

Statistical Analysis

Mean, standard error, minimum and maximum values, and frequencies of ECG parameters and HR were calculated using SPSS (Ver. 21.0). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Student's t‐test was used to compare parameters between experimental and control groups at 0, 5, 15, 30, and 60 min. Repeated‐measures ANOVA was employed to assess changes over time within groups, with Bonferroni post hoc tests for pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

All buzzards recovered safely from anaesthesia without complications and there were no significant differences (p = 0.845) in body weight between experimental (0.81 ± 0.13) and control groups (0.83 ± 0.25). All birds underwent surgical interventions appropriate to their orthopaedic conditions, followed by comprehensive postoperative care and medical management. Of these, 13 buzzards regained adequate flight ability and were subsequently released back into the wild, while three individuals with permanent flight impairment were transferred to rehabilitation centres for long‐term care.

ECG evaluations were conducted on 14 buzzards, as recordings from one buzzard in the experimental group (case 5) and one buzzard in the control group (case 1) were excluded due to excessive artefacts (experimental n = 7; control n = 7).

In the experimental group, head shaking and sneezing reflexes were observed in all birds immediately after the completion of IN administration; however, due to the controlled slow administration technique, these occurred following adequate mucosal contact, and no clinical signs of drug expulsion were noted. In contrast, birds in the control group exhibited marked stress behaviours, such as struggling and wing flapping, starting immediately from the moment of face mask placement. Consequently, although the induction period was significantly prolonged (p = 0.001) in the experimental group (11.88 ± 2.42 min) compared to the control group (4.50 ± 0.53 min), the transition to sedation was notably smooth and calm, occurring without the defensive agitation observed during mask induction. The presence and intensity of the third eyelid, pupillary, palpebral, pedal and cloacal reflexes, as well as jaw tone, were assessed intraoperatively. The times required to achieve sedation and anaesthetic induction endpoints are summarised in Table 1. There was no significant difference between groups in the time to appearance of the third eyelid reflex (p = 0.642) or loss of palpebral reflex (p = 0.090). However, the control group achieved sternal recumbency significantly faster (2.63 ± 0.74 min) compared to the experimental group (5.38 ± 1.69 min) (p = 0.010). Similarly, the time to loss of pedal and cloacal reflexes was significantly shorter in the control group (p = 0.010). The time to loss of jaw tone, allowing for endotracheal intubation, was 4.50 ± 0.53 min in the control group, whereas it was significantly prolonged to 11.88 ± 2.42 min in the IN premedication group (p = 0.001). In the control group, 38% of birds exhibited “+1” palpebral, pedal, and cloacal reflexes and “+1” jaw tone at 0 and 5 min. In contrast, 88% of birds in the experimental group displayed all assessed reflexes at 0 and 5 min, with intensities ranging from “+1” to “+2”.

Discussion

The main outcomes, namely the reduction in sevoflurane requirements, the maintenance of stable intraoperative parameters, and the absence of clinically relevant cardiac or respiratory complications, were observed in the premedicated group, indicating that premedication conferred these advantages.

In the present study, induction times (defined as loss of jaw tone allowing intubation) were significantly longer in the IN premedication group (approx. 12 min) compared to the mask induction group (approx. 4.5 min). This finding is expected, as inhalant anaesthetics like sevoflurane have a rapid alveolar uptake, whereas IN drugs require transmucosal absorption and systemic distribution to exert their central effects (Ludders et al. 1989; Sadegh 2013). Although the induction was slower in the experimental group, the onset of initial sedation signs (incoordination and third eyelid prolapse) occurred within 1.5–2.5 min. This allowed for a smoother transition to general anaesthesia with reduced handling stress, which is clinically advantageous for wild raptors despite the longer total induction time. From a clinical perspective, the approximately 7.5‐min delay in reaching intubation conditions in the IN group is considered an acceptable trade‐off for the significantly improved quality of induction. In wild birds, a rapid induction—as observed in the control group—often necessitates more forceful physical restraint, which can trigger a massive catecholamine release and increase the risk of cardiac arrhythmias and capture myopathy (Edling 2006). The gradual onset of sedation in the IN group allowed for a ‘low‐stress’ transition to general anaesthesia. This clinical benefit is further supported by the more stable cardiovascular data recorded in the premedicated group. Therefore, prioritising stress management over procedural speed in wildlife anaesthesia practice significantly enhances patient safety.

Although IM injections are widely used in avian medicine, and IN administration may require a slightly slower application time, the IM route carries inherent risks, especially in wild birds. During IM administration, physical restraint combined with the nociceptive stimulus of the needle can lead to violent struggling, occasionally resulting in needle‐induced injuries (Hawkins et al. 2025). Furthermore, wild raptors brought to the clinic with trauma may often exhibit limited muscle mass or underlying coagulopathies. In such clinical scenarios, the IN route provides a significant advantage by minimising the adverse reactions associated with IM injections and preventing the risk of iatrogenic haemorrhage (Doss et al. 2018). Additionally, IN administration minimises the artificial alteration of biochemical variables commonly seen following IM tissue trauma (Doss et al. 2018). Recent comparative studies in raptors, such as Great horned owls (Bubo virginianus), have shown that IN midazolam provides an onset of sedation, muscle relaxation, and physiological stability similar to IM administration, without the procedural risks (Hawkins et al. 2025). Therefore, despite a potentially longer application time, the IN route was specifically chosen in the current study to minimise iatrogenic stress, avoid additional muscle trauma, and ensure safe premedication in traumatised Buteo spp.

Cardiovascular findings indicated a gradual decline in heart rate across both groups, which reached statistical significance only in controls. Heart rates were within the reference ranges (Gunkel and Lafortune 2005; Yaygingul 2020). This is most likely attributable to dose‐dependent depression from higher SVS, whereas premedication reduced inhalant demand and attenuated cardiovascular depression (Figure 4) (Çeçen et al. 2009). Our SVS data support this interpretation, showing significantly lower requirements in the premedicated group.

Comparison of sevoflurane vaporiser settings (SVS, Vol%) (A), and heart rate changes (B) during the anaesthetic period between the experimental and control groups.

APV remained unchanged, confirming the absence of adverse cardiac compromise, in line with previous echocardiographic evaluations in buzzards and other raptors (Straub et al. 2003; Beaufrère et al. 2012). Observed QRS complexes were negative and consistent with avian‐specific patterns (Espino et al. 2001). Electrocardiographic analysis similarly revealed no arrhythmias or conduction disturbances, and the observed QT prolongation in the control group was consistent with the negative relation between heart rate and QT interval (Jervell and Lange‐Nielsen 1956; Lecocq et al. 1989). These findings are consistent with previous reports in buzzards, parrots, pigeons, and geese, indicating that midazolam–butorphanol combinations provide effective sedation without adverse cardiac effects (Kubiak et al. 2016; Zaheer et al. 2020; Altundağ et al. 2021; Anjana et al. 2021).

Respiratory rate remained within expected physiological ranges, indicating that the IN protocol did not induce respiratory depression. These findings support earlier reports demonstrating the respiratory safety of IN midazolam or midazolam–butorphanol combinations in avian species (Ludders et al. 1989; Joyner et al. 2008; Altundağ et al. 2021). Oxygen saturation values were also preserved, with only a minor transient reduction at 15 min (p = 0.045), most likely related to technical variation in probe positioning rather than true hypoxaemia.

Body temperature decreased by approximately 3–4°C in both groups, consistent with previous studies reporting thermoregulatory impairment during avian anaesthesia. Despite the provision of supportive heating, moderate hypothermia still occurred, highlighting the importance of thermal support during prolonged procedures (Uzun et al. 2003; Chan et al. 2013; Yaygingul et al. 2023). In the present study, pe'co 2 values were consistently lower than typical avian reference ranges. While this finding was initially considered in relation to apparatus dead space, it is more likely attributable to the dilution and ‘washout’ effect inherent to the use of non‐rebreathing systems in small avian patients. Specifically, the high fresh gas flow rate (2 L/min) relative to the small tidal volume of the buzzards can cause exhaled CO2 to be diluted by the fresh gas jet before it reaches the sampling line (Edling 2006). This effect is further compounded by the high velocity of the fresh gas flow within the Jackson‐Rees circuit, which may displace the alveolar gas sample. Additionally, the use of uncuffed endotracheal tubes, which are standard in avian medicine to prevent tracheal mucosal damage, allows for potential peritubal gas leaks that further contribute to underestimated pe'co 2 readings. Therefore, pe'co 2 values in this study were interpreted primarily for trend monitoring rather than as absolute indicators of arterial CO2 tension.

Certain limitations of this study must also be considered. The number of birds was limited, reflecting the availability of clinical cases, and trauma heterogeneity may have influenced anaesthetic responses, although randomisation was employed to mitigate this bias. The control group received no premedication, which, while representing a standard field or clinical approach for rapid induction in some settings, does present ethical considerations regarding stress; however, this design was necessary to establish a clear baseline for the efficacy of the IN protocol. In addition, two electrocardiographic records were excluded due to artefacts, and the observation period was restricted to 60 min, which does not reflect longer surgical procedures.

Conclusion

The findings should be generalised with caution. This investigation was confined to buzzards, and applicability to other raptor species or to birds with differing physiological conditions remains uncertain. Its ease of administration, minimally invasive nature, and analgesic efficacy provide clear advantages over other routes. Optimal administration requires management of sneezing and head shaking to ensure mucosal absorption. In this study, similar reactions were observed immediately after IN administration of the midazolam–butorphanol combination, with birds’ heads carefully positioned and restrained to ensure adequate mucosal contact and drug absorption. Common adverse effects of IN administration include sneezing and head shaking, which can lead to partial expulsion of the drug and reduced efficacy (Mans 2014; Araghi et al. 2016; Altundağ et al. 2021).

The selection of both the chemical agent and the route of administration should consider species, health status, procedure type and duration, available clinical equipment, and clinician experience (İşler et al. 2018). Previous studies in buzzards, ostriches, zebra finches and various parrot species have shown that IN administration of benzodiazepines, alpha‐2 agonists or opioids, alone or in combination, can achieve effective sedation at appropriate doses (Vesal and Eskandari 2006; Schaffer et al. 2016; Bigham and Zamani Moghaddam 2013; Doss et al. 2018).

Further studies are required to refine dosage regimens, explore pharmacokinetics and establish interspecies protocols.

In conclusion, IN midazolam–butorphanol premedication appears to be a safe and practical option in the buzzards included in this study, providing reduced sevoflurane requirements while maintaining stable cardiopulmonary function. Its minimally invasive administration and the absence of adverse effects support clinical use, although validation in larger populations and other species is warranted.
