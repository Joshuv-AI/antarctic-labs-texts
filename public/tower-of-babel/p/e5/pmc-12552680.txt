Seismic stability analysis of cracked compound slopes considering soil heterogeneity
Authors: Lili Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a seismic stability assessment framework for heterogeneous compound slopes with cracks, for example, highway embankment slopes. Based on limit analysis method, a kinematical approach incorporating a discretization technique is adopted to account for both global and local instabilities of cracked compound slopes under seismic excitations. The pseudo-static approach is applied to describe seismic forces. The soil heterogeneity is considered as linear variation along the depth. An implicit formula for stability factor (or safety factor) is derived through the work rate balance equation. The upper-bound solutions are calculated by combining a bisectional method with an optimization algorithm. The results are thoroughly verified through comparison with existing studies and finite element analysis. The results show that slope geometry plays a dominant role in governing slope stability and failure behavior. In particular, increasing the upper slope inclination significantly reduces the slope stability and induces a transition from local to global failure, especially under a larger depth coefficient. The presence of cracks further weakens slope stability, with their depth and location highly sensitive to geometric configuration. Furthermore, seismic excitation deepens cracks and shifts them upslope, inducing a transition from local to global failure as horizontal acceleration coefficient increases. Soil heterogeneity influences failure mechanisms that stronger upper soil deepens cracks via shear transition and shifts failure surfaces forward, exhibiting a “seesaw effect”. The findings provide the guidance for the design of stable and crack-resistance slope engineering, particularly in topographically complex or geohazard-prone regions.Keywords: Seismic slope stability, Limit analysis, Cracks, Soil heterogeneity, Failure mechanismSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

The accuracy assessment to slope stability under complex conditions is essential to achieving resilient and environmentally responsible land development1–3. In slope stability analysis, not only the properties of the soil but also the geometric characteristics of the slope plays a significant role in determining slope stability4–6. In highway construction, excavation and embankment operations, or in the stabilization unstable slopes, it is often necessary to cut or flatten slopes, resulting in stepped or polygonal slopes with varying slope inclinations, for example, highway embankment slopes7, which is defined as compound slopes. Hence, it is of great necessity to conduct stability analysis of such compound slopes. Different from the simple slopes, not only global instability but also local instability could occur for compound slopes. Accurate identification of potential failure patterns is key to the stability assessment of compound slopes. For example, Zeng et al.8 proposed a finite element method using the deformation energy principle, which enables stability analysis of compound slopes without the need to predefine a sliding surface. They found that the failure pattern of compound slopes changes from global failure to local failure when the dimensionless depth coefficient increases. Meanwhile, local failure is more prone to occur if the difference between upper and lower slope inclinations becomes large. Similarly, Li et al.9 derived analytical expression for safety factor associated with both global and local failure mechanisms through the limit equilibrium method. They calculated the safety factor of a three-stage slope with different failure patterns. Results revealed that the minimum is found when local failure took place, rather than the occurrence of global failure. However, the aforementioned methods either require multiple material parameters or fail to provide a rigorous limit value of loads.

Limit analysis method, grounded in the theory of plastic collapse, offers an alternative approach. Using the kinematical approach on the basis of the upper-bound theorem, the rigorous upper-bound limit loads no less than the actual collapse loads are obtained through a kinematically admissible failure mechanism10. This method applies to complex geometries and boundary conditions, features simple computation, and can effectively incorporate external loads (e.g., seismic forces, pore water pressure and surcharges)11–15. Stability of compound slopes have been extensively analysed recently using the upper-bound theorem. For example, Rao et al.16 established two-dimensional stability charts for compound slopes under seismic excitations. Zhang and Yang17 proposed a unified three-dimensional analytical framework to take the global and local instabilities of compound slopes into account. They revealed that the geometric characteristics of slopes significantly influence the failure patterns. In addition, for analysis through a finite element limit analysis method, Lim et al.18 applied both Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion and Hoek-Brown failure criteria to stability analysis of different types slopes and made a detailed explanation to the associated failure mechanisms.

Most of the aforementioned studies, however, focus on the intact homogeneous slopes. In practical, due to geological structural processes and human activity, the soil mass commonly exhibits heterogeneity and anisotropy, which affects the slope stability19–25. Numerous studies have been conducted on the stability of slopes considering heterogeneity and anisotropy of cohesion. For example, Chen10 explored five typical forms of cohesion heterogeneity and anisotropy in two-dimensional stability analysis of slopes. Li et al.26 furthered the studies into three-dimensional analysis under seismic excitations. The same finding was presented that the slopes become unstable as the heterogeneity coefficient decreases or the anisotropy coefficient increases. Because the traditional kinematical approach fails to account for the heterogeneity of the internal friction angle, Qin and Chian27 developed a kinematically admissible failure mechanism using a discretization technique, which can effectively address this issue. Based on this method, Sun et al.28 presented the stability analysis of slopes involving the spatially varying internal friction angle. As for the anisotropy of internal friction angle, Duncan and Seed29, AI-Karni and AI Shamrani30 revealed its insignificant influence on slope stability. Meanwhile, the soil mass is compressive material with low tensile strength. Under external actions such as earthquakes, excavation, or wetting–drying cycles, tensile stresses may exceed the tensile strength of the soil, resulting in tension cracks across the slope surface or top surface of slopes31. As a result, these cracks can severely weaken slope stability and trigger collapse of slopes. For example, Utili32 and Michalowski33 introduced a vertical tension crack into log-spiral failure mechanism of simple slopes. Both dry and wet cracks are considered. The former explored the variation of the depth and location of cracks in detail when slopes are about to collapse. The latter focused on how pore water pressure affects slopes with cracks, developing corresponding stability charts. He et al.34 and Rao et al.35 extended the studies of Utili and Michalowski to three-dimensional analyses, respectively. The similar conclusion was drawn that a huge reduction in critical height is found for steep slopes, especially in the two-dimensional case34,35. In addition, Hou et al.36 investigated the heterogeneous slopes with cracks based on a discrete kinematical approach. The linear variations along the depth of both cohesion and internal friction angle were considered. However, few studies have addressed the influence how cracks affect the stability of heterogeneous compound slopes.

This study aims at assessing the seismic stability of cracked compound slopes with heterogeneous soil mass. Within the framework of limit analysis, a failure mechanism satisfying kinematic admissibility is constructed using a discretization technique derived from the upper-bound theorem27. A vertical tension crack is introduced into this failure mechanism. The pseudo-static approach is employed to account for the seismic forces during an earthquake. The heterogeneity of both cohesion and internal friction angle is considered. Afterwards, the work rate balance equation is formulated by equating external work rates to internal energy dissipation. An optimization algorithm integrated with the bisection method is used to compute upper-bound solutions of the stability factor or safety factor. Results are verified through comparison with published data and finite element simulations. The analysis focuses specifically on the variations of tension cracks in compound slopes under static and seismic conditions, focusing on crack depth, location, and critical failure mechanisms. Furthermore, the effects of soil heterogeneity on tension cracks are investigated. The findings complement previous researches on compound slope stability and support the development of geotechnically sound, risk-informed, and slope engineering for use in the built environments.

Methodology

This section presents the seismic stability assessment of cracked compound slopes with soil heterogeneity based on a kinematic method with a discretization technique. The construction of the proposed failure mechanism is introduced, followed by the derivation of expressions for the external work rates due to both soil self-weight and seismic forces and for the internal energy dissipation rate along the failure surface. Finally, the stability factor and safety factor of slopes are calculated through the work rate balance equation.

Kinematical approach with a discretization technique

Under the limit analysis framework, the kinematical approach based on the upper-bound theorem assumes that the soil mass is perfectly plastic and conforms to the Mohr–Coulomb failure criterion. Deformation is governed by the associated flow rule, where the velocity vector forms an angle equal to the internal friction angle with the tangent to the failure surface at each point. A discretized kinematically admissible failure mechanism is constructed accordingly, replacing traditional log-spiral curve with a failure surface consisting of infinitesimal straight lines27.

Figure 1 illustrates a typical sliding-type failure of a highway embankment slope. The upper segment of the failure surface corresponds to tensile failure, whereas the lower portion primarily exhibits shear failure. Therefore, the proposed discrete failure mechanism of a compound slope with cracks is established, as described in Fig. 2a. A global failure is presented. The total slope height is H. The upper and lower slope inclinations are βu and βl, respectively. The height of upper portion of the slope is defined as αH where α is a dimensionless depth coefficient, ranging from 0.0 to 1.0. Chen10 found that the below-toe failure typically occurs under the case of the internal friction angle less than 5° and the slope inclination no larger than 30°. Those conditions are not common in highway embankment slopes. Therefore, the below-toe failure is not considered in this paper. Instead, assuming that the failure surface intersects the slope toe, namely toe failure, a vertical tension crack with depth of d is introduced in the rear portion of the slope to account for the inability to bear soil mass tensile stresses. The sliding mass is assumed to rotate rigidly about point O at an angular velocity ω. Apart from the global instability, local instability is common occurrence when compound slopes collapse. According to Rao et al.16 and Zhang and Yang17, the geometric characteristics of compound slopes significantly influence the failure patterns. For a convex compound slopes (βu < βl), failure surface might go through lower portion of slopes, resembling simple slope failure with a top surface inclined at angle α to the horizontal; this is classified as local failure I (Fig. 2b). For concave slopes (βu > βl), failure may occur through the upper slope portion, defined as local failure II (Fig. 2c).

Verification

The stability factor and safety factor are computed to assess the stability of cracked compound slopes with soil heterogeneity. The proposed method’s accuracy and effectiveness are verified by comparing the calculated results with those from the traditional kinematical approach and finite element method. The comparison includes stability factor, safety factor, and critical failure surface.

First, the solutions of Rao et al.16 and He et al.34 are presented for comparison to demonstrate equivalence of the proposed discrete failure mechanism. The traditional kinematical approach was employed in their studies to analyze slope stability, despite focusing on different slope types. For simple homogeneous slopes with the crack, He et al.34 adapted the strategy described by Utili32 and extended it to three-dimensional stability analysis. In this paper, the compound heterogeneous slope degenerates into a simple homogeneous slope by setting βl = βu, c0 = ch, φ0 = φh and the values of γ and c0 to be the same. Then, the stability factor of a simple homogeneous slope with the crack can be easily obtained. Figure 5a shows the comparison of stability factor of He et al.34 and this paper. The results of this paper are highly in accordance with those of He et al.34, which indicates the effectiveness of introducing a tension crack into the discrete failure mechanism. For compound homogeneous slopes without the crack, Rao et al.16 calculated the stability factor and established stability charts under seismic excitations. The stability factor for compound homogeneous slopes without cracks was obtained by setting z = 0.0, c0 = ch and φ0 = φh. The results were compared with those reported by Rao et al.16, as presented in Fig. 5b. The results produced by the proposed method are consistent with those from the traditional kinematically admissible failure mechanism under static and seismic conditions. Furthermore, the comparison shows the ability to identify different failure patterns of the proposed discrete failure mechanism.

Comparison of stability factor: (a) for simple slopes with the crack under βl = βu; (b) for compound slopes without the crack under βl = 45°, φ0 = 20° and α = 0.5.

The finite element limit analysis method (FELAM) offers the advantage of calculating the safety factor without assuming a predefined failure mechanism; however, it is difficult to introduce tension cracks into finite element models. Therefore, FELAM results are provided to validate the proposed method’s applicability, especially in identifying critical failure surfaces for compound slopes without cracks. Additionally, the results of Zhang and Yang17 are also presented in Fig. 6. As for compound homogeneous slopes, two types of slopes are adopted: a convex slope and a concave slope. Both slopes have the height of 10 m. The homogeneous soil mass properties used are unit weight 20 kN/m³, cohesion 6 kPa, and internal friction angle 30°. As depicted in Fig. 6, despite the different methods, the difference of FS is within 1%. The critical failure surfaces are consistent with each other. As for the comparison of compound heterogeneous slope stability, the soil properties of above two slopes are changed as follows: γ = 20 kN/m³, c0 = 6 kPa, ch=16 kPa, φ0 = 20° and φh = 30°. Figure 7 presents that the calculated FS is smaller than that of FELAM with difference less than 7%. Notably, the critical failure surfaces are highly consistent. The comparison indicates that the proposed method can well capture the failure patterns of compound slopes. Meanwhile, the values of four independent variables corresponding to the upper-bound solutions are also given, offering insight into the optimization process.

Result and discussion

Stability factor and failure pattern

The stability factor is commonly used to evaluate slope stability at the limit equilibrium state, corresponding to FS = 1.0. To investigate the stability of homogeneous slopes, set c0 = ch and φ0 = φh. Figure 8 presents the variation of stability factor for different values of βu ranging from 10° to 80° while βl remains unchanged, under the case of α = 0.3, 0.5, 0.7. Take βl = 45° and 60° as examples. Whether the crack is considered or not, stability factor is negatively correlated with βu. The value of α significantly influences the rate of this decrease, especially in the case of βu ranging from the value corresponding to the beginning of global failure (βc) to the value equal to βl. Specifically, the larger the α is, the more pronounced the extent of the decrease will be. That can be attributed to the compound slopes becoming overall steeper for large α when βu increases. Furthermore, α is positively correlated with stability factor when βu < βl while becoming negatively correlated with stability factor when βu > βl.

Stability factor of compound slopes under static condition: (a) βl = 45°; (b) βl = 60°.

The presence of a cracks reduces slope stability compared to intact slopes. The magnitude of this reduction in stability factor depends on the value of α. For βu < βc and βu > βl, a larger α amplifies the difference in stability factor between intact and cracked compound slopes while narrows the difference for βc < βu < βl. As for the failure pattern of compound slopes, global failure generally takes place. Local failure occurs when βu < 30° or βu > 70°. The range where local failure happens is influenced by βl. Specifically, a larger βl tends to enlarge the extent of local failure Ⅰ while reducing the extent of local failure Ⅱ. Interestingly, the presence of the crack increases the possibility of local failure occurrence.

Therefore, it can be concluded that the slope inclinations βu and βl along with the depth coefficient α determine the slope geometry, resulting in different failure patterns of slope in compound slopes. Furthermore, the slope geometry is a key factor influencing the difference in slope stability between intact and cracked compound slopes. To minimize the adverse effect of a tension crack, βu is recommended to be set to βc for different values of α.

Depth and location of the crack

Once the upper bound solutions of stability factor are found, dimensionless parameters d/H and X/L are calculated to analyze the crack depth and location. As illustrated in Fig. 2a, X represents the horizontal distance from the crack location to the slope toe, while L denotes the horizontal distance from the slope crest to the slope toe.

Figures 9 and 10 present the variation in crack depth and location for different values of βu under the cases of α = 0.3, 0.5, 0.7. As expected, a larger βl induces a deeper crack depth due to the overall slope inclination becoming steeper. Specifically, the crack depth slightly increases when local failure Ⅰ occurs while it linearly increases when global failure takes places. Meanwhile, the crack location gradually moves away from the slope toe and mutations in its location happens in the mutual conversion between local and global failure. Notably, α has a significant influence on the depth and location of the crack. As shown in Fig. 9, on the occurrence of global failure, the larger the α is, the greater the influence of βu on the crack depth becomes. In addition, the crack depth decreases as α increases for a convex compound slope (βl > βu). This may be because larger α causes the gravity of the soil mass to be concentrated at the rear part of the upper portion of the slope. This promotes tensile failure near the slope face surface layer, thereby reducing the crack depth. However, an opposite variation can be observed for a concave compound slope (βl < βu). It can be attributed to that the steeper the βu is, the more significant the tensile failure trend will be. It can be attributed to the enhanced tensile stress at the rear part of the slope induced by larger βu, which prompts the crack depth to be deeper.

Depth of the crack of compound slopes under static condition: (a) βl = 45°; (b) βl = 60°.

Location of the crack of compound slopes under static condition: (a) βl = 45°; (b) βl = 60°.

Apart from that, Fig. 10 indicates that the impact of α on the crack location is further controlled by βu. When βu < βc, the crack location becomes closer to the slope toe as α increases. The opposite trend can be observed when βu > βl. When βc < βu < βl, the crack location is hardly affected by α, indicating that the slope failure path tends to be stable and thus the influence of slope geometry on the failure pattern is weakened. This phenomenon is quite different from the results of simple slopes.

Figure 11 shows critical failure surfaces of cracked compound slopes with fixed βl for various values of βu and α. It can be observed that both βu and α dominate the variation of the compound slope contour. When βu < βc, the failure of compound slopes is characterized by local failure I. Larger α makes the gravity more concentrated in the area above the slope toe, which is prone to form a concentrated stress zone. Therefore, the crack location moves towards the slope toe. As βu increases to βc, the failure pattern of compound slopes begins to transition. Larger α causes subsidence in the upper slope section, moving the crack toward the slope top surface and away from the slope toe, indicating tensile failure advancing toward the slope surface. When βu > βc, the compound slope is transformed into a typical concave slope. The steep upper section generates a significant tensile stress zone beneath the slope surface. Larger α amplifies this tensile zone near the rear edge, shifting the crack location toward the slope face, leading to the crack location moving towards the slope face. However, due to the slope geometry, the crack is restricted from developing upward, resulting in the crack location being closer to the slope toe instead.

Effect of seismic force on slope stability and the crack

Figure 12 illustrates the effect of seismic forces on the stability of cracked compound slopes, focusing on the stability factor, crack depth and location, and critical failure surfaces. As shown in Fig. 12a, stability factor decreases with increasing kh, indicating that the presence of an earthquake significantly weakens slope stability. The amplitude of decrease in stability factor is notably affect by βu. In terms of the failure pattern, the seismic excitation broadens the range of global failure while narrowing the zones of two local failures. It can be attributed to the fact that the need for increased energy dissipation along the failure surface to counterbalance the additional external work rate introduced by seismic forces. Consequently, the failure surface extends deeper and further into the interior of the slope. Figure 12b shows that the crack becomes deeper as kh increases, particularly under global failure conditions, while the change is less pronounced during local failures. The increase in horizontal inertial force leads to enhanced tensile stress in the rear region and around the slope transition point (point C in Fig. 2a), promoting deeper crack formation. However, local failure is generally characterized by shorter failure surfaces and involves only a limited portion of the slope. Since the magnitude of the seismic force is proportional to the volume of the sliding soil mass, the influence on crack depth under local failures is relatively minor. Figure 12c shows that the location of the crack shifts away from the slope toe (point D in Fig. 2a) towards the rear region of slopes as kh increases. This backward migration is a result of the evolving failure mechanism, where increasing seismic forces promote deeper and more internally located failure surfaces within the slope, as well illustrated in Fig. 12d.

Effect of soil heterogeneity on slope stability and the crack

Compared with traditional kinematical approach, the proposed method can well address the problem of soil heterogeneity in slope stability analysis, especially for the heterogeneity of internal friction angle of soil. As shown in Fig. 13, the slope stability improves with increasing c0 and φ0 owing to the enhanced shear resistance of the upper soil. The increase in stability factor is most pronounced in local failure Ⅰ, becomes moderate in global failure and remains stable in local failure Ⅱ. Furthermore, increasing c0 and φ0 broaden the range of local failure Ⅰ while narrowing that of local failure Ⅱ, with the zone of global failure keeping relatively unchanged.

However, Fig. 14 reveals that the crack depth becomes deeper with higher shear strength of soil, especially on the occurrence of global failure. This mainly stems from the fact that the elevated shear strength leads to higher tensile strength of soil, making it difficult for tensile cracks to form in the shallow zone.

To satisfy the requirements of failure path continuity and minimum energy consumption, the failure surface migrates deeper into the slope body, resulting in increased crack depth. This reflects the transition of the failure mechanism from shallow tensile cracking to deep shear sliding. For local failures, crack depth remains relatively stable, showing only a slight increase when cohesion is heterogeneous. Figure 15 depicts that the crack location progressively shifts toward the slope toe as c0 and φ0 increase. With greater shear strength, forming a failure surface in the rear portion of the slope becomes energetically less favourable due to the increased external work needed to mobilize the stronger soil. Consequently, the failure surface advances toward the front of the slope.

Figure 16 further demonstrates the effect of soil heterogeneity on the critical failure surfaces of cracked compound slopes. As c0 and φ0 increase, the upper portion of the failure surface becomes more inclined toward the shallow slope region, while the lower portion penetrates deeper into the slope. This results in a deeper and broader failure mechanism, consistent with the trends observed for crack depth and location in Figs. 14 and 15. This behavior resembles the “seesaw effect”: soil with higher shear strength promotes shallow failure, while weaker soil induces deeper failure zones.

Conclusions

Based on the kinematical approach incorporating a discretization technique, this paper primarily investigates the seismic stability of cracked compound slopes, focusing on the stability factor, crack depth and location, and critical failure surfaces. The pseudo-static approach is employed to account for seismic forces. The soil heterogeneity is considered. The proposed failure mechanism can identify the global and location instabilities of slopes. Calculated results were validated against previously reported solutions and finite element analyses. The following are the main findings:

(1) The geometric characteristics of compound slopes, which are defined by upper slope inclination (βu), lower slope inclination (βₗ), and dimensionless depth coefficient (α), govern the variation in stability factors and determine the transition between local and global failure patterns. As βu increases, slope stability consistently decreases, with a more pronounced reduction observed at higher α values.

(2) Cracks markedly reduce compound slope stability. Crack depth and location are strongly influenced by the slope geometry. In concave compound slopes (βu>βₗ), cracks tend to be deeper and closer to the slope face; in convex compound slopes (βu<βₗ), cracks are shallower and shift toward the toe. These variations reflect the evolution of failure mechanisms associated with geometric changes.

(3) Seismic force significantly alters the failure mechanism of cracked compound slopes. As the horizontal acceleration coefficient (kh) increases, critical failure surfaces extend deeper, transitioning from local failure to global failure. Meanwhile, cracks become deeper and shift the location upslope.

(4) Soil heterogeneity greatly influences the failure mechanism of cracked compound slopes. As shear strength of upper portion enhances, slope stability improves and local failure Ⅰ becomes more dominant. Crack depth increases due to the transition from tensile to shear failure, and crack location shifts toward the slope toe as failure surfaces move forward. This reflects a “seesaw effect,” where stronger upper soil resists shallow cracking, pushing the failure surface deeper while maintaining equilibrium between strength distribution and energy dissipation.

It should be noted that, in the present application, the limit analysis approach assumes perfectly plastic soil behavior, which does not capture the peak and residual shear strengths of the soil. The associated flow rule may overestimate the true dilatancy of the geomaterial, as it neglects variations in internal friction angle or rupture angle during deformation. Moreover, only the onset of slope failure is considered, without addressing progressive landslides. Despite these limitations, the calculated limit loads still provide a rigorous upper bound for any kinematically admissible mechanism, and the proposed method offers guidance for evaluating and improving the seismic stability of cracked compound slopes in practical slope engineering.
