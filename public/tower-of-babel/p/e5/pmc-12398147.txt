Integrating experiential learning theory with innovation and entrepreneurship education: a qualitative study on Chinese medical students
Authors: Yanlin Wang, Yichen Fu, Xuesen Wu, Haiyin Deng, Yize Ruan, Chuanna Liu, Changxin Chen, Yuzhan Gao, Hualiang You, Chengping Sun, Yaru Liu, Xu Zhang, Jingchun Zhang, Jia Huang, Sheng Dong, Jingsong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional medical education models emphasizing didactic knowledge transmission inadequately prepare healthcare professionals for complex challenges in modern medicine. Innovation and Entrepreneurship Education (IEE) offers opportunities for fostering innovative thinking, yet limited research systematically examines how medical students experience entrepreneurial learning processes. Accordingly, this study applies Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) to examine how medical students engage with IEE and develop entrepreneurial competencies within healthcare contexts.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 35 medical students from four Chinese universities who participated in IEE programs (2019–2024). Data were analyzed using thematic analysis, following Kolb’s four-stage experiential learning cycle.FindingsMedical students’ participation in IEE maps clearly onto Kolb’s ELT cycle, yet with adaptations shaped by their clinical training. In Concrete Experience phase, students encounter technical, market, and outcome uncertainty (e.g., students described struggling with product design due to limited entrepreneurial knowledge). During Reflective Observation phase, they assess failures as learning opportunities through clinical systems thinking; one team improved innovation by repeatedly re-evaluating unmet clinical needs. Abstract Conceptualization phase involves synthesizing insights into broader models through interdisciplinary dialogue, such as redesigning product materials through interdisciplinary dialogue and expert consultation. In Active Experimentation phase, students apply conceptual models while weighing career implications; for example, some plan to develop medical devices post-graduation, while others reframe their professional identity to include entrepreneurship.DiscussionThe integration of ELT with IEE addresses unique epistemological challenges medical students face when transitioning between evidence-based clinical reasoning and entrepreneurial thinking. Our adapted framework provides theoretical justification for navigating these contradictions through four interconnected stages connected by transitional processes of reflect, synthesize, apply, and iterate. However, institutional constraints including limited resources, technical capabilities, and regulatory barriers significantly impact learning effectiveness.ConclusionThis study makes three theoretical contributions: extending ELT through domain-specific adaptations for medical contexts, establishing a modified framework emphasizing transitional processes, and demonstrating how institutional contexts shape experiential learning outcomes. The integration offers promising pathways for developing innovative capabilities needed in contemporary healthcare by fostering graduates equipped to address complex challenges through entrepreneurial thinking and action. Background

When asked to reflect on the limitations of traditional medical education, one medical undergraduate student stated:

“Usually, most of the time is spent on learning professional knowledge from books. I feel that my thinking is rather rigid, and there is a lack of thinking and exercise related to innovation.” (Interviewee, N8, 21-year-old male, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student).

This candid reflection encapsulates a broader concern shared by many of our interviewees: traditional models of medical education, which emphasize the didactic transmission of knowledge, are insufficient for cultivating the innovative thinking and adaptive problem-solving abilities essential for contemporary medical practice [1]. As the field of medicine continues to evolve rapidly—driven by advances in genomics, artificial intelligence, and other emerging technologies—there is a growing consensus that static methods of instruction are inadequate. This necessitates an educational paradigm shift toward more dynamic, creative, and interdisciplinary approaches [2].

The prevalence of innovation and entrepreneurship education

Innovation and Entrepreneurship Education (IEE) has played a pivotal role in reshaping educational paradigms across disciplines. Initially introduced at Harvard Business School in 1947, IEE has since gained international recognition for fostering knowledge integration, innovation, and practical application [3]. This educational framework nurtures a pioneering spirit among students, encouraging them to challenge conventional thinking and devise creative solutions to complex problems, thereby addressing pressing societal needs [4]. IEE is broadly defined as the process of acquiring the competencies necessary to generate innovative ideas and transform them into actionable outcomes, such as products or initiatives [5]. For instance, Grailer III JG et al. developed an innovation and entrepreneurship training program centered on experiential learning, culminating in a team-based capstone project. Students are tasked with creating a comprehensive pitch deck for a 15-minute presentation, supported by either an executive summary of a business plan or a preliminary Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) proposal [6].

Empirical research underscores the value of entrepreneurial education at the university level, demonstrating its positive influence on students’ entrepreneurial attitudes and intentions [7–9]. Furthermore, such education enhances students’ entrepreneurial self-efficacy, which, in turn, strengthens their motivation and likelihood of pursuing entrepreneurial endeavors [10, 11]. Additional studies have shown that entrepreneurship education not only encourages students to consider entrepreneurship as a viable career path but also equips them with essential skills for launching and sustaining new ventures [12]. Findings by Mahendra et al. highlight the role of motivation and attitude in mediating the relationship between entrepreneurship education and students’ perceived viability and feasibility of entrepreneurship [13].

However, most existing studies on the impact of entrepreneurship education employ quantitative methodologies. These studies often focus on measuring entrepreneurial intentions or evaluating the efficacy of experiential learning models [13–15], yet they tend to overlook how students experience and internalize each stage of the learning process. In contrast, a qualitative approach, particularly through in-depth interviews, offers rich insights into students’ lived experiences. This method allows for a more nuanced understanding of how students engage with and derive meaning from their educational experiences [16]. While some qualitative studies have explored experiential learning in entrepreneurship [17], they often fall short of fully examining the interconnectedness of Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) stages or identifying the practical challenges students encounter during their learning journeys.

The landscape of innovation and entrepreneurship education in Chinese universities

As technological and industrial advancements intensify, there is an increasing demand for professionals who possess both technical expertise and entrepreneurial acumen. In response, IEE is being leveraged to train medical students not only in scientific and clinical competencies but also in research autonomy and project-based innovation [18, 19]. Recognizing this urgent need, China has taken significant steps to promote IEE, exemplified by the establishment of the National Innovation and Entrepreneurship Training Program for College Students (I&E). Launched in 2011 following policy recommendations from the Ministry of Education in 2010, the program aims to strengthen students’ innovative and entrepreneurship capabilities [20].

By 2021, Hunan Province alone had approved 11,394 I&E projects with a combined funding total of 87.15 million yuan, involving nearly 50,000 students. Medical schools have demonstrated substantial engagement; in 2023 alone, over 2,100 medical undergraduates at Central South University and 61.4% of clinical medicine majors at Yangtze University participated in IEE initiatives [19, 21–23]. Reflecting the nationwide emphasis, the 2023 Innovation Index reports that IEE programs have been implemented in 1,275 undergraduate and 1,545 vocational colleges across China, encompassing nearly all medical institutions. Among them, Wenzhou Medical University stands out, ranking highest among medical universities and earning national recognition as an Innovation and Entrepreneurship Education Practice Base, as well as hosting a National University Science Park [24]. Taking Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine as an example, innovation and entrepreneurship education is first carried out by offering I&E courses. Students enrolled in these courses are encouraged to freely develop medicine-related projects based on their innovative ideas. Under the guidance of professional instructors, they continually refine their innovative thinking, learn to analyze market pain points, and write corresponding business plans. Ultimately, they present their designed products in the form of PowerPoint presentations to participate in various innovation and entrepreneurship competitions.

Research on the benefits of IEE for medical students in China has primarily focused on its capacity to enhance creativity [25, 26], mental well-being [27], and curricular integration [28]. However, there remains a gap in the application of pedagogical theories to systematically evaluate and improve these programs. A theoretical framework could yield valuable insights for optimizing curriculum design, instructional methods, and overall educational strategies, thereby advancing the effectiveness of IEE in medical education.

Experiential learning theory as a framework for IEE

To address the identified gap, the present study adopts Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) as an analytical framework to examine medical students’ engagement with IEE. Originally developed by David A. Kolb, ELT offers a comprehensive model for understanding learning as a process grounded in personal experience, emphasizing the active role of learners in constructing knowledge through a cyclical pattern of experience, reflection, conceptualization, and experimentation [29] (see Fig. 1). According to ELT, knowledge is “grasped” through concrete experience and abstract conceptualization, and then “transformed” via reflective observation and active experimentation [30]. The learning cycle begins with a concrete experience, followed by reflection, which informs abstract understanding. This knowledge is then applied in new contexts, completing the cycle [30]. Learners can enter the cycle at any point, but effective learning requires progression through all four stages in sequence [31, 32].

Kolb’s Experiential Learning Model, as presented by Abdulwahed and Nagy [36]

This theoretical model is particularly well-suited to the context of IEE in medical education, as it closely aligns with the iterative and dynamic nature of entrepreneurial practice. The emphasis on active experimentation, critical reflection, and continuous adaptation resonates with the core demands of innovation in healthcare—where solutions must be both ethically responsible and practically viable. While other learning theories, such as Transformative Learning Theory [33] and Constructivist Learning Theory [34, 35], focus on perspective transformation and knowledge construction, respectively, they often lack ELT’s structured feedback loop and practical congruence with the trial-and-error methodology inherent in entrepreneurial activity, especially within complex, high-stakes medical settings.

This study utilizes semi-structured interviews to examine how medical students engage with each stage of the ELT cycle—Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active Experimentation—within the context of IEE. The findings illuminate how students evolve from passive recipients of knowledge to active agents of innovation, empowered to address the complex and evolving challenges of modern healthcare. By applying ELT as an analytical lens, this research sheds light on the pedagogical mechanisms underpinning IEE and highlights the value of experiential learning in shaping future medical professionals’ entrepreneurial competencies.

Research question

How do medical students experience and navigate the four stages of Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory (Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active Experimentation) within Innovation and Entrepreneurship Education programs, and what factors facilitate or hinder their progression through the complete learning cycle in developing entrepreneurial competencies for healthcare innovation?

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted over a period from January 2022 to October 2024. The study adopts a qualitative research design, employing semi-structured interviews to collect detailed and descriptive data from participants. This interview method facilitates reciprocal interaction between the interviewer and interviewee, allowing for the flexibility to ask follow-up questions based on participants’ responses and enabling interviewees to articulate their perspectives verbally [37]. In addition, content analysis is used to examine interviewees’ personal experiences and perspectives in depth. As noted by Hsieh and Shannon [38], content analysis allows for the direct extraction of concepts from data collected through interviews and other methodologies. This analytical process involves encoding the data, followed by the identification of subcategories, categories, and overarching themes within the text.

Study participants

The recruitment process utilized open-access channels, with most participants sourced from the primary research host institution (Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine), necessitated by logistical and geographic limitations. To enhance sample diversity, seven additional participants were intentionally included from external universities. The final sample (N = 35) was recruited from four distinct higher education institutions: Fujian University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Beijing Sport University, Xiamen Medical College, and Huaqiao University (see Supplementary Material 1 and Table 1). A purposive sampling method was employed, enabling a nuanced and in-depth exploration of the research phenomenon by selecting participants with direct, contextually relevant experiences [39]. By strategically selecting participants who possess varied characteristics—including age, gender, academic progression, and engagement with innovation and entrepreneurship initiatives—the sampling approach ensures a rich, multifaceted investigative landscape.

Inclusion Criteria: Eligible participants were those who had engaged in activities related to IEE, including but not limited to courses, projects, or competitions, between 2019 and 2024. All participants were required to be willing to share their experiences relevant to the study’s aims.

Data collection

The interviews were conducted under the supervision of the first and second authors (YW and YF), both of whom hold doctoral degrees in sociology and have expertise in qualitative methodologies. Additional interviewers underwent comprehensive training in qualitative research and interviewing techniques. Prior to the formal data collection, a series of three pilot interviews with individuals independent of the research samples were conducted to refine the interview protocol and ensure its efficacy in eliciting relevant data.

Semi-structured, face-to-face interviews were conducted, each lasting between 30 and 60 mins. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was achieved—defined as the point at which no new information or themes emerged [40]. Saturation occurred after 24 interviews; however, following Hennink et al.’s [41] recommendation for post-saturation sampling, we extended the participant pool to 35 individuals to rigorously validate thematic stability. The subsequent 11 interviews confirmed the initial findings, with no emergent themes, thus substantiating the robustness of our data saturation approach.

Preceding each interview, researchers provided participants with a comprehensive overview delineating the purpose and scope of the study, subsequently obtaining written consent. Assurances were extended regarding the confidentiality of all participant information, emphasizing its exclusive utilization for research purposes, with interview transcripts anonymized through coding procedures. Participants retained the right to withdraw from the study at any juncture without repercussion. Employing comprehensive recording apparatus, all interviews were documented in their entirety.

Data analysis

Data analysis proceeded concomitantly with the interview process. Transcripts underwent scrutiny employing inductive thematic analysis, as proposed by Braun and Clarke [42], supported by NVivo 11 Pro software (QSR International). The analytical framework followed the methodological steps outlined by Graneheim and Lundman [43], with coding facilitated by QSR International, as recommended by Zamawe [44].

Researchers transcribed each interview verbatim, subsequently segmenting the transcripts into discrete units. The units were then scrutinized to extract meaningful content, to which one or more codes were assigned. Through iterative analysis and cross-referencing with other coded segments, analogous or congruent codes were organized into subcategories, ultimately culminating in the development of a hierarchical list of subcategories. Themes emerged from the discerned disparities and correlations between these subcategories, thus furnishing a cohesive framework for the data analysis process underlying the transcriptional interviews (see Table 2).

The research team comprises individuals with backgrounds in medical education, sociology, and entrepreneurship studies. Some team members had prior experience with IEE programs, which facilitated rapport with participants but could potentially introduce confirmation bias. Therefore, the assurance of data credibility and reliability adheres to the four standards outlined by Lincoln and Guba [45], encompassing credibility, reliability, confirmability, and transferability.

Findings: the ELT learning circle embedded in IEE

Our analysis reveals that medical students’ experiences in Innovation and Entrepreneurship Education (IEE) align with Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) cycle but with distinct characteristics specific to the medical education context. The four stages—Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active Experimentation—manifest uniquely in medical entrepreneurship education, shaped by the clinical training background of participants and the healthcare context. Students experience these stages with varying levels of engagement and success, with some struggling to complete the full learning cycle due to individual, programmatic, and institutional factors.

Concrete Experience (CE): initial challenge and uncertainty

ELT posits that Concrete Experience constitutes the foundational stage of the learning cycle, wherein direct engagement with novel situations establishes the groundwork for subsequent reflection and comprehension. Kolb and Kolb [46] assert that authentic learning emerges through immediate involvement in unfamiliar contexts, which serves as the catalyst for deeper understanding. Within the domain of medical entrepreneurship, this initial phase is distinguished by initial challenges and uncertainties that differentiate it from conventional entrepreneurship education paradigms. Medical students, who are oriented toward evidence-based reasoning and structured clinical protocols, encounter difficulty when confronted with the inherent ambiguity characteristic of entrepreneurial endeavors.

(1) Technical uncertainty: product design and commercialization gaps

Medical students encounter significant challenges in understanding core entrepreneurial processes, particularly in product design and commercialization strategies. As one participant (N3, 23-year-old female, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student) articulated: “In our ventures into innovation and entrepreneurship, we realized the need for a structured framework. This framework would encompass product design, iterative upgrades, and strategies for marketing and sales promotion. However, our understanding of these elements was rudimentary, and we faced challenges in translating this theoretical knowledge into practical applications.” The majority of medical students lacking prior entrepreneurial exposure manifest profound disorientation when confronting the technical complexities of product development. One participant (N7, 27-year-old female, Clinical Medicine [Integrated Chinese & Western Medicine], second-year Ph.D. candidate) expressed this uncertainty: “At the beginning of the innovation and entrepreneurship project [Qingda Granules – Antihypertensive Medication for Hypertention], I was really not sure, and I had no relevant experience before. I felt that I could not find the clue.” This sense of confusion was compounded by a lack of experience and real-world case studies, leaving many students unsure of how to move from abstract ideas to viable, market-ready solutions.

(2) Market uncertainty: limited healthcare commercial landscape knowledge

Students demonstrated significant knowledge gaps regarding the healthcare sector’s commercial dynamics and market validation processes. This uncertainty stems from their predominantly clinical training background, which emphasizes patient care over market considerations. Participant N7 (27-year-old female, Clinical Medicine [Integrated Chinese & Western Medicine], second-year Ph.D. candidate) reflected: “Our understanding of market demands was insufficient… the transition from individual efforts to commercial operations posed an enormous challenge.” The healthcare innovation context presents unique market complexities that distinguish it from other entrepreneurial domains, requiring students to navigate regulatory frameworks and clinical validation processes simultaneously with traditional market considerations.

(3) Outcome uncertainty: unpredictable returns on investment

Students expressed considerable anxiety regarding the unpredictable nature of entrepreneurial outcomes, particularly given their substantial investment of time and resources. This uncertainty manifests as concern over the disconnect between effort expended and tangible results achieved. As described by N15 (22-year-old female, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student): “After three to four months of dedicated effort, you may ultimately secure only a third-place award or a silver prize, or even achieve nothing at all.” This outcome uncertainty reflects the inherent risk-averse nature of medical training, where predictable protocols and evidence-based outcomes are prioritized, creating tension with entrepreneurship’s inherent unpredictability.

(4) Enhanced resilience among students with prior entrepreneurial exposure

Notably, students with previous exposure to entrepreneurship concepts (constituting approximately 31.4% of our sample) demonstrated enhanced adaptive capacities during the uncertainty phase. As one such participant (N12, 20-year-old male, Speech and Language Therapy, third-year undergraduate student) observed: “Through previous engagement in innovative entrepreneurship, I have enhanced multifaceted competencies encompassing learning agility, stress resilience, organizational skills, and communication capabilities. This development enables me to respond with greater composure when confronting uncertain scenarios.” These findings suggest that early entrepreneurial exposure can significantly enhance students’ capacity to navigate ambiguity and develop resilience essential for entrepreneurial success.

Reflective Observation (RO): introspection and critical analysis of challenges

Following initial experiential encounters, students engage in systematic reflection that transforms challenges into learning opportunities, characterized by distinctive modalities shaped by their clinical training background.

(1) Systems-oriented reflection considering healthcare implications

Medical students demonstrate unique reflective practices that contextualize innovation within broader healthcare system dynamics. Their clinical training enables systems-thinking approaches that consider innovation as interconnected processes rather than isolated inventions. One participant (N3, 23-year-old female, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student) provided comprehensive systems perspective: “Clinical medicine serves as both the origin and continuum of technological innovation. The process begins with identifying clinical problems that drive the development of medical devices. During device prototyping, iterative cycles of experimental validation and regulatory testing are conducted. Subsequently, clinical deployment enables real-world patient application, which necessitates systematic collection of patient experience data and feedback for product optimization. Only after resolving all critical pain points through this feedback loop can the device be validated as a mature solution and reintegrated into clinical practice.” This systems-oriented reflection distinguishes medical students’ learning processes from conventional entrepreneurship education, embedding innovation within clinical care continuums.

(2) Cyclical process of introspective reflection and reassessment

Students engage in iterative reflection cycles that progressively refine their understanding and approaches. This cyclical process enables systematic learning from setbacks and continuous improvement. One participant (N15, 22-year-old female, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student) described this iterative process: “Our team has gone through the process of negation again and again until we find something [Wrist-Ankle Acupuncture Device for Dysmenorrhea Treatment] more innovative than other projects. If we don’t insist, there may be no innovation in the end.” The cyclical nature of reflection demonstrates students’ capacity to persist through iterative failure cycles, transforming setbacks into learning opportunities essential for entrepreneurial development.

(3) Transformation of initial failures into innovation opportunities

Through systematic reflection, students develop capabilities to reframe failures as valuable learning experiences that inform future strategic approaches. This transformation represents a fundamental shift from traditional medical education’s emphasis on error avoidance to entrepreneurship’s acceptance of failure as necessary for innovation. One participant (N13, 22-year-old male, Rehabilitation Medicine and Physiotherapy, first-year Master student) articulated this transformative process: “Innovation is the key to the success of the project [Children’s Robot], but it is also the most difficult part… Only through continuous learning, literature search, and clinical observation and research, can we have some ideas slowly.”

(4) Clinical training background shaping distinctive reflection modalities

Students’ clinical training provides distinctive frameworks for reflection that emphasize evidence-based analysis and systematic observation. This background enables rigorous analytical approaches to entrepreneurial challenges while maintaining focus on patient care implications and clinical validation requirements. The integration of clinical training perspectives creates unique reflection modalities that distinguish medical entrepreneurship education from other disciplinary contexts, enhancing the depth and rigor of reflective practices. A doctoral student (N34, 30-year-old male, Rehabilitation Medicine [Integrated Chinese & Western Medicine], third-year Ph.D. candidate) shared, “I observed that conventional delivery of manual rehabilitation therapy, whether physical therapy or occupational therapy, in hospital settings is predominantly one-on-one. Each patient routinely requires approximately 30 minutes per session, resulting in significant labor costs and low efficiency. Consequently, I propose developing a rehabilitation robot through medical-engineering integration to enhance clinical rehabilitation efficiency.” This example illustrates how clinical training not only sharpens students’ ability to identify inefficiencies within real-world healthcare settings but also drives the formulation of innovative, evidence-informed solutions that bridge clinical needs with technological advancement.

Abstract Conceptualization (AO): Theoretical integration and conceptual development

The Abstract Conceptualization stage involves synthesizing insights from reflection into coherent theoretical frameworks, presenting particular challenges for medical students transitioning from concrete clinical knowledge to abstract entrepreneurial principles.

(1) Synthesis of industry pain points into feasible solutions

Students develop capabilities to transform specific industry challenges into innovative entrepreneurial solutions through systematic conceptualization processes. One participant (N21, 21-year-old male, Public Health Administration, third-year undergraduate student) articulated this synthesis: “We identify industry pain points through practical observations, formulate more feasible solutions based on these pain points, and then convert these solutions into specific innovative entrepreneurship practices.” This synthesis process enables students to bridge clinical observations with market opportunities, creating feasible solutions that address real-world healthcare challenges.

(2) Collaborative discussions and consensus-building processes

Students engage in collaborative conceptualization processes that integrate diverse perspectives and expertise. These discussions facilitate refinement of theoretical frameworks and ensure practical applicability. One participant (N2, 20-year-old male, Physical Therapy, third-year undergraduate student) emphasized collaborative importance: “Regarding the material selection for the product’s robotic arm, we conducted discussions with team members. Although initial conflicts arose due to limited mutual understanding and divergent perspectives, we ultimately reached a consensus on material selection through further communication with students possessing relevant academic backgrounds during these discussions.” Collaborative processes enable students to leverage collective knowledge and overcome individual conceptual limitations.

(3) Integration of disciplinary perspectives with mentor guidance

Students benefit significantly from mentor guidance in developing conceptual frameworks, particularly in bridging disciplinary knowledge with entrepreneurial principles. A postgraduate participant (N18, 23-year-old male, Rehabilitation Medicine and Physiotherapy, first-year Master student) highlighted mentor value: “When conceptualizing innovative points independently, we might not refine them with precision. It may be necessary to engage in discussions with specialized instructors, who can provide feedback on potential gaps in innovation. We then make adjustments accordingly. Through iterative cycles of this process, we can more effectively identify innovative points.” Mentor guidance provides essential scaffolding for conceptual development, enabling students to refine theoretical frameworks through expert feedback.

(4) Adaptation from rigid textbook knowledge to innovation-oriented thinking

Students must undergo significant epistemological transformation from rigid, protocol-driven medical education to flexible, innovation-oriented thinking essential for entrepreneurship. One participant (N8, 21-year-old male, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student) described this transformation: “I previously maintained a rigid mindset, focusing solely on textbook-based professional knowledge while lacking innovation-oriented thinking and training. After participating in innovation-focused courses, I began integrating medical perspectives into my analyses, enabling me to generate concrete ideas for entrepreneurial challenges that previously lacked clarity.” This adaptation represents a fundamental shift in epistemological approaches essential for successful entrepreneurial engagement.

Active Experimentation (AE): Practical application and future planning

The final stage involves applying conceptual frameworks to real-world contexts while planning future entrepreneurial trajectories, characterized by recognition of experiential maturity needs and distinctive healthcare innovation constraints.

(1) Entrepreneurial intentions with recognition of experiential maturity needs

Students express clear entrepreneurial intentions while acknowledging the necessity for additional preparation before full entrepreneurial commitment. One participant (N12, 20-year-old male, Speech and Language Therapy, third-year undergraduate student) expressed entrepreneurial motivation: “I want to start my own business. At present, our project [Wrist-Ankle Acupuncture Device for Dysmenorrhea Treatment; QiyinTong-Speech Therapy Device] is very advantageous, so I would like to continue to try after graduation.” However, students demonstrate realistic assessment of preparation requirements. One participant (N32, 22-year-old female, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student) cautioned: “If you just graduated, your understanding of society is not deep enough. If you start a business without some experience, you will be a fat sheep waiting to be slaughtered.” This pragmatic outlook reflects a common sentiment among students: while passion drives their entrepreneurial ambitions, they recognize the importance of accumulating practical experience and industry insight before fully committing to independent ventures.

(2) Distinctive implementation patterns reflecting healthcare innovation constraints

Medical students must navigate unique implementation challenges specific to healthcare innovation, including regulatory compliance, clinical validation, and patient safety considerations. Unlike entrepreneurship in other sectors, healthcare innovation requires simultaneous navigation of clinical and market validation processes within stringent regulatory frameworks. These distinctive patterns necessitate specialized support and extended timelines for implementation compared to conventional entrepreneurship contexts.

The complexity of healthcare innovation becomes evident when examining the regulatory landscape that governs medical device development. One participant (N8, 21-year-old male, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student) shared, “A significant drawback in our medical industry is the excessively long time required. One issue to consider is that if we directly attempt to use self-developed products, like a spinal bed, for patient treatment without medical device certification, people may perceive this as illegal or against regulations. In my view, developing a medical device requires a very long cycle. We must continuously refine it, which could be a decade long process of development.” This extended development timeline is further complicated by the heightened safety and efficacy standards required in healthcare settings. One participant (N2, 20-year-old male, Physical Therapy, third-year undergraduate student) also shared, “For medical applications, compared to other fields, greater emphasis must be placed on safety assurance and product practicality. We must consider whether patients can use the product more comfortably and derive tangible benefits while ensuring safety. Therefore, I believe implementing products in healthcare is particularly demanding.”

These insights reveal that healthcare innovation implementation is characterized by a dual burden of regulatory compliance and clinical effectiveness, creating implementation patterns that are fundamentally different from other entrepreneurial contexts. The combination of extended development cycles, rigorous safety requirements, and the need for both clinical and market validation creates a unique entrepreneurial landscape that requires specialized knowledge, resources, and patience.

(3) Career trajectory alignment with personal values and long-term objectives

Students engage in careful consideration of entrepreneurship’s alignment with personal values and long-term career objectives. One participant (N11, 23-year-old male, Rehabilitation Medicine and Physiotherapy, second-year Master student) reflected: “When starting a business, a lot of things need to be accumulated. What is your life value orientation? Whether you want to work or start a business is a different way of life and needs to be carefully considered.” This highlights that for many students, entrepreneurship is not merely a career option but a life choice that must resonate with their personal identity, purpose, and vision for long-term fulfillment.

(4) Expanded professional prospects within medical field innovation

Entrepreneurial exposure significantly broadens students’ career perspectives, encouraging exploration of diverse innovation opportunities within medical fields. Another participant (N1, 24-year-old male, Rehabilitation Medicine and Physiotherapy, first-year Master’s student) articulated expanded perspectives: “At present, I still want to be a doctor more. After all, it is a medical major, and most of my classmates want to be a doctor. But after participating in IEE, I will also have more choices and consider doing other work or entrepreneurship in the medical field, such as research and development of medical devices.” Such experiences prompt students to envision career paths beyond traditional clinical roles, fostering a mindset that embraces interdisciplinary collaboration, innovation, and the potential to drive systemic change within healthcare.

Discussion

Integration of ELT with IEE: A theoretically grounded framework for medical students’ entrepreneurial development

The integration of Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) with Innovation and Entrepreneurship Education (IEE) constructs an adapted theoretical framework for fostering medical students’ entrepreneurial and innovative capabilities. This study expands the application of ELT by tailoring it to the context of medical entrepreneurship, which fills a critical gap in the IEE literature: It addresses the shortage of theoretical analysis focusing on students’ learning experiences. While previous research has applied ELT to general entrepreneurship education [47, 48], our framework uniquely addresses the inherent tensions between evidence-based medical training and entrepreneurial skill development, thereby providing a more targeted and practical theoretical support for cultivating medical students’ innovative and entrepreneurial competencies.

This theoretical contribution is significant because medical students face unique epistemological challenges when transitioning between clinical reasoning (which prioritizes evidence, protocols, and risk minimization) and entrepreneurial thinking (which embraces uncertainty, ambiguity, experimentation, and calculated risk-taking). Our findings reveal that students with prior entrepreneurial exposure (31.4% of our sample) demonstrated enhanced resilience during initial uncertainty phases, while those without such background experienced profound disorientation.

Our adapted framework provides a structured approach to navigate these contradictions through four interconnected stages: Concrete Experience (CE), Reflective Observation (RO), Abstract Conceptualization (AC), and Active Experimentation (AE). This reconceptualization addresses a theoretical gap identified by Morris et al. [49], who argue that entrepreneurship education often lacks clear theoretical foundations specific to disciplinary contexts. Our model provides robust theoretical justification for how medical students develop entrepreneurial competencies while navigating the unique constraints and opportunities within healthcare systems.

How ELT facilitates innovation skills development in medical context

Our adapted framework provides a structured approach to navigate the dynamics between clinical and entrepreneurial thinking through the ELT four-stage cyclical model, connected by the transitional processes of reflect, synthesize, apply, and iterate (see Fig. 2).

The ELT learning circle embedded in IEE

(1) Reflect: Concrete Experience → Reflective Observation

Medical students’ entrepreneurial learning begins with encountering three unique forms of uncertainty: technical uncertainty (unfamiliarity with business concepts and innovation processes), market uncertainty (unclear understanding of healthcare market dynamics and value creation), and outcome uncertainty (difficulty reconciling clinician and entrepreneur roles). These uncertainties create disorientation that becomes the catalyst for deep learning, which posits that disorienting dilemmas or concrete experiences initiate the learning cycle. In this context, the ambiguity of entrepreneurship serves as a critical divergence from the structured certainty of clinical routines, compelling learners to confront and adapt to a new domain.

Unlike general entrepreneurship students who may approach innovation primarily through market opportunity recognition [50], medical students demonstrate systems-oriented reflection—their concerns extend beyond immediate project challenges to encompass broader healthcare system implications, regulatory frameworks, and ethical considerations. This finding extends ELT literature by demonstrating how disciplinary background fundamentally shapes the nature of concrete experiences and subsequent learning processes [46]. Specifically, this integrative epistemological frame, shaped by prior socialization in high-stakes clinical settings, underpins medical students’ systems-oriented thinking and influences how they engage with complexity, collaboration, and identity within healthcare environments [51, 52].

The transition to Reflective Observation is marked by students’ ability to transform initial discomfort into structured introspection. One student (N28, 21-year-old female, Physical Therapy, third-year undergraduate student) illustrated this transformation: “Initially, I was completely unaware of what entrepreneurship and innovation entailed, as well as how to draft a business plan. Through repeated presentations, I was able to reflect on the questions posed by teachers and peers, respond accordingly, and thereby enhance the project’s [Brain-Computer Interface Upper Limb Rehabilitation Robot] quality.” This reflection process diverges markedly from clinical reflection, which typically focuses on diagnostic accuracy or treatment outcomes. In entrepreneurial contexts, students must reflect on ambiguous problems with multiple valid solutions, which demonstrates an epistemological shift that challenges their evidence-based training. This aligns with Kolb’s notion of learning as a process where knowledge is created through the transformation of experience, particularly when learners are pushed to reinterpret familiar epistemological schemas [29, 53].

(2) Synthesize: Reflective Observation → Abstract Conceptualization

The synthesis phase represents the most challenging transition in the learning cycle, with our findings revealing substantial difficulty among medical students in transitioning from specific observational data to general entrepreneurial principles. This difficulty stems from the need to integrate two fundamentally different epistemological frameworks: medical knowledge (emphasizing evidence, standardization, and risk minimization) and entrepreneurial knowledge (emphasizing opportunity recognition, innovation, and calculated risk-taking). Kolb [29] underscores this as a shift from Reflective Observation to Abstract Conceptualization, wherein learners construct theoretical insights from practical reflection. However, as Boon and Van Baalen [54] notes, professional knowledge is often tacit and domain-specific, creating friction when learners confront cross-disciplinary integration.

A pediatric project leader (N29, 21-year-old male, Occupational Therapy, third-year undergraduate student) exemplified successful synthesis: “Our project [Eye-Tracking-Based Screening System for Children with Autism] faced significant setbacks due to data loss, almost leading us to give up while managing our rigorous academic workload. Under our mentor’s guidance, we persevered and devised an innovative approach for autism screening in children based on eye-tracking technology.” This case demonstrates how synthesis involves not just combining knowledge domains but transforming setbacks into opportunities for innovation, which demonstrates a process Politis [55] identifies as entrepreneurial learning through prior failure and reflection.

The challenge of synthesis is compounded by epistemological friction, the resistanceencountered when attempting to merge different ways of knowing [54]. Medical Medicalstudents must learn to value uncertainty and ambiguity as sources of opportunity rather than problems to be eliminated, fundamentally challenging their clinical training. As Billett [56] emphasizes, learning across boundaries requires the negotiation of meaning meaning between culturally and epistemologically distinct practices. This finding suggests that effective entrepreneurship education for medical students requires explicit attention to epistemological differences rather than assuming students can naturally integrate different knowledge systems.

(3) Apply: Abstract Conceptualization → Active Experimentation

The application phase proved challenging, with our findings revealing that medical students face unique difficulties in transitioning from conceptual models to active experimentation. This challenge reflects the unique demands of medical entrepreneurship, which requires simultaneous clinical and market validation—a dual imperative that significantly complicates implementation compared to general entrepreneurship education. In Kolb’s model, this shift corresponds to Active Experimentation, where learners test new ideas in the real world.

A doctoral student (N34, 30-year-old male, Rehabilitation Medicine [Integrated Chinese & Western Medicine], third-year Ph.D. candidate) described his approach to developing an upper limb rehabilitation robot: “Initially, we conducted clinical studies to identify established movement patterns. These were synthesized into a standardized training protocol, which we mechanized using modern technology. When initial market feedback highlighted cost barriers, we reengineered the system to prioritize affordability without compromising clinical efficacy.” This example illustrates how successful application requires continuous navigation between clinical requirements and market constraints, which can be seen as a complex optimization process that extends traditional ELT assumptions about linear progression from concept to application.

Furthermore, as Mezirow [33] explains in Transformative Learning Theory, application becomes a site of transformation when individuals reframe their assumptions through active problem-solving. The application phase also revealed the critical importance of institutional support. Students in programs with established industry partnerships demonstrated significantly higher success rates in transitioning to active experimentation.

(4) Iterate: Active Experimentation → Concrete Experience

Our findings suggest significant institutional and temporal constraints on iterative learning, with important implications for program design, as incomplete cycles may limit the development of entrepreneurial resilience and adaptive capacity. Students who successfully engaged in iteration described processes of continuous refinement based on multiple forms of feedback. One participant (N6) noted: “Upon discovering discrepancies between project [Wrist-Ankle Acupuncture Device for Dysmenorrhea Treatment] content and competition criteria, we engaged in deep reflection based on expert feedback and made every effort to correct these issues, often amending our business plans over a hundred times. A spirit of perseverance and a clear direction of effort are crucial for overcoming challenges in medical innovation.”

This iterative process in medical entrepreneurship differs significantly from other fields due to healthcare’s complex regulatory environment, ethical considerations, and extended validation timelines. According to Kolb & Kolb [46], experiential learning spaces must support recursive engagement, allowing learners to cycle repeatedly through experience, reflection, and adaptation. This process builds adaptive expertise [57] by encouraging learners to apply knowledge flexibly across varied contexts, which is crucial in domains like medicine and entrepreneurship, where knowledge must be continually reshaped to meet new challenges.

(5) Concrete Experience ↔ Abstract Conceptualization: Reflect to Synthesize

Kolb’s model extends beyond sequential stages to include two critical dimensions (i.e., prehension and transformation) that enrich the learning process in IEE contexts. The prehension dimension between Concrete Experience and Abstract Conceptualization underscores the process of reflecting on real-world experiences to synthesize theoretical insights. This dimension is particularly salient in IEE, as students grapple with unfamiliar challenges such as business model development, market feasibility analysis, and healthcare innovation. For instance, designing a novel healthcare device may compel students to develop a framework that integrates clinical efficacy with market sustainability.

Such integrative synthesis aligns with recursive process of professional learning anchored in the construction of knowledge through practice, enabling the conceptual transfer of capacities across domains. It ensures that theoretical constructs are not only grounded in experiential knowledge but also directly applicable to practical scenarios, fulfilling Kolb’s premise that learning is the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience.

(6) Active Experimentation ↔ Reflective Observation: Apply to Iterate

The transformation dimension between Active Experimentation and Reflective Observation highlights the iterative cycle of applying knowledge and refining strategies based on reflective feedback. This interplay allows students to critically evaluate the outcomes of their entrepreneurial efforts. A student (N18, 23-year-old male, Rehabilitation Medicine and Physiotherapy, first-year Master student) mentioned that when confronted with abstract issues, he tends to utilize AI tools to enhance situational awareness. By employing this method, he is able to assess the correctness of existing solutions. Ultimately, he continually iterates and refines his innovative proposals through practice. This dynamic exemplifies how Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, through recursive engagement with abstract conceptualization and active experimentation, generates novel epistemological frameworks essential for entrepreneurship, that is, transcending adaptive responses to create new possibilities.

Barriers to effective ELT integration in IEE

Institutional and resource constraints

Despite the benefits of IEE, medical students face several constraints that impact their entrepreneurial endeavors. These constraints disrupt different stages of the learning cycle, hampering the development of entrepreneurial competencies.

Venture capital and resource availability represent primary limitations that disproportionately affect the Concrete Experience and Active Experimentation phases. As noted by a student (N28, 21-year-old female, Physical Therapy, third-year undergraduate student), medical students often lack access to the technical resources and expertise needed to develop and implement innovative solutions: “Even with a brilliant medical concept, we couldn’t proceed past basic prototyping because specialized fabrication equipment wasn’t available. We had to modify our entire approach to work with available resources, which compromised the potential clinical impact of our solution.” This statement reveals how resource constraints fundamentally alter the learning experience—students learn to compromise innovation for feasibility rather than pursuing optimal solutions. This creates a bifurcated learning process where some students develop workarounds that enhance creative problem-solving, while others become frustrated and disengage from entrepreneurial activities altogether. Additionally, students are often forced to rely on external collaborators for technical skills and financial support, which underscores the need for better resource allocation and support structures within medical schools.

Technical capabilities, particularly expertise in computer technology and marketing, also pose significant challenges that interrupt the Abstract Conceptualization and Active Experimentation phases of ELT. As a participant (N2, 20-year-old male, Physical Therapy, third-year undergraduate student) described: “We needed motion tracking algorithms not available within our medical school. Without technical partners, we spent months trying to develop skills that engineering students acquire in their first year.” This gap between medical training and technical requirements makes students struggle to integrate technical components into their entrepreneurial frameworks. This extends Kolb’s theory by demonstrating how domain-specific knowledge gaps can hinder learning cycles.

Financial support and project guidance are critical factors that influence all phases of the ELT cycle in medical entrepreneurship education. “With sufficient funding, we could have conducted proper market validation studies rather than relying on convenience samples. Our learning was compromised because we couldn’t test assumptions with real stakeholders.” (N24, 21-year-old male, Physical Therapy, fourth-year undergraduate student) This quote illustrates how financial constraints limit not just implementation but the very ability to learn through Active Experimentation.

Market and Policy Environment factors also play a role in constraining entrepreneurial activities. Government policies, such as those related to COVID-19, and the overall suitability of the market for new ventures are significant challenges. In terms of Concrete Experience, students must navigate a complex landscape of regulations and market conditions. Reflective Observation in this context involves evaluating how these externalities limit the scope of their projects, often leading to a reconsideration of ideas or a scaling back of ambitious goals. As students move to Active Experimentation, regulatory and market constraints limit their ability to fully test ideas in a real-world context. Advocacy for more supportive policies and a favorable entrepreneurial climate could mitigate these constraints.

Implications for medical education reform

The integration of ELT into medical entrepreneurship education holds significant implications for broader medical education reform. As Kolb emphasized, effective learning requires not just experience, but the active transformation of that experience into concepts and action [29]. Our findings suggest that effective entrepreneurship education requires fundamental reconsideration of how medical students learn, that is, moving from knowledge acquisition to knowledge application and creation.

A key implication emerges from our analysis: effective entrepreneurship education requires faculty to serve as mentors and connectors rather than solely as content experts. One IEE supervisor reflected that teaching entrepreneurship to medical students prompted a fundamental shift in their teaching approach. Rather than acting as an authority figure, they assumed the role of a guide, supporting students as they navigated uncertainty. This aligns with the facilitative role of educators in experiential learning as emphasized by Kolb & Kolb [46].

Such reflections highlight how entrepreneurship education can catalyze broader pedagogical reform in medical education, promoting experiential approaches that may better equip students to manage the complexities and uncertainties of modern medical practice.

Chinese context and global relevance

In contrast to many other countries, IEE in China is notable for its expansive scale and deep integration into higher education. Within the medical domain, IEE has been elevated as a strategic priority, positioning students at the intersection of interdisciplinary innovation and professional development. These opportunities align closely with Kolb’s ELT model, offering a structured cycle of experience, reflection, conceptualization, and experimentation that enriches medical education.

By recognizing IEE as one of the five core competencies for medical professionals, Chinese institutions highlight the role of experiential learning in bridging the gap between theoretical instruction and practical application. However, the ambitious scale of China’s IEE initiative also introduces epistemological and logistical burdens, particularly when entrepreneurial content lacks direct clinical connection. Moreover, the vast number of participants in IEE programs can dilute the quality of mentorship and individualized guidance, both of which are essential for meaningful Reflective Observation and Abstract Conceptualization. Without structured reflection and mentorship, students may cycle through experiences without achieving deep learning.

To optimize the IEE experience, more discipline-specific programs are required, which bridge entrepreneurial learning with clinical realities. These should emphasize the Reflective Observation and Abstract Conceptualization stages of ELT, enabling students to develop nuanced understandings of how innovation principles apply in healthcare. Additionally, personalized mentorship is equally critical. ELT posits that transformation of experience into knowledge is greatly facilitated by skilled facilitation [46]. With dedicated mentorship, students can more confidently navigate innovation in healthcare and cultivate the integrative thinking essential for modern medical practice.

Globally, China’s experience offers valuable lessons. As healthcare systems grapple with rapid change and uncertainty, integrating IEE offers a promising strategy to prepare clinicians who are both technically proficient and adaptively innovative. China’s systematic approach, while not without challenges, exemplifies how national policy, curriculum integration, and experiential principles can be aligned to embed entrepreneurial thinking in professional training.

Conclusion

The integration of Experiential Learning Theory with Innovation and Entrepreneurship Education provides a robust framework for fostering entrepreneurial competencies among medical students, addressing the growing need for innovative thinking in healthcare delivery and medical practice. Our study makes three significant theoretical contributions: first, it extends ELT by identifying domain-specific adaptations required for effective entrepreneurship education in medical contexts; second, it establishes a modified ELT framework that emphasizes the transitional processes between learning stages; and third, it demonstrates how institutional and cultural contexts fundamentally shape experiential learning outcomes in entrepreneurship education. By engaging in our adapted ELT process—connecting Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active Experimentation through the processes of reflect, synthesize, apply, and iterate—students develop critical thinking, problem-solving, and adaptability essential for navigating healthcare innovation. However, the effectiveness of this learning cycle is mediated by several factors that merit consideration in program design and implementation.

Actionable recommendations for medical educators

To optimize the integration of Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory (ELT) into medical entrepreneurship education, several actionable strategies can be implemented. First, educators should create integrated clinical-entrepreneurial experiences by embedding innovation tasks directly into clinical rotations, such as process improvement or needs assessment; for example, surgical students could identify and prototype solutions to instrument handling inefficiencies. Second, structured reflection protocols should be developed and incorporated into existing coursework to guide students in connecting clinical observations with entrepreneurial opportunities. Third, interdisciplinary teaching teams comprising clinical and entrepreneurship faculty can co-develop and co-teach modules, ensuring both medical relevance and entrepreneurial rigor. Fourth, assessment methods must be designed to evaluate students’ progression through the ELT cycle, using rubrics that recognize diverse forms of entrepreneurial thinking beyond traditional business creation. Finally, ELT principles can be infused into problem-based learning (PBL) by modifying clinical cases to include entrepreneurial components, encouraging students to propose innovative solutions that improve healthcare delivery. Collectively, these strategies embed entrepreneurial thinking into core medical training, enhancing the relevance, depth, and impact of experiential learning in medical education.

Limitations and future research

Our study has several limitations that future research should address. First, while our qualitative approach provides rich insights into student experiences, it cannot measure the quantitative impact of ELT-based entrepreneurship education on specific innovation skills or career outcomes. Future research should combine qualitative insights with survey or experimental studies to measure the actual impact of IEE on students’ innovation capabilities and career trajectories.

Second, our focus on formal entrepreneurship programs may overlook valuable informal entrepreneurial learning. Future studies should examine how informal learning complements structured programs and how institutions can better support these organic learning processes.

Third, despite our attention to the Chinese context, cross-cultural comparative studies are needed to further illuminate how different educational systems and cultural contexts shape entrepreneurial learning in medicine. Such research would enhance our understanding of both universal principles and context-specific adaptations required for effective entrepreneurship education globally.

In conclusion, integrating ELT with medical entrepreneurship education offers promising pathways for developing the innovative capabilities needed in contemporary healthcare. By addressing the identified barriers and implementing structured approaches that respect the unique characteristics of medical education, institutions can foster graduates equipped to address complex healthcare challenges through entrepreneurial thinking and action. The continued refinement of these educational approaches, informed by rigorous research and attentive to both local contexts and global trends, will be essential for preparing medical professionals who can lead healthcare innovation in increasingly complex environments.
