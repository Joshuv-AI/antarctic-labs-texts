Adrenal Remodeling and Dysfunction Parallel Age‐Dependent Hypertension in Spontaneously Hypertensive Rats
Authors: Gaomin He, Tao Luo, Anping Zeng, Xinquan Wang, Yidan Wang, Yuhang Lv, Dan Wang, Jindong Wan, Sen Liu, Jixin Hou, Shuangtao Ma, Yi Yang, Peijian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe adrenal gland plays a pivotal role in blood pressure regulation through the secretion of corticosteroids and catecholamines. While its contribution to secondary hypertension is well recognized, its role in essential hypertension remains incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate whether structural and functional adrenal alterations precede or accompany the development of hypertension and to define the temporal relationship between adrenal remodeling and blood pressure elevation.MethodsWe systematically examined adrenal morphology, hormone secretion, enzyme expression, ultrastructure, and mitochondrial function in spontaneously hypertensive rats and normotensive Wistar–Kyoto controls across key developmental stages from ages 4 to 24 weeks.ResultsSpontaneously hypertensive rats developed progressive hypertension beginning at age 8 weeks, accompanied by disrupted circadian blood pressure rhythms. Notably, levels of aldosterone, corticosterone, and catecholamines were elevated in spontaneously hypertensive rats as early as 4 weeks, before overt hypertension. These hormonal changes were associated with zona glomerulosa thickening, increased Ki‐67–positive proliferative activity, and upregulation of steroidogenic and catecholaminergic enzymes (aldosterone synthase, 11β‐hydroxylase, and tyrosine hydroxylase). Ultrastructural remodeling included dense‐core granule accumulation, smooth endoplasmic reticulum expansion, mitochondrial swelling, and cristae disruption. Functionally, adrenal ATP production and mitochondrial membrane potential were reduced, while oxidative stress intensified, as shown by decreased superoxide dismutase and glutathione and increased malondialdehyde levels.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that adrenal hyperfunction and structural remodeling preceded and contributed to the onset and progression of hypertension in spontaneously hypertensive rats. This study highlights the adrenal gland as a dynamic and early modulator in essential hypertension and a promising target for therapeutic intervention.Keywords: adrenal gland, aging, mitochondria, oxidative stress, spontaneously hypertensive ratSubject Categories: Hypertension

Hypertension is one of the most prevalent and persistent chronic conditions worldwide and remains a leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and renal diseases. 1 In China alone, ≈27.9% of adults aged >18 years are affected, totaling >245 million individuals. 2 Despite the widespread use of antihypertensive therapies, the biological mechanisms driving the initiation and progression of essential hypertension remain incompletely understood. The pathogenesis of hypertension is multifactorial, involving heightened sympathetic nervous system activity, activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, oxidative stress, and abnormalities in adrenal hormone secretion. 3 , 4 The central role of the adrenal gland in blood pressure regulation is well established; however, its role in essential hypertension remains underinvestigated. 5 , 6

In addition to adrenal‐derived hormones, abnormalities in central sympathetic regulation, ganglionic neurotransmission, and enhanced peripheral adrenergic vasoconstriction are well‐established contributors to the pathogenesis of genetic hypertension. Notably, catecholamines released from sympathetic nerve terminals exert a dominant influence on vascular tone and cardiac output, often exceeding the effects of circulating catecholamines. These neural and vascular mechanisms provide critical context for interpreting adrenal alterations and suggest that adrenal dysfunction likely interacts with, rather than replaces, other key pathways in hypertension.

Traditionally, adrenal dysfunction has been primarily associated with secondary forms of hypertension, such as primary aldosteronism or pheochromocytoma. However, recent population‐based studies reveal that dysregulated adrenal hormone secretion—including elevated aldosterone and catecholamines—is also commonly observed in essential hypertension, implying a broader physiological relevance. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Aldosterone, secreted by the zona glomerulosa, serves as the final effector of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system cascade and plays a vital role in fluid and electrolyte balance. 11 In addition, glucocorticoids and adrenal medullary catecholamines are potent modulators of vascular tone and cardiac output. 12 , 13

Our prior observations in spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHRs) revealed marked elevations in adrenal hormones, including aldosterone, corticosterone, and catecholamines, compared with normotensive Wistar–Kyoto (WKY) controls. 14 However, the temporal dynamics and structural underpinnings of adrenal dysregulation throughout the course of hypertension development remain poorly characterized. The SHR model, widely recognized as a representative animal model of essential hypertension, exhibits a well‐defined progression of blood pressure elevation: normal levels at age 4 weeks, rapid increases from age 8 to 12 weeks, and a stable hypertensive state beyond age 12 weeks. 15 , 16 This time‐dependent trajectory offers a valuable opportunity to dissect the functional and structural remodeling of the adrenal gland during the transition from normotension to sustained hypertension. In this study, we aimed to systematically investigate spatiotemporal changes in adrenal hormone production, enzyme expression, cellular morphology, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress in SHRs compared with age‐matched WKY rats. By integrating endocrine, histological, and ultrastructural analyses across key developmental time points (4–24 weeks), we sought to elucidate the role of adrenal remodeling in the pathogenesis of age‐dependent hypertension.

Methods

Data Availability

The data underlying this article are available in the article. All the data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Animals

Male SHRs (n=60) and normotensive WKY rats (n=60), aged 3 weeks, were obtained from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License No. SCXK [Beijing] 2020‐0004). All animals were housed in a specific pathogen‐free facility at Chengdu Medical College under controlled environmental conditions (12‐hour light/dark cycle, constant temperature and humidity) with ad libitum access to standard chow (purchased from Chengdu Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. Chengdu, China; No. SCXK [Chuan] 2019‐028) and water. At ages 4, 8, 12, 16, and 24 weeks, 6 rats from each group were randomly selected for terminal analysis. Following blood pressure measurement and blood sampling, animals were euthanized by overdose of inhaled isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation, and adrenal glands were harvested for subsequent histological, biochemical, and molecular assessments.

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes and the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chengdu Medical College (Approval No. [2024] 044).

Blood Pressure Measurement

Systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) were measured weekly in conscious rats using a noninvasive tail‐cuff system (BP‐2010, Softron Biotechnology, Beijing, China), as previously described. 17 For continuous 24‐hour blood pressure monitoring, telemetry was performed using the HD‐S10 system (Data Sciences International, St. Paul, MN). Briefly, under general anesthesia with inhaled isoflurane, using 5% for induction and 1% to 2% for maintenance, a catheter (TAIJI; RWD, Shenzhen, China) was surgically implanted into the abdominal aorta. 18 , 19 Following a 1‐week recovery period, blood pressure and heart rate were recorded continuously at a sampling rate of 500 Hz. Data were analyzed in 60‐second segments, with average systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, MAP, and heart rate values calculated every 10 minutes to assess circadian fluctuations.

Biochemical Measurements

Plasma and adrenal tissue homogenates were prepared as previously described. 20 Hormone concentrations, including aldosterone, corticosterone, epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine, testosterone, renin, and angiotensin II, were quantified using commercial ELISA kits. Adrenal homogenates were separately analyzed for cortical hormones (aldosterone, corticosterone, testosterone) and medullary catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine). Oxidative stress parameters, including ATP content, superoxide dismutase activity, glutathione levels, and malondialdehyde concentrations, were assessed using colorimetric detection kits. Absorbance readings were obtained using a Cmax Plus microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) following manufacturer‐recommended wavelength settings. The reagents used included aldosterone (ab136933) and corticosterone (ab323743) ELISA kits from Abcam (Waltham, MA); catecholamine (MM‐0350R1, MM‐0556R1, MM‐0211R1, MM‐0577R1) and testosterone ELISA kits from Meimian Industrial (Jiangsu, China); renin ELISA kit (ERA52RB) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA); dopamine ELISA kit (RE59161) from IBL International (Hamburg, Germany); BCA protein assay kit (AS1086) from Aspen Biotechnology (Wuhan, China); superoxide dismutase (A001‐3), glutathione (A006‐2), ATP (A095‐1), and malondialdehyde (A003‐1) assay kits from Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China); JC‐1 mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (ICT944) from Bio‐Rad Laboratories (Hercules, CA); Masson's trichrome staining kit (1.00485) from MilliporeSigma (Burlington, MA); and DAPI (ab104139) from Abcam.

Adrenal Tissue Metabolomics Analysis

Targeted metabolomic profiling of adrenal tissues was performed in SHRs at ages 4, 8, and 24 weeks. Metabolites were extracted from freshly isolated adrenal glands and analyzed using ultra‐performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry. Data preprocessing included peak identification, alignment, and normalization. Differential metabolites were identified on the basis of variable importance in projection >1, fold change ≥2 or ≤0.5, and P<0.05 (Student's t test). Hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted to visualize dynamic changes in metabolite abundance (rows: metabolites; columns: samples; red=high abundance, blue=low abundance). Volcano plots were generated to depict significantly altered metabolites between groups, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify significantly impacted metabolic pathways.

Histology

Adrenal glands were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Serial sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin to evaluate overall adrenal architecture and with Masson's trichrome (MilliporeSigma) to assess interstitial fibrosis. Stained slides were imaged using a digital microscope system (VS200, OLYMPUS, Beijing, China). Quantification of fibrotic area was performed using ImageJ FIJI software, with results expressed as the percentage of fibrosis relative to the total adrenal area.

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin‐embedded adrenal sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by incubation with a rabbit anti–Ki‐67 primary antibody or an isotype‐matched negative control for 2 hours at room temperature. After PBS washes, sections were incubated with an HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit secondary antibody. Hematoxylin was used as a nuclear counterstain. Whole‐slide imaging was performed using a digital scanner (Olympus VS200), and ImageJ FIJI software was used to quantify Ki‐67 expression by calculating the integrated optical density of positively stained nuclei. 21

Immunofluorescence Staining

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed on paraffin‐embedded adrenal sections using the Opal tyramide signal amplification system (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). Following deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: tyrosine hydroxylase (1:400, PA1‐4679; ThermoFisher Scientific), CYP11B2 (aldosterone synthase; 1:100, abs120434; Absin), and CYP11B1 (11β‐hydroxylase; 1:100, sc‐377 248; Santa Cruz Biotechnology). After washing, HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, followed by sequential labeling with Opal fluorophores (520, 570, and 620 nm). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Slides were scanned using a whole‐slide fluorescence imaging system (Olympus VS200), and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ FIJI software.

Western Blotting

Adrenal tissue proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors and quantified using the BCA protein assay kit (AS1086; Aspen Biotechnology). Equal amounts of total protein were separated on 10% SDS‐PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk in tris‐buffered saline and Polysorbate 20 for 1 hour at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: tyrosine hydroxylase (1:1000, PA1‐4679; ThermoFisher Scientific), CYP11B2 (1:500, abs120434; Absin), CYP11B1 (1:1000, sc‐377 248; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX), and β‐actin (1:1000, ab8226; Abcam) as the internal loading control. Following incubation with species‐appropriate HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ FIJI software and normalized to β‐actin. All experiments were independently repeated 3 times to ensure reproducibility.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Adrenal tissue samples were processed for transmission electron microscopy as previously described. 22 Briefly, adrenal glands were sectioned into 2‐ to 3‐mm3 blocks and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde followed by 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C. After dehydration through a graded acetone series, tissues were embedded in epoxy resin. Semithin sections stained with toluidine blue were used to identify cortical regions of interest. Ultrathin sections (60–90 nm) were prepared, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined using a JEM‐1400FLASH transmission electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at magnifications ranging from 1000× to 30 000×. Mitochondrial morphology and dense‐core chromaffin granules were analyzed, and granule density was quantified using ImageJ FIJI software.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assessment

Mitochondrial membrane potential in adrenal tissues was assessed using the JC‐1 assay kit (ICT944, Bio‐Rad Laboratories), which detects potential‐dependent accumulation of JC‐1 dye within mitochondria. Upon membrane polarization, JC‐1 forms red fluorescent aggregates; in depolarized mitochondria, it remains as green fluorescent monomers. Fresh adrenal tissue sections were incubated with JC‐1 dye (10 μmol/L) for 30 minutes at 37 °C, followed by 3 PBS washes. Fluorescent signals were immediately captured using a fluorescence microscope, and the red/green fluorescence intensity ratio was calculated using ImageJ FIJI to quantify mitochondrial membrane potential.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean±SEM. Comparisons between 2 groups were made using the unpaired Student's t test. For comparisons involving multiple groups and time points, 2‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used. For untargeted metabolomics, metabolite intensities were log‐transformed and compared between groups using Student's t test. To account for multiple testing, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied to evaluate the overall false discovery burden. Differential metabolites were identified on the basis of a combination of unadjusted P value, fold change, and biological relevance, with false discovery rate used to assess result robustness rather than as a strict exclusion criterion. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, and pathways with false discovery rate–corrected P values <0.05 were considered significantly enriched. 23 A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Progressive Blood Pressure Elevation and Circadian Disruption in SHRs

Longitudinal blood pressure monitoring demonstrated that SHRs developed hypertension in an age‐dependent manner. While no significant systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, or MAP were observed at 4 weeks compared with WKY controls, SHRs began exhibiting significantly elevated blood pressures from 8 weeks onward. This hypertensive trend progressively intensified through 24 weeks (Figure 1A through 1C). Complementary telemetry recordings revealed intact circadian oscillations of MAP in both groups at 4 weeks; however, SHRs exhibited a marked elevation and flattening of the diurnal rhythm from 8 to 24 weeks, consistent with disrupted autonomic regulation (Figure 1D through 1H). Body weight increased steadily with age in both SHR and WKY rats (Table1). SHRs consistently exhibited slightly lower body weights compared with age‐matched WKY rats; however, the differences were not statistically significant at any time point (4–24 weeks). These findings indicate that the blood pressure elevations in SHRs were not attributable to differences in body mass or obesity‐related effects.

Adrenal‐Associated Hormone Profiles in SHRs Exhibit Early and Sustained Dysregulation

Plasma hormone profiling revealed early and progressive endocrine alterations in SHRs (Figure 2). Norepinephrine and renin were already elevated at 4 weeks compared with WKY rats (Figure 2D and 2G). From 8 weeks onward, SHRs showed significant increases in aldosterone, corticosterone, epinephrine, dopamine, and testosterone, with levels remaining elevated through 24 weeks (Figure 2A through 2F). Angiotensin II also increased progressively with age in SHRs (Figure 2H). WKY rats displayed only mild age‐related fluctuations. These results indicate that early norepinephrine and renin activation precede broader adrenal hormonal upregulation, which becomes prominent after 8 weeks.

Structural Remodeling and Increased Proliferation in the Adrenal Cortex of SHRs

Histological analysis revealed progressive remodeling of the adrenal cortex in SHRs. Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed an age‐dependent thickening of the zona glomerulosa in both SHRs and WKY rats, with consistently greater zona glomerulosa thickness observed in SHRs across all time points (Figure 5A). Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated that interstitial fibrosis increased with age in both strains; however, there were no significant differences in fibrotic area between SHRs and WKY rats (Figure 5B and 5D). Ki‐67 immunohistochemistry identified the zona glomerulosa as the primary proliferative zone. SHRs exhibited significantly elevated Ki‐67 expression at 4 and 8 weeks compared with WKY controls, indicating enhanced cellular proliferation during early stages of hypertension. From 12 weeks onward, Ki‐67 expression remained stable and comparable between the 2 groups (Figure 5C and 5E). These findings suggest that adrenal cortical remodeling in SHRs is characterized by early proliferative activation and persistent zona glomerulosa hypertrophy, potentially contributing to sustained hormonal overproduction during the progression of hypertension.

Enhanced Expression of Steroidogenic and Catecholaminergic Enzymes in SHR Adrenal Tissue

Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated marked age‐dependent upregulation of key biosynthetic enzymes in SHRs. CYP11B2, localized to the zona glomerulosa, and CYP11B1, expressed in the zona fasciculata, both showed increased signal intensity beginning at 8 weeks (Figure 6A through 6C). Tyrosine hydroxylase, confined to the adrenal medulla, was similarly upregulated (Figure 6D). These findings were corroborated by Western blotting, which showed progressively elevated expression of CYP11B2, CYP11B1, and tyrosine hydroxylase in SHR adrenal tissue compared with WKY rats (Figure 7A through 7D), confirming enhanced biosynthetic capacity for corticosteroids and catecholamines during hypertension progression. Heatmap analysis of CYP11B1‐related metabolites revealed progressive increases in corticosterone, 21‐deoxycorticosterone, cortisone, aldosterone, and 17α‐hydroxyprogesterone with advancing age in SHRs (Figure 7E), indicating enhanced steroidogenic activity.

Ultrastructural Alterations and Bioenergetic Dysfunction in SHR Adrenal Glands

Transmission electron microscopy revealed pronounced ultrastructural remodeling in SHRs compared with WKY rats. In zona glomerulosa, SHRs exhibited a progressive increase in smooth endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial swelling from 8 weeks onward (Figure 8A, 8D, and 8E). Zona fasciculata regions in SHRs showed greater lipid droplet accumulation over time (Figure 8B, 8F, and 8G). In the medulla, SHR chromaffin cells displayed a significant increase in dense‐core granule density (Figure 8C and 8I), while granule size remained unchanged (Figure 8H).

JC‐1 staining revealed a significant reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential across all SHR time points compared with WKY controls (Figure 9A and 9B). Functionally, SHRs exhibited reduced adrenal ATP production beginning at 8 weeks (Figure 9C), indicating bioenergetic impairment, which was likely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction. Consistent with mitochondrial dysregulation, adrenal tissues from SHRs demonstrated a progressive increase in oxidative stress markers. Antioxidant enzyme activities, including superoxide dismutase and glutathione, were significantly decreased in SHRs from 8 weeks onward (Figure 9D and 9E), while malondialdehyde, a lipid peroxidation marker, was significantly elevated with age (Figure 9F). These findings indicate a disruption of mitochondrial and redox homeostasis in the SHR adrenal gland that is associated with adrenal hyperactivity and excessive hormone production.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that functional and structural remodeling of the adrenal gland precedes and parallels the development of age‐dependent hypertension in SHRs. By systematically analyzing endocrine, histological, ultrastructural, and mitochondrial parameters across defined developmental stages, we demonstrate that adrenal dysregulation is not merely a consequence of elevated blood pressure but may serve as a critical initiator and amplifier of hypertensive pathophysiology.

Our findings demonstrate that SHRs exhibit elevated adrenal cortical and medullary hormone production, including aldosterone, corticosterone, and catecholamines, as early as age 4 to 8 weeks, preceding significant increases in systolic and MAP. Importantly, the strong concordance between noninvasive tail‐cuff measurements and continuous telemetric recordings across developmental stages supports the robustness of the observed blood pressure trajectory and circadian alterations. Although SHRs tended to have slightly lower body weight than WKY rats across ages, these differences were not statistically significant and are consistent with reported strain‐specific growth patterns. 24 Metabolomic profiling further reveals a progressive, stage‐dependent reprogramming of adrenal biochemical activity during hypertension development. At 8 weeks, early enrichment of steroid hormone biosynthesis and aldosterone‐regulated sodium reabsorption pathways indicates that metabolic remodeling occurs before the establishment of sustained hypertension. By 24 weeks, the adrenal metabolic landscape is dominated by enhanced steroidogenesis and catecholamine synthesis, in parallel with morphological, ultrastructural, and biochemical evidence of adrenal hyperfunction. The concurrent enrichment of adrenergic signaling and neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction pathways at later stages underscores the integrated role of adrenal metabolism in amplifying sympathetic drive. Collectively, these results support the concept that adrenal metabolic remodeling is not merely a consequence of elevated blood pressure but an active, dynamic process that emerges in the preclinical phase and contributes to the pathogenesis of age‐dependent hypertension in SHRs. This is consistent with previous reports suggesting that adrenal overactivity may serve as an initiating rather than solely downstream mechanism in essential hypertension. 25 , 26 The progressive overproduction of hormones in both plasma and adrenal tissue reinforces the presence of an intrinsic adrenal phenotype in SHRs that predisposes them to a hypertensive state. Consistent with the early increase in cardiac output described by Smith and Hutchins in prehypertensive SHRs, 27 the early rise in adrenal norepinephrine and renin observed in our study suggests that adrenal‐driven sympathetic and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system activation may contribute to these initial hemodynamic alterations preceding overt hypertension.

Histological and immunohistochemical analyses revealed that SHRs exhibit early and sustained thickening of the zona glomerulosa, increased Ki‐67 expression indicating active cell proliferation, and upregulation of key steroidogenic and catecholamine biosynthesis enzymes (CYP11B2, CYP11B1, and tyrosine hydroxylase). These alterations were not observed in age‐matched WKY rats, underscoring the specificity of adrenal remodeling in the SHR model. Such findings support the hypothesis that adrenal hyperplasia and enzymatic upregulation facilitate the sustained overproduction of aldosterone and catecholamines, thereby contributing to persistent vasoconstriction, fluid retention, and blood pressure elevation. This phenotype is further supported by ultrastructural studies. In line with the classic work of Nickerson et al, 28 , 29 we observed increased smooth endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial remodeling in the SHR adrenal cortex, alongside an age‐dependent accumulation of dense‐core granules in medullary chromaffin cells. These features are indicative of enhanced secretory activity and biosynthetic demand. Our data extend these findings by demonstrating that such morphological changes are accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, implicating bioenergetic failure as a compounding factor in adrenal pathology.

A novel aspect of this study is the identification of mitochondrial impairment in SHR adrenal glands. JC‐1 fluorescence imaging showed a significant and progressive decline in mitochondrial membrane potential, while biochemical assays revealed decreased ATP levels and antioxidant capacity (superoxide dismutase, glutathione) alongside increased lipid peroxidation (malondialdehyde). These findings suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are central to adrenal aging and hyperactivity in hypertension. Mitochondrial health is essential for steroidogenesis and catecholamine synthesis, and our data support a model in which compromised mitochondrial function may exacerbate hormonal dysregulation and tissue remodeling.

The underlying drivers of this adrenal phenotype in SHRs are likely multifactorial. Previous transcriptomic studies have shown that SHRs possess a dysregulated adrenal circadian clock, including phase‐advanced expression of core clock genes and aberrant overexpression of E4 promoter‐binding protein 4, a repressor of steroidogenic transcription. 30 Recent studies demonstrate that adrenal brain and muscle ARNT‐like protein 1 is essential for normal aldosterone synthesis and diurnal blood pressure variation. 31 In parallel, aldosterone's renal effector serum‐ and glucocorticoid‐induced kinase 1 has been shown to exhibit clock‐controlled expression that shapes the circadian rhythm of sodium handling and blood pressure. 32 These molecular disturbances may contribute to both hormonal hypersecretion and disrupted diurnal blood pressure patterns, which we observed from 8 weeks onward. Our prior work further supports this causative relationship: Partial ablation of adrenal tissue in SHRs via ethanol injection led to significant reductions in blood pressure, confirming the adrenal gland's central role in disease initiation. 14

Although mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress were observed in SHRs, these changes likely represent secondary, maladaptive consequences of prolonged adrenal stimulation rather than a limiting defect in early hormone production. Adrenal hyperactivity in SHRs emerges at 4 to 8 weeks, when mitochondrial ultrastructure is still largely intact, indicating that increased hormone synthesis initially occurs under relatively preserved bioenergetic conditions. With sustained sympathetic and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system activation, chronic steroidogenic and catecholaminergic demand imposes a high metabolic load, leading to reactive oxygen species accumulation, mitochondrial swelling, and partial loss of membrane potential observed at later ages. Importantly, adrenal steroidogenesis can be maintained even under reduced oxidative phosphorylation because adrenal cells can compensate through glycolysis and alternative metabolic substrates, allowing hormone production to persist despite emerging mitochondrial impairment. Thus, mitochondrial dysfunction develops after and as a consequence of adrenal hyperactivity, rather than contradicting it. The emergence of adrenal structural remodeling between 4 and 8 weeks also parallels the onset of sexual maturity in rats, suggesting that sex hormones may influence early adrenal phenotypes. Prior studies have shown that androgens and estrogens can modulate adrenal cortical growth, steroidogenic enzyme expression, and catecholamine biosynthesis. 33 , 34 Therefore, shifts in sex hormone levels during puberty may interact with the genetic background of SHRs to amplify adrenal activation during this critical developmental window. These findings highlight the need for future work assessing sex‐specific adrenal maturation and hormone interactions in hypertension progression.

The translational relevance of these findings is underscored by recent human studies reporting abnormal aldosterone and catecholamine profiles in patients with essential hypertension, even in the absence of adrenal tumors or classical hyperaldosteronism. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Our work provides a mechanistic foundation for these clinical observations and suggests that adrenal‐targeted strategies, whether pharmacologic, genomic, or chronobiological, may offer therapeutic benefit in early‐stage or treatment‐resistant hypertension.

In summary, our study provides comprehensive evidence that adrenal gland remodeling in SHRs precedes and parallels the development of hypertension. Through a combination of endocrine, structural, and mitochondrial alterations, the adrenal gland emerges as a pivotal organ in the pathogenesis of age‐related hypertension. These findings call for renewed attention to adrenal physiology in essential hypertension and lay the groundwork for adrenal‐centered preventive and therapeutic interventions.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not evaluate developmental time points earlier than 4 weeks or beyond 24 weeks, potentially missing critical windows of adrenal programming and long‐term remodeling. Second, while our analyses distinguished cortical and medullary zones, deeper spatial resolution (eg, single‐cell RNA sequencing or laser capture microdissection) is needed to resolve region‐ and cell‐type–specific transcriptional changes. Third, while we observed altered circadian patterns of MAP, we did not directly measure time‐of‐day–dependent hormone fluctuations. Future studies incorporating circadian sampling and adrenal‐specific gene manipulation are warranted to further clarify temporal mechanisms and therapeutic targets. Fourth, this study was conducted exclusively in male rats; therefore, future studies are needed to determine whether comparable adrenal remodeling occurs in females during hypertension development.
