A binuclear metallohydrolase model for RelA/SpoT-Homolog (RSH) hydrolases
Authors: Rich W. Zhou, Berti Manisa, Boyuan Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
When challenged by starvation, bacterial organisms synthesize guanosine pentaphosphate and tetraphosphate, collectively denoted as (p)ppGpp, as second messengers to reprogram metabolism toward slower growth and enhanced stress tolerance. When starvation is alleviated, the RelA-SpoT Homolog (RSH) hydrolases downregulate (p)ppGpp, cleaving the 3′-diphosphate to produce GTP or GDP. Metazoan RSH hydrolases possess phosphatase activity responsible for converting cytoplasmic NADPH to NADH in mammalian cells. Inhibitor development for this family may therefore provide therapies to combat bacterial infection or metabolic dysregulation. Despite the availability of dozens of high-resolution structures, catalytic mechanisms of RSH hydrolases have remained poorly understood. All RSH hydrolases tightly bind a Mn2+ near its active center, which is believed sufficient for hydrolase activity. In contrast to this notion, we demonstrate, using the (p)ppGpp hydrolase SpoT from Acinetobacter baumannii, that a second divalent cation, presumably a Mg2+ under physiological conditions, is required for efficient catalysis. We also show that SpoT preferentially cleaves 3′-diphosphate over 3′-phosphate substrates, likely due to a key coordination between the β-phosphate and the second metal center. Metazoan RSH hydrolase replaces this β-phosphate with the side chain of an aspartate residue, thereby functioning as a phosphatase. We propose a binuclear metallohydrolase model where an invariant ED (Glu-Asp) diad, previously believed to activate the water nucleophile, instead coordinates to a Mg2+ center. The refined molecular and evolutionary blueprint of RSH hydrolases will provide a more reliable foundation for the development of small-molecule inhibitors of this important enzyme family.

Guanosine pentaphosphate or tetraphosphate, collectively denoted as (p)ppGpp, are crucial bacterial second messengers. Accumulation of (p)ppGpp slows growth and drives antibiotic persistence in diverse pathogenic organisms (1, 2, 3, 4). Enzymes involved in metabolizing (p)ppGpp are referred to as RelA/SpoT Homolog (RSH) proteins (5). First discovered in Escherichia coli, RelA and SpoT are two paralogous proteins that, when stimulated by starvation, synthesize pppGpp and ppGpp by transferring the β and γ phosphates from ATP to GTP or GDP (Fig. 1A, top scheme). (6, 7). SpoT also possesses a hydrolase domain, which cleaves the 3′-diphosphate from (p)ppGpp, converting them back to GTP or GDP once starvation is resolved. RelA’s hydrolase domain is catalytically inactive, making SpoT an essential protein for its unique ability to downregulate the growth-inhibiting (p)ppGpp. In addition to long, bifunctional RSH proteins like SpoT, single-domain RSH synthetases and hydrolases are found widespread across all domains of life. Metazoans, which lack synthetase, possess single-domain hydrolases known as MESH (metazoan RSH hydrolase). MESH from Drosophila melanogaster and Homo sapiens both hydrolyze ppGpp despite the apparent absence of ppGpp in metazoans (8). The human Mesh1 also acts as a phosphatase, hydrolyzing NADPH to NADH and preventing ferroptosis (Fig. 1A, bottom scheme) (9). Despite the availability of dozens of atomic-resolution structures (8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15), two prevailing ideas about RSH hydrolase catalytic mechanisms contradict recent experimental data. First, all RSH hydrolases are devised of four invariant residues, two histidines and two aspartates, hereafter referred to as the 2H2D motif, to accommodate a Mn2+ (Fig. 1, B and C) (14). Since buffers containing Mn2+ as the only divalent metal cation can support RSH hydrolase activity, while Mg2+-only buffers sometimes cannot (8, 14), this Mn2+ center was therefore thought sufficient for catalysis (10), and the possibility of a second divalent metal cation center has never been considered. Although the tetradentate 2H2D site is expected to bind Mn2+ with sub-μM affinity (given the 20-nM KD of nitrilotriacetate–Mn2+ complex) (16), several recent studies show that RSH hydrolases are activated by Mn2+ at much higher concentrations, with EC50 on the order of 100 μM to 1 mM (13, 17). These data suggest additional roles of divalent cations in RSH hydrolase activity. The other problematic idea involves the invariant Glu-Asp (ED) diad near the Mn2+ center (Fig. 1D in green). In 2004, Hogg et al. proposed that the ED diad activates the water nucleophile to attack the 3′-α-phosphate of ppGpp (10). Due to the constraints of the nucleophilic substitution at phosphorus (SN2(P)) mechanism (18), the water nucleophile must attack from the backside of the leaving 3′-oxygen. The recently published ppGpp-bound RSH hydrolase structures, nevertheless, show that ppGpp blocks the ED diad from accessing the ideal attacking position (Fig. 1D, dashed circle) (12, 13). Thus, the function of the ED diad and activation mechanism of the water nucleophile should be revisited. In this work, we interrogated roles of divalent metals in RSH hydrolase activity, which leads to a revised catalytic model involving a second divalent metal cation center that resolves the above-mentioned discrepancies.

Results

To clarify the requirements of divalent metal cations for ppGpp hydrolysis, we chose SpoTAb from Acinetobacter baumannii as our model RSH hydrolase for its excellent stability in solution and the availability of a ppGpp-bound crystal structure (13). ppGpp hydrolysis was monitored in real time using pyruvate kinase (PK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) to couple the production of GDP to NADH consumption, which is then detectable by the decrease of absorbance at 340 nm (Fig. S1A). We first determined Km = 34.4 μM for ppGpp (Fig. S1B) and used 500 μM ppGpp in all subsequent assays to saturate the active site. Purified recombinant SpoTAb has poor activity unless millimolar levels of Mn2+ or Mg2+ were supplemented (Fig. 2A). SpoTAb reaches half-maximal activity at EC50 = 1.76 mM free Mg2+ or EC50 = 530 μM free Mn2+ (Fig. 2A). Importantly, Mg2+ and Mn2+ bind ppGpp at 1:1 stoichiometry with 125 μM and 5.5 μM KD, respectively (Fig. S2), and their complex with ppGpp were excluded from free cation concentration calculation. In addition to this low-affinity cation requirement of Mn2+ or Mg2+, SpoTAb was also reported to specifically require Mn2+ despite the presence of excess Mg2+ 13. We readily reproduced this result: SpoTAb fully activated by 10 mM MgCl2 is inhibited by 86% upon addition of 1 mM EDTA (Fig. 2B). EDTA has five orders of magnitude higher affinity for Mn2+ than Mg2+, thus effectively displacing Mn2+` despite the presence of excess free Mg2+. Metal analysis using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry revealed that despite the necessity for Mn2+, recombinant SpoTAb does not significantly copurify with Mn2⁺, with only 3.4% of SpoTAb being metalated in the protein stock (Fig. S3A). To identify the source of Mn2+ that activates SpoTAb in Mg2+-only buffers (Fig. 2B, left bar), we utilized Mn2⁺-adsorbing Chelex-100 resin. By conducting reactions that omitted all enzyme-coupling components, we discovered that the reaction buffer and ppGpp substrate contained sufficient Mn2⁺ to substantially metalate SpoTAb. Pretreatment of the buffer and ppGpp with Chelex-100 significantly reduced hydrolase activity (Fig. S3, B and C). Addition of 1 μM MnCl2 activated SpoTAb hydrolase to saturation, suggesting a strong affinity for Mn2+ with a KD in the nM range or lower (Fig. S3, B and C). Together, these data indicate that SpoTAb–ppGpp complex contains two distinct metal-binding sites necessary for efficient substrate hydrolysis: a high-affinity, 2H2D-bound M1 site for Mn2+ (Fig. 2C, black circle) and a previously unreported, low-affinity M2 site accepting Mg2+ or Mn2+ (Fig. 2C, gray circle).Figure 2SpoTAbactivity requires divalent-cation binding at two distinct sites.A and B, SpoTAb activity (A) as a function of free Mg2+ or Mn2+ concentration and (B) in the presence of 10 mM MgCl2 with or without 1 mM EDTA. To determine the affinity of Mn2+ binding at M1 site, we harnessed the Mn2+-for-Mg2+ substitution equilibrium of EDTA complexes to control free [Mn2+] at low levels resembling conjugate acid-base pair controlling pH. We assembled hydrolysis reactions for SpoTAb in the presence of 5 mM MgSO4, 1 mM EDTA, and MnCl2 varying between 50 and 900 μM such that [Mn2+] ranged from 1.5 to 820 nM. Procedures to estimate free [Mn2+] are described in Experimental Methods. Because all reactions contain abundant Mg2+, hydrolase activity should scale with Mn2+ occupancy at M1 site. We therefore plotted hydrolase activity with estimated free [Mn2+] and fitted the isotherm to Hill’s equation, which provides an apparent KD = 39 nM and a Hill coefficient = 1.0, supporting the Mn2+ binding at the 2H2D site revealed in crystal structures (Fig. 2D). Intriguingly, increasing free Mg2+ concentration from 4 mM concentration to 29 mM in these reactions increased the apparent KD for Mn2+ to 116 nM (Fig. 2D). We believe this increase is due to direct competition between Mg2+ and Mn2+ for the M1 site and thus calculated the KD = 8.6 mM for Mg2+ and 29 nM for Mn2+, respectively (See Supporting Experimental Methods). To measure SpoTAb activity when Mg2+ occupies both M1 and M2, we titrated ppGpp hydrolysis reactions containing 1 mM EDTA with Mg2+ (Fig. 2E). Using KD, M2 = 1.76 mM (EC50 of Mg2+ for M2 site, Fig. 2A) and KD, M1 = 8.6 mM (calculated KD of Mg2+ for M1), our data fitted robustly to a model wherein Mg2+ bound to both sites is required for activity (Fig. 2E). The poor turnover number indicates that Mg2+ binding at M1 site is not favorable for catalysis. Taken together, our data strongly suggest that SpoTAb is a binuclear metallohydrolase (See Fig. 2F for a summary of SpoTAb’s metal-binding properties).

Where is the M2 site located in the SpoTAb–ppGpp complex? All RSH hydrolases belong to a clade of HD-domain superfamily. These phosphatases all feature a 2H2D motif that typically binds to a metal in a mononuclear configuration (19). A notable exception is the binuclear YqeK from Streptococcus pyogenes, a non-RSH member of this subfamily that hydrolyzes diadenosine tetraphosphate (Ap4A). The solved structure (Fig. 3A in pink) models the catalytic site with 2 Mg2+ ions (20). While the first Mg2+ binds at the 2H2D site, the second Mg2+ is sandwiched between ADP, a product of YqeKSp, and an aspartate residue in the 2H2D motif (Fig. 3A). Using the align function in PyMOL (21), we superimposed five YqeKSp α-helices surrounding the 2H2D motif to those in SpoTAb with an RMSD = 3.0 Å across 80 Cα atoms (Fig. 3A. See Fig. 3B for sequence alignment). Intriguingly, the second Mg2+ in the YqeKSp model indicated a plausible location for the M2 site in SpoTAb (Fig. 3C, green sphere) surrounded by oxyanions of Asp79, both 3′-phosphates of ppGpp, and the ED diad consisting of Glu82 and Asp83. We therefore propose the essential role of ED diad is for binding and positioning the M2 cation (Fig. 3, C and D). To test this idea, we mutated Glu82 to glutamine or Asp83 to asparagine in SpoTAb, which would destabilize M2-cation binding. The E82Q mutant exhibited no hydrolase activity, which echoes the complete loss of activity of many RSH hydrolases upon mutation at this site (9, 10, 13) (Fig. 3E, left plot). Similarly, the D83N mutant had poor activity when supplemented with Mg2+ (Fig. 3E, right plot). Strikingly, however, this mutant was substantially activated by Mn2+. Since the mutation does not affect the M1 site defined by the 2H2D motif, activation of SpoTAb(D83N) by millimolar-level of Mn2+ must be due to an increase of Mn2+ occupancy at the M2 site. Compared to the wildtype (WT), which was fully activated by 1.5 mM free Mn2+ (Fig. 2A), activity of the D83N mutant did not reach saturation with 7 mM (Fig. 3E), suggesting a decreased affinity of M2 for Mn2+. This finding strongly supports that Asp83 is a ligand for the M2 site metal. In addition to organizing oxyanions around the active center, the M2-site metal also locates close to the backside of the leaving 3′-oxygen, indicating that it may bind, with its last available valency, the water nucleophile in the precatalytic SpoTAb–ppGpp complex (Fig. 3D). The coordination between 3′-β-phosphate and M2 in our model implies a preference of pyrophosphoester over phosphoester substrates for all RSH hydrolases (Fig. 4A, left and middle). This would contradict the NADPH phosphatase activity of MESH (9). In fact, phosphatase versus pyrophosphohydrolase specificity has not been carefully studied for RSH hydrolases. Because SpoTAb and human Mesh1 both hydrolyze ppGpp (8, 13), we compared their activities on ppGpp versus a substrate missing the 3′-β-phosphate, ppGp (Fig. 4B). While SpoTAb is unable to efficiently hydrolyze ppGp in agreement to the prediction of our model, we observed an inverted specificity for Mesh1, which hydrolyzes ppGpp at a rate 0.14x that of ppGp (Fig. 4B). This result suggests the molecular adaptation of Mesh1 to its phosphatase function. Assuming Mesh1 also adopts our proposed binuclear metallohydrolase mechanism, it must provide an oxyanion moiety to coordinate M2 in place of the missing 3′-β-phosphate in ppGp (Fig. 4A, right). Inspection of Mesh1 crystal structure reveals Asp91, a residue specifically conserved in all MESH, as an attractive candidate of M2 ligand (Fig. 4C in yellow stick model. See Fig. 3B for sequence alignment). Mutating Mesh1 Asp91 to alanine had little effect on ppGpp hydrolysis (Fig. 4B, compare middle and right white bars), suggesting that during ppGpp hydrolysis, the 3′-β-phosphate coordinates M2 in both WT and mutant Mesh1. In contrast, the D91A mutant suffers a 26-fold loss of activity on ppGp as neither 3′-β-phosphate nor Asp91 is available for binding the M2-site metal (Fig. 4A, middle). In addition to MESH, a promiscuous bacterial RSH hydrolase, SAHXc from Xanthomonas campestris capable of hydrolyzing NADPH also possesses an aspartate at this position (Fig. 3B). Discussion

In this study, we have delineated the divalent metal-cation requirements for the RSH hydrolase, SpoTAb. The SpoT–ppGpp complex provides two distinct metal binding sites, M1 and M2. The M1 site disfavors Mg2+ likely due to specificity imparted by histidines in the 2H2D motif (22). Mn2+ activates ppGpp hydrolysis by SpoTAb with a KD = 29 nM. This affinity is much stronger than the previously reported KD = 35 μM based on isothermal titration calorimetry experiments conducted in the absence of ppGpp (13). These results are consistent with the strong contribution of ppGpp to Mn2+ binding revealed in crystal structures (12, 13). The major advancement of this study is the introduction of the M2 site to account for the low-affinity requirement of Mg2+ for SpoTAb activity. The M2 site accepts Mn2+ but does not bind it much stronger than Mg2+ (Fig. 2A). Thus, Mg2+ is likely the physiological cofactor for M2 site due to its much higher free-cation concentration in Gram-negative bacteria compared to Mn2+ (23). Although our placement of the M2 site is largely guided by the structural alignment between the SpoTAb–ppGpp complex and a distantly related hydrolase, YqeKSp, this placement is likely correct for two reasons. First, the M2 site organizes three oxyanions not bound to the M1 center: the 3′-β-phosphate from the substrate and two side-chain carboxylates of the ED diad. This arrangement alleviates electrostatic repulsion among these oxyanions, enhances the electrophilicity of the 3′-α-phosphorus, and likely positions a deprotonated water molecule optimally for attacking the 3′-α-phosphorus. More importantly, the postulated interaction between M2 center and 3′-β-phosphate has led to the identification of a sequence determinant, namely an Asp conserved in all MESH that is crucial for phosphatase activity.

Our primary concern with the binuclear metallohydrolase model is the absence of the M2-site metal from all high-resolution structures of RSH hydrolases. This absence might result from ppGpp's strong contribution to the M2-site, where it acts as a bidentate ligand, suggesting that the M2-site metal may bind only to preformed enzyme–substrate complexes. Presently, only two ppGpp-bound crystal structures of RSH hydrolases exist, both derived from WT enzymes (12, 13). These structures likely differ substantially from the precatalytic conformation: the ppGpp would otherwise have been hydrolyzed by the active center. Consequently, using the SpoTAb-ppGpp structure to model our system (Fig. 3C) yields ambiguous results concerning the sixth ligand of the M1-site Mn2+ alongside the side chains of the 2H2D motif and the 3′-α-phosphate of ppGpp. In the crystal structure, the Tyr51 phenoxyl group and the 2′-OH of ppGpp are positioned about 4 Å from the M1 center (Fig. 3D, gray), but either ligand could move closer for tight coordination. Alternatively, in the precatalytic complex, this moiety may be contributed by the deprotonated water nucleophile, which bridges both metal centers as observed in other binuclear metallohydrolases (24). We propose that the ability of the M1 center to interchange among different ligands provides the necessary flexibility for catalysis in RSH enzymes. Resolving these ambiguities may require RSH hydrolase structures complexed with nonhydrolyzable substrate analogs (25). Regardless, our work points toward a binuclear metallohydrolase mechanism that will be instrumental in guiding future structural and computational studies to fully elucidate the catalytic cycle of RSH hydrolases. Moreover, understanding these mechanisms could inform the development of novel RSH hydrolase inhibitors. This would offer new avenues into drug discovery for both antimicrobial persistence and ferroptosis-associated diseases.

Experimental procedures

Recombinant protein production and purification

SpoTAb, Mesh1, and mutants thereof were expressed as N terminally His6-Sumo-tagged proteins in E. coli BL21(DE3). Expression strains were grown in LB, and expression was induced with 400 μM IPTG at 18 oC for 20 h. Proteins were subject to Ni-NTA purification and then treated with Sumo protease. Tag-free target proteins were refined over a Superdex-200 increase (10/300) column equilibrated in 20 mM Hepes-Na pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP for Mesh1 and 20 mM Hepes-Na pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 500 mM KCl, and 1 mM TCEP for SpoTAb.

Synthesis of ppGpp and ppGp

ppGpp was synthesized using purified B. subtilis SasB (26) as previously described (27). Briefly, the reaction contained 20 mM Tris-HCl 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 25 mM MgCl2, 1 mM TCEP, 10 mM GDP, 12 mmol ATP, and 1 μM SasB and reached completion at 37 oC in 3 h. ppGp was obtained by cleaving 20 μmol ppGpp with 1 unit RNase T2 in 20 mM sodium acetate 4.6, 150 mM NaCl, and 1 mM TCEP at 37oC for 2 h (28). Both nucleotides were purified using MonoQ 10/100 anion-exchange column at pH = 8.0 using a NaCl gradient and was precipitated by the addition of first LiCl (to 1 M concentration) and then absolute ethanol (to 80% v/v).

Metal quantification using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry

Hundred microliter purified recombinant SpoTAb (25 μM) or MnCl2 standards were mixed with 100 μl nitric acid (67–70% w/v). After incubation at 95 oC for 2 h, the digest was diluted to 2 ml with Milli-Q water and analyzed using an Agilent 7900 instrument.

Simulation of free Mn2+ in the presence of Mg2+, EDTA, and ppGpp

Given the deprotonation equilibrium constant of mono- and di-protonated EDTA, Ka4 = 10-10.24 and Ka3 = 10-6.16, and the dissociation constants of KD,MgEDTA = 10-8.79 for Mg-EDTA, 1 mM EDTA should be completely complexed by Mg2+ when free [Mg2+] is present at millimolar levels. For the same reason, ppGpp (0.5 mM) also predominantly forms Mg–ppGpp complex (KD, MgppGpp = 126 μM). Substitution of Mg2+ in Mg–EDTA complex for Mn2+ is highly favorable given KD,MnEDTA = 10-13.89:(1)Keq1=[Mg2+][MnEDTA][Mn2+][MgEDTA]=KD,MgEDTAKD,MnEDTA=1.3×105

Substitution of Mg2+ in Mg–ppGpp complex is much less favorable given KD,MnppGpp = 5.5 μM:(2)Keq2=[Mg2+][MnppGpp][Mn2+][MgppGpp]=KD,MgppGppKD,MnppGpp=23

Thus, with addition of MnCl2, near-complete substitution of MgEDTA is expected, giving rise to [MnEDTA] at the concentration of added MnCl2 and cause [MgEDTA] to decrease and free [Mg2+] to increase by the same amount. Free [Mn2+] is then calculated based on Equation (1). For each condition, we also calculate [MnppGpp] based on this [Mn2+] and Equation (2) and free EDTA (sum of all protonation states) to show that they are negligible relative to [MnEDTA].
