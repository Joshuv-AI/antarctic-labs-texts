From Belief to Behavior: Closing the Knowledge–Practice Gap in Irritable Bowel Syndrome Self-management
Authors: Jung-Wook Kim
Published: 2026

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) management has steadily shifted toward multimodal care, where non-pharmacological strategies—dietary modification, gut–brain behavioral therapies, and structured self-management—are not optional add-ons but core components of effective treatment.1-4 Yet, in routine practice, clinicians repeatedly encounter a familiar gap: many patients express strong interest in lifestyle or mind–body approaches, but few translate that interest into sustained daily practice.5,6 This “belief-behavior gap” is not simply poor adherence. It reflects the complexity of IBS self-care (multiple steps, trial-and-error, symptom fluctuations), variable access to multidisciplinary support, and, importantly, the difference between knowing about an option and knowing how to do it.

In this issue of the Journal of Neurogastroenterology and Motility, the authors address this gap by assessing patients’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) regarding non-pharmacological IBS management using a dedicated questionnaire, and by linking these domains to symptom severity and quality of life.7 Their key message is straightforward and clinically relevant: knowledge is more closely linked to real-world practice than attitude alone, and the pathway from knowledge to practice appears to be direct rather than mediated by attitude (Figure). In the full cohort, the knowledge–practice correlation (r = 0.364) was stronger than the attitude–practice correlation (r = 0.151), and multivariable linear regression identified knowledge (β = 0.399, P < 0.001) as the strongest predictor of practice. Structural equation modeling further suggested a largely direct knowledge-to-practice pathway (β = 0.356, P = 0.006), with a non-significant attitude-to-practice path and no evidence of meaningful mediation by attitude. This pattern was consistent in stratified analyses: correlations among KAP domains were not significant in the low-knowledge group, whereas the knowledge–practice association remained strongest in the high-knowledge group. Put simply, positive attitudes may be common, but they do not reliably translate into action. This aligns with everyday clinical experience—agreeing that “diet matters” or “stress matters” is not the same as having a workable plan (what to change, for how long, and what to do next if symptoms persist).

Knowledge-to-practice pathway and implementation levers in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) self-management. The knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) framework highlights that knowledge (K) shows a stronger link to practice (P) than attitude (A), suggesting that improving practice depends more on strengthening procedural knowledge than on favorable beliefs alone.7 Practical implementation levers to increase practice include: (1) micro-education (brief script + checklist with a clear next step), (2) a feasible care pathway (stepwise menu with referral routes), and (3) structured follow-up (scheduled reassessment and troubleshooting to iteratively refine the plan).

A useful way to interpret these findings is to distinguish declarative knowledge from procedural (actionable) knowledge. Declarative knowledge is awareness—patients can name an intervention or describe it in general terms (eg, “I have heard of the low fermentable oligo-, di-, mono-saccharides, and polyols (FODMAP) diet” or “stress affects IBS”). Procedural knowledge is practical competence—patients can describe the steps, the time frame, and the decision points, and they have a plan for common barriers (eg, “I know the steps and the time-limit of restriction, I can identify FODMAP categories, I know when to reintroduce, and I have a plan if symptoms worsen”). IBS self-management depends heavily on procedural knowledge.8,9 Many guideline-supported non-pharmacological therapies are inherently stepwise.4,10 Dietary strategies, for example, often require a structured trial period and reassessment; more complex diets require staged reintroduction and personalization to avoid unnecessary restriction.4 These dietary strategies may require cultural and regional adaptation (eg, staple foods, shared dishes, and fermented sauces and pastes), locally validated food lists, and access to dietitian support to enable successful implementation.11,12 Gut-directed behavioral therapies are effective when patients can access and engage with them, but uptake is strongly influenced by feasibility, referral pathways, and follow-up.2,3,13 Even relatively simple measures, such as soluble fiber, regular physical activity, sleep and stress routines, require reinforcement and troubleshooting because symptoms wax and wane.2-4 In short, IBS self-management is not one intervention but a sequence of decisions over time, and this is precisely where actionable knowledge becomes therapeutic.

From a clinical implementation standpoint, this paper suggests 3 practical actions (Figure). First, deliver micro-education that is short, standardized, and explicitly behavior-linked. A brief script plus a one-page checklist can outperform longer explanations if it ends with a clear next step (what to do this week; how to monitor; and when to reassess).14,15 Second, embed non-pharmacological care into a feasible care pathway rather than a one-time recommendation. This means having an accessible “menu” with a stepwise sequence (first-line, second-line, and escalation), and clear referral options (dietitian, gut–brain behavioral therapy resources, and digital tools for tracking).4,16 Third, practice improves when education is paired with structured follow-up. Because IBS symptoms naturally wax and wane, without follow-up, patients may abandon effective strategies during the first setback or over-restrict diets in ways that worsen quality of life.9 Brief, scheduled reassessment transforms knowledge into iterative problem-solving.

At the same time, the interpretation of KAP findings should consider cohort structure. In the present study, a large proportion of participants had a relatively short disease duration, most had not received prior treatment, and a subset were minimally symptomatic at the time of assessment. These features can systematically lower practice scores and shape the relationship between symptom severity and practice. Rather than weakening the message, this highlights a key clinical question “when is the optimal window for education?” Newly diagnosed patients may be a high-yield target for structured self-management programs before maladaptive coping patterns and unnecessary restrictions become entrenched. Conversely, patients with longstanding or more severe symptoms may require more intensive support—especially when psychological comorbidity, sleep disturbance, or limited access to specialized care becomes a barrier to implementation.17,18

The next step is to move from cross-sectional association to actionable implementation research. Pragmatic trials could test whether brief, procedural education packages—delivered in clinic or digitally and paired with follow-up—improve practice and patient-centered outcomes over time. Future work should also measure practice as behaviors (quality of dietary adherence, completion of reintroduction phases, engagement with behavioral therapy sessions, and sustained activity), rather than relying only on score thresholds. Finally, broader validation of KAP instruments across health systems and care settings will clarify which components are universal and which are context-dependent.

In summary, this study reinforces a message that IBS clinicians already sense but rarely quantify, “positive attitudes are not enough.” The clinical lever is actionable knowledge embedded within a feasible care pathway—one that lowers the barrier between “I agree” and “I do.” As IBS management continues to evolve toward patient-centered, multimodal care, closing the knowledge–practice gap may be one of the most practical ways to improve real-world uptake of evidence-based non-pharmacological strategies.
