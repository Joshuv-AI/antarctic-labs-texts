USP4-Mediated deubiquitination of A2AR suppresses autophagy-dependent ferroptosis in gastric cancer
Authors: Xuan Zhao, Sheng Lu, Zhaoyu Xiang, Min Yan, Zheng-Gang Zhu, Feng Dong, Chao Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) are crucial for regulating the degradation of specific proteins and represent a novel therapeutic direction in cancer. In gastric cancer, USP4 levels are significantly elevated, though its therapeutic potential remains underexplored. Our study demonstrates that USP4 plays a pivotal role in gastric cancer cells via the autophagy-dependent ferroptosis pathway. Specifically, USP4 selectively removes Lys48-linked polyubiquitin chains through its deubiquitination activity at the C311 site, stabilizing the A2AR protein. This action precisely modulates autophagy and inhibits ferroptosis, thereby promoting gastric cancer progression. Additionally, our findings indicate that targeting USP4 or A2AR can activate autophagy and restore the ferroptosis process, which is essential for autophagy-dependent ferroptosis. Consequently, the USP4-A2AR signaling pathway is critically important for the survival of gastric cancer cells and represents a potential therapeutic target for gastric cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05826-2.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) represents a significant global health concern, ranking as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related deaths and the fifth most prevalent type of cancer [1]. Delayed diagnosis of advanced disease results in extremely limited patient survival, typically no more than one year [2]. Radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies are the mainstay of treatment, but often trigger multidrug resistance and tumor recurrence [3]. These challenges highlight the urgent need to explore underlying molecular mechanisms to develop more effective therapeutic strategies.

Ferroptosis, an emerging mode of cell death, is distinguished by the accumulation of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the subsequent lipid peroxidation reaction [4]. It has shown promising therapeutic potential across various malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and lung cancer [5–7]. In the context of gastric cancer, ferroptosis exerts a profound influence on the tumor microenvironment, regulating a range of cellular processes including proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and drug sensitivity [8, 9]. Specifically, the autophagic process is a pivotal factor in the initiation and execution of ferroptosis, whereby it promotes the degradation of ferritin and the release of ferric ions, thus increasing the intracellular iron concentration [10]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms linking autophagy-dependent ferroptosis and gastric cancer remain insufficiently understood.

Ubiquitin-specific protease 4 (USP4) is a deubiquitinating enzyme involeved in regulating several pivotal aspects of cancer progression [11, 12]. By specifically removing ubiquitination modifications on key proteins, this enzyme plays a pivotal role in regulating signaling pathways such as, TGF-β, and NF-κB, which in turn affects the proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis processes of tumor cells [11, 13, 14]. USP4 has been reported to affect immune cell function within the tumor microenvironment, including T regulatory cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes, influencing immune surveillance and escape mechanisms [15]. The high expression of USP4 in gastric cancer is closely associated with a poor prognosis for patients, and its function in promoting the growth of gastric cancer cells and tumor progression underscores its importance as a potential target for gastric cancer therapy [16]. While previous studies have explored USP4’s role in cancer, its involvement in ferroptosis has not been clearly defined.

This study investigates a novel mechanistic link between USP4 and autophagy-dependent ferroptosis in gastric cancer. We found that USP4 expression is significantly upregulated in gastric cancer tissues and that USP4 inhibits the ubiquitination and degradation of the A2AR protein by cleaving its Lys48-linked polyubiquitin chains. This stabilizes A2AR and suppresses autophagy-dependent ferroptosis. Our experimental data demonstrate that targeting USP4 or A2AR attenuates the malignant progression of gastric cancer and suggest a potentially new therapeutic strategy involving the USP4–A2AR axis.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

The human gastric cancer cell lines MGC-803, SNU-5, AGS, MKN-1 and HEK-293T used in this study were derived from the Typical Culture Collection Center in the United States. Cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% Fetal bovine serum, 100 units/mL penicillin and 100 mg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

In vivo xenograft model

All animal experiments were conducted at Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine in strict compliance with the National Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were duly approved by Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Ethics Committee. The objective of the experiments was to induce subcutaneous tumor formation, which was achieved by injecting MGC-803 cells (1 × 10⁷) subcutaneously into both sides of female nude mice (5–6 weeks of age, five per group). Tumor diameters were measured at three-day intervals, and the volume was calculated using the formula V = 0.52 × L × W². Upon reaching the predetermined endpoint for tumor loading, the mice were euthanized using inhaled carbon dioxide.

IHC staining of patient tissue

Tissue samples were Fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde For a period of 6 to 48 h at room temperature. Following Fixation, the samples were subjected to a graded ethanol dehydration process and subsequently cleared with Xylene. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin, cured, and transformed into wax blocks. The resulting wax blocks were then sliced into sections of 3–5 μm in thickness and affixed to slides. Subsequently, the sections were treated with Xylene and ethanol in order to dewax and hydrate them. Antigen repair was conducted through thermal induction, utilizing sodium citrate or Tris-EDTA buffer. To reduce the incidence of non-specific staining, the sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Subsequently, non-specific binding sites were blocked using a protein blocking solution, after which specific primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature. Signal staining was achieved by using a chromogenic agent (e.g., DAB), and cell nuclei were contrast stained with hematoxylin to enhance cellular morphology contrast. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, washed, and sealed with resin for microscopic observation.

H&E staining

The observation of pathological changes was conducted through the utilisation of the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining technique. The experimental animals, rats, were initially treated with 0.9% heparinised saline to reduce the interference of blood components during cardiac perfusion. This was Followed by Fixation of the spinal cord using 4% paraformaldehyde, which was employed to maintain the integrity and stability of the tissue structure. Following Fixation, the spinal cord tissues were paraffin-embedded to facilitate sectioning operations, with the thickness of the sections strictly controlled at 4 μm to ensure uniformity and staining of the sections. After H&E staining, the sections were observed in detail under a light microscope (Model BX51, Manufacturer Olympus, located in Tokyo, Japan) to evaluate the histopathological changes.

Determination of lipid peroxidation, GSH, and iron content.

The concentrations of MDA and 4-HNE in cell lysates were determined using the Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit, while the concentrations of GSH were quantified using the Glutathione Assay Kit. The iron ion concentration was measured using the Iron Assay Kit. All assays were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to gain comprehensive insight into the mechanisms of oxidative damage and antioxidant defense in cells.

Plasmids and shRNAs

To inhibit intracellular USP4 and A2AR expression, specific short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) were designed to target these genes. To facilitate overexpression of USP4 and A2AR, their coding sequences (CDS) were cloned into pcDNA3.1 + vectors, with the corresponding transcript identifiers USP4 (NM_001251877.2) and A2AR (NM_000675.6). To enhance USP4 expression in vivo, the adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector pAAV-PHP.eB-hSyn-USP4-3×FLAG-WPRE (referred to as AAV-USP4) was utilized, which was provided by Obio Technology (Shanghai, China). To downregulate A2AR expression in vivo, AAV vectors expressing shRNA targeting A2AR or a control shRNA were prepared. The viral solution was diluted to 250 µL with 0.9% NaCl at a viral titer of 3 × 10¹¹ viral genomes/mL. Myc-tagged A2AR and flag-tagged USP4 (wild-type or mutant) were constructed on pcDNA3.1 + vectors for use in immunoprecipitation experiments.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were isolated from cell and tissue samples and quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit From Beyotime. The separated proteins were subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membranes were incubated with 5% BSA For one hour at room temperature, Followed by an overnight incubation with the primary antibody at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibody (against rabbit and mouse IgG, dilution ratio 1:3000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for two hours at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized by ECL reagent, and protein expression levels were analyzed by ImageJ software.

Immunoprecipitation

The cells were harvested and lysed using a cell immunoprecipitation (lysis) buffer that contained protease inhibitors provided by Biotronik. Following lysis at 4 °C For 30 min, the cells were subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 × g For 30 min, with the resulting supernatant subsequently utilized in subsequent experiments. Meanwhile, a portion of the lysate was reserved for subsequent Western blot analysis. The remaining lysate was incubated with a specific primary antibody and protein A/G beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking to Facilitate the immunoprecipitation of the target protein. Following the completion of the immunoprecipitation step, the samples were subjected to centrifugation at 3,000 × g For 5 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, four washes with lysis buffer were conducted, and finally, the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in a water bath for Western blot analysis.

Double-labeled adenovirus mRFP-GFP-LC3 transfection

Prior to transfection, the cells must undergo a rigorous pre-treatment process to guarantee optimal transfection efficiency. Subsequently, the virus solution was combined with cell culture medium and introduced to gastric cancer cell lines for transfection. During the transfection process, co-transfection agents, such as polybrene, were introduced to enhance the infection efficiency. Following transfection, the cells are incubated in culture medium containing the virus for a specified period, typically four hours. Subsequently, the culture medium is replenished with fresh culture medium to the original volume, and incubation is continued. On the second day after transfection, the fresh culture medium is replaced to remove residual virus, and the expression of GFP and RFP is observed by fluorescence microscopy to assess the formation of autophagosomes and autophagolysosomes.

MitoTracker mitochondrial staining

To track intracellular mitochondria, a 1 mM MitoTracker storage solution was prepared and stored in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at −20 °C in a light-protected environment. In the course of the experiments, the storage solution was diluted to a working concentration of 25–500 nM and incubated with the cells at 37 °C For 15–45 min. Following staining, the cells were rinsed with medium and Fixed with 2–4% formaldehyde, after which they were subsequently washed. At the researcher’s discretion, cells may be permeabilized in a buffer solution containing Triton X-100. Subsequently, the results of the staining were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Transmission electron microscopy

In the preparation of transmission electron microscopy samples, gastric cancer cells were initially treated and Fixed in a pre-cooled 2% glutaraldehyde solution For a period of two hours at 4 °C. Subsequently, the samples were treated with 2% uranyl acetate for a period of two hours in order to enhance contrast. Upon completion of the staining process, the samples were subjected to a series of increasing acetone concentration gradients (From 50 to 100%) to facilitate dehydration. Subsequently, the samples were embedded and cut into ultrathin sections following the completion of the dehydration step. Subsequently, observations were conducted using an FEI Tecnai transmission electron microscope in Hillsboro, Oregon, USA, for high-resolution visualization of cellular structures.

Analysis of cell death and cell viability

In the course of the experiment, both propidium iodide (PI) staining and the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) were employed. Propidium iodide staining is a method for identifying cell death due to its ability to bind to DNA, which can be observed under a fluorescent microscope. In contrast, the CCK-8 kit employs a quantitative assessment of cell viability through the detection of metabolic activity within living cells. This approach is based on the principle that WST-8 is reduced to a water-soluble yellow formazan product (formazan dye) by dehydrogenase enzymes in the presence of an electron carrier. The generation of Formazan dye is proportional to the number of living cells, as a result of the reaction between 1-methoxy-5-methylphenazinium dimethyl sulfate (1-Methoxy PMS). In the initial stage of the experimental manipulation, gastric cancer cells were cultured in 96-well plates following the administration of a specific CCK-8 reagent treatment. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ For Four hours. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm.

Transwell assay

Firstly, 50 µl of serum-free medium was added to each well and incubated For 30 min prior to the commencement of the experiment. Subsequently, the starved gastric cancer cells were treated with trypsin and washed with PBS, before being resuspended in serum-free medium containing BSA and adjusted to the appropriate concentration. Subsequently, 100 µl of the cell suspension was introduced to the upper chamber of the Transwell, while the lower chamber was Filled with medium containing 10% FBS. Following a 12- to 48-hour incubation period, the chambers were removed, washed with PBS, Fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with crystal violet. Finally, five fields of view were randomly selected under a microscope, and the number of cells crossing the membrane was counted to assess the migration ability of gastric cancer cells.

Oxidative stress detection

To ascertain the intracellular levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), the DCFH-DA solution was diluted to a concentration of 10 µmol/L and subsequently added to the serum-free medium, in accordance with the instructions provided in the reactive oxygen species assay kit. Following the removal of the medium, the DCFH-DA solution was added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C For 20 min. Subsequently, ROS levels were assessed by washing the cells three times with PBS to remove any unbound DCFH-DA, and finally by observing the fluorescence intensity of DCF using fluorescence microscopy.

CFDA SE Staining

A 5 mm CFDA SE storage solution should be prepared and stored in anhydrous DMSO at −20 °C, protected From light. For experimental purposes, the solution should be diluted to a concentration of 0.5-5 µM in serum-free medium. The gastric cancer cells were collected, adjusted to a concentration of 1–5 × 10⁵ cells/tube, mixed with the working solution, and incubated For a period of 10–30 min at room temperature or 37 °C, protected From light. Following the termination of the reaction, the cells were washed with cold medium, resuspended, and incubated at 37 °C. The extent of cell proliferation was determined by measuring the fluorescence intensity using flow cytometry or fluorescence microscopy.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from the experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), reflecting the pooled results of three or more independent experiments. Critical statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Software 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA) to guarantee the precision and dependability of the data. In order to ascertain whether there were any statistically significant differences between two sets of data, an unpaired two-tailed t-test was employed. In instances where a comparison was to be made between more than two groups of data, the one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was selected as the most appropriate statistical tool to explore the differences between the various groups. To further refine these differences, multiple comparisons were subsequently corrected using Tukey’s HSD (Honest Significant Difference) post hoc test to control For the probability of Type I error. In this study, a p-value of less than 0.05 was set as the threshold of statistical significance to determine the significance of the results.

Results

Elevated USP4 expression is associated with malignant progression of gastric cancer

A systematic analysis revealed that the expression of the USP4 was significantly elevated in gastric cancer tissues (Fig. 1A, B), and this high expression correlated with a poor prognosis for gastric cancer patients (Fig. 1C). Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis confirmed that the expression level of USP4 was markedly increased in gastric cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1D). Consistently, IHC staining and Western blot experiments of paired tumor and adjacent tissues from five gastric cancer patients further verified that USP4 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (Fig. 1E). These findings indicate that USP4 plays a crucial role in the malignant progression of gastric cancer, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target. Moreover, the expression level of USP4 was assessed in various gastric cancer cell lines, revealing upregulation in AGS, MKN-1, MGC-803, and SNU-5 cell lines, with particularly high levels observed in MGC-803 and SNU-5 (Fig. 1G). Conversely, the overexpression of USP4 was observed to promote cell proliferation and survivability (Fig. S1A-B). Furthermore, the knockdown of USP4 was observed to significantly inhibit the migration and invasion abilities of gastric cancer cells (Fig. 2D), reduce the number of cells adhering to vascular endothelial cells (Fig. 2E), and regulate the expression of markers of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). This was evidenced by the up-regulation of E-cadherin and the down-regulation of N-cadherin and Vimentin (Fig. 2F). Of particular importance was the discovery that the knockdown of USP4 induces ferroptosis in gastric cancer cells. This process is accompanied by glutathione (GSH) depletion, increased lipid peroxidation, and accumulation of Fe²⁺ (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, USP4 knockdown resulted in the upregulation of ACSL4 expression while simultaneously downregulating FTH1 and GPX4 (Fig. 2H), leading to an increased accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Fig. 2I). In contrast, the overexpression of USP4 has been demonstrated to suppress ferroptosis events (Fig. S1F), regulate the expression levels of ACSL4, NCOA4, FTH1, and GPX4 (Fig. S1G), inhibit ROS accumulation (Fig. S1H).

Further experiments demonstrated that the knockdown of USP4 exhibited a synergistic inhibitory effect on the viability of gastric cancer cells when combined with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin (Fig. 2J). Conversely, the overexpression of USP4 was observed to reverse the inhibitory effect of the ferroptosis activator on cell survival (Fig. S1I). These findings provide a novel perspective on USP4 as a potential target for gastric cancer therapy and offer a scientific foundation for the prospective utilisation of ferroptosis in gastric cancer treatment.

USP4-mediated autophagy regulation in ferroptosis process of gastric cancer cells

Recent studies are progressively revealing that ferroptosis is not an isolated phenomenon; rather, it is intricately linked to various cellular processes [17]. Notably, ferroptosis interrelates with autophagy, a process that facilitates ferroptosis by degrading ferritin, an iron-storage protein, thereby regulating iron levels and the accumulation of ROS [18]. Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (MtROS) play a pivotal role in the processes of ferroptosis and autophagy [19]. The accumulation of MtROS is not only a defining characteristic of ferroptosis but also a key factor in promoting elevated levels of autophagy. Our observations indicated that downregulation of USP4 expression resulted in a reduction in mitochondrial number, whereas USP4 overexpression led to an increase in mitochondrial number (Fig. 3A, F). This suggests a potential role for USP4 in regulating the autophagy process associated with ferroptosis. The cellular autophagy flux was evaluated by transfecting gastric cancer cells with the mRFP-GFP-LC3 virus. The experimental results demonstrated that sh-USP4 markedly augmented autophagic flux (Fig. 3B, C). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy revealed a notable increase in the number of autophagosomes following USP4 knockdown (Fig. 3D), accompanied by an elevation in ATG5 protein levels and a reduction in P62 protein levels (Fig. 3E). Conversely, USP4 overexpression markedly impeded the autophagic process, as indicated by enhanced cell survival, diminished autophagic flux, and elevated P62 protein levels accompanied by reduced ATG5 protein levels (Fig. 3G-I). To investigate whether the regulation of ferroptosis process by USP4 is dependent on autophagy, the activation of autophagy induced by USP4 knockdown was inhibited by the use of chloroquine (CQ) or knockdown of ATG5 (Fig. 4A, D). The findings demonstrated that the inhibition of autophagy markedly reversed the ferroptosis induced by sh-USP4 in gastric cancer cells. Specifically, the inhibition of autophagy resulted in a significant attenuation of sh-USP4-induced GSH depletion, lipid peroxidation, and Fe²⁺ accumulation (Fig. 4B, E). Moreover, autophagy inhibition resulted in the downregulation of the expression of the ferroptosis-related protein ASCL4, as well as the upregulation of the expression of the ferroptosis inhibitory proteins FTH1 and GPX4 (Fig. 4C, F). Further, we utilized Erastin to activate ferroptosis suppressed by USP4 overexpression. It was observed that the onset of ferroptosis was accompanied by the activation of autophagy. Specifically, Erastin significantly promoted the transformation of LC3II inhibited by USP4 (Fig. 4G), increased the expression of ATG7, Beclin1, and ATG5 (Fig. 4H), and decreased the expression of P62 (Fig. 4I). Concomitantly, Erastin also enhanced the inhibition of autophagic flux by USP4 (Fig. 4J). To further investigate the role of A2AR in autophagy regulation, we analyzed autophagy-related markers in gastric cancer cells (Fig. S4). Western blot analysis showed that A2AR knockdown significantly increased the levels of LC3-I, LC3-II, ATG5, ATG7, and Beclin 1, while decreasing p62 expression, suggesting enhanced autophagic flux. In contrast, A2AR overexpression suppressed these autophagy markers and led to p62 accumulation. Treatment with chloroquine (CQ) and rapamycin partially reversed the autophagy-inhibitory or stimulatory effects, respectively. Additionally, confocal imaging of GFP-mRFP-LC3 revealed increased autolysosome formation in A2AR-silenced cells and reduced puncta in A2AR-overexpressing cells, further confirming that A2AR acts as a regulator of autophagy in gastric cancer cells.

Knockdown of USP4 was further demonstrated to reduce the protein level of A2AR (Fig. 5F). Co-IP experiments provided confirmation of the interaction between USP4 and A2AR under endogenous conditions (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, co-transfection of USP4 with a FLAG tag and A2AR with a Myc tag in 293 T cells provided additional validation of their interaction under exogenous conditions (Fig. 5H).

Our investigation into the regulatory mechanism of USP4 on A2AR revealed that USP4 does not modulate A2AR levels via the protein synthesis pathway (Fig. 5I). Using the proteasome inhibitor MG132, we found that the reduction in A2AR levels following USP4 knockdown was reversible (Fig. 5J), indicating that USP4’s regulatory effect on A2AR is mediated by its deubiquitination activity. Further experiments confirmed that USP4 knockdown significantly increased A2AR ubiquitination, while USP4 overexpression decreased it, validating that USP4 modulates A2AR through its deubiquitinating enzymatic function (Fig. 5K, L). To determine the specificity of USP4 in A2AR deubiquitination, we engineered plasmids expressing ubiquitin chains with different linkages. Results showed that USP4 selectively cleaved Lys48-linked polyubiquitin chains, with no significant effect on Lys63-linked chains (Fig. 5M). To elucidate the role of Lys48-linked ubiquitination in USP4-mediated A2AR deubiquitination, we expressed Lys48-resistant (Lys48R) ubiquitin in USP4-depleted HEK 293 T cells. This expression mitigated the reduction in A2AR levels induced by USP4 knockdown, confirming the critical role of Lys48-linked ubiquitination in USP4-regulated A2AR degradation (Fig. 5N). Furthermore, transfection with a mutant plasmid containing the key deubiquitinating enzyme site of USP4 resulted in a significant loss of USP4-mediated A2AR deubiquitination (Fig. 5O). GST ubiquitination assays confirmed that the C311 residue is essential for USP4’s deubiquitination activity, and the mutation significantly increased A2AR ubiquitination (Fig. 5P).

Discussion

Our findings reveal a previously uncharacterized role of USP4 in the modulation of autophagy-dependent ferroptosis in gastric cancer. The data indicate that USP4 promotes tumor progression by stabilizing A2AR, which inhibits autophagy and ferroptosis. This inhibitory effect was validated through molecular and cellular assays demonstrating that silencing either USP4 or A2AR enhances ferroptosis and impairs cell viability. Furthermore, the combination of CPI-444, a selective inhibitor of USP4 and A2AR, and vialinolin A was found to enhance the therapeutic effect, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the clinical development of novel combination drug therapy strategies (Fig. 8). Our study expands on these findings by demonstrating that USP4 contributes to gastric cancer progression through regulation of the autophagy–ferroptosis axis, highlighting a new dimension of its oncogenic potential.Fig. 8USP4 plays a key role in regulating autophagy-dependent ferroptosis in gastric cancer cells. The relationship between USP4 and oncogenic progression is supported by existing literature across multiple cancer types [5–7]. A study demonstrated that USP4 specifically interacts with transforming growth factor-β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) and stabilizes TAK1 protein in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cells through deubiquitination [20]. This, in turn, promotes ESCC proliferation, migration, and invasion through the MEK/ERK signaling pathway [20]. Moreover, USP4 exerts a synergistic effect with USP15 on the proliferation and migration of lung cancer cells by regulating the selective splicing of SRSF1 [21]. The nuclear localization of IRF3 is inhibited by USP4, which suppresses K63 polyubiquitination of IRF3. This, in turn, reduces the immune response of colorectal cancer cells [15]. The aforementioned results indicate that USP4 may play a role in cancerous processes through its deubiquitinating activity. However, the molecular mechanism of USP4 in gastric cancer remains unclear. In this study, we confirmed that USP4 expression is upregulated in gastric cancer and significantly promotes malignant progression of gastric cancer by analyzing patient tissue samples, evaluating cellular protein expression levels, and performing molecular biology experiments. Knockdown of USP4 significantly inhibited the malignant phenotype and survival of gastric cancer cells. Further research will be conducted to elucidate the potential molecular mechanisms of USP4 in gastric cancer.

Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death that is closely associated with disturbances in iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation, plays an important role in the development of gastric cancer [22]. Recent advances have demonstrated that the regulation of ferroptosis is a complex process involving multiple dimensions, including precise control of iron metabolism [23], oxidative stress response [24], rigorous regulation of gene expression [25], and interaction with other cell death pathways [26]. Specific autophagy subtypes, including ferritin autophagy, lipid autophagy, biophagy, and molecular chaperone-mediated autophagy, have been identified as playing a pivotal role in the regulation of ferroptosis [27]. These autophagic processes exert a significant influence on the execution of ferroptosis by targeting key substrates, including ferritin and lipid droplets [27]. While prior research has established the involvement of autophagy in ferroptosis, our study provides mechanistic insights into how USP4 inhibits this pathway by stabilizing A2AR. We further demonstrate that this suppression promotes gastric cancer malignancy, suggesting therapeutic relevance for modulating this axis. Further studies will be conducted to elucidate the molecular mechanism by which USP4 regulates autophagy-dependent ferroptosis, with the aim of identifying novel molecular targets for the treatment of gastric cancer.

The adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) is a high affinity adenosine G protein-coupled receptor [28, 29] expressed on a variety of immune cells including regulatory T cells, cytotoxic T cells and macrophages [30]. In the tumour microenvironment, A2AR activates G proteins by binding to adenosine, thereby triggering the cAMP/PKA/CREB signalling pathway, which plays an important role in protecting normal organs from immune cell autoimmune responses [31]. In cancer, however, the role of the A2AR shifts to promote tumour immune escape [32]. Many solid tumours activate the A2AR pathway by promoting adenosine accumulation, which inhibits the immune response of immune cells and thus promotes immune escape of tumour cells in the tumour microenvironment [33, 34]. Thus, blocking the adenosine pathway can inhibit the progression of many solid tumours, and A2AR inhibitors have emerged as a promising new target in cancer therapy [35, 36]. While A2AR’s immunomodulatory function is established, its connection to autophagy and ferroptosis remains underexplored. In this study, we demonstrate that A2AR contributes to the inhibition of autophagy-dependent ferroptosis in gastric cancer, and that USP4 stabilizes A2AR by deubiquitination at Lys48, further supporting its anti-ferroptotic function. Further mechanistic studies revealed that USP4 plays a pivotal role in the regulation of ferroptosis through A2AR. USP4 selectively removes Lys48-linked polyubiquitin chains through its deubiquitination activity at the C311 site, stabilises A2AR protein, and promotes autophagy-dependent ferroptosis in gastric cancer cells. Furthermore, this study demonstrated that sh-A2AR markedly reversed USP4-induced gastric cancer cell survival, suggesting a potential novel therapeutic target for gastric cancer treatment.

In the treatment of gastric cancer, the investigation of combination therapeutic strategies has become a prominent area of research [37]. We also evaluated a combination treatment strategy using Vialinin A (a selective USP4 inhibitor) and CPI-444 (an A2AR inhibitor), which significantly enhanced ferroptosis in gastric cancer cells in vitro and in vivo. These results suggest potential for combined targeting of the USP4–A2AR axis as an innovative therapeutic approach for gastric cancer. In light of these findings, the present study offers compelling evidence in support of a combined therapeutic approach for gastric cancer, while simultaneously establishing a scientific foundation for future clinical applications.

Fig. S4A2AR negatively regulates autophagy in gastric cancer cells. A. Western blot analysis of LC3-I and LC3-II expression in SNU-5 and AGS cells. Knockdown of A2AR markedly increased LC3-I and LC3-II levels, indicating enhanced autophagosome formation, whereas A2AR overexpression reduced their expression, suggesting autophagy suppression. The autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ) and the autophagy inducer rapamycin were used to partially reverse these trends. B. Western blot analysis of ATG7, Beclin 1, and ATG5 showed their upregulation upon A2AR knockdown and downregulation upon A2AR overexpression, further supporting A2AR as a negative regulator of autophagy. C. Western blot analysis of p62, a selective autophagy substrate, revealed decreased expression following A2AR knockdown and increased accumulation after A2AR overexpression, consistent with the observed changes in autophagic flux. D. Fluorescence imaging of cells expressing tandem GFP-mRFP-LC3 demonstrated increased red puncta (autolysosomes) in A2AR knockdown cells, indicative of enhanced autophagic flux, while A2AR overexpression suppressed this process. Quantification of red/green puncta ratios further confirmed A2AR as a negative regulator of autophagy. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P< 0.001. (PNG 1.29 MB) High Resolution Image (TIF 31.7 MB)
