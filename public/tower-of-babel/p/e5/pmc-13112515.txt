Enhancing Poly‐ε‐Caprolactone Implant Performance: Synergistic Surface Modifications With Osteon‐Like Microstructure, Cold Atmospheric Plasma, and Extracellular Matrix Coating
Authors: Matthias Vostatek, Elettra Verin, Marvin Tamm, Christoph Mehofer, Markus Wellenzohn, Karl H. Schneider, Stefan Toegel, Francesco Moscato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSurface modifications of polycaprolactone (PCL) implants influence osseointegration by altering cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. Osteon‐like microtopography has been shown to enhance these parameters compared to untreated PCL. Additional treatments, such as cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) and extracellular matrix (ECM) coatings, may further improve bioactivity. This study evaluates and compares the osteogenic potential of osteon‐like microtopography, CAP treatment on non‐microstructured PCL, ECM coating on non‐microstructured PCL, and their combination. Modified PCL samples were analyzed for cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation using primary human mesenchymal stem cells. Compared to osteon‐like microtopography, CAP‐treated and ECM‐coated surfaces the combination of all three increased cell adhesion by nearly two‐fold. Cell proliferation followed a similar trend, with the combination treatment showing the greatest enhancement by day 10. Alkaline phosphatase activity peaked with osteon‐like microtopography, while mineralization was maximized with the combination treatment. In summary, the integration of osteon‐like microtopography with CAP treatment and ECM coating synergistically improves cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, offering a promising strategy for optimizing PCL implants to improve osseointegration.

Introduction

Cell adhesion is crucial for the survival of anchorage‐dependent cells within the extracellular matrix. This process supports essential cellular functions, including migration, proliferation, and differentiation, and also facilitates vital communication between cells and their environment, which is integral to tissue development and maintenance [1, 2]. In tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, artificial surfaces are designed to mimic the natural properties of the extracellular matrix, thus regulating cell adhesion behaviors [3]. Gaining insight into cell interactions with these surfaces is essential for advancing material science and life sciences, particularly in the biomedical field, to predict and manage potential biocompatibility issues and the functional performance of biomaterials [4].

Addressing bone defects and injuries remains a significant challenge in regenerative medicine [5]. As such, research has increasingly focused on developing biomaterials for bone tissue engineering that meet the stringent structural, mechanical, and biological requirements necessary for effective bone regeneration [6]. Osseointegration, a concept introduced by Per‐Ingvar Brånemark in 1976, describes the direct structural and functional connection between living bone and the surface of an implant [7]. This concept has since expanded to include a wide range of biomaterials capable of achieving osseointegration [8]. The process of osseointegration involves three primary phases: osteoconduction, de novo bone formation, and bone remodeling [9].

Osteoconduction is the initial phase where osteogenic cells migrate to the implant surface, initiating new bone formation [10]. At the interface, de novo bone formation involves a series of sequential reactions, including cell binding, chemotaxis, and differentiation [11, 12]. The initial recruitment of cells and subsequent bone formation highlight the importance of implant surface properties and biological factors [13]. Bone remodeling, a complex and dynamic process, involves the binding of fibrin and fibronectin, cell chemotaxis, and differentiation [14]. Despite advancements in bone implants, failures still occur due to inadequate osseointegration, leading to implant loosening [15, 16]. This ongoing challenge has driven efforts to develop novel implant materials that enhance osseointegration [17, 18].

The surface properties of implants, including their chemical, biochemical, and biophysical characteristics, play a crucial role in mediating interactions between cells and bone implants [19]. These interactions significantly impact cellular responses both in vivo and in vitro [20]. A comprehensive understanding of the interplay between biomaterials and cells is essential, as it directly influences cell attachment, proliferation, differentiation, and the integration of biomaterials into host tissue. Various surface treatments have been developed to enhance the biological properties of implant surfaces, thereby promoting osseointegration by facilitating bone formation and improving implant stability [21, 22, 23].

Recent advancements in miniaturization techniques, such as two‐photon polymerization printing, have significantly enhanced our understanding of how surface microtopography affects cell behavior [24]. These effects include changes in cell adhesion, migration, cytoskeletal organization, genome regulation, and differentiation [25]. In a previous study, we demonstrated that osteon‐like microtopography can substantially enhance cell attachment, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [26]. By mimicking the natural structure of bone, osteon‐like microtopography creates a favorable environment for cell adhesion and subsequent proliferation, ensuring a stable interface between the implant and the surrounding tissue [26].

Cold atmospheric plasma (CAP) is another promising technology that generates non‐thermal plasma at atmospheric pressure, consisting of ions and electrons [27]. Over the past century, CAP has evolved significantly and has found applications in various fields, particularly in medicine. CAP's ability to eradicate bacteria and biofilms, promote tissue regeneration, and sterilize surfaces has made it invaluable for enhancing the performance of medical and dental implants [28]. CAP offers several benefits for dental implants that improve osseointegration [29]. CAP's strong antibacterial properties reduce the presence of bacteria on implant surfaces, preventing infections such as peri‐implantitis, which can compromise implant stability and success [30]. Additionally, CAP can modify the surface chemistry and topography of implants, increasing their hydrophilicity and surface roughness [31]. These modifications enhance the bioactivity of the implant surfaces, promoting better cell adhesion and proliferation, which are critical for successful osseointegration [27].

Moreover, CAP treatment has been shown to promote cell viability and proliferation on implant materials like titanium and zirconia, aiding in the healing process around the implant [27]. This noninvasive and safe method can modify implant surfaces without damaging healthy tissues, offering a significant advantage over traditional methods [32]. By improving the initial interaction between the implant surface and the surrounding tissue, CAP enhances the stability and long‐term effectiveness of dental implants [33]. Studies have demonstrated CAP's potential to inhibit bacterial activity, modify surface properties to reduce bacterial colonization, and promote tissue integration, making it a promising approach for improving the outcomes of dental implant therapies [34].

The extracellular matrix (ECM) also plays a critical role in enhancing the osseointegration of implants [35]. Coating implants with ECM components has emerged as a promising method to improve osseointegration [36]. The ECM, particularly type I collagen, which constitutes about 90% of the ECM, can be immobilized on implant surfaces to create an environment that promotes bone formation [37]. Collagen coatings enhance the bone‐to‐implant contact, facilitating osteoblast adhesion and promoting bone production. This is achieved through the interaction of collagen with integrin receptors, initiating the bone formation process [38]. Additionally, non‐collagenous ECM components, such as glycosaminoglycan chondroitin sulphate (CS), can be incorporated into the coatings. CS interacts with growth factors, modulating their activity and further aiding ossification [39]. Research has shown that implants coated with collagen result in higher bone‐implant contact compared to uncoated implants [40]. Incorporating non‐collagenous compounds and growth factors like BMP‐4 into collagen coatings could potentially enhance this effect, leading to increased bone volume and improved implant stability [41]. Thus, coating implants with ECM components, particularly collagen, can significantly benefit osseointegration by enhancing the initial stability and promoting sustained bone growth around the implant.

Polycaprolactone (PCL) is a biodegradable polyester that has gained significant attention in the field of biomedical engineering due to its mechanical properties, ease of processing, and biocompatibility [42]. Its slow degradation rate makes it particularly suitable for long‐term implants, allowing for gradual tissue integration and healing [43]. In bone tissue engineering, PCL scaffolds serve as a foundation for cell attachment and growth, providing structural support while the body regenerates bone tissue [44].

Combining various surface treatments on PCL scaffolds may act synergistically, presenting an innovative strategy to improve osseointegration. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate and compare the pro‐osteogenic potential of osteon‐like microstructures, cold atmospheric plasma treatment, and extracellular matrix protein coatings. Furthermore, it investigates the potential synergistic effects when these methods are combined. In this context, the study focuses on the comparative evaluation of functional biological outcomes resulting from synergistic surface modifications, rather than on detailed mechanistic elucidation of cell‐material interactions. Osteon‐like microtopography was selected as a reference in this study, based on previous research demonstrating its superior performance over blank PCL across various biological parameters, including cell adhesion, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation [26]. Building on this established baseline, the present work focuses on evaluating whether additional chemical and biochemical surface modification can further enhance cell behavior beyond this established baseline. By exploring these interactions, we seek to deepen our understanding of how surface modifications can be optimized to enhance osseointegration, ultimately improving the performance and longevity of biomedical implants. The outcomes of this research could significantly contribute to the advancement of bone tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, paving the way for the development of more effective and durable implant materials.

Results and Discussion

Structured and coated implants are of significant importance in biomedical engineering, with the aim of improving healing rates and reducing complication rates post‐surgery through various surface treatments of implants [45]. Previous studies have examined the influence of individual structures, materials, or separate coatings, demonstrating positive outcomes [46]. However, only a few data are available on the synergistic effect of different treatments. This study compares the effects of osteon‐like microtopography, cold atmospheric plasma treatment, extracellular matrix coatings, and their combination on osteogenic differentiation. Osteon‐like microtopography was selected as a reference due to previous findings demonstrating its superior performance over untreated PCL in promoting cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation [26]. The present analysis, therefore, focuses on relative and synergistic improvements beyond this optimized baseline.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) measurements were performed on three sample types: untreated, argon plasma‐treated, and ECM‐treated. Each sample exhibited distinct surface characteristics (Figure 1).

The untreated sample, scanned over a 10 µm ×10 µm area, displayed a relatively smooth surface with minimal topographical variation. The arithmetical mean height (Sa) was 34.95 nm, the root mean square height (Sq) was 46.23 nm, and the maximum height (Sz) reached 373.1 nm. No discernible micro‐ or nanostructures were observed, and the surface morphology reflected the baseline characteristics typical of untreated PCL.

In contrast, the argon plasma‐treated sample demonstrated notable alterations in surface morphology. The AFM revealed dot‐like topographical features, consistent with localized microstructural modifications induced by plasma exposure. The surface showed increased roughness, with a Sa of 47.74 nm, Sq of 59.25 nm, and Sz of 381.6 nm. These values indicate a moderate rise in surface irregularity compared to the untreated control. Argon plasma treatment is well known to enhance surface roughness and, consequently, improve cellular responses by promoting adhesion and proliferation, as documented in prior studies [47, 48].

The ECM‐treated sample displayed the most complex surface architecture. Surface roughness was reduced compared to the other groups with a Sa of 21.25 nm, Sq of 28.83 nm, and Sz of 247.9 nm. However, the sample featured distinct needle‐like structures (56–113 nm in diameter) and thread‐like structures (200–300 nm), suggestive of collagen fibers. These nanoscale features closely resemble natural extracellular matrix components, offering a biomimetic topography that facilitates cell attachment and growth [49].

Overall, AFM measurements illustrate differences in surface roughness and morphology among the sample groups. The complex surface topology after ECM coating offers diverse cellular cues, promoting adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [50]. Such topographical variations can enhance mechanical interlocking with cells, supporting integration and tissue formation [1]. These findings underscore the role of surface topography in designing biomaterials for tissue engineering, highlighting how surface modifications can impact biological responses.

Energy‐dispersive X‐ray (EDX) measurements were conducted to determine the elemental composition of osteon‐like microstructured PCL, CAP‐treated PCL, and ECM‐coated PCL surfaces (Figure 2). Microstructured PCL (Osteon‐like) was composed of 67.38% carbon and 32.62% oxygen, aligning with the molecular structure of PCL, which consists predominantly of carbon and oxygen atoms [51]. The high carbon content reflects the polymer backbone, while oxygen corresponds to the ester groups characteristic of PCL [51]. For CAP‐treated PCL, the composition was slightly altered to 68.24% carbon and 31.76% oxygen. This minimal difference suggests that CAP treatment primarily affects surface properties, such as wettability and surface energy, without significantly changing the elemental composition. CAP treatment, known for enhancing cell attachment and proliferation, achieves this by increasing surface roughness and hydrophilicity [32]. In contrast, ECM‐coated PCL exhibited a more diverse elemental profile with 51.11% carbon, 24.37% oxygen, 13.91% nitrogen, 6.81% sodium, 2.76% chloride, and 1.04% sulfur. The nitrogen and sulfur indicate ECM component deposition, as these elements are common in proteins and biomolecules present in the ECM, contributing to a biologically active surface that promotes cell proliferation and attachment [52]. The sodium and chloride likely originate from the buffer in the ECM mixture. The additional nitrogen and sulfur provide binding sites and biochemical cues essential for cell attachment and proliferation [53]. These findings confirm that surface modifications, such as ECM coating, can significantly alter surface chemistry to better mimic natural extracellular matrix conditions, supporting enhanced cell proliferation and attachment as observed in previous studies on ECM‐coated surfaces for tissue engineering [52, 54]. While EDX provides comparative compositional information confirming ECM deposition, it does not resolve specific chemical functional groups or surface charge states, which were therefore not assessed in the present study. Surface wettability plays a critical role in the success of bone implants by influencing biological interactions at the implant‐tissue interface (Figure 3). Increased wettability, typically achieved through surface modifications, enhances initial protein adsorption, supporting cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation [55]. This improved wettability fosters a bioactive layer that promotes bone cell activities, leading to enhanced integration of the implant with bone [56]. Moreover, surfaces with high wettability are more resistant to bacterial colonization, reducing infection risks and implant failure [57]. Thus, optimizing implant wettability is crucial for performance, longevity, and successful surgical outcomes. Contact angle measurements were used to assess the wettability of various PCL surface treatments. The osteon‐like microtopography displayed a contact angle of 79.7 ± 3.2°, indicating moderate hydrophobicity. Compared to Osteon‐like microtopography, CAP treatment reduced the contact angle to 50.6 ± 2.5°, increasing surface hydrophilicity by altering surface energy to favor protein adsorption and subsequent cell interactions [58]. The ECM‐treated surface exhibited an even lower contact angle of 40.9 ± 2.2°, compared to osteon‐like microtopography and CAP treatment, reflecting further enhanced hydrophilicity due to the presence of extracellular matrix components like proteins and glycoproteins, which create a more bioactive interface for cell proliferation [59]. Combining all three treatments resulted in the lowest contact angle, 20.6 ± 4.2°, indicating a highly hydrophilic surface. This pronounced increase in wettability is likely the result of a synergistic interplay between hierarchical surface topography and chemical surface modification. While CAP treatment and ECM coating independently enhance surface hydrophilicity through chemical and biochemical cues, the microstructured surface may further amplify this effect by increasing the effective surface area and promoting capillary wetting. Together, these combined morphological and chemical contributions lead to a highly hydrophilic surface that is expected to favor protein adsorption, cell spreading, and focal adhesion formation, all of which are critical for effective osseointegration.

Cell adhesion measurements were conducted to evaluate the number of human mesenchymal stromal cells (hMSCs) adhering to PCL surfaces subjected to different treatments (Figure 4). Osteon‐like microtopography supported an average of 23 ± 8 cells per image section, CAP treatment supported 13 ± 4 cells, ECM treatment supported 17 ± 4 cells, and the combination of all three treatments yielded the highest adhesion at 33 ± 9 cells per image section. In this study, the osteon‐like microstructured PCL served as the reference condition, as this bioinspired topography has previously been shown to outperform unstructured PCL substrates in terms of cell attachment [26]. The osteon‐like microtopography, relative to CAP treatment and ECM coating, enhanced cell attachment by mimicking the natural bone structure, providing physical cues that facilitate focal adhesion formation, a key process for cell anchorage and subsequent cellular function [26]. These microtopographic features are instrumental in promoting cell behavior conducive to osseointegration. Although CAP treatment and ECM coating increased surface wettability, early cell adhesion was not governed by wettability alone, indicating that topographical cues can dominate initial cell‐surface interactions. The combination treatment, with the highest adhesion (33 ± 9 cells per section), highlighted the synergistic effects of integrating osteon‐like microtopography, CAP treatment, and ECM coating. The topography provides structural cues for cell attachment, CAP treatment enhances surface hydrophilicity and energy for better protein adsorption, and ECM coating contributes biochemical signals that promote cell proliferation and attachment [26, 60, 61]. Only when these cues were combined did increased wettability translate into significantly enhanced cell adhesion. Interestingly, ECM‐treated samples showed improved adhesion with a contact angle of 30°, differing from Dowling et al.’s reported peak adhesion at approximately 64° [62]. Cell adhesion depends on receptor binding to surface proteins, which are secreted by cells and adhere to the substrate [63]. Xu et al. suggested protein adhesion peaks at around 60°, indicating that adhesion varies with incubation time and protein secretion [64]. These discrepancies further support the notion that optimal cell adhesion depends on a complex interplay of surface chemistry, topography, and other factors rather than on wettability alone. Future studies with extended incubation could further clarify whether ECM treatment consistently outperforms plasma‐treated surfaces in cell adhesion [65]. The findings underscore the importance of combining topographical, chemical, and biochemical surface modifications to optimize biomaterials for clinical applications. This integrated approach enhances cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, providing a comprehensive strategy for improving osseointegration and the biological performance of biomaterials.

Cell proliferation was evaluated using XTT absorbance measurements over a 10‐day period on PCL surfaces treated with osteon‐like microtopography, CAP, ECM, and their combination (Figure 5). On day 4, the combination treatment showed the highest absorbance (0.1288 ± 0.0021), indicating superior cell proliferation compared to ECM‐treated (0.1188 ± 0.0067), CAP‐treated (0.1200 ± 0.0085), and osteon‐like microtopography‐treated surfaces (0.1145 ± 0.0094). The ECM's bioactive molecules likely played a key role in promoting early cell growth [66]. By day 7, the trend continued, with the combination treatment again achieving the highest absorbance (0.1510 ± 0.0047), followed by ECM‐treated (0.1383 ± 0.0068), CAP‐treated (0.1353 ± 0.0048), and osteon‐like microtopography‐treated surfaces (0.1218 ± 0.0056). This sustained increase suggests that the combination treatment provides a more conducive environment for ongoing cell proliferation. The enhanced hydrophilicity and surface energy introduced by CAP treatment also contributed to improved proliferation rates [67]. On day 10, the combination treatment maintained the highest proliferation rate (0.1885 ± 0.0058), with ECM‐treated surfaces closely following (0.1858 ± 0.0124). CAP‐treated (0.1673 ± 0.0159) and osteon‐like microtopography‐treated surfaces (0.1452 ± 0.0118) showed comparatively lower absorbance values. The consistent superiority of the combination treatment highlights its synergistic effect, integrating structural, chemical, and biochemical modifications to optimize cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation. The findings align with prior research, such as that by Arredondo et al., which demonstrated the positive impact of protein‐treated polymer surfaces on hMSC proliferation [68]. This study emphasizes the value of combining treatments to create biomaterials that enhance osseointegration, improving clinical outcomes through better cell‐material interactions.

The impact of various surface treatments on osteogenic differentiation was evaluated using Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) activity and Alizarin Red staining. ALP activity, a key osteogenic differentiation marker [69], showed distinct trends over 7 and 14 days (Figure 6). On day 7, CAP treatment exhibited the highest ALP activity (19.5822 ± 2.3476 µmol/L/min), followed by ECM treatment (18.5346 ± 2.6247 µmol/L/min), osteon‐like microtopography (15.1009 ± 0.5279 µmol/L/min), and the combined treatment (13.3227 ± 0.4918 µmol/L/min). By day 14, ALP activity increased significantly across all groups, with osteon‐like microtopography showing the highest activity (99.9561 ± 4.5974 µmol/L/min), followed by CAP (93.8737 ± 8.9774 µmol/L/min), the combined treatment (89.0923 ± 5.7048 µmol/L/min), and ECM (78.5521 ± 4.0251 µmol/L/min). This time‐dependent increase in ALP activity indicates progressive osteogenic differentiation across all surfaces, with CAP treatment and osteon‐like microtopography particularly effective in promoting early osteogenic activity, likely due to their influence on surface chemistry and microtopographical cues [19, 70]. Alizarin Red staining, used to quantify calcium deposition as a marker of mineralization, highlighted the benefits of the combination treatment (Figure 7). On day 7, the combination treatment had the highest staining intensity (0.1046 ± 0.0010), followed by ECM (0.0991 ± 0.0017), CAP (0.0926 ± 0.0024), and osteon‐like microtopography (0.0890 ± 0.0019). By day 14, the combined treatment again demonstrated the highest intensity (0.7830 ± 0.0244), outperforming CAP (0.7085 ± 0.1451), ECM (0.3982 ± 0.0185), and osteon‐like microtopography (0.3273 ± 0.0037). Since mineralization was assessed up to day 14, these values represent the strongest mineral deposition observed within the investigated time window rather than the absolute temporal maximum. Osteogenic differentiation is a dynamic process in which early markers such as ALP rise and subsequently decline as matrix mineralization progresses. In this context, the elevated ALP activity accompanied by emerging mineral deposition observed for CAP treatment may reflect an earlier or intermediate stage of osteogenic differentiation, whereas the comparatively lower ALP activity combined with substantially enhanced mineralization on the combined treatment is consistent with a more advanced stage of osteogenic maturation at the assessed time point. The CAP treatment's effectiveness, especially at day 14, may be attributed to increased reactive oxygen species and surface energy modifications that enhance cell differentiation, consistent with Eggers et al.’s findings [71].

While CAP treatment appears to predominantly stimulate early osteogenic differentiation, ECM coating contributes to enhanced cell proliferation and supports both differentiation and mineralization. Importantly, only the combined treatment consistently translated the individual effects into markedly enhanced mineral deposition. These findings suggest that each treatment uniquely supports osteogenic differentiation, with the combination treatment modulating the kinetics of differentiation and mineralization to yield the most advanced osteogenic phenotype within the investigated time frame, underscoring its potential for biomaterial applications.

The present study was designed as a comparative, application‐oriented in vitro evaluation of synergistic surface modification strategies and is therefore subject to several limitations. Surface characterization was limited to topographical analysis and comparative elemental composition, and did not include detailed chemical functional group or surface charge analyses. Protein adsorption assays were not performed, as direct comparison across all surface conditions would be confounded by the presence of pre‐adsorbed ECM proteins on ECM‐coated and combined substrates. Furthermore, biological assessments were restricted to functional outcomes, including cell attachment, proliferation, and osteogenic differentiation, without investigation of focal adhesion formation, cytoskeletal organization, or molecular osteogenic markers at the gene or protein level. Finally, in vivo validation of osseointegration was beyond the scope of the present work. Future studies will focus on detailed surface chemical characterization, mechanistic analysis of cell‐material interactions, and in vivo evaluation to further elucidate the pathways underlying the observed biological responses and to assess translational potential.

Conclusion

The data collectively demonstrate that each surface modification contributes distinct and complementary effects to osseointegraton. Osteon‐like microtopography provides a biomimetic structural baseline that supports early cell attachment. CAP treatment primarily enhances early osteogenic differentiation, as reflected by elevated ALP activity, while ECM coating supports cell proliferation in a time‐dependent manner, with pronounced effects at later culture stages. Importantly, the combined treatment consistently matches or exceeds the best‐performing individual modification across all biological readouts, including cell attachment, proliferation, and mineralization. Taken together, the biological outcomes suggest that while individual treatments preferentially influence specific stages of cell response, their combination promotes a more advanced osteogenic maturation state at the assessed time point. This integrated approach leverages synergistic topographical, chemical, and biochemical cues to create an optimized PCL surface with strong potential to enhance osseointegration and improve clinical implant performance.

These conclusions are drawn from comparative analyses relative to osteon‐like microstructured PCL, which served as a biologically validated reference surface based on prior studies.

Experimental Section/Methods

Microstructured PCL Design and Manufacturing

Osteon‐like microstructures were created to be investigated for their role in influencing pro‐osteogenic differentiation potential of MSCs compared to cold atmospheric plasma treated, ECM treated, and combination of the aforementioned.

In this study, the term osteon‐like refers to a bioinspired surface architecture that abstracts selected organizational features of cortical bone osteons rather than replicating their full anatomical complexity. The design comprises concentric circular grooves (1 µm width, 5 µm spacing) intended to emulate the radial lamellar arrangement characteristic of osteons at a simplified microscale. In addition, hemispherical pits with a diameter of 5 µm were incorporated as lacunae‐like features to provide localized cell‐interaction sites. While these elements do not reproduce the native dimensions, they capture key spatial and hierarchical cues of osteonal organization, thereby justifying the use of the term osteon‐like.

The patterned substrates were fabricated using a multi‐step replication process to overcome the mechanical fragility of the two‐photon‐polymerized master. First, the osteon‐like microstructure was designed as a negative (mold) in OpenSCAD and fabricated by multiphoton lithography using an UpBrix photoresin (Upbrix, UpNano GmbH, Vienna, Austria) and a NanoOne printer (UpNano GmbH, Vienna Austria). As the resulting UpBrix mold was brittle and unsuitable for repeated demolding, it was used only once to generate a polydimethylsiloxane positive (PDMS, SYLGARD 184 Silicone Elastomer kit, Dow Corning, France).

From this PDMS positive, an epoxy (Epoxy Resin Crystal Clear Kit, SigWong, China) mold was produced, which accurately preserved the microfeatures but exhibited poor demolding properties. To enable robust replication, this epoxy mold was therefore used to cast an intermediate poly‐ε‐caprolactone (Mw = 80.000, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) positive. A final PDMS mold was fabricated from this PCL intermediate and used for all subsequent sample production.

For the preparation of the substrates, PCL (Mw = 37.000, Polysciences, Inc., Warrington, USA) was melted into the PDMS mold at 120°C for 30 min and solidified at −20°C for 90 min. Non‐structured PCL substrates were fabricated following the same replication and casting procedure, using unpatterned molds, and were used for cold‐atmospheric plasma treatment and extracellular matrix protein coating.

Cold Atmospheric Plasma Treatment

An argon plasma was generated using a radio frequency plasma generator (PlasmaTact 15, Adtec Europe Ltd, Twickenham, UK) at an argon flow of 4.9 slm mbar, and 7.5 W. Subsequently, non‐microstructured samples were positioned under the generated plasma beam at a distance of two cm and treated for 5 min. After the time had elapsed, the samples were fixed in a 24‐well plate and cultured with cells the following day.

Preparation of ECM

The details of the procedure have been previously reported by Rohringer et al. [73]. In summary, placental tissues collected from term Caesarean sections (37/0–40/0 weeks gestation) with informed maternal consent and institutional ethics committee approval (EK:1602/2018) were utilized. After removing the amnion and umbilical cord, the chorion layer was separated from the basal tissue, frozen at −80°C for at least 24 h, and prepared for decellularization. The decellularization involved treating pooled chorionic tissue from eight placentas with 2% Triton X‐100 (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) and 0.02% EDTA (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) across three days, each day followed by a 1x PBS rinse. To ensure complete DNA removal, an overnight treatment with 0.04% DNase (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) was applied after intensive PBS washing. Sterilization was achieved using 0.18% peracetic acid in 4.8% ethanol (both Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) for 2.5 h, followed by freezing, lyophilizing, and cryomilling to create a powdered form. All steps after sterilization were conducted under sterile conditions. The resulting chorionic powder was digested in acidic conditions (HCl – 0.1 м; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) using porcine gastric mucosa pepsin (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), with digestion occurring over 48 h at room temperature. Finally, the gel was neutralized (NaOH – 1 м; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) and salt concentration adjusted to 1x with 10x PBS, yielding a chorion‐based extracellular matrix suitable for further use.

Extracellular Matrix Coating

The existing 2% ECM solution was diluted to 0.2% with phosphate‐buffered saline. The non‐microstructured samples were fixed in a 24‐well plate and covered with 1 mL each of the diluted ECM solution. Subsequently, the samples were incubated on a shaker at room temperature for 2 h. The liquid present in the well was then not removed, and the samples were dried overnight at room temperature.

Combination Treatment

This sample underwent a sequential treatment process: (i) structuring with the osteon‐like microtopography, (ii) cold atmospheric plasma treatment, and (iii) ECM coating. The rationale for this combined approach was to integrate the physical cues of osteon‐like topography, the surface activation properties of CAP, and the bioactive signals of ECM to optimize osseointegration.

Morphological Evaluation of the Surface: Atomic Force Microscopy

Using an optical microscope (Leica, Leica DM2700M, Wetzlar, Germany) and a 3D profilometer (Keyence, VR‐5000, Mechelen, Belgium), the samples were pre‐analysed to determine the surface texture and topography. This also allowed the identification of corresponding points and patterns on the surface to guide the AFM measurements. The samples were cut to size for the AFM measurements and prepared for measurement on a substrate holder. The AFM (Nanosurf NaioAFM, Liestal, Switzerland) was first used to take an overview image of all the samples in a 50 µm × 50 µm measurement area, using the orientation points as a guide. Areas of interest were then identified, and close‐up images (down to 2 µm × 2 µm) were taken to measure the needle and thread‐like structures, particularly their diameters. The software used was Gwyddion, a modular program for the visualization and analysis of data from scanning probe microscopy. All measurements were made in triplicates and statistically averaged.

Dispersive X‐ray Spectroscopy

The samples were coated with a thin layer of gold using a sputter coater (Q150R ES, Quorum, Edinburgh, UK) before being mounted on the SEM (EVO MA10, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). All SEM images were captured at a magnification of 2000x. Quantitative elemental analysis via EDX was conducted to evaluate and compare the atomic percentages of carbon (C), oxygen (O), nitrogen (N), sodium (Na), chloride (Cl), and phosphorus (P). Each element was identified by its characteristic wavelength on the x‐axis, shown as a peak, with the peak intensity displayed on the y‐axis.

Surface Wettability: Water Contact Angle Measurement

The wettability properties of microstructures were assessed by measuring the contact angle of a water droplet on the respective microstructured surface, using a contact angle goniometer (DSA25E, Krüss, Hamburg, Germany) via the sessile drop method with a 2 µL distilled water droplet. The average contact angle and standard deviation were calculated based on measurements of five different discs for each microtopography, as well as the non‐structured negative control.

Primary Human Mesenchymal Stem Cell Harvesting

In total, human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) isolated from spongiosa samples of ten donors were obtained from the ViBiMeD biobank of the Department of Orthopedics and Trauma Surgery (EK‐No. 1822/2017). This biobank includes clinical waste tissue from patients undergoing surgery at the department. All patients had donated their waste tissues for scientific research with written informed consent. The ethics committee of the Medical University of Vienna approved the use of the specimens for the present study (EK‐No. 1138/2024).

Cell Culture

Isolated primary hMSCs from the ten donors were pooled, with equal cell count for each patient, and cultured in α‐MEM (Gibco, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), containing 5% human platelet lysate fibrinogen depleted (hPLFD, ELAREM, PLBioScience GmbH, Aachen, Germany), and 1% Anti‐Anti (Antibiotic‐Antimycotic, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37°C. Upon reaching 80% confluency, hMSCs were passaged and used for biocompatibility tests. For assays measuring differentiation potential and mineralization capability, culturing media were replaced with osteogenic differentiation media comprising 200 mм β‐glycerol (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), 100 mм ascorbic acid (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA), and 1 mм dexamethasone (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) in α‐MEM full medium (+1% Anti‐Anti, 5% hPLFD).

Cell Attachment Assay

Initially, hMSCs were resuspended in media, and the concentration was adjusted to 100 000 cells mL−1. The structured and treated PCL samples underwent a triple cleaning process in a 70% ethanol ultrasonic bath for 5 min. Then, the samples were immobilized in a 24‐well plate. Each sample was covered with 1 mL of cell suspension and underwent a 2‐h incubation period at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Afterward, the samples underwent an upside‐down centrifugation at 60 g for five min at room temperature, followed by a three min washing step with 1xPBS. Subsequently, 600 µL of a 1% glutaraldehyde‐PBS solution (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) were applied to each sample and incubated for 15 min. The 1% glutaraldehyde‐PBS solution was removed, and

600 µL of a 0.1% crystal violet solution (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) were added to the samples, shaken, and incubated for 25 min. Following this incubation period, the unbound dye was removed using deionized water.

The previously centrifuged, fixed, and stained samples were analyzed with a Keyence VHX‐7000 digital microscope (Keyence International, Mechelen, Belgium) at 500‐fold magnification. This involved capturing five images at various locations for each sample. Subsequently, the cell count of the stained samples was assessed using Image J.

Cell adhesion was evaluated at 2 h to selectively assess early cell‐substrate attachment, as centrifugation‐based adhesion assays are known to lose sensitivity at longer adhesion times due to progressive adhesion strengthening [74].

Cell Proliferation Evaluation: XTT Viability Assay

Cell viability and proliferation were assessed using the XTT Cell Viability Assay (CyQUANT, Invitrogen, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) adapted from the manufacturer's instructions to a 24‐well format. In short, three samples per treatment were immobilized in a 24‐well plate, and 1000 cells were seeded on each sample in a volume of 0.5 mL (2000 cells mL−1). The cells were incubated for 4, 7, and 10 days at 37°C. Three cell‐free wells were used as controls. On the corresponding days, 350 µL of freshly prepared tetrazolium‐based dye solution was added to each well. After 4 h of incubation, viability was quantified in terms of absorbance, measured at 450 and 660 nm (reference wavelength) with a Spark Multimode microplate reader (Tecan group Ltd., Maennedorf, Switzerland). Alkaline Phosophatase (ALP) Activity

ALP activity was measured using Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) according to the manufacturers protocol. Briefly, three samples per treatment were immobilized in a 24‐well plate, and 1000 cells were seeded on each sample in a volume of 0.5 mL (2000 cells mL−1). The cells were then incubated in osteogenic differentiation medium for 7 and 14 days, respectively, at 37°C. At the corresponding days, cells were washed with 1x PBS and lysed with 0.2% Triton X‐100 (SERVA Electrophoresis GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) by shaking for 20 min at room temperature. 50 µL of the sample was transferred into a clear, flat‐bottom 96‐well plate. The working solution was prepared by mixing assay buffer with Mg acetate (0.2 м), and p‐nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP, 1 м) at a ratio of 200:5:2, at the required volume. 150 µL of this working reagent was added to each well of the 96‐well plate with the sample. Additionally, 200 µL of purified water and 200 µL of calibrator were added in triplicates to separate wells of the 96‐well plate. The plate was tapped briefly to mix the solutions. Absorbance was read in 4 min intervals at a wavelength of 405 nm. ALP activity was calculated as given in the manual of the assay.

Alizarin Red Staining

To detect calcification ARS was performed. Therefore, three samples each were immobilized in a 24‐well plate, and 5 × 10^4 were seeded onto each sample in a volume of 0.5 mL (1 × 10^5 cells/mL). The cells were incubated in osteogenic differentiation medium for 7 and 14 days, respectively, at 37°C. At the corresponding days, cells were washed with 1x PBS and fixed with 7.5% formaldehyde (SAV Liquid Production GmbH, Flintsbach am Inn, Germany) for 30 min. Followed by another washing step and stained by alizarin red staining solution for 30 mins, in the dark, at room temperature. The staining solution was removed, and the samples thoroughly rinsed and stored dry until imaging. Imaging was processed using a Keyence VHX‐7000 digital microscope (Keyence International, Mechelen, Belgium). For quantification, 500 µL of 100 mм Cetylperidinium chloride (CPC, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 2 h. The solution was transferred into a 96‐well plate and measured at 405 nm with a Spark Multimode microplate reader (Tecan group Ltd., Maennedorf, Switzerland).

Statistical Analysis

For statistical analysis and visual presentation of data, GraphPad Prism (V10, GraphPad Software, Boston, USA) was used. The statistical analysis encompassed the centrifugation adhesion test and contact angle measurements, utilizing one‐way ANOVA. The XTT assay, ALP assay, and Alizarin Red quantification were subjected to a two‐way ANOVA. Following ANOVA calculations, post hoc Bonferroni‐corrected t‐tests were performed between individual groups. This correction aimed to mitigate Type I error resulting from multiple testing without excessively diminishing test power. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Mean values with standard deviation are depicted in the graphs.
