Does osteogenesis imperfecta predispose infants to metaphyseal fractures? A systematic review
Authors: Ella Riley, Michael Paddock, Sara Elgarwany, Joanna Fairhurst, Kath Halliday, Annmarie Jeanes, Karl Johnson, Amaka C Offiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe notion that osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) predisposes children to metaphyseal fractures is controversial. This review aimed to ascertain the prevalence of metaphyseal fractures in children with OI less than 2 years of age.Materials and methodsMedline, CINAHL and Web of Science were searched for studies reporting metaphyseal fractures in children with OI less than 2 years of age. Articles not retrievable, not published in English, or where ‘metaphyseal’ fracture was not defined/illustrated, were excluded. Article quality was independently assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme and Case Reports tools.ResultsOf 298 retrieved abstracts, 82 were duplicates: 216 articles were screened by title and abstract, excluding 186. Hand search of the remaining 30 articles identified two further articles. Only four articles were included: (1) 4/41 children with OI aged between 1 day and 3 years with ‘metaphyseal’ fractures; (2) a case series including a 16-month-old boy with three ‘metaphyseal’ fractures. Abuse concerns remained despite a type IV OI diagnosis (not genetically confirmed); (3) a 7-month-old girl with two ‘corner’ fractures suspected to have OI and to have been abused; and (4) a pair of siblings with ‘metaphyseal’ fractures and type XI OI (FKBP10 mutation).ConclusionsThere is limited evidence that OI predisposes infants and young children to metaphyseal fractures. The two diagnoses are not mutually exclusive and until further large studies are conducted, physical abuse should always be considered in infants and young children with metaphyseal fractures, even in those with confirmed OI.Keywords: Child Abuse, Infant, Child Health

Introduction

Osteogenesis imperfecta (OI) is an inherited disorder of connective tissue defined by fragile bones which fracture following minimal trauma.1 It is a rare disorder with an estimated incidence of around 1 per 20 000, and varies clinically from mild to lethal.2 Typical features include short stature, long bone, spine and skull deformities, dentinogenesis imperfecta and blue or grey sclerae.2 Most cases result from a mutation of the COL1A1 or COL1A2 genes and the inheritance pattern can be autosomal dominant, recessive or X-linked.1 There are many types of OI with additional variants continuing to be identified.1 Types I–IV are the most frequent: types II and III are the most severe, and type IV is most often confused with inflicted injury.1 3 The management of patients with OI requires multidisciplinary team input from several paediatric, medical and surgical specialists supported by allied health professionals, such as physiotherapists and occupational therapists. Bisphosphonate therapy remains the cornerstone of medical management to improve bone fragility, whereas patients with more severe OI may require orthopaedic intervention such as placement of intramedullary rods.1

Following cutaneous injury (bruising), fractures are the second most common manifestation of physical abuse in infants and young children, with the highest incidence in the first year of life.3 4 Metaphyseal fractures are highly specific for physical abuse and are also known as classic metaphyseal lesions (CMLs), and metaphyseal ‘corner’ or ‘bucket-handle’ fractures due to their appearance on tangential and angled radiographic projections, respectively.5 They are most often visualised in the tibiae, femora and humeri6 and are thought to result from gripping, pulling and twisting of the limb resulting in separation of the primary and secondary spongiosa of the metaphysis through a series of microfractures.4 6 Aside from inflicted injury, these fractures can sometimes occur in infants during a difficult birth in which a significant application of force was applied during extraction.5 Metadiaphyseal fractures are commonly referred to as ‘metaphyseal’ fractures; however, they are a distinct, separate entity. These fractures, which occur further away from the physis up to and including the junction of the metaphysis and diaphysis of the bone, and (like diaphyseal fractures) are common injuries with low specificity for inflicted injury.5 As such, ‘classic metaphyseal’ and ‘metadiaphyseal’ fractures should be clearly distinguished. Throughout this review, we used the term ‘metaphyseal’ fracture to refer explicitly to CMLs, that is, those fractures highly associated with inflicted injury.

Distinguishing between OI and child abuse in infants can be challenging given that children with OI experience multiple low energy fractures in the course of normal day-to-day handling without a clear history of trauma.7 While OI may be suggested as the cause of metaphyseal fractures in infants and young children with unexplained fractures, this is debated.8 As such, we sought to identify the published prevalence of metaphyseal fractures in patients with OI in the first 2 years of life.

Materials and methods

In April 2025, Medline, CINAHL and Web of Science were systematically searched from 1946, 1900 and 1982, respectively. No time limit was selected. The search strategy is outlined in online supplemental material 1.

Published studies of children with OI aged less than 2 years of age reporting the presence and number of classic metaphyseal fractures were eligible for inclusion. Articles not able to be retrieved, not published in English, or where the authors neither defined nor illustrated their meaning of ‘metaphyseal’ fracture were excluded. Studies concerning ‘brittle bone disease’ were excluded as this is not a recognised condition.5

One author (ER) searched for and evaluated all abstracts and full articles. The quality of the included articles was independently determined by three authors (a medical student and two paediatric radiology consultants with 4 and 22 years of experience, respectively) using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP)9 and Case Reports (CARE)10 tools. All published radiographs of ‘metaphyseal’ fractures illustrated in the final included articles were independently reviewed by seven paediatric radiology consultants with experience ranging from 4 to 31 years’, of which five act (or have previously acted) as expert witnesses in cases of suspected child abuse. Accepting that this did not represent clinical practice, radiologists were asked to: (1) score image quality as: non-diagnostic; diagnostic, but inferior to clinical practice; or diagnostic, similar to clinical practice; (2) whether they agreed, did not agree or were unsure if a metaphyseal fracture was present, and if so, its location and (3) whether they agreed that the illustrated case had the radiographic appearances consistent with a diagnosis of OI. All radiologists were blinded to the scoring of others. For an image to be deemed of diagnostic quality or demonstrate a metaphyseal fracture, at least five of the seven reviewers had to have scored it as such.

A meta-analysis could not be performed due to the limited number of heterogeneous articles retrieved. Descriptive statistics were used to analyse the data using Microsoft Excel for Mac, version 16.57 (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this systematic review.

A cohort study of 41 children with OI with a total of 316 radiographic examinations reviewed. The author defined ‘metaphyseal’ fractures as ‘a small flake detached near the epiphysial line’ and included two radiographs (figure 2).

In seven patients, ‘small metaphyseal’ fractures were reported similar to those associated with inflicted injury, age range 1 day to 3 years. Patient sex was not reported. All exhibited clear ‘obvious generalised skeletal disease’ ie, osteoporosis with abnormally modelled bones and ‘major fractures’ but neither the OI type nor fracture definition was stated. The number of ‘metaphyseal’ fractures ranged from one to four, and Wormian bones were reported in all seven patients. In 6/7 patients (one patient had no follow-up), further fractures were reported after the diagnosis of OI, although no other ‘metaphyseal’ fractures were reported.

There are several limitations of Astley’s article. It is assumed that as sole author, he reviewed all radiographs. The seven patients with ‘metaphyseal’ fractures did not have OI confirmed genetically (this article was published in 1979); the diagnosis was based on the radiographic features of osteoporosis, Wormian bones, abnormal bone modelling and ‘major fractures’. Moreover, imaging equipment, image quality and diagnostic accuracy has advanced significantly since this article was published. Multiple conditions can present in a similar fashion to OI, such as hypophosphatasia, hyperphosphatasia or panostotic fibrous dysplasia.37 Wormian bones are not specific to OI and may be considered a normal anatomical variant: they are only considered abnormal when there are more than ten present.38 The author states that ‘there was obvious generalised bone disease so that confusion with non-accidental injury did not occur’. However, the two are not mutually exclusive and children with OI may still experience (all forms of) abuse; thus, this is a significant limitation of the paper.39 No birth history was provided for any patient which would have been particularly relevant for the four patients aged between 1 day and 1 month of age given that birth trauma has been reported to result in metaphyseal fractures.5

Following an independent review, the image quality in this article was insufficient to reliably identify or exclude metaphyseal fractures (table 3).

Gahagan et al

A case series of three patients with OI and fractures that were initially diagnosed as abusive. Of three patients, only one (a 16-month-old boy) had a left distal tibial ‘metaphyseal’ fracture reported to occur after falling down five steps while being held by a babysitter and a previous distal right tibial fracture, reported to occur after falling down a step with the child’s mother. A skeletal survey revealed a healing fracture of the left distal radius and healing fractures of the right distal tibial and fibular metaphyses.

The child had no reported bruises or blue sclerae, but a 4-year-old sibling had been diagnosed with OI. A skin biopsy was sent for collagen analysis which revealed an abnormal population of type 1 procollagen. A diagnosis of type IV OI was subsequently made but no confirmatory genetic testing was performed. The mother hired a new babysitter and there were no further fractures after 19 months. Despite the diagnosis of OI, there remained concerns that the metaphyseal fracture resulted from abuse.

Following an independent review, the image quality in this article was insufficient to reliably identify or exclude metaphyseal fractures (table 3).

Nieke et al

A case report of a 7-month-old girl who presented with left-sided parietal swelling. The mother claimed the infant had fallen from her arms 4 days earlier. A head CT scan revealed three parietal skull fractures at different stages of healing (one acute and two older fractures). A skeletal survey identified four additional fractures, including bilateral tibial ‘corner fractures’ and an older right femoral ‘metaphyseal fracture’. The infant had grey/blue sclerae and was short with macrocephaly. Genetic testing was not available. While the case was reported to the local police, it was eventually closed with no conviction. Notably, the fractures were felt to be attributed to abuse.

Following an independent review, although the images were felt to be of diagnostic quality by at least five reviewers, consensus was not reached regarding the presence of metaphyseal fractures (table 3).

Bobyn et al

A case report of a pair of siblings with multiple ‘metaphyseal corner’ fractures. The older sibling (a 15-month-old boy) was investigated after his primary care physician observed that he had asymmetrically aligned shoulders. On examination, he had short stature, slightly blue sclerae, lower limb hyper-flexibility, flat feet and kyphosis. The birth history was unremarkable with no positive family history of OI or multiple fractures. There was a history of a splinted left wrist contracture. Initial radiographs demonstrated multiple posterior rib fractures. The subsequent skeletal survey identified a left tibial ‘metaphyseal corner’ fracture, bilateral ulnar fractures and multiple Wormian bones. Genetic testing revealed an FKBP10 pathogenic mutation, known to cause OI type XI (Bruck syndrome). The sibling was tested and found to carry the same variant. A skeletal survey obtained shortly after an uneventful birth showed posterior rib fractures, bilateral ‘metaphyseal corner’ fractures and a left ulnar fracture.

Following an independent review, one of three included radiographs (figure 3) was felt to be of diagnostic quality by at least five of the reviewers, with at least five reviewers agreeing that the image demonstrated a metaphyseal fracture (table 3).

Discussion

This systematic review identified four articles in which the authors defined and reported ‘metaphyseal’ fractures in children aged less than 2 years with OI. The included articles published eight radiographs with nine ‘metaphyseal’ fractures. Only 3/8 radiographs were deemed to be of diagnostic quality, of which only one reached consensus for the presence of a CML (table 3). The four articles were deemed to be low or intermediate quality when assessed using the CASP and CARE tools (table 2).

As fractures are common to both OI and abuse, fracture pattern and type may be helpful in distinguishing between the two.5 There is limited evidence on how to differentiate OI from inflicted injury as the pattern of fractures can be similar in both, including fractures at different stages of healing, or fractures in non-ambulatory infants.3 The situation is further complicated by the fact that OI and inflicted injury are not mutually exclusive diagnoses.39 While the significant emotional toll on the family unit is recognised when an infant or young child is suspected of having been physically abused, clinicians are duty bound to investigate when such suspicions arise or are reported. Therefore, evidence is needed with regards to whether metaphyseal fractures, injuries considered highly specific for physical abuse, also occur in OI. Anecdotally, the senior author of this manuscript, who has (on a clinical basis) reviewed the images of over 500 children with OI spanning 14 years, could not recall identifying a single classic metaphyseal fracture, as opposed to metadiaphyseal fractures and metaphyseal spurs which were identified and differ in both morphology and causation from classic metaphyseal fractures.

This systematic review has shown a paucity of evidence, with only four eligible articles identified with poor quality images deemed not to be diagnostic of classic metaphyseal fractures, except for the article by Bobyn et al (table 3) which reports siblings with Bruck syndrome.14 This recessive form of OI is associated with severe bone fragility and congenital joint contractures40 which may predispose infants and children to fractures when their arms and legs are straightened during everyday activities and normal handling (dressing, bathing, or changing nappies). More research in this subgroup of OI patients is required.

Whereas Bobyn et al identified 78 children with OI and ‘metaphyseal fractures’ in 14 eligible studies, we only identified 11 children in four studies. This discrepancy can be accounted for by our strict inclusion criteria requiring authors to have either defined or illustrated what was meant by ‘metaphyseal’ fracture. While this reduced the number of eligible papers, we felt that this was central to the research question, since the term ‘metaphyseal’ fracture is often used synonymously with metadiaphyseal fractures by authors. An article by Peddada et al reported that ‘metaphyseal’ fractures were more common in children without OI (33%) compared with children with OI (12%).27 The article did not define ‘metaphyseal’ fracture and so there may be evidence of metaphyseal fractures in children with OI, although sparse. Classic metaphyseal fractures traverse the metaphysis parallel to the physis, resulting from specific forces and are highly associated with physical abuse. ‘Metadiaphyseal’ fractures typically involve both the diaphysis and metaphysis and are common following accidental trauma (figure 4). We believe a more appropriate term when describing the fractures highly associated with inflicted injury would be ‘classic metaphyseal fracture’ as ‘classic metaphyseal lesion’ fails to convey the injury is a fracture (resulting from trauma).

A large retrospective review of radiographs of children with OI is required to ascertain true prevalence. Given that children with OI may be abused, it could be argued that if OI predisposes to metaphyseal fractures, then the prevalence in children with OI should be higher than its prevalence in the general population.

This study is significantly limited by the small number of low-quality articles retrieved. As such, a meta-analysis could not be performed. It is perhaps not surprising that the images were rated of poor quality (table 3) given that they were read from the published articles rather than the original analogue or Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) images. We did not reach out to the authors to obtain the original analogue radiographs from the two papers over 30 years old, and it would be highly unlikely that the images would be able to be obtained. Furthermore, we were unable to reach consensus as to whether the images demonstrated a metaphyseal fracture, which is a limitation of this review. This lack of agreement is due to the poor image quality and poor study reporting conditions, for example, limited radiographic projections, lack of follow-up imaging to assess evolution and absence of contralateral projections for comparison which further hindered imaging assessment. These circumstances do not reflect clinical practice and underscore the responsibility of journal editors to ensure that published images adequately reflect the pathology they purport to reflect. As such, we believe that we have not been able to conclusively answer the question we set ourselves.

Conclusions

There is limited, poor-quality evidence to support the hypothesis that OI predisposes infants and young children to classic metaphyseal fractures; as such, we have been unable to ascertain the prevalence of these injuries in infants and young children with OI. This systematic review yielded only one convincing case of a ‘metaphyseal’ fracture in a child with Bruck syndrome. In current clinical practice, investigations surrounding suspected physical abuse are often ceased if a diagnosis of OI is confirmed. However, given the current level of evidence, and until more robust data are available through large observational and/or finite elemental analysis studies, any classic metaphyseal fracture in any infant or young child should be thoroughly and appropriately investigated to exclude inflicted injury, even with the knowledge of confirmed OI.
