Neoplastic Foot Ulcers: A Multicentre Retrospective Study
Authors: Agata Janowska, Valentina Dini, Marco Romanelli, Nicolò Mori, Morganti Riccardo, Paola Monari, Andrea Michelerio, Linda Tognetti, Pietro Rubegni, Yuri Merli, Cosimo Misciali, Federica Trovato, Antonio Di Guardo, Francesca Passarelli, Maria Chiara Collina, Alessia Paganelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNeoplastic foot ulcers are particularly challenging for dermatologists and specialists in hard‐to‐heal wounds because their location and comorbidities can delay accurate diagnosis. We performed a multicentre, retrospective study analysing clinical and histological data, focusing on neoplastic foot ulcers collected over the past 5 years. We evaluated patients' demographic characteristics, clinical features, histological diagnosis, tumour onset and ulcer site. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, v.30. In total, 106 patients affected by foot skin ulcers were enrolled in the present protocol (52 women, 54 men). The mean age was 70.15 years. In most cases, the neoplastic ulcer was due to primary tumour ulceration. The dorsum of the foot was the most common site of neoplastic ulcers (66%). From a clinical perspective, most lesions were classified as ulcerated nodules (n = 58), ulcerated plaques (n = 8) or hypergranulating ulcers (n = 25), all with thickened, atypical edges. Histological diagnoses included melanoma (n = 45), non‐melanoma skin cancers (n = 34), benign tumours (n = 18) and rarer malignancies (n = 9). A statistically significant correlation was found between histotype and clinical lesion type, and between age and histotype. The study provided preliminary data on the clinical and histological characteristics of neoplastic foot ulcers, warranting further exploration in a prospective, multicentre study.

Introduction

A neoplastic ulcer is defined as a localised area of tissue loss caused either by cancer‐driven skin invasion and destruction or by malignant transformation occurring in a chronically damaged or ulcerated area [1]. In fact, both cutaneous neoplasms (including melanoma, non‐melanoma skin cancers [NMSCs], or rarer tumours) and skin metastases tend to undergo ulceration during their growth and progression [2]. Neoplastic transformation in a chronic environment (e.g., chronic ulcers, burn scars, fistulas, or chronic osteomyelitis) typically indicates a Marjolin ulcer, with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) as the most common aetiology [3].

Neoplastic ulcers are clinically characterised by an atypical appearance, atypical localisation and/or lack of response to optimal wound care treatment. More specifically, the wound bed frequently demonstrates hypergranulation, with possible fibrinous or necrotic components, pseudoepithelial proliferation and a tendency to bleed easily or spontaneously. The edges are usually thickened and indurated and progressively enlarge despite the use of advanced dressings [4]. Neoplastic foot ulcers are particularly challenging for dermatologists and specialists in hard‐to‐heal wounds because the foot is a common site of typical ulcers (vascular, diabetic and pressure ulcers), and comorbidities, such as diabetes or venous insufficiency, can delay accurate diagnosis [5]. Moreover, the foot is a relatively overlooked site for dermatological examination, potentially contributing to diagnostic delay. In cases of clinical suspicion, a skin biopsy should be performed for histopathological examination to exclude an underlying neoplasm. Multiple biopsies are usually recommended, both at the centre of the lesion and at the periphery; histopathological examination of non‐ulcerated areas is often essential for accurate diagnosis [6]. Diagnostic delay in neoplastic foot ulcers is associated with a poor prognosis, although new systemic therapies have been developed in recent years, particularly for non‐melanoma and melanoma skin tumours [7].

Surgery is the gold standard of treatment for most neoplastic ulcers; however, a multidisciplinary approach is required in locally advanced cases [8]. In addition to local curative therapies, such as radiotherapy or electrochemotherapy, and systemic regimens tailored to the histological diagnosis, it is essential to control symptoms (pain, itching, and odour) with appropriate dressings and local approaches [9].

Whilst typical foot ulcers have been extensively described in scientific literature, neoplastic foot ulcers are poorly characterised. Our study aimed to characterise these lesions with patient demographics, clinical features and histology to facilitate early diagnosis.

Materials and Methods

We performed a multicentre, retrospective study analysing clinical and histological data, focusing on neoplastic foot ulcers collected over the past 5 years (January 2020 to January 2025). All members of the Skin Ulcer Study Group of the Italian Society of Dermatology and Sexually Transmitted Diseases (SIDeMaST) were invited to participate in the study. Both clinical and histopathological data, as well as a dedicated skin ulcer outpatient service (attesting to the centre's expertise), were included as inclusion criteria. Four national referral centres participated in the investigation. Electronic medical records and pathology databases were used as source documents for data collection. Collected variables included patient's demographic characteristics (sex, age), clinical features (ulcerated nodule, ulcerated plaque or hypergranulating ulcer) and histological diagnosis—including melanoma, NMSC (basal cell carcinoma or BCC, SCC and basosquamous carcinoma), other rare malignant tumours and benign tumours, onset (primary tumour or tumour on chronic ulcer) and the site of the ulcer (dorsal or plantar). For melanomas, Breslow thickness was stratified by site of onset (dorsal or plantar). The CEAVNO Ethics Committee approved the study protocol.

Statistics

Categorical variables were described with absolute frequencies and percentages, whilst continuous variables were summarised as means with standard deviation. Associations between histologic subtype and foot localisation or clinical presentation were evaluated using the chi‐square test, whilst differences in age across histologic subtypes were assessed using one‐way ANOVA. A two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, v.30.

Results

In total, 106 patients affected by foot skin ulcers were enrolled in the present protocol (52 women, 54 men). Mean age was 70.15 (±17.6 DS) years. In most cases, the neoplastic ulcer was due to primary tumour ulceration, but also cancerisation of a chronic ulcer was reported in a minority of cases. The dorsum of the foot was the most common site of neoplastic ulcers (66%). From a clinical perspective, most lesions were classified as ulcerated nodules (n = 58), ulcerated plaques (n = 8) or hypergranulating ulcers (n = 25), with thickened and atypical edges (Figure 1). In 15 cases, onset and clinical features were missing. Histological diagnoses included melanoma (n = 45), NMSC (n = 34), benign tumours (n = 18) and rarer malignancies (n = 9). Of the 13 Marjolin ulcers, 12 were histologically SCC and 1 was a melanoma. The onset of Marjolin ulcers occurred in a chronic environment of diabetic foot ulcers (n = 4), pressure injuries (n = 2), arteropathy (n = 1), mixed venous–arterial ulcer (n = 1) and hydroxyurea‐induced ulcer (n = 1); in 4 cases, data were missing. All NMSCs, melanomas and rare tumours were histologically invasive and ulcerated. Histotype distribution did not differ significantly by foot location (Table 2). However, there were descriptive trends: carcinomas and benign lesions were more frequent in the dorsal area (79.4%), and melanomas were similarly distributed between the dorsal and the plantar regions (53.3% vs. 46.7%). A statistically significant correlation was found between histotype and clinical lesion type (ulcerated nodule, ulcerated plaque, hypergranulating ulcer). In fact, whilst SCC predominantly presented as hypergranulating ulcers with pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia (84.6%), BCC, melanoma and benign lesions more often presented as ulcerated nodules (Figure 2). A strong association was also found between age and histotype (Figure 3). Benign lesions had a much lower mean age (55 years), whereas carcinomas and melanomas had a much higher average age (73 years). As such, our data suggest that age was a reliable predictor of malignancy.

Median Breslow thickness for melanoma cases was 5.46 mm for dorsal localisation and 5.68 mm for plantar localisation. The average age at diagnosis for foot melanoma was 73.27 (±11.64). In 11 cases of melanoma, there was a history of previous trauma.

Discussion

In this multicentre retrospective study, we characterised the clinical and histopathological spectrum of neoplastic foot ulcers, providing a detailed overview of their morphologic variability and diagnostic implications. The predominance of dorsal localisation is in line with prior reports suggesting that cumulative ultraviolet exposure contributes to the pathogenesis of foot carcinomas, particularly NMSC [10]. In contrast, the nearly equal distribution of melanomas between dorsal and plantar regions reinforces the distinctive biological behaviour of acral melanomas, which are often unrelated to ultraviolet exposure and instead associated with mechanical stress or distinct mutational pathways, such as KIT or TERT promoter alterations [11]. Interestingly, Breslow thickness did not differ substantially between dorsal and plantar melanomas, suggesting that diagnostic delay remains a universal challenge across sites. Given that the median Breslow thickness exceeds 5 mm in both groups, our findings highlight the persistent late detection of foot melanomas, a problem repeatedly emphasised in the literature [12]. Enhanced clinical suspicion and earlier biopsy of atypical or persistent ulcers are therefore warranted to improve prognostic outcomes. Diagnostic delay is a key point for prognosis and treatment modality. Toussaint et al. observed, in a 10‐year study, a mean time to correct diagnosis of 44.4 months for ulcerated skin tumours on the legs and feet [13]. Our data further emphasise the need for early biopsy in presence of ulcerated nodules and hypergranulating ulcers, especially in aged patients.

Notably, we observed a higher frequency of melanomas compared with NMSC. Although this finding appears to contrast with previous reports identifying NMSC as the predominant tumour type in neoplastic ulcers [13], our analysis was restricted to foot lesions. It highlights the relevance of site‐specific risk stratification. The observed age distribution further supports the distinct biological nature of these cancers. Benign tumours occurred in significantly younger patients, whereas malignant cancers, particularly NMSC and melanoma, were predominantly diagnosed in the elderly, consistent with cumulative mutagenic exposure, immune senescence and chronic microtrauma as contributing factors [14]. This also aligns with the literature, which shows that NMSCs occur more often in older subjects and that ulcerative melanomas with acral localisation are more common in older subjects than UV‐related melanomas [15].

Of note, a consistent part of selected cases (n = 13) was due to chronic ulcer cancerisation. The relatively frequent occurrence of Marjolin ulcers (particularly in diabetic and pressure ulcers) indicates that long‐standing, non‐healing wounds in high‐risk settings may contribute to delayed diagnosis, underscoring the need for heightened vigilance and systematic histopathological assessment in these contexts. Unfortunately, the retrospective design of the study allowed the collection of partial data on all initial ulcer characteristics, aetiology, duration and evolution, which represents a limitation that warrants investigation in future prospective studies. According to available literature, approximately 1%–2% of chronic wounds can undergo neoplastic transformation, with SCC being the most common form, followed by BCC [16]. In a 9‐year series of 866 chronic ulcers, 7% of biopsies had neoplastic aetiology [17]. This underscores the importance of performing biopsies in chronic ulcers that do not tend to heal despite standard therapies. SCC on chronic ulcers, in particular, is more aggressive than primary SCC and presents metastasis at diagnosis in 27%–30% of cases [18]. BCC is the most common skin cancer of all, but it typically affects photoexposed areas and only rarely affects the foot [19]. In our series, conversely, we found more melanomas than skin carcinomas. Ulcerated melanoma of the foot frequently localises at the plantar level, and studies show an association with trauma that is not so often present in other sites [20].

A prospective German study of 107 patients with plantar melanoma found that 30% were initially mistaken for benign conditions such as chronic ulcers or warts [21]. Notably, the presence of perilesional pigment residues can facilitate melanoma diagnosis; however, hyperkeratosis, which is often present on the foot, and the absence of pigment can lead to misdiagnosis as angioma, eczema or wart‐like lesions [22]. Consistent with prior literature, our data also encompass a series of rare ulcerated tumours, such as Kaposi's sarcoma [23], which is frequently localised to the feet, lymphoma [24] and leiomyosarcoma [25]. Benign lesions such as pyogenic granuloma have also been analysed as mimickers of malignant cancers, due to their rapid exuberant nodular growth pattern and frequent bleeding.

This type of lesion often affects young patients; growth occurs very rapidly, and they may present with a pedicled, soft consistency and a less infiltrative papular or nodular appearance; however, histological confirmation is necessary due to the risk of diagnostic error [26]. Clinical presentation can provide clues to the suspected histology of neoplastic ulcers. All tumours arising from chronic ulcers were hypergranulating, whereas in primary tumours, we observed three distinct clinical aspects. The presence of a granulating ulcer, especially with pseudoepithelium, is highly suggestive of SCC, whereas a hard, ulcerated nodule is more likely to represent melanoma or BCC. Our case series aligns with the literature on Marjolin ulcers [27]. In 92% of cases, they were SCCs on chronic ulceration. As we have seen in our case series, a typical ulcer can mask the underlying tumour. The presence of comorbidities such as diabetes, arterial disease, venous insufficiency and pressure injuries should be carefully evaluated together with the clinical characteristics and evolution of the lesions [28]. In the literature, tumours on pressure ulcers occur less frequently than on vascular ulcers or diabetic foot ulcers [29]. Melanoma presenting as a Marjolin ulcer is even rarer, particularly when it happens on a pressure ulcer [30]. The appearance of hypergranulating, shiny tissue that bleeds easily should be monitored, and if in doubt, multiple biopsies should be performed to confirm the diagnosis. Because there is typically underlying ulceration, the biopsy may initially be negative [31]. Dermoscopy of ulcerative lesions can be complex due to changes in dermoscopic features [32].

Incorporating non‐invasive imaging of the non‐ulcerated edge may be a valuable adjunct to improve diagnostic accuracy and facilitate early recognition of malignancy. Evaluating ulcer borders with LC‐OCT (line‐field confocal optical coherence tomography) or confocal microscopy could improve accuracy [33].

The study's strengths include its multicentre design and systematic clinicopathologic correlation. At the same time, its retrospective nature, lack of clinical data, comorbidities, systemic therapies and the relatively limited sample size of rarer malignancies represent inherent limitations. Future prospective studies incorporating dermoscopy or other non‐invasive diagnostic techniques, along with molecular data, may further refine diagnostic algorithms and clarify pathogenetic mechanisms specific to acral sites.

Conclusions

The study provided preliminary data on the clinical and histological characteristics of neoplastic foot ulcers, warranting further exploration in a prospective, multicentre study with a larger case series that incorporates non‐invasive imaging assessment of foot lesions.
