Efficacy and safety of Korean red ginseng as adjunctive therapy in type 2 diabetes mellitus: a 12-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
Authors: Ji Sun Nam, Jeong Gyu Kim, Chul Woo Ahn, YuSik Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKorean Red Ginseng (KRG) has been investigated as adjunctive therapy for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), but randomized controlled evidence evaluating metabolic efficacy and short-term safety in patients receiving stable oral antidiabetic therapy remains limited.MethodsIn this 12-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 70 adults with well-controlled T2DM receiving stable oral antidiabetic medications were assigned to KRG (2 g/day) or placebo. Outcomes included oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)-derived insulin, C-peptide, and glucagon responses, insulin sensitivity indices, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), HbA1c, oxidative stress markers, antioxidant capacity, lipid profiles, and renal and hepatic safety parameters. Between-group comparisons used baseline-adjusted ANCOVA with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction; efficacy analyses included participants with post-baseline data (n = 65).ResultsCompared with placebo, KRG was associated with lower 120-min OGTT insulin (adjusted mean difference −6.992, 95% CI −14.205 to −0.222; p = 0.047) and higher ISI(0–120) (5.400, 0.972 to 9.829; p = 0.018). KRG was also associated with lower oxidized low-density lipoprotein (−5.645, −10.054 to −1.238; p = 0.013), malondialdehyde (−0.571, −1.090 to −0.053; p = 0.031), total cholesterol (−12.633, −24.752 to −0.515; p = 0.041), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (−10.171, −18.130 to −2.211; p = 0.013). FPG, 2-h glucose, and HbA1c did not differ significantly between groups. No hypoglycemic events occurred, and renal and hepatic safety markers remained stable.ConclusionsKRG was well tolerated and associated with favorable effects on selected indices of postprandial insulin sensitivity, lipid peroxidation markers, and lipid profile in this well-controlled T2DM population. Its short-term metabolic effects may be more apparent in post-load insulin dynamics than in conventional glycemic markers.Keywords: Adjunctive therapy, Insulin sensitivity, Korean red ginseng, Oxidative stress, Panax ginseng meyer, Randomized controlled trial, Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Panax ginseng Meyer (P. ginseng) has been widely used in traditional East Asian medicine for its broad therapeutic potential and health-promoting properties [1]. Korean Red Ginseng (KRG), a steamed and dried form produced from fresh P. ginseng by repeated high-temperature processing, has been reported to exhibit enhanced pharmacological efficacy owing to heat-induced structural modifications of its active constituents [2]. Given the global rise in the prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), together with its progressive course and the limitations of conventional pharmacotherapy [3], interest has increased in complementary strategies such as herbal supplementation to enhance glycemic control and attenuate disease-related complications [3,4]. Accumulating preclinical and clinical data indicate that KRG supports vital organ function and confers broad benefits across diverse chronic diseases, thereby attracting substantial scientific interest and leading to its growing use and evaluation as an adjunctive therapy in patients with T2DM [2,5].

Preclinical studies using T2DM models consistently demonstrate that KRG improves glucose homeostasis [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. These investigations implicate enhanced mitochondrial biogenesis [6,9], upregulation of glucose transporter [6,8,9], and activation of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [6] and Akt signaling [7,10] as key mechanisms, accompanied by reductions in oxidative stress [10,11] and systemic inflammation [9,10], both central to the pathogenesis of insulin resistance (IR). While preclinical studies provide strong mechanistic evidence supporting the beneficial effects of KRG on glucose metabolism, clinical evidence specifically evaluating its efficacy remains limited. A recent meta-analysis of 21 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reported that supplementation with various forms of ginseng significantly reduced fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and improved IR [12]. However, only a limited number of clinical studies have specifically examined KRG in individuals with impaired glucose regulation or T2DM. Some RCTs have demonstrated improvements in post-load glucose regulation and insulin sensitivity with KRG supplementation [13,14], whereas others have reported no significant glycemic effects [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. More recently, a randomized controlled trial in patients with T2DM and impaired glucose regulation reported reductions in FPG, post-load glucose regulation, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) following KRG supplementation [20]. Taken together, the available clinical evidence remains heterogeneous, and the therapeutic efficacy of KRG for glycemic control in patients with prediabetes and T2DM has not been fully established.

Despite its widespread use and perceived safety, the use of ginseng, including KRG, has raised concerns regarding potential renal and hepatic adverse effects and interactions with antidiabetic medications. Although no direct evidence links ginseng to nephrotoxicity or chronic kidney disease (CKD) progression, epidemiological data indicated a higher CKD prevalence among ginseng/red ginseng users than non-users, an association that persisted after adjustment for age-related differences in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and excluding individuals with major CKD risk factors [19]. Similarly, although KRG has been reported to exert hepatoprotective effects, its complex mixture of bioactive constituents, some of which have yet to be fully characterized, raises concern about potential unforeseen hepatic effects [21]. A pooled analysis of RCTs found no clinically significant hepatotoxicity with ginseng at conventional dosages, but did report a modest yet statistically significant increase in bilirubin, particularly at higher doses or in individuals with pre-existing conditions [22]. Furthermore, KRG has been shown to modulate hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes involved in drug metabolism [23]. Since many antidiabetic medications also inhibit or otherwise alter P450 activity [24] and KRG has antidiabetic effects, concomitant administration of KRG and antidiabetic agents may increase the risk of drug–herb interactions and hypoglycemia [25], as suggested by a previous trial in which several patients receiving antidiabetic medications experienced hypoglycemic episodes leading to withdrawal [13]. Although another RCT of KRG in dysglycemic individuals reported no apparent abnormalities in liver or kidney safety indicators, the available clinical safety data remain limited, particularly in patients with established T2DM [20]. Collectively, these findings, although not establishing causality, highlight the need to evaluate potential renal, hepatic, and pharmacokinetic risk alongside therapeutic efficacy when assessing KRG supplementation in patients with T2DM.

Given the limited and inconclusive clinical evidence regarding KRG's efficacy in T2DM, and the variability in its phytochemical [26] and pharmacological profiles [27] across preparation methods, well-controlled trials specifically evaluating KRG are warranted. Moreover, reported herb–drug interactions and organ-specific effects highlight the need to assess both the efficacy and safety of KRG, particularly in patients receiving oral antidiabetic medications. Accordingly, we conducted a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy and safety of KRG supplementation as an adjunct to standard oral therapy in the management of T2DM.

Materials/subjects and methods

Participants and study design

Eligible participants were adults aged 19 to 75 years with T2DM of <15 years’ duration and well-controlled glycemia, defined as HbA1c < 58 mmol/mol (<7.5%), no use of exogenous insulin, treatment with ≤2 oral glucose-lowering agents, and no changes in antidiabetic medications during the preceding 6 months. Additional inclusion criteria were no regular use of herbal supplements within 6 months, no history of malignancy, not being pregnant or breastfeeding at enrollment, and no major surgery within the past year. The target sample size was determined at the study-design stage using G*Power Ver. 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on a representative efficacy outcome from a previous randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled KRG trial, an independent two-sample comparison with a two-sided α = 0.05, 90% statistical power, and a standardized effect size of 0.868 required 58 participants (29 per group). Assuming an approximately 20% dropout rate, the target enrollment was set at 70 participants (35 per group).

Of 87 individuals screened, 70 met the eligibility criteria and were enrolled in a 12-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial and allocated 1:1 to KRG or placebo. For randomization, study staff generated the computer-based random allocation sequence and enrolled participants, and the hospital pharmacy assigned participants to interventions using coded containers. Study products (500 mg tablets) were identical in appearance and were pre-packaged in numbered boxes according to the randomization code; participants took two tablets twice daily (total daily dose 2 g). Each participant received a unique identification code linked to the randomization schedule. Investigators, clinical research coordinators, pharmacists, and participants remained blinded to treatment allocation until study completion. To monitor potential hypoglycemic episodes, all participants performed self-monitoring of blood glucose using a personal glucometer (STANDARD Mentor BT; SD BIOSENSOR, Seoul, Korea) and were instructed to obtain at least 50 measurements during the intervention, including measurements taken when experiencing symptoms suggestive of hypoglycemia or any subjective discomfort; logs were reviewed for symptomatic and asymptomatic hypoglycemia. Participants were instructed at enrollment to maintain their usual diet, physical activity, medication use, and other lifestyle habits throughout the 12-week intervention and to avoid intentional lifestyle changes that could affect clinical and biochemical outcomes. Although formal life logs were not collected, clinical research coordinators contacted participants by telephone once per month during the intervention period to monitor adherence to study procedures, confirm maintenance of usual habits, review self-monitoring of blood glucose, and assess hypoglycemic episodes or other adverse events. Participants were also encouraged to contact the research team if any lifestyle changes, health-related issues, or study-related concerns occurred during the trial.

Of the 70 randomized participants, five did not complete the 12-week follow-up assessment and their data were not included in the efficacy analyses because consent for use of study data was withdrawn. In the KRG group, one participant withdrew consent. In the placebo group, four participants did not complete follow-up (one due to an adverse event (skin rash), two due to withdrawal of consent, and one due to non-compliance with the intervention regimen (<80% adherence)). Consequently, 65 participants with post-baseline outcome data comprised the primary efficacy analysis set and also met the criteria for the per-protocol set (Fig. 1). The primary efficacy analyses were conducted in the modified intention-to-treat (full analysis) set, defined as all randomized participants with available follow-up outcome data. Participants who withdrew consent for data use were excluded from analyses in accordance with institutional requirements.

All procedures involving human participants complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments and with the Recommendations for the Conduct, Reporting, Editing and Publication of Scholarly Work in Medical Journals. This trial was registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS), Republic of Korea (Registration No. KCT0011311), and the protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 3-2023-0091). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Preparation of KRG and placebo tablet

KRG and placebo tablets were manufactured by the Korean Ginseng Research Institute of Korea Ginseng Corporation (Daejeon, Korea), following a traditional Korean preparation method [28], adapted to comply with ISO 19610:2017 standards. Briefly, six-year-old Korean ginseng roots were steamed at 95 °C for 90 min, then subjected to seven aqueous extractions at 85 °C for 10 h. The combined extracts were vacuum-concentrated at 60 °C to ≥60° Brix. The concentrated extract was subsequently spray-dried to produce a light brown powder, which was used to formulate the KRG tablets. Ginsenoside composition was analyzed using an HPLC system (Waters Alliance HPLC 2695, Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA), and the results and representative HPLC chromatogram are presented in Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 1, respectively. The placebo tablets were formulated from inert excipients (primarily cellulose and maltodextrin) to closely match the KRG tablets in appearance, taste, texture, size, shape, color, and packaging, thereby maintaining blinding throughout the study.

Anthropometric and biochemical analyses

Body weight (BW), height, and body mass index (BMI) were measured using an electronic stadiometer (BSM370, BioSpace, Seoul, Korea). Waist circumference (WC) was measured with a circumference measuring tape (SECA201, SECA, Hamburg, Germany). All anthropometric measurements were performed twice at a standardized time and averaged.

Glycemic parameters included HbA1c, FPG, insulin, glucagon, and C-peptide. A 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was performed, with plasma glucose, insulin, glucagon, and C-peptide measured at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min; area under the curve (AUC) for each variable was calculated using the trapezoidal rule. Insulin sensitivity indices, including Homeostatic Model Assessment (HOMA) models for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR, HOMA2-IR) and for insulin sensitivity (HOMA2-%S), Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI), Insulin Sensitivity Index (ISI0–120), Stumvoll Index, Avignon Index, and Matsuda Index, and Beta-cell function indices, including HOMA-β, HOMA2-β, the insulinogenic index (IGI), C-Peptide-to-Glucose ratio (CPRI), and the disposal index (DI), were derived using established equations [29]. Insulin (90095, CrystalChem, Itasca, IL, USA), C-peptide (80-CPTHU-E01.1, ALPCO, Salem, NH, USA), and glucagon (MBS700120, MyBioSource, San Diego, CA, USA) were quantified using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits according to the manufacturers' instructions.

To evaluate antioxidant effects, serum markers of oxidative damage and serum antioxidant capacity were measured. Lipid peroxidation was assessed by malondialdehyde (MDA; MBS263626, MyBioSource) and oxidized low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (oxLDL; 10-1143-01, Mercodia, Uppsala, Sweden). Protein oxidation was assessed by advanced oxidation protein products (AOPPs; MBS2700483, MyBioSource) and protein carbonyls (PCs; MBS161516, MyBioSource). Serum antioxidant capacity was evaluated using oxygen radical absorbance capacity (ORAC; STA-345, Cell Biolabs, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC; XAN-5040, Cell Biolabs, Inc.), and ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP; STA-859, Cell Biolabs, Inc.).

Renal and hepatic functions were monitored in accordance with the study's safety objectives. Renal function was evaluated by blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), eGFR, and urinary kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1; DKM100, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) as a marker of tubular damage. Hepatic function was evaluated by serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and serum albumin.

Lipid profiles included total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and triglycerides (TG). FPG, TC, HDL-C, LDL-C, TG, albumin, BUN, creatinine, ALT, and AST were analyzed using the ADVIA Chemistry XPT system (Siemens Healthineers, Tarrytown, NY, USA).

The prespecified primary outcomes, as defined in the IRB-approved protocol and clinical trial registration, comprised both efficacy and safety domains. Efficacy outcomes included glycemic parameters assessed under fasting and OGTT conditions, oxidative stress markers, and antioxidant capacity indices. Safety outcomes included hypoglycemic events and renal and hepatic function markers. Secondary outcomes included anthropometric parameters and blood lipid profiles.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD unless otherwise indicated. Distributional assumptions were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Within-group changes from baseline to follow-up were assessed separately for each outcome using paired t-tests for approximately normally distributed variables and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed variables. These within-group analyses were considered descriptive and were not used as the primary basis for treatment-effect inference. The main between-group inference for each endpoint at follow-up was conducted using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with the follow-up value as the dependent variable, treatment group as a fixed factor, and the corresponding baseline value as a covariate. To control for multiple testing across related outcomes, p-values from baseline-adjusted ANCOVA models were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure within each outcome domain as presented in each table. Results from ANCOVA are reported as adjusted mean differences between groups with 95% confidence intervals and FDR-adjusted two-sided p-values. A two-sided adjusted p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The effects of KRG on glycemic and hormonal parameters

As shown in Table 2, Table 3, baseline-adjusted ANCOVA indicated modest between-group differences in selected post-load insulin-related outcomes after 12 weeks of KRG supplementation. Compared with placebo, KRG was associated with a lower insulin concentration at 120 min during OGTT, with an adjusted mean difference of −6.992 (95% CI −14.205 to −0.222; p = 0.047), and a higher ISI(0–120), with an adjusted mean difference of 5.400 (95% CI 0.972 to 9.829; p = 0.018). Within the KRG group, I_AUC decreased from 4710.25 ± 1807.76 to 4220.38 ± 1793.19, and ISI(0–120) increased from 42.23 ± 13.01 to 45.81 ± 13.69, whereas corresponding changes in the placebo group were not significant. The Stumvoll insulin sensitivity index increased within the KRG group from 0.050 ± 0.03 to 0.059 ± 0.03, with no significant change in the placebo group; however, the between-group ANCOVA effect was not statistically significant. In the placebo group, HbA1c and Gcg120 increased over 12 weeks; however, these variables did not show significant between-group differences in baseline-adjusted ANCOVA. No significant within- or between-group differences were observed in other glycemic or hormonal parameters. Overall, KRG supplementation did not materially affect fasting glycemia or indices of β-cell function, but baseline-adjusted ANCOVA supported modest between-group differences in late-phase post-load insulin and ISI(0–120).

The effects of KRG on oxidative stress and antioxidant capacity

As shown in Table 4, 12 weeks of KRG supplementation was associated with selective improvements in oxidative stress markers, whereas systemic antioxidant capacity did not change significantly. Baseline-adjusted ANCOVA demonstrated lower follow-up oxLDL in the KRG group than in the placebo, with an adjusted mean difference of −5.645 (95% CI −10.054 to −1.238; p = 0.013). Follow-up MDA levels were also lower with KRG, yielding an adjusted mean difference of −0.571 relative to placebo (95% CI −1.090 to −0.053; p = 0.031). Within-group analyses showed that oxLDL decreased in the KRG group, whereas MDA increased in the placebo group. Other oxidative protein markers, including AOPPs and PCs, remained unchanged in both groups. Despite these favorable changes in lipid oxidation, no significant improvements were observed in serum antioxidant capacity indices (ORAC, TEAC, FRAP) in either group. These findings suggest that KRG may preferentially attenuate lipid peroxidation–related oxidative damage, whereas systemic antioxidant capacity indices remained largely unchanged.

Safety outcomes: hypoglycemic events, renal and hepatic function

As presented in Table 5, no hypoglycemic events were reported in either group throughout the intervention, indicating that concomitant KRG and oral antidiabetic medications did not increase the risk of clinically apparent hypoglycemia. Renal function markers, including ACR, eGFR, BUN, and urinary KIM-1, remained stable in both groups, with no significant within-group changes and no between-group differences observed in baseline-adjusted ANCOVA. Similarly, hepatic function parameters (ALT, AST, serum albumin) did not change significantly in either group. These findings indicate that 12-week KRG supplementation was well tolerated and not associated with adverse renal or hepatic effects in patients with T2DM receiving stable antidiabetic therapy.

Discussion

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of KRG as adjunctive therapy in patients with well-controlled T2DM receiving stable oral treatment. Importantly, the study incorporated serial OGTT-based hormonal phenotyping, enabling assessment of insulin, C-peptide, and glucagon responses in addition to conventional glycemic endpoints. Over 12 weeks, baseline-adjusted ANCOVA demonstrated modest but statistically significant between-group differences in selected postprandial insulin-related outcomes, characterized by lower late-phase post-load insulin concentrations and higher ISI(0–120) in the KRG group relative to placebo, suggesting a favorable shift in postprandial insulin sensitivity. Baseline-adjusted ANCOVA further demonstrated significant between-group differences in lipid-related oxidative stress markers and lipid parameters, with lower follow-up oxLDL, MDA, TC, and LDL-C levels in the KRG group relative to placebo. No hypoglycemic events or adverse renal or hepatic changes were observed, supporting the short-term safety and tolerability of KRG in this population. Collectively, these findings suggest that KRG supplementation may confer modest benefits on postprandial insulin sensitivity, lipid peroxidation–related oxidative damage, and selected lipid parameters, while maintaining a favorable short-term safety profile, although interpretation should account for the modest magnitude of the observed changes and the short intervention duration.

Although ginseng supplementation has been reported to improve glycemic parameters in individuals with prediabetes or T2DM [12], clinical data specifically evaluating the antidiabetic effects of KRG remain inconsistent [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20]], in contrast to the consistently observed beneficial outcomes in preclinical models [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. This variability may partially reflect heterogeneity in ginsenoside profiles determined by ginseng species and processing conditions [30]. In addition, because ginsenosides account for only 1–7% of the dried ginseng weight, non-ginsenoside fractions may also contribute to its antidiabetic effects of KRG [31]. Differences in study populations, baseline glycemic status, intervention dose and formulation, and analytic approach across trials may further contribute to the variability in clinical findings. These considerations warrant continued investigation employing well-defined, standardized KRG preparations to clarify its mechanisms of action and therapeutic potential in T2DM. In this study, baseline-adjusted analyses showed that 12 weeks of KRG supplementation was associated with lower late-phase post-load insulin and higher ISI(0–120) relative to placebo, suggesting a favorable shift in postprandial insulin sensitivity in patients with well-controlled T2DM. The concomitant within-group increase in the Stumvoll index in the KRG group was directionally consistent with this pattern, although the corresponding between-group difference was not statistically significant. Because OGTT insulin responses reflect the interplay between insulin sensitivity and β-cell secretion, and OGTT insulin peak-time patterns have been associated with insulin sensitivity and subsequent diabetes risk, late-phase OGTT insulin is commonly interpreted as a marker of altered insulin demand when considered alongside the concomitant glucose trajectory and OGTT-derived insulin sensitivity indices [32]. Given the physiological relevance of late-phase OGTT insulin responses, the observed reduction in post-load insulin demand may represent a potentially beneficial insulin-sparing response in the absence of measurable changes in conventional glycemic markers. These findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of selective effects on post-load insulin-related dynamics rather than broad improvement in conventional glycemic control. These findings are consistent with previous clinical studies, in which KRG reduced insulin demand during OGTT [13,14]. Our study extends this literature by characterizing serial insulin, C-peptide, and glucagon responses at 0, 30, 60, and 120 min during OGTT together with derived indices of insulin sensitivity and β-cell function, thereby providing a more granular assessment of post-load hormonal regulation than studies limited to fasting insulin or conventional glucose indices. Clinically, the present findings suggest that, in patients with well-controlled T2DM, the short-term glycemic effect of KRG may be more apparent in postprandial insulin dynamics than in conventional fasting or long-term glycemic markers; nevertheless, the heterogeneity and limited certainty of prior clinical evidence indicate that larger and longer trials using standardized preparations are required to define its therapeutic relevance.

Although prior trials reporting favorable metabolic effects of KRG generally employed higher daily doses (5-6 g/day) [13,14], our findings suggest that modest metabolic benefits, particularly in post-load insulin dynamics and insulin sensitivity, may also be achieved at a lower dose of 2 g/day in patients with well-controlled T2DM. However, unlike a recent study that reported significant reductions in FPG, 2-h glucose, and HbA1c after 12 weeks of KRG supplementation at 2.8 g/day [20], we observed no significant changes in FPG or HbA1c, consistent with several previous clinical trials [[13], [14], [15], [16],18,29]. This discrepancy is unlikely to be explained by dose alone and may instead reflect differences in study population, baseline glycemic status, and the sensitivity of the parameters assessed, as our cohort included only patients with established, well-controlled T2DM receiving stable oral antidiabetic therapy, whereas the comparator study enrolled a broader dysglycemic population comprising both T2DM and impaired glucose regulation. Given that HbA1c reflects integrated glycemic exposure over time and is influenced by fasting and postprandial glucose, diabetes duration, insulin resistance, medication adherence, and lifestyle factors [33], the absence of a significant HbA1c change does not necessarily preclude short-term effects on postprandial insulin dynamics. In this context, the observed differences in late-phase post-load insulin and ISI(0–120) may indicate that short-term KRG effects are more readily captured by dynamic OGTT-derived indices than by fasting glucose or HbA1c. Although lower late-phase post-load insulin and higher ISI(0–120) may indicate improved postprandial insulin efficiency, the clinical relevance of these changes remains to be established, particularly because FPG, 2-h glucose, and HbA1c were not significantly altered. Future studies incorporating longer intervention periods and structured monitoring of diet, physical activity, medication adherence, and other lifestyle factors may help clarify the therapeutic potential of KRG for glycemic control in T2DM. The precise physiological mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear because multiple mechanisms have been proposed [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]], and the principal active constituents have yet to be definitively established [34]. Recent network pharmacology analyses of ginseng T2DM-related models have identified kaempferol, β-sitosterol, ginsenoside Rh2, stigmasterol, and fumarine as key active compounds targeting TNF, IL-1β, Akt1, PTGS2, Bcl2, and Jun, supporting a multicomponent, multi-target, multi-pathway mode of action involving regulation of inflammation and oxidative stress, and Akt1-mediated insulin signaling representing a plausible mechanistic basis for its glycemic effects [35]. These mechanistic insights, together with previous findings and our own observations, support further investigations using standardized KRG preparations to clarify its antidiabetic properties and better define its therapeutic role in T2DM.

Oxidative stress, characterized by excessive generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during metabolic processes, is a critical contributor to the pathophysiology of T2DM and related metabolic disorders [36], making its control an important therapeutic objective [37]. In this context, KRG has emerged as a candidate intervention due to its reported capacity to modulate antioxidant defense systems and attenuate oxidative stress in preclinical and clinical settings [38]. In the present study, baseline-adjusted ANCOVA showed lower follow-up oxLDL and MDA levels in the KRG group relative to placebo after 12 weeks of supplementation, indicating significant between-group differences in lipid peroxidation–related oxidative stress markers. These findings suggest that KRG may preferentially attenuate oxidative lipid damage in patients with well-controlled T2DM, which may partly contribute to its observed metabolic effects. By contrast, systemic antioxidant capacity measures (ORAC, TEAC, and FRAP) and protein oxidation markers (AOPPs and PCs) did not change significantly, suggesting that the anti-oxidative effects observed in this study were more closely related to lipid peroxidation pathways than to a generalized enhancement of circulating antioxidant capacity. These effects are thought to be mediated largely by ginsenosides, including Rb1, Re, Rg1, Rg3, and F2, which activate critical redox-regulatory pathways such as KEAP1/Nrf2, SIRT3/FOXO3, and MAPK, thereby upregulating antioxidant enzymes, limiting lipid peroxidation, beneficially modulating gut microbiota composition, and improving overall oxidative balance [39]. Consistent with our findings, KRG has been shown to reduce MDA accumulation in the corpus cavernosum [40] and to attenuate lipid peroxidation while enhancing insulin signaling via Akt phosphorylation in skeletal muscle [10]. Clinical studies with healthy individuals and patients with cancer also have demonstrated that KRG mitigates oxidative damage induced by physiological or pathological stress, further supporting its anti-oxidative potential [38]. Given the limited data in T2DM, our results add to this evidence base and suggest that KRG selectively modulates lipid-focused oxidative pathways, which may contribute to its therapeutic potential in oxidative stress–related metabolic complications.

KRG, through ginsenosides, has been shown to modulate sterol metabolism via estrogen receptor and AMPK signaling and regulation of lipid-regulatory genes such as SREBP-1c and PPARα in hepatocytes, thereby enhancing cholesterol clearance and reducing lipogenesis [41]. Ginsenosides may also promote cholesterol efflux and reduce de novo cholesterol synthesis by activating FXR-CYP7A1 [42] signaling and modulating gut microbiota, including increases in Akkermansia muciniphila that enhance bile acid turnover and oxysterol clearance [43]. Clinical and meta-analytic evidence indicate that KRG produces modest but consistent reductions in TC and LDL-C, with greater effects in individuals with metabolic disturbances [44]. In this study, although within-group reductions in TC and LDL-C were not statistically significant, baseline-adjusted ANCOVA indicated lower follow-up TC and LDL-C levels in the KRG group relative to placebo. These between-group differences are consistent with the proposed lipid-modulating mechanisms of KRG and may have contributed, at least in part, to the lower serum oxLDL levels observed after supplementation. Although oxLDL and LDL-C are correlated in the general population [45], this relationship appears less consistent in T2DM [46], in which oxidative lipoprotein modification may be influenced more strongly by redox imbalance than by LDL-C concentration alone [47]. The concurrent between-group differences in LDL-C and oxLDL suggest that KRG may influence both cholesterol burden and oxidative modification of lipoproteins. In patients with T2DM, in whom oxLDL may reflect redox imbalance beyond LDL-C concentration alone, the lower oxLDL levels observed after KRG supplementation may indicate anti-oxidative activity directed toward lipid peroxidation in addition to potential lipid-modulating effects. Thus, while LDL-C lowering remains clinically relevant, the between-group difference in oxLDL may reflect an additional effect of KRG on oxidative lipid modification, supporting its potential dual role in modulating lipid metabolism and oxidative stress in T2DM.

Given prior concerns regarding potential adverse effects of ginseng, including KRG, the evaluation of safety outcomes was another key objective in this study. Although potential renal [19] and hepatic [22] toxicity, as well as drug-metabolizing enzymes such as cytochrome P450 [23], have been suggested, the available evidence remains inconclusive. Therefore, the potential for herb–drug interactions in patients receiving antidiabetic therapy warrants careful assessment [24]. Another RCT of KRG in individuals with T2DM and impaired glucose regulation reported no adverse effects and no apparent abnormalities in routine liver and kidney safety indicators [20]. In contrast, a previous trial administering KRG at 6 g/day in patients with T2DM reported no significant renal or hepatic toxicity but observed hypoglycemic episodes that led to participant withdrawal, raising concerns about potential interactions with glucose-lowering agents. Our study showed that 12-week supplementation with a lower KRG dose of 2 g/day was well tolerated in patients receiving stable antidiabetic therapy, with no hypoglycemic events and no significant changes in renal and hepatic function markers. Because our cohort consisted exclusively of patients with established T2DM receiving stable oral antidiabetic medications, and because hypoglycemic events were specifically monitored alongside renal and hepatic biomarkers, these findings provide clinically relevant safety data for this treatment context. Together, these findings support the short-term tolerability of moderate-dose KRG in patients with T2DM receiving stable oral antidiabetic therapy, as the observed between-group metabolic and anti-oxidative differences occurred alongside a favorable hypoglycemic, renal, and hepatic safety profile.

In summary, 12-week supplementation with Korean Red Ginseng (2 g/day) as an adjunct to standard oral therapy in individuals with T2DM was associated with favorable effects on selected indices of postprandial insulin sensitivity, markers of lipid peroxidation, and lipid profile, without evidence of hypoglycemia or adverse renal or hepatic effects. In the context of emerging clinical evidence indicating that KRG may also improve conventional glycemic indices in broader dysglycemic populations [20], our findings suggest that, in well-controlled T2DM, the more readily detectable short-term effects may lie in post-load hormonal dynamics, lipid-related oxidative stress, and selected lipid measures rather than fasting or long-term glycemic markers. These findings support the short-term tolerability of KRG as an adjunctive intervention and suggest potential metabolic and anti-oxidative benefits that warrant confirmation in larger and longer trials using standardized preparations and more detailed lifestyle monitoring.
