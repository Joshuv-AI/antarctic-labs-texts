Venetoclax/FluBu2 RIC transplant followed by all-oral venetoclax/decitabine maintenance for poor-risk MDS/AML†
Authors: Jacqueline S Garcia, Haesook T Kim, H Moses Murdock, Anna Bosch-Vilaseca, Kevin M Panaro, Felicia Lim, Jade Fiorilla, Elise Auriemma, Jennifer Brock, Mahasweta Gooptu, Vincent T Ho, Corey S Cutler, Roman Shapiro, Amar H Kelkar, Gregory A Abel, Daniel J DeAngelo, Richard M Stone, Denbaa Bat-Erdene, Jeremy Ryan, Geoffrey Fell, Anthony Letai, Jerome Ritz, R Coleman Lindsley, Joseph H Antin, Robert J Soiffer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Ven/FluBu2 transplant followed by Ven/Dec-c maintenance is safe, feasible, and efficacious with a 2-year OS of 77%.•Serial molecular MRD revealed favorable outcomes with non-TP53 MRD before transplant and possibility of TP53-MRD clearance on maintenance.

Introduction

Disease relapse is the primary cause of treatment failure for patients with myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) that undergo reduced-intensity chemotherapy (RIC) allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT). Risk factors for relapse include adverse risk mutations at diagnosis and detectable molecular residual disease (MRD) before transplantation.1,2 Conditioning chemotherapy intensification can partially mitigate relapse risk, however, in older patients, those with comorbidities, and disease characterized by select adverse risk mutations such as TP53, broadly increasing chemotherapy intensity incurs additional toxicity without improving relapse-free survival (RFS).3 Posttransplant maintenance therapy is another potential strategy to mitigate relapse risk by controlling the myeloid neoplasm until the graft-versus-leukemia benefits are effective. Currently, there are no approved regimens for posttransplant maintenance for MDS/AML, although the FLT3-inhibitor gilteritinib has demonstrated significant RFS benefit in a prespecified subgroup analysis in those with detectable FLT3–internal tandem duplication MRD peritransplant.4 Hypomethylating agents (HMA) have been studied as posttransplant maintenance for patients with MDS/AML with high-risk features; however no significant difference in RFS or overall survival (OS) has been detected in randomized studies, including subcutaneous (SC) azacitidine (Aza) vs observation, suggesting that HMA alone is insufficient5 or that benefit may be limited to high-risk subgroups.6

We previously showed the safety and efficacy with the addition of the oral B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) inhibitor venetoclax to fludarabine/busulfan (Ven/FluBu2) RIC chemotherapy,7 and then the addition of posttransplant maintenance therapy with combination Ven/Aza in patients with poor-risk MDS/AML.8 We hypothesized that preemptive combination Ven/HMA maintenance therapy could prevent posttransplant relapse based on achievement of MRD negativity in the frontline treatment setting.9,10 The 2-year OS for those who received Ven/Aza maintenance after Ven/FluBu2 transplantation was 67% (95% confidence interval [CI], 43-83) and progression-free survival (PFS) was 59% (95% CI, 36-76). However, repeated parenteral administration of IV/SC Aza was inconvenient and only 41% successfully completed protocol therapy.8 We now aim to determine the extent to which an all-oral maintenance regimen could promote treatment adherence and tolerability, while preventing relapse until the benefits of transplant occur. We report the safety and efficacy of combination Ven with decitabine-cedazuridine (Ven/Dec-c) maintenance therapy after Ven/FluBu2 RIC HCT in patients with poor-risk MDS/AML.

Methods

Trial design and oversight

This is an open-label, phase 1 study of posttransplant maintenance therapy with combination Ven/Dec-c in patients with high-risk MDS/AML after Ven/FluBu2 RIC HCT conducted at the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute (DFCI). The study was approved by the institutional review board, and participants provided informed written consent (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03613532; DFCI #18-283 cohort 3).

End points

The primary outcomes were to determine safety, tolerability, and a recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) of Ven/Dec-c posttransplant maintenance. Secondary end points included OS, PFS, cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR), nonrelapse mortality (NRM), and cumulative incidence of acute and chronic graft-versus-host disease (GVHD). Exploratory end points at prespecified time points included serial measurements of molecular (next-generation sequencing [NGS]) MRD, immune reconstitution assessments, and quality of life (QOL) by the Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Bone Marrow Transplantation (FACT-BMT) and the 3-level version of EuroQol Group-5D (EQ-5D-3L).11,12

Eligibility

Adult patients were enrolled between 8 March 2022 to 17 October 2023, and were required to have an 8/8 HLA-matched donor and a diagnosis of AML with adverse risk (European LeukemiaNet 2022 criteria [ELN 2022]) or multiparametric flow cytometry (MFC) MRD positivity of >0.1%; or MDS with International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) intermediate 2 and high, or mutation in TP53 or RAS pathway; or MDS/myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) with mutation in ASXL1.3,13,14 At initial screening (<28 days before HCT), bone marrow blasts were required to be <5% for AML and ≤10% for MDS or MDS/MPN. Prior receipt of Ven therapy was allowed.

Study conditioning and maintenance

Patients received Ven/FluBu2 RIC chemotherapy, as previously described,7 followed by peripheral blood stem cell infusion (day 0), and GVHD prophylaxis with tacrolimus (Tac) starting on day −3 and low-dose methotrexate (Mtx) on days +1, 3, 6, and 11 (Tac/Mtx) in outpatient or inpatient settings. Our trial initiated before results from the BMT CTN 1703 trial,15 and at that time, Tac/Mtx was the DFCI standard GVHD prophylaxis for RIC transplant. Patients were rescreened at day +28 (+14-day window) for maintenance initiation between day +42 to day +90 after HCT and requirements included: (1) no morphologic disease (≤5% marrow blasts; and MFC MRD positivity allowed); (2) no overall grade 2 to 4 acute GVHD requiring prednisone of >0.5 mg/kg daily, but allowed upon resolution or on prednisone of ≤0.5 mg/kg daily; (3) adequate organ function defined as total bilirubin of <2× the upper limit of normal, aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase or <3× the upper limit of normal, creatinine clearance of >30 mL/min; and (4) absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of ≥1.0 × 103/μL and platelet count of ≥50 × 103/μL, both without growth factor or transfusion support. Maintenance therapy included Ven 400 mg on days 1 through 14 in combination with fixed-dose tablet 35 mg Dec and 100 mg cedazuridine (Ven/Dec-c) on days 1, 3, and 5 for those on dose level 1 (DL1), or on days 1, 2, and 3 for those on DL2, every 42 days for 8 cycles. Laboratory parameters were obtained weekly during maintenance cycle 1 and on days 1 and 15 of subsequent cycles. ANC of ≥1.0 × 103/μL and platelets of ≥50 × 103/μL were required to start each cycle. Prophylactic antibacterial agents were required during cycle 1 and recommended for ANC of <0.5 × 103/μL in subsequent cycles. Antifungal prophylaxis was not mandated per institutional practice16 but permitted with venetoclax-dose modifications per label. Dose reductions were also allowed for new onset or recurrent grade 4 thrombocytopenia or neutropenia lasting >7 days (first, reduce Ven dose by 50%; second, shorten Ven duration by 7 days). Tac taper began after day +90 and discontinued by 6 to 9 months in absence of GVHD. Donor lymphocyte infusion (DLI) and alternative antileukemic therapy were not permitted.

Safety, response, and QOL assessments

All patients were included in safety and efficacy analyses. Adverse events (AE) were assessed per National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. Dose-limiting toxicities were captured during the first 42 days of maintenance cycle 1 and defined as any treatment-related death, failure of ANC recovery to ≥0.5 × 103/μL or platelet recovery to <25 × 103/μL for >2 weeks beyond cycle length, and any nonhematologic grade 4 toxicities. Bone marrow biopsies including clinical NGS panel (88-gene Rapid Heme Panel, ∼3% variant allele frequency [VAF] sensitivity)17 and MFC MRD (estimated lower level of detection of <0.02%; Hematologics, Inc) were performed for disease response assessments at day +100, 6 months, and 12 months after transplant or sooner if suspected relapse. Responses were determined according to International Working Group 2006 criteria for MDS, or ELN 2022 for AML.13,18 For TP53 allelic state, those with ≥2 TP53 mutations or ≥1 point mutation in combination with TP53 copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity, TP53 deletion, or chromosome 17/17p deletion by karyotype were classified as TP53 multihit, whereas those with a single TP53 point mutation without loss of heterozygosity were classified as TP53 single hit.19 FACT-BMT and EQ-5D-3L were collected during screening, and at the beginning of cycles 1, 2, and 4.

Exploratory studies

At the end of study, research error-corrected NGS-MRD (0.01% VAF sensitivity) was performed on matched, serial marrow samples at screening, day +28, day +100, 6 months, and 12 months. Immune studies were performed on peripheral blood before and on maintenance. Additional details on study design and exploratory studies are provided in the supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were based on the data cutoff date of 31 March 2025. Clinical characteristics, safety, and laboratory data were summarized descriptively. OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. CIR and NRM were estimated in competing risk frameworks, treating each other as a competing event. Cumulative incidence of acute and chronic GVHD were estimated in the competing risks framework, treating relapse or death without developing GVHD as a competing event. To identify factors associated with outcome, univariable Cox regression analysis was performed for OS, and Fine and Gray competing risks regression analysis was performed for relapse. Because of limited event size, multivariable analysis was not performed. QOL instruments were analyzed descriptively, with repeated measures analysis performed to test changes in scores for each subscale over time. All P values were 2-sided at the significance level of .05. Multiplicity was not considered. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC), and R version 4.3.2 (the CRAN project; www.cran.r-project.org).

Results

Patient and disease characteristics

Thirty patients received Ven/FluBu2 RIC HCT with intent to receive Ven/Dec-c maintenance (supplemental Figure 1), including 15 (50%) patients with AML, 14 (47%) with MDS, and 1 (3%) with MDS/MPN (Table 1). The median age was 69 years (range, 39-78) with 28 (93%) aged ≥60 years, and most were male (67%). All but 2 patients had 8/8 HLA-matched unrelated donors. Among 15 patients with AML, 13 (87%) had ELN 2022 adverse risk and 8 (27%) had therapy-related disease. By Revised IPSS and Molecular IPSS classifications,20,21 93% of patients with MDS or MDS/MPN had high- or very-high–risk disease (supplemental Figure 2); and 28 of 30 (93%) had <5% marrow blasts. Nineteen (63%) received previous Ven-based therapy. The median HCT-specific comorbidity index/age score was 3 (range, 0-7). Mutations in TP53 were detected by clinical NGS in 19 of 30 (63%) patients at diagnosis, including presence of 15 of 19 (79%) multihit and 4 of 19 (21%) single-hit allelic states.19 Among 21 consecutive patients of TP53-mutated MDS/AML who received RIC transplants in the 19 months the trial was open to enrollment at our institute, 19 were transplanted on trial and 2 received haploidentical transplants based on donor availability.

Early post-HCT outcomes

Safety was captured during the Ven/FluBu2 conditioning period (supplemental Table 1). Among 25 of 30 (83%) patients whose blood counts entered a nadir after conditioning chemotherapy, the median time to engraftment was 14 days (range, 6-18) for neutrophils and 17 days (range, 12-29) for platelets. Median donor chimerism for granulocytes was 100% at day +28 and at day +100, and for CD3+ T cells was 76% (interquartile range [IQR], 62-85) at day +28 and 79% (IQR, 67-92) at day +100 (supplemental Table 2). There were no primary or secondary graft failures. In the first weeks of HCT, there were three grade 2 uncomplicated infections (two urinary tract infections and one Clostridioides difficile infection). Acute GVHD grade II to IV occurred in 5 of 30 (four grade II events and one grade III event; supplemental Figure 3). Cumulative incidence of acute GVHD at 6-months was 13% (95% CI, 4.1-28) for grade II-IV and 3.3% (95% CI, 0.2-15) for grade 3/4. At 12-months, the cumulative incidence of acute GVHD was 16.7% (95% CI, 6-32) for grade II-IV. Among patients who received maintenance, the 1-year cumulative incidence of moderate/severe chronic GVHD was 31% (95% CI, 14-49), with only 1 patient experiencing severe disease (supplemental Table 3). Tac was tapered off after a median of 232 days (IQR, 170-301). No DLI was administered.

Maintenance: feasibility and toxicity

Of 30 patients, 26 (87%) received maintenance Ven/Dec-c, including 13 on DL1 and 13 on DL2 (supplemental Table 4); 4 (13%) patients did not start maintenance due to early morphologic relapse (n = 3, between 45 and 62 days) and 1 withdrew due to travel logistics. Maintenance started at a median of 55 days after transplant (range, 41-106). Median maintenance treatment duration was 308 days (IQR, 187-318). Median of 8 maintenance cycles were delivered (range, 1-8). Treatment completion rate was 65% (17/26). Nine patients discontinued maintenance due to relapse (n = 7) or GVHD (n = 2). Most common grade 3/4 treatment-emergent AE were decreased neutrophil count (92%), platelet count (54%), and white blood cell count (81%; Table 2; supplemental Table 5). Neutrophils nadired at day 29 (DL1, median 0.5 × 103/μL [IQR, 0.3 × 103 to 1.3 × 103/μL]; DL2, 0.6 × 103/μL [IQR, 0.3 × 103 to 1.2 × 103/μL]) and platelets at day 15 (DL1, median 77 × 103/μL [IQR, 45 × 103 to 94 × 103/μL]; DL2, 100 × 103/μL [IQR, 69 × 103 to 113 × 103/μL]; Figure 1). Although growth factors and transfusions were permitted during maintenance, no growth factors were administered and only 1 patient required transfusions (this patient had posttransplant pure red cell aplasia after ABO incompatibility). During maintenance, infections were uncommon, including 1 event of asymptomatic cytomegalovirus viremia (grade 2) and 1 event of pansensitive Klebsiella bacteremia complicated by grade 3 febrile neutropenia/sepsis, which was controlled in <24 hours. Gastrointestinal treatment-emergent AE were predominantly grade 1. Ven/Dec-c dose hold/reduction unrelated to drug-drug interactions occurred in 5 of 26 (19%) patients, including 1 patient requiring venetoclax dose reduction for uncomplicated recurrent neutropenia. No dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were observed. The RP2D of maintenance was determined to be Dec-c by mouth, daily on days 1 through 3, in combination with Ven 400 mg by mouth, daily on days 1 through 14.

Clinical outcomes

No treatment-related deaths occurred (NRM, 0%). Median donor chimerism for granulocytes was 100% at 6-months and 12-months, and CD3+ T cells was 83% (IQR, 78-91) at 6-months and 99% (IQR, 98-100) at 12-months. Median OS and PFS have not yet been reached. After a median follow-up time of 25.1 months (range, 15-33) in the intent-to-treat (ITT) population, 2-year OS was 67% (95% CI, 47-80), PFS was 53% (95% CI, 33-70), and CIR was 47% (95% CI, 26-65; Figure 2A; supplemental Table 6). Among 26 patients who received maintenance (modified ITT), 2-year OS was 77% (95% CI, 55-89), PFS was 62% (95% CI, 38-79), NRM was 0%, and CIR was 38% (95% CI, 18-59; Figure 2B). Among 14 of 30 patients who experienced relapsed disease, relapse occurred in 4 before, 7 during, and 3 after maintenance was completed (these three patients relapsed after an additional 215, 369, and 651 days, respectively).

Outcomes after Ven/FluBu2 allogeneic HCT followed by Ven/Dec-c maintenance. Kaplan-Meier estimates of OS, PFS, and CIR for the overall ITT population (A) and for the modified ITT population (B) that received the planned Ven/Dec-c maintenance therapy, with 95% CIs reported for each outcome.

Univariable analysis did not identify risk factors that affected OS, PFS, or likelihood of relapse, including age, donor recipient sex mismatch, cytomegalovirus serostatus, disease histology (MDS vs AML), previous Ven exposure, HCT-specific comorbidity index, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, presence of TP53 mutation at diagnosis, pre-HCT MFC MRD positivity, and disease risk index. At time of this report, 10 of 15 patients with AML and 9 of 15 patients with MDS remain alive. Clinical NGS was positive in 21 of 30 (70%) at screening, 6 of 30 (20%) at day +28, 6 of 28 (21%) at day +100, 6 of 26 (23%) at 6 months, and 0 of 19 (0%) at 12 months. MFC MRD positivity was identified in 12 of 30 (40%) at screening, 3 of 30 (10%) at day +28, 5 of 28 (18%) at day +100, 7 of 25 (28%) at 6 months, and 1 of 19 (5%) at 12 months (supplemental Figure 4). Despite MFC MRD negativity at day +28 in 27 of 30 patients, 11 of these 27 (41%) later had morphologic relapse, indicating that day +28 MRD negativity provides premature reassurance, which we previously reported.8

Subgroup analyses across pooled trial cohorts

To date, a total of 79 patients with MDS/AML received Ven/FluBu2 transplants on trial in three sequentially enrolled cohorts (Cohort 1, no planned maintenance7; Cohort 2, Ven/Aza maintenance8; and Cohort 3, Ven/Dec-c maintenance) with a median follow-up time of 35 months (range, 15-75). To assess the relative impact of modifying conditioning vs prophylactic maintenance therapy, we pooled cohorts and compared outcomes among those who received Ven/FluBu2 RIC transplantation, but had no evidence of morphologic relapse before day +70 and otherwise could be eligible for maintenance therapy (n = 67/79, “maintenance eligible cohort”). No differences were observed a difference in 2-year OS (P = .65), PFS (P = .69), or CIR (P = .47; supplemental Figure 5). Limitations include follow-up time and differences in study population in Cohort 1 vs Cohorts 2 and 3; for instance, only 2 of 19 (11%) who had previous Ven exposure and only 5 who had multihit TP53-mutated state in Cohort 1.7 Because the trial was enriched for those with extremely high relapse risk, we evaluated the impact of diagnostic TP53 allelic state on clinical outcome for all patients who received Ven/FluBu2 transplantations. Among the ITT population (N = 79), we observed a significantly improved 2-year OS in those with wild-type (77%; 95% CI, 58-88) vs single-hit TP53 (56%; 95% CI, 27-78) vs multihit TP53 (47%; 95% CI, 29-62; Figure 3A; P = .01). Two-year PFS was also significantly better in those with wild-type (74%; 95% CI, 55-86) vs single-hit TP53 (43%; 95% CI, 18-66) vs multihit TP53 (31%; 95% CI, 16-47; Figure 3B; P = .003). These significant differences based on TP53-mutation status were also preserved in the maintenance eligible cohort (n = 67), with improved 2-year OS (P = .003) and PFS (P = .0005; supplemental Table 7).

Exploratory studies

Serial NGS-MRD

We used ultrasensitive targeted error-corrected sequencing to identify and quantify gene mutations (referred to as NGS-MRD) in matched bone marrow specimens collected at screening (<28 days before transplant), day +28, day +100, 6 months, 12 months, or relapse/end of treatment (supplemental Tables 8 and 9). Based on routine clinical sequencing at diagnosis, 27 of 30 patients had trackable molecular alterations, 2 had no mutations despite complete assessment, and 1 had incomplete assessment. At screening, 25 of 27 patients (96.3%) had at least 1 persistent driver mutation, with 1 patient having a known driver mutation despite missing diagnostic sample, which we collectively defined as NGS-MRD positive (Figure 4A). In total, 19 of 27 (70%) patients had persistent MRD positivity at day +28 and/or day +100, including 16 of 19 patients (84%) with a TP53 mutation and 3 of 8 (38%) patients without a TP53 mutation. In matched bone marrow biopsy samples collected at maintenance screening (day +28), NGS-MRD was detected in 12 of 27 (44%), compared to 6 of 30 (20%) by clinical NGS (3% VAF sensitivity) and 3 of 30 (10%) by flow MRD assays.

In this study, 19 of 19 patients (100%) with TP53-mutated disease had detectable TP53 mutations before HCT (median VAF of 1.79% [range, 0.1%-39.6%]; Figure 4B). There was no difference in survival for those with pre-HCT TP53 based on VAF cutoff of ≥1% vs <1% (P = .6; Figure 4C), as previously described in TP53-mutated MDS.19 Patients with pre-HCT non-TP53 mutations had favorable outcomes, including 7 of 9 (78%) patients alive in durable remission. Among TP53-mutated patients (n = 18) alive at day +100 after HCT, 4 were TP53-MRD negative and 14 were TP53-MRD positive, of whom 7 of 14 (50%) subsequently had morphologic relapse (Figure 4D). TP53-MRD clearance was achieved on maintenance in the remaining 7 of 14 patients (50%) at 6 months (n = 5) and at 12 months (n = 2); 6 of these 7 patients had TP53 multihit mutations.

Donor immune reconstitution

Months 3 to 6 after HCT is a critical window for immune reconstitution. We thus performed exploratory BH3 profiling on immune cells in the peripheral blood to evaluate for increased vulnerability on therapy. We observed a trend in increasing BCL2 vulnerability in CD19+ B cells over time, and no differences in overall baseline apoptotic priming were observed in CD4+ or CD8+ T cells, regulatory T cells, or B cells (supplemental Figure 6). We next examined the impact of therapy on donor immune reconstitution at 1, 2, 3, (or day +100), 6, and 12 months after transplant (supplemental Figure 7). Exploratory studies confirmed no impedance in the recovery of CD3+ T cells, CD4+ T cells (conventional and regulatory), CD8+ T cells, or CD56+ natural killer cells on maintenance compared with historical controls (matched by FluBu2 and Tac/Mtx GVHD prophylaxis; supplemental Tables 10 and 11). CD19+ B-cell expansion was blunted as early as day +100 and throughout the first year of transplant (median, 60 cells per μL [IQR, 32.6-91.4] vs median 24.5 cells per μL [IQR, 7.2-53.2]; P = .007). We,8 and others,22 previously reported this B-cell pattern in patients who received maintenance Ven/Aza after transplant. Because T-cell exhaustion is a potential mechanism for leukemia relapse after transplant,23 we performed an exploratory subset analysis and found no changes in CD8+PD1+ or CD8+TIM3+ expression based on TP53-mutated disease status, but observed an early relative reduction in CD8+TIM3+ expression among those on maintenance vs historical control.

Patient-reported outcomes

As another measure of treatment tolerability, we explored QOL before and during the first 4 cycles (supplemental Table 12). Although FACT-BMT scores were generally stable across domains before and during the three times points after HCT, the emotional well-being domain improved during maintenance (supplemental Figure 8; P = .008). The overall health score of the EQ-5D visual analog scale was also largely stable, for example with a mean of 75.5 during screening and a mean of 76.5 on day 1 of cycle 2.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first report of safety, feasibility, and efficacy with fully oral Ven/Dec-c after transplant maintenance therapy in patients with poor-risk MDS and AML undergoing Ven/FluBu2 with Tac/Mtx GVHD prophylaxis. Myelosuppression is an expected concern with Ven/HMA therapy; however, at the reduced doses of maintenance treatment provided in this trial, neutropenia was uncomplicated. Treatment dose delays/reductions were infrequent (19%), and no growth factors were needed to facilitate count recovery. Infections were infrequent with antibacterial prophylaxis. We observed 0% NRM, which we similarly observed with Ven/FluBu2 transplants in our previously published cohort,8 and a very low rate of acute GVHD. QOL as measured by 2 validated instruments was overall stable. As posttransplant cyclophosphamide was recently established as the standard prophylaxis for RIC transplants,15 we just completed enrollment to a separate patient cohort to assess the safety Ven/FluBu2 followed by posttransplant cyclophosphamide GVHD prophylaxis and feasibility of prophylactic maintenance initiation in this context (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03613532, Cohort 4).24

Ven/FluBu2 transplantation with Tac/Mtx GVHD prophylaxis was well tolerated, which enabled outpatient transplantation, prompt maintenance initiation (87%), and importantly therapy completion (65%). With this regimen, >80% received ≥4 cycles, and a median of 8 cycles were delivered. This is in contrast to the French multicenter study (GFM-DACORAL-DLI), which had similar 1-year disease-free survival, in which 67% of patients who underwent RIC Fludarabine, cytarabine, and amsacrine (FLAMSA) transplantation went on to receive single-agent Dec-c maintenance with or without DLI, and only 14 of 34 (41%) completed all 10 planned protocol-cycles.25 Also this is in contrast to the randomized study of single-agent Aza maintenance vs observation for MDS/AML, in which only 28% completed all 12-planned Aza cycles and only a median of 4 cycles was delivered.5 Because IV/SC maintenance regimen requires at least60 infusion encounters, an all-oral approach reduces patient burden. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the benefits of HMA/Ven have not been confirmed for frontline intermediate- or higher-risk MDS or as posttransplant maintenance.26,27 However, HMA/Ven is an active AML regimen in frontline, MRD, and relapsed AML settings. Given the encouraging clinical outcomes and demonstration of tolerability with Ven/Dec-c in our study, we propose the potential clinical utility may be limited to those with exceedingly high risk and can be further focused on those with persistent MRD.

Because this study was not randomized, we cannot conclude the extent to which Ven/FluBu2 (modified RIC) and/or Ven/Dec-c (prophylactic maintenance) contributed to the encouraging outcomes. As a next step, the National Cancer Institute Myelomatch/BMT CTN randomized trial (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06954987) will assess the addition of Ven to RIC chemotherapy transplantation for patients with AML in first complete remission with day +100 event-free survival (event defined as MFC MRD positivity, morphologic relapse, or death) as the primary end point. Results are also eagerly awaited from ongoing maintenance trials with oral HMA alone (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT04173533, NCT04742634, and NCT04980404) and combination Ven/Aza (NCT04161885, details on not meeting primary OS end point are not available), which will provide additional insight on the role of maintenance. We caution that maintenance trials enrolling broad AML subgroups or non–adverse risk disease may be underpowered to assess benefit in high-risk subgroups (such as those enrolled in this trial) who are most likely to experience relapse. In our study, we observed favorable survival after transplant in high-risk subgroups without diagnostic mutation in TP53. Even so, outcomes based on TP53 allelic state for those that received Ven/Dec-c maintenance were striking compared with previously reported data in which OS in patients with TP53 mutations who receive RIC HCT was 23% at 3 years.3,19,28

Despite universally poor-risk disease diagnostics in our study population, monitoring NGS-MRD status (presence vs absence, TP53 vs non-TP53) at serial time points revealed potential therapeutic opportunities in the relapse preventive (MRD negative) and relapse preemptive (MRD positive) settings. Patients with detectable non-TP53 MRD before transplant had outstanding outcomes. We found that all patients with TP53-mutated disease had persistent pre-HCT TP53-MRD positivity, and that deeper reduction of pre-HCT TP53 VAF was not associated with improved post-HCT survival, which reinforces emerging data that quantitative TP53 VAF reduction is not a requirement for proceeding to transplant.1,19,29 Among TP53-mutated patients, a minority achieved TP53-mutation clearance by day +100, and these patients also had exceptional outcomes. Among 14 patients with detectable TP53-MRD at day +100, 7 of 14 (50%) experience relapsed disease and died early, suggesting modified conditioning was insufficient to prevent relapse in these cases. The remaining 7 of 14 (50%) cleared their TP53-mutated clone during maintenance. Thus, for patients with TP53 disease, NGS-MRD status at day +100 after HCT appears to be a clinically relevant time point, and our results indicate that the suppression of TP53 clones after HCT more closely correlates with improved outcomes than pretransplant factors. Lastly, all relapses were preceded by the reemergence of NGS-MRD positivity, indicating a therapeutic opportunity. Together, these results indicate that serial NGS-MRD may be a valuable tool for the dynamic assessment of relapse risk and for guiding relapse-mitigation strategies.

A concern that clinicians may have about adding even all-oral maintenance therapies after the intense experience of HCT is that exhausted patients will have decreases in QOL associated with such a strategy. Our exploratory analysis of QOL suggest that this may not be the case, and that emotional well-being may even improve in later post-HCT maintenance cycles. Although our data cannot tell us what would have happened to QOL without maintenance therapy, indeed it may have gotten better rather than stayed stable, our QOL findings are informative with respect to post-HCT maintenance approaches.

We conclude that the safety and promising efficacy of Ven/FluBu2 with Tac/Mtx GVHD prophylaxis followed by Ven/Dec-c maintenance therapy support continued development of this entire regimen in MDS/AML, particularly in poor-risk subgroups and in those with detectable pre-HCT MRD. We recommend inclusion of serial NGS-MRD measurements in transplant studies to identify patients most likely to benefit from modified transplant approaches and to elucidate treatment opportunities.
