Comparison of clinical and histopathological features between serum IgG4-negative and serum IgG4-positive patients with type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis: A single-center, retrospective study
Authors: Xiaorong Tian, Dongling Wan, Hanbin Zhu, Wanshun Li, Yuyan Zhou, Hanxiao Cui, Jiaheng Xu, Zhenghui Yang, Jiayu Li, Deyu Zhang, Liqi Sun, Zhendong Jin, Haojie Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:In type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP), serum immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) has a complex correlation with pathology. In this study, clinical/histopathological traits of serum IgG4-negative/positive (N/P) patients were compared, and serum IgG4-tissue IgG4 correlations were explored to aid accurate diagnosis/typing.Material and Methods:A retrospective analysis was performed on 78 type 1 AIP patients (screened from 160 eligible cases through inclusion/exclusion criteria, International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria-confirmed at Changhai Hospital (December 2019-2023). At diagnosis, patients with serum IgG4 <135 mg/dL (n = 19) were assigned to the serum IgG4-N group, and those with serum IgG4 ≥135 mg/dL (n = 59) were assigned to the serum IgG4-P group. Clinical baseline data, laboratory indicators, imaging features (computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging/endoscopic ultrasound guided [EUS]), and histopathological findings (from EUS fine-needle aspiration/fine-needle biopsy [EUS-FNA/FNB] or surgery) were compared. Statistical analysis (R 4.4.0) was conducted with Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests (categorical data) and Mann‒Whitney U tests (continuous data); Spearman correlation was used to assess serum IgG4-tissue IgG4 links. A Bonferroni-corrected P <0.050 was considered to indicate statistical significance.Results:No significant differences in sex, age, most comorbidities (e.g., hypertension), or symptoms (e.g., abdominal discomfort) were observed between the serum IgG4-N and serum IgG4-P groups (all P > 0.050). The rate of bile duct stenosis was higher in the serum IgG4-P group than in the serum IgG4-N group (55.93% vs. 26.32%, P = 0.047), as were the rates of EUS examination (84.75% vs. 52.63%, P = 0.010), EUS-FNA/FNB (84.75% vs. 52.63%, P = 0.010). The 84.75% vs 52.63% (P = 0.010) represents the EUS examination receiving rate between serum IgG4-P and serum IgG4-N groups, rather than the diagnostic positive rate of FNA/FNB subtypes. The serum IgG4-P group also had a higher histopathological confirmation rate (71.19% vs. 36.84%, P = 0.015) and a greater median number of tissue IgG4-P cells (36 vs. 8 cells per high-power field (HPF), P = 0.035). In contrast, the serum IgG4-N group had a higher median carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) level (32.14 vs. 5.97 U/mL, P = 0.035), a higher rate of long/multiple pancreatic duct narrow sections (30% vs. 2%, P = 0.013), and a higher surgical rate (47.37% vs. 15.25%, P = 0.010). Across both groups, no significant correlation was detected between serum IgG4 levels and tissue IgG4-P plasma cell counts (Spearman’s rho = 0.113, P = 0.322; linear regression coefficient = 0.004, P = 0.538).Conclusion:Serum IgG4 typing is associated with the clinical-pathological features of type 1 AIP. The serum IgG4-P group often shows biliary duct stenosis and diffuse pancreatic enlargement, with a reliance on EUS puncture. The serum IgG4-N group has higher CA19-9 and a higher surgical rate. Pathological examination remains critical (no serum-tissue IgG4 association). This study supports optimized, accurate diagnosis and stratified management of type 1 AIP.

INTRODUCTION

Autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP), first proposed by Yoshida et al.[1], is classified into types 1 and 2 and, recently, “type 3 (unspecified).”[2,3] Type 1 AIP, also referred to as lymphoplasmacytic sclerosing pancreatitis, is a key manifestation of immunoglobulin G4-related disease (IgG4-RD), defined by periductal lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, storiform fibrosis, occlusive phlebitis, and often elevated serum IgG4.[2,4,5] It is more common in Asians than Europeans/ Americans,[6] and while AIP accounts for only 6% of chronic pancreatitis,[7,8] its prevalence has risen (e.g., the incidence of type 1 AIP in Japan has increased from 0.8 to 3.1/100,000, 2007-2016),[9] warranting clinical attention.

The 2011 International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria (ICDC) integrates serum IgG4 levels, imaging, extrapancreatic involvement, pathology, and glucocorticoid response for AIP diagnosis.[10] Long recognized that as the core serological biomarker for type 1 AIP, serum IgG4 is a subtype of IgG: It maintains immune homeostasis by participating in immune regulation and suppressing excessive inflammation, and its abnormal elevation is closely linked to the immune-mediated inflammatory mechanism of type 1 AIP.[11] A study has shown that the sensitivity and specificity of serum IgG4 for type 1 AIP diagnosis are 72 and 93%, respectively.[12] However, 10-30% of type 1 AIP patients have normal serum IgG4 levels (<135 mg/dL), defined as serum IgG4-negative (IgG4-N).[13,14] In contrast, patients with serum IgG4 concentrations ≥135 mg/dL are classified as serum IgG4-positive (IgG4-P). These serum IgG4-N patients face significant diagnostic dilemmas: their misdiagnosis rate is notably higher. In addition, for those with pancreas-limited disease, the rate of unnecessary surgical interventions is significantly higher than that in serum IgG4-P patients, due mainly to the difficulty in distinguishing AIP from pancreatic cancer.[15] Studies on differences between serum IgG4-N and serum IgG4-P patients are conflicting (e.g., recurrence rate discrepancies).[13,14] Pathology is critical for diagnosis (ICDC requires >10 IgG4-P cells/high-power field [HPF]),[10] with most tissue obtained through endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration/biopsy (EUS-FNA/FNB).[16,17] However, existing research focuses on EUS-FNA/FNB method/needle type effects on diagnosis,[16-19] with few studies comparing histopathological features (e.g., tissue IgG4 count, confirmation rate) between serum IgG4-N and serum IgG4-P patients or exploring serum IgG4-tissue IgG4 correlation,[15] a gap limiting type 1 AIP typing and management.

This study retrospectively analyzed 78 type 1 AIP patients (Changhai Hospital Affiliated with Naval Medical University, Dec 2019-2023) stratified by serum IgG4 (serum IgG4-N: <135 mg/dL; serum IgG4-P: ≥135 mg/dL). Clinical baseline, laboratory/imaging data, diagnosis-treatment strategies, and histopathology were compared between groups, and serum IgG4-tissue IgG4 correlations were explored. The goal was to clarify the association of serum IgG4 typing with type 1 AIP clinicopathological features, providing a basis for accurate diagnosis and stratified management.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design

This study initially included 160 patients who were diagnosed with type 1 AIP at the hospital between December 2019-2023. On the basis of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, data from 78 patients were ultimately included in the subsequent retrospective analysis. This study aimed to compare the clinical and histopathological characteristics between serum IgG4-N (IgG4 <135 mg/dL) and serum IgG4-P (IgG4 ≥135 mg/dL) patients with type 1 AIP and explore the correlation between serum IgG4 levels and the number of tissue IgG4-P plasma cells. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and its protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Changhai Hospital (Ethics No.: CHEC2025-402). As this was a retrospective study, it was exempt from obtaining informed consent from patients. Patient anonymity was strictly maintained throughout the study, and no figures in this research posed a risk of patient identification.

In accordance with the 2018 Japanese Pancreas Society criteria, an serum IgG4 concentration ≥135 mg/dL was designated as the threshold for identifying elevated IgG4 levels. Patients with a serum IgG4 level ≥135 mg/dL were classified into the “IgG4-positive group,” whereas those with a serum IgG4 level <135 mg/dL were classified into the “IgG4-negative group.”[20] No additional control group was established, as the study focused on comparing the two subgroups within type 1 AIP.

Baseline characteristics and laboratory tests

Baseline characteristics included sex, age, main symptoms at admission, smoking history, drinking history, family history, and medical history of diabetes and hypertension. In the medical records, patients who smoked ≥5 cigarettes/day were considered to abuse tobacco and were recorded as having a smoking history.[21] Drinking history was defined as alcohol consumption ≥50 g/day.[22] Family history was recorded if first-degree relatives (e.g., parents, children) or second-degree relatives (e.g., grandparents) of patients had a history of diseases related to immune regulation or pancreatic lesions, such as autoimmune diseases (especially IgG4-RD, Sjögren’s syndrome) and pancreatic diseases. Registered laboratory tests included mainly serum IgG4, total bilirubin (TB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and CA19-9 levels. All laboratory tests were conducted following standard clinical protocols at Changhai Hospital.

Imaging examinations

Imaging data included mainly computed tomography (CT) (plain scan and enhanced scan) (Aquilion ONE, Canon Medical Systems Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (plain scan and enhanced scan) (3.0 T Signa HDxt and MR750, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA; MAGNETOM 3 T Skyra and 1.5 T Avanto, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), and EUS.

EUS examinations were performed using either an EUS system with an EU-ME1 or EU-ME2 model (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), an Aloka Alpha-5 model (Aloka, Tokyo, Japan), or a UMG20-29R ultrasonic probe (frequency: 20 MHz; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Among the registered patients, most had both CT and MRI data. For study purposes, the results of the two were not clearly distinguished; a more detailed and accurate description was used as the standard. The plain and enhanced scan results of both methods were evaluated, and the more accurate results were recorded. Some patients who underwent surgery had no EUS-related results. In accordance with the ICDC,[10] the specific involvement of the pancreatic parenchyma (PP) and ducts was determined in all three types of imaging data. On the basis of the collected case data, the PP was ultimately categorized into three types: Diffuse pancreatic enlargement, focal mass, and essentially normal. Pancreatic duct imaging results were mainly categorized as follows: Long-segment/multiple-segment stricture, focal main pancreatic duct (PD) dilatation, essentially normal, PD penetration sign, and unclear description of the PD. Cases with rare PD penetration signs and unclear PD descriptions were classified as “others.” Extrapancreatic organ involvement was counted in the three sets of imaging data, with the most accurate and comprehensive description as the final statistical result. All the imaging evaluations were conducted by experienced radiologists from Changhai Hospital with more than 15 years of experience in imaging interpretation. “To evaluate interobserver agreement for imaging assessments, 20% of the CT/MRI/EUS images were randomly selected (n = 16). Two experienced radiologists (with ≥15 years of experience in pancreatic imaging) independently performed blind evaluations of these images (grouping information was masked). Interrater consistency was quantified using the kappa statistic.”

Histopathological diagnosis of type 1 AIP

All histopathological samples in this study were obtained through EUS-FNA/FNB or surgery. For EUS-FNA/FNB, endoscopists involved in the study had 8–30 years of practice on average, performed no fewer than 1,000 EUS-related procedures annually, and used puncture needles from multiple manufacturers: Wilson-Cook (Wilson-Cook Medical, Inc., Winston-Salem, NC, USA), Boston Scientific (Boston Scientific Corporation, Medi-Globe GmbH, Achenmühle, Germany), Olympus (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and COOK Echo Tip series (ECHO-22, ECHO-25, ECHO-HD-22-C, ECHO-HD-25-C; Cook Medical, Winston-Salem, NC, USA).

Surgical pathological samples were obtained by pancreatic surgeons from Changhai Hospital who had 10–15 years of experience on average and high-level surgical skills. After the obtained pancreatic tissues were fixed in formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (thickness: 3-4 μm), hematoxylin- eosin (HE) and IgG4 immunohistochemical staining procedures were performed sequentially. For HE staining, HE Y (Beyotime, Cat. No.: C0105S, China) solutions were used. The procedures included deparaffinization and rehydration, nuclear staining with hematoxylin (room temperature, 5-8 min), differentiation with hydrochloric acid-ethanol, blue staining with ammonia water, and cytoplasmic staining with eosin (3-5 min). The process was completed by gradient ethanol dehydration, xylene clearing, and mounting with neutral balsam. For IgG4 immunohistochemical staining, the key reagents used were rabbit anti-human IgG4 monoclonal primary antibody (Abcam, Cat. No.: ab109493, dilution 1:2000, UK), enzyme-labeled goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer (ZSGB-BIO, Cat. No.: PV-6000D, China), 10 mM citrate antigen retrieval buffer (pH 6.0) (Absin, Cat. No.: abs9249, China), and a DAB chromogenic kit (ZSGB-BIO, Cat. No.: PV-6000D, China). Deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking with 3% hydrogen peroxide and 5% bovine serum albumin were performed, after which the sections were incubated with primary antibody at 4°C overnight, followed by secondary antibody at 37°C for 30 min. After the DAB chromogenic reaction (monitored under a microscope for 5-8 min) and hematoxylin counterstaining, dehydration, clearing, and mounting were finally completed to detect IgG4-P plasma cells in the tissues.

The main histopathological indicators included (1) periductal lymphoplasmacytic infiltration with fibrosis and no granulocyte infiltration, (2) storiform fibrosis, (3) occlusive phlebitis, and (4) a large number of IgG4-P cells (>10 cells/HPF). Samples meeting at least 3 of the above indicators were classified as Level 1, and those meeting at least 2 were classified as Level 2. Both Level 1 and Level 2 were regarded as confirmed histopathological diagnoses.[10] For samples not meeting the above confirmed criteria, two non-confirmed categories were defined as follows: [10] (1) suspected type 1 AIP: Cases meeting 1 Level 2 indicator (e.g., serum IgG4 1-2× upper limit of normal, 1 core pathological feature of type 1 AIP, or suggestive imaging findings) plus a positive steroid response but failing to reach the definitive diagnosis threshold and (2) insufficient histopathological evidence for type 1 AIP: Cases lacking core diagnostic features of type 1 AIP (e.g., IgG4-P plasma cells <10 cells/HPF, no periductal lymphoplasmacytic infiltration or storiform fibrosis) or with inadequate specimen quality precluding effective pathological evaluation, and no supportive serological/imaging evidence.

Histopathological interpretation was conducted by experienced pathologists from Changhai Hospital with more than 15 years of experience in pathological interpretation; any discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a senior pathologist with more than 20 years of experience. “For interobserver agreement of pathological assessments, 20% of the histopathological slides (n = 16) were randomly selected. Two senior pathologists (with ≥15 years of experience in pancreatic pathology) independently conducted blinded reviews of these slides. The kappa statistic was used to measure the consistency of pathological classification (confirmed/suspected/insufficient evidence).”

Statistical analysis

All the statistical calculations, charts, and figures were generated using R software (Version 4.4.0; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages) [n(%)], with the Chi-square test (χ2 test) or Fisher’s exact test used for between-group comparisons based on the data distribution. The normality of continuous variables was assessed through the Shapiro-Wilk test, confirming all continuous variables were skewed (all P <0.05). Continuous variables with skewed distributions are expressed as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) [M(Q1, Q3)], and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for between-group comparisons. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was used to evaluate the association between serum IgG4 levels and tissue IgG4 levels. Bonferroni correction was applied to P values for multiple tests in all intergroup comparisons and correlation analyses. All the statistical analyses utilized two-tailed tests, with P <0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

RESULTS

Overall characteristics of the study subjects

A total of 78 patients with pancreatic diseases were enrolled in the study. Males accounted for 83.33% (65/78) and females accounted for 16.67% (13/78), which is consistent with the clinical feature of male predominance in AIP. The median age was 61.5 years (IQR, 52.25-69 years), which was concentrated in the middle-aged and elderly population and aligned with the typical age of onset for AIP.

Among the comorbidities, diabetes mellitus (23.08%) and hypertension (16.67%) were the most common. Only 1 patient (1.28%) had a family history of pancreatic diseases, a finding that might be attributed to the small sample size. A history of smoking (38.46%) and alcohol consumption (19.23%) was noted; these factors might contribute to disease development through chronic pancreatic injury.

The main clinical symptoms were abdominal discomfort (62.82%) and obstructive jaundice (46.15%); 24.36% of patients presented with both, indicating biliary-pancreatic obstruction in some cases. Weight loss (11.54%) and diarrhea (5.13%) were relatively rare, suggesting that the disease might affect pancreatic exocrine function, but to a mild degree [Table 1].

The median serum IgG4 concentration was 360 mg/dL, with 75.64% (59/78) of patients having serum IgG4 concentrations ≥135 mg/dL, indicating that the study population was predominantly composed of patients with IgG4-related disease. Certain liver function indicators were elevated, which was consistent with cholestasis caused by obstructive jaundice. The median CA19-9 concentration was 7 U/mL, and most values were within the normal range, potentially suggesting a low likelihood of malignant tumors.

EUS was performed in 76.92% of the patients (60/78). Diffuse pancreatic enlargement (48.72%) and bile duct stenosis (48.72%) were common findings, consistent with the AIP manifestations. Twenty-seven patients (34.62%) were suspected of having malignancy, necessitating pathological differentiation, which highlighted the importance and necessity of pathological diagnosis.

EUS-guided puncture was performed in 60 patients (76.92%). For needle selection, 22G needles were predominantly used (57.69%), this choice helps balance the yield of tissue samples and procedural safety. In terms of puncture sites, the procedure was mainly concentrated in the pancreatic body (37.18%) and head (34.62%).

Pathologically, 61.54% (48/78) had >10 IgG4-P plasma cells per HPF [Table 1].

Distribution of different clinical features in the serum IgG4-N and serum IgG4-P groups

There were no significant differences in gender distribution between the serum IgG4-N group (serum IgG4 <135 mg/dL) and the serum IgG4-P group (serum IgG4 ≥135 mg/dL) (P = 0.724) [Figure 1a(a)].

In terms of clinical symptoms, abdominal discomfort (P = 0.758) [Figure 1a(b)] and weight loss (P = 0.210) [Figure S1a(a)] were more common in the serum IgG4-N group; in contrast, obstructive jaundice (P = 0.230) [Figure 1a(c)], abdominal discomfort combined with obstructive jaundice (P = 1.000) [Figure S1a(b)], and diarrhea (P = 0.567) [Figure S1a(c)] were more prevalent in the serum IgG4-P group. The proportion of patients with diabetes was higher in the serum IgG4-P group (P = 0.211) [Figure S1a(d)]; however, the prevalence of smoking history was slightly higher in the serum IgG4-N group (P = 0.518), while there were no significant differences in the incidence of hypertension (P = 1.000) or alcohol consumption history (P = 1.000) between the two groups [Figure S1a(e-g)]. A family history of pancreatic diseases was rare in both groups (P = 0.244), which might be attributed to the small sample size [Figure S1a(h)].

In terms of imaging findings, the distribution of imaging-related malignant tumor signs (imaging malignant tumor [MT]) was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.286) [Figure 1a(d)]. With respect to CT/MRI findings, focal masses were more prominent in the serum IgG4-P group (P = 0.880) [Figure S1a(i)], whereas the proportion of PD stenosis was greater in the serum IgG4-N group (P = 0.289) [Figure S1a(j)].

EUS was performed more often in the serum IgG4-P group (P = 0.010) [Figure S1a(k)], and the distribution of EUS punctures was comparable between the two groups (P = 0.010) [Figure S1a(l)]. Diffuse pancreatic enlargement and focal masses were more prominent in the serum IgG4-P group (P = 0.043) [Figure 1a(e)]. There were significant differences in the distribution of PD (EUS-PD) signs between the two groups (P = 0.003) [Figure 1a(f)]. The proportions of bile duct stenosis (P = 0.047; Figure 1a(g)) and kidney involvement (P = 0.567; Figure 1a(h)) were greater in the serum IgG4-P group, whereas the distribution of retroperitoneal fibrosis was similar between the two groups (P = 1.000) [Figure 1a(i)].

The surgical rate was higher in the serum IgG4-N group (P = 0.010) [Figure 1a(j)]. In contrast, EUS-FNA/FNB was more commonly used in the serum IgG4-P group (P = 0.024) [Figure 1a(k)]. Both groups preferred 22G needles for puncture, whereas 19G and 25G needles were less frequently used (P = 0.013) [Figure 1a(l)]; this pattern was also observed in the supplementary analysis of needle type distribution (P = 0.042) [Figure S1a(m)]. In addition, the histopathological confirmation rate was significantly higher in the serum IgG4-P group (P = 0.007) [Figure 1a(m)]; and the proportion of patients with high-level pathological evidence (Level 1 and Level 2) was also significantly higher in this group (P = 0.024) [Figure 1a(n)]. Furthermore, a higher proportion of patients in the serum IgG4-P group had >10 IgG4-P cells/HPF (P = 0.083) [Figure 1a(o)]. After further stratifying the IgG4-P cell counts into more detailed subgroups (0–10, 10–20, 20–50, >50 cells/HPF), the trend of intergroup differences in cell count distribution reflected by Figure 1a(p) was consistent with that of Figure 1a(o) (P = 0.221) [Figure 1a(p)].

No significant differences in age distribution were observed between the two groups, with both groups predominantly composed of middle-aged and elderly individuals (P = 0.495) [Figure 1b(a)]. Liver function indicators (TB, ALT, AST) were similar between the serum IgG4-N and serum IgG4-P groups (P = 0.334, P = 0.784, P = 0.903, respectively), while elevated CA19-9 levels were more common in the serum IgG4-N group (P = 0.035) [Figure 1b(b-e)].

Differences in clinical features between the serum IgG4-N and serum IgG4-P groups

No statistically significant differences were detected between the serum IgG4-N and serum IgG4-P groups in terms of gender, age, comorbidities (hypertension, smoking/ alcohol consumption history), or most clinical symptoms (abdominal discomfort, diarrhea, etc.) (all P > 0.050) [Table S1]. The proportion of patients with obstructive jaundice was larger in the serum IgG4-P group (50.85% vs. 31.58%, P = 0.230), but the difference was not significant; the proportion of weight loss was slightly larger in the serum IgG4-N group (21.05%), which was also not statistically significant (P = 0.210) [Table S1].

The core differences between the two groups were concentrated in serum IgG4 and CA19-9 levels: the median serum IgG4 concentration in the serum IgG4-P group (715 mg/dL vs. 67 mg/dL, P < 0.001) was significantly greater than that in the serum IgG4-N group, verifying the validity of the grouping; the median CA19-9 concentration in the serum IgG4-N group (32.14 U/mL vs. 5.97 U/mL, P = 0.035) was significantly greater than that in the serum IgG4-P group. No significant differences in TB, ALT, or AST levels were detected between the two groups (all P > 0.05) [Table S2].

To verify the reliability of the imaging assessments, we conducted an interobserver agreement analysis: Twenty percent of the CT/MRI/EUS images were randomly selected and independently evaluated by two senior radiologists (with ≥15 years of pancreatic imaging experience) in a blinded manner, yielding a kappa value of 0.82 (excellent agreement).

The incidence of bile duct stenosis was significantly higher in the serum IgG4-P group (55.93% vs. 26.32%, P = 0.047) than in the serum IgG4-N group [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

In this study, clinical data from 78 patients with type 1 AIP admitted to Changhai Hospital Affiliated with Naval Medical University were analyzed, and the serum IgG4 level was used as the core stratification index. We systematically compared clinical characteristics, imaging findings, diagnosis-treatment strategies, and histopathological features between the serum IgG4-N (serum IgG4 <135 mg/dL) and serum IgG4-P (serum IgG4 ≥135 mg/dL) groups and explored the association between serum IgG4 levels and the number of tissue IgG4-P plasma cells.

Notably, this study fills a critical gap in the diagnosis and treatment of type 1 AIP in Chinese populations. Existing international studies include Japanese studies (including nationwide surveys[14] and prospective multicenter trials[23]), Korean single-center analyses,[15] Western cohort studies on pancreatic damage,[24] and discussions on relapse risk factors,[25] all have confirmed the value of serum IgG4 stratification, but their findings have limitations for Chinese clinical practice: Katz et al.[24] focused on pancreatic damage (exocrine/endocrine insufficiency) in Western populations and lacked data on serum IgG4-stratified puncture strategies; Jaber et al.[25] highlighted the association of high serum IgG4/IgA with relapse but lacked an analysis of IgG4-negative AIP diagnosis and Chinese-specific misdiagnosis factors. Domestic studies on Chinese populations have focused mainly on differential diagnosis from pancreatic cancer,[26] stratification by pancreatic enlargement type[27] or single-dimensional correlation between serum IgG4 and clinical features[28] and lack the systematic integration of “serum IgG4 stratification+EUS puncture+serum-tissue IgG4 correlation” as well as analyses of CA19-9-related misdiagnosis clues. The present study, which is based on 78 Chinese type 1 AIP patients, is the first to address these core clinical dilemmas, providing targeted evidence for Chinese clinical practice.

Compared with the serum IgG4-N group, the serum IgG4-P group had a higher incidence of obstructive jaundice (50.85% vs. 31.58%, P = 0.230) and bile duct stenosis (55.93% vs. 26.32%, P = 0.047), which aligns with findings from Japanese, American, and Chinese scholars and relevant international studies.[14,24,25,28-30] A nationwide study by Japanese scholar Sano et al.[14] revealed that type 1 AIP patients with serum IgG4 ≥135 mg/dL had a 40.7% biliary involvement rate, which was higher than the 30.6% rate in serum IgG4-N patients; Zhou et al.[28] and Katz et al.[24] also confirmed that high serum IgG4 levels are associated with more active immune status, higher biliary involvement, and increased pancreatic damage (exocrine/endocrine insufficiency), collectively supporting our results. The serum IgG4-N group also had a significantly higher median level of the tumor marker CA19-9 (32.14 U/mL vs. 5.97 U/mL in the serum IgG4-P group, P = 0.035), which is consistent with the findings of Chinese scholars.[26] Chong et al.[31] further verified that elevated CA19-9 in IgG4-RD is linked to the disease itself rather than to serum IgG4 interference. These findings supplement the high misdiagnosis rate of focal AIP (47.06%) reported by Han et al.[27] and address the diagnostic challenge of serum IgG4-N AIP highlighted by Jaber et al.,[25] clarifying that “serum IgG4 negativity + CA19-9 elevation” is the core driver of misdiagnosis in Chinese patients, especially those with focal lesions. Owing to the lack of serological diagnostic support, the serum IgG4-N group had a higher proportion of patients with suspected malignancy (MT) by imaging (47.37% vs. 30.51% in the serum IgG4-P group). Since AIP and pancreatic cancer have overlapping imaging features, clinicians often use surgery to rule out malignancy, which explains why the serum IgG4-N group had a significantly higher surgical rate (47.37% vs. 15.25% in the serum IgG4-P group, P = 0.010). This finding is consistent with the conclusions of the Korean scholar Paik et al.[15] (75% vs. 26%, P = 0.018) and the Japanese scholars Sano et al.[14] (9.2% vs. 4.6%) and Katz et al.[24] (8% pancreatic resection rate in AIP patients), confirming that serum IgG4-N type 1 AIP has a globally higher surgical rate, which is particularly prominent in Chinese populations due to the high misdiagnosis risk.

For the serum IgG4-P group, serological indicators suggested the presence of AIP, so clinicians tended to use EUS to confirm typical imaging features and perform EUS-FNA/ FNB for histopathological evidence, avoiding surgical trauma. In contrast, the serum IgG4-N group often presented seronegativity and PD strictures; their imaging results overlapped strongly with those of pancreatic cancer and chronic pancreatitis, making noninvasive diagnosis difficult and leading to higher surgical rates. Notably, both groups predominantly used 22G needles for EUS puncture (60% in the serum IgG4-N group; 78% in the serum IgG4-P group). The puncture sites were concentrated in the pancreatic body or head, with no significant intergroup differences (P > 0.050). This finding was likely because 22G needles balance tissue sampling quality and safety, reducing the risk of complications such as bleeding and pancreatic fistula, which is consistent with international studies confirming the superiority of 22G needles in terms of diagnostic yield and safety.[1,19,23] More importantly, our results highlighted that the applicability of 22G needles was not affected by serum IgG4 levels (no significant intergroup differences in puncture effectiveness, P > 0.050). These findings are consistent with those of international studies,[19,23,32] which confirm the superiority of 22G needles in terms of diagnostic yield and safety. This observation fills the gap in the “serum IgG4-stratified puncture strategy” in Chinese clinical practice[27] and complements the diagnostic limitations mentioned by Jaber et al.[25] (lack of high-sensitivity autoantibodies for serum IgG4-N AIP), as domestic studies[28,33] and Western studies[24] have not addressed needle type selection for different serum IgG4 subtypes.

As a key diagnostic criterion for type 1 AIP, the association between histopathology and serum IgG4 typing was a core focus of the present study. The serum IgG4-P group had a significantly higher histopathological confirmation rate (71.19% vs. 36.84% in the serum IgG4-N group, P = 0.015), a higher median number of tissue IgG4-P cells (36 cells/HPF vs. 8 cells/HPF, P = 0.035), a lower proportion of 0 cells/HPF (16.95% vs. 47.37%, P = 0.013), and a lower proportion of suspected type 1 AIP (13.56% vs. 47.37%, P = 0.004). This finding suggested a potential link between serum IgG4 levels and tissue inflammatory infiltration: the serum IgG4-P group had more significant systemic immune activation, leading to more prominent pancreatic pathological features (e.g., dense IgG4-P plasma cell infiltration, storiform fibrosis, and occlusive phlebitis) that make it easier to meet the ICDC pathological confirmation standards (Level 1 or Level 2).[14,15] Consistent with the findings of Katz et al.,[24] the serum IgG4-P patients in our study also showed more extensive organ involvement, which might be associated with greater disease severity. We also observed “serum-tissue IgG4 dissociation:” 42.11% (8/19) of serum IgG4-N patients still had tissue IgG4 >10 cells/HPF, and 38.98% (23/59) of serum IgG4-P patients had tissue IgG4 ≤10 cells/HPF, which is consistent with results from Sano et al.,[14] Paik et al.,[15] and Katz et.al.,[24] but was quantified for the 1st time in Chinese populations. This finding emphasizes that even when serum IgG4 is negative, EUS-FNA/FNB should be performed if type 1 AIP is clinically suspected (e.g., EUS showing diffuse pancreatic enlargement) to avoid missed diagnosis, addressing the unmet diagnostic need for serum IgG4-N AIP mentioned by Jaber et.al.;[25] conversely, patients with elevated serum IgG4 require pathological confirmation to avoid misdiagnosis of other IgG4-RD as pancreatic involvement.[34]

Through Spearman correlation analysis and linear regression, we further confirmed that there was no significant association between serum IgG4 levels and tissue IgG4-P plasma cell counts (rho = 0.113, P = 0.322; regression coefficient = 0.004, P = 0.538), which is supported by studies from different countries and regions.[15,24,28] Unlike international studies that have only qualitatively described this dissociation,[14,15] our study provides quantitative data in Chinese populations, negating the simplified logic of “using serum IgG4 levels to replace histopathological examination.” This finding reinforces that type 1 AIP diagnosis must follow the “multidimensional integration” principle of the ICDC, combining serology, imaging, pathology, and treatment response, which is especially critical for Chinese clinical practice with high misdiagnosis rates[26,27] and addressing the need for comprehensive diagnostic strategies highlighted by Jaber et.al.[25]

This study has three key innovations. First, it is the first systematic analysis of serum IgG4 stratification in Chinese type 1 AIP patients, complementing domestic studies stratified by enlargement type[27] and Western studies focusing on pancreatic damage[24] to form a “subtype + morphology + puncture strategy” triple-layer diagnostic framework. Second, it quantifies the “serum-tissue IgG4 dissociation” phenomenon in Chinese patients, providing more actionable diagnostic guidance than international qualitative descriptions[14,15,24] and addressing the diagnostic challenge of serum IgG4-N AIP mentioned by Jaber et.al.[25] Third, it confirms that 22G needles are the preferred choice for EUS puncture in Chinese patients regardless of serum IgG4 status, adapting international needle selection recommendations[19,32] to the needs of Chinese primary hospitals (prioritizing safety and operability), which is not covered in Western cohort studies.[24]

The strengths of this study are as follows. This study focuses on serum IgG4 stratification in type 1 AIP, a topic with limited data in Chinese populations, and it systematically clarifies differences in clinical, imaging, and pathological features between groups, as well as the lack of correlation between serum IgG4 and tissue IgG4-P cells, providing practical guidance for clinical diagnosis. However, as a single-center retrospective study, this study has several limitations. First, owing to the rarity of type 1 AIP, the small sample size (78 cases) may have prevented some statistical indicators from reaching significance, and single-center data may introduce selection bias; future multicenter prospective studies are needed to verify the results. Second, the lack of long-term follow-up data precluded the ability to evaluate the association between serum IgG4 typing and patient prognosis (e.g., recurrence rate, glucocorticoid dependence rate, incidence of pancreatic insufficiency), which is consistent with the research gap mentioned by Jaber et.al.[25] and Katz et.al.[24] and should be addressed by extending the follow-up duration. Third, we did not include the ICDC diagnostic indicator of glucocorticoid treatment response and did not explore the association between IgA levels and relapse;[25] some patients with suspected AIP may not have received trial glucocorticoid treatment, limiting further diagnosis confirmation and potentially affecting typing accuracy. Fourth, compared with Han et.al.’s[27] analysis of pancreatic enlargement types and Katz et.al.[24] in the assessment of pancreatic exocrine/endocrine function, we did not explore the interaction between serum IgG4 type and enlargement type, nor did we systematically evaluate EPI or diabetes, which could be directions for future research.

This study adds to the available evidence by highlighting that serum IgG4 typing reflects differences in clinical features and diagnosis-treatment needs in patients with type 1 AIP. This finding emphasizes the importance of pathological examination regardless of serum IgG4 levels, helping avoid missed diagnoses or misdiagnoses and reducing unnecessary surgery, which may improve patient care by guiding more targeted diagnostic strategies. Future studies are needed that include expanded sample sizes and multicenter prospective approaches to reduce bias, explore the association between serum IgG4 typing and prognosis, and establish a combined diagnostic model integrating “serum-imaging-pathology-treatment response” to improve the accurate diagnosis and stratified management of type 1 AIP. Given the rising mortality and substantial public health burden of pancreatic cancer in China,[35] the targeted diagnostic insights from this study are particularly meaningful for optimizing the differential diagnosis of pancreatic diseases and reducing overtreatment.

SUMMARY

In summary, this study compared serum IgG4 typing in 78 single-center patients with type 1 AIP, confirming that the serum IgG4-P (serum IgG4 ≥135 mg/dL) group was more likely to have bile duct stricture and diffuse pancreatic enlargement and tended to be diagnosed through EUS puncture, whereas the serum IgG4-N (serum IgG4 <135 mg/ dL) group had higher CA19-9 levels, a higher surgical rate, and a lower histopathological confirmation rate, requiring strengthened clinical preoperative puncture evaluation. This study also revealed no significant correlation between serum IgG4 levels and tissue IgG4-P plasma cell count, with pathological examination remaining among the most important diagnostic criteria. These findings provide practical evidence to optimize the clinical diagnostic pathway of type 1 AIP, supporting differentiated diagnosis and treatment strategies based on serum IgG4 typing to reduce misdiagnosis, missed diagnosis, and overtreatment and improve patient prognosis.
