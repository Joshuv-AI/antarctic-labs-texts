Patient safety as an ethical interface: alignment between staff safety culture, speaking up climate and inpatient perceptions in Romania: a cross-sectional multi-informant survey
Authors: Andrada-Georgiana Nacu, Dan-Alexandru Constantin, Liliana Marcela Rogozea
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient safety emerges where clinical risk governance intersects with ethical duties of non-maleficence, respect for autonomy, and institutional accountability. Safety culture surveys and patient-reported experience measures often evolve on separate analytic tracks, leaving uncertainty about how staff-facing metrics translate into patient-facing ethical practices such as disclosure after harm.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory cross-sectional multi-informant survey in an acute-care hospital in Romania. Healthcare workers (defined as physicians, nurses, hospital auxiliary staff, and other clinical/non-clinical personnel involved in care delivery or immediate supervision; n = 104) completed the AHRQ Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture version 2.0 and the Speaking Up About Patient Safety Questionnaire, capturing safety culture composites, speaking up behaviour, and psychological safety. Inpatients (n = 101) completed a project-specific, not externally validated structured questionnaire covering perceived safety, communication and information, consent and involvement, confidentiality and complaint mechanisms, coordination and organisational reliability, observed safety practices, and experiences of incident communication. We conducted exploratory analyses including composite means, percent positive scores, internal consistency estimates, and examined ward-level associations.ResultsStaff composites peaked for teamwork (mean 3.84/5; 74.3% positive) and communication about error (3.74/5; 70.8% positive), while staffing and work pace (2.74/5; 29.3% positive), response to error (31.7% positive), and hospital management support (34.0% positive) showed marked compression. Patients rated coordination and organisation (mean 3.67/5) and consent and involvement (3.60/5) higher than communication and information (3.42/5). Eleven patients (10.9%) perceived a safety incident; among them, ratings for apology, explanation and follow-up clustered at the lower end of the scale (mean 1.73/5). Ward-level overall safety culture scores showed negligible associations with patient global safety, perceived safety and recommendation. Exploratory ward-group analyses suggested that patient-rated coordination aligned more closely with handoffs and information exchange than with global safety culture measures. Other cross-level associations were inconsistent and were interpreted cautiously because of broad clinical-area aggregation and limited between-group variation. Favourable speaking up climate correlated with overall safety culture (r = 0.484, p < 0.001) and management support (r = 0.547, p < 0.001).ConclusionsIn this setting, patient-perceived safety aligned more closely with interface processes than with global culture scores, and incident communication surfaced as a salient ethical deficit. Strategies integrating psychological safety, management responsiveness, and structured disclosure practices warrant prospective evaluation as potential ways to advance moral accountability and risk governance. Given the cross-sectional, single-site design and modest sample size, these associations should be interpreted as exploratory and non-causal.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-15056-2.

Background

Avoidable harm in hospitals remains a recurring feature of modern healthcare, despite decades of safety science and governance reforms. The ethical weight of preventable injury became globally visible after large-scale estimates of iatrogenic harm, which framed patient safety as a core dimension of clinical quality and public trust [1]. Contemporary policy continues to treat avoidable harm as a systemic, measurable phenomenon, placing organisational learning, transparency and patient engagement within a shared accountability agenda [2].

Safety failures reach beyond clinical outcomes. They disrupt the fiduciary relationship between patients and professionals, destabilise autonomy when information is incomplete or distorted, and generate distributive consequences when harmed patients absorb burdens that were meant to be buffered by institutional safeguards [3]. From a disclosure perspective, error has ethical salience precisely because it creates a claim to truth-telling, explanation and, where warranted, apology. The disclosure literature describes these duties as intertwined with learning: candid communication supports restorative responses for patients and creates conditions for organisational sensemaking rather than defensive concealment [4–6].

Safety science has long treated adverse events as products of interactions between people, technologies and organisational constraints. Reason’s system approach highlights how latent conditions shape the probability that frontline actions culminate in harm [7]. Donabedian’s structure-process-outcome triad offers a complementary frame for empirical work, allowing ethical processes such as informed consent, privacy protection and continuity of information to be analysed as quality mediators, not as decorative principles appended to clinical effectiveness [8].

Within this landscape, safety culture functions as an institutional memory of what becomes speakable, reportable and correctable. A substantial evidence base links stronger safety culture profiles to improved reporting climates and learning practices, even when causal pathways remain complex in observational designs [9, 10]. Yet culture is enacted through micro-interactions: interprofessional coordination, handoffs, and day-to-day communication under time pressure. Patients encounter these interactions directly and translate them into judgements about safety and fairness, which aligns with evidence connecting patient experience to clinical safety and effectiveness [11].

The ethical agency of healthcare workers in safety-critical contexts often crystallises around speaking up. Voice in the face of risk entails epistemic work—identifying hazards, articulating uncertainty, and interrupting routine—and moral work, because escalation may challenge hierarchy and solidarity norms. Qualitative syntheses describe predictable barriers: fear of blame, anticipated futility, and pressure to preserve collegial relationships, all of which channel concerns into silence even when staff recognise potential harm [12]. Survey evidence in psychiatric hospitals similarly indicates that resignation and climate shape safety voice [13]. Speaking up climates are shaped by psychological safety, defined as the felt permission to express doubt and dissent without anticipatory punishment or humiliation [14].

Empirical measurement tools have emerged to operationalise these constructs. The AHRQ Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (version 2.0) captures domains such as teamwork, staffing, organisational learning, management support and handoffs, providing both composite means and percent positive indicators for benchmarking [15]. The Speaking Up About Patient Safety Questionnaire extends this approach by distinguishing perceived concerns, actual voice behaviour, climate features and resignation, thereby positioning speaking up as an ethically relevant practice embedded in organisational conditions rather than as a purely individual trait [16].

Legal and regulatory environments shape how these ethical processes unfold. Romanian legislation recognises patients’ rights to information, consent, privacy and access to complaint mechanisms, establishing an explicit rights-based vocabulary for day-to-day hospital interactions [17]. The broader healthcare reform framework addresses malpractice and professional liability, creating incentives that may influence disclosure practices and institutional responses to incidents [18, 19]. Infection prevention and surveillance norms codify reporting obligations for healthcare-associated infections, illustrating how public health regulation intersects with safety governance at the clinical interface [20]. Comparative analyses of patient complaints in European settings indicate that rights violations frequently concentrate around communication, respect and organisational responsiveness, reinforcing the relevance of patient-reported data for ethical oversight [21].

Against this backdrop, the theoretical motivation for the present study was that patient safety can be understood as an ethical interface: staff safety culture and speaking-up climate represent internal organisational conditions, whereas patients encounter safety through communication, consent, privacy, coordination and disclosure. A specific gap remains because staff-facing safety culture measures and patient-reported safety experiences are frequently studied separately, with limited evidence on whether they align within the same clinical areas in Eastern European settings. The research question was: how do staff safety culture and speaking-up climate relate to inpatient perceptions of ethically salient safety processes in a Romanian acute-care hospital? We therefore assessed (i) healthcare workers’ safety culture and speaking up climate; (ii) patients’ perceptions across domains that map onto autonomy, confidentiality, coordination and disclosure; and (iii) cross-level associations between ward-level staff composites and patient-reported perception domains/items. This design was intended to illuminate where ethical commitments are visible to patients and where they remain confined to staff-facing narratives.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional multi-informant survey was undertaken in a Romanian acute-care hospital, treating patient safety as a relational construct expressed through organisational culture, professional voice and patient-facing ethical processes. Patient data were collected between 25 November 2025 and 20 January 2026, while healthcare worker data were gathered between 6 December 2025 and 3 January 2026.

Participants and recruitment

Healthcare workers were eligible when employed in the institution and involved in clinical care or immediate supervisory roles. In this manuscript, “healthcare workers” refers to the physicians, nurses, hospital auxiliary staff, and other clinical/non-clinical personnel represented in Table 1; these groups differ in authority, responsibility and frequency of patient contact, so aggregation was treated as a pragmatic analytical choice and a methodological limitation. Recruitment combined purposive invitation within clinical areas with snowball dissemination through internal digital channels; participation occurred through an anonymous online form. Patients were approached during hospitalisation and invited to participate when they had been admitted for at least 48 h, were clinically stable, and could complete a Romanian-language questionnaire. Exclusion criteria comprised acute distress, major cognitive impairment, severe psychiatric disturbance preventing participation, or inability to provide informed consent. Eligibility was assessed during recruitment through direct interaction with the patient as part of the informed consent process. Patients were included only if they were able to understand the study information, provide informed consent, and complete the Romanian-language questionnaire independently. No formal cognitive screening instrument (e.g., MMSE or MoCA) was administered.

Patient questionnaires were administered in person by the researcher in a single session of approximately 10–15 min. Healthcare worker questionnaires were self-administered online, with completion time comparable to the patient survey. Neither survey collected direct identifiers; demographic variables were limited to those required for descriptive and adjusted analyses.

Measures

Healthcare worker safety culture was measured with the AHRQ Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture, version 2.0 [15]. The instrument comprises 32 items grouped into ten composites: teamwork; staffing and work pace; organisational learning and continuous improvement; response to error; supervisor/manager/clinical leader support; communication about error; communication openness; reporting patient safety events; hospital management support for patient safety; and handoffs and information exchange. Items use five-point agreement or frequency response scales. Composite scores were calculated as mean item scores after reverse coding of negatively worded items. Percent positive scores were computed according to the user guide, collapsing the top two response categories for positively framed items and the bottom two categories for negatively framed items [15]. Two single items captured global patient safety grade and number of events reported during the preceding 12 months.

Speaking up dynamics were assessed using the Speaking Up About Patient Safety Questionnaire [16]. The questionnaire distinguishes perceived frequency of safety concerns, withholding voice, and speaking up actions (five-point frequency scales), alongside climate dimensions measured on seven-point scales: psychological safety, encouraging environment and resignation. A favourable speaking up climate index was derived by averaging psychological safety and encouraging environment while applying reverse coding to resignation.

Patients completed a project-specific structured questionnaire developed for this study. The instrument was ad hoc and had not undergone external psychometric validation; consequently, the patient measures were interpreted as patient-reported perceptions or experiences rather than objective clinical outcomes. It was grounded in patient rights and disclosure ethics and aligned conceptually with Donabedian’s structure-process-outcome frame [8]. Items were organised into six domains scored on five-point Likert scales: perceived safety; communication and information; consent and involvement; confidentiality and access to complaint mechanisms; coordination and organisational reliability; and observed safety practices. The instrument contained global items assessing overall perception of safety and willingness to recommend the hospital. Respondents were asked whether they believed a safety incident had occurred during their admission; those reporting an incident rated subsequent communication using items reflecting apology, explanation, information about consequences and follow-up. Internal consistency was examined descriptively, but factor analysis, test-retest reliability and external validation were beyond the scope of this single-site exploratory study.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in SPSS (version 29). Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics, item distributions and domain scores. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha at the composite/domain level, recognising known sensitivity of alpha to small numbers of items and heterogeneous constructs in safety climate instruments [22]. No formal a priori sample size or power calculation was performed; the achieved sample was pragmatic, reflecting eligible participants available during the data-collection window. Inferential analyses were therefore treated as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

To explore potential alignment between staff-facing and patient-facing measures, healthcare worker composite scores were averaged within three broad clinical areas (medical, surgical and intensive care). Patient responses were then grouped according to the corresponding clinical area. Because staff-side measures were available only at the level of these three aggregated clinical areas, the resulting cross-level analyses were treated as exploratory signal-detection exercises rather than confirmatory ward-level inference. Correlation and regression coefficients were therefore interpreted descriptively and hypothesis-generating, with particular caution regarding statistical significance estimates.

Multivariable linear regression models estimated associations between selected staff composites and patient-reported domains/items while adjusting for patient age, sex, prior hospitalisation and admission type (emergency vs. scheduled). Two-sided p values below 0.05 are reported for descriptive transparency and interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of the analyses. Because sample sizes within professions and ward groups were limited, and because detailed diagnosis/severity variables were not available, role-stratified staff models and diagnosis/severity-stratified patient models were not estimated; these analyses were hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Exploratory stratified analyses were considered but not performed due to insufficient cell sizes and instability of estimates.

Ethical considerations

Data collection proceeded after institutional authorisation. Participants received written and verbal information describing study objectives, voluntary participation, confidentiality and the absence of any consequences for care or employment. Completion of the questionnaires followed provision of informed consent, and the dataset was stored in de-identified form on password-protected devices.

Results

Participant characteristics are reported in Table 1 (healthcare workers) and Table 2 (patients). Among healthcare workers (n = 104), nurses represented 42.3% and physicians 19.2%; 70.2% reported daily patient contact. Clinical areas included medical (44.2%), surgical (25.0%), intensive care (11.5%) and outpatient services (12.5%). Patients (n = 101) had a mean age of 39.9 years (SD 13.8) and were predominantly female (79.2%). Emergency admissions accounted for 65.3%, and 89.1% reported previous hospitalisation. Length of stay was right-skewed (median 5 days; range 1-150). The patient dataset captured admission type, previous hospitalisation, length of stay and ward type, but did not include diagnostic categories or validated severity-of-illness scores. Cognitive impairment was an exclusion criterion rather than a measured covariate, so cognitive status could not be examined analytically.

Healthcare worker safety culture composites ranged from 2.74 to 3.84 on the five-point scale (Table 3). Teamwork showed the highest mean (3.84, SD 0.58) with 74.3% positive responses, followed by communication about error (3.74, SD 0.65; 70.8% positive) and organisational learning and continuous improvement (3.70, SD 0.69; 68.3% positive). Staffing and work pace had the lowest mean (2.74, SD 0.84) and the lowest percent positive score (29.3%). Response to error (31.7% positive) and hospital management support for patient safety (34.0% positive) displayed similar constraints, indicating limited confidence that errors are met with constructive, non-punitive responses. In the preceding 12 months, 45.2% of respondents reported no events, while 8.7% reported more than five events.

Global patient safety grade ratings clustered around the mid-range: 31.7% rated safety as acceptable, 24.0% as good, and 14.4% as very good, whereas 29.8% selected poor or very poor.

Speaking up measures suggested moderate visibility of concerns and moderate enactment of voice (Table 4). The mean score for perceived concerns was 3.05 (SD 0.79) and the mean score for speaking up actions was 3.00 (SD 0.74), while withholding voice averaged 2.42 (SD 0.68). On the seven-point climate scales, psychological safety averaged 4.65 (SD 1.22) and encouraging environment 4.41 (SD 1.31); resignation averaged 2.87 (SD 1.17). The composite favourable speaking up climate index was 4.41 (SD 0.65). A vignette concerning hand hygiene yielded a mean likelihood of speaking up of 3.31 (SD 1.37).

Favourable speaking up climate correlated with the overall safety culture index (r = 0.484, p < 0.001) and with hospital management support for patient safety (r = 0.547, p < 0.001). Speaking up actions correlated strongly with communication openness (r = 0.628, p < 0.001), consistent with the conceptual coupling between voice enactment and communicative norms.

Patient domain scores (Table 5) were situated in the mid-range of the five-point scale. Coordination and organisational reliability had the highest mean (3.67, SD 1.21), followed by consent and involvement (3.60, SD 1.22). Communication and information had the lowest domain mean (3.42, SD 1.20). Item-level patterns indicated lower ratings for responsiveness to call signals (mean 2.83, SD 1.72) and for explanations of treatment-associated risks (mean 2.83, SD 1.55). Global ratings showed a mean overall safety score of 3.42 (SD 1.57) and a mean willingness-to-recommend score of 3.07 (SD 1.59).

Eleven patients (10.9%) believed that a safety incident occurred during their admission. Among these respondents, ratings of incident communication and follow-up were low, with the open disclosure composite averaging 1.73 (SD 1.19). Apology, disclosure of the cause, and information about consequences all clustered at the lower end of the scale, suggesting limited realisation of candour-oriented responses in the subset of admissions perceived as involving harm.

In ward-level analyses (see Table 6), overall safety culture scores showed negligible associations with patient global safety, perceived safety and willingness to recommend the hospital (all |r|≤0.031; p ≥ 0.758). Multivariable models adjusting for patient characteristics confirmed the absence of an independent association between ward-level overall safety culture and patient global safety (standardised β=-0.012, p = 0.906). Emergency admission was independently associated with lower global safety ratings (β=-0.287, p = 0.004).

Exploratory cross-level comparisons suggested that higher handoff and information-exchange scores tended to align with higher patient-rated coordination and organisational reliability, whereas teamwork scores showed an inverse pattern. Given that staff measures were aggregated across only three broad clinical areas, these coefficients should be interpreted as descriptive signals rather than evidence of statistically robust ward-level relationships.

In adjusted regression models, handoffs and information exchange were associated with higher patient coordination scores (β = 0.231, p = 0.026), while teamwork was associated with lower scores (β=-0.211, p = 0.036). Communication openness did not show an independent association with patient communication and information ratings after adjustment (β=-0.005, p = 0.962), and emergency admission remained associated with lower communication ratings (β=-0.337, p = 0.001). Given the modest samples, broad ward aggregation and low between-ward variance, these coefficients were interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

By integrating staff safety culture, speaking up climate and patient perceptions, this study treated patient safety as an ethical interface rather than as a purely technical outcome. Three exploratory patterns emerged that warrant cautious interpretation. Global safety culture scores demonstrated minimal translation into patient-perceived safety and recommendation. Ward-level handoffs and information exchange showed the clearest positive relationship with patient-perceived coordination, while teamwork displayed an inverse relationship. In the subset of admissions perceived as involving a safety incident, patient ratings of apology, explanation and follow-up were markedly low, indicating a disclosure gap with direct ethical implications. All three patterns are interpreted as correlational, hypothesis-generating observations rather than causal relationships.

The weak association between staff-facing overall culture scores and patient-reported perception domains/items echoes a recurrent challenge in safety measurement: global culture composites capture internal norms and learning dispositions, whereas patients infer safety through concrete experiences of communication, responsiveness and continuity. The disjunction may reflect different epistemic positions. Healthcare workers observe near misses, latent hazards and resource constraints; patients encounter the interpretive surface of the system, where tone, transparency and coordination act as proxies for competence and care. Systematic evidence links patient experience to safety and effectiveness, yet the relationship is mediated by relational mechanisms rather than by organisational narratives captured through staff surveys [11]. Conceptual accounts have framed patients as vigilant partners in safety whose observations can complement clinician-centred reporting [23].

The signal emerging from handoffs and information exchange supports a process-oriented interpretation consistent with Donabedian’s model: coordination failures become legible to patients because they manifest as repeated questioning, contradictory instructions, delays or missed care. Multicentre patient surveys describe communication and staffing failures as prominent categories of patient-reported safety concerns [24]. The present findings align with that literature, suggesting that improvements in transfer communication and information continuity may be more visible to patients than abstract shifts in safety culture.

The inverse relationship between teamwork scores and patient-rated coordination merits careful interpretation. Measurement non-equivalence across professional groups and limited between-ward variance can generate counterintuitive coefficients, particularly when composite reliability is modest. Internal consistency in safety culture instruments can be uneven across domains and translations, partly because composites combine heterogeneous behavioural expectations and draw on few items [22]. Because the ward-level analysis compared a small number of broad clinical areas and the teamwork composite displayed restricted between-ward variance, the inverse coefficient may reflect ecological aggregation, residual confounding or statistical instability rather than a substantive negative effect of teamwork. In addition, strong within-team cohesion may coexist with weak boundary spanning across professions or units; patients are sensitive to boundary failures, even when intrateam relationships appear functional to staff. This finding should therefore be treated as a signal for process mapping and qualitative follow-up, not as evidence that teamwork reduces patient-rated coordination.

Staffing and work pace emerged as the weakest safety culture domain, accompanied by low confidence in constructive responses to error and limited perceived management support. From an ethical lens, chronic understaffing represents more than an operational inconvenience: it redistributes risk from institutions to patients and constrains professionals’ capacity to deliver care aligned with beneficence and respect for persons. The culture profile suggests that frontline staff may anticipate blame or futility when errors occur, conditions known to suppress reporting and learning [9, 10]. Such climates can produce moral distress, especially when clinicians perceive foreseeable harm but lack institutional resources or protection to enact safer workflows.

Speaking up findings provide an additional layer. The moderate favourable climate score and its correlation with management support indicate that leadership behaviour shapes the moral space within which staff decide whether to interrupt unsafe trajectories. Qualitative syntheses of safety voice describe how hierarchy, relationship concerns and expected inaction influence the calculus of speaking up [12]. Surveys in psychiatric hospital settings similarly show that interpersonal and organisational climates shape safety voice, with resignation reflecting learned expectations of futility [13]. The strong correlation between speaking up actions and communication openness in the current dataset reinforces the view that voice depends on interactional norms, not on individual assertiveness alone [16].

Patient domain scores pointed to relative strengths in consent and involvement and weaker performance in communication and information. The item-level pattern—particularly low ratings for risk explanations—has direct implications for autonomy. Consent processes in acute care frequently oscillate between formal documentation and meaningful deliberation; patients assess whether information is tailored, comprehensible and candid about uncertainty. When risk communication is shallow, autonomy becomes procedural rather than substantive, and the ethical content of consent is weakened even when signatures are obtained.

Patients rated confidentiality and access to complaint mechanisms in the mid-range. Rights-based frameworks treat privacy protection and access to redress as constitutive of respectful care, shaping trust and willingness to disclose sensitive information. Analyses of patient complaints in European contexts show recurrent emphasis on respect, communication and organisational responsiveness, signalling that rights violations are experienced as relational harms with safety implications [21].

The open disclosure signal is ethically striking. Disclosure scholarship has framed apology, explanation and future prevention commitments as central components of candour, with benefits for patient trust and organisational learning [4–6]. The low disclosure scores among patients who perceived incidents suggest that disclosure practices may be constrained by fear of liability, fragmented responsibility, or the absence of organisational routines that support clinicians during difficult conversations. In legal environments where malpractice frameworks loom large, defensive communication can become a predictable response. Romanian law articulates patient rights to information, consent and dignity [17], while malpractice regulation defines liability and redress mechanisms [18, 19]. These frameworks can incentivise silence when institutions lack structured disclosure processes that decouple transparency from personal blame.

Infection surveillance norms exemplify how regulation can embed safety practices within routine work by specifying reporting and control procedures [20]. Comparable institutionalisation for disclosure remains uneven across jurisdictions, and Romania lacks a long-established duty-of-candour regime. In such settings, disclosure tends to rely on professional ethics and local policy rather than on explicit statutory articulation, leaving variability across wards and clinicians.

Practically, these findings support a dual agenda: strengthening the ethical texture of patient-facing processes and consolidating staff-facing psychological safety. Interventions that standardise handoffs, improve information continuity and reduce delays warrant prospective evaluation because they are plausibly connected to patient-reported coordination. In parallel, leadership walkrounds, feedback loops following incident reports, and training in disclosure conversations can widen the space for voice and candour. Patient surveys that capture disclosure and consent processes can complement staff safety culture diagnostics by identifying where ethical commitments fail to become visible at the bedside.

Several limitations temper inference. First, the study was conducted in a single hospital with non-probability recruitment and a modest sample (104 healthcare workers and 101 patients), limiting generalisability and statistical power. No formal a priori power calculation was performed; weak or inconsistent associations may therefore reflect insufficient power as much as substantive absence of relationship. The ward-level analyses relied on aggregation across three broad clinical areas, which reduces granularity, produces low between-ward variance and may obscure within-ward differences. This level of aggregation may also introduce ecological bias, whereby associations observed at the group level do not reflect individual-level relationships. In addition, the cross-informant analyses relied on aggregation of staff measures into only three broad clinical areas. This substantially reduced between-group variation and limits the validity of conventional significance testing. As a result, the reported cross-level associations should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of stable ward-level effects.

Second, the healthcare worker sample combined physicians, nurses, hospital auxiliary staff and other clinical/non-clinical personnel. Although this reflected the multi-professional nature of safety culture, aggregating roles may mask clinically meaningful differences in responsibility, authority and patient contact. Role-stratified analysis was not undertaken because cell sizes were too small.

Third, the patient questionnaire was developed for this project and has not been externally validated. Cronbach’s alpha provided only a limited internal-consistency estimate; no factor analysis, test-retest assessment or external reliability validation was performed. The patient measures should therefore be interpreted as patient-reported perceptions or experiences, not objective clinical outcomes. Future studies should validate the questionnaire in larger samples, multiple hospitals and distinct patient groups before using it for benchmarking or causal inference.

Fourth, selection bias and social desirability bias may have affected both surveys. Staff participation through voluntary online recruitment may have attracted individuals with stronger safety attitudes, while in-person patient recruitment excluded acutely distressed or cognitively impaired patients and may have favoured more stable or communicative inpatients. Common method bias may also affect patient-reported domains, as multiple constructs were measured within the same instrument, potentially inflating observed associations. Detailed diagnosis categories, severity of illness and cognitive status were not recorded as analytical covariates; emergency admission and length of stay were only crude proxies. These unmeasured factors may confound patient perceptions, particularly communication and coordination ratings.

Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal claims and remains vulnerable to residual confounding and same-source bias within patient-reported domains. Patient incident reporting is perception-based and may under-detect clinical adverse events; nevertheless, patient-reported safety concerns have demonstrated capacity to surface communication and coordination failures that remain invisible to formal reporting systems [24].

Despite these constraints, the study offers a multi-perspective view of patient safety in which ethical obligations—information, consent, privacy and disclosure—are treated as measurable processes rather than aspirational statements. The results invite safety governance that attends to both organisational learning climates and the relational signals through which patients infer safety. These findings should be interpreted within an exploratory framework and require replication in larger, multi-centre studies using validated patient-reported measures.

Conclusions

In this multi-informant survey, staff safety culture and speaking up climate revealed strengths in teamwork and learning alongside substantial weaknesses in staffing, management support and non-punitive response. Patient perceptions clustered around mid-scale ratings, with communication and information as the most fragile domain and open disclosure as the clearest ethical gap among admissions perceived as involving harm. Global culture scores showed little correspondence with patient-reported perceptions, whereas handoffs and information exchange were associated with patient-perceived coordination. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory, non-causal associations from a single Romanian hospital. Safety strategies that integrate disclosure routines, leadership support for voice and robust transfer communication warrant prospective evaluation to determine whether they strengthen ethical accountability and patient trust.
