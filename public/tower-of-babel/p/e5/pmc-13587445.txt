Deep learning analysis of ECGs detects Cardiovascular–Kidney–Metabolic syndrome burden in people with diabetes: a report from the Silesia Diabetes-Heart Project
Authors: Oliwia Janota-Sosińska, Qinkai Yu, Krzysztof Irlik, Hanna Kwiendacz, Aleksandra Włosowicz-Momot, Patrycja Pabis, Wiktoria Wójcik, Anna Olejarz, Julia Piaśnik, Uazman Alam, Yalin Zheng, Janusz Gumprecht, Gregory Y. H. Lip, Katarzyna Nabrdalik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome refers to the co-occurrence of obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease. However, it is underdiagnosed due to silent clinical nature of the early stages of its components and subsequent siloed medical care. Electrocardiography (ECG) is an inexpensive and widely available diagnostic tool but its utility in automated detection of CKM syndrome has not been previously explored.ObjectiveTo develop and evaluate deep learning models for predicting CKM syndrome using scanned limb and augmented limb leads ECGs images in people with diabetes.MethodsClinical data of adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes enrolled in the prospective Silesia Diabetes-Heart Project were analyzed. CKM syndrome was defined by the presence of either CKD [estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and/or urine albumin to creatinine ratio (UACR) ≥ 30 mg/g) or established CVD. High-resolution scanned ECG tracings were processed into lead-specific inputs to train ResNet-50-based convolutional neural network (CNN) models, including single- and dual-channel variants. Class imbalance was addressed using resampling strategies, and model performance was evaluated using standard classification metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), with confidence intervals estimated by bootstrap resampling.ResultsAmong 2779 participants, 492 (17.7%) met criteria for CKM syndrome. The best-performing individual model was a dual-channel ResNet-50 with soft voting ensemble, achieving an AUROC of 0.8199 (95% CI 0.7549–0.8795), F1-score of 0.7213 (95% CI 0.6404–0.7957), accuracy of 0.7385, and balanced precision and recall. Ensemble models consistently outperformed individual architectures, particularly in handling class imbalance and improving generalization.ConclusionDeep learning applied to scanned ECG image data predicts CKM syndrome in individuals with diabetes with reasonable accuracy. This approach holds promise as a low-cost, scalable risk stratification tool and which could augment clinical decision-making in settings particularly with limited access to advanced diagnostics.Trial registration The study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05626413).Graphical abstract

Introduction

The cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic (CKM) syndrome is characterized by interrelated and mutually reinforcing metabolic, kidney, and cardiovascular abnormalities, arising from the complex interactions of obesity, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease (CKD), and encompassing the full spectrum of cardiovascular risk factors and cardiovascular disease (CVD), including heart failure (HF), atrial fibrillation (AF), coronary artery disease (CAD), stroke, and peripheral artery disease (PAD). The syndrome is classified into stages from 0 to 4 reflecting its progressive nature with no risk factors on stage 0 where primordial prevention is possible. At stage 4 where clinical CVD is present in people with metabolic risk factors or CKD integrated multidisciplinary care should be driven [1].

As a syndrome, CKM reflects a shared underlying pathophysiology rather than a single formal diagnosis and includes both individuals at increased risk for CVD and those with established CVD [1]. People living with diabetes are disproportionately affected by these overlapping conditions, which often coexist and amplify each other’s progression, resulting in significantly elevated risks of hospitalization, organ failure, and premature death [2]. Moreover, the asymptomatic early stages of CKM-related conditions, combined with fragmented and siloed medical care, hinder comprehensive diagnosis and optimal patient management. Consequently, CKM syndrome remains underdiagnosed, highlighting early detection—particularly at initial stages—as a key priority in preventive cardiometabolic care.

Existing approaches to CKM risk stratification often rely on laboratory and imaging-based assessments including kidney function tests, albuminuria measurements, echocardiography, and vascular imaging which is often costly and unavailable in resource-limited settings [1]. In contrast, the 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is a widely used, non-invasive, and low-cost tool that is routinely performed in people with diabetes, particularly during hospital admission. Although traditionally used for diagnosis of cardiac ischaemia and arrythmias, emerging evidence suggests that ECGs may contain electrophysiological signatures associated with a range of systemic diseases, including CKD and cardiometabolic dysfunction [3–5].

In recent years, deep learning, a subfield of artificial intelligence (AI) that uses multi-layered neural networks to learn hierarchical representations from raw data has demonstrated good-to-exceptional performance in medical image analysis, including ECG interpretation [6]. Unlike traditional machine learning approaches that require handcrafted feature extraction, deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), are capable of automatically identifying complex, non-linear patterns in data, even when these patterns are not obvious to human experts [7]. These methods have demonstrated the ability to predict various cardiovascular and renal conditions from ECG, including left ventricular dysfunction, AF, and decreased glomerular filtration rate [3, 6, 8]. However, most existing models rely on digital ECG waveform data, which may not be accessible in all clinical settings, especially where ECGs are still recorded and stored on paper.

In this study, we aimed to explore whether deep learning models can identify individuals with CKM syndrome directly from scanned limb and augmented limb leads ECG images, without access to raw signal data. Using a large cohort of hospitalized adults with diabetes from the Silesia Diabetes–Heart Project, we developed and evaluated deep CNNs trained to distinguish individuals with and without CKM-related comorbidities. Our approach leverages the ubiquity of ECG in routine care and the potential of deep learning to extract subtle risk patterns from analogue image formats. We hypothesized that this method could serve as a scalable, low-cost screening tool for CKM syndrome and support earlier risk detection in a broad range of healthcare environments.

Methods

Study design and setting

The Silesia Diabetes-Heart Project is a prospective, single-centre, observational study of people with diabetes hospitalized in the Department of Internal Diseases, Diabetology and Nephrology in Zabrze, Poland. The registry includes adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes who were hospitalized there between January 2015 and March 2023. Consecutive eligible individuals were enrolled. This analysis is limited to data from the baseline hospitalisation, including ECG, clinical, and laboratory parameters collected during the inpatient stay. The study received approval from the institutional ethics committee, complies with Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki, and is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05626413). Patients’ written informed consent was waived by the Ethical Committee of Medical University of Silesia (exception no. PCN/0022/KB/126/20) since only anonymized patients’ hospital data was analyzed.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility for this cross-sectional study required a diagnosis of diabetes type 1 or 2 along with a resting 12-lead ECG and measurements of serum creatinine and a spot urine sample, all obtained at hospital admission. Participants were excluded in cases of missing data for these key variables, as well as in the presence of diabetes types other than type 1 or type 2, in-hospital death during the index admission, or terminal-stage malignancy. Clinical and biochemical data were abstracted from electronic medical records, based on tests performed during the index hospital admission and documented medical history.

Definition of cardiovascular–kidney–metabolic syndrome

In this study, CKM syndrome was defined when a participant with diabetes exhibited either CKD or established CVD that was in stage 2 of CKM syndrome at least [9]. Chronic kidney disease was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) of at least 30 mg/g, with abnormalities confirmed on at least two separate measurements at least three months apart, fulfilling the KDIGO chronicity criterion [10]. The Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula for eGFR calculation was used based on the serum creatinine concentrations and demographic data. The UACR was determined based on the urine albumin and urine creatinine concentrations assessed in the morning urine spots and the mean value was calculated. CVD was deemed present if the medical record documented CAD, HF, AF, prior stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA), PAD, or carotid artery stenosis. Individuals who met CKM diagnostic criteria presented the CKM-positive (CKM+) group. Participants who had neither kidney involvement nor CVD were classified as CKM-negative (CKM−). No statistical imputation was performed because the predictive model relies exclusively on image‑based features extracted from the ECG, and additional covariates were used only for descriptive purposes.

ECG acquisition

Resting ECGs were obtained following a minimum of five minutes of supine rest, using standard equipment available in the hospital ward at the time of admission. Acquisition followed routine hospital protocols. All recordings were made at 50 mm/s and 10 mm/mV and capture a standard 10‑second rhythm strip. Tracings were visually inspected, and only those with major artefacts interfering with interpretation were excluded. ECGs were printed on thermal paper and scanned at high resolution (600 dpi or higher) using uniform scanning procedures. Personal identifiers, including patient and technician data, were removed prior to analysis to ensure anonymization. The ECG machines used did not produce automated interpretations or interval annotations on the tracing. Demographic data were printed on a separate attached sheet, not on the tracing pages used as model input. The visible content of model input images was therefore limited to the waveform grid, lead labels, voltage scale, paper speed, and date–time stamp. For subsequent analysis each scan was separated into distinct anatomically coherent lead groups referred to as standard limb leads (I, II, III), augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF), and precordial leads (V1–V6).

Image preprocessing

Each participant had 2–4 scanned ECG images derived from routine paper printouts. Images were grouped by lead content:a images contained signals from leads I, II, and III.b images contained signals from leads aVR, aVL, and aVF.c and d images included signals from the precordial leads V1–V6.

In our dataset, the limb leads images a and b had a consistent and reproducible layout across patients. In contrast, the precordial leads were stored in more than one printing format, with some ECGs presenting all six precordial leads on a single page and others splitting them across two pages. This resulted in differences in page structure, lead positioning, and occasional missing precordial pages. To maintain a standardized input representation and limit heterogeneity related to format variation and missingness, model development was restricted to the limb leads pages a and b.

Each person was labelled with a binary outcome indicating the presence (1) or absence (0) of CKM comorbidities. Preprocessing was performed to exclude persons with missing data or poor image quality and only the ones with complete data were included for the subsequent analysis. Also, different sampling strategies were applied to tackle the class imbalance problems.

Resampling strategy

Two resampling approaches were applied to mitigate the effects of class imbalance problems: (i) Downsampling (baseline): The majority class was randomly downsampled to match the number of minority class. (ii) Upsampling: The minority class (label 1) was augmented to match the number of the majority class. For the upsampling strategy, minority-class images in the training set were augmented using color jittering (factor = 0.3) and random rotations within ± 15° to reduce overfitting and improve robustness during model training. All resampling and augmentation procedures were performed only within the training data and were not applied to held-out test images.

Model architecture

We employed the ResNet-50 architecture [11] pre-trained on ImageNet [12] as the backbone network for ECG-based CKM comorbidity prediction. Three model variants were developed:Single-channel ResNet-50: This model uses a single ResNet-50 backbone for both ‘a’ and ‘b’ images, followed by a classification head consisting of a fully connected layer (2048 × 512), ReLU activation, a dropout layer with a dropout rate of 0.3, and a final fully connected layer (512 × 2) for the binary classification.Dual-channel ResNet-50 (late fusion): This architecture consists of two independent ResNet-50 backbones processing a and b ECG images separately. The feature outputs from both branches are concatenated before being passed through a fully connected layer (4096 × 512), ReLU activation, dropout (rate = 0.3), and a final output layer (512 × 2).Dual-channel ResNet-50 with shared weights: To reduce number of model parameters and encourage joint representation learning, we also implemented a dual-channel late fusion model where the two branches shared weights. The a and b images were input into the same ResNet-50 backbone independently, and their features were concatenated and processed through the same classification head as in the standard late fusion model.

Ensemble strategy

To improve the prediction accuracy, robustness and stability, we further implemented two ensemble strategies using predictions from multiple base models: hard voting and soft voting. Hard voting ensemble: Final prediction was determined by majority voting from several trained models. If at least two out of three models predicted “CKM present,” the final label was assigned as positive. Soft voting ensemble: Probabilistic outputs (softmax scores) from the base models were averaged, and the final label was assigned based on the maximum of the averaged probabilities. This approach allowed better incorporation of prediction confidence.

The ensembles combined multiple trained instances of the single-channel and dual-channel models, both with and without resampling. Performance was evaluated using the same testing set, enabling direct comparison with individual models.

Implementation details

All the ECG images were resized to 768 × 768 pixels before input to the models. We used the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1e−5. Each model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 16. Experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel i9-14900 K CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU. The training process employed five-fold cross-validation. No randomness was introduced during testing. We fixed the random seed at 42 to ensure the reproducibility of the results.

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity and secondary analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness and clinical interpretability of the ECG-deep learning (ECG-DL) model. First, to examine whether the model performance was influenced by age- or sex-related ECG differences rather than CKM-related features, we performed a matched sensitivity analysis. Each CKM+ participant was matched to one CKM− participant of the same sex and similar age, allowing a maximum age difference of 5 years. Second, because the held-out test set had a higher CKM+ proportion than the source cohort, we performed a resampling analysis to reflect the original CKM prevalence of 17.7%. CKM− participants in the held-out test set were retained, and CKM+ participants were randomly sampled to restore the source-cohort prevalence. This process was repeated 1000 times. Model discrimination, PPV, NPV, and calibration were then assessed. Third, we assessed model performance across clinically relevant subgroups, including sex/gender, diabetes type, age group, and CKD/CVD status. We also performed exploratory analyses for related clinical endpoints to better understand what disease components contributed to the model signal. These endpoints included CKD alone, CVD alone, CKD without known CVD, CVD without CKD, and combined CKD/CVD.

Performance evaluation

To evaluate the performance of the artificial intelligence (AI) models in predicting CKM syndrome from ECG images, a comprehensive set of classification metrics was used. A confusion matrix was generated to compare the model-predicted labels against the ground truth labels for each test sample. Based on this, a classification report was produced, including the commonly used evaluation metrics: accuracy, precision, recall (also known as sensitivity), F1-score and Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC). To assess the reliability of these performance metrics, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using 1000 bootstrap resamples for precision, recall, F1-score and AUC.

Compared with CKM− individuals, those CKM+ were older (p < 0.001), more often female (p = 0.013), heavier with higher BMI (p < 0.001) and had longer-standing diabetes (p < 0.001). CKM was associated with a substantially reduced renal function (eGFR, p ≤ 0.001). CVD was highly prevalent in the CKM+ group, consistent with our definition of CKM syndrome. Medication patterns reflected this CV risk: CKM+ individuals were more likely to receive statins, β-blockers and calcium-channel blockers, but less likely to be treated with metformin or an SGLT2 inhibitor (all p ≤ 0.001).

For model development, the analytical cohort comprised 2287 CKM− and 492 CKM+ participants. The dataset was first divided into a training/development set and a fixed held-out test set. After paired-image availability checks, 762 records were included in the training/development set and 195 records were retained as the held-out test set for final evaluation. The held-out test set included 88 CKM+ and 107 CKM− participants. Class imbalance was then addressed within the training/development set using two strategies. For the downsampling strategy, CKM− participants were randomly selected to match the number of CKM+ participants, resulting in a balanced training/development dataset. For the upsampling strategy, CKM+ images in the training/development set were augmented using color jittering and random rotation to reduce overfitting and improve robustness during model training. This yielded 4360 training/development image samples. The held-out test set remained unchanged and contained only original, non-augmented ECG images.

Classification performance

To enhance robustness, two ensemble strategies were tested. The hard voting ensemble yielded an accuracy of 0.71, precision of 0.64 (95% CI 0.55–0.73) recall of 0.80 (95% CI 0.71–0.88), F1-score of 0.71 (95% CI 0.64–0.78), and AUROC 0.80 (95% CI 0.74–0.86). The soft voting ensemble further improved performance, achieving the best accuracy of 0.74, precision of 0.69 (95% CI 0.60–0.79), recall of 0.75 (95% CI 0.66–0.84), F1-score of 0.72 (95% CI 0.64–0.80), and the highest AUROC among all models: 0.82 (95% CI 0.75–0.88). Overall, ensemble strategies, particularly soft voting demonstrated superior and more stable performance in identifying CKM syndrome from ECG images, suggesting their potential utility in clinical decision support.

Sensitivity and secondary analyses

The age- and sex-matched sensitivity analysis included 86 participants, with 43 CKM+ and 43 CKM− participants. After matching, age was well balanced between groups, with mean age 71.4 versus 70.9 years, median age 72 versus 72 years, and a standardized mean difference of 0.051; sex distribution was identical between groups. In this matched subset, the soft-voting ensemble achieved an AUROC of 0.790 (95% CI, 0.689–0.885), with sensitivity of 0.767, specificity of 0.674, and accuracy of 0.721.

In the natural-prevalence resampling analysis, the soft-voting ensemble achieved a mean AUROC of 0.820 (2.5th–97.5th percentile, 0.743–0.888) and a mean AUPRC of 0.559 across 1000 iterations approximating the source-cohort CKM prevalence of 17.7%. The mean PPV was 0.373 and the mean NPV was 0.932, with mean sensitivity and specificity of 0.752 and 0.729, respectively. Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated deep learning models based on ResNet-50 architectures which predicted the presence of CKM syndrome from scanned ECG images in individuals with diabetes. Our findings demonstrate that CNNs can extract clinically meaningful information from ECG image data. These results suggest that ECGs contain electrophysiological patterns associated with CKM syndrome and can be effectively analyzed using AI techniques. Although traditional clinical variables such as age, sex, BMI, diabetes duration, and diabetes type were not included as direct model inputs, ECG morphology may still encode physiological variation associated with these factors, particularly age- and sex-related cardiac electrical differences.

To further assess potential confounding, age- and sex-matched sensitivity analyses indicated that model performance was not driven solely by demographic imbalance. Subgroup analyses across sex/gender, diabetes type, age categories, and CKD/CVD status further supported the internal robustness of the model; however, residual confounding from measured or unmeasured clinical variables cannot be fully excluded, and subgroup-level results should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in strata with a limited number of CKM+ cases.

The ECG deep learning model was explicitly trained to detect a composite CKM-related phenotype defined by the presence of chronic kidney disease and/or established cardiovascular disease, rather than to distinguish mutually exclusive renal-only or cardiovascular-only states. Accordingly, while the model demonstrated good discrimination for CKD alone and CVD alone, its strongest performance was observed for the combined CKD/CVD endpoint, which reflects the intended training target. In contrast, isolated CKD without CVD and isolated CVD without CKD represent narrower phenotypes that were not the primary learning objective, and the lower AUROC values in these subgroups likely reflect endpoint mismatch together with reduced statistical power due to smaller case numbers rather than a true lack of signal detection.

People with CKM syndrome (CKM+) differed clinically from those without CKM (CKM−). The CKM+ group demonstrated less favorable cardiometabolic profile with higher body mass index, longer diabetes duration and higher blood pressure. As expected, based on the case definition, kidney function was worse in the group with CKM syndrome. Notably, lower LDL cholesterol and HbA1c in CKM+ participants may reflect differences in background preventive therapy and the influence of advanced comorbidity (including CKD) on these biomarkers, rather than a more favorable metabolic profile. Overall, these data highlight a CKM+ population characterized by advanced age, greater cardiometabolic burden, extensive micro- and macrovascular complications, and complex pharmacotherapy, reflecting the compounded risk associated with the syndrome.

In our cohort, more than 17% of people with diabetes met the diagnostic criteria for CKM syndrome with at least stage 2, indicating that a clinically meaningful proportion of the study group experience the combined burden of metabolic, kidney, and CV dysfunction. Similar data was present in people from Europe based on the only published large analysis of data from UK Biobank which indicated that 1% of participants had stage 3, and 13% stage 4 of CKM syndrome [13]. These corresponds also to the data from the United States where stage 3 of CKM syndrome was present in 4.7% and stage 4 in 8.1% of participants [14]. Slightly different numbers were observed in Asian population where prevalence of CKM syndrome in stage 3 was 7.3% people in stage 4 in 2.8% participants, which may be related to the heterogeneity of the CKM syndrome itself as well as studied population and need further investigation [15].

The present findings are consistent with prior work demonstrating the predictive potential of deep learning applied to ECGs for a range of CV and renal conditions [16]. Recent methodological advances in this field have also been summarized by Ding et al. [17], who highlighted key challenges in inter-patient variability, model generalization, and clinical translation of deep learning-based ECG interpretation, further supporting the relevance of robust validation strategies in our study design. In a recently published study, a random forest model of ECG diagnosed angiography-proven coronary occlusion in the setting of heart attack, which was markedly superior over physician diagnosis [18]. Prior studies have applied deep learning architectures to ECG interpretation [8, 19]. For example, Raghunath et al. developed a deep neural network capable of predicting new-onset AF from a sinus rhythm ECG, underscoring the capacity of deep learning to detect subtle electrical signatures preceding overt disease [20]. More recently, Holmstrom et al. [3] and Kwon et al. [6] extended these approaches to renal outcomes, demonstrating that ECG-based deep learning models can identify individuals with reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m2) or CKD.

Compared with these studies, our model achieved similar discriminative performance despite using scanned paper ECG images rather than digital waveform data. This distinction is relevant in settings where ECGs are archived as scanned paper tracings rather than using its digital form. The ability to extract predictive signals from image-based tracings therefore represents a step toward scalable and equitable deployment of AI-enabled screening tools. Furthermore, by targeting CKM syndrome as an integrated construct, our model simultaneously encompasses CV and renal risk, whereas previous ECG-based studies typically focused on single-organ outcomes such as HF, AF, or CKD alone [3, 6, 20, 21]. This multidimensional approach aligns with the emerging view of CKM syndrome as a systemic disorder characterized by intertwined cardiac, renal, and metabolic dysfunction [1, 9].

Among the tested architectures, ensemble models, particularly the soft-voting dual-channel ResNet-50 provided the most robust and balanced performance, mitigating class imbalance and improving generalization. This is consistent with recent literature showing that ensemble strategies can improve model calibration and reproducibility in heterogeneous clinical datasets [22]. The moderately good yet clinically meaningful AUROC suggests that ECG-based image analysis could serve as a feasible pre-screening tool to flag high-risk individuals for further laboratory or imaging assessment, especially where eGFR or albuminuria testing is not routinely available.

Physiologically, the ECG changes captured by deep learning may reflect a combination of structural, electrophysiological, and metabolic alterations common to CKM syndrome, including myocardial remodeling, autonomic dysfunction, electrolyte imbalance, and uremic toxicity, all of which may subtly influence cardiac conduction patterns [23, 24]. These signals are often imperceptible to human interpretation but can be detected by convolutional neural networks trained on large-scale datasets.

The application of deep learning to ECG image data offers several potential advantages. First, ECGs are universally available, inexpensive, and repeatable, making them ideal for large-scale risk stratification. Second, scanning and digitizing existing paper ECG archives could enable retrospective CKM risk profiling in millions of individuals without additional data collection. Third, AI-enhanced ECG interpretation could complement current CKM screening algorithms by integrating cardiovascular and renal risk within a single model, thereby facilitating holistic management of people with diabetes.

Nevertheless, integration into clinical workflows requires caution. Model interpretability remains a challenge, and visualization techniques should be used to identify the ECG regions contributing most strongly to model predictions [25]. Although representative Grad-CAM heatmaps suggested partial overlap with clinically relevant ECG waveform regions, these visualizations should be interpreted as supportive but not definitive evidence of physiological feature localization. Moreover, the use of scanned ECG images introduces inherent limitations in signal fidelity compared with raw digital waveforms, and the absence of matched digital ECG data precluded direct comparison between image-based and signal-based modeling approaches. Nevertheless, prior work has demonstrated that automated digitization of scanned paper ECGs can recover waveform quality with correlation coefficients of 96–99% relative to native digital recordings, supporting the validity of image-based ECG analysis as a complementary approach [26]. In addition, restricting input to standard and augmented limb-leads pages improved consistency across participants but excluded precordial leads, which may contain important diagnostic information related to structural heart disease, myocardial ischemia, and ventricular abnormalities. Transparent reporting, external validation, and algorithmic fairness assessments will be essential before such tools can be safely implemented in practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference or evaluation of temporal prediction. The sample was derived from a single regional cohort (Silesia Diabetes–Heart Project), which may limit generalizability across populations. Although bootstrapped confidence intervals were used to estimate statistical robustness, external validation on independent datasets is necessary to confirm model stability. Moreover, because only image-based ECGs were analyzed, temporal waveform features such as QRS variability or P-wave duration could not be directly assessed. Future studies should evaluate whether integrating image- and signal-level representations or combining ECG data with clinical and biochemical features can further improve prediction accuracy.

Further research should evaluate prospective prediction of incident CKM-related outcomes and assess clinical utility in real-world workflows. Multimodal models that integrate ECGs with demographic, laboratory, and imaging data may provide more comprehensive risk assessment. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses are warranted to evaluate whether AI-based ECG screening could reduce healthcare expenditures through earlier detection and intervention.

In conclusion, this proof-of-concept study demonstrates that deep learning applied to scanned ECG images can predict CKM-related comorbidities in individuals with diabetes with moderate-to-high accuracy. While external validation in independent cohorts is required before any clinical application can be considered, these results suggest potential utility of ECG-based deep learning as a low-cost, scalable solution for integrated cardiometabolic and renal risk assessment.
