Developmental health and well-being of refugee children in resettlement contexts: population-level findings from Australia and Canada
Authors: Monique Gagné Petteni, Ben Edwards, Martin Guhn, Magdalena Janus, Anne Gadermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are critical global gaps in our understanding of the developmental health and well-being of children from refugee backgrounds in resettlement country contexts. Canada and Australia, two of the top refugee resettlement countries in the world that collect the same early childhood development data at a population level, are well positioned to make an important contribution. The study aimed to examine the developmental health and well-being of children from refugee backgrounds in Canada and Australia in comparison to children from immigrant (non-refugee) and non-immigrant backgrounds.MethodsThis cross-national, population-level study linked administrative data (demographic and immigration information) to teacher-reported Early Development Instrument (EDI) child development data (the Australian version is called the AvEDI). The study included kindergarten-aged children with an EDI/AvEDI record between 2009 and 2018 from first-generation refugee, first-generation immigrant and non-immigrant backgrounds. Developmental health and well-being were measured across five broad domains of functioning: communication skills and general knowledge, language and cognitive development, emotional maturity, social competence and physical health and well-being.ResultsThe study included 187 962 children in British Columbia, Canada (727 first-generation refugee and 7308 first-generation immigrant children) and 1 162 126 children from across Australia (3779 first-generation refugee and 49 925 first-generation immigrant children). In both Canada and Australia, children from refugee backgrounds had significantly lower scores on average than their non-immigrant peers across all five domains. Generally, immigrant (non-refugee) background predicted scores that were either significantly higher or not significantly different from their non-immigrant peers. Patterns of association in both Canada and Australia were remarkably similar.ConclusionThe evidence supports a broad, holistic approach to meeting the unique needs of children from refugee backgrounds – the similarity in findings across Canada and Australia suggests an international, collaborative approach could be fruitful.

We know that refugee children face unique, intersecting pre-migration and post-migration challenges that can impact their ability to adapt and thrive in resettlement country contexts. However, findings tend to be limited to small, demographically, culturally and/or linguistically distinct sub-populations and focused on singular domains of development.

Introduction

The number of resettled refugees hit a record high in 2024, 19% more than in the year previous, according to the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR).1 A disproportionately large number of refugees are children – with an estimated 41% percent of refugees being under the age of 18.2 When it comes to our understanding of the experiences and outcomes of refugee children, there are continuing concerns over critical data shortcomings, including data quality, missingness, and a lack of internationally comparable data and research.3 Canada and Australia stand to make an important contribution by coordinating efforts. From a global perspective, the two countries have many similarities: Both countries are regularly among the top three refugee resettlement countries globally (in addition to the USA).1 Both countries have relatively similar socio-demographic profiles, labour markets, and long histories of immigration with a heavy focus on admitting highly skilled migrants in addition to well-established family reunification and humanitarian resettlement programmes.4–6 Finally, both countries have been collecting the same teacher-reported questionnaire data on the developmental health and well-being of children at the population level called the Early Development Instrument (EDI; the Australian version of the EDI is known as the AvEDI). These similarities offer the unique opportunity to study the developmental health and well-being of children from refugee backgrounds, both within and across Canada and Australia.

Refugees, as defined by the UNHCR, are people who have fled their home country to escape conflict, violence or persecution.7 This definition distinguishes them from other migrant groups in that they are often fleeing their home countries for the sake of survival and seeking refuge from extreme conditions such as war.8 As a result of these experiences, young refugees can face multiple intersecting challenges, including mental health challenges associated with pre-migration adversity and trauma (eg, violence, persecution and the loss of family)9 10, coupled with post-migration struggles, including schooling, language, cultural and economic barriers.11 12 Perhaps as a result of this, much of the research pertaining to the well-being of refugees has narrowed in on specific psychiatric symptomatology.13 Although this work is exceedingly valuable, it has left a gap in our understanding of the broader picture of developmental health and well-being for refugee children. While attention has been given to the early development of children from immigrant backgrounds more generally,14 there is a distinct lack of research that specifically focuses on refugee children in early childhood. Addressing these gaps, this study examines the early developmental challenges and strengths across multiple domains of functioning for refugee children who resettle in early childhood – to provide critical information into where, as a global community, we are meeting and failing to meet the needs of resettled refugee children.

The current study

The purpose of the study was to investigate the developmental health and well-being of first-generation refugee children resettled in Canada and Australia by leveraging EDI/AvEDI data collected in kindergarten in both countries. The objectives were as follows: (1) to predict EDI/AvEDI scores across five domains of developmental health and well-being (communication skills and general knowledge, language and cognitive development, emotional maturity, social competence, physical health and well-being) for children from first-generation refugee backgrounds, in comparison to children from first-generation immigrant (non-refugee) and non-immigrant backgrounds in Canada and Australia. (2) To determine how refugee country of origin is associated with EDI/AvEDI scores for refugee children resettled in Canada and Australia. (3) To examine similarities and differences in developmental health and well-being for refugee children in the Canadian and Australian resettlement contexts.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This population-level cross-sectional study used linked, administrative and survey data on children’s developmental health and well-being in kindergarten in Canada and Australia. Data were collected through a teacher-reported questionnaire that is administered routinely in both countries at a population level (known as the EDI in Canada15; the Australian version of the EDI is now known as the AvEDI and is collected as part of the Australian Early Development Census; AEDC).16 All children in British Columbia, Canada and across Australia with an EDI/AvEDI record between 2009 and 2018 were included. In both countries, the EDI/AEDC datasets were linked to additional population-based administrative data at the person level to capture the demographic information required for the study. In Canada, this included migration records from Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC)17 and demographic information from the BC Ministry of Health (MOH). Ministry of Education and Child Care data helped to facilitate the linkage across data sources.18 Canadian data were linked by a trusted third-party (Population Data BC) using a probabilistic-deterministic approach. In Australia, data were linked to census and government migration records via the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ Australian Census and Migrant Integrated Data Set in order to identify child refugee status probabilistically (see online supplemental appendix A for details of the methodology). The University of British Columbia Behavioural Research Ethics Board (BREB H19-02775) in Canada and the Australian National University Human Research Ethics Committee (ANU HREC 2019/032) in Australia reviewed and approved this study. STROBE reporting guidelines were used to draft the manuscript (see STROBE reporting checklist). BMJ patient and public involvement statement: Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Measures

Early childhood developmental health and well-being

The EDI/AvEDI questionnaire completed by teachers was used to measure children’s development in five broad domains in kindergarten. Originally developed in Canada,19 it was later adapted for use in Australia with some minor item modifications (eg, the decision to eliminate some items that did not seem to impact scale precision; details on the adaptation process are described elsewhere).20 The questionnaire assesses five broad domains of development: (1) communication skills and general knowledge (eight items; eg, ‘How would you rate this child’s ability to use language effectively in English?’), (2) language and cognitive development (26 items; eg, ‘Would you say that this child is able to recognise numbers 1–10?’), (3) social competence (26 items in Canada; 24 items in Australia; eg, ‘How would you rate this child’s ability to get along with peers?’), (4) emotional maturity (28 items in Canada; 26 items in Australia; eg, ‘Would you say that this child is nervous, high-strung, or tense?’), and (5) physical health and well-being (13 items in Canada; 12 in Australia; eg, ‘How would you rate this child’s energy throughout the day?’). Items are scored from 0 (lowest score) to 10 (highest score). Mean domain scores range from 0 to 10 (higher scores indicate better developmental status). Since 1999, EDI data have been collected at a population level across the province of British Columbia in multi-year waves (generally 3 year waves). In Australia, the AvEDI has been collected as part of the AEDC national census every 3 years since 2009.21 There is an ever-growing body of work that has psychometrically examined and validated use of the EDI in many countries, including Australia and Canada.22 23 This includes evidence for retest-reliability, inter-rater reliability, differential item functioning and overall domain factor structure.19 24 25 This also includes evidence that the measure has similar psychometric properties (eg, factor loadings and internal consistency of domains and subdomains) across countries (eg, Canada and Australia) and language/culture groups.23 24

Migration variables

In Canada, children’s migration status and country of origin were determined from migration records through IRCC’s Permanent Resident Database.17 Children who were admitted through the economic or family immigration categories were coded as immigrants. Children (or their parents) who arrived under the refugee category were coded as refugees. Children with no IRCC migration record were coded as non-immigrants. In Australia, country of origin, year of arrival and language spoken at home was collected as part of the AEDC, and this information was used to identify refugee and immigrant status. Using linked census and migration data from the Australian Census and Migrant Integrated Dataset,26 country of birth, year of arrival and language spoken at home were used to estimate age--specific probabilities of being a child refugee (details in online supplemental appendix A).

Demographic variables

Information on child sex (female=1, male=0; gender data not available), age (converted to 15 AEDC age categories; ranging from 0 = < 3 years, 8 months to 15 = > 7 years, 6 months) and English as a second language status (ESL; also known as English language learner status in Canada; ESL=1, not ESL=0) were collected as part of the EDI (based on school registration records). Children living in metropolitan areas in kindergarten (ie, large, densely populated metropolitan centres vs rural/remote areas; 1=metropolitan, 0=not metropolitan) were identified in both countries using national geographical classifications (in Canada, Statistics Canada’s Census Metropolitan Area (CMA) classification from the MOH Consolidation File). In Australia, remoteness was measured using the Australian Statistical Geography Standard from the Australian Bureau of Statistics.27

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables in Canada and Australia for each sub-group (refugee, immigrant and non-immigrant). Intraclass correlation (ICC) values were examined at the teacher/assessor level and found to be above the 0.15 threshold (in both countries). Given this, we employed a nested multilevel regression model (MLM) approach to account for teacher/assessor-level differences/bias. The degree to which refugee and immigration status (vs non-immigrant status) predicted child development scores across the five EDI/AvEDI domains was assessed by five separate models: at level 1 (child level), in addition to refugee and immigrant status, each model adjusted for sex, age, ESL status and metropolitan area. The models were nested by (ie, adjusted for) teacher/assessor at level 2. A second series of models for each EDI domain were run to examine differences by refugee country of origin (this included Sudan, Iran, Iraq, Syria, Afghanistan, other country, in addition to Myanmar in the Australian models). This second MLM series included the same variables (with the exception of refugee status, now replaced by specific refugee countries of origin) and retained the same reference group (ie, the non-immigrant group). Analyses present 95% CIs and p-values, whereby p<.05 was considered statistically significant. The resulting standardised regression coefficients (and CIs) were plotted in two figures to show the similarities and differences in the magnitude of associations across countries and domains of development. Analyses were conducted in 2024/25 using STATA 16.1 software.

Missing data

Missing data for all covariates and outcomes ranged from 0%–1.8%. Given the combination of very low proportions of missing data and large sample size, missing data were deleted listwise in all analyses without any expected impact on the results. In Canada, teacher IDs were applied inconsistently in some cases, leading to 3.4% missing data. Due to this, ICC values may be under-estimated in the Canadian results.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Demographic and EDI/AvEDI mean scores for refugee, immigrant and non-immigrant children in Australia and Canada are presented in table 1. The study included 187 962 children with EDI data in British Columbia, Canada, including 727 first-generation refugee and 7308 immigrant children. There were 1 162 126 Australian children who had EDI data, including 3779 first-generation refugee and 49 925 first-generation immigrant children. Sex and age were similar across refugee, immigrant and non-immigrant groups. A higher proportion of refugee and immigrant children resided in metropolitan areas and were ESL learners, in comparison to non-immigrants in both countries. On average, the refugee and immigrant groups had been in Canada and Australia for a little over 2 years (in Canada, 2.38 and 2.52 years, respectively; in Australia, 2.25 and 2.67 years, respectively, all arriving sometime between birth and kindergarten).

Developmental health and well-being of refugee and immigrant children in Canada and Australia

Table 2 presents regression results predicting EDI developmental domain scores for refugee, immigrant, and non-immigrant children in Canada and Australia. In Canada and Australia, being a first-generation refugee was significantly associated with lower EDI scores across all five domains, even after accounting for sex, age, ESL and metropolitan area. In both countries, this relationship was strongest in the language and cognitive development, and communication skills and general knowledge domains. Being a first-generation immigrant (non-refugee) in Canada and Australia was also significantly associated with lower communication skills and general knowledge scores. However, this group had significantly higher scores or no significant difference in scores in all other domains in comparison to the non-immigrant group. Figure 1 provides a visual representation of the magnitude of the associations for refugee and immigrant children in both countries across the five domains. Being female, older, and from a metropolitan area was associated with higher EDI scores in all five domains across both countries. Having ESL status was associated with lower scores in both countries, except for the physical health and well-being domain in Australia, which was not significantly associated with ESL status.

Developmental health and well-being by refugee country of origin

Table 3 presents regression results that examine differences in associations by refugee country of origin (reference group: non-immigrants). The strongest associations were in the language and cognitive development and the communication skills and general knowledge domains, whereby each refugee country of origin group (ie, Sudan, Iran, Iraq, Syria, Afghanistan and other) was associated with lower scores. There was one exception to this: In Canada, being from Sudan was not significantly predictive of communication skills and general knowledge. For the remaining domains, refugee source country offered little predictive utility, particularly in the Canadian context, due to wide confidence intervals (see table 3). In Australia, originating from Myanmar and Afghanistan was associated with significantly higher scores in physical health and well-being (than their non-immigrant peers). Discussion

The purpose of the study was to investigate the developmental health and well-being of first-generation refugee children resettled in Canada and Australia, in comparison to their immigrant (non-refugee) and non-immigrant peers. In both Canada and Australia, refugee background predicted significantly lower developmental health and well-being in kindergarten across all five domains of development: communication skills and general knowledge, language and cognitive development, social competence, emotional maturity and physical health and well-being. These findings emphasise the need for a holistic approach to supporting children from refugee backgrounds – one that addresses functioning across multiple domains. This is in line with calls for a comprehensive, integrated approach to supporting children from refugee backgrounds.28 The overall patterns of association were found to be remarkably similar in the Canadian and Australian contexts. This offers evidence for commonalities in the early development of resettled refugee children and that an international, collaborative approach to supporting children and young families from refugee backgrounds could be fruitful. Given the similarities in the developmental profiles identified, collaboration across countries, such as the sharing of knowledge and resources (eg, policy/programming successes and lessons learnt) may prove beneficial in identifying the best ways to support the developmental health and well-being of refugee children in resettlement country contexts.

The current study findings also served to highlight the early developmental differences in resettled children from immigrant (non-refugee) and refugee backgrounds: In both Canada and Australia, immigrant background predicted scores that were significantly higher or not significantly different from their non-immigrant peers (exception: children from immigrant backgrounds had significantly lower scores in communication skills and general knowledge vs their non-immigrant peers). Canada and Australia (along with the United States) are the largest destinations for skilled immigrants in the world – together attracting approximately two-thirds of all skilled migrants globally.29 In both countries, the majority of newcomers are admitted through a skilled immigration pathway and are selected based on specific labour market criteria (eg, education, skills and experience and language ability).29 Indeed, children from immigrant backgrounds in resettlement countries such as Canada, Australia and the USA have been found to generally do well across a range of developmental outcomes, and this is often attributed to the selective nature of the immigration process and the associated socio-demographic advantages that children in this group may have (eg, parents with higher-than-average education levels).30 The study findings underscore that children from immigrant and refugee backgrounds are likely to require different types and levels of support on school entry. Based on the study findings, children from immigrant backgrounds may require support with communication skills, whereas children from refugee backgrounds are more likely to require greater support and a constellation of support across multiple domains of functioning. Furthermore, the differences identified across immigrant and refugee groups warrant a follow-up examination of the underlying drivers of these differences. For example, a notable next step would be further investigation into the modifiable, systemic barriers that are experienced specifically by refugees within and across Canadian and Australian contexts.

The refugee country of origin analyses served to highlight that specific refugee country-of-origin subgroups were not driving the overall pattern of findings for refugee children. Patterns of association in communication skills and general knowledge and language and cognitive development generally followed the overall refugee group patterns with minor variations across country groups. In the physical health and well-being, emotional maturity and social competence domains, refugee children’s source countries offered little predictive utility, particularly in the Canadian context, due to wide confidence intervals (see table 3), although some noteworthy differences were observed. In Australia, children from Afghanistan were scored significantly higher than their non-immigrant peers in the physical health and well-being domain (in Canada, this association was not significant). Children from Myanmar also had significantly higher scores than their non-immigrant peers in the physical health and well-being domain in Australia (this group was not included in the Canadian analysis due to insufficient sample size). Although the reasons for these nuances remain unclear, future strengths-based research on the specific modifiable resettlement country context factors that positively impact the developmental outcomes of children from refugee backgrounds may help illuminate (eg, culturally appropriate supports, sense of community, accessible programming).

Study limitations and future directions

The study has a number of limitations to consider. The smaller Canadian dataset led to some sample size limitations in the country-of-origin analyses. Wide CIs were observed in the Canadian context for some country groups, rendering interpretation more difficult (for the Sudanese and Syrian groups in particular). Given the Canadian data was restricted to one province (no comparable national dataset was available), we take caution in generalising it to other provinces in Canada whose contexts and refugee populations may differ. In Australia, refugee status was inferred probabilistically, and due to this methodological limitation, there is some risk of misclassification. For example, we note that for children originating from Iran and Iraq, EDI scores in the language and cognitive development domains were somewhat lower in the Canadian context. This may be partly due to misclassification: in Australia, it is possible that some children who arrived as immigrants were incorrectly classified as refugees (or vice-versa), which could have attenuated the associations found for the refugee groups in Australia.

Although the study was able to account for a number of socio-demographic factors available in both datasets, other important factors would help to further tease apart findings. Important pre-migration and post-migration factors, such as exposure to violence and time spent in refugee camps before resettlement would offer additional insight. Understanding the impact of larger, systemic influences, such as specific refugee resettlement policies and programmes both within and across countries would also help to illuminate our understanding. For example, in Canada, refugees can be resettled through different streams (eg, the Government-Assisted Refugee and the Privately Sponsored Refugee programmes), each of which offers different supports post-migration and may be associated with different childhood developmental outcomes. Future research that accounts for these factors and captures the latest waves of refugee resettlement (beyond 2018) may contribute additional insights into variations in the development of children from refugee backgrounds.

Finally, we note that it will be important to delve more deeply into the patterns that underlie the five broad domains of development measured in the current study – including unique developmental strengths that may be masked by overarching developmental patterns for children from refugee backgrounds. More nuanced research approaches (eg, qualitative and mixed methodologies) will be important for examining the unique, underlying developmental strengths children from refugee backgrounds bring and in particular, how those strengths can be used to support their overall developmental health and well-being. Indeed, the quantitative study findings offer only a piece of the puzzle. In line with current best practice,31 incorporating the voices of refugee children and their families is fundamental to ensuring that policies and supports in early childhood are culturally appropriate, relevant, meaningful and effective.

Conclusion

The current study offered a unique population-based, holistic view of the developmental health and well-being of children from refugee backgrounds in Canada and Australia. To our knowledge, the study is the first of its kind to leverage large, population-based data from across two prominent resettlement countries globally to investigate the developmental health and well-being of children from refugee backgrounds. Altogether, findings from both countries highlight the need for an enhanced, comprehensive approach to early childhood supports for resettled refugee children that spans multiple domains of development. The findings add to the literature by identifying key similarities across the two refugee resettlement contexts and offering cross-national insights into how best to meet the needs of children from refugee backgrounds. Finally, the study showcases the importance of high-quality, comparable data for monitoring the early childhood developmental health and well-being of refugee children globally to ensure they are receiving the early supports they need across national and international contexts.
