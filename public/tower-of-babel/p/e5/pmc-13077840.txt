Sex differences in blood pressure and physical activity among socioeconomically vulnerable Portuguese schoolchildren aged 6 to 10 years: insights from the BeE-school Project
Authors: Neiva Leite, Maiara Cristina Tadiotto, Ana Duarte, Claudia Augusto, Maria José Silva, Caroline Brand, Jorge Mota, Beatriz Pereira, Rafaela Rosário
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBlood pressure (BP) measurements are clinical parameters that are rarely used in routine assessments of schoolchildren under 10 years of age. However, changes in lifestyle and excess weight have led to the presence of early hypertension in the pediatric population. The aim of this study was to analyze the frequency of hypertensive measures in schoolchildren according to sex and whether they are related to anthropometric measurements, sedentary behavior, and physical activity practices during weekdays and on weekends.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 735 Portuguese children living in socioeconomical vulnerable (380 boys and 355 girls) aged 6 to 10 years old. Body mass, height, waist circumference (WC), systolic (SBP) and diastolic BP (DPB) were assessed. Body mass index (BMI), BMI z-score (BMI-z), tri-ponderal mass index (TMI) and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) were calculated. Sedentary behavior, and physical activity levels were determined by accelerometry. BP was classified using four categories: normal, elevated BP, hypertension (isolated SBP), and hypertension (DBP or both). Descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA and generalized linear models were used.ResultsThe frequency of hypertension DBP or both found in girls (9.5%) did differ significantly from that observed in boys (5.6%). Girls had higher DBP mean and risk of elevated BP of 1.79 times compared to boys. In relation to the practice of physical activities during weekdays and weekends, the girls had a higher mean of light physical activity and lower moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) (p < 0.001) than boys. Hypertensive girls (DBP or both) had higher TMI (p = 0.015) and WHtR (p = 0.023) than normotensive. The odds of SBP hypertension are higher with high TMI (OR = 2.39) and WHtR (OR = 2.67), just as hypertension (DBP or both) is associated with high BMI-z (OR = 3.39) and insufficient weekend MVPA (OR = 3.61).ConclusionGirls had a higher mean of DBP, risk of elevated BP and lower MVPA than boys. Hypertensive girls have a higher TMI and WHtR means than normotensive. Schoolchildren under 10 years with excessive BMI-z, TMI, WHtR or insufficient weekend MVPA have a higher risk of hypertension.Keywords: Adiposity, Children, Blood pressure, Hypertension, Physical activity

Introduction

Hypertensive measures have increased in the pediatric population of several countries [1], including children and adolescents in Portugal [2]. In children, the cutoff points for diagnosis vary according to age, sex and height, with those whose values are in the 90th percentile being classified as elevated blood pressure (BP) and those whose values are equal to or greater than the 95th percentile being classified as hypertensive [3], standardization that favors future research [4]. Epidemiological data on hypertensive measures are scarce in children, due to the lack of routine BP collection [5].

In Portugal, 4.5% of children aged 6 to 9 years have elevated BP, while 3.7% are classified as hypertensive. The percentages are higher according to nutritional status, waist circumference (WC) and body fat percentage, with a higher incidence in obese children, reaching 13.8% of elevated BP and 8.6% of hypertension [6]. Among adolescents, boys have a higher frequency of high BP than girls [7, 8], but in children this difference is not clear. Evidence indicates that boys and girls have different BP profiles during childhood, with differences in systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) levels [6, 8].

Thus, the increase in hypertension occurs mainly due to the presence of obesity, inadequate eating habits, longer screen time and less physical activity [9]. Sedentary behavior (SB) for more than 2 h per day, regardless of whether physical activity is sufficient or insufficient, leads to a greater risk of an increase in the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) in adolescents [10]. Cross-sectional studies have evaluated the tri-ponderal mass index (TMI) and its relationship with elevated BP in children and adolescents [11], some found that the TMI can discriminate body fat distribution more accurately than body mass index (BMI) [12] and higher TMI levels were related to higher BP in girls [13]. A longitudinal study demonstrated that the influence of TMI on BP is stronger than the reverse [14]. The presence of central obesity increases the risk of high BP in schoolchildren by six times, while engaging in sufficient moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) reduces the risk of increased DBP [8].

The school environment represents an opportunity to promote health education, as observed in the educational intervention involving teachers, family members and children in the BeE-school Project, whose results after 16 weeks demonstrated significant improvements in anthropometric measurements of the schoolchildren [15]. One of the key advantages of the school setting is its structured nature: on school weekdays, children follow routines that offer both intentional (e.g., physical education, recess, active breaks) and unintentional (e.g., transitions between classes, walking to school) opportunities to be physically active. In contrast, such structured prompts are often absent on unstructured days such as weekends, potentially leading to lower activity levels and increased SB [16].

In this context, physical education classes can contribute to increasing levels of physical activity [17] and encourage students to meet the recommendations of 60 min of daily physical activity [18], aiming to maintain health and physical fitness [19]. Boys tend to engage in more MVPA than girls [20], but there is a progressive decline in physical fitness in children and adolescents, especially in those with lower fitness, as well as a clear improvement in active individuals [21].

The clinical practice of hypertension screening is imperative in the approach to the pediatric population and with the support of family members [4]. The results of educational interventions demonstrate the importance of epidemiological studies and monitoring of anthropometric measurements and BP in schoolchildren [22], as a means of prevention and health surveillance for the child and youth population. There is a scientific gap in studying children under 10 years old, with clinical outcome in hypertensive measures and its relationship with lifestyle, anthropometric measures and physical activity during the week and on weekends.

Therefore, it is important to seek information that will add scientific knowledge at earlier stages of life, avoiding negative impacts on children’s health. Thus, the aim of this study was to analyze the frequency of hypertensive measures in Portuguese schoolchildren living in socioeconomical vulnerable in both sexes and whether they are related to anthropometric measurements, sedentary behavior, or level of physical activity practices during the weekdays and on weekends.

Methods

Characterization of the study

This study is transversal and part of BeE-school Project, a cluster-randomized trial, which was conducted in schoolchildren from the city of Braga, Portugal [15].

Sample selection and data collection location

The sample selection process was conducted in ten primary schools located in economically and socially disadvantaged areas characterized by poverty and social exclusion. The inclusion criteria were applied: (a) children from primary schools prone to vulnerability; (b) age between 6 and 10 years; (c) no contraindications to carrying out assessments and tests. The exclusion criteria were: (a) non-participation in all the assessments; (b) children with impairments, whether cognitive or physical, that could compromise data collection. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Life and Health Sciences (CE.CVS 009/2022) of the University of Minho and registered in the Clinical Trials database/platform (NCT05395364). All parents and/or guardians were informed and signed an informed consent form, in accordance with the criteria of the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki), and the children were asked to assent to participate before the procedures.

Participants

The sample consisted of 735 children (380 boys and 355 girls) between the ages of 6 and 10. The schoolchildren were separated according to sex and divided into four categories according to BP: (a) Group 1: normotensive; (b) Group 2: Elevated BP; (c) Group 3: hypertension (isolate SBP); and (d) Group 4: DBP hypertension without or with SBP hypertension (both) [4, 5]. The classification highlights the increase in isolated SBP (Group 3) and the isolated DBP or both (Group 4) as a diagnosis of hypertension [4, 5].

A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 to determine whether the sample size of 735 participants was sufficient to detect a statistically significant effect in a logistic regression model. The analysis was based on an expected odds ratio of 1.8, a two-tailed test, an alpha level of 0.05, and a baseline event probability of 0.05. The results indicated a statistical power of 95.4%, demonstrating that the sample was adequate to detect meaningful associations.

Somatic maturation

Biological maturity was estimated by using the somatic maturation approach by determining the distance in from peak height velocity (PHV). This approach is structured on a mathematical model based on height, age, and sex. The prediction of age at PHV was determined by subtracting from the maturity-offset from the chronological age [23].

Anthropometrics

Anthropometric measurements were taken by trained researchers using standardized procedures. These measurements were taken with the children lightly dressed and without shoes. Body mass and height measurements were recorded using a pediatric scale/stadiometer (SECA 799), and body mass was rounded to the nearest 100 g. Two measurements were taken, and the procedure was repeated in case of doubt or uncertainty about the measurement. BMI was calculated by the ratio of body mass to height squared (kg∙m-2) and converted into standard deviation (SD) scores (BMI-z) adjusted for specific age and sex categories [24]. BMI-z was used for adiposity status according to cutoff points and subsequently categorized into three groups as follows: normal weight (SD = 1), overweight (SD > 1 and < 2) and obesity (SD ≥ 2).

Tri-ponderal mass index (TMI), expressed in k∙gm-3, was calculated by the division of body mass by height cubed and classified according to cutoff points proposed by Niu et al. [25]. WC was measured in centimeters using a flexible, inextensible anthropometric tape (resolution of 0.1 cm), just 1 cm above the iliac crest, parallel to the ground, with the individual standing, abdomen relaxed, arms alongside the body and feet together [26]. The waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was calculated as WC/height, and classified according to cutoff points proposed by Nambiar, Hughes, Davies [27].

Blood pressure and heart rate at rest

Two heart rates at rest and BP measurements (SBP and DBP) were taken after resting in a sitting position for 10 min. In case of doubt or uncertainty about the measurement, the procedure was repeated. SBP and DBP were measured on the right arm at heart level, using a previously calibrated digital sphygmomanometer, with the cuff size appropriate to the individual’s arm circumference. Mean blood pressure (MBP) was calculated using the sum of one-third of the SBP and two-thirds of the DBP. The mean BP measurement was considered for classification. Elevated BP for 6 and 10-year-olds was defined as values ≥ 90th percentile, and < 95th, according to sex, age, and height percentile for children. BP values ≥ 95th percentile were classified as hypertensive [3, 4].

Sedentary behavior and physical activity

The students were instructed to wear the triaxial Actigraph wGT3X-BT accelerometer (Actigraph LLC, Pensacola, FL, USA) continuously for 24 hours a day over a 7-day period on the dominant wrist, except during water-related activities such as bathing or swimming. The term “triaxial” refers to the device’s ability to measure acceleration across three axes (X, Y, and Z), which allows a more comprehensive capture of movement patterns and enhances the accuracy of physical activity assessment. The decision to position the device on the dominant side was made to provide greater comfort for the participants and to facilitate verification of the device’s use for the researchers.

The research team conducted on-site visits to schools in order to place accelerometer devices on the participants. At the time, they guided the students with detailed instructions and documentation sheets to record pertinent information regarding the children’s daily activities and sleep patterns from the preceding night, including instances of non-wear and specific bedtime and wake-up times. After a week, the research team returned to the schools to retrieve the accelerometers, download data, and prepare the data for analysis. Data was excluded when accelerometer usage time was < 16 h per day, on a minimum of three weekdays and one weekend day.

The data were accessed and analyzed by the specific Actigraph wGT3X-BT software. The periods of physical activities were classified according to the acceleration intensity thresholds established by Hildebrand et al. [28], according to the Euclidean Norm Minus One (ENMO). SB was defined as intensity of physical activity with ENMO < 35.0 mg, light physical activity (LPA) as ENMO ≥ 35.0 and ≤ 200.0 mg, and MVPA as ENMO > 200.0 mg. The mean time of SB was recorded as hours/day. The time spent on LPA and MVPA was recorded as the mean of minutes per week and on the weekend, with sample participants being dichotomized into insufficiently active (< 60 min/day) and sufficiently active (≥ 60 min/day) [18].

Statistical analysis

Data normality was verified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and the assumption of homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and absolute and relative percentages, were used to characterize the sample. The differences between the sexes were verified by the Mann-Whitney test. For comparisons between the BP groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA one-way) with post-hoc Bonferroni and generalized linear models were used. Bootstrapping procedures (10000 re-samplings; 95% IC BCa) were performed to obtain greater reliability of the results, to correct deviations from normality in the sample distribution and to account for differences between group sizes. Differences in the frequency between groups were analyzed using logistic regression. The significance level was p ≤ 0.05. The data was processed using SPSS version 29.0.1.

Results

A total of 735 Portuguese children, comprising 380 boys and 355 girls, aged 6 to 10 years, participated in the study. Boys and girls had similar mean ages and anthropometric characteristics. However, girls had higher DBP mean (p = 0.009) and heart rate at rest (p = 0.003) than boys. In relation to physical activities during the week and weekends, girls practiced higher LPA and less MVPA than boys (p < 0.001). SB time was similar between sexes on weekdays, but girls showed significantly less SB than boys on the weekend (p = 0.016). Children who are insufficiently active have a higher risk of hypertensive measures than active, OR (CI95%) was 3.61 (1.30 to 10.0, p = 0.014) in hypertension (DBP or both). Discussion

The main results showed higher mean DBP and lower participation in MVPA in girls compared to boys. Furthermore, hypertensive girls presented increased TMI and WHtR compared to normotensive girls. Concerning age, somatic maturation anthropometry, boys and girls presented similar measurements. These results are partially similar to those found in Spanish girls, who presented higher mean DBP, but higher WC than boys [29]. In fact, studies do not perceive anthropometric differences and adiposity distribution between Portuguese boys and girls in the prepubertal phase [30]. It is possible that both sexes who have excess adiposity are associated with inadequate nutrition [31]. Rodrigues et al. [6] found that hypertensive measures were more frequent in those children who practiced fewer physical activities. Prepubescent girls tend to practice less MVPA than boys [32], difference that increases after the age of 14, with a significant decrease being found in MVPA in girls, around 47 min for each additional year of age [33]. Lower engagement in MVPA at early ages may contribute to chronic elevations in DBP, which is more closely related to peripheral vascular resistance and lifestyle behaviors. In contrast, the participation of pre-pubertal Portuguese girls in MVPA is four times higher when mothers are sufficiently active, demonstrating the importance of family example [34].

The World Health Organization (WHO) establishes that the pediatric population must practice 60 min of MVPA daily, totaling 420 min per week [18], but boys and girls tend to reduce their MVPA [33]. On the other hand, recent research [35] analyzed the studies that were the basis for the WHO recommendations on the ideal amount of physical activity, SB and sleep in early childhood. The authors do not refute the importance of healthy lifestyle habits at this stage, but they highlight that the construction of the WHO recommendations was mainly based on the analysis of studies with results related to obesity. This is particularly relevant when interpreting BP outcomes, as evidence linking physical activity patterns to pediatric hypertension is still emerging. They support the goals of behavioral surveillance but point out that there are few studies and limited evidence in the child population, suggesting the importance of more studies at early stages, with the development of public health interventions to promote healthy behavior.

Evidence from a recent study conducted in China [36] indicates that increased SB during out-of-school hours is associated with a higher likelihood of overweight and obesity in children and adolescents. The study demonstrated linear dose-response relationship between time spent in sleep, MVPA, SB, and excess body mass. Notably, the substitution of 10 min of SB with sleep or MVPA was associated with a 4.4% and 3.7% reduction in the risk of overweight and obesity, respectively. Given the close relationship between excess adiposity and elevated BP, particularly DBP, these findings may help explain early hypertension patterns in children with high sedentary exposure. These results highlight the importance of behavioral modifications during leisure time and support the integration of sufficient sleep and regular physical activity into children’s routines to prevent cardiometabolic outcomes.

However, the pediatric population has not been engaging in recommended levels of MVPA [37] and have a predominance of SB [38]. It is important to establish limits to build a healthier routine with family members, as children and adolescents tend to exceed the maximum daily screen time established for different age groups [39], a factor that can increase the initial onset of chronic-degenerative disorders, such as hypertensive measures. A recent review highlights the importance of primary assessment of risk factors in children and adolescents, such as high BP, a pre-existing and often undiagnosed condition that leads to a greater propensity for developing cardiovascular diseases in adulthood [40]. Early findings are made difficult by its silent nature and also by the low routine of BP measurement in this population [41].

The diagnosis of hypertension is made by changes in SBP or/and DBP [3]. However, the SBP response can be influenced by emotional factors, such as fear and apprehension of the pediatric population, at the time of measurement [42, 43]. Additionally, the nature of epidemiological studies, where measurements are obtained outside routine clinical monitoring, may also impact SBP values [44, 45]. This reinforces the relevance of DBP as a complementary and potentially more stable marker of chronic BP alterations in children. In overweight children and adolescents, 24-hour BP monitoring confirmed elevated BP and hypertension in approximately one third of participants of those initially identified in office assessment. These findings suggest a white coat phenomenon, with a correlation of 0.39 between office SBP and mean daytime SBP [46].

The innovation of our proposal was to analyze, as a diagnosis of hypertension, mainly the results related to the increase in DBP, as they represent signs of variations in peripheral vascular resistance [47]. However, we also evaluate the isolated increase in SBP, even though we know that sometimes it can be associated with the adrenergic discharge caused by the stress of measurements and not necessarily because the child is in fact hypertensive. The DBP seems to better represent chronic changes and greater responses to lifestyle changes in hypertensive adolescents [48] and is better related to the analysis of the practice of MVPA [8]. Furthermore, the frequency of hypertension according to SBP was excessively high as found in studies with children and adolescents [49, 50], even above values found in adults.

In our study, hypertensive girls (DBP or both) had higher TMI and WHtR means than normotensive girls, but these differences did not occur among boys. In clinical practice, authors suggest that TMI can be used as an effective predictor for the diagnosis of hypertension, especially in girls and older adolescents [13]. Girls with increased WHtR and family history of hypertension have a higher risk of hypertension [51]. Central obesity increases the risk of high BP in schoolchildren by six times and general obesity by three times, with DBP being protected by regular physical activity [8]. Reduced MVPA and increased screen time are associated with reduced physical fitness and increased adiposity [6, 33], while adolescents who regularly practice physical activities and have less SB have a better adiposity profile [52]. Portuguese children and adolescents with lower cardiorespiratory fitness have greater adiposity [30]. However, in the present study, girls did not show differences in MVPA between the different BP categories. In contrast, boys with hypertensive (DBP or both) were more active than those with normal BP, but the frequency of hypertensive measures was lower than girls. This finding can be explained by sex-specific patterns of spontaneous physical activity, residual confounding related to growth and maturation, or possible compensatory increases in activity among boys with elevated BP.

In this study, the frequency of elevated BP and hypertension found in girls was similar to boys. These findings are different from Rodrigues et al. [6] and Martin-Espinosa et al. [29], who found that girls had a higher frequency of hypertensive measures than boys, respectively, in Portuguese (♀=4.3% and ♂=3.1%) and Spanish schoolchildren (♀=18.7% and ♂=15.1%). Other studies found a higher frequency of hypertension in girls when considering DBP (♀=16.6% and ♂=10.3%) and in boys in the analysis of SBP [50]. In girls the value was higher than that found in a similar age group in Lisbon [6], but lower in a broader age range in Asian children and adolescents [50]. The demographic characteristics, measurement methodology and whether hypertension is diagnosed using SBP or DBP may influence differences in results between different studies in children.

Additionally, our participants were similar in proportion to the categories of anthropometric measurements in the four classifications of BP; however, insufficient MVPA (weekend) presented a higher frequency of hypertension. Rodrigues et al. [6] found a higher frequency of hypertensive measures in participants with greater adiposity depicted by BMI, WC and skin folds. However, different results regarding which anthropometric measurements are associated with arterial hypertension, with BMI representing total adiposity and WC representing central adiposity. During puberty, studies published over a decade ago found BMI more strongly associated with hypertensive measures [53, 54]. However, more recent research suggests that WC [8, 55] and TMI [12, 14] have a stronger association with BP. This shift may reflect changes of lifestyle over recent years, particularly increased screen time and reduced MVPA, which are often associated with prolonged sitting., SB has been linked to increased WC in children [56] and higher WHtR in adolescents [10]. Adolescent girls spend more time sitting and have greater adiposity, as well as less abdominal strength than boys [57]. In contrast, the detrimental relationship between screen time and WC may be attenuated by higher levels of cardiorespiratory fitness in pediatric population [58]. Additionally, reducing 1 h of screen time per MVPA was associated with lower overall adiposity, SBP and DBP, as well as replacing it with sleep time was associated with lower central adiposity in children aged 6 to 11 years [59].

In the assessment of hypertension risk and dichotomous analyses, our results demonstrated that girls have a risk of hypertension, but the girls did not present anthropometric differences or greater adiposity than boys. Generally, there are no anthropometric differences in the prepubertal phase [30], however there are differences in adherence to MVPA [33]. Like this, the higher risk of hypertensive measures in girls under 10 years of age is not explained by greater physiological adiposity, since its greater accumulation is a characteristic of hormonal changes in the female pubertal stage [60]. Higher adiposity in girls persists in the next stages of life [61]. In contrast, during adolescence, boys present more hypertensive measures than girls [8, 11]. Therefore, other factors than adiposity may be involved in the appearance of hypertensive measures in children under 10 years of age.

Our results demonstrated that excessive adiposity is a risk factor for hypertension measures. TMI and WHtR are associated with elevated BP and DBP hypertension, while BMI-z for DBP hypertension. It is worth noting that changes in lifestyle and excess central adiposity have led to the presence of initial hypertension in the pediatric population [8] and increased TMI [13]. We found that lower participation in weekend MVPA is associated with a higher risk of DBP hypertension in children, precisely when children are developing activities with their families. This reinforces the importance of investigating the presence of hypertensive measures in childhood and their relationship with insufficient MVPA during the week and on weekends, especially in children under 10 years of age. In the prepubertal phase, children’s physical activities on weekends depend on family participation. The importance of family encouragement and modeling in the practice of MVPA, as found in Portuguese girls [34], can improve engagement in weekend activities and to reduce the chance of the appearance of hypertensive measures.

The school environment represents an opportunity to promote health education and can reduce overall adiposity [15], as well as identify problems related to SB and insufficient physical activity. It was observed that the lower development of physical fitness components is associated with obesity, and that engaging in physical activities at school improves the physical health of obese adolescents [62]. Boys and girls show similar benefits in adiposity measures after lifestyle interventions [61], which reinforces the importance of educational measures for children and adolescents. Studies demonstrate a reduction of SBP and DBP with regular practice of physical exercises in children and adolescents [63] and in hypertensive adolescents [48], being considered the most effective non-pharmacological therapy for hypertensive adolescents and young adults [64].

A key strength of the present study lies in its comprehensive and multidimensional approach to assessing health indicators in school-aged children, including objective measures of physical activity using accelerometry and BP measurements in a real-world school setting. The use of standardized protocols for data collection and the inclusion of a large, diverse sample contribute to the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Moreover, the use of accelerometers over a seven-day period aligns with best practices for children aged 6 to 10 years, as supported by previous studies in similar populations [32]. This duration is considered adequate for capturing habitual MVPA, particularly in this age group. In younger children (under 6 years), 3–4 days of data collection is generally recommended for MVPA and 4–7 days for LPA [65], further validating our methodological choices.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, BP measurements were conducted in schools and on a single day, which may introduce variability due to contextual factors such as emotional state (e.g., fear or stress) at the time of measurement. These readings were not confirmed using ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM), which is considered the gold standard for BP diagnosis in pediatric populations due to its superior diagnostic accuracy [46]. The absence of ABPM confirmation may have led to potential misclassification of hypertension status, particularly in cases of white coat hypertension. Nevertheless, it is important to note that few studies have established normative ABPM values in children and adolescents [66], making this method still challenging to implement on a large scale.

Future research could benefit from the inclusion of ABPM to enhance the accuracy of BP classification. Additionally, longitudinal data would be valuable for understanding the long-term health implications of the observed associations. This study identified the BP measurements are clinical and non-invasive parameters that can be used in the evaluation of schoolchildren. Hypertension has increased in the pediatric population, but the BP measurements are rarely used in routine assessments of schoolchildren. There is a lack of national and international epidemiological studies that evaluate hypertensive measures (pre-hypertension and arterial hypertension) in the age group of 6 to 10 years. The risk of developing arterial hypertension increases when the abdominal circumference is high [8], as well as being greater in the presence of a sedentary lifestyle and a family history of hypertension [41, 51, 67]. Thus, the prevention of hypertensive measures in childhood is associated with encouraging the regular practice of MVPA and reducing screen time.

Conclusions

Changes in lifestyle and excess weight have led to the presence of early hypertensive in the pediatric population. It is highlighted that BP measurements are clinical and non-invasive parameters that can be used in early evaluation. Prepubescent schoolchildren did not present differences in relation to adiposity, but girls had a higher risk of hypertensive measurements and do not practice MVPA within the recommendations for children, like boys. Hypertensive girls have a higher TMI and WHtR means than normotensive, but hypertensive boys did not show anthropometric differences. While increased TMI, WHtR and BMI have been shown to be anthropometric measures with better potential for clinical screening of hypertensive measures in children 6 to 10 years of age. We conclude that schoolchildren under 10 years with excessive TMI, WHtR, BMI-z or insufficient weekend MVPA have a higher risk of hypertension. We highlight the importance of practicing MVPA on weekends and family participation in preventing hypertensive measures in pre-pubertal children.
