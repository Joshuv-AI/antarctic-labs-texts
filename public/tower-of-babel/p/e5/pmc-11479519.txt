Comparison of third-generation minimally invasive surgery, split-drill osteotomy, and triplanar chevron osteotomy for treating mild to moderate hallux valgus with metatarsalgia in middle-aged women: An observational study
Authors: Jianming Cao, Xuan Guo, Yu Zhang, Xin Wang, Yulong Ni, Chao Niu, Ziyang Yuan, Lihui Xu, Fengqi Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This retrospective study compared the clinical efficacy of third-generation minimally invasive surgery (3rd-MIS) split-drill osteotomy with soft tissue release to that of triplanar chevron osteotomy (TCO) for treating mild to moderate hallux valgus (HV) with metatarsalgia in middle-aged women. This study compared the efficacy of 3rd-MIS using split-drill osteotomy to that of TCO in treating mild to moderate HV with metatarsalgia in 52 middle-aged women from March 2022 to June 2023. Retrospectively analyzing a total of 52 patients (26 patients per group), we employed 2 distinct surgical methods across 2 groups in this study: the TCO group received a traditional TCO accompanied by soft tissue release, whereas the MIS group underwent a split-drill osteotomy with soft tissue release. The study compared the operation time, onset of weight-bearing, osteotomy healing, blood loss, pre- and postsurgery X-ray measurements, metatarsalgia, and foot function scores. In the TCO group, complications included peri-incisional numbness, elevation of the first metatarsal head with metatarsalgia, and bone hyperplasia with stiffness at the MTPJ in one patient each. In the MIS group, bunions were successfully corrected without significant complications like bone nonunion or avascular necrosis of the metatarsal head, and the metatarsal length was preserved. Significant differences in operation time, weight-bearing onset, healing, and blood loss were detected between groups (P < .05). Preoperatively, there were no significant differences in hallux valgus angle, 1–2 intermetatarsal angle, or distal metatarsal articular angle among the groups (P > .05). Nonetheless, notable postoperative differences were observed (P < .05). Foot function and pain scores, assessed by the American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society and the Visual Analogue Scale, showed significant improvements in both groups following surgery. Both surgical techniques effectively corrected the HV angle without significant changes in the first metatarsal length. The MIS group experienced significantly less pain on the second day postsurgery, as indicated by lower Visual Analogue Scale scores (P < .05). Both techniques were similarly effective in treating metatarsalgia. 3rd-MIS split-drill osteotomy with soft tissue release offers several advantages over TCO techniques for treating mild to moderate HV with metatarsalgia in middle-aged women, such as reduced trauma, quicker healing, smaller scars, and less pain after surgery.

1. Introduction

Hallux valgus (HV), marked by the hallux toe’s lateral deviation and the first metatarsal bone’s medial deviation or potential dislocation, predominantly affects women, particularly in their youth and middle age.[1] Often beginning in the teenage years, this condition typically worsens with time. The majority of researchers agree that footwear, genetics, rheumatoid arthritis, and posterior tibial tendon dysfunction are critical contributing factors. Conservative treatment is typically the first line of management and includes measures such as the use of orthotic devices, physical therapy, and pain management strategies. These approaches aim to alleviate symptoms, improve foot function, and delay the need for surgical intervention. However, when conservative treatments fail to provide adequate relief or when the deformity progresses significantly, surgical correction may be indicated. Surgical intervention remains the definitive approach for correcting this deformity, with over a hundred techniques available worldwide, each offering distinct advantages.

Chevron osteotomy (CO) continues to be the predominant surgical technique. The technique, however, has undergone modifications leading to the development of variants, such as the triplanar chevron osteotomy (TCO).[2] Despite its innovations, the traditional large-incision technique carries risks, including delayed healing; possible nerve and vessel damage; soft tissue trauma; and complications such as stiffness, adhesion, and necrosis at the first metatarsophalangeal joint (MTPJ).[3] Additionally, conventional methods may lead to substantial bone loss because of oscillation of the saw blade during osteotomy. While the TCO is designed to maintain or increase the first metatarsal length (FML) by modifying the saw blade angle, further study on its practical application in larger samples and its effectiveness in preventing postoperative complications is needed.

Conversely, minimally invasive surgery (MIS) is becoming increasingly popular due to its benefits, such as smaller incisions, less scarring, minimal trauma, quicker recovery, and less postoperative pain, achieving results comparable to those of conventional surgical methods.[4,5] Recent advancements in minimally invasive bunion valgus surgery have concentrated on less invasive techniques for osteotomy fixation to achieve superior surgical outcomes. Techniques like the Reverdin-Isham osteotomy and the Minimally Invasive Chevron-Akin procedure are often cited in the literature for their effectiveness and favorable outcomes.[6] This innovative approach is gradually being integrated into clinical practice. To date, no reports have documented the use of third-generation MIS (3rd-MIS) for treating HV with metatarsalgia in middle-aged women, especially in comparison with the traditional large-incision TCO technique.

This study evaluated the clinical efficacy of third-generation minimally invasive techniques, specifically split-drill osteotomy (MIS group), against traditional TCO (TCO group), which includes soft tissue release. Between March 2022 and June 2023, 52 middle-aged female patients with mild to moderate HV and metatarsalgia received treatment at the Foot and Ankle Surgery Department of the 3rd Hospital of Hebei Medical University. A retrospective analysis comparing pre- and postoperative imaging parameters was conducted. The parameters analyzed included the hallux valgus angle (HVA), 1–2 intermetatarsal angle (IMA), distal metatarsal articular angle (DMAA), FML, American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS), and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores, and occurrence of metatarsalgia, among other surgical indicators.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Patient groups and demographics

This study retrospectively analyzed the medical records of patients hospitalized for bunion valgus surgery at the Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University between March 2022 and June 2023. With ethical approval from the hospital’s review committee, all participants provided written informed consent.

Data from 2 groups comprising 52 middle-aged women with mild to moderate HV (totaling 52 feet, 26 feet per group) were collected and analyzed. These groups were distinguished by the presence of plantar callosities and metatarsalgia. Anterior-lateral radiographs were obtained for all patients, either at outpatient visits or postadmission, to measure the HVA, IMA, and DMAA. Multiple experienced foot and ankle surgeons performed the measurements. To assess interobserver variability, we employed repeated measurement methods and calculated the intraclass correlation coefficient for consistency. The intraclass correlation coefficient values were all above 0.85, indicating high reliability and consistency among the measurements. We employed the 3rd-MIS technique of split-drill osteotomy and TCO, both complemented by soft tissue release surgery, to compare pre- and postoperative imaging data and clinical outcomes. Osteotomy ends were secured with hollow compression screws or Kirschner wires. Detailed records of postoperative complications and recovery were maintained, with an average follow-up duration of 12 months.

2.2. Surgical preparation and methods

Two seasoned foot and ankle surgeons within the department conducted all HV surgeries. Patients, placed in a supine position, received either spinal canal or nerve block anesthesia, with a tourniquet applied to the ankle to manage bleeding. The type of anesthesia used could impact early mobilization and pain control, as noted in relevant literature.[7] The surgical equipment and instruments used varied between the MIS group and the TCO group. For the MIS group, the essential tools included a C-arm X-ray machine for intraoperative imaging, the NSK minimally invasive foot and ankle dynamic system, and a specialized split drill for precise bone cutting (Fig. 2). Additionally, a minimally invasive reduction instrument, standard orthopedic surgical instruments, Kirschner wires for temporary fixation, and a pendulum saw power unit (manufactured by Chongqing Xishan, Chongqing, China), along with its corresponding saw blade, were used. The utilization of such specialized equipment aims to facilitate accurate bone cuts, minimize tissue damage, and improve postoperative outcomes.

NSK power and split-drill: essential minimally invasive surgical equipment, highlighting the NSK power unit and split drill.

2.3. Soft tissue release surgery

Soft tissue release surgery was performed in both groups. Initially, a 1 cm incision was made between the first and second metatarsal bones at their distal ends. The incision was meticulously dissected in layers to safeguard the terminal branch of the deep peroneal nerve. The contracted adductor pollicis muscle at the base of the proximal phalanx was exposed and then cut. The intermetatarsal and the suspensory ligaments of the sesamoid were both severed. The thumb was then repositioned inwards. If the medial joint capsule was notably thickened and contracted, immediate and thorough release was needed.

2.4. TCO group

A longitudinal incision, approximately 4 to 5 cm long, was made along the medial side of the first MTPJ. The skin and joint capsule were incised, and a spindle-shaped segment of the thickened joint capsule was resected to fully expose the enlarged head of the MTPJ. Using a swing saw, the osteophyte on the medial side of the first MTPJ was removed, and the osteotomy line was angled between approximately 45° and 60°. A Chevron osteotomy spanning 3 planes was performed with a goose saw (refer to Figs. 3–5). The metatarsal head was laterally displaced and temporarily stabilized using a Kirschner wire. Final fixation of the metatarsal head, without elevation or rotation, was confirmed by fluoroscopy and achieved using a fully threaded cannulated screw. The joint capsule was closed with absorbable sutures, followed by the application of a sterile dressing. A pressure dressing was applied in a figure-8 configuration.

2.5. Minimally invasive surgery group

With the aid of fluoroscopic imaging, a 1.5 mm Kirschner wire was used to pinpoint the osteotomy site. A split drill was used to transversely cut the metatarsal bone 1 cm below the metatarsal head, external to the joint (Fig. 6). An approximately 2.0 mm long incision was made on the inner side of the metatarsal head. The split drill was inserted through the small incision at a controlled speed. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the metatarsal bone was cut transversely following placement of the Kirschner wire. Abundant normal saline irrigation was used during the osteotomy to prevent thermal injury. The separated metatarsal head was laterally displaced. Fluoroscopic imaging confirmed that the displacement of the metatarsal head was limited to less than half the width of the metatarsal shaft. Subsequently, 2 titanium Kirschner wires (or cannulated screws) were inserted from the dorsal aspect of the metatarsal base towards the head for fixation. Following fixation, anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopies confirmed the metatarsal head’s correct elevation, rotation, length, and positioning (Fig. 7). A 1 mm longitudinal incision was performed on the medial aspect of the MTPJ capsule, and a split drill was used to remove proliferated osteophytes and part of the joint capsule. The 3 small incisions were sealed using absorbable sutures (Fig. 8). The sections were covered with a sterile dressing and wrapped in a figure-8 bandage.

2.6. Postoperative management

The bandaging technique is critical in the postoperative care of minimally invasive bunion surgery. Proper bandaging methods are vital for maintaining soft tissue balance and preventing recurrence.[8,9] The essential bandaging steps involved placing gauze between the first and second toes and then wrapping it around the medial side of the big toe. Simultaneously, inwards pressure was applied to the MPTJ to ensure slight plantar flexion of the large toe with an “8” shaped wrap. The bandage was secured around the ankle joint, leaving the distal toes and toenails exposed to monitor blood supply and encourage early functional exercises. Routine antibiotic administration was complemented by changing dressings every other day. From the third to fourth day after surgery, patients could wear special non-weight-bearing footwear and begin to move around. Sutures were removed 2 weeks after the surgery. Physical therapy began after the wound healed, with standard bandaging techniques advised to keep the big toe in a neutral position for up to 6 weeks following the surgery. X-ray imaging was performed 4 to 6 weeks after surgery. After confirming bone healing, the Kirschner wires were removed, enabling the patient to advance to full weight-bearing activities. At the final follow-up, the patient underwent standing front and side X-ray examinations to evaluate the HVA, IMA, DMAA, and FML.

2.7. Outcome measures

The study enrolled 26 middle-aged women in each group: the TCO group (aged 41–63, average age 51.62 ± 5.27) and the minimally invasive group (aged 40–65, average age 52.50 ± 6.90) (Table 1). The basic characteristics of both groups, such as age and the number of metatarsalgia cases, related to patients’ clinical history, were compared and no significant differences were found (P > .05). Surgical outcomes, such as operation duration, weight-bearing time, osteotomy healing time, bleeding volume, and changes in X-ray parameters (HVA, IMA, DMAA, and FML), were compared between the groups from presurgery to the final follow-up. Due to the visual disturbances observed in all 5 metatarsals on lateral X-rays, we assessed the FML—the length between the proximal and distal articular surfaces of the first metatarsal bone—using anteroposterior non-weight-bearing foot radiographs taken before and after surgery[10] (Fig. 9). Pain and functional status were evaluated with the VAS[11] and the AOFAS score,[12] respectively, before surgery, on the second day post-surgery, and at the last follow-up. The scoring criteria were as follows: 85 to 100 indicated excellent results, 75 to 85 indicated good results, 60 to 75 indicated fair results (including the lowest value), and scores below 60 indicated poor results.

2.8. Data analysis

This study analyzed various clinical data variables (see Supplementary File 1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N695), which help assess the overall health status of the patients. Data analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 18.0, IBM, Armonk, NY). The various angular parameters of patients with HV are presented as the means ± standard deviation. For normally distributed data, two-sample t tests were used, while Mann–Whitney U test was used for data that did not follow a normal distribution. Additionally, the Pearson chi-square test was employed to analyze categorical data statistically. A P value below .05 was considered statistically significant. The test outcomes showed significant differences (see Supplementary File 2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N696).

3. Results

Patients were monitored for 12 to 15 months post-surgery, with an average follow-up of 12 months. Bone healing at the osteotomy site was successful, and the HV deformity was corrected in all cases. Within the TCO group, one patient developed elevation of the first metatarsal head and metatarsalgia postsurgery, while another experienced bone hyperplasia at the MTPJ and stiffness in the first MTPJ. In the minimally invasive group, all bunions were corrected without complications such as bone nonunion, skin necrosis, numbness, or avascular necrosis of the metatarsal head, and no shortening of the metatarsal length was observed. The comparison of surgical indicators, including operation time, weight-bearing time, osteotomy healing time, and blood loss, between the 2 groups revealed statistically significant differences (P < .05), as presented in Table 2. No significant differences in HVA, IMA, or DMAA were observed between the 2 groups before surgery, but the differences between pre- and postsurgery values were statistically significant (P < .05). The FML did not significantly differ pre- and postsurgery between the groups (P > .05). This demonstrates that both surgical techniques are equally effective in correcting the angle of HV deformity, yielding satisfactory results without significant changes in the FML. The preoperative AOFAS scores for the MIS and TCO groups were 44.08 ± 6.75 and 43.54 ± 3.91 points, respectively, showing no significant difference (P = .726 > 0.05); the postoperative scores were 83.15 ± 5.63 and 84.50 ± 5.85 points, respectively, with no significant difference (P = .402 > 0.05). Nonetheless, the marked increase in postoperative scores for both groups (P < .001) demonstrated that each surgical method substantially improved foot function. Both groups experienced a significant reduction in VAS scores after surgery (P < .001). Notably, on the second day postsurgery, the MIS group reported significantly lower VAS scores (P = .002 < 0.05), indicating reduced pain and increased patient satisfaction. Furthermore, the incidence of metatarsalgia decreased post-surgery, with 7 patients in the TCO group and 5 in the MIS group. However, this difference was not statistically significant (P = .835). This suggests that both techniques are equally effective in treating conditions related to plantar callosities and metatarsalgia. 4. Discussion

Chevron osteotomy is a frequently employed technique for treating mild to moderate HV.[13] Numerous studies have confirmed its effectiveness in rectifying HV deformities and reducing symptoms.[14] However, dissatisfaction with the surgical results arises in some patients due to issues such as bone absorption, displacement, or dorsal angulation at the osteotomy site, which can result in metatarsalgia. Metatarsalgia, broadly referred to as forefoot pain, has a range of pathological and physiological causes. Frequently, it is not the osteotomy itself but rather insufficient surgical planning or execution that may result in metatarsalgia as a potential complication when treating HV. In our previous research, we introduced a new method, TCO, which, compared to CO, showed no significant difference in correctional ability but was superior in increasing the FML. However, TCO, involving traditional extensive incisions, is prone to thermally damaging soft tissues and bones, potentially resulting in delayed or nonunion at the osteotomy site and even damage to neurovascular structures, leading to complications, such as numbness and necrosis of the metatarsal heads. Over the past 2 to 3 decades, MIS has been increasingly applied to correct HV deformities, achieving significant clinical success with benefits, such as minimal trauma, smaller postoperative scars, quicker patient recovery, and fewer complications.[15,16] However, to date, no studies have compared MIS with TCO, particularly in the treatment of mild to moderate HV with metatarsalgia in middle-aged women. This study sought to evaluate 3rd-MIS and TCO in treating HV patients by examining radiological, surgical, and clinical outcomes as well as patient satisfaction. The findings indicate that both 3rd-MIS and TCO significantly improved scores for HVA, IMA, DMAA, VAS, and AOFAS. Additionally, the postoperative FML in the MIS group was 72.11 ± 1.24 mm, which was not significantly different from the preoperative FML of 71.38 ± 1.70 mm (P = .083 > .05). In contrast, TCO showed a slight increase in the postoperative FML compared to the preoperative FML (P = .004). This observation is consistent with our previous findings that TCO can, to some extent, contribute to an increase in the length of the first metatarsal. However, the difference in the postoperative FML between the 2 groups was not statistically significant (P = .068). Moreover, both surgical techniques demonstrated similar effectiveness in treating and preventing metatarsalgia. Our study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of HV correction by comparing the MIS and TCO techniques, with the intention of providing orthopedic surgeons with a novel treatment alternative for HV that is accompanied by plantar callosities and metatarsalgia while reducing the complications linked to traditional surgical methods.

TCO, including TCO, involves a long incision on the medial side of the MTPJ.[17] This procedure involves opening the joint capsule, exposing the metatarsal head, performing an osteotomy, and then tightening and suturing the joint capsule after advancement and fixation. Long-term inactivity of the joint capsule can lead to joint stiffness, making it difficult to restore normal function, causing instability and resulting in abnormal internal friction, which may accelerate joint degeneration. Furthermore, lateral displacement of the osteotomy site can result in the elevation or depression of the metatarsal head, leading to transfer metatarsalgia.[18] Using an oscillating saw for osteotomy frequently causes bone loss and postoperative shortening of the first metatarsal.[19] Our 3rd-MIS split-drill osteotomy does not require periosteum separation or cause joint capsule damage, significantly reducing soft tissue trauma. The procedure ensures rapid healing of the surgical incision and minimizes disruption to the blood supply of the bone at the osteotomy site. Continuous saline irrigation during the procedure reduces thermal damage to the bone, facilitating faster bone healing and preserving blood vessels and nerves. This significantly lowers the risk of postoperative complications. The use of a fine cracked drill bit resulted in minimal loss of the metatarsal bone, with the FML remaining largely unchanged postosteotomy (P = .083). The procedure is straightforward, and because of the minimal incision size, there is almost no scarring postsurgery. Moreover, when comparing surgical indicators, the MIS group significantly outperformed the TCO group in weight-bearing time, osteotomy healing time, and bleeding volume (P < .05). However, due to the frequent fluoroscopy required for minimally invasive procedures, the operation time was longer in this group than in the TCO group. The frequent use of fluoroscopy in the MIS procedure resulted in longer operation times than those in the TCO group (49.27 ± 4.85 minutes vs 29.96 ± 2.52 minutes, respectively) (P < .05). In the MIS approach, 3 small minimally invasive incisions were made: one incision on the lateral side of the first MTPJ to sever the adductor pollicis muscle, another lateral to the joint capsule under the first metatarsal head for bone cutting, and a third inside the MTPJ capsule to excise the protruding bone and parts of the capsule. Owing to minimal surgical trauma and incisions, patients in the MIS group experienced significantly less pain postsurgery, as evidenced by lower VAS scores on the second day postsurgery compared to those in the TCO group (P = .002). The treatment effectiveness for plantar callosities and metatarsalgia was similar between the groups (P = .835), with fewer cases postsurgery than presurgery. Given the minimal trauma, rapid recovery, and precise outcomes associated with the 3rd-MIS technique, this technique has clear advantages over traditional TCO methods.

Recently, minimally invasive surgery for HV has become increasingly popular over traditional open surgery due to its numerous advantages, such as smaller incisions, aesthetic outcomes, minimal trauma, faster recovery, and less postoperative pain. The first-generation MIS technique utilized an intra-articular oblique osteotomy of the metatarsal head, derived from Reverdin’s procedure. This technique corrects deformities through the lateral joint capsule hinge but lacks internal fixation,[4] resulting in complication rates ranging from 5% to 73%.[20] Bösch and his team introduced a second-generation MIS technique that consists of a percutaneous distal transverse osteotomy at the metatarsal neck, secured with Kirschner wires.[21] However, this method may lead to higher rates of pin tract infection and greater instability. The third-generation MIS HV technique, which evolved from the CO, is performed under C-arm fluoroscopy through 3 to 4 small incisions using specialized minimally invasive power tools and a split drill, with a fixation system to stabilize the osteotomy site and correct the HVA, IMA, and DMAA. This generation further optimizes and refines the previous techniques, resulting in more stable therapeutic effects and fewer complications. Ongoing research compares the clinical efficacy of MIS with that of traditional open surgery and among different MIS techniques. A recent 5-year randomized controlled trial comparing minimally invasive CO with open surgery revealed no significant differences in clinical outcomes (AOFAS scores, VAS ratings, and patient satisfaction), radiological results, joint degeneration, or range of motion.[16] These findings closely align with our research, but MIS procedures require multiple fluoroscopies, significantly prolonging operation times compared to those of the TCO group, likely related to the surgeon’s experience and skill, necessitating a steep learning curve and proficiency.

In our earlier studies, we found that TCO is an effective treatment for mild to moderate HV and for preventing transfer metatarsalgia. The present study demonstrated that both MIS and TCO effectively corrected HVA and IMA, with no notable differences in postoperative outcomes between the groups. Nevertheless, the MIS approach results in less trauma. Furthermore, we assessed the DMAA in our patients and found that both MIS and TCO techniques could correct it in HV patients, although there was some variability among observers in measuring the DMAA. Favorable postoperative AOFAS and VAS scores were associated with both methods. Notably, the AOFAS score for the MIS technique increased significantly from a preoperative average of 44.08 ± 6.75 to a postoperative average of 83.15 ± 5.63, while the VAS score dropped from 5.31 ± 1.05 to 1.73 ± 0.45 at the last follow-up. Similar improvements were observed in the TCO group; however, the differences between the 2 groups were not statistically significant (P > .05). The improvements in function and outcomes from our surgical techniques aligned with those from other osteotomies, yielding favorable therapeutic results. Additionally, 3rd-MIS also offers advantages, such as less trauma, shorter hospital stays, less early postoperative pain, and the ability to bear weight early in the recovery process.[22,23] The practical implications of these findings are significant, providing surgeons with a viable, less invasive option that does not compromise effectiveness, thereby potentially improving patient outcomes and satisfaction.

Our study has several limitations. Notably, all surgeries were performed by surgeons from the same department without employing a randomized or double-blind design. Moreover, the present study had a limited sample size of HV patients; future investigations should prioritize the recruitment of a larger cohort for MIS treatment. Despite the advantages of minimal surgical trauma and scarring, MIS has significant limitations due to its steep learning curve and potential for high complication rates among untrained physicians. Additionally, the significant intraoperative radiation exposure, lengthy fluoroscopy times, and potential damage to the metatarsal heads and surrounding soft tissues warrant careful consideration. Moreover, the short follow-up time should be emphasized. While many studies have validated the effectiveness of MIS for HV correction, demonstrating clinical and radiological results similar to open surgery, the variation in complication rates between techniques highlights the need for high-quality research to further verify the benefits and drawbacks of MIS vs open surgical methods. However, our findings suggest that MIS results in less trauma and faster clinical recovery in the treatment of mild to moderate HV with metatarsalgia in middle-aged women than TCO.

5. Conclusions

Based on the premise of maintaining the original anatomical structure of the metatarsal bone, 3rd-MIS for HV yields outcomes comparable to those of TCO. After surgery, the length of the metatarsal bone remains largely unchanged, avoiding shifts in weight-bearing points and ensuring uniform pressure distribution among the bones. Although the 3rd-MIS technique requires slightly more surgical time due to the use of fluoroscopy, it significantly excels in aspects such as weight-bearing time, osteotomy healing, and reduced bleeding. Additionally, it achieves lower VAS scores on the second day after surgery, signaling minimal trauma, smaller scars, quicker recovery, and less postoperative pain. Thus, the benefits of 3rd-MIS split-drill osteotomy over TCO signify a novel approach for the surgical management of mild to moderate HV accompanied by metatarsalgia.
