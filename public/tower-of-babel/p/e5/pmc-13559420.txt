Diagnosing lung cancer invading the pulmonary artery using ultra-low-dose CT pulmonary angiography: a feasibility study
Authors: Jiandong Guo, Xuelin Zhang, Yongyi Li, Xiongfei Chen, Huirong Su, Yue Zhou, Yongjun Zhang, Jianjun Shen, Shasha Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComputed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) is widely used for evaluating pulmonary artery invasion (PAI) in lung cancer, but conventional protocols involve relatively high radiation dose and contrast medium usage. The feasibility of a 60-kVp “triple-low” protocol combining low tube voltage, reduced contrast volume, and reduced injection rate in preserving image quality and diagnostic performance remains to be determined. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of a 60-kVp “triple-low” CTPA protocol for assessing pulmonary artery invasion in patients with lung cancer.MethodsIn this prospective randomized study, 90 patients were assigned to routine dose (RD) CTPA (120 kVp, 50 mL, 4.5 mL/s; n=45) or ultra-low-dose (ULD) CTPA (60 kVp, 35 mL, 3.5 mL/s; n=45). Two senior thoracic radiologists established the reference standard by consensus after reviewing available pathological, imaging, and clinical records. Subjective image quality was evaluated using a 5-point Likert scale across multiple parameters, including enhancement homogeneity and vascular sharpness. Radiation dose was recorded, and the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) were measured at the main, right, and left pulmonary arteries. Two additional readers independently assessed PAI; receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis against the reference standard yielded the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy.ResultsULD reduced effective dose (ED) from 3.59 to 0.72 mSv (~80%). Subjective scores were comparable between protocols for enhancement homogeneity, vascular sharpness, and diagnostic confidence (all P>0.05). SNR and CNR were similar overall, with a small decrease at the left pulmonary artery in the ULD group. Diagnostic performance for PAI was comparable between ULD and RD for both readers (reader 1: AUC 0.936 vs. 0.940; reader 2: 0.936 vs. 0.925; both P>0.05).ConclusionsA 60-kVp triple-low CTPA protocol achieved ~80% dose reduction while maintaining image quality and diagnostic accuracy for evaluating PAI, supporting clinical implementation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A 60-kVp “triple-low” computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) protocol achieved approximately 80% radiation dose reduction while largely preserving image quality compared with routine dose CTPA, with comparable diagnostic accuracy for assessing pulmonary artery invasion (PAI) in lung cancer patients.

What is known and what is new?

• Ultra-low-dose CT has been primarily used for pulmonary embolism diagnosis to reduce radiation exposure, while its application in lung cancer, particularly for assessing PAI, remains limited.

• A 60-kVp “triple-low” CTPA protocol enables substantial radiation dose reduction while preserving image quality and diagnostic performance for assessing PAI in lung cancer patients.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The proposed 60-kVp “triple-low” CTPA protocol should be considered for incorporation into routine clinical protocols for lung cancer evaluation to reduce radiation exposure, particularly in patients requiring repeated imaging, while preserving diagnostic confidence for PAI assessment.

Introduction

Background

Pulmonary artery invasion (PAI) by lung cancer is an important imaging finding that affects tumor staging, surgical resectability, and the choice of surgical technique (1,2). Accurate preoperative assessment of PAI is therefore essential to avoid unnecessary surgery and to guide optimal therapeutic strategies. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) provides high-resolution visualization of the pulmonary arterial tree down to the subsegmental level and enables detailed evaluation of vascular morphology and patency (3-6). Accordingly, CTPA has become a valuable imaging modality for assessing pulmonary artery involvement in lung cancer (7,8). However, CTPA requires exposure to ionizing radiation and iodinated contrast material. In accordance with the as low as reasonably achievable principle, minimizing radiation dose remains imperative (9,10). In addition, contrast-associated kidney injury and adverse reactions to iodinated contrast media are of particular concern in oncology patients, who often undergo repeated imaging examinations (11). Therefore, development of a CTPA protocol with reduced radiation dose, lower contrast volume, and optimized injection parameters, while preserving diagnostic accuracy for assessing PAI, is of substantial clinical value.

Rationale and knowledge gap

Recent studies have investigated various dose- and contrast-optimization strategies for CTPA while maintaining diagnostic image quality. These advances have primarily focused on pulmonary embolism and include approaches such as reduced tube voltage, automatic tube current modulation, reduced contrast volume, and optimized injection protocols with lower injection rates (12,13). Such strategies have demonstrated the feasibility of achieving substantial reductions in radiation exposure and contrast material without compromising diagnostic confidence for pulmonary embolism (14,15). However, previous low-dose CTPA studies have primarily focused on pulmonary embolism detection, where the main diagnostic task is the identification of intraluminal filling defects (13-15). In contrast, evaluation of tumor-related PAI requires assessment of the tumor-vessel interface. Nevertheless, data regarding the evaluation of PAI in lung cancer, especially under low-dose acquisition protocols, remain limited.

Objective

Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate a triple-low 60-kVp CTPA protocol, characterized by ultra-low radiation dose, reduced contrast volume, and low injection rate, in terms of image quality for routine pulmonary artery assessment and diagnostic performance for detecting PAI by lung cancer, using a standard-dose protocol as the reference. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0933/rc).

Methods

Patient population

This prospective study enrolled 104 consecutive patients who underwent CTPA at our institution between November 2024 and July 2025. According to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 90 patients were finally included in the study, comprising 45 men and 45 women aged 30–84 years [mean ± standard deviation (SD), 56±10 years]. Among them, 42 patients had histopathologically confirmed lung cancer, including 9 confirmed by surgical resection, 12 by bronchoscopic biopsy, and 21 by CT-guided percutaneous biopsy. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) indication for CTPA examination at our institution; (II) absence of severe image artifacts; (III) complete clinical data; and (IV) body mass index (BMI) <27.0 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria included (I) inability to cooperate with breath-holding; (II) allergy to iodinated contrast medium; and (III) hepatic, renal, or thyroid dysfunction precluding contrast use. Patients were allocated to the routine dose (RD) or ultra-low-dose (ULD) protocol before CTPA examination using computer-generated stratified randomization according to lung cancer status. Patients with confirmed lung cancer and those without lung cancer were randomized separately in a 1:1 ratio, resulting in 45 patients in each group, with 21 lung cancer patients in each group. The overall study design, patient enrollment, and grouping process are illustrated in Figure 1. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Hospital (Futian) of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine [approval No. GZYL(KY)-2024-103 and protocol code F-GZSY-LL-GZCX-14-2-1], and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

CT acquisition and image post-processing

All CT examinations were performed on a 320-slice CT scanner (uCT 860, United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China). Patients were scanned in the supine position, head first with arms raised, during a single breath-hold from the thoracic inlet to below the costophrenic angles. The RD and ULD groups were scanned at 120 and 60 kVp, respectively, with identical parameters otherwise: automatic tube current modulation (reference, 120 mAs), gantry rotation time 0.35 s, collimation width 80 mm, and pitch 0.89. Bolus tracking was performed at the main pulmonary artery with a 100 HU threshold for automatic triggering.

Non-ionic iodinated contrast medium (iopamidol, 350 mgI/mL; Hengrui Pharma, Lianyungang, China) was injected through the antecubital vein using a power injector. The RD group received 50 mL of contrast at 4.5 mL/s, and the ULD group received 35 mL at 3.5 mL/s, each followed by 25 mL of saline at the same rate. Images were reconstructed using hybrid iterative reconstruction (HIR) with 1.0-mm slice thickness and interval and a 512×512 matrix. For image evaluation and visualization, multiplanar reconstruction (MPR), maximum-intensity projection (MIP), three-dimensional volume rendering (VR), and shaded-surface display (SSD) images were generated.

Image quality evaluation

Image quality was evaluated both objectively and subjectively. For objective analysis, a radiologist with 22 years of diagnostic experience placed regions of interest (ROIs) at the main pulmonary artery and its right and left branches. The CT attenuation of the paravertebral muscle (PM) at the same level was used as the background signal, and image noise was defined as the SD of the PM attenuation. The mean CT value and noise (SD) were measured for each ROI, ensuring consistent size, shape, and position across both groups while avoiding tumor emboli and motion artifacts. Diagnostic analysis of PAI by lung cancer

A hierarchical reference standard was used to determine PAI. When pathological evidence was available, it was considered supportive of PAI if the pathological specimen was obtained from the suspected site of tumor-vessel involvement and demonstrated pulmonary arterial wall invasion or intraluminal tumor thrombus. For cases without direct pathological evidence of PAI, the final diagnosis was established using a consensus imaging-based reference standard. Currently, there are no universally accepted imaging criteria for assessing PAI by lung cancer. In accordance with previous studies (16,17), the imaging-based assessment was performed using axial CTPA images together with MPR and MIP reconstructions. Cases showing a preserved fat plane between the tumor and the pulmonary artery, or smooth tumor-vessel contact involving less than 50% of the vessel circumference without luminal deformity or narrowing, were classified as non-invasive. In contrast, cases showing tumor encasement involving 50% or more of the vessel circumference, marked luminal narrowing or deformation, occlusion, or intraluminal filling defects contiguous with the tumor were classified as invasive.

The reference standard was established by two senior thoracic radiologists with 22 and 24 years of diagnostic experience, respectively. They jointly reviewed all lung cancer cases by integrating available pathological findings, all available imaging findings, clinical records, and follow-up imaging findings when available. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached, and the consensus diagnosis was used as the final reference standard. Two additional radiologists with 8 and 10 years of experience, who were blinded to the reference standard and clinical information, independently assessed the presence of PAI. Interobserver agreement between the two readers was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistics. Diagnostic performance of the two readers was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, including calculation of the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). AUCs between the RD and ULD protocols were compared using the DeLong test for independent ROC curves.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0. The chi-square test was used to compare sex distribution between groups. Age, BMI, radiation dose parameters (CTDIvol, DLP, and ED), contrast medium volume, and SNR were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and were compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables, including CNR and subjective image quality scores, are presented as median [25th percentile (P25), 75th percentile (P75)] and were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. All tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Interobserver agreement between the two readers for PAI assessment was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistics. Kappa values were interpreted as follows: κ ≤0.20, poor agreement; 0.20< κ ≤0.40, fair agreement; 0.40< κ ≤0.60, moderate agreement; 0.60< κ ≤0.80, good agreement; and 0.80< κ ≤1.00, excellent agreement. Diagnostic performance for PAI was evaluated by ROC curve analysis, with calculation of the AUC, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and corresponding 95% CIs. Comparisons of AUCs between the RD and ULD groups were performed using the DeLong test.

Results

Patient characteristics

Among the 90 enrolled patients, 42 had lung cancer, including 23 in the left lung and 19 in the right lung. Histologic subtypes included squamous cell carcinoma (n=12), adenocarcinoma (n=18), adenosquamous carcinoma (n=2), and small-cell carcinoma (n=10). Pathologic confirmation was obtained by surgical resection (n=9), bronchoscopic biopsy (n=12), or percutaneous cutting biopsy (n=21). All patients were randomly assigned before examination to either the RD group (n=45) or the ULD group (n=45). Randomization was stratified by the presence of lung cancer: the 42 patients with lung cancer were evenly allocated to the two groups (n=21 each), and the remaining 48 patients without lung cancer were randomly assigned. Comparison of diagnostic performance between the two protocols

Among the 42 patients with histopathologically confirmed lung cancer, 21 were diagnosed with PAI according to the final reference standard. Among these 21 patients, direct pathological evidence supporting PAI was available in 13 patients. Interobserver agreement between reader 1 and reader 2 for PAI assessment was excellent in both protocols, with Cohen’s kappa values of 0.871 (95% CI: 0.698–1.000) in the RD group and 0.825 (95% CI: 0.634–1.000) in the ULD group. Although the kappa value was slightly lower in the ULD group, both values indicated excellent agreement, suggesting that the diagnostic criteria remained reproducible under the ULD protocol. Quantitative analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in diagnostic performance between the ULD and RD protocols for either reader (all P>0.05). Both readers achieved high diagnostic performance under both protocols. The AUC values ranged from 0.925 to 0.940, with sensitivity ranging from 0.900 to 0.909, specificity from 0.941 to 0.971, and accuracy from 0.933 to 0.956 (Table 5). These results indicate that the ULD protocol maintained diagnostic performance comparable to that of the RD protocol for assessing PAI, despite a substantial reduction in radiation dose.

Discussion

Key findings

In this study, a 60-kVp triple-low CTPA protocol enabled an approximately 80% reduction in radiation dose while preserving clinically acceptable image quality and maintaining comparable diagnostic performance for PAI compared with RD CTPA. Although the ULD protocol was associated with lower overall subjective image quality and reduced CNR in the left pulmonary artery, enhancement homogeneity, vascular sharpness, and diagnostic confidence remained comparable between protocols. These findings suggest that modest reductions in selected image quality parameters may be acceptable, provided that the key imaging features required for assessing tumor-vessel relationships are adequately preserved.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations. An important strength of this study is that it extends the application of low-dose CTPA beyond pulmonary embolism detection to the assessment of tumor-related PAI. Compared with pulmonary embolism, evaluation of PAI by lung cancer requires more detailed assessment of the tumor-vessel interface, including vessel encasement, luminal deformation, occlusion, and intraluminal tumor-related filling defects (18). The preservation of diagnostic performance under the 60-kVp triple-low protocol therefore supports its potential clinical value in a more demanding diagnostic scenario. In addition, despite modest reductions in overall subjective image quality and local CNR, diagnostic confidence and diagnostic performance were maintained, supporting the clinical feasibility of dose reduction in this setting. The inclusion of both subjective and objective image assessments further strengthened the evaluation of the balance between radiation dose and imaging performance. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center prospective feasibility study with a relatively small sample size, and a formal sample size calculation was not performed before patient enrollment. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require validation in larger multicenter studies. Second, patients with BMI ≥27 kg/m2 were excluded, which limits the generalizability of the findings to overweight or obese populations. Because image noise at 60 kVp may increase substantially in patients with higher BMI, the applicability of this triple-low protocol in such patients requires further investigation. Third, subgroup analyses according to tumor histologic subtype, tumor location, and degree of vascular involvement were not performed because of the limited sample size. Fourth, direct pathological confirmation of PAI was not available in all patients; therefore, a consensus imaging-based reference standard was used for cases without direct pathological evidence. Finally, advanced reconstruction techniques, such as deep learning-based reconstruction, were not incorporated and may further improve image quality under ULD conditions. Future studies with larger and more diverse populations are needed to validate these findings and further optimize the protocol.

Comparison with similar research

Our results should be interpreted in the context of previous studies on dose-optimized CTPA. Prior work has shown that low-kVp CTPA can reduce radiation exposure and contrast material use while maintaining acceptable image quality (13). Similar observations have been reported in prior CTPA studies (14,19), in which objective image quality was reduced, whereas subjective diagnostic quality or diagnostic confidence remained acceptable for reliable diagnosis. However, those studies were mainly performed in the setting of pulmonary embolism rather than lung cancer-related PAI assessment.

Explanations of findings

The present study extends these observations by showing that a 60-kVp protocol combined with both reduced contrast volume and a lower injection rate can still support reliable evaluation of PAI. This point is clinically relevant because assessment of PAI is more demanding than routine pulmonary artery visualization alone. Unlike general vascular enhancement tasks, PAI assessment depends on clear delineation of the tumor-vessel interface and associated luminal changes. Therefore, preservation of diagnostic confidence and diagnostic performance is more important than preservation of the best overall visual impression. The observed reduction in CNR in the left pulmonary artery may reflect regional effects and inter-patient variability and may also be influenced by differences in lesion laterality between groups.

Implications and actions needed

From a clinical perspective, accurate preoperative evaluation of PAI is important for surgical planning and risk assessment (16,17,20). In this setting, the value of a dose-optimized CTPA protocol lies not simply in reducing radiation exposure or contrast material use, but in maintaining the diagnostic information required for evaluating tumor-vessel relationships. Our findings suggest that the triple-low protocol was able to preserve this clinically relevant information despite modest reductions in selected image quality parameters. This may be particularly relevant in oncology patients who often undergo repeated imaging examinations or in those for whom contrast load is a concern (21).

Conclusions

In conclusion, a 60-kVp triple-low CTPA protocol substantially reduced radiation dose and contrast material use while preserving diagnostic performance for PAI in lung cancer. Despite a modest reduction in selected image quality parameters, diagnostic confidence remained comparable to that of the RD protocol. These findings support the clinical feasibility of this dose- and contrast-sparing strategy for preoperative evaluation and treatment planning in patients with suspected PAI.
