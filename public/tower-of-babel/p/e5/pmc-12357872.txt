Long-term analysis of land cover changes resulting from mining activities: Strzelin case study (S–W Poland)
Authors: K Romańczukiewicz, J Górniak-Zimroz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study describes the results of research work on the long-term analysis of land cover changes caused by mining activities in the Strzelin quarries between 1884 and 2023. The primary aim of this study was to analyse long-term landscape changes resulting from granite and gneiss extraction, using a land cover classification adapted to mining conditions. To achieve this, a methodological framework was developed that combines historical cartographic sources with geospatial data, enabling a reconstruction of post-mining land transformations. The framework incorporated rarely utilized historical maps such as 19th-century Messtischblatt maps, with spatial datasets including orthophotos and geospatial databases. The methodology is based on georeferencing and manual vectorisation of open-access data, followed by classification and topological validation to ensure spatial consistency across the time series. The results of the study indicate significant changes in the landscape, in particular an almost threefold increase in the area of mining sites between 2004 and 2023, as well as the appearance of spontaneous vegetation succession and formation of water bodies in areas where mines have been liquidated. The study also documents parallel urbanisation trends and infrastructure development, highlighting the complex interaction between industrial activity and socio-environmental processes. This work contributes to research on land use and land cover (LULC) by extending the time frame of the analysis beyond the limitations of satellite era data and filling a gap in the literature on land use changes caused by mining activities. The proposed approach is transferable to small and medium-sized areas and supports evidence-based spatial planning, land reclamation, and heritage preservation in post-industrial environments on the basis of actions taken in Strzelin and Mikoszów.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15881-1.Keywords: Land cover, Open pit mining, Geospatial data, LULCSubject terms: Environmental impact, Geology

Introduction

Land cover mapping together with land cover assessment are fundamental aspects within ecosystem monitoring and management1,2. These actions have a crucial role in analysing the nature of landscapes to enable the development of effective land management strategies and making environmental policy. Changes in land use and land cover (LULC) are a core aspect of environmental monitoring and urban planning3–5, which, over the years, allow to understand the environmental6, ecological7 or social1,8 changes. In order to understand key changes in LULC forms, a long-term perspective is needed to separate trends from annual variability. A historical reconstruction of land-use change through the period of highest human population growth or over the years in which spatial data are available is necessary to correctly understand the occurring change3,9–11.

Rapid development in the economic and social spheres are generating increased demand for mineral resources on a global scale12. The conduct of mining activities affects the surrounding causing changes in the landscape, land cover, environment12–15 and in the quality of life of residents in the immediate vicinity of mining areas12,16–18. It is therefore essential to monitor and study the changes that occur under the influence of mining activities19. The initiation of mining activities is most often associated with the long-term exploitation of a deposit20. The effects of mining activities, in many cases become apparent many years after the end of exploitation13.

Mineral resources are an essential raw material for existing and growing industries, among others: transport and construction, but the environmental degradation that follows their exploitation is its inevitable consequence of change16,20. Open-pit mineral extraction generates two categories of environmental problems: depletion of non-renewable resources and environmental degradation21 through deterioration of groundwater and soils conditions14,22, air pollution21,23, changes in vegetation and animal species diversity24,25, increased noise26 and ground vibrations26,27. Environmental changes can also affect the health of the nearby community28,29. Additionally, mining is associated with changes in LULC30, due to the frequent transformation of land use to other land uses, as well as the settlement of land near urbanised areas conditioned by job search16,31. In order to forecast and document changes occurring in the form of land cover, reports, surveying documentation32 and maps showing the state of areas under mining activities are produced33. As cartographic studies, these maps are a valuable source of information for analysing changes in the land cover of areas under mining activities, while at the same time making it possible to identify ways in which these areas have been transformed over the years33,34.

LULC products are gradually evolving towards a more precise land classification from a field survey approach, to a land cover classification based on geospatial data, with higher spatial and temporal resolution10,35. A current development is the use of technologies such as deep learning or cloud computing36,37. The use of remote sensing and advanced computational tools makes it possible to analyse large amounts of data from satellite imagery on a global, regional or local scale19,37–40. Analysing what changes have occurred over the long term also makes use of historical material that is available for times before remote sensing, especially for areas of small extent36,38,41. However, mapping of LULCs using traditional methods requires greater resources. Studies for analysis involving a long-term perspective often combine traditional methods providing valuable historical data with modern methods36,42.

Articles published to date on the use of digital data and historical materials in the context of studies of LULC changes in mining areas have varied in time scope and types of materials43–50. After their analysis, it was found that LULC changes are detected by various computational methods (np.: supervised classification43,45,47–49, temporal decision tree50, Geographically Weighted Regression44. The input data for the analyses were satellite data allowing observation of land cover changes. These data have been available since the 1970s as part of the Earth Resources Technology Satellite (ERTS-1) and Landsat programme run by NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey51.

It was also found that these studies lacked analysis of historical and topographic data from analogue maps. From these maps, information about the beginning of mining activity and subsequent changes occurring under its influence can be obtained. Therefore, the research methodology proposes adding analogue maps to the long-term analysis of land cover changes in mining areas. The methodology also proposed a land use and land cover class pattern for mining areas (LULC MA). This study proposes a hybrid methodological framework that integrates analogue cartographic sources, topographic maps, orthophotos, and vector databases to detect long-term land cover changes. Unlike many existing LULC studies limited to post-1970 satellite imagery, our approach allows analysis dating back to the 19th century. The aim of this study was to map long-term changes in land cover in an area undergoing dynamic changes caused by mining activities. The analysis covered the period from the 1890s to the 2020s, enabling the identification of changes and assessment of the impact of mining on the surrounding areas. The methodology used, combining archival cartographic materials, orthophotographs and geospatial data, ensured a continuous analysis covering more than 120 years. The method includes georeferencing, digitisation, classification, and topological validation, making it applicable to small- and medium-scale post-mining sites in other regions. In addition to detecting spatial and temporal patterns of land cover change, the proposed approach can provide indirect insights into the technological evolution of mining operations. Although detailed historical records of mining technologies, particularly for the pre-1980s period, are infrequent and not publicly accessible, their integration in future research could substantially enhance the interpretative value of long-term land cover reconstructions. This would also strengthen the technical significance of this methodology, enabling its use in research connecting long-term environmental changes with mining engineering practices.

Materials and methods

LULC analysis is the process of studying and assessing changes in the landscape and its components over a specific period of time. It involves comparing different sources of land cover information, such as satellite imagery3,36,52,53, laser scanning data (Light Detection and Ranging-LiDAR)54, analogue maps11,41, reports and field measurements6 or geographical information systems (GIS) techniques34,55 to identify changes that have occurred. Remote sensing (RS) techniques and geographic information systems show advantages over conventional land monitoring methods and measurements for assessing the long- and short-term dynamics of landscape change40, particularly in mining areas10,56. Land cover changes are very often represented by maps, which can graphically show the overall change that is occurring in a given area9. Local change encompasses a number of changes, resulting from various processes and factors that influence regional and global change57. Land cover is studied in different directions, i.e. towards socio-economic changes41,58, environmental changes59 or dynamic events of local and global scale55. Depending on the used source materials, the method applied for the analysis and the accuracy of the results obtained, the identification of changes is performed on the basis of specific land cover forms40,60.The spatial relations described between land cover class changes are crucial for understanding past patterns and consequences of change, to inform future urban planning strategies3,5,9, risk management61 and environmental monitoring42 in the study area.

Land changes in areas where mining activities are carried out are a special case of land cover changes41,60. The mining company, after the mining activity is completed, has the responsibility to restore the state as resembling the pre-mining condition as possible. In addition, by means of recultivation, it should restore the ecological succession of the ecosystem and make the area attractive both economically and recreationally. The area where exploitation takes place is always undergoing dynamic transformation. Its relief, land cover both in the mining area and in the areas in its immediate vicinity are changing10,34,55,62.

This section presents a generalised methodological workflow for analysing land cover changes over long temporal scales, particularly in post-industrial or mining-impacted environments. The framework is exemplified through the Strzelin case study. Figure 1 shows the long-term land cover change analysis methodology developed to identify land cover and environmental changes occurring in areas affected by mining activities. The study was performed using open source data. The use of open source data makes it possible to apply the methodology to the analysis of land cover changes in any area under mining activity. As mentioned earlier, after completing their activities, mining companies are required to restore the land to a condition as close as possible to its pre-mining state. In many areas, mining activities have been carried out for many years, and it is important for mining companies to obtain information about the original condition of the land. Therefore, the methodology proposes a comprehensive analysis based on analog historical and topographic data as well as digital topographic data. Such a comprehensive analysis will provide knowledge about changes in land cover and allow the entrepreneur to restore the original conditions after the end of operations or to carry out work to bring the area closer to its original state. The presented methodology is universal. It can be applied to analyze changes in land cover of any area degraded by industrial activity. It also allows for landscape reconstruction in a broader time frame.

Selection of research objects

The study area is located in the town of Strzelin and the village of Mikoszów in S-W Poland (Fig. 2). Mining activities in this area started in the 13th century. However, the greatest growth occurred in the second half of the 19th century due to the expansion of quarries, where granite began to be extracted on an industrial scale. The quarries in the area operated until the start of the Second World War, as a result of which they were largely destroyed and the pits were filled with water. After the war, extraction and mining operations resumed in 1946. Until the 1990s, the quarries were in a state-owned company. It was not until the 1990s that the site was transferred into private possession and granite is still being extracted from the deposit today as part of private operations. Currently, the Strzelin quarry is owned by Mineral Polska Sp. z o.o., which is part of the international concern Strabag Sp. z o.o63,64.

Location of the study area—data from65,66.

Exploitation of raw materials from the “Strzelin’’ deposit is currently carried out in the quarries of Strzelin I and Strzelin II (Fig. 3a, b) using the longwall system. From the west, the Strzelin I and II quarry borders the “Mikoszów’’ quarry (Fig. 3c, d) where gneiss and granite are periodically extracted using the longwall system. Furthermore, to the south-west of the Strzelin quarry, there is the granite quarry “Mikoszów Wieś”, where periodic excavation activities are carried out. The study area was selected on the basis of the boundaries of the mining area of the analysed mines (Central Geological Database from The Polish Geological Institute - National Research Institute (PGI-NRI). Due to the way in which the mine is operated and the type of material extracted, the greatest inconveniences and changes occur immediately surrounding the mine and the area of influence of mining activities. An additional factor that is a significant inconvenience in terms of safety and noise is the used explosives. For this purpose, the maximum extent of the rockfall scatter hazard zone caused by the use of explosive blasting agents during mining, of 500 meters, was also taken into account in the study area, as determined by the Ordinance Minister of Energy of 9 November 2016 on specific requirements for the storage and use of blasting agents and blasting equipment in a mining operation67.

Selection and evaluation of the quality of source data

To obtain data, the study used methods of acquiring spatial data in the form of raster data (analogue historical maps, analogue topographic maps, orthophotos, mining maps) and vector data (topographic databases). Due to the lack of a suitable study containing the necessary descriptive and graphical information for the analysis of land cover changes caused by mining activities within the town of Strzelin and Mikoszów, open-data sources belonging to state resources and mining facility resources were used. The visualisation of land cover on analogue maps from different time frames differs from the representation of the corresponding areas in spatial databases. Due to common characteristics it was possible to identify and analyse land cover changes occurring in the selected study area. Common features include the study area, the presentation of objects and phenomena by symbols or the colour scale used. Despite technological developments, changes in mapping tools and techniques, and the introduction of spatial databases, each data source allows the state of the study area to be represented graphically over a selected time range.

Among the data collected, most was historical material in the form of analogue topographic maps from the Messtischblatt series. The map collection of the Messtischblatt was published from the end of the 19th century until 1944 and covered the entire area belonging to the Third Reich. Due to the location of Strzelin, which belonged to German territory until the end of the Second World War, historical materials were sourced from German institutions. At that time, the name of the town of Strzelin was fully German and was called Strehlen. The same applies to Mikoszów, which was called Niklasdorf. Maps from the Messtischblatt series were produced at a scale of 1:25,000. The maps were produced on the basis of field photographs, with the specific position of each corner not including a cartographic grid. Maps made in the late 19th century provided the source for maps made in later years. Work on the Messtischblatt resumed in 1919, but no field measurements were taken, only updated data acquired before the war68–70. Materials with a much smaller scale from 1856, 1873, 1893, 1940, 1943, and 1944 are Maps of the German Reich - Karte des Deutschen Reiches at a scale of 1:100,000, maps of the territory of the Austrian monarchy, and aerial maps at a scale of 1:300,000-500,000. They were supplementary to other cartographic material presenting the topography of the area. The scale of the maps used required the generalisation of the map content, which had a negative impact on the amount of provided information. In later years, after the end of the Second World War, Strzelin was incorporated into Polish territory, which had an impact on the cartographic materials collected. The first maps created in Polish were topographic maps at a scale of 1:25,000 and 1:10,000 from 1983 to 1997. On these maps, the degree of generalization of the content is significantly less relative to material at scales above 1:50,000. The topographic maps were based on field measurements, photogrammetric methods or combined methods. The most reliable data in study is orthophoto, which shows the actual image of the land surface. The source material for orthophotos is data from photogrammetric measurements and satellite images. The study area includes images from 2004, 2010, 2013, 2016, 2019, 2020, 2021, 2022 and 2023. The orthophotos used in the study were obtained from national spatial data repositories, mainly from the Head Office of Geodesy and Cartography65. The spatial resolution of these images ranged from 0.10 m to 0.50 m per pixel, depending on the year of acquisition. Although the official metadata does not specify the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) value for horizontal positioning accuracy, the orthophotos are produced in accordance with the technical standards set out in the Polish law, Regulation of the Minister of Development and Technology of 16 December 2022 on databases of aerial and satellite imagery, orthophotos and numerical terrain models71.

Digital spatial databases are another highly relevant source of information. Two databases were selected for the study: The Database of Topographic Objects with a detail of 1:10000 (Polish abbr. BDOT10k) and CORINE Land Cover (CLC). BDOT10k is a vector digital database. The organisation, creation, updating, maintenance and sharing is governed by the Regulation of the Minister of Development, Labour and Technology of 27 July 2021 on the database of topographic objects and the database of general geographic objects, as well as standard cartographic studies72. The database locates all topographic objects with descriptions in an attribute table that contains object features and cartographic codes for each scale of topographic maps. The purpose of creating the database was the need for digital methods of acquiring and managing spatial data. The database is provided in the form of XML files that can be imported into GIS-type software such as QGIS. BDOT10k covers a thematic range that includes 9 basic groups of thematic layers for level 1 data detail. A special group is the group of thematic layers on land cover, in the form of polygons, covering surface water, built-up areas, forest or wooded areas, shrub vegetation, permanent crops, grassland and agricultural crops, communication areas, wasteland, squares, landfills, pits and dumps and other undeveloped land65. A significantly more generalized database is the CLC database. It contains land cover classes at 3 levels of detail. The CLC programme belongs to the pan-European component of the Copernicus land monitoring service, which is also coordinated by the European Environment Agency. In addition to land cover form layers, it is possible to obtain change layers that visualise changes in land cover minimum mapping unit of 5 hectares. Data from the CLC programme covers land use across Europe. Due to the high level of generalisation, the data are not authoritative in small area studies73. In the territory of Poland, the Institute of Geodesy and Cartography and the Chief Inspectorate for Environmental Protection were the main contractors for the various stages of the project.

All acquired datasets, including their names, formats, coordinate systems, source institutions, acquisition methods and estimated positional accuracy, are described in detail in Appendix A. This appendix contains a summary listing all sources, along with their scale or resolution and measurement techniques. The acquired data were assessed for quality in accordance with the ISO 19,157 standard Geographic information…74, with the aim of selecting datasets with the greatest utility for further research.The data were analysed in terms of five main elements, namely completeness, logical consistency, location accuracy, temporal accuracy and thematic accuracy. In addition, file structure, record correctness and naming conventions were verified for each dataset. The results of this suitability assessment, together with the comparative data table, are presented in Appendix A. The adopted time frame for the analysis directly reflects both the availability and thematic relevance of the datasets.

The comparison highlights differences in data characteristics: for instance, the 19th-century Messtischblatt maps at 1:25,000 scale, based on field surveys and manual cartographic drafting, have a positional accuracy of approximately 25–30 m, whereas modern orthophotos acquired through digital aerial photogrammetry offer 0.25–0.5 m resolution. Similarly, mining maps at 1:1,000 scale, prepared using total station and classical measurements techniques, achieve sub-meter precision, making them the most accurate source in the dataset. Nevertheless, due to the lack of systematic measurements, the source is not very beneficial in this type of analysis.

Despite the limitations in accuracy, historical maps remain the only available source of spatial information for the study area in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, making them indispensable for reconstructing long-term landscape changes. These variations in scale, acquisition technique, and precision were explicitly considered during georeferencing, vectorisation, and topological validation, ensuring that all datasets could be reliably integrated into a common spatial framework.

Scanning

The process of converting archival cartographic materials and maps in analogue form into digital form, into a raster file, is called map scanning. The next step is calibration, which makes it possible to assign a specific spatial reference system to the scanned maps. Calibration enables, apart from defining the geodetic system, to remove distortions and errors occurring on the raster, caused by scanning and deformations of the paper map75–77. In the study, analogue historical maps and analogue topographic maps were scanned by the source data providers (Appendix A). The maps were saved as rasters, which were georeferenced in the ‘1992’ coordinate system.

Georeferencing and calibration

Map georeferencing process allows a spatial reference to files in raster form, i.e. to perform a transformation of the local layout of an analogue map to a geodetic coordinate system. The most common approach involves fitting known points with defined coordinates to a spatial grid78. Contemporary cartographic map editing involves referencing mapping parameters tied to a global geodetic datum. Historic maps, on the other hand, usually do not have off-grid information or a legend. For this reason, a calibration method based on fixed points on maps or by fitting from a reference layer is permitted for maps that have been mapped in archival layouts or in layouts that are difficult to identify79–84. Permanent points include parcel boundary points, horizontal and altitude reference levels and other permanent characteristic points. In many cases where the map does not contain permanent elements, it is possible to calibrate the maps with the communication network (in particular railway and road infrastructure) and with fixed points on the maps such as buildings, in particular churches, administrative buildings and railway buildings.These objects provide a reliable spatial reference for map alignment, as they are physically durable, easily identifiable across various map editions, and typically remain unaffected by local land use transformations. The number of control points ranged from 10 to 25 per map, depending on the scale, extent, and legibility of each sheet. The spatial distribution of control points was optimised to reduce local distortions, with special attention paid to quarry zones and built-up areas.

An additional factor to consider when working with analogue maps is map deformation. The most important measure of map fit accuracy in the georeferencing process is the RMSE85. Its value should be consistent with the error value for the scale and type of map made. According to76, the calibration score is the position of the control points, the actual points and the type of transformation. It is calculated by summing the squares of the individual errors (a), dividing this sum by the number of values considered (n) and taking the square root of the resulting quotient86,87:

In historical mapping studies, with calibration not based on the grid, but only on points located in communication areas and buildings, the RMSE error can take on very large values despite a well-executed calibration. While higher RMSE values were observed for some early 20th-century materials, they remain consistent with values reported in other historical geospatial studies88. An affine transformation was used to perform the analysis. In the case of the historical maps used in the research work, the method of fitting the raster to characteristic control points located on road and rail infrastructure and on buildings from 2021 from BDOT10k (Fig. 6). In the Georeferencing module of ESRI ArcGIS Pro software, control points were created for each of the analysed maps and the maps were georeferenced. When performing the raster fit of the 1919 map (Geologische Karte - based on the map Messtischblattz 3078 Strehlen 1912), difficulties were encountered in dividing the map into 20 parts and attaching them to the material base. In this case, it was necessary to precisely cut the map sections and calibrate each section separately. The difficulty with this kind of map development is matching each fragment to a previously matched part. The number of control points ranged from 10 to 25 per map, depending on the scale, extent, and legibility of each sheet. The spatial distribution of control points was optimised to reduce local distortions, with special attention paid to quarry zones and built-up areas.

Vectorization

Vectorisation is the complex process of converting information from data in analogue form, such as paper maps, by calibrating and transforming the information into vector form. This process involves identifying and graphically representing map objects using point, line and polygon geometry in the form of thematic layers57,90. Depending on the scale, the method of representation and the resolution of the map scan, the content of the maps differs. Symbols on historical maps often have more ornate and artistic forms, embellishments, calligraphic fonts, and hand-drawn details. Also, the condition and storage of the maps has a key impact on the correctness of reading the symbols on the map. In the study, the content of the maps was subjected to a vectorisation process considering the division of the data into the adopted land cover classes. Difficulties were encountered in the study when reading symbols and signs due to different scales or the degree of generalisation91. Appendix C shows examples of differences in land cover forms identified in historic cartographic material. For vectorisation, the study used the hand-digitisation method from the screen, which involves manually plotting the beginnings, ends, and inflection points of objects from a computer screen using a computer mouse. The vectorised object is created by manually redrawing the content of the map, thus preserving the topology between the individual map elements64,76,92. The vectorisation of the maps was done in ESRI’s ArcGIS Pro software.

In the research, historical analogue maps, topographic maps and orthophotos were analysed and vectorised as part of the identification of land cover changes. Orthophotos are characterised by high quality and resolution of elements. This allows accurate identification of land cover forms, subject only to error due to the human factor. In the research work, due to the small area of the study, the orthophotos were digitised manually on a computer screen using ESRI ArcGIS Pro software, instead of classification based on training fields, which are recommended for areas with a much larger surface area93. The positional accuracy of vectorised features derived from historical analogue maps is inherently limited by the original map scale, generalisation methods and printing or scanning distortions. Therefore, while it was not possible to determine exact RMS error values for these maps, relative spatial accuracy was controlled through manual adjustment and alignment with modern orthophoto images. Thematic accuracy was maintained by harmonising land cover classification across datasets. As a result, the final outputs are suitable for analysing long-term trends in land cover at the landscape scale, while acknowledging the limitations of early cartographic sources.

Digital data processing

For the digital data from BDOT10k, it was necessary to classify and group layers. For this purpose, the Land Cover category was used, which was processed according to level 3 of detail of land cover information72. Due to the low level of detail of the Land Cover class “Land under wheeled roads, railways and airports”, which in the study area only includes land under the carriageway of a main road, the carriageway of a local road, access road or other hard surface road, and land under a railway track, the SKJZ vector-class “Roadway” was additionally used. Each of the database objects was assigned to the respective land cover forms. Conversely, the newly created class “Communication areas” includes, in addition to the class Land under wheeled roads, railways and airports, a new layer based on the SKJZ class. The roadways in the SKJZ layer are represented as lines with information about each object in the attribute table. Buffers with a radius equal to half the width value contained in the layer’s attribute table were made based on the width of the roadway. With the method used, it was possible to represent the actual course and surface of the road infrastructure. Subsequently, the Terrain class under vehicular, rail and airport roads was merged with the road layer with polygon geometry. This made it possible to unify the data in terms of communication areas with information from analogue maps and orthophotos with the BDOT10k database. Layer topology analysis

After processing the data according to the procedures shown in Fig. 8, a layer topology analysis was performed for all data in the last step for the developed land cover classes. A layer topology analysis is necessary to verify the results. The analysis was performed in ArcGIS Pro software using the Overlay Layers tool. When vectorising maps, especially historical maps, there are topology errors mainly due to image processing methods and the quality of the operator. In order to increase the reliability and accuracy of GIS analyses, studies with incorrect topology require correction of detected errors to preserve geometric relationships between objects. The topology defines the correctness and integrity of the data and also describes the neighbourhood, overlap and containment of objects. Duplication of points, overlapping lines and loops - intersection of lines (or contours) with each other are typical geometry faults94. Most topological errors identified in this study were the result of manual digitisation, including slight gaps between polygon vertices, small overlaps or duplicate nodes. These were systematically detected through ArcGIS Pro topology rules and corrected using snapping, merging, and gap-filling procedures. In the study area, the area covered a sufficiently small area that potential topology errors could have affected the results of the land cover class areas. Additionally, the influence of input data resolution was observed, when higher-resolution orthophotos allowed precise vectorisation of small features (e.g. roads under 2 m wide, drainage ditches), the historical maps with lower resolution (e.g. scanned 1:25,000 prints) often introduced misalignments due to symbol generalisation. Applying the same validation and correction workflow to all temporal datasets ensured that any detected spatial inconsistencies were eliminated before further analysis. By performing a topology analysis of the layers, the errors are less than 0.02% of the total area of the study area. The topological validation covered all temporal datasets and all land cover classes generated from different sources. The relatively small size of the study area allowed for detailed inspection and correction of spatial and topological inconsistencies during vectorisation. Selected differences and interpretation challenges related to historical map sources are illustrated in Appendix C.

Results

The study analysed changes in land cover resulting from mining activities in the Strzelin and Mikoszów deposits in the years 1884–2023. The study was based on historical cartographic materials, topographic maps, orthophotos and digital databases. Based on the analysis of land cover forms, 8 land cover classes were selected for further study: anthropogenic land, agricultural land, communication land, forest land, green land, hydrographic land, mining land and other land not classified in the other classes. Twelve thematic maps depicting land cover for the years 1884, 1893, 1919, 1940, 1983, 1997, 2004, 2010, 2015, 2020, 2022 and 2023 were produced.

The classified, vectorised and structured data made it possible to compare with each other information from different periods for the selected study area. The method of data analysis presented allowed the identification of a general trend in the dynamics of land cover change and development between 1884 and 2023. A detailed analysis of land cover is included in Appendix D. A summary table shows the area of land cover in the classes selected for analysis, and graphs illustrate the changes taking place. The calculations were made using ESRI’s ArcGIS Pro software. It was calculated that the total area of the study area is 473,616 ha. The summed areas of all land cover classes for individual years do not equal the area of the study area. This may be due to topological errors occurring. During the vectorisation and processing of the digital data, a topology check of the area was performed, which showed that there were no topological errors. The deviations shown from the area of the study area do not exceed 0.02% and were therefore considered insignificant and not affecting the final results.

The operation of a mining company in the immediate vicinity of an urban center causes many changes to the landscape. In the study, the greatest changes were identified in the mining areas, which have significantly increased in area. The main reason for this was the developing continuous exploitation of granite and gneiss in Strzelin and intermittent exploitation in Mikoszów. From 1884 to 1940, the size of the area of the mining areas was almost the same, while the greatest dynamism occurred from 2004 to 2023. On the analogue maps, the pits are divided into many smaller parts, representing mine excavations and dumps. After 2004, there was an increase in the area of the mining areas. The most intensive changes in land cover caused by mining activities have occurred in the central part of the area, between Strzelin and Mikoszów, where Strzelin I and Strzelin II quarries are located. The pits were enlarged, and inputs and extracted raw material began to be stored directly next to the pits. This is in accordance with the acquisition of the ‘Strzelin’ quarries by Kruszywa Strzelin Sp. z o.o. after 2004. Consecutively, during the following years, also after the takeover of the “Strzelin” quarry by Mineral Polska Sp. z o.o., the extraction relatively increased, until the highest production was reached in 2022. Within the Strzelin quarry and its surroundings there are elements of the old industrial infrastructure which, despite the time, still influence the land cover and landscape. Characteristic objects include a linotor crane and a Derrik-type crane, devices dating from earlier stages of exploitation, located in the area of the Strzelin I quarry. Equally important spatial elements are the historical external dumps and aggregate heaps in the area of Strzelin II, which have shaped the morphology of the area for decades. These objects are permanent anthropogenic forms present in the landscape, not only as traces of mining activity, but also as potential resources of cultural and industrial heritage. This caused the area occupied by the mining areas to double. In the following years, the quarry areas underwent a significant transformation and increased in size by more than 2 times (2015, 2020) and almost 3 times (2022).

Alongside the expansion of the quarrying areas, there is an increase in the area of built-up areas, including those related to technical and administrative infrastructure. The increase in development in the direct surroundings of the quarries mainly concerns industrial facilities such as crushers, conveyor belts, workshops and office facilities. At the same time, new residential and service facilities have appeared outside the immediate mining zone, especially in the northern and eastern parts of the study area, indicating a parallel process of suburbanisation which is not directly related to mining activities.

Analysis of granite and gneiss extraction data at the Strzelin I and II quarry indicates a clear correlation between the increase in extraction intensity and changes in land cover, visible in cartographic analyses and orthophotos. Particularly dynamic development of exploitation took place after 2007, which was reflected in the expansion of technological infrastructure and the increase in the area of mining areas, mainly at the expense of agricultural land and greenery. At the same time, there were changes in the organisational structure of the plant, including changes in the land authority which may have influenced the intensification of mining activities and the adoption of new land use directions. Since 1992, an annual review of the site’s activities has been kept, allowing both the dynamics of mining and its spatial-environmental consequences to be tracked. Figure 9 shows a graph of granite and gneiss extraction from 1992 to 2024 at the Strzelin quarry. Particularly important is the year 2010, which, despite the threefold increase in the mining area, showed a significant decrease in the volume of granite extraction (down to 149 thousand tonnes). It was not until the following year, i.e. 2011, that extraction increased rapidly, reaching a then-record 875.36 thousand tonnes. Such a significant discrepancy between the expansion of the mining range and the production of raw material may indicate a transitional period in the operation of the plant, involving, among other things, the preparation of new mining infrastructure, technological investments and management reorganisation.

Granite and gneiss extraction in the years 1992–2024 in the Strzelin quarry. Data from96.

An analysis of archival sources indicates that significant technological changes took place in the exploitation of the deposit during the period under study. From manual operation using wedges and drilling (late 19th, till early 20th century), through the adoption of gantry cranes and cableways (1920–1930 s), to full mechanisation using pneumatic drills and explosives (post-war period). Since the 1990s, diamond wire cutting and improved blasting techniques have been used, which have increased the precision of extraction and changed the spatial pattern of the excavation. These changes, combined with periodic changes in mine management, were reflected in the analysis of changes in land cover, particularly in the expansion of the active mining area, successive changes in green areas along with changes in the direction of mining activity, and the reorganisation of spoil heaps visible on classification maps for subsequent periods.

It is also important to note the transport system that has been formed in the immediate vicinity of the quarries. Access, transportation and service roads, formed for the purposes of quarrying and transporting the excavated material and have significantly transformed the land cover, which was previously occupied by agricultural and grassland. Some of these routes, although originally industrial, still function today as local paved roads or residential paths. As the workings became deeper and the slopes increased, this structure was gradually reduced, auxiliary roads were eliminated and the communication system simplified. As a result, a network of main technological roads was consolidated, adapted to the changing morphological conditions of the terrain and the logistical requirements of the mining operation.

The analysis of land cover changes within the Strzelin and Mikoszów quarries allows the identification of both the real processes of vegetation succession and the planned directions of reclamation. In the south-western part of the study area, covering the former workings in the Mikoszów Wieś area, systematic transformation of post-mining areas into areas of green unplanted areas and forest succession is observed. Reducing the intensity of excavation at these locations has enabled spontaneous regeneration of the vegetation cover, visible on the orthophotos from 2010 to 2023 in the form of the development of shrubby plants and young trees. These processes indicate a natural sub-succession, partly supported by reclamation activities. At the same time, in accordance with the planning documentation and the directions for the reclamation of post-mining areas, target forms of reclamation were planned for selected areas of Strzelin and Mikoszów. For the Strzelin I and II quarries, forestry reclamation and mixed reclamation, combining natural and utilitarian functions, were planned. A different character was forecast for the post-mining areas in Mikoszów, where the direction of technical reclamation was indicated for the function of a disposal site96 In documented cases, areas currently undergoing vegetation succession were formerly utilized for mining purposes but were subsequently abandoned as a result of the cessation of extraction activities. A representative example includes quarries Mikoszów and Mikoszów Wieś where exploitation has occasionally continued. Despite the absence of formal reclamation measures, these areas exhibit distinct indicators of spontaneous natural succession, including progressive re-vegetation. This process reflects the capacity of post-industrial landscapes to undergo autonomous ecological regeneration under favorable conditions.

Changes have also taken place in the areas directly bordering mining sites (Appendix E). Until the ‘80s, it was predominantly agricultural land that bordered areas owned by mining companies. From 1983 onwards it was greenfields, woodland and, to a much lesser extent, agricultural land. The greatest decline in agricultural land occurred between 1940 and 1983. Also evident are the directions of land reclamation where mining activities are no longer taking place, namely the green areas and the woodlands in the central part of the study area.

Since 1983, there has been a significant change in the surface area of water bodies, particularly in the Mikoszów quarry. These transformations are directly related to mining activities. The presence of water in these areas is primarily a result of discontinued dewatering operations, which allowed the pits to begin accumulating water naturally. Quarry basins, often located below the groundwater level, are gradually filled through a combination of groundwater inflow, precipitation, and surface runoff. As a result, water areas appear and expand particularly in inactive or less intensively operated parts of the quarry. This phenomenon illustrates the hydrological and landscape evolution of post-mining environments. In the case of the Mikoszów quarry, no water reservoirs were observed until the early 1980s. A water reservoir began to form between 1983 and 1997, a development that corresponds to a period of reduced extraction and a lack of technical water disposal. From 2004 onwards, the water surface area gradually decreased, partly due to overgrowth of the shallow zones with vegetation and partly due to the resumption of mining activities in this part of the pit. By 2023, part of the former reservoir area had been incorporated into the extraction zones, while other parts had turned into green areas, indicating a transition towards ecological succession. A similar process occurred in the Strzelin I quarry, where a water reservoir formed after dewatering operations were discontinued. The Strzelin I reservoir is currently fed by direct precipitation, surface runoff, and groundwater inflow from the immediate catchment area, as well as by occasional unmeasured discharges from a sump in the Strzelin II quarry. Due to limited access, the water level is not systematically monitored, and its fluctuations depend on rainfall, surface inflow, and evaporation.

Discussion

Landscape reconstruction in the context of mining-related changes

The studies carried out confirmed that mining activities have a significant impact on land cover change, which is also associated with both environmental and socio-economic transformations. However, mining activities cannot be discontinued as mineral resources are one of the basic materials used in many industries. What can be done is to monitor the changes taking place in order to control them.

In addition to mining-related changes, the development of Strzelin as a local transport and service hub has contributed to the spatial transformation of the area. The town’s location along national and regional transport routes has encouraged new residential and service investments, particularly in the northern and eastern parts of the study area. These changes, although not directly linked to quarry operations, have had a noticeable impact on land cover dynamics. However, some of the newly developed residential areas are located in close proximity to active mining sites, which raises concerns related to land use conflicts and environmental risks such as excessive noise, dust, and exposure to blasting activities. The long-term reconstruction of land cover change presented in this study provides a valuable historical baseline for understanding the cumulative effects of mining and urban development on landscape transformation. By identifying when and where significant changes occurred, it becomes possible to distinguish between short-term land use fluctuations and sustained spatial trends.

Methodological approach and accuracy of results

Currently, studies to monitor LULC variability in mining areas use methods based on the application of satellite data43–50,97,98. These methods are cost-effective and provide the possibility to obtain reliable results in a relatively short time. Compared to other land cover change studies, this study offers integration of historical and contemporary geospatial data for long-term analysis of mining-related transformations. Many existing LULC studies rely solely on satellite imagery, such as Landsat or CORINE datasets, which provide spatial resolutions between 10 and 30 m and a minimum mapping unit of 5 hectares. The data have adequate spatial, radiometric, temporal resolution and usually include analysis for large areas. They are particularly useful for regional-scale monitoring and have been widely applied in the assessment of vegetation dynamics, land degradation and urban expansion. Spectral indices such as NDVI, NDBI, and SAVI are frequently used to quantify vegetation cover, impervious surfaces or soil conditions. While many contemporary studies using machine learning or supervised classification techniques achieve overall accuracy rates between 75% and 95%43,44,47, these methods are typically based on training data derived from modern periods and are limited in their applicability. However, such datasets typically provide reliable information only from the 1970s onward and are limited in their ability to reconstruct earlier land use transitions. These methods are suitable for large-scale regional analyses but are often insufficient for capturing subtle land cover changes in smaller post-mining areas. In contrast, the present study integrates analogue historical maps (1:25,000 to 1:10,000) with recent orthophotos of very high spatial resolution (0.1–0.5 m), enabling the reconstruction of land cover trajectories extending back to 1884. Other researchers in similar studies did not consider such a broad time range43–50. They have analysed only digital data enabling terrain mapping since the 1970s. In the proposed methodology, the inclusion of analogue historical data in the analysis of changes in mining area coverage is an innovative approach despite high RMSE values. This broader temporal scope allows for the identification of land use changes associated with the initial development, expansion and transformation of mining areas.

One of the innovative aspects of the study is the integration of long-term historical cartographic data, covering the period from 1884 to 2023. While many contemporary studies analysing land cover changes in mining regions focus on recent decades, often constrained by the availability of satellite imagery and digital databases, this research utilises a broad temporal range by incorporating analogue topographic maps, historic German military surveys, and post-war Polish cartography. This approach is of particular value in the context of the study of small and medium-scale mining operations, such as those located in Strzelin and Mikoszów, where detailed remote sensing data is not always available for earlier periods. The inclusion of such a broad historical timeline facilitates an accurate assessment of land use change, particularly in the context of social and industrial change.

The use of digital data in research, supplemented by the analysis of analogue historical materials, makes it possible to carry out identification of changes that have occurred in areas with much lower levels of raw material extraction, e.g. rock materials exploited in small area mines. The analysis of historical data also provides an extension of the period of the analysis performed, as well as a wider scale of identification of environmental changes over the years. The spatial and temporal irregularity of the materials and the different quality of the data, understood through resolution and legibility, scale inconsistencies, as well as classification discrepancies of the legends, pose the greatest difficulties in carrying out studies based on historical and digital data. Different levels of detail in the data also represented a significant difficulty in the context of the analysis, requiring the involvement of additional efforts in order to understand and interpret the collected information. In spite of these difficulties in identifying objects on some of the maps, the study produced land cover maps and summaries that allowed the identification of changes that had been seen in the Strzelin and Mikoszów areas. Although the results clearly demonstrate the spatial dynamics of land cover change, their interpretation must take into account the limitations of the applied methodology. The integration of analogue and digital datasets gives rise to challenges related to spatial resolution, georeferencing accuracy, symbol interpretation, and classification consistency over time. In particular, historical maps (1884–1940) exhibit lower geometric precision and higher generalisation (primarily because of the used scale), which may impact the reliability of changes mapped at fine spatial scales. The positioning accuracy of historical maps, despite georeferencing, can vary, affecting the delineation of smaller land cover features. The RMSE values of map georeferencing ranged from 0.4 to 20.2 m depending on the age, quality, and scale of the source map (Table 1). The error values obtained for historical cartographic maps are comparable to other studies based on the georeferencing of old maps. In the case of archival cartographic sources, the primary factors leading to inaccuracy were attributed to the physical condition of the original materials. These included paper deformation over time, shrinkage or stretching of sheets and distortions introduced during scanning. Such problems have also been documented in previous studies on historical map calibration, where physical degradation of materials and scanning-related distortions were found to influence the positional accuracy of georeferenced outputs101,102.

In this study, the georeferencing of historical cartographic materials was performed using a careful selection of evenly distributed control points, prioritising stable and persistent features visible both in the historical maps and in the field or in the vector database. In situations where localised distortions remained after the initial transformation, additional control points were placed in the corresponding areas to enhance the spatial alignment. This approach is consistent with previous research in mining areas, where the identification of durable reference features and the iterative refinement of control points distribution proved essential for ensuring the geometric consistency of multi-temporal spatial datasets4,77,103.

These uncertainties must be considered when interpreting changes in narrow or fragmented land classes, such as green spaces or traffic areas. Topological correctness of digitised vector layers was ensured by applying standard topology rules (e.g. no overlaps, no gaps) within QGIS and ArcGIS environments. Errors such as sliver polygons and dangles were identified and corrected manually. In cases of high geometric complexity, particularly for narrow land use features like small roads or streams, additional snapping and simplification routines were used to ensure data consistency across layers.

The main potential errors in the study stem from: (1) spatial distortions during georeferencing of historical maps; (2) differences in classification schemes between maps; (3) generalisation of features in analogue data and (4) manual vectorisation errors. These factors may lead to over- or underestimation of specific land cover types. However, the topology verification performed and the minimal discrepancy in area calculations (< 0.02%) suggest that such errors had limited impact on overall analysis. It is important to proceed with caution when interpreting changes in small-scale land cover classes, particularly those derived from pre-satellite cartographic sources, due to inherent limitations.

Practical applicability for Spatial planning

The analysis is based on high-resolution datasets, which significantly enhance the reliability of land cover classification and change detection. However, due to its retrospective character, the study does not incorporate field-based validation. In the context of long term historical assessments, ground measurements are inherently limited to current conditions and therefore cannot provide direct validation of past land cover conditions. Nevertheless, if the objective is to monitor ongoing or future land cover dynamics in mining areas, field surveys should be planned as part of a consistent and repeated observation framework. Such campaigns, aligned with remote sensing acquisitions, would provide valuable reference data for improving classification accuracy and supporting predictive modelling or machine learning-based approaches.

Moreover, the detailed spatio-temporal insights obtained from this approach can support evidence-based spatial planning by identifying zones of historical land use intensity, areas of potential environmental conflict and regions suitable for restoration or redevelopment. This is particularly relevant in post-mining landscapes, where balancing economic reuse with ecological recovery poses significant challenges. For example, in the Strzelin mining area, the application of identified land cover classes to current local spatial development plans (adopted in 2004, 2006, 2021 and 2024) allows us to detect differences between planned and actual land use. In some cases, reclaimed mining areas designated in planning documents for industrial or service purposes remain unused or have undergone spontaneous regeneration, transforming into grasslands and scrubland. On the other hand, parts of areas classified in plans as agricultural land have been converted into residential plots, often near active mining sites, potentially increasing exposure to noise, dust and vibrations caused by mining operations. These comparisons can provide urban planners with insights into how planning assumptions differ from actual conditions on the ground, enabling more targeted updates to land use policies and development strategies. The methodology demonstrated in this study is not limited to the Strzelin region. It can be adapted to other small- and medium-scale mining sites with historical cartographic coverage. The main prerequisite is the availability of analogue map series and orthophotos. The workflow, including georeferencing, classification harmonisation, and topology correction, ensures applicability in varied geographic and historical contexts.

Conclusions

Land cover is determined by many anthropogenic, environmental or geological factors. Currently, human activity is the main aspect that determines changes in land cover and land use. One of these factors is ongoing mining activities. Cartographic studies make it possible to graphically illustrate both the social and environmental changes that occurred during industrial-scale mining activities in Strzelin from the 19th to the 21th century. Mining activities directly affected environmental factors and the living and working comfort of the inhabitants. The identification of changes that occur in a given area can be complemented by expert opinions and environmental measurements, together with maps made on the basis of the measurement results.

The results of the study demonstrate long-term changes in land cover in the Strzelin and Mikoszów quarries and surrounding areas, driven primarily by quarrying activities, but also influenced by other processes such as urban expansion, natural succession, and hydrological transformations. A key strength of the research lies in the hybrid methodological approach combining more than 30 historical and digital data sources, including analogue cartographic materials, orthophotos, and digital vector databases. This approach allowed for an extended temporal perspective rarely encountered in studies of mining-induced land use change, which typically focus on satellite data available only from the 1980s onward. The results of the study show that the most significant changes occurred in the central part of the analysed area, between the Strzelin I and II quarries. Mining areas increased by nearly 200% between 2004 and 2023, while some former mining areas exhibited natural regrowth of vegetation. Simultaneously, a noticeable expansion of technical and industrial infrastructure was observed in the surroundings of the pits. On the other hand, post-mining areas in Mikoszów and Mikoszów Wieś showed signs of natural vegetation succession, in some cases leading to the formation of new green or forested areas. In addition, changes in hydrography were identified — including the appearance of water reservoirs in inactive pits. Also, a major contribution of this study is its statistical and spatial analysis. Through consistent vectorisation and classification harmonisation of heterogeneous sources, the methodology enabled the generation of high-resolution land cover maps and reliable quantitative summaries. Despite challenges related to scale inconsistencies, generalisation, and symbology differences across historical maps, expert-guided manual vectorisation allowed accurate identification of land cover categories. RMSE values for georeferencing ranged from 0.4 to 20.2 m, remaining within acceptable thresholds for historical cartographic analysis and comparable to values reported in similar studies. The scale and level of detail of the analysed materials enabled the generation of accurate land cover maps and change statistics for a relatively small area that had been affected by mining operations. Due to the lack of a standardised interpretation key, manual digitisation was applied to all historical materials. Modern methods such as neural networks or machine learning were not applied due to the insufficient number of training samples. The study emphasises the practical challenges associated with interpreting historical maps, including varying degrees of generalisation, inconsistent symbology, and the absence of standardised legends. The resolution of these issues required the implementation of a manual vectorisation process, guided by expert interpretation. While this method ensures higher accuracy for small areas, it is time-consuming and limits scalability. The comparison revealed a number of elements that negatively affect the correct identification of land cover forms in historical products, which include: the lack of a standardised degree of generalisation, the symbolism of cartographic elements or the way the map is stored and produced. Each factor influences the subsequent interpretation of the map content.

The study results can support sustainable spatial planning, environmental management, and post-mining land reclamation. Quantitative analysis of land cover changes can facilitate the identification of areas of ecological value, zones requiring protection, and sectors at risk of land use conflicts. Despite the absence of environmental monitoring stations within the study area, the maps derived from this analysis provide a fundamental baseline for future field verification and environmental assessments.

The prospects for future LULC research in the case of mining sites can be expanded to include the use of more modern methods, combining them with the automatic digitisation of cartographic material on an RGB basis or on the basis of a constructed database with text fields for different types of historical maps specific to the region or the time of their creation. Beyond the reconstruction of historical landscape changes, the presented methodology demonstrates strong potential for practical application in spatial planning and sustainable development, particularly in post-mining regions. The workflow developed in this study enables detailed identification of land use dynamics at a fine spatial scale, which can support informed decision-making regarding land reclamation, conflict mitigation, and environmental monitoring. Due to its modular structure and reliance on widely available cartographic sources, the approach can be effectively transferred to other areas where long-term land cover information is needed but high-resolution satellite data is lacking.
