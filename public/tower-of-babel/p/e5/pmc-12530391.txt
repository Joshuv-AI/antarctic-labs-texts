Skill mix changes in healthcare professions during the COVID-19 pandemic: a scoping review
Authors: Natalia Petka-Nosal, Iwona A Bielska, Katarzyna Badora-Musiał, Katarzyna Nowak-Zając, Alicja Domagała, Małgorzata Gałązka-Sobotka, Iwona Kowalska-Bobko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objective of the scoping review was to systematise the existing knowledge about skill mix changes among the healthcare workforce during the COVID-19 pandemic.DesignScoping review according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Review.Data sourcesFive databases including CINAHL Ultimate, Web of Science, Medline, Embase and Scopus were searched in August 2024.Eligibility criteriaThe review encompassed original research studies published from January 2020 to August 2024, on the skill mix of healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic. Quantitative and qualitative studies were included without geographical or linguistic restrictions.Data extraction and synthesisData were independently extracted by two researchers, capturing details such as publication year, study title, country, target population, study purpose and methodology, sample size, analysed variables, results and recommendations.ResultsA total of 13 563 records were identified in the databases of which 3962 remained for abstract review. 32 articles were included in the final analysis. 17 of the 32 papers were from Western and Southern Europe. The healthcare professions which were described in the studies were physicians, nurses, midwives, paramedics, pharmacists, physiotherapists, occupational therapists and medical assistants, of which the majority of the studies were conducted among nurses (n=16), pharmacists (n=11) and physicians (n=6). Most studies (n=9) concerned the adding of new tasks/roles and reallocating tasks in combination with teamwork (n=8). Research covered a range of topics, including psychological aspects of work, patient safety, work reorganisation, training and collaboration. Many studies focused on the challenges related to skill mix, such as the blurring of responsibilities and role ambiguity.ConclusionsThe research summarised in this review demonstrates the impact of implementing skill mix changes on healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in the area of mental health. The research highlights the importance of adaptation in response to pressures among healthcare professions and the entire system. Further research is needed to examine the long-term impact of skill mix on healthcare workers across regions and professions in crisis situations.

Introduction

The crisis in health workforce staffing levels is affecting many countries across the world. The WHO estimates that there will be a shortage of around 10 million health workers by 2030.1 In addition, healthcare systems are challenged by an ageing workforce population and a lack of generational replacement of medical staff. The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated these problems. To mitigate the negative impact of the pandemic, several systemic and human resource solutions were implemented to address the staff shortages that were felt within numerous health organisations. One such solution was the skill mix of medical and perimedical staff.2

The concept of skill mix is defined differently depending on the context and industry. General definitions of skill mix refer to the combination of skills or roles and positions within a given institution or system. In the context of healthcare, the aim of an appropriate skill mix is to ensure adequate access to healthcare services and the effective allocation of human resources and their competencies. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) defines changes in skill mix as modifications in the tasks and competencies of healthcare workers, as well as in the organisational structure. These changes may involve expanding the scope of duties, replacing one type of worker with another, or working across professional boundaries, delegating tasks to a higher or lower level within a single field, and innovations involving the creation of new positions.3 During the COVID-19 pandemic, new roles were created across many countries around the world to address the staff shortages resulting from the rapidly evolving public health situation. One of the most common examples of skill mix was the enabling of pharmacists to administer vaccinations, test for COVID-19 and treat infected patients.4 5 Many changes during the pandemic affected nurses, such as the adaptation to working in intensive care units.6 There were also structural changes related to new management models.710 Furthermore, skill mix applied to resident doctors and trainees who contributed to tasks for which they may not have received adequate training before the pandemic.11 12 There were many situations where junior doctors were not supervised by consultants and had to make decisions on their own.13 In addition, resident doctors or physician specialists with training in other disciplines performed tasks in intensive care, infectious diseases or pulmonology.1316 Other professionals, such as physiotherapists, laboratory diagnosticians, medical assistants or coordinators, were also involved in the process of acquiring new skills and creating treatment teams.17 18

There are no reviews or reports that outline the skill mix that took place among healthcare professions during the pandemic. Such a review is required to identify how the pandemic transformed professions and influenced the current state of skill mix, as well as to pinpoint the research gaps on this topic to guide future research endeavours. The objective of this scoping review is therefore to systematise knowledge from studies overviewing the skill mix changes that occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic among healthcare professions.

Methods

This review employs the scoping review methodology, as outlined by Arksey and O’Malley,19 which is regarded as the gold standard for reviews in the field of health policy and care.20 The review protocol has been registered with the Open Science Framework database (DOI no: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/WEAFX). The review is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Review checklist.

Following the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies typology, skill mix can be categorised into three forms: (1) task reallocation, (2) the addition of new tasks/roles and (3) the introduction or modification of teamwork. Task reallocation involves the redistribution of responsibilities among doctors, nurses, pharmacists and other healthcare professionals. The addition of new tasks or roles involves those that were not previously or regularly performed by the profession. Finally, changes to the way that two or more professions work together constitute the third type of skill mix.21 In this review, this typology was used to categorise the skill mix described in the selected studies. This approach is used by the WHO and the European Observatory on Health Systems and Policies.21 Some papers described more than one type of skill mix and this was also noted where applicable.

Search strategy

The databases used for the search were Web of Science, Medline (PubMed), Embase, CINAHL Ultimate and Scopus. The articles were sought out based on the following search terms (search date: July 2023): “COVID-19”, “medical staff”, “medical workforce”, “medical personnel”, “healthcare staff”, “healthcare workforce”, “healthcare personnel”, “midwife”, “nurse”, “physician”, “doctor”, “medician” and “medical assistant”, “coordinator”, “healthcare coordinator”, “physiotherapist”, “pharmacist”, “paramedic”, “laboratory diagnostician” and “surgeon’s assistant”. Additionally, the search included terms related to “skill mix”, “staff mix”, “task shifting”, “task delegation” and words relating only to competence such as “skill”, “competence” and “task”. The keywords were selected based on a preliminary review of the published and grey literature, and after consultation with healthcare experts. A full search strategy for all databases is provided in online supplemental appendix A of this article.

The first search was conducted in July 2023 and was updated in September 2024. The search results were collated using the Rayann.ai and Mendeley software. Two researchers independently reviewed articles based on the titles and abstracts using the Rayann.ai tool to determine their suitability for further review. In the event of a conflict, a discussion with a third researcher was held to reach a resolution. Subsequently, the full-text articles were subjected to a second independent review by two researchers and conflict resolution by a third reviewer, where needed. The review included articles published between January 2020 and August 2024 that presented original research on skill mix among health professionals. There were no geographic or language restrictions. As the goal of this scoping review was to summarise studies that examined the changes implemented during the pandemic, the following types of publications were excluded from the review: literature reviews, conference abstracts, posters and intervention studies, such as randomised controlled trials. Additionally, studies that were not directly related to skill mix, articles describing only policies or legislation without describing the changes to healthcare professions or in healthcare facilities, studies conducted among students or patients and articles on new technologies in the field of healthcare were excluded from the analysis. If the full text of the article was not available in the open access version or in the university holdings, the authors of the publication were contacted.

Data extraction

The data extraction process was conducted by two researchers independently with the collection and synthesis of data performed in Microsoft Excel. The data abstraction sheet included the year of publication, the title of the study, the country in which the study was conducted, the target population, the purpose and methodology of the study, the sample size, the variables analysed, the study results and the recommendations.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public were not involved in this review due to the worldwide character of the review.

Results

A total of 13 563 records were identified across five databases: CINAHL Ultimate (1800 records), Web of Science (4869 records), Medline (PubMed) (3298 records), Embase (1353 records) and Scopus (2243 records). After the removal of duplicates, 3962 records remained. Following the review of the abstracts and application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 82 full-text articles were selected and finally, 32 articles that met the inclusion criteria were included in the analysis (figure 1). Study characteristics

A limited number of studies were published during the initial 2-year period of the pandemic with the greatest number of articles coming out in 2022 (n=16) (table 1). Of the 32 published studies, 17 were conducted in Europe and 1 study included 39 European countries.22 Furthermore, one study was conducted on an international scale, encompassing 111 countries.23 The studies included in the analysis were mostly qualitative in nature and were conducted primarily using methods such as cross-sectional surveys, semi-structured interviews, in-depth individual interviews and focus groups. Three studies employed more than one research method, namely a cross-sectional survey together with a focus group interview,24 a focus group interview together with in-depth individual interviews25 and a semi-structured interview with a focus group interview.26 One study used a patient simulation method whereby randomly selected pharmacies were visited by a patient complaining of symptoms typical of COVID-19.27 Subsequently, the pharmacist’s response was then observed and documented in a standardised format. Furthermore, one study was based on an analysis of the medical records of patients among whom hospital pharmacists carried out the pharmaceutical care intervention. This study included a total of 5655 individuals and 484 medical records.28

Types of skill mix in the included studies

The majority of the studies included in the review pertained to adding new tasks/roles (n=9) with eight of them conducted among pharmacists. The studies from France,29 England,30 Jordan31 and Canada32 were mainly focused on giving pharmacists new skills related to vaccination and COVID-19 screening tests. In the French study,29 pharmacists also educated patients on hygiene, social distancing and vaccination, provided masks and hand sanitisers and adapted pharmacy premises to the sanitary regime. Pharmacists working in immunisation centres, whose role was not only to administer vaccinations but also to promote preventive behaviour, were included in the study conducted in England.30 Issues related to vaccination promotion and health education were also addressed in a study from the USA.33 This study involved pharmacists who had been trained in vaccine promotion and 90% of those surveyed felt that educational interventions by pharmacists were effective and increased the number of people who chose to be vaccinated. A cross-sectional study conducted in Jordan34 aimed to identify pharmacists’ perceptions of their new roles in health education and pharmaceutical care and to identify possible solutions during the COVID-19 pandemic around the addition of new tasks. Another study was based on a simulated survey methodology. It was conducted in Lebanon27 and focused on assessing the level of pharmaceutical care as a new role for pharmacists. One study was conducted among physiotherapists and occupational therapists in the USA. This study looked at new roles, mainly in terms of patient education and taking on some of the tasks of respiratory therapists.26

Eight studies were concerned with the reallocation of tasks and the introduction or change in teamwork. The participants in these studies were nurses (n=7), physiotherapists (n=2), doctors (n=1), medical assistants (n=1), midwives (n=1), paramedics (n=1) and pharmacists (n=1). Two of the studies were conducted in Canada. One focused on changes in daily work tasks and reorganisation of the ward,35 while the other mainly described the self-assessment of competencies of non-specialist nurses who were transferred to an intensive care unit.24 This issue was also addressed in a Swedish study36 that included not only nurses but also physicians, nursing assistants and physiotherapists. This study involved professionals for whom skill mix solutions were implemented, mainly related to modifications associated with the establishment of temporary intensive care units. Changes in teamwork and task reallocation were also described in a cross-sectional survey of nurses and midwives in Ireland.37 Hospitals where inpatient wards had been reconfigured and medical staff redeployed were included in the study. Nurses and midwives were required to retrain in infection prevention control, improve critical care skills and documentation, and were faced with new management models. Nurses’ experiences of management changes and working together as part of the care team were also described in a UK study38 of intensive care units. One aspect of implementing the skill mix was the assignment of management roles to nurses who had not previously undertaken such work. In the UK, research was also carried out on nurses’ preparation for transfer, emotional impact, safety and support, self-assessed competence and reflection.39 Another study, also from the UK,25 was a survey of professionals working with older people that included nurses, physiotherapists, paramedics and pharmacists who had been transferred to other clinical areas. Lastly, a study from Germany40 was conducted among medical assistants working in primary care who had experienced new tasks and challenges as a result of the COVID-19 pandemic. The main areas studied were collaboration, practice organisation and satisfaction.

The introduction of changing teamwork was the subject of studies conducted among nurses (n=5) and doctors (n=3). The research from Belgium,41 Sweden42 and the UK7 focused mainly on aspects related to changes in care models, including taking on management roles. Such activities were associated with shifts in attitudes towards colleagues, especially when unexpectedly promoted to a managerial position. A survey was also conducted among nurse managers in Austria,43 focusing on their relationships with superiors and subordinates in the context of new work demands. A qualitative study conducted in Ireland44 among a range of professionals (nurses, doctors and physiotherapists) who were part of the care team investigated changes in leadership and teamwork during the COVID-19 pandemic. Another study was internationally based22 and surveyed respiratory physicians. It looked at whether doctors had received adequate training on treating COVID-19 and how their work was affected by being transferred to COVID-19 treatment units.

Five studies were concerned with the reallocation of tasks. One study in Norway,45 involving paediatric nurses, looked at their transfer to adult wards. This study also examined the self-assessment of professional competencies during the first wave of the pandemic. There was also a survey of nurses in China working in temporary intensive care units converted from infectious disease wards. The aim was to develop recommendations for the effective establishment and management of temporary intensive care units.46 A further study was based on an analysis of medical records and was conducted in the United Arab Emirates.28 It analysed interventions by hospital pharmacists and found that most were related to the use of antibiotic therapy in COVID-19 and that the rate of physician acceptance of these interventions was almost 95%. Another study related to the implementation of task reallocation was carried out in the UK among doctors in training and concerned the transfer of laryngology trainees to other specialties.47 Furthermore, an international survey of primary care physicians from 111 countries was conducted, focusing on the performance of tasks outside the clinical specialty.23

Four studies were identified that fit the typology of task reallocation and task addition. The first was a study conducted in Japan48 among doctors and nurses who evaluated the work of pharmacists. This study looked at the allocation of new competencies to pharmacists and their position in the treatment team. The second was a qualitative study of nurses in the USA.49 It collected data on new roles in the institution, how the transition to the new roles was managed and the support received in the process. The third study conducted in South Korea also involved nurses. It looked at how nurses coped with new tasks related to non-clinical duties and the care of intensive care patients.50 The final study was a cross-sectional survey of Italian51 hospital pharmacists. The survey focused on pharmaceutical care and the acquisition of new competencies in this area.

Two studies related to the addition of new roles were also included in the analysis, both conducted among pharmacists. The first was a cross-sectional study carried out in Jordan,34 which aimed to identify pharmacists’ perceptions of their new roles in health education and pharmaceutical care and to identify possible solutions during the COVID-19 pandemic around the addition of new tasks. Another study was based on a simulated survey methodology. It was conducted in Lebanon27 and focused on assessing the level of pharmaceutical care as a new role for pharmacists.

Impact on healthcare professions

The majority of studies focused on the psychological aspects of work and the impact of the skill mix on the stress levels, responsibility and trust, emotional state or coping with change among the healthcare professionals.7 23 32 35 36 38 41 44 47 The second most common area of research was patient safety with a particular focus on mental and physical well-being.7 23 27 36 38 41 42 47 Additionally, research was conducted on work reorganisation. This research examined the assumption of new responsibilities, including the implementation of increased flexibility and adaptability, as well as the fostering of autonomy and leadership.25 35 40 41 47 49 A number of studies also concentrated on the difficulties and obstacles related to the skill mix, including blurring of responsibilities and competencies, as well as the experience of role ambiguity.7 25 26 30 36 41 The research in the field of education referred primarily to the learning needs and priorities related to conferring new competencies and self-assessment of competency levels.22 24 27 34 48 52 Furthermore, respondents were queried on their perception of teamwork, including their relationships with colleagues and superiors.23 37 40 43 Systemic solutions and institutional facilitation related to the skill mix were a significant topic of discussion in the studies included in the analysis. They focused primarily on the regulations related to the performance of vaccination and screening by pharmacists.30 32 52 The largest number of studies included in the review concerned nurses and, as can be seen in figure 3, the studies conducted in this group dealt with a wide array of topics. Research has shown that implementing skill mix solutions during the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with experiencing role ambiguity and trust issues.37 41 In addition, the studies suggested that increased levels of stress and anxiety related not only to COVID-19 itself, but also to the changes associated with having to perform new tasks for which staff may have felt unprepared.38 44 These role modifications had an impact on nurses’ personal lives, including family life and feelings of burnout.35 42 Nurses were required to lead therapeutic and care teams, which could have disrupted teamwork.41 In some studies, nurses noted reduced patient safety as a result of the dilution of responsibility and reorganisation of care.7 36 42 Experiences of loss of control over clinical practice were also reported, which could have had serious consequences for the mental and physical well-being of patients.41 In addition, two studies showed competency deficits among the nurses in the context of crisis situations.24 37 Lastly, a survey of nurse managers showed satisfactory levels of cooperation and cohesion, but highlighted problems of communication and transparency in the implementation of the skill mix.43

Discussion

The studies, which included a total of 32 publications, concentrated primarily on Western Europe. There was one multinational study covering 111 countries and one study covering 39 European countries. The research methods most frequently employed were qualitative, including cross-sectional surveys, semi-structured interviews, individual in-depth interviews and focus groups. Furthermore, one study used the patient simulation method, while another was based on the analysis of medical records. The majority of the studies involved nurses, pharmacists and doctors, with the primary themes being the reorganisation of work, changing teamwork dynamics, the psychological aspects of work and patient safety. The research also looked at difficulties and barriers related to the professional competencies and training needs of health professionals.

There is a paucity of research on the skill mix of health professionals during the COVID-19 pandemic in some geographic areas, such as in Australia, South America and Central and Eastern European countries. Few studies were identified among physiotherapists, medical assistants and paramedics. The review did not identify any studies conducted among laboratory diagnosticians or medical coordinators. A limited number of publications were conducted during the initial 2 years of the pandemic, with the majority of the papers conducted in 2022. Studies with different themes related to changes in the skill mix were included in this review. The largest number of studies dealt with the psychological aspects of work, patient safety, dilution of responsibility and needs related to educational systems.

A multitude of studies has demonstrated the negative impact that the pandemic had on the mental health of healthcare workers.53 High patient mortality rates, uncomfortable working conditions, staff shortages and lack of personal protective equipment are just some of the reasons why mental health was adversely affected.5457 A particularly large number of studies focused on stress, perceived anxiety and coping with change. These studies suggest that the skill mix changes implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic may have had a negative impact on the psychological well-being of healthcare workers due to the assumption of new responsibilities and role ambiguity combined with modifications to the leadership structures. However, there is a lack of long-term studies showing the effects of these changes over time. In addition, most of the research in this area was conducted among nurses, so it is difficult to generalise these findings to other healthcare professionals.

Studies on the dilution of responsibility and role ambiguity during the COVID-19 pandemic were also included in the review. These qualitative studies suggest that a sense of limited control over clinical practice may have had a negative impact on patient health. However, there is a gap in quantitative studies on this topic that would show a statistical correlation between the implementation of skill mix changes and patient outcome indicators, such as mortality or adverse events.

Many of the articles represented in this review address the issue of role blurring and ambiguity in accountability. Such blurring may lead to heightened stress, feelings of confusion and deterioration in teamwork. However, research that focuses on developing solutions or institutional frameworks outlining the roles and responsibilities in the case of a pandemic crisis is scant. Furthermore, the included research also identified problems with communication and information flow. Therefore, future studies are needed to show how communication can be improved during crises.

The review also shows that the reorganisation of work in times of crisis has resulted in nurses and other staff taking on new leadership roles that require greater flexibility and adaptability. More research is needed on how these new leadership roles might be supported through training and formal leadership development programmes. Further research will be valuable in exploring how leadership can be supported in health professionals who have not previously been prepared for it. Another important issue to systematically explore is the need for additional training among healthcare professionals with new responsibilities arising during the pandemic. Systemic and institutional aspects of the skill mix have been reviewed in this paper, particularly in areas such as vaccination and screening. However, there is a lack of research on how national regulations may support or hinder the effective implementation of the skill mix during a crisis. Future emergency research should assess how policy frameworks can be optimised to enable smoother transitions in the skill mix, particularly in relation to changing health system needs.

It is worth noting that this review concerns changes implemented in crisis situations. The implementation of skill mix solutions in situations of population threat and without a proper plan is difficult and may lead to a multitude of problems. In light of this, it is critical for leadership in healthcare facilities, professional regulatory bodies and health system planning, along with representatives of the healthcare professions, to review the effects and implications of skill mix changes during the COVID-19 pandemic to make future evidence-based decisions. Moreover, preparedness plans are required to be created and routinely updated to swiftly respond to the need for workforce changes during emergencies, as well as to closely monitor these changes once implemented.

Limitations

Although the study provides valuable insights into the skill mix changes of healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic, several limitations should be considered. The search strategy focused on peer-reviewed articles indexed in scientific databases, potentially excluding relevant studies published in the grey literature. The included studies used different research methods, including qualitative and quantitative approaches. In addition, the studies varied in terms of sample size, target population and healthcare setting, which may affect the applicability of the findings to different contexts. Although the review covered a wide range of issues related to skill mix, certain aspects, such as the impact on patient outcomes or cost-effectiveness, were not explored in depth. Despite the absence of geographical restrictions, most of the included studies originated from Europe. This may be a potential limitation of the drawn conclusions, given the different healthcare systems around the world.

Despite these limitations, the findings of the review have valuable implications for health policy, practice and research. Addressing these limitations in future studies can further enhance our understanding of the evolving role of health professionals and inform strategies to optimise workforce deployment in crisis situations. One of the strengths of this review is that there were no geographical or language restrictions, so studies from different health systems around the world could be found.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first scoping review to synthesise research on changes in the skill mix among healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic. This review makes an important contribution to understanding the impact of the pandemic on the work of health professionals and highlights many of the needs and challenges of adapting to new working conditions. Importantly, most of the studies focused on nurses, pharmacists and doctors, highlighting the crucial role of these professionals in providing effective healthcare during a crisis. This scoping review’s findings indicate a need for further research into the impact of skill mix on patient care and the healthcare workforce, as well as the identification of best practices. Moreover, research should be carried out on a larger scale to include many geographical regions and varied professional groups. In closing, if health systems focus on an evidence-based approach to skill mix changes through carefully allocating sufficient resources, planning thoroughly and maintaining transparency, they can significantly improve the effectiveness of their health workforce, contributing to the development of strong, efficient and sustainable health systems.
