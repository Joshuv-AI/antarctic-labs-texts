The development and validation of a self-reinforcement scale for repeated blood donation
Authors: Huahua Hu, Phoenix K H Mo, Qiuyue Hu, Yangang Nie, Hujiao Kuang, Joseph T F Lau, Wei Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current study aimed to develop and validate a self-reinforcement scale for repeated blood donation (SRS-RBD). In Study 1, six items were created, and the factor structure of SRS-RBD was explored among 155 Chinese student blood donors. In Study 2, SRS-RBD was validated among 164 donors. In Study 3, a total of 850 blood donors were invited to participate in an online-survey measuring SRS-RBD at baseline, and their RBD behavior was captured 6 months later. A two-factor structure of SRS-RBD was established in Study 1 and further validated in Study 2 and Study 3. The SRS-RBD demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity. A cut-off score of 20 was suggested as having the best performance to identify donors who had a high chance to re-donate. The SRS-RBD can be considered reliable in measuring the self-reinforcement of RBD among blood donors. The scale may be a useful tool in blood donation practice and research programs.Keywords: Self-reinforcement, Scale development, Validation, Repeated blood donation, Blood donors

Introduction

In China, the blood supply and public health are seriously challenged by the blood shortage [1]. The blood shortage status is expected to worsen as eligible donors may decrease, and blood demand may increase in the future [2]. Retaining blood donors and encouraging repeated blood donation (RBD) are essential for a secure and enough supply of blood. RBD means donating blood twice or more in the past [3]. However, donor loss was found to be high, both in China and abroad. For example, a population study among 3, 226, 571 donors in China reported that the loss of young donors (aged 18–25) reached 73. 8% [4]. Another large-scale study among 539,063 donors followed for 3 to 6 years in the US reported that 49% of first-time-donors were lost within six years [5]. It is thus critical to reveal the factors of RBD. Such findings could inform the design of targeted interventions to promote RBD.

According to social cognitive theory (SCT) and reinforcement learning model (RLM), self-reinforcement refers to the experience of positive and negative self-evaluative responses to one’s own conduct, which influences the likelihood of that behavior being repeated [6–8]. These self-evaluative reactions serve to either encourage or discourage repeated performance of the behavior, depending on whether the feedback is positive or negative [9]. It is closely related to intrinsic motivation and sustained behavioral engagement. Empirical studies have demonstrated significant associations between reinforcement and health behaviors/intentions such as self-care behaviors, substance use and intention to perform physical activity [10–12]. According to protection-motivation-theory [PMT, 13, 14], the positive relationship between adverse reaction or negative experience with blood donation (BD) and donor attrition can be attributed to the fact that negative experience makes donor feel threatened and perceive lower outcome expectation and self-efficacy of RBD. To protect themselves from being harmed, donors may reduce donation frequency or lapse [e.g., 15, 16]. In sum, it is expected that the negative aspects of self-reinforcement would be strong factors in deterring RBD.

Self-reinforcement may play an important role in predicting RBD. A previous study has indicated that, compared to beliefs, feelings towards BD tended to be more related to people’s overall attitude towards RBD [17]. Systematic review and meta-analysis studies have suggested that certain reinforcers (such as donation satisfaction, happiness) were positively associated with RBD (r = .09 to 0.27), while others (such as adverse events, discomfort) were negatively associated with RBD (r = − .40 to − 0.09) [18–20]. It was reported that donation satisfaction, as a positive experience, was associated with increased retention intention through improved attitude, while vasovagal reaction, as a negative experience, was related to reduced retention intention through decreased attitude [21]. Another positive feeling, pride, is evidenced to be higher among RBD than first-time donors [22]. In contrast, a survey reported that the bad experience in the first donation was the major reason for the loss of blood donors [23]. Another survey reported that blood donors who experienced discomfort and unpleasantness were less likely to re-donate [24]. Donation anxiety was indicated to be associated with decreased RBD intention among American blood donors [25]. Furthermore, it is imperative to establish the relationship between self-reinforcement and RBD as interventions through increasing reinforcement and reducing negative emotions could be effective [10].

Nevertheless, the concept of self-reinforcement has not been well defined in previous studies although these self-evaluative responses (e.g., satisfaction, pride, discomfort, anxiety) were examined for their associations with RBD. Through previous studies [21, 22, 24], we were only able to identify some positive and negative experiences (e.g., satisfaction, pride, discomfort) associated with RBD, which we believe are important components of self-reinforcement. It is important to conduct a systematic investigation. A reinforcement scale for BD does not exist in the literature, which limits the examination and understanding of RBD. Existing measurements of reinforcement are specific to other health behaviors such as smoking [e.g., 26], which is not suitable for the exploration of RBD. For instance, items of smoking reinforcement (e.g., I crave a cigarette to provide relief from withdrawal; I crave a cigarette to provide pleasure) are inappropriate and difficult to modify to fit the context of BD. Therefore, the current study fills a significant gap in the field of RBD research. The self-reinforcement scale aims to measure the extent to which blood donors experience self-evaluative responses—both positive and negative—following their donation behavior. When these responses increase the likelihood of repeating the behavior, they are considered self-reinforcement; when they decrease that likelihood, they are referred to as punishment [6–8]. Together, these form the positive and negative dimensions of the scale.

As part of this study, the validity of the self-reinforcement scale for RBD (SRS-RBD) developed was examined in relation to six external variables (state-anxiety, affective-attitude toward BD, situational-motivation of BD, donation satisfaction, RBD intention and RBD behavior) to determine its convergence and criterion validity. First, negative experience is a key risk factor for anxiety avoidance/escape behaviors and protecting oneself [27], and donation anxiety is revealed as an important barrier to both BD and RBD [25, 28]. Second, feelings and emotions shape one’s affective attitude toward certain objects/behavior [29]. Self-reinforcement, which reflects the functions of experiences and emotions on the reoccurrence of certain behaviors, is thus associated with the affective component of attitude. Third, self-reinforcement is considered as the key mechanism underlying the influence of rewards/external reinforcement on intrinsic motivation [30] and thus is closely related to the situational motivation experienced by individuals during an activity [31]. Forth, donation satisfaction, referred to as the perceived level of pleasure/contentment derived from the donation process, is considered as one element of self-reinforcement because it is an inner evaluation of past donation experiences and significantly increases RBD intention and behavior [21, 32, 33]. It is thus expected to be associated with self-reinforcement. Lastly, since self-reinforcements are defined as self-evaluated responses to past BD behavior that respectively increase RBD behavior, they are therefore expected to be associated with increased RBD intention and behavior. The first three variables (state-anxiety, affective-attitude regarding BD, situational-motivation of BD) were used for convergent validity, and the last three variables (satisfaction and RBD intention and RBD behavior) were used for criterion validity.

Three studies aimed at developing and validating a scale to measure self-reinforcement of RBD were conducted. This study also aimed to examine a cut-off score for identifying donors with a high chance of re-donating in the future. This threshold is important because it allows practitioners and researchers to effectively screen and categorize donors. Blood centers can use this scale to identify potential donors for recruitment and to detect those less likely to return, allowing for more personalized follow-up efforts. The factor structures of SRS-RBD, as well as the reliability and validity will be examined. We hypothesized that the positive dimension of SRS-RBD would positively correlate with affective-attitude towards BD, situational-motivation for BD, satisfaction and RBD intention/behavior, whereas the negative dimension would negatively correlate with these variables; SRS-RBD’s positive dimension would negatively correlate with state-anxiety, while its negative dimension would positively correlate with state-anxiety.

Study 1 Development and examination of factor structure of the SRS-RBD

Methods

Item generation

We conducted a literature review in order to indicate the concept of self-reinforcement and the associated domain/dimension, identify potential items within the domain/dimension and identify existing self-reinforcement or reinforcement scales. This is a deductive method for item generation. In the SCT and RLM, self-reinforcement is understood to be the positive and negative internal and self-evaluative responses to an individual’s behavior that increases or decreases their chances of repeated behavior (e.g., satisfaction/discomfort after donating) [6, 34–36]. Trait and state positive and negative affect are associated with psychobiological and psychodynamic constructs of sensitivity to signals of reward and punishment [37]. It is argued that emotions are states elicited by reinforcing stimuli, which are also rewarding and punishing reinforcers that may increase or decrease the occurrence of behavior [38–40]. Thus, the positive and negative emotions/affect generated during the past donation experience may work as rewarding and punishing reinforcers of RBD. Searches were conducted using MEDLINE, Web of Science, EMBASE, China-National-Knowledge-Infrastructure, Wan-Fang database, and Sinomed with terms pertaining to self-reinforcement of RBD (“reinforce*” OR “experience” OR “self-praise” “satisfaction” OR “anxiety” OR “feelings” OR “emotion*”) AND “blood” AND (“donat*” OR “donor*” OR “re-donat*”) AND (“first-time” OR “first” OR “new” OR “repeat*” OR “active” OR “inactive” OR “frequency” OR “novice” OR “early career” OR “lapsed” OR “loss”) to identify constructs related to self-reinforcement. No existing scale for self-reinforcement of BD could be located. Therefore, literature of both qualitative and quantitative studies about the experiences, self-evaluative responses, and emotions/affect in past donations were reviewed, and relevant items were extracted. These items were then grouped into two dimensions: positive and negative.

In-depth interview to pilot study

An in-depth interview, an expert consultation, and a pilot study were conducted. The details of these procedures can be found in the study that developed an outcome expectation scale [41]. The in-depth interview was conducted online by the first author with 10 blood donors. These donors were Chinese-speaking, voluntary blood donors aged 18–55 years who had previously donated whole blood. They were briefed about the background, purpose, and confidentiality at the beginning of the interview. No incentives were provided for their participation. The participants were asked about their positive and negative experiences and their emotions or feelings in the process of BD and RBD (e.g., What has your experience of donating blood been like? What positive/negative feelings did you experience in your past donation process? ). Most donors do not experience any feelings or emotions. Some donors responded with experience of blood donation reactions, worry, satisfaction, pride, sense of fulfillment etc. Their responses were used to generate the items of the self-reinforcement scale. A total of 11 items, 6 for the positive dimension and 5 for the negative dimension were thus generated based on the literature review and in-depth interview. Following expert consultation, five items were removed due to redundancy (e.g., worry was redundant with nervousness/unease, sense of fulfillment was redundant with satisfaction) or because they did not align with the conceptual definition of reinforcement (e.g., blood donation reactions, i.e., reactions such as hyperaemia, swelling, pallor, sweating, dizziness, nausea etc.). Six items—three positive and three negative—were retained and revised for further evaluation. In the pilot study, participants were asked to reflect on the intensity of their feelings related to their past BD experiences. The response range is 1–4 (1 = None, 4 = Intense).

Examination of factor structure

Participants and procedures

In December 2020 and February 2021, a convenience sample of university students from Dongguan City, China, was recruited. The reason university students were selected as participants in the present study was that student donors and those between the ages of 18 and 25 make up the majority of blood donors in China [e.g., 42]. The recruitment details are outlined in a previous study [41]. One hundred and fifty-five sample were included in the analysis for examining the factor structure of the SRS-RBD. Of the 155 participants, 64.5% (100) were girls, 60% (93) aged 18–20 and 40% (62) aged 21–32. The mean age was 20.23 (SD = 1.94).

Instruments

This survey included background information questions regarding gender, age, and the set of 6 self-developed items measuring self-reinforcement. Participants were required to rate the extent to which they experienced each of the 6 items during their past donation. The 6 items were divided into two dimensions: the negative dimension (“Have experienced nervousness/unease”, “Have experienced discomfort”, “Have experienced unpleasantness”) and the positive dimension (“Have experienced satisfaction”, “Have experienced happiness/pleasure”, “Have experienced proudness”). Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale which ranged from 1 (None) to 4 (Intense).

Data analysis

Each item was analyzed in terms of its mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis values, and item-to-total correlation. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed with SPSS 21.0 version. The Promax rotation method was used in the present study [43, 44]. The extraction method used was principal axis factoring (PAF). To determine the optimal number of factors to retain, a parallel analysis was employed [45]. Using the parameters in our dataset, we generated 1,000 random datasets and determined the appropriate eigenvalue through parallel analysis. A cut-off value of 95% was used for comparing the eigenvalues. Cronbach’s alpha, composite-reliability (CR), and average-variance-extracted were calculated as indicators of the internal reliability of SRS-RBD.

Results

Item analyses of SRS-RBD

The 6 items of the SRS-RBD showed slight negative skewness (M = 0.09) and slight positive kurtosis (M = 0.49), with absolute values of skewness and kurtosis ranging from 0 to 2 and 0 to 3, respectively. The item-to-total correlations of all items ranged from 0.51 to 0.74 (p < .001), which were higher than 0.40. The 6 items were all included for further analysis.

Exploratory factor analysis

The data were suitable for factor analysis, as indicated by the results of the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin test (KMO = 0.72) and Bartlett’s test (p < .001). The scree plot and PAF with eigenvalues greater than 1 resulted in two factors, accounting for 77.6% of the overall variance. Second, the results of the parallel analysis indicated that we should retain two factors from our sample. The eigenvalues ranged from 1.94 to 2.71.

The two factors were respectively named “negative experiences” (item 1–3, α = 0.77) and “positive experiences” (item 4–6, α = 0.91) as the factors include items about the negative and positive emotions/experiences generated during the past donation. The Pearson correlation revealed a non-significant intercorrelation between factors (see Table 1). Two subscales with “negative experiences” factors being negative and “positive experiences” factors being positive will be created for further examination.

Summary and discussion

Study 1 presents the development of the SRS-RBD. An initial pool of 11 items was generated, which was refined to a final set of 6 items that met all item-level criteria through expert consultations and EFA. Two factors (positive experiences and negative experiences) were identified, accounting for 77.6% of the total variance. The positive dimension demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.91), while the negative dimension showed acceptable reliability (α = 0.77). Results suggest that the SRS-RBD successfully captures both self-reinforcement (positive) and punishment (negative) experiences that influence blood donation behavior. Based on these findings, Study 2 will further evaluate the factor structure and assess the scale’s ability to predict RBD intention.

Study 2 Factor structure validation of the SRS-RBD

Methods

Study 2 was conducted to confirm the factor structures of the SRS-RBD determined by the results of EFA in Study 1. In addition, the convergent validity, criteria validity, and reliability of the SRS-RBD were examined. SRS-RBD factor structure, reliability, and validity were cross-validated in a sample of blood donors recruited at blood donation sites.

Participants and procedures

Convenience sampling was used. A second sample (i.e., Sample 2) was selected from blood collection sites instead of colleges, following the inclusion and exclusion criteria as in Study 1. The recruitment and sample details are described in a previous study [41]. The data obtained from 164 participants (79 females, 85 males; Mage = 25.68 ± 7.87) was adopted for data analysis.

Instruments

Demographic questions such as age, gender were included. The self-developed 6-item SRS-RBD, as well as measures (which have been published elsewhere) for examining the criteria validity and convergent validity were included in the online survey:

Donation Intention Scale (DIS). The DIS was used to test the predictor validity of the SRS-RBD. We modified this scale [46, 47] to measure donors’ RBD intention within the next 12 months. The next 12 months was used instead of the next 6 months used by Oosterhof, Heuvelman [46]. This was because the minimal donation interval in China was 6 months, and most donors returned within 6–12 months [4]. BD was used to replace money donation. This modified scale has shown good reliability and validity among Chinese blood donors [48]. The scale has 2 items. The response range is 1–5 (1 = Not at all likely, 5 = Very much likely). Sample items include “I’m going to donate blood in the following 12 months”. The Cronbach’s α was 0.92.

Donation Attitude Scale (DAS). The 3-item affective subscale of DAS [49], which indicates a person’s affective attitude regarding BD, was used to examine the convergent validity of the SRS-RBD. Participants respond by indicating their agreement with the items. It is a 7-point scale that ranges from 1 to 7 (1 = Not at all like me, 7 = Very much like me). Sample items include “It is pleasant for me to donate blood in the next year.” and “It is enjoyable for me to donate blood in the next year.” Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.88.

Situational Motivation Scale (SMS). The SMS [31, 50] was used to test the convergent validity of the SRS-RBD. It consists of 16 items assessing the motivation that individuals experience when participating in a particular activity [31, 50]. This activity refers to BD in the present study. Participants were required to rate on a 7-point Likert scale that ranged from 1 to 7 (1 = Not at all like me, 7 = Very much like me). Cronbach’s α of this scale was 0.84.

Short form of Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-6). The STAI-6 [51, 52] was used to examine the convergent validity of SRS-RBD. It is a widely used scale to assess anxiety and consists of six items (e.g., I feel calm; I feel content). The STAI-6 is a 4-point Likert scale that ranges from 1 to 4 (1 = Not at all, 4 = Very much so). Cronbach’s α of the scale in the present study was 0.71.

Blood donation satisfaction scale (BDSS). The BDSS [53] was utilized to examine the concurrent validity of SRS-RBD. It consists of five items evaluating the degree to which blood donors are satisfied with the most recent BD event [53]. The scale is a five-point Likert scale, which ranges from 1 to 5 (1 = Not at all, 5 = Extremely). Examples of items include ratings of how satisfied with staff explaining procedures and their efforts to make donors comfortable. Cronbach’s α was 0.95.

Data analysis

The same procedure was conducted as in a previous study [41]. A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the fixed-variance method was performed by Mplus 8. A Maximum Likelihood Robust Estimator (MLR) was set up in the CFA to handle the non-normality data (average skewness and kurtosis were 0.09 and 0.49). A configuration metric scalar model was used to test the measurement invariance across gender. Analysis of the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve by the SPSS 21.0 version was conducted to identify an appropriate cut-off point of SRS-RBD.

Results

Confirmatory factor analysis

We conducted CFA using the MLR method to test the structure of SRS-RBD produced in the EFA. The two-factor construct of SRS-RBD was well-supported by the data (Y-B χ2/ [9] = 1.33, CFI = 0.990, TLI = 0.984, RMSEA = 0.046) (Table 1).

Measurement invariance

Table 2 illustrates that configurable, metric, and scalar invariance models fit the data across genders. Scalar and metric invariance between genders was demonstrated by the small differences in fit indices between configural metric scalar models (ΔCFI < 0.01, ΔRMSEA < 0.015, ΔSRMR < 0.030) [54]. Considering that two model fit indices reached ideal levels (CFI = 1.000 and RMSEA = 0.000)—a phenomenon that can be observed in studies with relatively small sample sizes (n = 164; group 1 n = 79, group 2 n = 85 ) [55, 56]—we conducted a follow-up measurement invariance test in Study 3 with a larger sample.

Tests for reliability

The Cronbach’s α of the positive and negative subscale and the total SRS-RBD were 0.90, 0.84, and 0.68, respectively. The composite reliability of the positive and negative subscale and the total SRS-RBD were 0.90, 0.84, and 0.93, respectively. The average variance extracted was 0.75, 0.63, and 0.69 for the positive, negative subscale and the total SRS-RBD, respectively.

Criterion validity and convergent validity

The convergent validity of SRS-RBD was evaluated by testing the correlation coefficients related to the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory [51], the affective subscale of the donation attitude scale [49], and the situational-motivation scale [31]. Results exhibited that SRS-RBD had moderate associations with state-trait anxiety (r = -.454, p < .001), affective donation attitude (r = .540, p < .001) and donation motivation (r = .451, p < .001). This suggests that SRS-RBD had satisfactory convergent validity. Positive and negative subscales of SRS-RBD were also associated with state-trait anxiety, affective donation attitude and donation motivation (see Table 3). These results suggest that both subscales of SRS-RBD had an adequate convergent validity.

For the criterion validity, the concurrent validity of SRS-RBD was tested by evaluating its correlation with the blood donation satisfaction scale [53]. Results showed that SRS-RBD had a moderate positive association with overall satisfaction of BD (r = .480, p < .001), suggesting that SRS-RBD had a good concurrent validity. Results exhibited that SRS-RBD had a moderate positive association with re-donation intention (r = .304, p < .001). Cut-off point for determining donors who are likely to re-donate

Figure 1 illustrates the relationship between the self-reinforcement scores and the likelihood of re-donation at each possible threshold. Approximately 66% of the data gathered was within the area under the curve (95% CI: 0.56 to 0.75). The Youden Index reached its highest level when the SRS-RBD score was 20. Meanwhile, the sensitivity and specificity were 50% and 78%, respectively. Furthermore, donors scored 20 or above on SRS-RBD exhibited significantly higher BD attitude and RBD intention than their counterparts (see Table 4). The findings indicate that the cut score is valid in screening donors who possess positive attitudes and higher intentions toward RBD. The cutoff score of 20 was therefore suggested as the threshold for identifying the potential repeated donors. A donor who scores over 20 is considered likely to re-donate in the upcoming year. The cutoff value of 20 was established to serve as a practical tool for identifying blood donors who are more likely to re-donate in the future. This threshold is important because it allows practitioners and researchers to effectively screen and categorize donors who might have a higher likelihood of engaging in RBD. Blood centers can use this scale to identify potential donors for recruitment and to detect those less likely to return, allowing for more personalized follow-up efforts. The scale can guide blood centers to prioritize interventions that enhance positive donation experiences (e.g., appreciation messages, donor recognition) and reduce negative ones (e.g., minimizing discomfort or anxiety through education and on-site support), ultimately aiding in donor retention. It can also be used to evaluate the impact of donor engagement and retention initiatives.

Summary and discussion

Study 2 offers further insight into the structure and validity of the SRS-RBD. CFA supported the two-factor structure identified in Study 1, confirming that the scale effectively measures positive and negative experiences. Similar to Study 1, scores from each dimension were found to show strong internal consistency and reliability. Additionally, the scale’s predictive validity was tested by examining its ability to predict RBD intention. The results showed that higher scores on the positive dimension were associated with increased RBD intention, while higher scores on the negative dimension were associated with decreased RBD intention. These findings suggest that positive experiences (such as satisfaction and pride) encourage future donations, whereas negative experiences (such as anxiety and discomfort) deter them. Overall, the results of Study 2 provide strong support for the SRS-RBD as a valid and reliable tool for predicting blood donation intention. The study highlights the importance of positive experiences in promoting repeated donations and underscores the need to address negative experiences to promote donor retention. These promising findings pave the way for further exploration of the scale in Study 3, which tests its reliability, generalizability and validity in a larger sample.

Study 3 Further examination of the SRS-RBD validity

Study 3 was used to further examine the predictive validity and the measurement invariance of the SRS-RBD. We hypothesized that SRS-RBD would be positively associated with RBD intention and predict RBD behavior at 6-month follow-up.

Methods

Participants and procedures

A longitudinal study consisted of a 15-minute online survey was conducted at baseline, and RBD behavior was tracked at a 6-month follow-up. A separate sample of Chinese speaking blood donors who had given blood in the previous three years and were eligible to re-donate were included in the current study. Exclusion criteria of participants were the same as Study 1 and Study 2. Eligible donors were contacted via the short message service (SMS) platform of the Blood Center of Zhejiang Province (BCZJP) and briefed about the background and purpose of the study. The SMS invited donors to complete an online survey through the link included in the message. Those who clicked on the link could see the detailed background and purpose of the study. Those who agreed to participate were asked to provide informed consent, followed by the online survey. After approaching 18,967 donors through SMS, a total of 1493 responses (7.8% response rate) were received. The 7.8% response rate may be attributed to the donor profile at BCZJP, which primarily comprises individuals aged 18–25, a highly mobile demographic. Due to frequent changes in residence or contact information, only about 60–70% of SMS invitations were successfully delivered. Delivery failures were likely caused by canceled mobile numbers, outdated contact information, or donors having left Hangzhou. Similar response rates have been reported in other studies [57, 58]. Of these participants, 1025 fulfilled the inclusion criteria and further completed the questionnaire. A total of 175 questionnaires were further excluded due to potential quality issues (e.g., incorrect answers to instructed items). The final effective sample included 850 valid questionnaires (390 females, 460 males; Mage = 25.29 ± 7.16) that were used for analysis. 32% were new donors, and 68% were repeat donors. The likelihood of re-donating within 12 months was approximately 74% among repeated donors, compared to only 26% among new donors. At the 6-month follow-up (November 2021), the re-donation behavior of these donors was checked from the database of BCZJP (an information system for blood collection and management in Zhejiang Province, BIS2.0).

Instruments

The self-developed 6-item SRS-RBD, background information, and RBD intention were measured at the baseline survey, while RBD behavior was measured at a 6-month follow-up. Considering that measurement invariance testing may become more effective with increasing response categories [59], a 7-point response scale of SRS-RBD was employed in the present study. The Cronbach’s α of SRS-RBD in the present study was 0.72.

RBD intention. The same Donation Intention Scale used in Study 2 was employed in Study 3 at the baseline survey. The Cronbach’s α of this scale in the present study was 0.95.

RBD behavior. RBD behavior was measured at 6-month follow-up by checking the donation record through the database of BCZJP. Participants with a donation record during a 6-month follow-up were coded 1, while those without were coded 0.

Data analysis

A configuration metric scalar model was used to test measurement invariance across genders. Independent Sample T-test and Pearson Chi-square test were conducted to examine the differences in RBD intention and RBD behavior for donors classified as high SRS and low SRS based on the cut-off score established in Study 2. Univariate and multiple logistic regression analyses were also used to test the predictive effect of baseline SRS-RBD on RBD behavior at 6-month follow-up. SPSS 21.0 version was used for the above analyses.

Results

Measurement invariance

The results of the configural, metric, and scalar model tests provided further support for measurement invariance across gender (see Table 2). Model fit indices were satisfactory, and both metric and scalar invariance were confirmed (ΔCFI < 0.01, ΔRMSEA < 0.015, ΔSRMR < 0.030) [54].

Predictive validity

Logistic regression analyses showed that SRS-RBD significantly and positively predicted RBD behavior at follow up, both before (ORu = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.13, 1.65, p < .01) and after (AOR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.07, 1.59, p < .01) adjusting for the significant demographic variables and baseline RBD (donation times). The negative dimension of SRS-RBD significantly and negatively predicted RBD behavior, both unadjusted (ORu = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.56, 0.86, p < .001) and adjusted (AOR = 0.77, 95% CI: 0.62, 0.97, p < .05). The positive dimension showed a marginally significant impact on RBD behavior in the unadjusted model (ORu = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.24, p = .056), which became significant after adjusting for the covariates (AOR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.00, 1.26, p < .05). Further, Independent Sample T-test and Pearson Chi-square test revealed that RBD intention (M ± SD: 4.72 ± 0.55 vs. 4.19 ± 0.88, p < .001) and the prevalence of RBD behavior at 6-month follow-up (26.1% vs. 19.9%, p < .05) was significantly higher among donors with higher scores of SRS-RBD (cut-off point 20 or above) than that with lower scores (lower than 20) (see Table 5). The findings indicate that SRS-RBD and its cut-off score were effective in predicting RBD. A satisfactory predictive validity of SRS-RBD was exhibited.

Discussion

Based on SCT and RLM, the current study developed and validated a 6-item self-report measurement of self-reinforcement for blood re-donation. Based on a systematic scale development process, the SRS-RBD items demonstrated good reliability, construct validity and criteria validity. The two-factor structure of self-reinforcement reflected donors’ motivations and barriers to RBD. Intervention programs aiming at promoting RBD may seek to promote positive experiences and minimize negative experiences. The present study also filled an important gap in the lack of self-reinforcement measurement in RBD and established a cut-off score for the newly developed measurement to determine potential repeat blood donors within the next year.

The present study identified two factors of SRS-RBD: positive experiences and negative experiences, which were respectively associated with increased and decreased levels of BD satisfaction, donation motivation, affective attitude, and re-donation intention. Positive experiences in past donations (self-reinforcement) may enhance donors’ positive attitudes toward BD, thereby increasing their intention and likelihood of RBD. In contrast, the negative experiences dimension of SRS-RBD was associated with lower RBD intention and predicted less RBD behavior. Negative experiences (punishment) may trigger threat appraisal, leading donors to reduce their intention for subsequent behavior to protect themselves. This finding is consistent with PMT [60, 61] and empirical studies indicating that higher negative experiences or emotions (e.g., anxiety, discomfort) were associated with decreased RBD intention and behavior [e.g., 15, 20, 25, 62]. Therefore, it was conceivable that self-reinforcement and punishment were associated with both RBD intention and behavior. Also, the overall SRS-RBD was found to significantly predict actual RBD behavior. The findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that past positive and negative experiences (e.g., donation satisfaction, adverse events, discomfort) were respectively associated with increased and decreased RBD [e.g., 18, 19, 20]. Self-evaluative responses that increase RBD include pride and satisfaction, while the response that decreases RBD is discomfort. This supports findings that donation satisfaction was associated with increased retention intention through improved attitude, while vasovagal reactions (a form of discomfort) were related to reduced retention intention through decreased attitude [21]. Similarly, a previous study reported that pride had a stronger association with RBD than first-time donation [22].

It is novel that the present study uses several common emotions and experiences as reinforcements of RBD (including intention and behavior) among blood donors in China. According to the cognitive-experiential-self-theory [63], one’s behavior is guided by both past experience and conscious appraisal of events. The functioning of past experience is called an experiential system, and this experiential system cannot function without affect/emotions [63]. Positive and negative affect/emotions thus influence schemas (a cognitive framework for organizing and interpreting information) and motivations for repeating behavior. Previous studies indicated that affect could be seen as a form of “sensation-based” reinforcement that increases behavior/intention associated with positive past outcomes while decreasing that with less positive outcomes [64, 65]. Moreover, one’s cognitions and outcome expectations of re-donation can be influenced by affect [63]. Feelings in BD have been indicated to be more associated with one’s overall attitude towards BD than beliefs [17]. Researchers have observed that prosocial behavior leads to positive emotions, such as happiness, which in turn reinforce prosocial behavior [66]. The findings of a meta-analysis study revealed that happiness predicted RBD [18], whereas the results of cross-sectional and longitudinal research found that unpleasant and anxious experiences in the past reduced RBD [24, 25, 67].

This study approached RBD from a reinforcement perspective, while our earlier work [41] explored it through outcome expectations, thereby considering both experiential and analytical aspects of decision-making. The findings align with dual-process theories of decision-making [63, 68–70]. For instance, the cognitive-experiential-self-theory [63] suggests that one’s behavior is influenced by both past experiences and conscious evaluations. The experiential systems guide individuals to seek pleasurable outcomes and avoid unpleasurable ones. In this context, reinforcement reflects the affective/emotional and experiential aspects of BD, whereas outcome expectations reflect pre-behavioral cognitive evaluations of potential outcomes. Together, these represent dual pathways—intuitive/emotion-based and analytical/thought-based—that donors may engage in when deciding whether to re-donate.

The current study has significant implications. Firstly, the concept of reinforcement is established and defined in the context of RBD, providing a novel perspective for understanding its influencing factors. Second, future empirical research will benefit from the newly developed SRS-RBD, as a lack of existing scale has hindered research about personal antecedents and interventions to promote RBD. Third, the SRS-RBD extends our understanding of self-reinforcement in RBD by focusing on emotional and self-evaluative responses. Interventions aimed at increasing positive emotions/experiences and decreasing negative ones during and after BD may be beneficial. Forth, the SRS-RBD can identify donor profiles that may be overlooked by the outcome expectation scale (OES). Five negative cases of having an intention to re-donate, which were falsely identified by the OES, were correctly identified as true-negative cases by SRS-RBD. Conversely, four true cases missed by the OES were accurately identified by the SRS-RBD. Therefore, SRS-RBD can serve as a valuable complement to the OES in cases where the purpose is to identify as many potential donors as possible during recruitment efforts. Fifth, the proposed cut-off score for the SRS-RBD can help blood centers identify donors who are more likely and less likely to return and personalize follow-up strategies accordingly. The scale can guide blood centers to prioritize interventions enhancing positive donation experiences (e.g., appreciation messages, donor recognition) and mitigating negative ones (e.g., reducing discomfort or anxiety through education and on-site support), ultimately aiding donor retention. It can also be used to evaluate the effectiveness of donor engagement and retention initiatives. These applications are currently being considered by BCZJP, and future studies may explore and optimize their implementation.

Several limitations exist in the present study. First, there may be response bias and inflated correlations between the factors and the validity measures since self-report measures were used. Future research would benefit from incorporating more objective indicators (e.g., using actual appointment bookings as a measure of RBD intention). Second, the assessment of the reliability of the SRS-RBD with the cross-sectional data were limited. Test-retest reliability should be examined with a longitudinal study design to provide stronger evidence for the scale’s reliability. Third, since EFA and CFA were performed on samples from different populations, which may limit the generalizability of the identified factor structures. Future studies can apply EFA and CFA to the same sample or test the scale in more diverse populations to confirm its structural validity. Lastly, a lack of other empirical measures of self-reinforcement weakens the validity of the SRS-RBD. We expected self-reinforcement to be positively correlated with donation satisfaction, RBD intention/behavior and motivation, and the affective attitude of donation, and negatively with trait-state anxiety based on its conception. It may still be necessary to evaluate the total score and subscale performance across various populations and contexts.

Conclusion

A theory-based scale measuring self-reinforcement for RBD was developed and validated. The SRS-RBD consists of two dimensions: positive experiences and negative experiences, which respectively reflect the motives and barriers to RBD. The scale demonstrated good reliability and validity and can be utilized in both research and practice to predict and promote RBD. Interventions aiming to increase positive experiences and decrease negative experiences of self-reinforcement may be effective in fostering RBD. Furthermore, a preliminary cut-off score for the SRS-RBD has been proposed. Donors with total scores above 20 may be considered more likely to engage in RBD, offering practical value for blood donor retention strategies.
