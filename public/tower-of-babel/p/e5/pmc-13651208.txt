The effect of expressive writing intervention for patients with reproductive trauma on anxiety, depression and stress: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Xiaoyu Wang, Mengye Yang, Yan Zhang, Yan Zhang, Xianwen Li, Ling Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReproductive trauma refers to traumatic experiences related to adverse reproductive events, particularly preterm birth, infertility and fetal loss. These experiences negatively impact women’s physical health and often lead to long-lasting psychological burdens such as anxiety, depression, and stress. Expressive writing intervention (EWI) is a positive psychological tool characterized by its universality and portability. It has shown beneficial effects in populations with chronic illnesses. Recently, EWI has been applied to those experiencing reproductive trauma. However, the specific efficacy of EWI for patients with reproductive trauma on anxiety, depression and stress is not yet fully ascertained. The aim of this study was to assess the effectiveness of EWI as a psychological intervention for women with reproductive trauma.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. Six databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane, Web of Science, CINAHL and PsycINFO, were searched from inception to June 8, 2026 for eligible studies. Two authors independently conducted literature screening, data extraction, and quality assessment according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager software. Prespecified subgroup analyses examined the effects of intervention duration, control conditions, and population type. Exploratory meta-regression analyses were used to assess the association between intervention dose and outcomes. And publication bias and sensitivity analyses were conducted where appropriate. We also employed TSA to assess evidence conclusiveness, and certainty of evidence was rated via GRADE.ResultsTen eligible studies with 838 participants were included in the final data set. Pooled analysis showed that EWI was not effective in reducing post-intervention anxiety and stress, whereas post-intervention depression showed a marginal effect (SMD = -0.35, 95% CI: -0.71 to -0.00, P = 0.05). In contrast, follow-up depression demonstrated a statistically significant effect (SMD = -0.82, 95% CI: -1.40 to -0.24, P = 0.006). Subgroup analyses showed that EWI may be effective in patients who have experienced preterm birth or fetal loss, and in studies with no-writing control conditions. Additionally, exploratory meta-regression analysis showed a potential positive association between intervention dose and improvement in follow-up stress outcomes. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of studies.ConclusionOur findings show that EWI may be beneficial in certain subgroups, such as women who have experienced fetal loss or preterm birth. However, the certainty of evidence remains limited, indicating that the current evidence is insufficient and that the effects of EWI across outcomes remains uncertain. Future studies should validate the effects of EWI through more high-quality randomized controlled trials.Trial registrationThe protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database with the registration ID CRD42024582557.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09607-y.Keywords: Reproductive trauma, Expressive writing, Mental health, Psychotherapy

Background

Reproductive trauma refers to any traumatic experiences associated with adverse reproductive events, which often have a profound impact on the entire family, especially on women [1]. Many women repeatedly experience emotional and physical trauma for months or even years due to the adverse reproductive events, which leads to lasting psychological burdens such as anxiety, depression, and stress [1]. While various forms of reproductive trauma exist, infertility, miscarriage, stillbirth, fetal distress during pregnancy or delivery, and premature birth remain the most extensively studied in the current literature [2]. A systematic review and meta-analysis involving 53,300 women with infertility summarized the results of 44 studies, indicating that the global prevalence rates of generalized anxiety disorder, depression, and stress among infertile patients are 13.3%, 31.6%, and 78.8%, respectively [3]. S et al. reported that miscarriage not only results in physiological consequences, but also significantly increases the risks of anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and even suicide [4]. A multicenter prospective cohort study involving 537 women who experienced miscarriage found that 18% met the diagnostic criteria for PTSD nine months after pregnancy loss, 17% exhibited moderate to severe anxiety, and 6% suffered from moderate to severe depression [5]. Furthermore, the incidence of postpartum depression among preterm women is as high as 40%, significantly exceeding that of mothers of term infants [6]. These findings underscore the substantial psychological burden faced by women following adverse reproductive events. Given the chronic nature of infertility and certain adverse reproductive events [1], clinical practitioners must gain a deeper understanding of this profound trauma and focus on how to effectively support this complex and expanding population over the long term. In this context, it is essential to evaluate existing psychological interventions and explore the suitable options that can better meet the needs of these patients.

Among the psychological interventions available for women who have experienced reproductive trauma, commonly employed methods include group cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and mindfulness-based therapies [7–9]. However, these interventions typically necessitate the involvement of professional psychologists and healthcare personnel, requiring considerable time and effort for preparation and implementation. Additionally, patients are often required to make frequent visits to healthcare facilities, which can be exceedingly exhausting [7]. Given the rising prevalence of anxiety, depression, and stress within this population [1], there is an urgent need to explore more economical and accessible interventions that can be autonomously implemented by the patients themselves.

Expressive Writing Intervention (EWI) is a low-cost, self-managed psychological intervention that has the potential for long-term application, particularly suited to the needs of patients with reproductive trauma. EWI encourages individuals to articulate their emotions and thoughts regarding traumatic events in written form, thereby promoting emotional regulation and psychological relief [10, 11]. In a typical EWI, participants are instructed to write daily for 3 to 5 consecutive days, with each session lasting 15 to 30 min, or to write weekly for 3 to 5 weeks [11]. And EWI typically focuses on specific topics; however, it does not require participants to structure their writing in any particular format, nor does it impose restrictions on grammar or logic [11]. Currently, EWI is widely employed among patients with chronic illnesses, cancer patients, and individuals who are bereaved, resulting in significant psychological improvements [12]. And there is a growing focus on its specific effects on patients with reproductive trauma, who often suffer from anxiety, depression, and stress [1].

However, the findings of related studies in this population reveal some controversies. For instance, Rabiepoor et al. reported that EWI significantly alleviates depression in mothers of preterm infants in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) [13]. Similarly, Qian found that applying EWI to patients who experienced fetal loss produced immediate effects, reducing symptoms of PTSD and helping patients swiftly adjust their emotional state [14]. While Renzi et al. indicated that EWI does not lead to significant improvements in anxiety and depression outcomes among infertile patients [15]. These discrepancies underscore the inconsistencies across studies. Currently, no systematic review has yet integrated the effects of EWI in patients with reproductive trauma, and the specific efficacy remains unclear.

This study aims to systematically assess both the post-intervention and longer-term follow-up effects of EWI on anxiety, depression, and stress in patients with reproductive trauma, defined as women who have experienced adverse reproductive events, including infertility, fetal loss, stillbirth, fetal distress during pregnancy or delivery, and preterm birth [2]. And the study also seeks to clarify how the effects of EWI vary across different intervention designs and specific types of reproductive trauma. We hope to provide clearer guidance for future clinical applications and studies, and to offer scientific evidence for optimizing psychological health intervention strategies for this population.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The study followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [16], and the protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database with the registration ID CRD42024582557.

Search strategy

In this study, searches were conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, and PsycINFO. A combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms was employed to identify all relevant studies from the inception of each database until June 8, 2026. The language was restricted to English or Chinese. And only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included, with the exact search terms detailed in Additional Table 1. Furthermore, we manually reviewed the reference lists of the included RCTs to uncover additional potential studies [17]. We used Zotero 7 software to remove repetitive articles. The search process was independently conducted by two authors (Wang Xiaoyu and Yang Mengye).

Eligibility criteria

Intervention

EWI is a psychosocial intervention aimed at promoting mental and physical health [10]. This study was not limited to typical EWI intervention doses and strategies. Narrative writing, which is derived from EWI, allows participants to describe their traumatic events or emotions in a narrative format [18], and was also included in this study.

Studies design

Only RCTs were deemed eligible for inclusion.

Population

This study included patients with reproductive trauma, which refers to any traumatic experiences associated with adverse reproductive events. Previous studies have reported that such events commonly include infertility, fetal loss, stillbirth, fetal distress during pregnancy or delivery, and preterm birth [2].

Control

The study included either blank control groups or neutral writing groups. In neutral writing, participants were instructed to describe events or scenes with maximal objectivity, avoiding subjective interpretation or emotional language [19]. For example, participants were asked to describe or plan daily activities in a neutral, emotionless tone [19].

Outcome measure

The primary outcomes of this study were anxiety, depression, and stress, extracted at post-intervention and at follow-up when available, to account for the potential delayed or longer-term effects of EWI [20, 21]. According to the American Psychiatric Association (APA) [22], anxiety is an emotion characterized by apprehension and somatic symptoms of tension. Depression is defined as a negative affective state, ranging from unhappiness and discontent to extreme feelings of sadness, pessimism, and despondency [22]. And stress is the physiological or psychological response to internal or external stressors, often manifested by increased negative emotions, palpitations, and other affective or behavioral changes [22]. Studies reporting these outcomes were included only if they employed widely recognized and psychometrically validated instruments (e.g., BDI-II, SCL-90, and EPDS for depression; STAI, SCL-90, and DASS for anxiety; PSS, COMPI, and ISS for stress).

Study selection

Two independent authors (Wang Xiaoyu and Yang Mengye) screened all studies based on eligibility criteria through a two-step process. In the first step, studies were screened based on titles, abstracts, and keywords. In the second step, full-text screening was performed for studies that either appeared to meet the inclusion criteria or for which eligibility was unclear. In cases of uncertainty or disagreement regarding the inclusion of a study, a third author (Jiang Ling) was consulted until a consensus was reached.

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted using Excel spreadsheets. A standardized data extraction tool, developed by one author (Yan Zhang), was used to collect data for quality assessment and content analysis from the included studies. Data extraction was conducted independently by two authors (Wang Xiaoyu and Yang Mengye) and subsequently double-checked to ensure accuracy and consistency. The extracted data included the following: study setting (e.g., publication year, country, sample size); study population and baseline characteristics; intervention and control conditions (e.g., frequency and duration); outcome measures and their timing; and risk of bias assessments. Any discrepancies in the extracted data were resolved through discussion or arbitration by the third author (Ling Jiang). For missing data, at least two direct requests were made to the study authors to obtain the necessary information.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently assessed by two authors (Xiaoyu Wang and Mengye Yang) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool 2.0 [23]. This tool evaluates potential sources of bias across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was classified as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” of bias in accordance with the tool’s predefined criteria. Any disagreements between authors were resolved through discussion with the third author (Ling Jiang) until a consensus was reached.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) software (Version 5.4.1, The Nordic Cochrane Centre). Effect sizes for post-intervention and follow-up outcomes were synthesized, and corresponding figures were generated. As outcomes were continuous and assessed using different measurement instruments across studies, standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated to estimate pooled effects [24]. When necessary, corresponding authors were contacted to obtain missing data, including mean values and standard deviations (SDs).

Heterogeneity and sensitivity

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic in accordance with the Cochrane’s recommendations [25]. A fixed-effect model was applied when P > 0.10 or I² < 50%, indicating low heterogeneity; otherwise, a random-effects model was used [26]. Effect sizes were interpreted following Cohen’s guidelines, with values of 0.20, 0.50, and 0.80 corresponding to small, moderate, and large effects, respectively [27]. And sensitivity analysis were performed using RevMan software (Version 5.4.1) by sequentially excluding individual studies to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates and to identify potential sources of heterogeneity.

Subgroup analysis

Given the presence of potential heterogeneity across studies, subgroup analysis were further conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and effect modifiers. The analysis examined predefined study characteristics, including intervention duration (≤ 3 days vs. > 3 days), control conditions (neutral writing vs. no writing), and population type among individuals experiencing reproductive trauma (infertility, preterm birth, and fetal loss). Due to the limited number of included studies, these findings are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution. Especially, the follow-up outcomes are reported for descriptive purposes only.

Exploratory meta-regression analysis

Given the limited number of included studies (fewer than 10 for each outcome), meta-regression was performed only as an exploratory supplement and are not intended to yield primary conclusions, with full acknowledgment of the risks of limited statistical power and model overfitting [28]. Following previous literature [29], meta-regression was conducted only for outcomes with at least four included studies. Analysis were performed using the metafor package in R (version 4.4.3) to examine the association between intervention dose (calculated as number of sessions × duration per session, in minutes) and effect sizes.

Trial sequential analysis

For the primary outcomes, trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed to assess the reliability of the cumulative evidence using TSA Viewer software (version 0.9.5.5 beta; Copenhagen Trial Unit, Centre for Clinical Intervention Research, Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark), available at http://www.ctu.dk/tsa [30]. TSA was used to estimate the required information size (RIS), defined as the number of participants required to confirm or reject a specified intervention effect in a meta-analysis, and to determine whether the cumulative Z-curve crossed the relevant trial sequential monitoring boundaries [31]. In this analysis, we estimated the RIS with a two-sided type I error rate of 5% and a power (type II error rate) of 80% [32].

Publication bias and GRADE evidence assessment

According to the Cochrane Handbook [33], funnel plots and Egger’s test are generally recommended when a meta-analysis includes at least 10 studies due to limited power with fewer studies. Considering the limited number of studies available for the outcomes in the present study, these methods were conducted solely as exploratory analysis, consistent with previous studies [34, 35], to provide a preliminary assessment of potential publication bias. In this study, funnel plots and Egger’s test were conducted in R (v4.4.3) using the metafor and meta packages for outcomes with at least four included studies.

The overall certainty of the evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [36]. It includes five considerations: risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, indirectness, and publication bias [37]. The certainty of the evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Following GRADE recommendations, a Summary of Findings table was generated using GRADEpro GDT software (https://www.gradepro.org/).

Results

Study selection

Study selection is illustrated in Fig. 1. A total of 4,034 relevant articles were identified, including 322 from PubMed, 1720 from Web of Science database, 457 from Embase, 1038 from Cochrane Library, 452 from PsycINFO, and 45 from CINAHL. In addition, one additional article was identified through manual screening of reference lists in relevant studies. Therefore, 4,035 studies were included. After removing duplicates using Zotero 7 software, 591 records were excluded. Two authors (Wang Xiaoyu and Yang Mengye) independently screened the titles and abstracts of 3,444 articles, excluding 3,397 irrelevant ones. Subsequently, 47 articles were selected for full-text review, of which 10 articles met the eligibility criteria for inclusion in the meta-analysis.

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 10 included studies. All studies were RCTs published in English from database inception to June 8, 2026, and were conducted in Denmark (n = 2), Iran (n = 4), Switzerland (n = 1), Greece (n = 1), China (n = 1), and Italy (n = 1). The studies included a total of 838 participants, with sample sizes ranging from 31 to 141. Study populations comprised women with infertility (n = 6), women following preterm birth (n = 3), and women who undergo pregnancy termination due to fetal loss (n = 1). All studies reported post-intervention outcomes for at least one of the following psychological variables: anxiety, depression, or stress. And six studies reported follow-up outcome data. Meta-analysis of post-intervention and follow-up outcomes and sensitivity analyses

Post-intervention outcomes

Anxiety

Figure 4 presents the pooled effects of EWI on post-intervention mental health outcomes. Five studies evaluated the impact of EWI on anxiety, involving a total of 494 participants. The results showed that the effect of EWI on anxiety was not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4A). And it’s important to note that there was substantial statistical heterogeneity across the included RCTs (I2 = 80%; P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis excluding Heidari et al. [38]demonstrated that the findings were robust, with heterogeneity substantially reduced under a fixed-effects model (I² = 19%) (Appendix Table 2).

Forest plots showing the pooled effects of EWI on post-intervention anxiety (A), depression (B), stress (C), and infertility-related stress (D)

Depression

The depression outcome was derived from pooled data from six RCTs involving 546 participants. EWI demonstrated a borderline significant reduction in participants’ depression scores (SMD = -0.35, 95% CI: -0.71 to -0.00, P = 0.05) (Fig. 4B). Similar to the anxiety outcome, substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed among the included RCTs (I² = 76%, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analysis further showed that exclusion of the studies by Frederiksen et al. or Renzi et al. [15, 39] resulted in statistically significant pooled effects (SMD = -0.47, 95% CI: -0.80 to -0.13, P = 0.007; SMD = -0.43, 95% CI: −0.83 to − 0.02, P = 0.04, respectively). Meanwhile, heterogeneity remained moderate to high across all sensitivity analyses (I² ranging from 64% to 81%) (Appendix Table 2).

Stress and infertility-related stress

The stress and infertility-related stress outcomes included eight and four studies, involving 645 and 332 participants, respectively. Meta-analysis showed that EWI had no statistically significant effects on stress or infertility-related stress (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4C and D). Sensitivity analysis showed that sequential removal of individual studies did not substantially alter the direction or statistical significance of the pooled estimates (Appendix Table 2), indicating that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall results.

Follow-up outcomes

Anxiety

Two studies evaluated the impact of EWI on anxiety at follow-up, involving a total of 239 participants. The pooled analysis showed that EWI did not significantly reduce anxiety compared with the control condition (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5A). However, only two studies were included in the analysis, and substantial heterogeneity was observed (I² = 80%, P < 0.001), which means the robustness of this finding should be interpreted with caution.

Forest plots of the meta-analysis for follow-up outcomes, including anxiety (A), depression (B), stress (C), and infertility-related stress (D)

Depression

The depression outcome was derived from four RCTs involving 384 participants. The pooled results demonstrated that EWI significantly reduced depression scores at follow-up (SMD = -0.82, 95% CI: -1.40 to -0.24, P = 0.006) (Fig. 5B). However, considerable heterogeneity was detected across studies (I² = 86%, P < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses showed that exclusion of the study by Frederiksen et al. [39]reduced heterogeneity (I² decreased from 86% to 57%), while the pooled effect remained statistically significant (SMD = -1.07, 95% CI: -1.48 to -0.65, P < 0.001) (Appendix Table 2). In contrast, removal of the study by Heidari et al. [38]rendered the pooled effect non-significant (SMD = -0.75, 95% CI: -1.54 to 0.04, P = 0.06), suggesting that the overall findings may be sensitive to the inclusion of individual studies.

Stress and infertility-related stress

Five studies involving 393 participants reported follow-up stress outcomes, and two studies involving 168 participants reported infertility-related stress outcomes. The pooled analysis showed that EWI also did not significantly reduce stress levels or infertility-related stress (P > 0.05) (Fig. 5C and D). Sensitivity analysis showed that, for stress outcome, exclusion of the study by Rabiepoor et al. [13]substantially reduced heterogeneity (I² decreased from 80% to 6%). Furthermore, after removal of the study by Frederiksen et al. [39], the pooled effect became statistically significant (SMD = -0.60, 95% CI: -1.08 to -0.13, P = 0.01) (Appendix Table 2). While for infertility-related stress, sensitivity analysis showed that exclusion of either study did not change the statistical significance of the pooled estimates (Appendix Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

Intervention duration subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis were conducted to investigate whether the effects of EWI on anxiety, depression, and stress varied according to intervention duration, control conditions, and population type among individuals with reproductive trauma at both post-intervention and follow-up assessments (Table 2 and Additional Table 3). At post-intervention, stratified analysis by intervention duration did not identify intervention duration as a source of heterogeneity for anxiety, depression, or stress (Table 2 and Additional Fig. 1). At follow-up, limited data were available. Although significant reductions in depression and stress were observed in the 3-day subgroup (P < 0.001), each subgroup was represented by only a single study (Additional Table 3 and Additional Fig. 4). These findings should be interpreted with caution.

Subgroup analysis of post-intervention outcomes by intervention duration, control conditions, and population type in individuals experiencing reproductive trauma

Control conditions subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis based on control conditions showed that EWI significantly reduced post-intervention depression compared with no-writing controls (SMD = -0.35, 95% CI: -0.67 to -0.02, P = 0.04, I² = 50%), whereas no significant effects were observed in the neutral-writing control group (P > 0.05). No significant effects on post-intervention anxiety or stress were observed in either control-condition subgroup (P > 0.05) (Table 2 and Additional Fig. 2). And at follow-up, significant improvements in depression and stress were observed only in studies using no-writing controls (Depression: SMD = -1.07, 95% CI: -1.84 to -0.30, P = 0.007, I² = 78%; Stress: SMD = -0.70, 95% CI: -1.25 to -0.14, P = 0.01, I² = 75%), whereas studies employing neutral-writing controls showed no significant effects for either outcome (P > 0.05) (Additional Table 3 and Additional Fig. 5).

Population type subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis stratified by population type showed significant reductions in depression among women who experienced preterm birth at both post-intervention and follow-up assessments (post-intervention: SMD = -0.48, 95% CI: -0.95 to -0.01, P = 0.05, I² = 52%; follow-up: SMD = -1.07, 95% CI: -1.84 to -0.30, p = 0.007, I² = 78%) (Table 2 and Additional Table 3, Additional Fig. 3 and Additional Fig. 6). In contrast, no significant effects were observed in women with infertility (P > 0.05). For post-intervention stress, EWI significantly reduced stress only among women who experienced fetal loss (SMD = -0.47, 95% CI: -0.89 to -0.05, p = 0.03), although this estimate was based on a single study (Table 2 and Additional Fig. 3). No significant effects were observed in women with infertility or preterm birth (P > 0.05). At follow-up, a significant effect was observed only in women with preterm birth (SMD = -0.84, 95% CI: -1.65 to -0.04, p = 0.04), with no significant effects in the other subgroups (P > 0.05) (Additional Table 3 and Additional Fig. 6).

Exploratory meta-regression analysis

Given the limited number of included studies, all meta-regression analysis were exploratory (Additional Fig. 7). For post-intervention outcomes, intervention dose was not significantly associated with anxiety (P = 0.67, R² = 0%) or depression (P = 0.38, R² = 0%). For stress, no statistically significant moderating effect was observed (P = 0.14), although intervention dose accounted for 24.02% of the between-study variance (R² = 24.02%).

For follow-up outcomes, intervention dose was not statistically significant for depression (P = 0.12), but its R2 value of 36.69% suggests a potential explanatory role on variance. In contrast, intervention dose was significantly associated with follow-up stress outcomes (P = 0.0021, R² = 84.40%), showing that it may be an important factor influencing the intervention effect.

Trial sequential analysis

Post-intervention outcomes

In Fig. 6, TSA was conducted for post-intervention anxiety, depression, and stress. The RIS was 7212 for anxiety (Fig. 6A), 1559 for depression (Fig. 6B), and 7202 for stress (Fig. 6C). For all three outcomes, the cumulative Z-curves neither crossed the trial sequential monitoring boundaries nor reached the RIS lines. These findings showed that the current evidence remains inconclusive regarding the immediate post-intervention effects of EWI on anxiety, depression, and stress in individuals experiencing reproductive trauma.

Trial sequential analysis (TSA) of the immediate post-intervention effects of EWI on (A) anxiety, B depression, and (C) stress. The x-axis shows the cumulative sample size, and the y-axis shows the cumulative Z-score. The required information size (RIS) was calculated as 7212 for anxiety, 1559 for depression, and 7202 for stress using a two-sided type I error of 5% and a power of 80%, with empirical estimates of mean differences and variances and model variance-based heterogeneity correction. The cumulative Z-curve (blue line) represents the accumulated intervention effect. The O’Brien–Fleming trial sequential monitoring boundaries account for random errors arising from repeated significance testing and heterogeneity among studies. A Z-curve crossing the monitoring boundary indicates sufficient evidence for a firm conclusion. Conversely, if the Z-curve neither crosses the monitoring boundary nor reaches the RIS, the current evidence remains inconclusive and additional adequately powered trials are required

Follow-up outcomes

For follow-up depression, TSA showed that the Z-curve crossed both the conventional significance boundary and the trial sequential monitoring boundary before reaching the RIS line (Fig. 7B). The accumulated sample size (n = 384) nearly reached the RIS, showing that the available evidence seems to be sufficient to support a beneficial effect of EWI on depression. And the current sample size appears sufficient for a reliable effect estimate. However, for follow-up anxiety and stress, the accumulated sample sizes (anxiety: n = 239; stress: n = 393) did not reach the RIS lines (Anxiety: RIS = 1,304; Stress: RIS = 819) (Fig. 7A and C). The cumulative Z-curves did not cross either the conventional significance boundaries or the trial sequential monitoring boundaries, indicating that the current evidence is insufficient to confirm or refute an intervention effect on anxiety or stress.

Publication bias and certainty of evidence (GRADE)

Given the limited number of studies included, the assessment of publication bias was exploratory. Visual inspection of the funnel plots showed slight asymmetry for some primary outcomes (Additional Fig. 8), whereas Egger’s test did not show significant evidence of publication bias (all P > 0.05; Additional Table 4). Therefore, publication bias cannot be ruled out, but the available evidence is insufficient to confirm its presence.

The certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for post-intervention stress and follow-up depression. For the remaining outcomes, the certainty of evidence was low or very low, mainly due to inconsistency and imprecision. Full details, including the rationale for downgrading, are provided in the GRADE Summary of Findings (Table 3).

Discussion

This meta-analysis included 10 studies and assessed both post-intervention and follow-up effects of EWI on anxiety, depression, and stress in patients with reproductive trauma. The main results showed that EWI may exert a modest beneficial effect on depression, whereas its effects on anxiety and stress were not statistically significant. Subgroup analyses suggested that control conditions (e.g., no-writing) and the type of reproductive trauma (e.g., preterm birth) may modify the efficacy of EWI. And exploratory meta-regression further showed that intervention dose may influence improvements in follow-up stress outcomes. However, given the limited number of included studies, all results should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, serving as a foundation for future high-quality studies. TSA confirmed that the current evidence remains insufficient, and the overall certainty of evidence was rated low to moderate, indicating that these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Compared with post-intervention outcomes, follow-up results seem to show a more beneficial trend of EWI for patients with reproductive trauma. This finding is consistent with the temporal characteristics of EWI effects reported in previous studies [20, 21, 35, 40]. Specifically, the effects of EWI may not emerge immediately, but instead gradually strengthen over time. A network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that EWI may initially elicit stronger negative emotional responses in the short term, likely due to the sustained expression of negative emotions [40]. However, over time, patients may achieve more complete emotional expression and psychological integration through the writing process, with effects gradually emerging, and ultimately leading to significant reductions in anxiety and depression [40]. Similarly, evidence has demonstrated that the effects of EWI may persist for up to 3 to 6 months after the intervention, further supporting its potential long-term psychological benefits [20, 21].

The substantial heterogeneity observed across the included studies warrants careful consideration. On the one hand, this heterogeneity may be attributed to the limited number of available studies; on the other hand, it may reflect differences in population types, control conditions, and intervention dosages. Patients who have experienced preterm birth or fetal loss tend to encounter sudden, acute stressors [41, 42]. EWI provides an effective outlet for emotional expression, and through cognitive reframing, enables this population to alleviate negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and stress [1, 11]. And our study also showed that, for patients who experienced preterm birth or fetal loss, EWI may partially alleviate depression and stress. However, no significant effects were observed among infertile women, which may be attributable to the unique and more complex psychological characteristics of this population [43]. Infertile women often undergo an extended period of fertility treatment, which itself constitutes a chronic stressor [43], and results in persistent and cumulative psychological distres [43, 44]. Additionally, evidence from large cohort studies has revealed that common adverse obstetric outcomes following in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET), such as preterm birth, dystocia, and stillbirth, occur at rates of 47.8%, 28.1%, and 2.8%, respectively, all significantly higher than those observed in naturally conceived pregnancies [45]. These findings show that infertile women may experience additional reproductive trauma, further exacerbating their psychological burden. Given these considerations, EWI with lower dosage or studies with short-term follow-up may not yield significant effects among infertile patients. As the number of relevant studies is currently limited, with only 1 or 2 studies focusing on certain subgroups, caution should be exercised in interpreting these results.

Control conditions could constitute a potential source of heterogeneity in EWI effects. In this study, when a no-writing control condition was used, EWI showed improvements in post-intervention depression, with similar effects observed for depression and stress at follow-up. In contrast, when the control condition was neutral writing, the effects of EWI did not reach statistical significance. This discrepancy may show that the intensity of control conditions could influence effect size estimation. In fact, in many EWI studies, reporting daily life activities was typically considered a ‘neutral’ topic [15]. However, in clinical practice, truly neutral writing is difficult to achieve, particularly in stressful contexts [15]. Previous studies have shown that such neutral writing can produce effects comparable to expressive writing, because patients with reproductive trauma largely wrote about the treatment or experience, both representing a focus in their lives [46]. Similarly, Harvey and Farrell also found no differences between emotional and neutral writing, whereas differences were observed between writing and no-writing groups [47]. And researchers hypothesised that writing, regardless of topic, may benefit individuals experiencing stressors. However, it should be noted that, in this study, the number of available studies was limited, and these findings require further validation in larger samples.

Regarding the intervention dosage of EWI, the current literature is inconsistent and lacks a standardized protocol. Empirical evidence have shown that higher-dose interventions (i.e., more frequent writing sessions and longer session durations) may yield more substantial effects [48]. Similarly, a meta-analysis of pregnancy-related psychological issues reported that EWI lasting three or more days were more effective than shorter interventions [35]. In the present study, exploratory meta-regression also showed that follow-up stress decreased as intervention dosage increased. However, the small number of included studies limits the robustness of this finding, and it should be interpreted as exploratory. Meanwhile, some studies reported that different populations may respond differently to EWI dosage, indicating that the minimal effective dose may vary across groups [49]. A systematic review and meta-analysis on the prevention of childbirth-related post-traumatic stress disorder found that healthy postpartum participants who engaged in EWI just once for 10 to 15 min experienced significant improvements in stress [50]. While for patients who have experienced preterm births, a higher intervention dose of EWI may be required to achieve significant effects on stress and depression [50]. These findings show that the optimal EWI dosage may be moderated by individuals’ baseline stress levels and clinical context. Future studies should focus on personalized intervention dosages, exploring the optimal dose for generating the quickest effects, as well as the minimal dose that can produce enduring outcomes in targeted populations.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, the study focused on patients with reproductive trauma. This population often experiences substantial emotional burden, yet evidence regarding low-cost and accessible psychological interventions for this group remains limited. This study evaluated the effects of EWI, which possesses these characteristics, on anxiety, depression, and stress. The findings may provide preliminary insights into the potential applicability of EWI in this population and may serve as a hypothesis-generating basis for future higher-quality studies. Second, we restricted inclusion to randomized controlled trials and fully explored heterogeneity by prespecified subgroup analysis and exploratory meta-regressions. In addition, TSA was applied to assess the robustness of cumulative evidence and control for random errors, and the GRADE approach was used to evaluate the certainty of evidence, yielding a relatively structured methodological framework. Third, both short-term post-intervention outcomes and long-term follow-up outcomes were analyzed, allowing a more comprehensive evaluation of the temporal effects of EWI.

At the same time, several limitations should be noted. First, the number of included studies was small, particularly for follow-up outcomes, limiting statistical power. In this context, supplementary analyses, including subgroup analyses, meta-regression analyses, TSA, and publication bias assessment, should be regarded as exploratory and interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Second, substantial heterogeneity was observed across the primary outcomes. To address this problem, subgroup and meta-regression analyses were conducted, while the limited number of available trials constrained our ability to fully account for this heterogeneity. Additionally, due to concerns regarding inconsistency and imprecision, the certainty of evidence across outcomes ranged from very low to moderate, which means the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further high-quality, large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

From a clinical practice perspective, the findings of this meta-analysis show that current evidence is insufficient to robustly support EWI as a universal intervention for reducing anxiety, depression, and stress among patients with reproductive trauma. Nevertheless, given its low cost, ease of implementation, and high feasibility, the potential practical value of EWI should not be overlooked in clinical settings, which are characterized by limited healthcare resources and high patient demand. Under standardized intervention protocols (e.g., optimization of intervention dosage tailored to target populations), EWI may help alleviate depression and stress to a certain extent, serving as an adjunctive psychological intervention with potential for sustained implementation in patients with reproductive trauma.

Future research should prioritize several directions. (1) Conduct larger, rigorously designed RCTs to improve the precision and stability of effect estimates. (2) Strengthen the assessment of long-term outcomes through longitudinal follow-up to further elucidate whether the effects of EWI exhibit delayed onset or sustained trajectories. (3) Establish an EWI group, a neutral writing group, and a no-writing control group, and further standardize the design and reporting of control conditions. In particular, writing topics, intervention dosage, and quality control criteria for neutral writing tasks should be clearly defined to enhance comparability across studies and to more accurately identify the specific effects of EWI. (4) Trial processes should be reported more consistently. Where possible, key methodological details (e.g., missing data handling strategies and analytic approaches) should be shared to enhance the reproducibility, transparency, and credibility of future evidence syntheses.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings showed that EWI may be beneficial in some subgroups, particularly among women who have experienced fetal loss or preterm birth, as well as in studies where the control condition involved no-writing interventions. And intervention dosage may also influence the effect of EWI. However, the certainty of evidence remains limited. Among all outcomes, only follow-up depression showed a potential beneficial effect (moderate evidence), whereas post-intervention stress showed no significant improvement (moderate evidence). The certainty of evidence for the remaining outcomes was low or very low, indicating that the current evidence is insufficient and that the effectiveness of EWI across outcomes remains uncertain. These observed effects should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should further validate the true effects of EWI through larger, methodologically rigorous randomized controlled trials.
