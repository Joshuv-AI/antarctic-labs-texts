Early-Life famine exposure and the risk of depression and anxiety in adulthood: evidence from the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC)
Authors: Hua Bai, Yandaijiu Wang, Yuhan Liu, Yuemei Feng, Yanjiao Wang, Fei Mi, Wei Zou, Junyi Liu, Xuehui Zhang, Qiong Meng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression and anxiety are among the most prevalent mental disorders globally and represent major public health challenges. The impact of early-life famine exposure on mental health has increasingly drawn attention. However, research on the association between early-life famine exposure and the risk of depression and anxiety in adulthood, especially across different ethnic backgrounds, remains scarce in worldwide.MethodsThe study is based on the baseline data from the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC), and includes 18,376 individuals who were born between 1939 and 1978 and experienced early life famine exposure. All participants underwent face-to-face interviews and physical examinations, and their anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the PHQ-2 and GAD-2 scales. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between early-life famine exposure and the risk of depression and anxiety in adulthood.ResultsThe study revealed that exposure to famine during childhood (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.33–2.05, p < 0.001) and adolescence (OR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.18–2.28, p = 0.003) was associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms in adulthood, especially among females ( childhood OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.47–2.39, p < 0.001; adolescence OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.36–2.81, p < 0.001). In the Han ethnic group, childhood famine exposure (OR = 1.92, 95% CI: 1.44–2.56, p < 0.001) was associated with a higher risk of depressive symptoms in later life. In the Yi ethnic group, fetal exposure to famine was associated with an increased risk of both depressive symptoms (OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.28–4.25, p = 0.005) and anxiety symptoms in adulthood (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.37–2.88, p < 0.001), whereas no associations were observed in the Bai ethnic group. Regarding anxiety symptoms, males exposed to famine during childhood (OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.33–0.85, p = 0.008) and adolescence (OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.13–0.78, p = 0.013) had a lower risk of developing anxiety symptoms in adulthood. Among the Yi population, fetal famine exposure was associated with a higher risk of adult anxiety symptoms (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.37–2.88, p < 0.001).ConclusionFamine exposure during childhood and adolescence is associated with altered risks of mental health disorders in adulthood, with patterns varying across ethnic groups. Early-life nutritional deprivation may exert long-term effects on mental health, and these associations appear to differ by gender and ethnicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07375-1.Keywords: Ethnicity, Early life, Famine exposure, Anxiety, Depression

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are among the most common mental disorders worldwide. According to the 2023 report by the World Health Organization and 2022 report by the Global Burden of Disease, approximately 280 million people globally suffer from, accounting for 3.8% of the world’s population, with 5% of adults being affected [1, 2]. In China, the number of depression rose from 34.4 million to 53.1 million during 1990 and 2021, marking a 54% increase [3] and imposing a significant global economic burden. Meanwhile, according to the most recent Global Burden of Disease study, the incidence of anxiety disorders increased by 47.19% during the recent 30-year cycle. Among 204 countries and regions, China reported the highest number of anxiety cases, followed by India, the United States, Brazil, and Indonesia [2].

The development of depression and anxiety is influenced by a complex interplay of biological, psychological, social, lifestyle, genetic, and epigenetic factors. The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis posits that early-life undernutrition not only impairs growth and development but also induces long-term biological adaptations that may permanently alter body structure and function. These changes can increase the risk of metabolic disorders in adulthood [3]. For instance, several studies have demonstrated associations between famine exposure and an elevated risk of metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [4, 5]. Recent research further suggests that the impact of famine exposure may extend beyond physical health, potentially exerting profound and lasting effects on mental health. A growing body of evidence indicates that prenatal famine exposure is associated with an increased risk of developing mental disorders later in life [6, 7]. However, population-based studies examining the broader mental health consequences of famine exposure remain limited.

It is known that significant variations in the prevalence of depression and anxiety across different cultures, regions, and countries [8–10], but known less whether ethnicity or national minority is also a susceptibility factor. Significant differences in the risk of depression and anxiety during pregnancy have been observed between Hispanic and non-Hispanic White populations [11]. That inspired us to think about whether ethnic differences alter the impact of famine exposure on depression and anxiety in a multi-ethnic region like China. Additionally, the Great Chinese Famine in 1959–1961 was one of the most devastating famines in modern history, estimated to have caused an excess mortality of 20 to 30 million people. This historical event provides a natural setting for studying the relationship between early-life famine exposure and long-term health outcomes in adulthood [12].

Previous research has demonstrated that childhood adversity, such as famine and war, is associated with sex-specific differences in health outcomes. Compared with men, women who experienced early-life nutritional deprivation have been found to be at higher risk of developing metabolic syndrome in adulthood and to suffer more long-term metabolic consequences [13]. Other studies have further shown that famine exposure during the fetal, childhood, or adolescent stages significantly increased the risk of hyperuricemia in women, whereas in men it was associated with a reduced risk [14]. With regard to mental health, some evidence suggests that sex may modify the association between early-life adverse exposures and adult psychological outcomes [15, 16]; however, the stage-specific effects of famine exposure on mental health across different developmental periods have not been well elucidated. Against the backdrop of gender-specific social roles in China, early-life famine—representing an extreme nutritional and psychological stressor—offers a unique opportunity to investigate sex differences in the long-term consequences of early adversity.

China has the largest number of ethnic minorities in the world. 56 ethnic minorities represent distinct genetic backgrounds, cultural traditions, social environments, and dietary habits. Due to the lack of large-scale multi-ethnic population cohorts in the past, the research on ethnic minority population health is very limited. The first ethnic minority cohort in China - the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC) - provides a unique perspective for exploring the racial differences in the impact of famine exposure on mental health. We aim to investigate Han, Bai, and Yi ethnic groups who experienced famine, analyzing the association between early-life famine exposure and the risk of depression and anxiety symptoms in adulthood across different genders and ethnic groups. Exploring this topic is valuable for understanding the relationship between ethnicity, environmental exposure, and mental health.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was part of a large-scale population-based cohort survey and adopted a stratified cluster sampling strategy. Field investigations were conducted in three ethnically diverse regions of Yunnan Province, China: Yongsheng County in Lijiang; Yongren and Wuding Counties in Chuxiong Prefecture, predominantly inhabited by the Yi ethnic group; and Heqing County in Dali Prefecture, home to a large Bai ethnic population. These counties are recognized as representative minority-inhabited areas in Yunnan, known for their distinctive ethnic cultures, social structures, and ecological governance practices [12].

Eligible participants were local residents aged 30 to 79 years who were capable of understanding and responding to interview questions. The baseline data collection took place between May 2018 and September 2019, during which a total of 23,143 individuals were enrolled.

Although there is no official documentation of the precise onset and conclusion of the Great Chinese Famine, most previous studies [17, 18] have defined the famine period as spanning from 1959 to 1961, typically ending in December 1961. Based on the participants’ birth dates, a total of 18,376 individuals were included in the final analysis.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) completion of the baseline survey and provision of valid informed consent; (2) birth date between October 1, 1939, and September 30, 1978. The exclusion criteria were: (1) birth during the transitional famine period (i.e., from October 1, 1958, to September 30, 1959, or from October 1, 1961, to September 30, 1962); (2) non-residency in Yunnan Province (non-registered local household); and (3) missing or incomplete demographic or clinical information.

Assessment of famine exposure

Famine exposure was assessed based on participants’ birth years. The famine occurred between January 1959 and December 1961, and archival verification from Yunnan confirmed that these regions indeed experienced famine during that period. Individuals born after October 1962 were not exposed to famine in utero. Participants were categorized into four groups based on their age during the famine: fetal period, childhood, and adolescence.

Participants were classified into four groups according to their birth dates and the corresponding life stage during the 1959–1961 Chinese famine: (1)Fetal-period famine exposure group–individuals born between October 1, 1959, and September 30, 1961, who were in the fetal stage (0 years old, unborn) during the famine.(2)Childhood famine exposure group–individuals born between October 1, 1949, and September 30, 1958, who were in childhood (1–9 years old) during the famine.(3)Adolescence famine exposure group–individuals born between October 1, 1943, and September 30, 1949, who were in adolescence (10–15 years old) during the famine.(4)Non-exposed group–individuals born between October 1, 1962, and September 30, 1978 (born after the famine; 40–56 years old at the baseline survey) and those born between October 1, 1939, and September 30, 1943 (late adolescence or early adulthood, 16–19 years old during the famine; 76–79 years old at the baseline survey).

Data collection procedures

The study involved three components: a structured questionnaire, physical examination, and laboratory testing.

Questionnaire survey

Face-to-face interviews were conducted by trained interviewers to collect information on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics and lifestyle factors, including smoking status, alcohol consumption, and annual household income. Smoking status was categorized as never smoker, current smoker, or former smoker, using standard definitions based on lifetime cigarette consumption and cessation history. “Current smoking” was defined as smoking at least one cigarette per day for more than six months.Alcohol consumption frequency was assessed based on self-reported drinking habits over the past years, “Current alcohol consumption” was defined as drinking alcohol at least once per week for more than six months.Annual household income bands were defined based on self-reported data from the 2018 baseline survey. In the study population, the median annual household income fell within the 20,000–99,999 CNY category. According to the sample distribution, the lowest income band (< 20,000 CNY) corresponded approximately to the bottom 46% of participants, the middle band (20,000–99,999 CNY) to the 46th–95th percentile range, and the highest band (≥ 100,000 CNY) to the top 5%. For context, the national median per capita disposable income in 2018 was 24,336 CNY (urban median: 36,413 CNY), which, assuming an average household size of 2.5 persons, corresponds to an estimated median household income of approximately 60,840 CNY—within the middle-income band in our study.

Physical and clinical examinations

Anthropometric measurements included height, weight, waist circumference, and hip circumference. These measurements were used to calculate body mass index (BMI = weight [kg]/height² [m²]) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). Participants with BMI ≥ 24.0 kg/m² were classified as overweight or obese.

Blood pressure was measured at least three times after a five-minute rest using an Omron HEM-8711 electronic sphygmomanometer. Hypertension was defined as: (a) systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg; or (b) a diagnosis of hypertension by a physician at a township-level or higher hospital.

Laboratory assessments

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from each participant for biochemical analyses. Laboratory tests included fasting blood glucose (FBG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Diabetes mellitus was defined as meeting any of the following criteria : (a) FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L after at least 8 h of fasting; (b) random blood glucose (RBG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L; (c) HbA1c ≥ 6.5%; or (d) physician-confirmed diagnosis from a township-level or higher hospital.

All measurements were performed by licensed medical professionals who had received standardized training. The instruments used for measurement and laboratory testing were validated and certified by local quality control authorities.

Assessment of depression and anxiety symptoms

Symptoms of depression and anxiety were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-2 (GAD-2), respectively [19]. Each scale ranges from 0 to 6 points. Scores of 3 or above indicated the presence of depressive or anxiety symptoms. Given that both tools are designed for screening rather than clinical diagnosis, outcomes are referred to as “depressive symptoms” or “anxiety symptoms” in this study. Participants with positive scores on both PHQ-2 and GAD-2 were considered to exhibit comorbid depressive and anxiety symptoms [20].

Reliability of mental health assessment tools

The internal consistency of the mental health assessment tools was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. In accordance with established psychometric standards, a coefficient greater than 0.70 was considered indicative of acceptable reliability, whereas values above 0.50 were deemed sufficient for exploratory analyses. Two screening instruments were assessed: the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) for depressive symptoms and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2) for anxiety symptoms. Both measures have demonstrated satisfactory reliability in prior studies among Chinese populations, with reported Cronbach’s alpha values of approximately 0.76 for the PHQ-2 [21] and 0.81 for the GAD-2 [22].

Covariates assessment

The selection of covariates was based on the results of univariate analyses, potential socio-economic and behavioral health factors, as well as core control variables identified in prior research [14, 23–25]. Key demographic factors included age, gender, ethnicity, and region. Adult socio-economic and behavioral variables encompassed educational attainment, household income, marital status, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Notably, socio-economic status is regarded as a critical potential moderator in the association between early-life famine exposure and mental health outcomes in adulthood [26, 27]. Marital status and health-related behaviors, such as smoking and drinking, serve as indicators of social support and lifestyle in adulthood. These factors are not only strongly associated with mental health and metabolic risks, but may also either amplify or mitigate the long-term effects of early adverse exposures. Additionally, existing health conditions—such as overweight/obesity, hypertension, and diabetes—may lie on the causal pathway linking famine exposure to depression and anxiety. A stepwise adjustment approach was employed in the inclusion of these covariates. Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, and region. Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking, alcohol consumption, marital status, educational level, and annual household income. Model 3 further adjusted for overweight status, hypertension, and diabetes to explore potential pathways through adult health status.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2 and Stata version 17.0. All hypothesis tests were two-sided, and p-values ≤ 0.05 were considered indicative of evidence against the null hypothesis. The Chi-square test was applied in categorical variables analysis and multivariable logistic regression models was used to examine the associations between famine exposure and adulthood depressive and anxiety symptoms. The odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval were reported.

To test the robustness of the research results under different definitions of non-exposure, we excluded individuals born between 1939 and 1943, who might have been affected by the socioeconomic consequences of the famine in age 18–20, and retained only those born between 1962 and 1978 as the control group for sensitivity analysis. The regression model and the stratified method were consistent with the previous.

Results

Basic characteristics of the study population by famine exposure groups

This study included a total of 18,376 participants, of whom 6,186 (33.7%) were male and 12,190 (66.3%) were female. In terms of ethnic composition, 8,416 (45.6%) were Han, 5,138 (28.0%) were Bai, and 4,822 (26.2%) were Yi. Based on the participants’ age at the time of famine exposure, they were categorized into four groups: 12,220 (66.5%) were classified as the non-exposed group, 762 (4.1%) as the fetal-exposed group, 4,150 (22.6%) as the childhood-exposed group, and 1,244 (6.8%) as the adolescent-exposed group. Among the participants, 17,110 (93.1%) had rural household registration, and 13,381 (72.8%) had an education level of elementary school or below. The majority of households reported an annual income ranging between 20,000 and 99,999 yuan (48.4%).

Differences were observed among the famine exposure groups in age, sex, ethnicity, household registration status, and body mass index (BMI) (p < 0.001). Regarding lifestyle behaviors, variations in smoking and alcohol consumption patterns were noted across the famine exposure groups (p < 0.001). Differences were also observed in the prevalence of diabetes and hypertension among the famine exposure groups (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Analysis of the impact of famine exposure on the risk of depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity

Figure 1 presents the associations between famine exposure at different life stages and the risk of depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity. Multivariable analyses were performed using three sequential models: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, and region; Model 2 additionally adjusted for smoking status, alcohol consumption, marital status, education level, and annual household income; and Model 3 further adjusted for overweight, hypertension, and diabetes.

Risk analysis of anxiety and depression symptoms across different famine exposure groups. Model 1: Adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, and region. Model 2: Further adjusted for smoking, alcohol consumption, marital status, education level, and household income based on Model 1. In the fully adjusted Model 3, childhood famine exposure (OR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.32–2.04, p < 0.001) and adolescence exposure (OR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.18–2.27, p = 0.003) were associated with a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms in adulthood compared with the non-exposed group. Estimates were consistent across all models.

For anxiety symptoms, fetal exposure showed a positive association in the unadjusted model; however, after full adjustment, no evidence of association was observed (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.00–1.77, p = 0.052). Childhood exposure (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.82–1.24, p = 0.973) and adolescence exposure (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.62–1.24, p = 0.457) were not associated with anxiety risk.

For comorbid depression and anxiety, no associations were identified across exposure groups in the fully adjusted model (fetal exposure: OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 0.85–1.88, p = 0.256; childhood exposure: OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.69–1.26, p = 0.656; adolescence exposure: OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.46–1.22, p = 0.239).

Gender and ethnic stratified analysis: the relationship between early life famine exposure and the risk of depression symptoms in adulthood

After stratifying the study population by sex and ethnicity and using the non-exposed group as the reference (Fig. 2), women exposed to famine during childhood (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.47–2.39, p < 0.001) and adolescence (OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.36–2.81, p < 0.001) had a higher prevalence of depressive symptoms in adulthood. No evidence of association was observed in men for any exposure period (childhood p = 0.805, adolescence p = 0.246).

In ethnicity-stratified analyses, fetal famine exposure among Yi participants was associated with a higher prevalence of anxiety symptoms in adulthood (OR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.37–2.88, p < 0.001). No associations were identified among Han or Bai participants. These patterns remained consistent in the fully adjusted model (Model 3). The odds ratio 95% CI of the Yi ethnic group (1.37–2.88) participants exposed to fetal famine did not overlap with that of the Han ethnic group (0.40–1.24), indicating a significant difference in the risk of anxiety symptoms between Yi and Han ethnic groups who were exposed to fetal famine. However, the difference was not obvious when compared with the Bai ethnic group.

However, the difference was not obvious when compared with the Bai ethnic group.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis results demonstrate that childhood famine exposure remains a significant risk factor for the development of depressive symptoms in adulthood (OR = 1.48, 95% CI = 1.09–2.00, p = 0.011) (Figure S1). Female childhood and adolescence famine exposed individuals still have the high risk of depression (Figure S2).

In contrast, adolescent famine exposure appears to act as a protective factor against the onset of anxiety disorders and comorbid depression (OR = 0.57, 95% CI = 0.36–0.90, p = 0.016; OR = 0.41, 95% CI = 0.21–0.80, p = 0.009) (Figure S1). Following further stratification by sex, this pattern was predominantly observed among males exposed to famine during childhood and adolescence (Figure S3), which aligns with the findings of the main analysis.

Notably, in this analysis, members of the Yi ethnic group who experienced famine exposure during the fetal period continued to show statistically significant associations with increased risks of depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity (depression: OR = 1.84, 95% CI = 1.15–2.95, p = 0.011; anxiety: OR = 1.99, 95% CI = 1.37–2.88, p < 0.001; comorbid: OR = 2.08, 95% CI = 1.18–3.66, p = 0.011) (Figures S2–4), further confirming the robustness of the elevated risk observed in the Yi ethnic fetal famine group.

Discussion

This study identified an independent association between exposure to famine during childhood and adolescence and an increased risk of developing depressive symptoms in adulthood among 18,376 individuals in the CMEC cohort. This association remained valid even after adjusting for potential factors. These findings are consistent with previous epidemiological evidence, suggesting that exposure to famine during critical developmental periods may make individuals more prone to mental health disorders in the future. Data from the Dutch Hunger Winter Study indicated that after experiencing famine exposure during pregnancy, the risk of schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety disorders increases [28]. The analysis of the Chinese Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) also reported that after severe famine exposure during early life, the risk of depression in later life is higher [29]. Our study expands this literature, demonstrating that the negative impact of famine exposure on mental health is not limited to the fetal period but also includes childhood and adolescence - these are critical periods for neurodevelopment, hormone regulation, and psychological social adaptation. Nutritional malnutrition during the fetal period can damage brain structure and neural circuits; childhood malnutrition may disrupt emotional regulation and stress response systems [30]; and insufficient nutrition during adolescence can affect identity formation, social integration, and later neurodevelopment [31]. The underlying mechanisms behind these associations may be related to the regulation of depression and anxiety by neurochemical imbalances, hormonal dysregulation, and changes in brain structure [32].

The exposure to famine is associated with increased susceptibility to depression in women, while paradoxically linked to a reduced risk of anxiety in men

The stratified analysis highlights significant gender differences in the relationship between famine exposure and the risks of depression and anxiety. Previous epidemiological studies have consistently shown that depression and anxiety present gender-specific patterns [33–35]. For example, Li [36], using data from the CHARLS cohort, demonstrated that early-life famine exposure (during pregnancy, childhood, and adolescence) significantly heightened the risk of depression in women but exerted no significant effect on men—findings that align closely with those of the present study. The heightened vulnerability among women may be attributed to gender-specific neurobiological mechanisms, heavier social role burdens, and unequal access to psychosocial resources. In traditional societies, women often bore the dual responsibilities of household labor and caregiving yet lacked adequate external support. The deprivation of resources during famine periods likely intensified these vulnerabilities [37–39]. Biologically, the stronger associations in women may also reflect differences in hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis reactivity, hormonal milieu, and stress-coping mechanisms.

Intriguingly, our findings suggest that famine exposure during childhood or adolescence was associated with a reduced risk of anxiety in adult men. Although counterintuitive, this pattern may be explained by gender-specific trajectories of brain development and stress responses. Women tend to exhibit stronger activation in prefrontal-limbic circuitry, heightening emotional sensitivity, whereas men are more reliant on amygdala-driven responses and overt coping strategies—such as aggression or substance use—potentially manifesting as “lowered anxiety” at the symptomatic level. Yi et al. [30] similarly proposed that famine experiences may foster adaptability and psychological resilience in men.

Sociocultural dynamics may also underlie these disparities. In traditional Chinese society, limited food supplies were often preferentially allocated to boys, resulting in girls experiencing more severe nutritional deficits and psychosocial stress during famine. This imbalance may have compounded women’s susceptibility to depression. Furthermore, survival bias cannot be overlooked: individuals of one gender with higher inherent risks may have faced greater mortality during famine, leaving a survivor population with altered baseline risk estimates in adulthood. Together, these mechanisms help explain the gendered patterns observed.

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior research. Yi et al. [30]. emphasized that famine exposure may enhance male independence and resilience, echoing our observation that early-life famine did not significantly predict adult depression in men. Conversely, women exposed to famine during childhood or adolescence showed markedly higher risks of adult depression (childhood: OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.47–2.39, p < 0.001; adolescence: OR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.36–2.81, p < 0.001). Similarly, Li et al. [40], analyzing 17,505 participants from CHARLS, reported significant associations between famine exposure and depression symptoms in women but not in men, a finding that resonates with our results. Specifically, our study further confirmed that women exposed to famine during childhood and adolescence were at significantly elevated risks, while men in these groups displayed no such association, and in some cases even reduced risks for anxiety (childhood: OR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.33–0.85, p = 0.008; adolescence: OR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.13–0.78, p = 0.013).

Ethnic differences in the association between famine exposure and the risk of depression and anxiety

This study further revealed significant ethnic differences in the psychological health effects of famine exposure. Specifically, childhood famine exposure significantly increased the risk of depression among Han individuals; among the Yi ethnic group, prenatal exposure was associated with heightened risks of depression, anxiety, and their comorbidity; whereas no significant effects were observed in the Bai population. These findings suggest that Yi individuals may be more vulnerable to early-life nutritional deprivation, while the Bai population might possess certain protective sociocultural or biological factors that buffer against such risks.

Previous studies have reported ethnic differences in both the prevalence and clinical manifestations of mental disorders [39], underscoring the critical role of cultural and sociodemographic contexts. The National Center for Health Statistics in the United States has also emphasized that race and ethnicity represent important social determinants of mental health [39]. In China, students from ethnic minority regions—particularly those in remote, border, and mountainous areas—exhibit significantly lower levels of mental health compared with the national average [41, 42], a disparity likely attributable to persistent inequalities in education, healthcare, and economic opportunities. Thus, the observed ethnic heterogeneity may reflect the combined effects of socioeconomic disparities and lifestyle differences.

Rapid economic development in Bai communities has improved access to public resources and mental health services [43]; in contrast, Yi communities often face geographical isolation, a monocultural economic structure, and greater vulnerability to disruptions in social and healthcare systems [44]. Historical variation in famine severity and recovery trajectories across different ethnic regions may also have contributed to these divergent outcomes.

Moreover, dietary habits and environmental factors should not be overlooked. The Bai population has a relatively high intake of dairy products, which has been linked to reduced risks of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases [45]. In contrast, Yi populations rely heavily on high-altitude agriculture and pastoralism, and are chronically exposed to cold, hypoxia, and intensive labor, which may cumulatively increase psychological vulnerability [46]. From a biological perspective, epigenetic mechanisms may constitute a crucial pathway linking early-life nutritional deprivation with mental health outcomes. Famine can alter gene expression through DNA methylation, histone modification, and related processes, thereby influencing HPA axis function and neurodevelopment [47, 48]. In addition, genetic susceptibility warrants attention: due to genetic drift and high rates of endogamy, Yi populations may carry specific neurodevelopment-related variants, which could further enhance long-term vulnerability.

By integrating data from multiple ethnic groups, delineating exposure windows across distinct stages of early life, and adjusting for socioeconomic factors, the robustness of our findings was confirmed through sensitivity and stratified analyses. Collectively, these results underscore the profound and enduring impact of famine as an early-life adversity on adult mental health. Importantly, they highlight the necessity of considering cultural and sociodemographic backgrounds in epidemiological research, while also pointing to the need for future studies to investigate gene–environment interactions as a potential explanatory mechanism for ethnic disparities.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings.

First, the analysis was restricted to three ethnic groups (Han, Bai, and Yi), which may limit the generalizability of the results to other minority populations that were not included in this study but may also be affected by early-life famine exposure in terms of mental health outcomes.

Second, although the PHQ-2 and GAD-2 are widely used as brief screening instruments for depression and anxiety, their relatively low sensitivity may fail to capture subthreshold or prodromal symptoms, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true prevalence of mental health problems.

Third, given the inherent correlations among participants’ age at famine exposure, age at survey, and birth cohort, it was not possible to fully disentangle age, period, and cohort effects. This inevitably introduces the theoretical risk of collinearity. Nevertheless, most existing famine-related studies have similarly relied on age to define exposure groups, and no more optimal alternative grouping variable has yet been established.

Finally, the covariates included in the analysis were relatively limited, and residual confounding due to unmeasured or unknown factors cannot be completely ruled out.

Conclusion

This study highlights the long-term mental health consequences of early-life famine exposure, revealing significant disparities across ethnic groups, particularly among the Yi ethnic minority, rural residents, and women. These findings offer novel insights into chronic disease prevention and maternal–child nutrition interventions. There is an urgent need to strengthen mental health education for vulnerable populations, enhance the capacity to identify and manage depression and anxiety, improve nutritional status and socioeconomic conditions, and expand access to primary-level mental health services. Such efforts are crucial to mitigating the adverse psychiatric effects of early-life nutritional deprivation.
