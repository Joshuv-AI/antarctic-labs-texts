Duke Activity Status Index Versus PROMIS Computer Adaptive Testing-Physical Function for Preoperative Assessment
Authors: Mark A. Nyman, Adam K. Jacob, John C. O'Horo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the performance of the patient-reported outcomes measurement information system computer adaptive testing-physical function (PROMIS CAT-PF) to the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) as a potential alternative physical function domain assessment to assist with preoperative risk assessment.Patients and MethodsPatients consisted of a retrospective convenience sample of all adults who had a DASI or PROMIS CAT-PF score obtained from January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2022, within 90 days prior to elective surgery. DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF obtained within one month before a stress test was used to assess the correlation between actual metabolic equivalents attained and the 2 patient-facing questionnaires. To assess the ability to predict adverse outcomes, we used a composite outcome of either death or myocardial injury within 30 days of the surgery.ResultsIn comparing DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF correlation to actual metabolic equivalents achieved on stress testing, the DASI Spearman’s coefficient of 0.46 (95% CI, 0.32-0.58) was numerically better than the PROMIS CAT-PF Spearman’s coefficient of 0.39 (95% CI, 0.21-0.52); however, the difference was not statistically significant. Using a composite outcome of either death or myocardial injury, DASI (area under the curve of 0.712) and PROMIS CAT-PF (area under the curve of 0.773) reported fair predictive utility. The 2 patient-reported physical function domain scores showed weak correlation (κ=0.509).ConclusionPROMIS CAT-PF performs as well as DASI, demonstrating a small incremental benefit in predicting adverse postoperative outcomes, and could be an alternative physical function domain assessment used in the preoperative evaluation of patients.

Patient self-reported exercise tolerance has been shown to predict in-hospital perioperative risk.1,2 Scored patient-facing questionnaires, such as the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI),3 can assist in the assessment of a patient’s overall physical fitness. The DASI, a 12 item yes/no questionnaire with items weighted to calculate a total score ranging from 0-58.2 points, was designed to assess functional capacity (higher=better capacity) to measure cardiovascular outcomes in cardiac rehabilitation. Though the DASI score’s correlation to actual patient metabolic output has been variable4,5 the DASI score has been shown to correlate with postoperative outcomes independently.6, 7, 8 However, self-reported functional capacity has been shown to lack the ability to improve the prediction of outcomes over standard clinical risk scores or clinical risk factors.9 Despite the small discriminatory power of DASI in preoperative evaluation, its use has been added as a standard recommendation in recent preoperative evaluation guidelines.10,11

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROM) continue to gain popularity, and specialty practices are increasingly using them in clinical practice to assist in assessing patient outcomes. With the increased use of PROMs within clinical practice, there is a growing problem of patient questionnaire burden as patients’ journey through various specialty groups and complete multiple PROMs. Patients prefer limiting questions to only those necessary and sufficient to collect necessary data—making redundant questions undesirable.12 Disease-specific legacy PROMs tend to measure various domains within one PROM. When patients see multiple specialists, legacy PROMs can result in patients being presented with redundant questions as each specialty queries using different legacy measures with overlapping domains of interest. Utilization of cross-cutting domain-specific questionnaires can help reduce patient questionnaire burden.13 One option available, Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)14 funded by the National Institute of Health, uses item response theory to reduce patient burden and measures domains that are of interest to patients and clinicians across specialties (for example: orthopedics, rheumatology, and physical medicine and rehabilitation are all interested in the domains of pain intensity and physical function). A patient-centered approach leverages cross-cutting domain-specific PROMs across specialty practices, allowing the collection of a domain score data point early in a patient’s journey that can be used across multiple specialties over a specified timeframe without requiring the patient to be reassessed to obtain the same domain data point.

Our institution’s neurosurgical, orthopedic, rheumatology, and physical medicine & rehabilitation departments use the PROMIS computer adaptive testing-physical function (PROMIS CAT-PF) score.15 The PROMIS CAT-PF produces a T-score between 20-80 with 50 being the average normal physical function (higher=better function), with 40-31 moderate physical function impairment and 30 or less severe physical function impairment (10 points are one standard deviation). Because we were already collecting a physical function domain score, PROMIS CAT-PF, collecting the DASI assessment in the perioperative period would be redundant. We hypothesized that the PROMIS CAT-PF score would perform as well as the DASI score in assessing the risk for poor perioperative cardiovascular outcomes.

Patients and Methods

The Mayo Clinic institutional review board reviewed this retrospective, secondary use of information research and deemed it exempt from the requirement for IRB approval (45 CFR 46.104d, category 4). This work was funded in part by Mayo Clinic Department of Medicine, Division of General Internal Medicine.

We assessed the computer adaptive testing version of the PROMIS CAT-PF rather than the available fixed-length short form. The CAT was run within our instance of Epic and administered to patients through the patient portal or a personal computer tablet in the lobby. To limit patient exposure to excess questions, we required a minimum of three questions, with stopping rules of (1) reaching a SE threshold of less than 3 T-score points or (2) a maximum of 5 questions. The DASI was collected by providers through interview inquiry and completion of a flowsheet within Epic.

Patient Selection

The cohort studied consisted of a retrospective convenience sample of all adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) who had a PROMIS CAT-PF or DASI score obtained from January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2022 within 90 days before elective surgery. For head-to-head comparisons between the 2 patient-facing questionnaires, we restricted the analysis to the cohort of patients who had completed both assessments. Patients without research authorization on file were excluded.

Stress Test Comparison

We used scores for DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF obtained within 1 month before a stress test to assess the correlation between actual metabolic equivalents (METS) attained and the 2 patient-facing questionnaires.

DASI and PROMIS Correlation

To assess DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF score's ability to predict adverse outcomes, we used a composite outcome of either death or myocardial injury (defined as the fifth-generation troponin value of 10 ng/L or greater for females and 15 ng/L or greater for males based on European Society of Cardiology guidelines)16 within 30 days of the surgery. We used a cut-point score of 34 or lower for DASI to predict higher risk for adverse outcomes, as this has been previously shown to correlate with poor postoperative cardiovascular outcomes7 and preoperative guidelines also suggest this cut-point.10,11 We used a cut-point score of 40 or lower for PROMIS-PF (ie, moderate to severe physical function impairment) to predict higher risk for an adverse postoperative outcome, as this has previously been shown to correlate with an increased risk of perioperative complications in colorectal surgical patients.17 If multiple measurements were taken, we used the value scored closest to the procedure’s date.

Statistical Analyses

Spearman’s correlation was used to determine the monotonic association between METS recorded during stress testing and patient self-reported functional status. A conventional approach to interpreting Spearman’s correlation coefficients is to consider 0.00-0.10 a negligible correlation; 0.10 and 0.39 a weak correlation; 0.40-0.69 a moderate correlation; 0.70-0.89 a strong correlation; and 0.90-1.00 a very strong correlation.18 To formally compare the Spearman correlations between METS and the 2 separate questionnaires, and given a small number of participants appeared in both samples, we used a nonparametric bootstrap approach to calculate statistically significant differences.

We used logistic regression with the composite outcome as the dependent variable. Unadjusted area under the curve (AUC) included age, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and primary surgical service. We have included odds ratios (OR) for each of the continuous variables.19 Model discrimination was assessed using AUC based on predicted probabilities and compared using DeLong’s test (tests whether 2 AUC curves are statistically different).20 The AUC can help assess the diagnostic performance of the score in predicting the outcome. An AUC of 1 is perfect, and an AUC of 0.5 is equivalent to a coin flip. Typically, the AUC is interpreted as AUC≥0.9 excellent; 0.8≤AUC<0.9 considerable; 0.7≤ AUC<0.8 fair; 0.6≤AUC< 0.7 poor; and 0.5≤AUC<0.6 fail.21

We used Cohens κ (unweighted) to measure agreement between dichotomized DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF scores. The κ can help distinguish the reliability or agreement between 2 tests. More recent trends in interpreting the κ result are stricter than Cohen’s original and suggest the level of agreement between 2 tests should be described through κ score as follows: 0-0.20 none; 0.21-0.39 minimal; 0.40-0.59 weak; 0.60-0.79 moderate; 0.80-0.90 strong; and above 0.90 almost perfect.22 We also provide traditional measures of diagnostic accuracy for each instrument, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV).19

Results

Table 1 describes the characteristics of the cohorts studied. The ’Both’ column represents all patients who had a complete DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF score. The 3 cohorts were different real-world patient populations. The PROMIS CAT-PF-only cohort was the largest cohort, younger in age and with a female majority. Of the surgical service lines, the orthopedic (30%-51%) and neurosurgical (14%-32%) service lines had higher representation within the overall studied populations. The DASI only cohort had more hepatobiliary & pancreas, head & neck, oncology, and urology cases and the PROMIS CAT-PF only cohort had more general, transplant, and gynecologic cases. All 3 cohorts were similar in the percent of patients with history of myocardial infarction, dementia, and peptic ulcer. The DASI only cohort had a heavier disease burden as evidenced by a higher CCI, though the CCI was mild overall. More than a third of the patients scored as elevated risk on the 2 scored questionnaires. Overall event rates for the composite endpoints of troponin elevation (2.8%-4.0%) and 30-day all-cause mortality (0.1%-0.4%) were low.

There were 142 patients who had a DASI score and 133 patients with a PROMIS CAT-PF score, within 30 days of a stress test. Utilizing these scores and the actual METS achieved (see scatter plots Figures 1 and 2), Spearman’s coefficient was 0.46 (95% CI, 0.32-0.58) for the DASI and 0.39 (95% CI, 0.21-0.52) for the PROMIS CAT-PF. In the cohort of patients who had both a DASI and a PROMIS CAT-PF score collected (n=18,433), using a model with each of the scores, Charlson Index, sex, age, and procedure (service name only), the AUC with DASI was 0.719 and 0.680 without (Delong Test P<.001) and the AUC with PROMIS CAT-PF was 0.710 and 0.680 without (Delong Test P<.001).

In applying the predetermined cut points of 34 for DASI and 40 for PROMIS CAT-PF, the κ between the 2 dichotomized score classifications was 0.509. Further, we noted the DASI had 22.0% sensitivity, 60.7% specificity, 1.7% PPV, and 96.1% NPV, and the PROMIS CAT-PF had 31.3% sensitivity, 60.0% specificity, 2.8% PPV, and 95.9% NPV.

Discussion

Our study adds to the limited data available in the literature showing a correlation between patient self-reported functional capacity and patient postoperative outcomes. Both patient-reported scores, the DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF, reported small incremental discrimination for postoperative outcomes when added to baseline clinical models and reported weak agreement with each other when dichotomized using prespecified thresholds (κ=0.509).

In comparing the 2 self-assessment questionnaires with actual METS achieved during stress testing, we found DASI to have a moderate and PROMIS CAT-PF to have a weak, monotonic correlation with actual METS on stress testing. Although DASI showed a numerically higher Spearman’s correlation than PROMIS CAT-PF, the difference was not statistically significant. The Spearman’s correlations of 0.46 and 0.39 were lower than Hlatky’s original finding of 0.58.3

In separate models assessing the utility of the 2 assessments across different cohorts, we found small but statistically significant improvement in the AUC for predicting the composite outcome with both the DASI (3.9%-5.3%) and PROMIS CAT-PF (2%-3%) instruments, though AUCs reflect only fair performance in predicting the outcome. This finding of small but statistically significant improvement in discrimination was similar to that reported by Wijeysundera et al6,7 in 2018 and 2020. Finally, in the set of patients who had both DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF scores, the DASI and the PROMIS CAT-PF both incrementally improved discriminatory capability by 3%-3.9%. It is important to note the discriminatory capability of both instruments was consistent across differing real-world patient cohorts, as current guidelines quote both Wijeysundera et al6 in 2018 and Wijeysundera et al7 in 2020, which were performed on the same base cohort.

Comparing the traditional markers of test performance (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV), the 2 scored questionnaires performed similarly. Though both scored assessments add to the preoperative evaluation of patients, it should be noted that over a third of the patients scored as higher risk, yet still went to surgery, suggesting other factors must be considered when assessing the preoperative patient. DASI or PROMIS CAT-PF can add incremental discriminatory capacity in preoperative settings, though small. As Lurati Buse et al9 suggested, caution should be applied in the use of self-reported functional capacity to guide clinical decisions in patients submitted to noncardiac surgery.

Limitations to our study include evaluating only those patients who had completed the patient self-assessments and not those without completed questionnaires. Patients self-reported the PROMIS CAT-PF through an electronic interface, whereas providers, through patient interview, collected the DASI. The patient population studied was overrepresented by orthopedic and neurosurgery cases. We report only outcomes occurring within our institution, it is possible we missed outcomes occurring outside of our institution. Generalizability of these findings may be weakened as only one institution participated, though participation was across several campuses in multiple states.

Conclusion

Current guidelines encourage the use of patient-reported outcome measures of functional capacity as an additional consideration in the assessment of patient surgical readiness. Though their contribution to overall risk assessment is small, we have shown that the DASI and PROMIS CAT-PF perform similarly in predicting outcomes in the presurgical population.
