Recurrent Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis With Aortomitral Curtain Destruction in an Elderly Patient
Authors: Tea-Terezija Cvetko, Jasmina Ćatić, Jelena Kursar, Ivica Šafradin, Vlatka Rešković Lukšić, Ivana Jurin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecurrent prosthetic valve infective endocarditis in older adults can present atypically and progress rapidly, requiring complex decisions.Case SummaryA 69-year-old man with prior bioprosthetic aortic and mitral valve replacement presented with progressive back pain without fever. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis. Transesophageal echocardiography showed large vegetations on both prostheses with periannular extension and an aortic root abscess. Despite intravenous ampicillin plus ceftriaxone, inflammatory markers and vegetation burden increased, prompting Endocarditis Team–recommended urgent redo surgery. Complete destruction of the intervalvular fibrosa required radical debridement, double valve replacement, and reconstruction of the aortomitral continuity (Commando procedure). The patient completed 6 weeks of targeted therapy; dental evaluation revealed advanced chronic periodontitis as the likely source.DiscussionUncontrolled prosthetic valve endocarditis warrants repeat imaging and timely multidisciplinary surgical management, even in elderly patients.Key Words: aortomitral curtain, Commando procedure, double valve surgery, infective endocarditis, prosthetic valve endocarditis

History of Presentation

A 69-year-old man presented with a 1-month history of progressive lower back pain and functional decline. He denied fever, chills, weight loss, or neurologic symptoms. On examination, he was afebrile (36.7 °C), blood pressure was 110/60 mm Hg, and heart rate 92 beats/min. A systolic murmur was audible at the left sternal border. No peripheral stigmata of endocarditis were observed.

Past Medical History

The patient had hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease (stage 3), coronary artery disease treated with multiple percutaneous interventions, chronic heart failure, and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation previously treated with pulmonary vein isolation.

Four years earlier, he underwent surgical replacement of native aortic and mitral valves for Streptococcus sanguinis infective endocarditis (IE) with implantation of bioprosthetic valves.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnoses included spondylodiscitis or degenerative spine disease, given the predominance of back pain and the absence of fever, as well as prosthetic valve thrombosis and recurrent IE. Culture-negative endocarditis and nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis were also considered. Positive blood cultures and echocardiographic evidence of prosthetic valve vegetations with periannular complications established the diagnosis of prosthetic valve IE.

Investigations

Laboratory tests showed a normal leukocyte count, a hemoglobin level of 95 g/L, a C-reactive protein level of 119 mg/L, and a creatinine level of 124 μmol/L.

Three sets of blood cultures obtained before antibiotic administration grew Enterococcus faecalis. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the disc diffusion method and interpreted according to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing standards. The isolate was categorized as susceptible to ampicillin. High-level aminoglycoside resistance was reported, eliminating the potential for synergistic gentamicin therapy. In addition, the patient had stage 3 chronic kidney disease, increasing the risk of aminoglycoside-associated nephrotoxicity. Therefore, combination therapy with ampicillin plus ceftriaxone was initiated in accordance with current guideline recommendations for prosthetic valve IE.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 50% with inferior wall hypokinesis. Echogenic masses highly suggestive of vegetations were identified on both prosthetic valves. A 10 × 16 mm vegetation was visualized on the bioprosthetic mitral valve, associated with moderate prosthetic stenosis (mean transvalvular gradient 9 mm Hg) (Video 1). The bioprosthetic aortic valve leaflets appeared thickened and restricted, with Doppler parameters consistent with moderate stenosis (calculated valve area 1.1 cm2) (Figure 1).

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) demonstrated prosthetic valve IE affecting both valves, with multiple mobile vegetations attached to the mitral bioprosthesis and its sewing ring (Videos 2 and 3). The aortic bioprosthesis exhibited leaflet thickening and a periannular abscess consistent with invasive infection (Figure 2, Video 4).

Four days after initiating targeted therapy for IE, the patient developed abdominal pain. Computed tomography demonstrated a splenic infarction, with no evidence of spinal infection (Figure 3).

Computed Tomography Demonstrating Splenic Infarction Consistent With an Embolic Complication and no Evidence of Spinal Infection

After a comprehensive diagnostic work-up, a diagnosis of IE involving both bioprosthetic valves was confirmed according to the Modified Duke Criteria. The major criteria were met by positive blood cultures with a typical microorganism from 2 or more separate sets and echocardiographic evidence of vegetations and an abscess. Minor criteria included the presence of predisposing prosthetic valves and a vascular phenomenon in the form of splenic infarction. Overall, the patient fulfilled the criteria for definite prosthetic valve IE by meeting 2 major criteria.

Management

Empiric ciprofloxacin and metronidazole were initiated at presentation because an intraabdominal or spinal source was initially suspected. After E faecalis grew in 3 sets of blood cultures, antimicrobial therapy was switched to intravenous ampicillin plus ceftriaxone. High-level aminoglycoside resistance and stage 3 chronic kidney disease precluded synergistic gentamicin therapy.

Despite appropriate targeted therapy, inflammatory markers remained elevated (C-reactive protein 143 mg/L). Ten days after initiation of directed antimicrobial treatment, repeat TTE and TEE demonstrated clear progression, with enlargement of vegetations, progression of mitral stenosis, and new aortic root abscess formation, indicating uncontrolled infection and prompting urgent surgical intervention (Figure 4).

Repeat Echocardiography After 10 Days of Targeted Therapy Demonstrating Enlargement of Vegetations, Progression of Mitral Stenosis, and Development/Progression of an Aortic Root Abscess Consistent With Uncontrolled Infection

The patient received 13 days of targeted intravenous antimicrobial therapy before surgery. Given echocardiographic progression and uncontrolled infection, the multidisciplinary Endocarditis Team recommended urgent redo surgery. Intraoperatively, both prosthetic valves were encased in vegetations with multiple abscess cavities and complete destruction of the intervalvular fibrosa (Figure 5). Extensive debridement required resection of the aortomitral curtain.

Intraoperative Findings of Extensive Vegetations and Abscess Cavities Involving Both Prosthetic Valves With Destruction of the Intervalvular Fibrosa (Aortomitral Curtain) Before Reconstruction

A Commando procedure was performed, including explantation of both prosthetic valves, drainage of abscesses, reconstruction of the intervalvular fibrous body using a folded bovine pericardial patch, followed by implantation of a 33-mm Epic Plus bioprosthetic mitral valve and a 23-mm Carpentier-Edwards PERIMOUNT Magna Ease biological aortic valve (Edwards Bovine Pericardial Patch). Intraoperative echocardiography confirmed good prosthetic function without residual infection.

Postoperatively, vancomycin was initiated empirically pending intraoperative culture results due to extensive periannular destruction and concern for potential polymicrobial or resistant infection. Once intraoperative cultures confirmed E faecalis without additional pathogens, antimicrobial therapy was streamlined, and the patient completed additional 6 weeks of targeted intravenous treatment from the date of surgery.

Candida albicans was isolated from bronchial aspirate during prolonged mechanical ventilation. In the absence of candidemia or radiographic evidence of invasive fungal infection, this finding was interpreted as colonization. Given critical postoperative status, short-course antifungal therapy was administered empirically and subsequently discontinued after clinical stabilization and negative follow-up cultures.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was complicated by prolonged ventilation, delirium, atrial fibrillation, new left bundle branch block, heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (LVEF 35%), and deconditioning. Two computed tomography scans of the brain were performed, and they did not show any new ischemic or hemorrhagic lesions.

At discharge after 67 days, TTE demonstrated normally functioning bioprosthetic aortic and mitral valves without residual vegetations or paravalvular leaks, and with transvalvular gradients within the expected range for prosthetic valves. LVEF was mildly reduced at approximately 45%, with persistent inferior wall hypokinesis. No pericardial effusion was present (Figure 6).

Postoperative Transthoracic Echocardiography Demonstrating Well-Seated Bioprosthetic Aortic and Mitral Valves Without Residual Vegetations or Paravalvular Leak

At 6-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable in NYHA functional class II without rehospitalization. He was afebrile, and inflammatory markers remained within the normal range. Follow-up TTE demonstrated a reduced LVEF of 45%, preserved prosthetic valve function without structural deterioration, prosthetic dehiscence, or new perivalvular complications. No recurrent vegetations were identified.

Dental evaluation revealed advanced chronic periodontitis and periapical osteitis, which were considered the most likely source of bacteremia and recurrent infection. Although E faecalis bacteremia has been associated with underlying gastrointestinal pathology, particularly colorectal neoplasia, multiple abdominal computed tomography examinations performed during hospitalization showed no evidence of gross neoplasm. Given the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms and identification of a plausible odontogenic source, further inpatient gastrointestinal work-up was deferred. Outpatient colonoscopic evaluation was recommended; however, the patient declined additional gastrointestinal investigation after discussion of potential risks and benefits.

Discussion

Infective endocarditis in elderly patients is increasingly prevalent due to the growing number of prosthetic valves, degenerative valvular disease, and health care–associated exposures.1,2 Recurrent IE remains a major therapeutic challenge and carries substantial morbidity and mortality.2,3 Prosthetic valve involvement predisposes to periannular extension, abscess formation, and structural destruction, all of which markedly worsen prognosis.

Elderly patients often present atypically, leading to diagnostic delay.2 In this case, the absence of fever and leukocytosis and the predominance of back pain initially suggested a noncardiac etiology. In patients with bacteremia and prominent back pain, vertebral osteomyelitis or spondylodiscitis should be considered; spinal magnetic resonance imaging is the most sensitive modality, whereas computed tomography may help exclude gross destructive changes. Early blood cultures and prompt TEE are essential. Detection of E faecalis bacteremia warrants evaluation for potential portals of entry, including dental and gastrointestinal sources. Some studies have reported an association between E faecalis bloodstream infection and colorectal neoplasia; colonoscopic evaluation may be considered when no clear source is identified and the patient is willing.4 In our patient, advanced periodontal disease was considered the most plausible source, and outpatient colonoscopy was declined.

Despite guideline-directed antimicrobial therapy with ampicillin plus ceftriaxone (a renal-sparing regimen effective for E faecalis endocarditis with high-level aminoglycoside resistance),5 this patient demonstrated vegetation progression and new aortic root abscess formation. The 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines define periannular extension and uncontrolled infection as Class I indications for urgent surgery.6 Periannular complications occur frequently in prosthetic valve IE and are associated with increased mortality,6 emphasizing the importance of repeat imaging when inflammatory markers fail to improve.

Surgical decision-making in elderly patients is complex, as frailty and comorbidity may discourage intervention. However, nationwide registry data suggest that surgery is associated with better long-term survival in selected older patients with left-sided IE.7 Although mortality remains higher compared with younger patients, outcomes in elderly cohorts can be acceptable when surgery is performed for guideline-based indications.8,9 In this case, radical debridement and reconstruction of the aortomitral curtain achieved definitive source control. The favorable midterm outcome supports guideline-directed surgical intervention when clear indications exist, regardless of chronological age.

Conclusions

Recurrent prosthetic valve IE may present atypically and progress despite appropriate antibiotics. Early multidisciplinary evaluation and timely surgical intervention—including complex reconstructive procedures—can be life-saving even in elderly, high-risk patients.
