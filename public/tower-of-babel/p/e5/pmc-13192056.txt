A cross-sectional multilevel study of bullying victimisation among adolescents: the role of body weight status and social capital across 38 countries
Authors: Ajeng Nova Dumpratiwi, Wen-Hsu Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsGiven the disadvantages of bullying victimisation on adolescent health, this study evaluates how adolescent body weight status, as the main individual-level factor, and social capital, as an essential country-level factor, affect bullying victimisation among adolescents globally.MethodsThe Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study 2017/2018 included 143,091 participants across 38 countries. It captures the main individual factor and the individual control factor. Adolescents were categorised as “Else (Thinness or Normal)” or “Overweight and Obese (OAO)” based on IOTF age- and gender-specific cut-off criteria. The 2015 Global Sustainable Competitiveness Index (GSCI) captured social capital at country-level, while the 2016 Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and the 2016 Global Gender Gap Index (GGI) served as country-level control variables. This study utilised a cross-sectional multilevel approach to address the study aims.ResultsOAO adolescents were significantly linked to an increased risk of bullying victimisation (OR = 1.17, 95% CI [1.13, 1.21], p < 0.01), consistent across male (OR = 1.17, 95% CI [1.12, 1.22], p < 0.01) and female adolescents (OR = 1.16, 95% CI [1.10, 1.23], p < 0.01). At the country level, higher social capital correlated with a reduced likelihood of being bullied (OR = 0.96, 95% CI [0.93, 0.99], p < 0.05). An important interaction between social capital and body weight status was noted (OR = 1.01, 95% CI [1.001, 1.01], p < 0.05). A statistically significant but substantively minimal cross-level interaction between body weight status and country social capital was identified, indicating low variation in the relationship across country contexts.ConclusionResearch demonstrates that bullying victimisation arises from individual vulnerabilities and overarching systemic factors. While social capital at the country-level typically provides protection, its efficacy in alleviating weight-related bullying differences is limited.

Justification for publication in BMC public health

This manuscript integrates macro-level social capital indicators and individual-level health risks across 38 countries to address a major global public health issue: bullying victimisation among adolescents. In maintaining with BMC Public Health's scope to publish research that advances knowledge and action on population health issues, the study uses a strong multilevel design. This study provides new approaches that are essential to scholars, decision-makers, and medical professionals worldwide by emphasising the relationship between body weight status and the country's social circumstances. The results directly contribute to the body of evidence in public health regarding the well-being of adolescents by endorsing cross-sector interventions.

Introduction

Olweus characterised bullying by three key elements: the intentional infliction of harm, repetition over time, and an imbalance of power between the perpetrator and the victim [1]. Bullying victimisation refers to the individual’s experience of repeated aggressive behaviour, in which they have difficulties in stopping it or defending themselves [2]. Given the substantial amount of time adolescents spend in school, bullying is recognised as a form of school violence. Bullying victimisation is becoming a global public health issue [3] and preventative priority [4]. The widespread occurrence of bullying across society, affecting individuals from childhood through adulthood in educational institutions and professions, represents a significant concern. Bullying can negatively impact adolescent development, resulting in non-suicidal self-harm, substance misuse, internalising disorders, and academic issues [5]. The detrimental effects of bullying on adolescent health cannot be overstated. Therefore, identifying the risk factors associated with bullying victimisation is essential, as it provides valuable insights for prevention efforts [3] and contributes to mitigating the development of negative life outcomes [6].

Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory [7] posits that individual development results from the interaction between personal characteristics and systemic factors. Accordingly, developmental outcomes such as bullying victimisation can be understood as the product of the dynamic interplay between individual attributes and the social contexts in which adolescents are embedded. While several previous studies have examined the interaction effects of individual factors [8] and contextual factors (e.g., social disorganisation) [9, 10] on bullying victimisation, there remains a limited understanding of how broader contextual factors, such as country-level influences, affect adolescent bullying victimisation. Consistent with this ecological perspective, the present research introduces a multilevel analytical framework that utilises a cross-country dataset to examine how individual weight status and social capital at the country-level collaboratively influence adolescent bullying victimisation.

At the individual level, body weight status has been recognised as a significant factor associated with bullying victimisation in adolescence. Previous research and meta-analyses reveal that adolescents with overweight or obesity (OAO) are particularly susceptible groups at an increased risk of experiencing bullying compared to their peers [11–13]. For example, a study of Canadian adolescents showed that both overweight and underweight adolescents are more likely to experience bullying and encounter related mental health challenges [14]. Weight-based victimisation is embedded in sociocultural norms that elevate thinness and stigmatise departures from perceived body ideals. The cultural preferences may influence perceptions of what constitutes "normal" versus "abnormal" weight status. As previous studies have demonstrated, deviation from socially accepted norms significantly increases the risk of victimisation or social estrangement among adolescents [15, 16]. Consequently, the prevailing societal trend of associating slimness with beauty standards, coupled with the rise of "sizeism"—a form of sociocultural discrimination based on body size—may heighten bullying risk for adolescents whose body weight does not conform to dominant ideals [17, 18].

At the contextual level, the prevalence of bullying victimisation demonstrates considerable variation between countries [19], reflecting broader social and structural conditions such as social poverty and the availability of social support [20, 21]. These cross-national variations emphasise the significance of investigating macro-level attributes that influence population susceptibility to bullying [22]. However, empirical evidence remains limited regarding how individual-level risk factors operate within differing national contexts. Specifically, there is limited comprehension of how structural factors interact with individual vulnerabilities to influence adolescents who encounter bullying [23].

Among these contextual determinants (e.g., familial, educational, and communal contexts), social capital is widely acknowledged as a key factor of population well-being and social behaviour [24–27]. Social capital is classically defined by Putnam as features of social organisation—such as trust, norms, and social networks—that facilitate coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit, conceptualised as a “public good” operating at the collective level [28]. From this perspective, social capital extends beyond interpersonal relationships and reflects structural attributes of societies that shape individuals’ daily environments and social interactions [10]. This aggregate conceptualisation provides a strong theoretical basis for examining social capital at the country level.

Previous studies have predominantly focused on social capital at the individual or community level [10, 29, 30], rendering these approaches potentially insufficient for cross-national analyses. This study employs the framework proposed by SolAbility Social Capital Index, which conceptualises social capital as a composite indicator of a country’s social stability and overall well-being [31, 32]. Within this framework, social capital at the country-level is understood as an external structural resource that facilitates stable social development and is accessible to citizens through institutional and societal arrangements. It is consistent with the understanding of social capital as “social resources” embedded within social systems [31]. The index encompasses five fundamental dimensions: health and healthcare factors (availability and affordability), quantitative equality within societies, freedom indicators, crime levels, and life satisfaction. Each dimension has been associated with a decrease in bullying victimisation through enhanced prevention and support, diminished social hierarchies, increased social cohesion, improved informal social control, and elevated collective well-being [24–27, 33–51]. Nevertheless, no research to date has investigated their collective impact as a country-level social capital concept concerning adolescent bullying victimisation. Despite this evidence, no previous study has systematically examined the association between social capital at the country-level, conceptualised as a multidimensional structural resource, and adolescent bullying victimisation.

Gender differences in bullying victimisation, weight status, and social capital

Gender differences are essential in understanding bullying victimisation and its risk factors. Cross-national studies show that males are more involved in bullying dynamics than females, both as perpetrators and victims, with differing forms and social meanings of victimisation by gender [38]. Gendered social norms shape differences in peer interactions and behavioural expectations during adolescence [52]. Body weight intersects with gender, as societal ideals regarding appearance, strength, and body size differ for males and females, influencing susceptibility to weight-related bullying [53, 54]. Empirical evidence on the interaction between gender and weight status in broader social contexts is limited, especially in cross-national studies.

Present study

Based on the above review, several gaps remain in understanding adolescent bullying victimisation. First, while previous studies have demonstrated that being OAO inconsistently increases the risk of being bullied among adolescents in different countries [12, 14, 18, 55], this study addresses this gap by exploring the association between weight status (i.e., OAO or non-OAO) and bullying victimisation across multiple countries. Drawing on the literature, we hypothesise that OAO adolescents are more likely to experience bullying. Second, although previous research has highlighted the importance of social capital in victimisation [56–58], the more recent conceptualisation of social capital at the country level and its association with bullying victimisation remain underexplored. Given the substantial variation in bullying victimisation rates across countries, investigating how broader country-level characteristics, specifically social capital, are linked to adolescent bullying victimisation is crucial. We hypothesise that adolescents residing in countries with higher levels of social capital are less likely to be bullied. Third, by employing a multilevel approach, this study can demonstrate the interrelationship of predictors at various levels on the outcomes of interest. Founded on the realising that in larger settings, people are often nested in higher-level units and hence susceptible to contextual impacts. To account for gender differences in bullying and weight-related stigma, this study expands this multilevel paradigm by analysing whether these correlations vary by sex. This helps determine whether body weight status, social capital, and bullying victimisation are similar for males and females. This allows us to evaluate whether the influence of body weight status on bullying victimisation changes and relies on social capital [59–61].

Methods

Data and study population

This study employed cross-sectional multilevel analysis to assess variables at individual and country levels, utilising and integrating multiple-level datasets. WHO-coordinated 2017/2018 Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) research provides individual-level data. HBSC was a cross-sectional study of 10–17-year-olds from 47 European, Central Asian, and North American countries with a 0.5-year mean deviation (N = 244,097). Sample classes from defined school years or grades can be randomly selected using two methods. 1) Computerised random sampling makes school class sampling easy. 2) Systematic sampling of the n-th class from the list involves a random starting point. Class count or total student enrolment can indicate the institution's size [62–64]. Social contexts, health habits, and adolescent well-being are covered by 120 variables in the 2017–2018 HBSC. It studies adolescent health in homes, schools, and social networks. Countries utilise a standardised questionnaire for comparison. All countries adhere to global HBSC guidelines. Optional modules may vary based on the country's interests, and all questions go through improvement and validation. The HBSC research collects self-reported data from adolescents globally through computer or paper-and-pencil questionnaires. The survey clarified to respondents that participation was voluntary and that the inquiry would remain confidential. Approval was granted by the IRB of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University (Approval No. NYCU114092AE).

Furthermore, in fulfilling the study's objective of taking into account country-level factors, the 2017/2018 HBSC dataset was combined with the 2015 Global Sustainable Competitiveness Index (GSCI), 2016 Global Gender Gap Index (GGI), and 2016 Gross Domestic Product (GDP). The 2015 GSCI captures the country's social capital index. SolAbility defined social capital for GSCI as the sum of social stability and the well-being (perceived or actual) of the entire population through these 5 indicators [32]: health and healthcare factors (availability and affordability), quantitative equality within societies, freedom indicators, crime levels, and life satisfaction. A score closer to 100 indicates better trust, civic involvement, and shared norms, suggesting country's social capital. The 2016 GGI calculates the equality between men and women in four critical areas: politics, education, health, and the economy. This is one way that the GGI monitors gender equality. Equality is the greatest attainable score, and inequality is the lowest. This equality standard is regarded as 1, which indicates full gender equality between women and men [65]. Finally, the 2016 GDP per capita in US dollars captures the country’s socioeconomic status. The GDP measures the value of final goods and services, including those bought by end users, produced in a country in a quarter or year. Everything created in a nation is included. The GDP includes market-produced commodities and services and nonmarket outputs such as government-provided defence and education [66]. Higher-income nations may have GDPs above $50,000 per capita, whereas lower-income nations may have only a few thousand dollars.

Initially, 43 countries were considered eligible based on the availability of country-level indicators. The final analytical sample comprised 143,091 adolescents from 38 countries, after omitting countries with significant missing data on essential study variables. Measures

Main outcome: bullying victimisation

The bullying victimisation variable was assessed by two inquiries from the 2017/2018 HBSC dataset: “How often have you been bullied at school in the past couple of months?” and “How often have you been cyberbullied at school in the past couple of months?”. The initial response options are “0 = Have not”, “1 = Once or twice”, “2 = 2–3 times per month”, “4 = Once a week”, and “5 = Several times a week”. Responses varied from “have not” to “several times a week.” In accordance with previous HBSC research, responses were categorised to indicate any experience of bullying victimisation, focusing on exposure rather than frequency or specific mechanisms. Through those initial responses, we classified the participating adolescents into two groups. Individuals who did not experience any type of bullying victimization ("0 = Have not") were coded as 0, whereas those who experienced any form of victimization ("1 = Once or twice", "2 = 2–3 times per month", "4 = Once a week", and "5 = Several times a week") were coded as 1.

Individual-level exposure: body weight status

Body weight status was determined using self-reported height and weight, using the following two questions: "What is your height without shoes?" “What is your weight in centimetres without shoes?” Data were measured in kilograms. Adolescents were categorised utilising age- and sex-specific cut-off points established by the International Obesity Task Force (IOTF). According to international growth reference curves, childhood and adolescent BMI levels predict adult BMI thresholds at age 18 [67]. Overweight status was determined by IOTF cut-off points at 25 kg/m2, whereas obesity was defined at 30 kg/m2. Thus, adolescents whose BMI reached or exceeded the IOTF cut-off for overweight or obesity for their age and sex were classified. Adolescents with a BMI at or below the IOTF obesity cut-off for their age and sex were classified as non-obese. For analytical purposes, weight status was classified into 0 = “Else (Thinness or Normal)” and 1 = “Overweight and Obese (OAO)” groups to improve cross-country comparability and provide sufficient statistical power.

Individual-level control variables

Demographic, psychological, and social factors are determined from the 2017/2018 HBSC data. Demographics included age and sex. Age was a continuous variable throughout the year, and teenagers self-reported their biological sex (0 = Male; 1 = Female). Psychological factors include life satisfaction, measured by "Here is a picture of a ladder. The top of the ladder, “10”, is the best possible life for you, and the bottom “0″ is the worst possible life for you. In general, where on the ladder do you feel you stand at the moment?”. This initial score was used in subsequent studies. Additionally, social aspects include school, family, and peers. For school, it will measure whether adolescents feel accepted by peers (“Other students accept me as I am”) and teachers (“I feel that my teachers accept me as I am”). These two questions offer response options of 1 = “Strongly agree”, 2 = “Agree”, 3 = “Neither/nor”, 4 = “Disagree”, and 5 = “Strongly disagree”. For the family setting, this study integrated three prior items on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree): “My family really tries to help me”, “I get the emotional help and support I need from my family”, and “I can talk about my problems with my family”. In addition to school and family settings, this study controlled for peer settings: “I can talk about my problems with my friends”. This item also uses a Likert scale ranging from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). A Likert scale from 1 to 7 indicates “very strongly disagree” to “very strongly agree.” All the variables are recoded to maintain consistency from lowest to highest.

Analytical approach

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 15.0, comprising two parts. A tabulation of population sample, individual- and country-level outcome factors was presented in the first section. The second section used the multilevel logistic regression models. The second analytical section has six models. The null model measures country bullying victimisation variance without contributing factors. We estimated the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) in the null model to investigate country-level factors on adolescent bullying victimisation [60]. Model 1 evaluated the impact of adolescents' body weight status and individual variables on bullying victimisation. Model 2 examined the effects of social capital alongside country factors such as GDP and GGI. Model 3 incorporated individual and country factors associated with the likelihood of bullying victimisation. Model 4 used a random slope model to evaluate how body weight affects bullying variance across countries. Model 5 examined how national social capital levels affect adolescents' body weight and bullying victimisation. Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria assessed model fit, and country-level variable multicollinearity was examined before model estimation. As with past HBSC studies, missing data were deleted listwise. Descriptive comparisons between included and excluded cases suggested broadly similar distributions of key demographic and psychosocial characteristics.

Before presenting the cross-country/regional results, we first examined the association between OAO and bullying victimization in each individual country/region. The findings consistently indicated that adolescents with OAO had higher odds of bullying victimization than their thin- or normal-weight peers (see Fig. 2), although not all associations reached statistical significance. The results of modelling the individual- and country-level factors on the outcome are shown in Table 3. The null model showed considerable between-country bullying victimisation variation with a random intercept variance of 0.25 (95% CI: 0.17, 0.36). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.071) implies that country variations explain 7.1% of bullying victimisation, justifying multilevel analysis. According to Models 1 and 3, OAO adolescents were more likely to be bullied than thin or normal-weight peers (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: [1.13, 1.21], p < 0.01). Discussion

This study employed a multilevel analytical approach to examine the associations between adolescents’ body weight status, country-level social capital, and experiences of bullying victimisation. In addition, higher levels of social capital at the country-level were associated with lower overall levels of bullying victimisation at the population level. A small but significant cross-level interaction further explained that although social capital generally protects adolescents, it does not fully eliminate vulnerability to weight-based bullying. The association between OAO and bullying was slightly stronger in countries with higher social capital. These findings highlight the importance of multilevel strategies that address both individual and structural factors when addressing the bullying issue among adolescents.

The individual-level findings align with numerous other studies indicating that adolescents with OAO are more susceptible to bullying compared to their counterparts without OAO [12, 14, 55, 68], even when considering life satisfaction, school relationships, and peer communication. Despite the insufficient study elucidating the mechanisms linking body weight status to bullying victimisation in teenagers, scholars broadly recognise weight-based victimisation as a plausible approach. Perceptions of normal vs abnormal weight are influenced by "sizeism" and societal norms that prioritise thinness as a beauty ideal, hence heightening the danger of bullying for individuals who do not conform. Consequently, additional research indicated that society often stigmatises and marginalises OAO adolescents [69]. Moreover, another study indicated that due to their physical appearance diverging from socially accepted body ideals, OAO adolescents are more susceptible to bullying [18].

This study indicates that country structural factors contribute to adolescents' experiences with bullying. Our findings support social capital at the country-level as a protective factor, revealing that adolescents in countries with stronger social capital are less likely to be bullied. Specifically, country social capital functions as a population-level protective environment, not as a comprehensive safeguard against individual vulnerability. However, it is still associated with a reduction in bullying victimisation among adolescents. Our research expands the focus by integrating a country perspective on social capital. Previous studies have examined social capital at both the individual and community levels [29, 70–72]. This study enhances the literature framework by modelling social capital at the country-level through five indicators: healthcare access, equality, freedom, safety, and life satisfaction. A study by Hennig & Laier in 2023 indicates that social capital, defined as “social resources” within systems, protects adolescents from bullying [31]. A robust health care system in a country-aids in preventing bullying through enhanced case reporting, access to care, and harm protection [33, 34, 73, 74]. Inequality in society creates power imbalances that may normalise and exacerbate bullying among adolescents [36–38]. Freedom fosters social cohesion and mutual trust, whereas its absence may increase social alienation and vulnerability [40, 75]. Crime develops conditions that promote exposure to dangerous behaviours, increasing people’s susceptibility to victimisation [76, 77]. Finally, higher life satisfaction levels are associated with more proactive, trusting, and cooperative communities, all of which reduce bullying involvement [56, 57]. These findings show that enhancing national social capital may prevent bullying by promoting inclusiveness, safety, and collective responsibility.

Previous research and these study findings generally suggest that obesity increases the risk of being bullied [12, 14, 55, 68] and social capital protects adolescents from victimisation [33, 34, 73, 74]. This study also demonstrated evidence of a weak cross-level interaction, suggesting that while national social capital generally protects against bullying, the disparity in bullying between OAO and non-OAO adolescents becomes slightly more prominent in situations with higher social capital. Importantly, this finding should not be interpreted as bullying being more frequent in countries with higher social capital; on the contrary, our results show that these countries tend to have lower overall levels of bullying. However, in such environments, where norms of health, inclusion, and well-being are strongly upheld, physical differences like obesity may become more noticeable [78–80]. This heightened visibility can lead to a slightly greater bullying gap between OAO adolescents, even though bullying itself remains less common overall. Related literature offers a potential explanation for why people with deviant physical appearances may stand out more in a high social capital country, making weight-based stigmatisation more evident [78–80]. Social capital frequently operates within systems or group memberships, which results in insiders being given privileges while those who do not fit the group ideal are marginalised [80, 81]. It describes how OAO adolescents, whose physical characteristics deviate from accepted health standards, may become more socially prominent and more susceptible to stigmatisation. Furthermore, strong social standards in a nation with stronger social capital can greatly influence people's judgment [79]. In summary, social capital is thought to promote well-being within the system, but it also works against "ousiders" of the system, limiting their access to social resources, particularly in preventing victimisation in this case [78]. In other words, even though the environment is generally safe, their strong internal norms and idealised expectations may unintentionally increase the visibility of physical deviance, reinforcing subtle forms of exclusion and weight-based victimisation.

Gender differences further complicate the variations in body weight, social capital, and experiences of bullying victimisation. Males generally report higher rates of both bullying and victimisation compared to girls, partly reflecting gendered norms that emphasise toughness and penalise deviation from expected masculine or feminine roles [38, 52]. Adolescents who challenge gender norms, regardless of sex, are at a higher risk of being bullied [82]. Body weight encounters criticism in industrialised nations. Slimmer women are more appealing, whereas muscular males are more attractive and stronger [53, 54], which contributes to weight-related stigma and bullying victimisation [12, 83]. However, scientific evidence on disparities in weight status and bullying victimisation differs by sex and context [12, 84, 85]. Although there are minor variations in statistical significance by sex, the nearly equal odds ratios and overlapping confidence intervals suggest very little meaningful sex-based difference in the magnitude of the association between country-level social capital and susceptibility to weight-related bullying. These findings indicate that gender substantially influences the social significance and expression of body weight stigma, yet the broader structural context (e.g. national social capital) functions similarly for both males and females. Social capital may additionally affect these gendered experiences by supporting existing social norms and shaping collective reactions to victimisation [28, 86].

These findings provide evidence that higher-level factors determine individual experience, especially among adolescents experiencing body-weight-related stigma. The study contribution is particularly essential given that previous studies related social capital more often emphasised interpersonal trust, cohesion, or community ties. By contrast, this study emphasises the role of macro-level structures by using the healthcare system, equality, freedom, crime, and life satisfaction within society as indicators of this novel perspective, which are shaped by governmental and institutional intervention. In addition, this study can expand the understanding of bullying and potential determining factors, as well as emphasise the relevance of structural interventions to bullying. Therefore, this study not only extends theoretical insight but also informs policy by offering evidence for governments and stakeholders to foster a protective social environment through the regulations they design and implement.

Strengths and limitations

This study enhances comprehension of the global prevalence of [37, 87] by utilising a validated index of national social conditions encompassing healthcare access, equality, freedom, safety, and life satisfaction [32]. The strengths comprise the utilisation of an extensive, standardised dataset spanning 38 countries, the implementation of multilevel modelling to address nested data [60, 88], and the careful selection and validation of theoretically informed indicators. Furthermore, gender-stratified analyses facilitated the exploration of intersectional disparities in victimisation, hence improving the clarity of the findings.

Notwithstanding these advantages, numerous constraints are present. Initially, social desirability and recall bias may influence self-reported data, and several measurements were not validated scales. In addition, this study examined traditional and cyberbullying as one bullying victimisation measure. Secondly, body weight status was categorised into two distinct groups, consequently simplifying its association with bullying while enhancing cross-national comparability. The cross-sectional methodology restricts causal inferences, and exclusion resulting from missing data may have led to cautious prevalence estimates. Future research should employ longitudinal and intersectional methodologies, alongside multisectoral and culturally contextualised strategies for bullying prevention. Furthermore, factors not included in this study, such as school climate, peer norms, family socioeconomic status, and other relevant cultural contexts, should be considered for future research.

Conclusion

This study presents cross-national evidence that individual and contextual factors influence bullying victimisation. OAO increases the risk of victimisation, while social capital at the country-level appears to protect against bullying. Social capital does not fully eliminate weight-based vulnerability, suggesting that stigma remains present even in protective settings. This study contributes to the literature by emphasising the importance of macro-level influences on adolescent well-being from an objective, structural perspective of social capital. These findings suggest that creating a protective environment against bullying requires structural interventions by government and public stakeholders through policy and social investment. This study models social capital through structural indicators such as healthcare access, equality, freedom, safety, and life satisfaction, building on prior research that focused mainly on interpersonal or community-level social capital. A robust healthcare system facilitates early detection and support for victimised adolescents, while enhanced equality and safety can reduce power imbalances that normalise bullying behaviours. Higher life satisfaction correlates with enhanced social well-being, associated with more cohesive and supportive communities. The study highlights the necessity of multilevel strategies addressing individual risk factors and broader societal contexts, identifying social capital at the county-level as a key protective factor for adolescent health and safety.
