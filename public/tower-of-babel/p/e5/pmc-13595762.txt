Social and attitudinal predictors of tobacco use among Peruvian adolescents: a Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) of the Theory of Planned Behavior using Global Youth Tobacco Survey 2019
Authors: Dennis Calle, Juana Cruz-Montero, Esteban Durand-Gonzáles, Myriam Griselda Lora-Loza, Luis Hualparuca-Olivera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe use of manufactured tobacco cigarettes and e-cigarettes continues to be an important determinant of adverse health outcomes during adolescence, especially in Latin America, despite existing prevention policies. In Peru, there is a stabilization in cigarette consumption and a notable increase in the use of Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems (ENDS), which requires new explanatory studies. Based on the Theory of Planned Behavior, the aim was to analyze how subjective norms and attitudes toward tobacco predict the intention and subsequent consumption of manufactured and e-cigarettes among Peruvian adolescents.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study using secondary data from the Global Youth Tobacco Survey 2019 in Peru. The sample included 2593 school adolescents aged 13 to 15 from different regions of the country. Structural Equation Modeling was applied to evaluate a partial version of the Theory of Planned Behavior including the effects of exposure to tobacco consumption in environments close to adolescents such as the home, school, and other settings (descriptive subjective norms), as well as attitudes toward tobacco on the intention to use tobacco products, and the predictive effect of this intention on frequency of manufactured and e-cigarette consumption. Perceived Behavioral Control was not included in the model due to an insufficient number of indicators.ResultsExposure to tobacco consumption at home and in adolescents’ everyday environments was a significant predictor of the intention to use tobacco products, whereas exposure at school showed a weaker and not significant effect. Negative attitudes toward tobacco predicted lower intention, indicating a protective cognitive factor. Together, these variables explained 32% of the variance in the intention to consume tobacco. In turn, the intention to use tobacco products was significantly associated with both manufactured cigarette use and e-cigarette use, with a stronger predictive effect for manufactured cigarettes than for e-cigarettes, explaining 79% and 37% of their variance, respectively.ConclusionsManufactured and e-cigarette consumption among Peruvian adolescents represents distinct behavioral outcomes that merit examination in relation to antecedents such as tobacco-related use intention, social norms, and attitudes. Preventive interventions should address both the family and public environment while also targeting adolescents’ cognitive perceptions about tobacco, especially in a context of increasing e-cigarette use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28356-3.Keywords: Tobacco consumption, Cigarette, Vapers, Adolescents, Theory of Planned Behavior

Background

Smoking is one of the leading public health problems worldwide. Each year it causes more than 7 million deaths and significant disease-related disability [1]. High tobacco use represents a major risk factor for the development of cardiovascular, respiratory, and other diseases, with potentially fatal consequences for adolescents [2]. Internationally, the current prevalence of tobacco use affects 19.3% of the population across 133 countries, with proportions of adolescent who smoke reaching 23.29% among males and 15.35% among females [3]. Furthermore, a study conducted in 144 countries reported that smoking initiation typically occurred between ages 12 and 13, coinciding with entry into secondary education [4]. In this context, the World Health Organization (WHO) warns of the need to strengthen scientific evidence to improve prevention of risks associated with manufactured cigarettes and other forms of tobacco, especially among youth populations [5].

In Latin America, 37 million of adolescents under the age of 15 use manufactured tobacco cigarettes, a still concerning statistic [6], while the Global School-based Student Health Survey (GSHS) reported that more than 40% of respondents in South American countries experienced secondhand exposure to tobacco use, which may increase the risk of initiating smoking. Thus, despite governmental prevention efforts, the prevalence of manufactured tobacco cigarettes remains unchanged in at least 40 countries [1, 7].

This public health and social problems have been no exception in Peru. A study using the Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS) assessed the prevalence of tobacco use among Peruvian adolescents in 2007, 2014, and 2019 [8, 9], and found a progressive decline in tobacco use among Peruvian adolescents aged 12 to 16, in 2019, while 36.3% reported exposure to secondhand smoke. On the other hand,, in a study of more than 1,500 students aged 11 to 17 in Lima and Callao, concluded that 12% used cigarettes, and that this nicotine addiction was influenced by perceived academic performance, peer pressure from friends who smoked, and other risky attitudes, whereas parental affection and monitoring reduced the likelihood of tobacco use [10]. More recently, reports from 2024 indicated that tobacco use among students aged 13 to 15 once again reached 9%, and Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems (ENDS) such as electronic cigarettes (e-cigarette) use increased to 10.8% [11]. This contrasts with the 2014–2019 period, when consumption had decreased from 9% to 7%, while e-cigarettes use rose from 2.4% to 6% [12]. Youth initiation and regular consumption of e-cigarettes may have increased prior to the recent 2024 Peruvian regulations prohibiting sales to minors, while flavor marketing and public figures normalized consumption, creating false perceptions regarding the potential health risks and the addictive potential of nicotine-containing e-cigarettes [13, 14].

The Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS 2019) is a database that can be highly useful for examining factors influencing tobacco-related problems in several countries, including Peru, where few updated studies have been conducted using these data [8, 15]. Although it is reliable and sponsored by PAHO [16], further studies are still needed in Peru compared to research conducted in other Latin American countries such as Brazil, Argentina, Chile, and Mexico, where findings based on this public data have been implemented. Although Peru ratified the Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, it remains one of the countries with the lowest compliance with Monitor, Protect, Offer, Warn, Enforce, Raise (MPOWER) strategies for tobacco control, highlighting the urgent need to analyze multiple factors that perpetuate this behavior [17, 18].

As a way to translate the understanding of tobacco and e-cigarette use into evidence among adolescents, it is pertinent to consider the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). This predictive model is situated within the social-cognitive framework for explaining behavior, which is not solely an individual matter of attitudes, but is also linked to social factors that give rise to intentions and subsequent behaviors [19]. In this context, at least three key factors influencing behavioral intentions are identified. The first refers to subjective norms, that is, exposure to and internalization of habits of significant others or media advertising within one’s immediate environment. The second component involves attitudes, which are formed based on an individual’s beliefs about the consequences and benefits of an activity, leading the person to assign a positive or negative value to performing it. The third factor concerns control beliefs, which are perceptions that hinder or facilitate the ease with which a person can carry out an action [20]. These aspects have been examined in various studies with adolescents, demonstrating how learning and social context can influence the adoption of healthy or harmful behaviors, as seen in research on adolescent sexual behavior, eating habits, and substance use [21–23].

The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB), applied to adolescent or potential people who smoke, has been validated in various countries and provides relevant evidence for its applicability in similar contexts. First, studies consistently show that social exposure to tobacco, including having family members or peers who smoke, as well as exposure to advertising, together with favorable attitudes toward consumption, predict a greater intention to use tobacco and direct use [24–26]. Additionally, greater perceived behavioral control, expressed as the ability to refuse cigarette offers, is associated with lower consumption intention, whereas low levels of such control increase that intention [26]. Exposure to anti-tobacco policies and messages, in turn, is associated with a higher likelihood of attempting to quit or directly ceasing tobacco use during adolescence [27, 28]. Second, other studies have shown that expressing an intention to consume tobacco within the next six months or one year was also a significant predictor of tobacco or similar product use [29, 30]. Taken together, this evidence suggests that isolated beliefs such as “smoking is harmful to health” do not necessarily constitute a sufficient protective factor, but must be analyzed in interaction with contexts of social exposure to tobacco and short- or long-term intentions, which may ultimately shape actual consumption [26].

Based on the Theory of Planned Behavior and the aforementioned evidence, the first hypothesis (H1) proposes that favorable attitudes or beliefs toward tobacco and greater social exposure to its use (family, peers, and advertising) positively predict adolescents’ intention to use tobacco, as these factors contribute to the normalization of consumption and the development of positive cognitive evaluations of the behavior. Likewise, the second hypothesis (H2) posits that the intention to use tobacco positively predicts actual tobacco consumption, insofar as intention represents the proximal antecedent of behavior and reflects the individual’s motivational readiness to perform it. Together, these hypotheses allow for a sequential examination of the cognitive and contextual mechanisms that lead to tobacco use during adolescence.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate a predictive model comprising two pathways: (1) the paths linking both social factors and attitudes with tobacco use intention, and (2) a path linking such intention with the separate consumption of tobacco (manufactured cigarettes) and e-cigarettes.

Methodology

Participants

This study was conducted using data from the 2019 Global Youth Tobacco Survey (GYTS), which evaluates tobacco use among secondary school adolescents in Peru [9]. This strictly adheres to GYTS methodological standards, which promote the direct comparability of findings at both national and global levels. In particular, it follows the WHO’s standard international age stratification, which prioritizes a homogeneous assessment of development, typically distinguishing between adolescents aged 13 to 15 and those older than 15, thereby maintaining consistency with global surveillance frameworks [1]. The survey was carried out by the National Center for Epidemiology, Prevention, and Disease Control, part of the Ministry of Health of Peru. It included students from 14 departments, including Lima, Callao, Ayacucho, Arequipa, Cajamarca, Cusco, Piura, Junín, San Martín, La Libertad, Lambayeque, Puno, and Loreto [31]. The original sample consisted of 4,148 students from first to fifth year of secondary education. After filtering for ages 13 to 15, the final sample comprised 2593 students.

Procedure

The 2019 GYTS Peru was conducted by the National Center for Epidemiology, Prevention, and Disease Control of the Ministry of Health of Peru, with technical support from the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [31]. It was specifically administered in public and private institutions, with an emphasis on obtaining results from the school population aged 13 to 15 years. The questionnaire was self-administered, anonymous, and completed using optical forms with automated reading, following parental consent.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee No. 00084-2025/CEI-PDE of Universidad César Vallejo. Since the research used an existing database, this adhered to the ethical guidelines of the WHO and CDC. This ensured that the original data collected by the Ministry of Health of Peru complied with parental consent and the protection of minors’ identities. This process was guided by the principles of respect for the dignity and autonomy of the participants.

Instrument

Global Youth Tobacco Survey Peru 2019 (GYTS) [32]. It is a self-report questionnaire sponsored by the World Health Organization (WHO) to measure indicators related to tobacco and e-cigarette use among adolescents, specifically frequency of use, exposure to secondhand smoke, and school adolescents’ attitudes toward tobacco.

From the full questionnaire, specific items were used for the following Planned Behavior Theory dimensions: Subjective Norms-1, were related to witnessing people smoking at school, with reliability measured by omega coefficient: 0.55. Subjective Norms-2, which included two questions about exposure to tobacco at home and in places frequented by adolescents, with an omega reliability of 0.73. Both measures of subjective norms are aligned with the concept of descriptive norms within the Reasoned Action framework, as they capture individuals’ perceptions of how other people behave. These perceptions may be interpreted as indirect social influences that shape one’s intentions and behavior [41]. Attitudes toward tobacco consisted of three questions regarding support for smoking bans, with omega reliability: 0.62. Tobacco use intentions were measured with two questions regarding acceptance of future consumption, with omega reliability: 0.81. Finally, two questions assessed current frequency of use of manufactured cigarettes, with omega reliability: 0.75, and e-cigarettes: 0.76.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the survey package in R [33] to apply sampling weights, primary sampling units (PSU), and stratification according to the standardized guidelines of the Global Youth Tobacco Survey [7]. The survey employed a standardized two-stage sampling design: schools were selected with probability proportional to enrollment size, and classes within selected schools were randomly chosen, where all students in those classes were eligible. The formulas used for weighting the data are available on the GYTS Peru 2019 website under the Sampling Sect [34]. Analyses focused primarily on Peruvian adolescents aged 13 to 15 years, the age group recommended by GYTS for its representativeness of the target population.

Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was based on the Theory of Planned Behavior [19], accounting for measurement error compared to previous studies. The model comprised two pathways: (1) paths linking social factors and attitudes to tobacco use intention, and (2) a path linking this intention to the separate consumption of tobacco (manufactured cigarettes) and e-cigarettes. We excluded the construct of perceived behavioral control due to the lack of those multiple indicators in the survey, which prevented robust analysis as a latent variable. Missing data analysis using Little’s test indicated a completely random missing mechanism (MCAR, p > 0.05) with missing values below 5% in the variables of interest. Pairwise deletion was applied, a method that preserves sample size and is efficient for handling missing data under least squares estimators in large samples [35, 36].

Structural modeling was conducted using the lavaan.survey package [37], accounting for the previously applied weights, PSU, and stratification. Binary and/or ordinal items were analyzed within a single analytical framework. Diagonally Weighted Least Squares (DWLS) estimator was used, which is the recommended standard for analysis with categorical indicators using polychoric and tetrachoric correlation matrices [38]. Model fit was evaluated using CFI and TLI indices (cutoff > 0.95), RMSEA (< 0.06), and SRMR (< 0.08), following the rigorous criteria proposed by Hu & Bentler [39].

Planned Behavior (PB) model

The evaluation of the structural model, which integrated descriptive subjective norms, attitudes, tobacco use intention, tobacco and e-cigarette consumption, demonstrated an overall adequate fit to the observed data. The goodness-of-fit statistic χ² was non-significant (χ² = 36.9, df = 57, p = 0.95), which indicates that the proposed model was consistent with the observed data structure. The comparative and non-normed fit indices were satisfactory (CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99), exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.95 [39]. Likewise, other error indices were optimal (RMSEA = 0.00), meeting the criterion of excellence of being below 0.06. Finally, the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR = 0.04) was also acceptable and confirmed the validity of the proposed structure to represent the relationships among the study variables.

After analyzing the structural model, first hypothesized paths were significant, except exposure to observing others smoking at school (Subjective Norms 1) – tobacco use intention (β = 0.06, p = 0.53). Exposure to tobacco at home and in the adolescent’s usual environments (Subjective Norms 2) positively predicted the tobacco use intention (INT; β = 0.42, p < 0.001). Negative attitudes toward tobacco (ATT) had an inverse path on the intention to use tobacco products (β = −0.29, p < 0.01). Regarding second paths, intention to use tobacco positively predicted the frequency of manufactured cigarette consumption (CON1; β = 0.89, p < 0.001) and e-cigarette consumption (CON2; β = 0.61, p < 0.001). Furthermore, other relationships were observed among social/attitudinal constructs: SN2–SN1 (r = 0.18, p < 0.05) and ATT–SN1 (r = − 0.27, p < 0.05).

Overall, the model explained 32% of the variance in the tobacco use intention (R² INT = 0.32, 32%), 79% of the variance in the frequency of tobacco (manufactured cigarette) consumption (R² CON1 = 0.79, 79%), and 37% of the variance in the frequency of e-cigarette use (R² INT = 0.37, 37%). All observed variables showed adequate factor loadings on their respective latent constructs, indicating an appropriate representation of the theoretical dimensions of the model. All loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.01). This is shown graphically in Fig. 1. Complementary analyses including participants aged 11–17 years yielded results largely consistent with the primary 13-15-year analysis. In summary, the model aimed to represent the theoretical assumptions of a partial version of the Theory of Planned Behavior, in which subjective norms and attitudinal factors influence adolescents’ tobacco use intention, which in turn predicts tobacco use and e-cigarette use behavior. Each structural path represents a directional relationship: exposure to tobacco use in proximal environments (home and public spaces) increased tobacco use intention, whereas negative attitudes toward tobacco reduced it. Furthermore, positive paths from intention to consumption indicated that stronger intentions to use tobacco predicted higher levels of actual cigarette and e-cigarette use, separately.

Discussion

This study represents one of the first studies applying the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB) through Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) in Peru. Although it employs a partial version of the model without the Perceived Behavioral Control component, findings revealed that tobacco consumption has precursors such as consumption intention, which is shaped by family and attitudinal variables among Peruvian adolescents, findings that are particularly relevant given the changing epidemiological context in the country. Although between 2014 and 2019 overall tobacco use among adolescents aged 13 to 15 declined from 9% to 7%, the use of e-cigarettes increased from 2.4% to 6% in Peru [12]. Moreover, recent findings indicate a concerning stabilization, placing tobacco consumption again at 9% while raising the use of vaping products to 10.8% [11].

Beyond prevalence estimates, this study included manufactured and e-cigarettes as separate outcomes, highlighting the need for coordinated prevention efforts [1].

Despite the widespread application of the Planned Behavior framework in studies on adolescent smoking in regions such as Asia, the Middle East, and Europe [23, 25, 40], there is a notable scarcity of research applying TPB specifically in the South American context. This gap in the literature makes the findings of the present study particularly valuable for evaluating and informing prevention policies adapted to the sociocultural realities of the Andean and South American regions.

The initial results of the model revealed that the exposure hypothesis effectively influenced tobacco use intention. Exposure to tobacco in the home and in the adolescent’s usual environments (subjective norms 2) was the strongest predictor of the intention to smoke, whereas exposure to others smoking at school (subjective norms 1) was not a significant predictor. It’s important to highlight that these indicators represent the descriptive dimension of subjective norms, with a meaning of perceived prevalence and social modeling, which is an established component of the TPB framework for capturing normative influence based on behaviors of others that predict intention to consume [41]. In that sense, they do not capture perceived approval or disapproval of what individuals should do, as oriented by others, and may reflect social learning rather than normative pressure in the strict TPB sense.

Overall, these findings are consistent with the international literature documenting the central role of the family environment in adolescent tobacco use. In Botswana, using the TPB framework, adolescents with mothers or female guardians who smoked were found to be 2.7 times more likely to have tried any form of tobacco, while those with fathers or male guardians who smoked were 1.7 times more likely to have tried cigarettes [25]. Similarly, in Saudi Arabia, studies employing TPB with GYTS data reported that subjective norms reflecting perceived social pressure were positively associated with tobacco use [24]. The relatively weaker statistical effect of exposure at school, although still relevant, may be explained by smoke-free policies in educational institutions that have reduced the visibility of smoking in that environment, or because it may be less direct or explanatory than the influence of closer peers in the school context [42].

On the other hand, it was expected that negative attitudes toward tobacco would have a protective effect on the tobacco use intention, which was confirmed in that individual cognitions prevented adolescents from engaging in high levels of tobacco use. This finding aligns with previous research such as that conducted in Saudi Arabia, where negative attitudes toward tobacco were inversely associated with tobacco use [24]. Likewise, in Indonesia and Botswana, attitudes describing emotional reward from tobacco were associated with a greater intention to smoke [25, 43]. Overall, these findings suggest that adolescents with greater risk awareness and rejection of smoking as a social behavior in any setting are more likely to report lower smoking intentions, particularly if they perceive exposure to others smoking at home or at school as unpleasant.

The second set of paths analyzed according to the Theory of Planned Behavior confirmed that tobacco use intention positively predicts consumption. This support for the idea that intention is an antecedent of actual consumption among adolescents is consistent with other international studies, including research in Saudi Arabia, China, and even studies on other products such as marijuana use among adolescents in the United States [24, 26, 30]. However, it is important to note that the predictive power of intention to use tobacco differed substantially, as it accounted for a much larger proportion of the variance in manufactured cigarette use (79%) than in e-cigarette use (37%). This differential association with e-cigarette use is empirically significant and is consistent with a recent systematic review reporting that adolescents who intend to try tobacco products may also be more susceptible to extending these intentions to the experimentation and increased use of e-cigarettes [44]. However, this lower proportion of explained variance may be due to the existence of other, stronger predictors, such as traditional or influencer advertising, appealing flavors, and the perceived harmlessness of this specific product, which should be examined in future studies. The findings of the present study suggest that in the Peruvian context there is a relatively strong translation of adolescents’ declared intentions into a higher frequency of e-cigarettes use in addition to manufactured cigarettes, which is consistent with the increasing use of these products in recent years [11].

Thus, the robustness of the intention-behavior link people who smoke is evident even in this South American context, highlighting the need for preventive approaches focused on modifying intentions as a strategy to reduce high levels of tobacco consumption behaviors. At the population level, for example, Law No. 32,159 (2024–2025) has been enacted, prohibiting tobacco use in public, enclosed, and transportation spaces; however, the current tax rate (73.3%) is still below the recommended 75%, leaving room for improvement [18].

The distinctive feature of the present TPB model that should be interpreted with caution is that perceived behavioral control (PBC) was not included. PBC refers to individuals’ perceptions of the ease or difficulty of performing a behavior, including refusal skills or environmental constraints, and has been identified in the international literature as another significant predictor of smoking intentions or behaviors among adolescents. This has been reported in studies with adolescents from Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, and Botswana, where PBC emerged as a relevant protective factor against smoking, and even findings reflecting an apparent sense of control over quitting smoking among people who smoke [24, 25, 43]. The omission of PBC in the present study may explain why the model accounted for only 32% of the variance in tobacco use intentions, a similar value to others reported, for example, 32% using the full set of TPB variables [43] .However, a key distinction is that in our study the SEM approach modeled measurement error, which was not done in previous studies using regression methods. Future research in Peruvian adolescent populations should incorporate measures of PBC to assess whether perceptions of self-efficacy and behavioral control over tobacco use add significant predictive power to the model.

Overall, the integration of social factors (subjective norms) and attitudinal factors in the model allows us to understand that adolescents’ tobacco use intention is not the result of unexpected or random events, but rather of a complex interaction between environmental pressures and individual cognitive evaluations. This underscores the need for multilevel interventions that simultaneously address the family environment and personal perceptions about tobacco.

According to the results, it is recommended that public preventive strategies or campaigns in Peru combine not only warnings about the risks of physical harm by combustible tobacco products and physiological damage (such as damage to the skin, organs, dental problems, etc.) [45], but also deeper interventions aimed at understanding the underlying external and internal motives for tobacco use, such as exposure to the social environment and the adolescent’s cognitive-attitudinal structure. Preventive interventions could focus not necessarily on framing consumption primarily as a long-term danger, since the identity of many adolescents may be shaped more strongly by the experiences and behaviors of close contacts or peers, which can reduce the effectiveness of health-risk messages. This dual approach is highlighted because, although Peru has made commendable progress in implementing population-level strategies addressing social norms (especially Monitor (M), Protect (P) and Offer (O) components from MPOWER framework), there is a gap about individual-level decision-making, which is still highly influenced by subtle industry tactics that need to be taken into account for anti-tobacco mass media campaigns (W) and bans on tobacco advertising (E) [18]. Particularly relevant is a preventive approach that recognizes that the initiation of consumption does not occur in isolation, but may emerge from the home environment or peer groups. For this reason, it is important to promote specialized programs aimed at parents and teachers within the school context, designed to prevent the formation of consumption intentions. These programs may include updated information about new Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems (ENDS), their harmful effects, and their growing presence on social media and streaming platforms, where influencers or models who use these products are often visible. Finally, recent evidence supports the implementation of comprehensive preventive strategies. For instance, a recent meta-analysis conducted in low- and middle-income countries indicates that joint programs are increasingly being considered. These programs tend to be experiential or practical in nature, aiming to avoid information fatigue and targeting parents, schools, and peers simultaneously. This approach recognizes that these actors constitute key risk factors that must be addressed in a coordinated manner in order to prevent the use and abuse of various nicotine products [46].

Limitations

One of the most important limitations is that the self-reported data from the GYTS may contain some information bias, particularly related to social desirability. Although anonymity and standardization reduce this risk, tobacco use may be underestimated among many adolescents. In addition, because our study employed a cross-sectional design, predictive relationships in the model should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causality. Also, it is important to recognize that out-of-school adolescents may have higher consumption than those attending school, so this data gap in Peru needs to be addressed. Despite this limitation, the GYTS remains an important and comprehensive standard for monitoring global trends, especially in low- and middle-income contexts such as Latin America. Another limitation to be addressed in future research is that the study did not examine in depth the patterns of use and the determinants of differential normalization across various types of tobacco consumption, which would help clarify whether the model’s effects vary depending on the product. Additionally, the omission of perceived behavioral control in the evaluated model due to the limited measurement of this relevant aspect for both tobacco users and non-users in the GYTS Peru 2019 may have influenced the variance explained in the final tobacco use behavior. Another consideration is that the results cannot be generalized to the entire country, as the study included data from only approximately half of the existing departments. Nevertheless, the sample was highly diverse, encompassing the three natural regions (coast, highlands, and jungle). It is imperative that future research strengthen representativeness in the jungle region, where the GYTS included only two departments, particularly by incorporating the department of Madre de Dios, which 2024 reports indicate has the highest levels of tobacco use among students [11]. More up to-date data on adolescents are needed to determine the progress of smoking and e-cigarette use prevention beyond advertising or sales bans, with greater emphasis on relevant individual, family, and social factors.

Conclusions

It was found that the use of manufactured tobacco cigarettes and Electronic Nicotine Delivery Systems among Peruvian adolescents is a structured and predictable phenomenon influenced by subjective or social norms and attitudinal factors. The first path identified that exposure to places where people smoke, including the home and school, as well as having negative attitudes toward cigarettes, were critical predictors of the tobacco use intention. The second path demonstrated that this future intention explained a significant proportion of the final tobacco use and e-cigarette use behavior. These findings suggest that Peruvian public policies should not only consider sales bans, but also address the family and school micro-environment and reduce the positive valence of tobacco consumption intention through the modification of affective attitudes, especially in a context of increasing electronic cigarette consumption. Multilevel strategies are imperative, treating intention as a variable of epidemiological control, which would allow the prevention of addiction before its chronic consolidation.
