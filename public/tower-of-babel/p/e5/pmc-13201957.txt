Expanded endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal resection of a rare concurrent sellar pituitary adenoma and suprasellar abscess: illustrative case

Sellar abscesses are rare entities, with fewer than 500 cases reported in the literature to date.1 The pathological basis of a sellar abscess is distinct from that of common sellar lesions like pituitary adenomas. Although both conditions can present with visual disturbances and headaches, their management principles differ fundamentally. The coexistence of these two independent pathologies in a single patient is exceptionally rare. The first postmortem identification of a pituitary abscess within an adenoma was reported by Whalley2 in 1952. Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging due to the lack of specific symptoms and pathognomonic imaging features. The endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal approach, offering superior visualization, minimally invasive access, and capability for precise dissection, provides an ideal solution for the simultaneous diagnosis and management of such composite lesions. This report presents a rare concurrent sellar pituitary adenoma and suprasellar abscess that was successfully treated via a single-stage endoscopic endonasal procedure.

Illustrative Case

A 47-year-old female was admitted with a 6-month history of progressive bilateral visual decline, which worsened over the preceding 2 weeks and was accompanied by vomiting. Neurological examination revealed bitemporal hemianopia. Preoperative endocrine evaluation revealed mildly decreased free thyroxine and cortisol levels, along with mildly elevated prolactin. Laboratory tests showed normal white blood cell count and C-reactive protein (CRP). Preoperative MRI demonstrated an irregular sellar-suprasellar mass, suggestive of a pituitary macroadenoma.

Preoperative gadolinium-enhanced brain MRI revealed an approximately 2.5 × 1.3–cm multicystic lesion with irregular rim enhancement, causing upward displacement of the optic chiasm. The radiological report favored a diagnosis of pituitary adenoma, while craniopharyngioma could not be excluded (Fig. 1). The patient underwent an expanded endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal approach for resection of the sellar lesion. Intraoperatively, typical pituitary adenoma tissue was encountered in the sellar region. The abscess was located in the suprasellar region. On opening the dura mater over the tuberculum sellae and the diaphragma sellae, thick yellow purulent material was observed (Fig. 2). Under endoscopic visualization, the intrasellar tumor tissue was completely resected, and the suprasellar abscess cavity was thoroughly evacuated along with its wall. Intraoperative samples of the pus and tissue were sent for microbiological culture and histopathological examination. Complete resection was followed by multilayer skull base reconstruction, which included a pedicled nasoseptal flap for cranial base repair. Histopathological examination revealed two distinct pathological entities. Sections from the sellar lesion showed nests of adenohypophyseal cells consistent with a nonfunctioning pituitary adenoma (Fig. 3A). Sections from the abscess wall demonstrated dense infiltration by neutrophils and necrotic debris, confirming acute suppurative inflammation (Fig. 3B). There was no evidence of infection within the adenoma tissue itself. Microbiological cultures of both pus and tissue samples were negative. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for synaptophysin and chromogranin A and negative for all pituitary hormones (prolactin, growth hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone, luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, and thyroid-stimulating hormone), consistent with a nonfunctioning pituitary neuroendocrine tumor. The postoperative diagnosis was established as “pituitary adenoma with concurrent sellar abscess.” The patient developed fever and transient diabetes insipidus postoperatively. Despite repeatedly negative CSF and abscess culture results, based on clear intraoperative and histopathological evidence of suppuration, the patient received a 6-week course of empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy (meropenem and vancomycin). Infection resolution was monitored by daily clinical assessment, temperature trends, serial white blood cell counts, CRP measurements, and CSF analysis. All parameters normalized by the 3rd week of antibiotic therapy. Her postoperative recovery was smooth, with significant improvement in visual symptoms prior to discharge.

At the 6-month outpatient follow-up, a repeat brain MRI study demonstrated complete resolution of the sellar lesion and decompression of the optic chiasm (Fig. 4). The patient's vision continued to improve, and her endocrine function remained stable. Discussion

Observations

This case presents several key observations relevant to the management of concurrent pituitary adenoma and suprasellar abscess. First, the patient’s clinical presentation—progressive visual decline over 6 months without systemic signs of infection—was indistinguishable from that of a typical nonfunctioning pituitary adenoma. Second, preoperative MRI revealed a multicystic lesion with irregular rim enhancement, initially raising suspicion for craniopharyngioma rather than infection. Third, the diagnosis of abscess was made only intraoperatively on encountering thick yellow purulent material in the suprasellar region, highlighting the limitations of preoperative imaging in detecting infection when it is encapsulated within tumor tissue. Fourth, histopathological analysis confirmed the coexistence of two distinct pathologies: a nonfunctioning pituitary adenoma and acute suppurative inflammation, with no evidence of infection within the adenoma itself. Finally, despite negative cultures, the patient responded favorably to a 6-week course of empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy guided by intraoperative findings and histopathological evidence of suppuration, underscoring the importance of treatment based on clinical and pathological correlation rather than microbiological results alone.

The diagnostic difficulty in our case reflects the nonspecific features of pituitary abscesses coexisting with adenomas. Our patient was afebrile with normal inflammatory markers—observed in up to 70% of reported cases1—likely due to the immunologically privileged sellar region or encapsulation of infection within tumor tissue. MRI showed no typical ring enhancement, as the small abscess was embedded within the larger adenoma. This aligns with reports that up to 80% of such cases are misdiagnosed preoperatively as pituitary adenoma or cyst.3,4 The key lesson is that when clinical presentation (e.g., acute visual deterioration) does not fully align with imaging, or when unexplained fever or infection risk factors are present, pituitary abscess should be considered. This awareness mandates meticulous intraoperative handling to prevent CSF contamination and necessitates sending specimens for both aerobic and anaerobic cultures.

Regarding pathogenesis, secondary abscesses within adenomas may arise from hematogenous spread, direct extension from adjacent infection, or secondary infection following tumor infarction or hemorrhage.4,5 Our patient had no identifiable source, suggesting possible subclinical bacteremia or bacterial colonization within an altered tumor microenvironment. Cultures were negative—a finding in up to 50% of pituitary abscesses1—potentially due to prior antibiotic use, inadequate sampling, or culture limitations.6

In terms of surgical considerations and technical nuances, the single-stage endoscopic endonasal approach offered two key advantages: minimally invasive diagnostic value (direct visualization and definitive diagnosis of both pathologies) and simultaneous therapeutic intervention (complete adenoma resection and abscess drainage within the same surgical field, avoiding staged procedures or transcranial approaches). Several technical nuances warrant emphasis. First, distinguishing tumor necrosis, hemorrhage, and true suppuration has profound therapeutic implications. In our case, the yellowish viscous nature of the aspirated material—without features of simple necrosis or blood products—raised suspicion for infection. Second, encountering pus mandates contamination control: generous irrigation, avoiding widespread arachnoid dissection until the purulent collection is controlled, and considering complete versus staged resection. In our judgment, complete single-stage resection offered optimal balance, as residual devitalized tumor could serve as a nidus for recurrence. Abscess wall resection must balance recurrence risk against morbidity when adherent to critical structures. In our case, the wall was readily separable, permitting complete resection. Regarding skull base reconstruction, although active infection theoretically increases meningitis and graft infection risk, single-stage reconstruction with a vascularized nasoseptal flap remains feasible provided the purulent collection is completely evacuated, the field is thoroughly irrigated, and broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment is continued. The flap’s robust vascular supply may even offer advantages by delivering antibiotic agents and immunocompetent cells to the surgical site. We encountered no postoperative infectious complications, supporting the safety of this approach.

Postoperative management adhered to principles for intracranial infections. Despite negative cultures, a 6-week course of empirical intravenous antibiotic treatment was administered based on intraoperative and histopathological evidence of suppuration. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus screening was negative. The choice of meropenem plus vancomycin provided broad-spectrum coverage with adequate blood-brain barrier penetration. Importantly, this regimen followed treatment guidelines for brain abscess and intracranial infection, not routine postoperative prophylaxis, as our patient presented with an established infection.

The literature on perioperative antibiotic use following endoscopic endonasal transsphenoidal surgery primarily addresses prophylaxis in patients without evidence of infection.7,8 However, our case involves an established intracranial infection—intraoperative purulence and histopathologically confirmed suppuration—warranting therapeutic management according to brain abscess guidelines. Vates et al.9 reviewed 24 pituitary abscess cases and noted that most present with mass effect rather than systemic infection; diagnosis is often intraoperative, and appropriate surgical drainage plus antibiotic therapy yields good outcomes. Awad et al.3 specifically studied abscesses within adenomas (23 cases), reporting visual disturbances (83%) and headache (65%) as predominant symptoms, with fever in only 39%; mortality reached 26% despite prompt intervention, underscoring the need for aggressive therapy. Machado et al.10 emphasized that culture-negative results are common and do not exclude the diagnosis, and that empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy with CNS penetration is essential. Crucially, these studies concern therapeutic management of established pituitary abscesses. Our case—a primary abscess coexisting with an adenoma, not a postoperative complication—falls under this therapeutic paradigm. Therefore, our choice of meropenem plus vancomycin was guided by brain abscess treatment principles, providing broad-spectrum coverage with reliable blood-brain barrier penetration in confirmed infection.

Lessons

This case carries several important messages for clinicians managing sellar lesions. First, it reinforces that “atypical” features, including rapid symptom progression, disproportionate pain, or unexplained constitutional symptoms, should prompt consideration of unusual pathologies including infection. Second, it highlights the critical importance of intraoperative suspicion and appropriate specimen handling when purulent material is encountered. Third, it demonstrates that even in the absence of preoperative diagnosis, adherence to sound surgical and antimicrobial principles can achieve excellent outcomes. From a broader perspective, this case contributes to the growing recognition that pituitary pathology exists along a spectrum rather than in discrete diagnostic categories. The coexistence of neoplastic and infectious processes within the same lesion challenges traditional dichotomous thinking and suggests complex interactions between the tumor microenvironment and host immune responses.

In conclusion, the coexistence of a pituitary adenoma and a sellar abscess, although rare and diagnostically challenging, can be effectively managed via a single-stage endoscopic endonasal procedure. This case emphasizes the importance of maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion for such complex dual pathologies. Neuroendoscopy provides an ideal, minimally invasive means for simultaneous diagnosis and treatment, while a complete postoperative antibiotic course based on histopathological confirmation of suppuration is fundamental to ensuring successful recovery.
