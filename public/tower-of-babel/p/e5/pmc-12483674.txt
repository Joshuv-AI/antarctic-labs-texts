Effect of Eimeria spp. and Staphylococcus aureus challenge on cecal microbiome in broilers
Authors: Venkata Sesha Reddy Choppa, Hamid Reza Rafieian Naeini, Daniel Junpyo Lee, Hemanth Reddy Katha, Hanseo Ko, Deependra Paneru, Younghoon Kim, Woo Kyun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Staphylococcus aureus, associated with Bacterial Chondronecrosis and Osteomyelitis (BCO), is a leading cause of lameness in modern-day broilers, often seen with concomitant gut health aberrations such as coccidiosis. This study aims to uncover changes in cecal microbiome which hasn’t been characterized well in broilers challenged with S. aureus and Eimeria spp. Day-old male Cobb broilers were randomly allocated to eight treatments with five replicates each receiving various doses (2 × 105 or 2 × 108 CFU/mL) and timings (Day 0 and/or 18) of S. aureus. A mixed challenge of E. acervulina (62,500 Oocysts), E. maxima (12,500 Oocysts), and E. tenella (12,500 Oocysts) were administered orally on day 14 to all treatments except non challenged control (T1). Cecal contents were collected on days 20 and 26 from each bird per replicate for microbial analysis and 16s rRNA gene sequencing. On days 20 and 26, significant changes among the treatments were found in alpha diversity (Shannon Index and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity) and beta diversity (unweighted and weighted UniFrac). Higher and /or repeated exposure of S. aureus induced greater microbial shifts which can be seen from taxonomic composition at the phylum and family levels where Actinobacteria, Microbacteraceae, and elevated Leuconostocaceae. On day 26, high dose treatments revealed higher Bacteroidetes with decreased firmicutes besides shift in their ratios. Furthermore, functional pathway analysis unveils alterations in lipid metabolism, osteoclast differentiation, MAPK signaling, Fc gamma R mediated phagocytosis which are directly or indirectly linked with bone health. The above changes were spotlighted among the co-challenged high dose S.aureus treatments indicating the link for BCO pathogenesis. This study demonstrates that co-challenge with Eimeria and S.aureus alters cecal microbiome in a dose and time dependent way, leading to possible predisposition of BCO in broilers. This marks the need of exploring microbiome targeted mitigation strategies alleviating the prevailing skeletal problems in poultry.

Introduction

Gut microbiota play a crucial role in maintaining health and well-being of the host. They adhere to the intestinal epithelium, forming a protective barrier against pathogenic bacteria colonization (Yegani and Korver, 2008). Furthermore, these bacteria produce short-chain fatty acids, vitamins, organic acids, and bacteriocins, which enhance host nutrition and immunity and stimulate non-pathogenic immune responses (Jeurissen et al., 2002; Apajalahti, 2005; Yegani and Korver, 2008; Mancabelli et al., 2016; Kumar et al., 2018). Conversely, microbiome alterations can promote colonization of pathogenic bacteria or vice versa which manifest as dysbiosis (Kumar et al., 2018). This could result from infectious and non-infectious stressors among which renowned contributors like environmental changes, dietary imbalances, mycotoxins, poor management, viral or bacterial infections, and coccidiosis plays key role (Yegani and Korver, 2008). This can progress to colonization of sterile bone matrix by pathogenic microbes, resulting in osteomyelitis and other osseus diseases. Although complex networks of events are essential in changing the state of bone metabolism, this could start from gastrointestinal (GI) tract of chickens where the established microbiota maintains gut homeostasis under stress-free and favorable environment conditions (Chen et al., 2022). Among the different regions, cecum has the most diverse and stable microbial population, including anaerobes (Oakley et al., 2014). Earlier studies on broilers did not emphasize the significance of Staphylococcus aureus in altering microbiomes. This is deemed to be important because of the involvement of this pathogen in causing osteomyelitis of femur or femoral head necrosis since this disease incidence was discovered in 1972 (Nairn and Watson, 1972; Ramser et al., 2021; Asnayanti et al., 2024). S. aureus can infiltrate all types of osseus tissues and persist in a quiescent state as quasi-dormant small colony variants (SCVs) (Yang et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2021). Studies have shown that SCV phenotypes can rapidly become virulent, leading to osteomyelitis (Tuchscherr et al., 2011). A major lacuna in understanding S. aureus induced osteomyelitis lies in the lack of data on associated microbiome alterations. This is particularly concerning given the gut’s role as a major organ involved in host metabolism and immune regulation. The objective of the current study is to evaluate changes in cecal microbiome of broilers challenged with Eimeria spp. and Staphylococcus aureus at different time points. Understanding these changes uncover the potential link between gut microbiota and BCO.

Materials and methods

Birds, diet, and challenge

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at University of Georgia (A2021 12-012). A total of 600 birds were allocated to 8 treatments and 5 replicates per treatment in battery cages. Each pen had 15 birds, and all the birds were provided with ad libitum basal diet and water. Furthermore, challenge of Staphylococcus aureus was given orally on day 0 and day 18 to all birds except Treatment 1 and 2. 12,500 of E. maxima, 12,500 of E. tenella, and 62,500 of E. acervulina were inoculated orally to all birds except Control (Treatment 1). One mL of inoculum given to birds was checked prior to inoculation to ensure desired oocyst counts. Treatment 1 (T1) is non-challenged control and Treatment 2 (T2) is Eimeria challenge control. Treatment 3 (T3) was challenged with S. aureus at 2 × 105 CFU only on day 0 and Treatment 4 (T4) was challenged with the same dose but only day 18 respectively. Similarly, Treatment 5 (T5) received 2 × 108 CFU of S. aureus only day 0 and Treatment 6 (T6) received same dose on day 18, respectively. Treatment 7 (T7) and Treatment 8 (T8) were challenged with 2 × 105 CFU and 2 × 108 CFU of S. aureus respectively on both days (day 0 and 18). Birds were orally inoculated with 0.5 mL and 1 mL for day 0 and 18, respectively, which were adjusted to desired CFU. Staphylococcus aureus preparation

Baird Parker agar (BD DIFCO, NJ, USA) with 3.5 % tellurite in 30% egg yolk suspension (Sigma, St louis, MO, USA) was used for isolation and enumeration of Staphylococcus aureus. Agar was prepared according to manufacturer’s guidelines. Primary stock of S. aureus was isolated from the 8 lame birds’ femoral heads from Poultry Research Center, University of Georgia, Athens, GA. Clinically lame birds were selected based on gait scoring, and the femur from these birds were collected in sterile packs (Whirl Pak®, Nasco Sampling LLC, Chicago, IL, USA) and transferred to buffered peptone water (Himedia LLC, PA, USA) and homogenized. Following this, homogenized mixture was transferred to Baird parker agar plate. S. aureus was presumptively identified using colony characteristics like black colonies with halo appearance further confirmation was done using coagulase test (Ollis et al., 1995; Capita et al., 2001; Horwitz and Latimer, 2007). Following the above steps, single colony was transferred to BHI broth and incubated for 36 hours and then centrifuged to get a pellet which was diluted with 4 mL of 0.1 % peptone water. Optical density of this solution was measured at 600 nm using a UV-Vis spectrometer (Genesys 10S UV-Vis, Thermofisher Scientific, NJ, USA). With known OD standard curve was created followed by serial dilution and plating to check CFU in respective dilutions. Desired concentration needed for inoculation was prepared on the day of inoculation and given to respective treatments on day 0 and 18.

Sample preparation for microbial analysis

Two birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation and sprayed with 70 % ethanol on the surface to reduce surface contaminants followed by collection of required tissues. Ceca and jejunum tissues from 2 birds were collected and transferred to a sterile filter bag (Whirl Pak®, Nasco Sampling LLC, Chicago, IL, USA). Following this, sample bags were individually weighed and added with 10 mL of buffered peptone water. Tissues were homogenized (Neutec, Seward Ltd, West Sussex, UK) with subsequent serial dilutions which were plated on BPA plates and enumerated following 24-36 hours of incubation at 37°C.

Microbiome analysis and preparation

Cecal contents were collected and transferred to liquid nitrogen and stored in -80οC for further analysis. DNA was extracted using QIAamp Fast DNA stool mini kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) and following manufacturer’s protocol. Initially, 40-50 mg of cecal content was transferred to nuclease free microcentrifuge tubes (Avantor, Radnor, PA) which were added with 0.2 silicon beads to facilitate disruption of contents. This was followed by implementation of manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and concentration was measured using Nanodrop 200 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and shipped to LC Sciences (Houston, TX, USA) for 16s rRNA gene sequencing. V3-V4 regions were amplified with primers 5′-CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′ (forward) and 5′-GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′ (reverse). Qiime2 was used to analyze gene sequences including alpha diversity, beta diversity, phylum and family level composition (Version 2024.10) (Bolyen et al., 2019; Choi et al., 2023). Amplicon specific variants (ASVs) were processed using DADA2 algorithm implemented in QIIME2. Furthermore, taxonomic classification used in the current study was based on Naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA reference database (release 138) restricted to 16S rRNA gene V4 region amplified with primers 515F/806R.

Microbial community functions were predicted using PICRUSt2 based on the ASV table and representative sequences generated from QIIME2 (Bolyen et al., 2019; Choi et al., 2023). KEGG pathway-level functional profiles were then analyzed using STAMP (Statistical Analysis of Metagenomic Profiles) to compare differences between the two groups. Statistical significance was assessed using Welch’s t-test, and features with P < 0.05 were considered significant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed on JMP pro 17 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC, USA). ANOVA for all the parameters after checking normality, independent, and homogeneity of variance and data deviating from the above criteria were analyzed using Kruskal Wallis test. Post-hoc analyses like Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference for ANOVA, and Wilcoxon Pair wise comparisons for Kruskal Wallis test were performed to identify significantly different parameters. Data is represented as means and standard error in the results below. P-value is set to less than 0.05.

S.aureus counts which were converted Log CFU/g which were analyzed using ANOVA and Tukey’s HSD. Similarly, alpha diversity parameters like Shannon, Evenness, and Faith’s PD were analyzed using ANOVA and Tukey HSD (Post-hoc). For Beta diversity, significant differences in distance from respective treatments can be identified using upper and lower decision limits from Dunnet’s least square means. The values above or below these limits are considered significant (P < 0.05). Unweighted and weighted UniFrac distance were computed from rarefied feature tables. Furthermore, principal coordinates analysis was applied to each matrix. To express each sample’s distance to control, Euclidean distance from that sample’s PCoA scored to the control group centroid. Current study analyzed this measure with a linear model and reported as least square means and standard error. Dunnet contrasts were used to compared distances between treatments. Furthermore, relative abundances were analyzed using Kruskal-Wallis and post-hoc tests like Steel-Dwass were used. Functional analyses were statistically analyzed using Welch’s t-test, and features with P < 0.05 were considered significant.

Discussion

All the treatments except T1 were challenged with Eimeria spp. in the present study. Other treatments inoculated with S. aureus (T3-T8) revealed increased bacterial counts on day 20, particularly in groups challenged with higher bacterial challenge (T5 (Day 0 S.aureus challenge) and T8 (Day 0 and 18 S.aureus challenge). Although the present study does not include treatment groups challenged with S.aureus only, this dramatic increase of S.aureus counts in the Eimeria spp. co-challenge allowed cecal colonization of S.aureus like other pathogens such as Clostridium perfringens and Salmonella (Pham et al., 2021).

Studies on Eimeria spp. revealed disrupted cecal microbiota indicating decline in beneficial bacteria allowing pathogens to dominate or alter the microbiome (Martynova-Van Kley et al., 2012; Macdonald et al., 2017; Madlala et al., 2021; Campos et al., 2022; Choi and Kim, 2022; Yu et al., 2023). To elaborate, these changes weaken the colonization resistance of gut thereby allowing domination of Enterobacteriaceae and Bacteroides (Martynova-Van Kley et al., 2012; Jebessa et al., 2022). This shift is not limited to above taxa, where increase in Proteobacteria besides a decrease in firmicutes were observed which strongly suggest opportunistic pathogens replacing beneficial fermentative bacteria (Pham et al., 2021; Choi and Kim, 2022). In the current study which was challenged with S.aureus and Eimeria (T3-T8) revealed disruptions in bacterial compositions. This would trigger pathological changes, including inflammation in multiple organs such as bone due to microbial translocation. Furthermore, a possible release of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) into the bloodstream would alter cytokine signaling and Wnt pathways (Ahlawat et al., 2021; Choppa and Kim, 2023; Choppa et al., 2023). Compliantly, decrease in Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio for some treatments (T2, T3, T6, T7) suggests possible lipopolysaccharides release into circulation (Aruwa et al., 2021; El-Ghany, 2021).

Our study has shown a significantly higher phylogenetic diversity with low challenge treatment groups (T1, T2, T3, and T4) which is measured by sum of branch lengths of phylogenetic tree connecting all species in the assemblage (Faith, 2018). Interestingly, a study on blood microbiome revealed BCO affected broilers’ microbial community structure with abundant genera like Staphylococcus, Granulicatella, and Microbacterium which was found to be partially relevant to the current results (Mandal et al., 2016). The findings suggest a potential temporal change in cecal microbiome adapting to the challenged environment which is influenced by early microbial exposure in developing latter microbial communities (Ramírez et al., 2020; De Jong et al., 2022; Kayal et al., 2022). The above statement can be further reinforced by the findings in beta diversity parameters which reveal significant differences with high doses of S. aureus challenge birds and low dose of this pathogen given on day 0 and 18 (T5-T8).

Although Staphylococcus spp. is of least concern in terms of dysbiosis potential, current study revealed altered Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio which was discussed earlier. This in relation to the above challenge would raise the concerns on the aberrations to microbiome associated with bone physiology (Mandal et al., 2016, 2020). These concerns would heighten, when Eimeria spp. challenge is prevailing in the host environment which alter the microbial diversity besides allowing the pathogenic bacteria to thrive by decreasing some beneficial bacterial families Lachnospiraceae and Faecalibacterium (Chen et al., 2020; Campos et al., 2022). Interestingly, present study shows decrease in abundance of Actinobacteria for treatments challenged with Eimeria and S. aureus at higher doses like 2 × 108 CFU on day 0 and/or day 18 along with low dose (2 × 105 CFU) on day 0 and 18 (T5, T6, T7, and T8) compared to non-challenged, only Eimeria challenged, Eimeria and S. aureus challenge on day 0 and day 18 groups (T1, T2, T3, and T4). This decrease in Actinobacteria has been reported to be associated with aberrations in bone homeostasis which are discussed below.(Cholewińska et al., 2021; Agabalaev and Pak, 2024; Wu et al., 2024) For instance, a study showed altering feed composition (fermented rapeseed meal addition) and housing conditions improved shift of cutaneous microbiota (increasing Firmicutes and Actinobacteria) (Cholewińska et al., 2021). Similarly, shift in fermentative microbes in litter is important in enhancing production of SCFAs (especially butyrate) which are associated with improved mineral absorption besides exerting anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties to promote bone formation and decrease bone resorption (Huang et al., 2023; Agabalaev and Pak, 2024). Although above findings reveal importance of Actinobacteria, it also hints importance of optimized feed and litter to maintain bone homeostasis even under challenged conditions (Eimeria and S. aureus).

Moreover, predicted functional pathways showed notable shifts in gut microbiome metabolism compared to non-challenged control (T1). Importantly, this analysis revealed shift in lipid metabolism through fatty acid elongation pathways, a pathway known to be upregulated during microbiome shift observed in the current findings (Zhao et al., 2018). Furthermore, enriched NOD-like receptor (NLR) pathway along with MAPK signaling represents maturation of IL-1beta and tumor necrosis factor alpha which accelerate bone resorption besides decreased osteoblast formation (Weng et al., 2024). In addition, day 20 family composition revealed an increase in Leuconostocaceae among T6, T7, and T8 compared to other treatments. Contrarily, Microbacteraceae percentage was lower for T5, T6, T7, and T8 treatments. Although these families are not well understood in chickens, leuconostoc are heterofermentative bacteria with symbiotic relationships to Lactococcus spp (Nieminen et al., 2014). Studies related to humans revealed, increase in Leuconostocaceae is positive sign since this enhances fermentation of carbohydrates which is associated with production of lactic acid and SCFA production (Forbes et al., 2016; Hati et al., 2019). In contrast, Microbacteraceae which is less understood have a positive correlation with complex feed degradation besides indirect association with SCFA production (Park et al., 1993). Furthermore, being a part of beneficial bacterial community, Microbacteriaceae resist pathogen colonization which is clearly lacking in the current study for the treatments challenged with Eimeria and Staphylococcus spp at higher or lower doses given on day 0 and or 18 (T5, T6, T7, and T8) (Abdel-Kafy et al., 2022). These differences in commensals (highly abundant Leuconostocaceae and less abundant Microbacteraceae) for the above treatments (T5-T8) could possibly represent the need for diverse microbiota. This opens the way for a need in understanding whether to promote or stabilize Leuconostocaceae and Microbacteraceae when mitigating BCO.

Conclusion

Dysbiosis induced by Eimeria spp. and S. aureus co-challenge disrupts microbial community structure in a time and dose dependent manner. Birds which were exposed to high S. aureus dose (2 × 108 CFU) on days 0 and 18 exhibited the most pronounced reduction in alpha diversity metrics besides significant beta diversity aberrations which were further narrowed down to marked increase in pathobionts (Leuconostocaceae) and decreased beneficial taxa (Actinobacteria). These shifts correlated with decreased Firmicutes to Bacteriodetes ratio representing changes of cecal microbiota potentially linked with LPS-driven inflammation. These findings suggest that S. aureus and Eimeria-induced dysbiosis would possibly facilitate bacterial chondronecrosis and osteomyelitis (BCO). Prospective studies should validate causal relationship of microbiome alterations in BCO pathogenesis besides exploring therapeutic strategies to restore microbial resilience.
