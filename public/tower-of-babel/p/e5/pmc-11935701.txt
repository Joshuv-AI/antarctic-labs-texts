Evaluating the Safety and Efficacy of Telemedicine Physician Assessments on a Mobile Stroke Unit: Protocol for a Prospective Open‐Label Blinded End‐Point Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Vignan Yogendrakumar, Anna H Balabanski, Hannah Johns, Leonid Churilov, Nicola K Parsons, James Beharry, Louise Weir, Nawaf Yassi, Henry Zhao, Alex Warwick, Skye Coote, Francesca Langenberg, Leigh Branagan, Wasseem Siddiqi, Andrew Bivard, Bruce C V Campbell, Geoffrey A Donnan, Stephen M Davis, for the MSU‐TELEMED Investigators
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMobile stroke units have been shown to deliver faster patient care and improve clinical outcomes. However, costs associated with staffing limit their use to densely populated cities. Using the Melbourne mobile stroke unit, we aim to evaluate the safety, timeliness, and resource efficiency of a telemedicine model, where the neurologist assesses a patient remotely, via telemedicine, compared with an onboard neurologist model. We hypothesize that, without compromising patient safety, the telemedicine model will provide timely care and superior resource efficiency.MethodsUsing a prospective, randomized, blinded end‐point controlled design, 270 participants consecutively assessed on the Melbourne mobile stroke unit over ≈12 months will be assigned into 2 arms: (1) telemedicine neurologist assessment (intervention) versus (2) onboard assessment (comparator). Enrollment is based on prospectively designated randomized days of neurologist review onboard versus telemedicine. The primary outcome will be the odds that a randomly selected participant in the telemedicine arm will have a better outcome than a randomly selected participant in the onboard arm, measured using a desirability‐of‐outcome ranking, an outcome measure that includes, in order of importance: (1) safety, (2) scene‐to‐treatment‐decision time metrics, and (3) resource usage. All participants within each arm will be compared with those in the other, resulting in a “win/tie/loss” distribution for telemedicine compared with the onboard model.ConclusionsThe study will establish whether use of a telemedicine neurologist delivers superior resource efficiency without compromising patient care. This would enable the broader use of mobile stroke units, particularly relevant to regions with limited access to neurologists, thus improving equity in access to time‐critical, lifesaving stroke care.RegistrationURL: clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT05991310.Keywords: mobile stroke unit, prehospital, stroke, telemedicine, trial designSubject Categories: Ischemic Stroke

Mobile stroke units (MSUs; “stroke ambulances”) are ambulances with built‐in computed tomography (CT) scanners and are traditionally staffed by a mobile stroke team of paramedics or emergency medical technicians, a stroke neurologist, nurse, and CT radiographer. MSUs provide time‐critical stroke care in the prehospital setting and, when compared with treatment within a conventional emergency department setting, have been shown to improve the clinical outcomes of patients presenting with an acute ischemic stroke, particularly by accelerating access to reperfusion therapies. 1 While MSUs improve clinical outcomes and provide faster access to time‐critical treatments, the up‐front costs associated with establishing and staffing MSUs typically limit their use to densely populated cities. 2 This leaves a critical gap in equitable stroke care for people living in smaller cities or outside of metropolitan areas. A systematic review of over 20 000 patients showed that MSUs displayed favorable cost–benefit ratios 3 and our recent economic analysis found that, when deployed in a metropolitan environment and compared with standard care, an MSU costs an additional AU$30 982 (approximately US$20 500) per disability‐adjusted life‐year avoided (95% CI, AU$21 142–$47 517 [approximately US$14 000–$31 500]). 4 , 5 Subsequent analysis has suggested that an MSU model with the use of a telemedicine neurologist could provide a more cost‐effective alternative to a conventional onboard model. 4

Telemedicine is now the standard of practice for many health services across the world and in the context of stroke, has been shown to improve timely access to reperfusion therapy. 6 The use of telemedicine within MSUs is increasing but is presently limited to a small number of services, partly due to a lack of high‐level evidence of benefit. Prior comparisons of onboard versus telemedicine models of MSU care have shown similar time‐to‐treatment metrics and thrombolytic decision agreement. 7 , 8 , 9 However, these studies were limited by small sample sizes and used study designs where the telemedicine assessment had the potential to be confounded by external factors. A head‐to‐head comparison of a telemedicine model to an onboard model within an MSU has yet to be performed. As such, clinical equipoise relating to the resource efficiency of the telemedicine model remains.

We propose a randomized controlled trial comparing the safety, timeliness, and resource efficiency of a telemedicine‐based staffing model to a conventional onboard model on a mobile stroke unit. Safety and efficacy will be measured using a desirability‐of‐outcome ranking (DOOR) model and a “win ratio” approach. 10 , 11 The use of this approach supports a primary outcome that incorporates multiple aspects of the condition and prioritizes patient‐focused research outcomes. We hypothesize that the telemedicine neurologist model will provide superior resource efficiency without compromising safety or timely delivery of care, when compared with an onboard model of care.

Methods

Study Registration

This protocol follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials Statement for Randomized Trials of Nonpharmacologic Treatments. 12 Registration with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05991310) was performed before study initiation. Any deviations incurred during the performance of the clinical trial will be described in the final report. The trial received ethics and governance approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Royal Melbourne Hospital (HREC 2023.110). The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article.

Study Design and Setting

This study is a prospective, randomized, open‐label blinded end‐point parallel‐arm, single‐center MSU trial comparing 2 staffing models: (1) a neurologist review of patients via telemedicine (intervention) versus (2) an onboard neurologist assessing patients in person (comparator). The Melbourne MSU has operated within Melbourne, Victoria, since 2017 and is Australia's first MSU. Based at the Royal Melbourne Hospital, a large comprehensive stroke center, the MSU operates within a 20‐km radius and services a population of 1.7 million people within metropolitan Melbourne, a city of over 5 million people. 13 Due to funding and staffing constraints, it currently operates 10 hours per day on weekdays.

Participants

All patients assessed by the Melbourne MSU will be screened for trial inclusion. The following criteria will be required for inclusion: (1) adult patients aged ≥18 years, (2) presenting within 24 hours of symptom onset or last known well, (3) who undergo assessment by the Melbourne MSU without cancellation before the MSU attending the patient. Participants will be excluded where (1) attendance of the Melbourne MSU is deemed unnecessary by either the local paramedic team or the MSU team on the basis of provided information before arrival on‐scene, (2) the patient presents significant medical or logistical challenges that greatly delay standard treatment, or (3) any other medical contraindication at the discretion of the investigator. This study assesses a new workflow, and all patients will receive therapy akin to the standard of care. As such, we received ethics approval for consent to be waived.

Randomization and Masking

The days the neurologist will be on board or on telemedicine will be randomized in a covariate‐adaptive manner. 14 Randomization will be undertaken by units of days and will be stratified by the level of nursing expertise, defined as Nurse practitioner versus clinical nurse consultant level of training, with an adaptive component to balance the number of patients reviewed by each nurse type with an onboard neurologist in person and telemedicine arms. Nurse practitioners are nurses with extensive experience in stroke who have undergone formal training (eg, a master's degree) with a specific focus in acute, subacute, and chronic stroke care. In contrast, clinical nurse consultants are nurses who may come from varying backgrounds (emergency, neurology, general practice) and are trained to manage stroke care specific to the MSU. The adaptive randomized schedules will be generated in a rolling fashion weekly. Given the nature of the intervention, masking is not practical. However, the primary outcome assessments will be evaluated centrally in a blinded fashion.

Procedures

In this study, we aim to compare 2 staffing models on the MSU. The standard team on the MSU includes 1 stroke nurse, 1 radiographer, and 2 paramedics. The stroke neurologist will either assess patients via telemedicine or on board (in‐person), as described below. The MSU is also accompanied by a local ambulance team, consisting of 2 paramedics.

Telemedicine Day (Intervention)

The neurologist performs an assessment remotely via telemedicine. A mobile app and tablet device with Internet connectivity are provided to the MSU crew, while the neurologist remains in hospital using a hospital‐based computer for communication and assessments. The mobile app is capable of establishing video calls, sharing medical notes and displaying MSU‐acquired brain imaging to support clinical decision making. At the time of the initial MSU call, the MSU stroke team will travel to the location of the emergency call. The telemedicine neurologist will also be alerted to the MSU call and will be on standby with the telemedicine system activated. Upon arrival on‐scene, the MSU stroke nurse and paramedic will liaise with local ambulance services to obtain relevant clinical details and perform an initial assessment. Following the initial assessment, the stroke nurse will activate the telemedicine videoconference call and review the patient with the telemedicine neurologist. The telemedicine neurologist and stroke nurse will perform a clinical examination. If the video call fails for any reason, the MSU team can revert to using a mobile phone to support clinical decision making. If required, imaging will be performed by the MSU team and evaluated remotely by the telemedicine neurologist.

Onboard Day (Control)

The onboard neurologist performs an in‐person clinical assessment. At the time of the initial MSU call, the MSU stroke team will travel to the location of the emergency 000 (911 in North America) call. Upon arrival on‐scene, the MSU stroke nurse, neurologist, and paramedic will liaise with local ambulance services to obtain clinical details and perform an initial assessment. The clinical examination will be performed by the neurologist and stroke nurse together. If required, imaging will be performed by the MSU team and will be assessed at the console available within the ambulance by the neurologist.

In either staffing model, if the clinical case is not in keeping with stroke or if no acute management can be offered, then the MSU team can stand down and the local ambulance team will take over care and determine the hospital destination for transport. If the neurologist cannot perform an adequate clinical assessment, the local ambulance team will transfer the patient to the closest stroke center, as per standard of care. If there is a decision to administer thrombolysis, the stroke neurologist and nurse will discuss treatment with the patient or designated medical treatment decision maker, where appropriate and able, to acquire assent. If the patient is unable to communicate or if the proxy decision maker is not available, the treating team will proceed with emergency treatment, as the standard of care. If thrombolysis is administered the MSU will transport the patient to the nearest stroke‐capable hospital, or bypass directly to an endovascular capable center if endovascular therapy is required. If a large‐vessel occlusion is discovered but the patient is not eligible for thrombolysis, then the MSU will contact the nearest endovascular‐capable center and facilitate transport, where upon arrival, the decision to undergo endovascular therapy will be made by the receiving hospital team. If an intracerebral hemorrhage is discovered on imaging, the MSU may facilitate transfer and assist with acute blood pressure lowering. This decision is made on a case‐by‐case basis at the discretion of the treating physician.

Outcomes

As a primary outcome we have elected to integrate multiple outcomes together using a DOOR and win ratio approach. In this model, several outcomes are selected and are ordered in preference/priority. A set of rules were developed that define for any 2 possible outcomes which outcome is preferred. Following pairwise comparisons of each patient in the treatment arm to each patient in the control arm, the resultant outcome is an estimate of the probability/odds that a random participant from the treatment group would have a better outcome than a random participant from the control group. This model allows for a win/loss/tie of outcomes, and not all components need to measure clinical benefit. Measures of efficiency, safety, and system‐level effects may be used in these models.

Primary Outcome

The odds that a randomly selected participant in the telemedicine neurologist arm will have a better outcome than a randomly selected participant in the onboard neurologist arm are measured using the DOOR approach, including, in order of importance: (1) safety, (2) scene‐to‐decision‐time metrics, and (3) resource efficiency. 10 To determine our primary outcome, for all the pairs of participants undergoing telemedicine and onboard MSU assessments, the following sequential decision‐making process will be performed (Figure, Table):

Safety assessment: If a participant in 1 treatment arm is achieving better safety than the comparator, this is defined as a “win” for that participant and a “loss” for the comparator. To ensure the safety of the intervention, the following safety endpoints will be examined: (a) neurologist unable to complete the assessment (either due to telemedicine failure or due to on‐scene limitations); (b) symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage within 48 hours of thrombolysis administration; (c) clinical deterioration during the assessment/before arrival in hospital. The safety variables collected are defined in Table. A participant will be classified as having a negative safety outcome if they experience at least 1 of the (a) to (c) end points. Having a negative safety outcome is classified as a loss compared with not having such an outcome for the purpose of the primary outcome comparison. All patient records will be reviewed by trial monitors who are blinded to treatment allocation, and any occurrence of our safety endpoints will be evaluated by an independent medical monitor who is not part of the treatment team. The medical monitor will determine whether the safety end point in question was associated with the care model used to assess and treat the patient, and whether the management of these safety end points before arrival in the hospital may have been impacted by the neurologist being on board versus via telemedicine. We selected a clinically meaningful time difference of 15 minutes on the basis of prior pilot evaluations of MSU telemedicine models elsewhere. 8 Bowry et al, observed a time difference in decision‐to‐treatment between an onboard versus a telemedicine neurology of 5 minutes. Other Outcomes

Secondary outcomes include individual time metrics and clinical outcomes for patients who receive thrombolysis on the MSU or who are transferred to a comprehensive stroke center for EVT following assessment by the MSU, including symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage and 90‐day modified Rankin Scale score (Table).

Outcome Assessment and Blinding

The study is open label, in that the design of this study prevents patients, caregivers, or health care staff from being blinded to the form of acute assessment and treatment the patient receives on the MSU. As such, our safety end points will first be evaluated by trial monitors who are blinded to treatment allocation, in accordance with a prospective, randomized, open‐label, blinded end‐point design. This information, in addition to the time metrics, will be given to our statistical team for analysis. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage will be assessed by central adjudication by an assessor who is blinded to treatment allocation.

Sample Size

We hypothesize that the telemedicine intervention will result in a distribution of win/tie/loss proportions of 0.5/0.2/0.3 that corresponds to the win odds (0.5 + 0.2/2)/(0.3 + 0.2/2) = 0.6/0.4 = 1.5 when ties are split equally between treatment groups. This estimation is conservative due to efficiency likely being substantially greater in the telemedicine arm. Recruiting 242 participants (121 per arm) would yield a power of 0.8 to observe such hypothesized treatment effect against the null hypothesis of no treatment effect (win odds = 1) assuming a 2‐sided αlevel of 0.05. 15 To account for 10% of potential losses due to nonevaluable data, the final sample size for the study is set at 270 participants. Based on previous data from the Melbourne MSU, we anticipate ≈10% of participants assessed will receive intravenous thrombolysis on the MSU, and 5% will require EVT. 13

Statistical Analysis

Our primary study end point will be the odds that a random participant treated through an MSU telemedicine assessment will have a more desirable DOOR scale outcome than a random participant treated by an onboard MSU neurologist (Figure). Such odds are referred to as the win ratio, 11 as it reflects the odds of a random participant treated via MSU telemedicine “winning” against a random participant treated via an onboard MSU model in a direct 1‐to‐1 comparison. A greater frequency of winners in the treatment group (win ratio statistically significantly >1) will demonstrate the superiority of a telemedicine model over the onboard model. If ties were to be observed, they would be split equally between the 2 groups to reflect the existing clinical equipoise. The analysis will be stratified by (1) baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score; and (2) whether a CT scan was performed, given that patients who do not undergo CT scanning may have been deemed unsuitable for intravenous thrombolysis on the MSU (either due to contraindications or as the diagnosis is favored to be a stroke mimic). Primary analysis will be conducted on an intention‐to‐treat basis using permutation tests as per US Food and Drug Administration guideline recommendations. 16 Secondary and exploratory outcomes will be investigated using appropriate regression models with the treatment group as an independent variable, adjusted by baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score and whether a CT scan was performed. The 90‐day analysis will be stratified or adjusted by age, baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score, reperfusion treatment, and/or premorbid mRS if the sample size of the participants for this secondary analysis is sufficiently large. Diagnostic accuracy will be assessed as a sensitivity analysis in a subset of patients brought to the Royal Melbourne Hospital following MSU assessment. Within each trial arm, the discharge diagnosis of these patients will be compared with the suspected diagnosis made on the MSU. An agreement percentage and κstatistic will be calculated and presented. In cases in which there is a disagreement between the MSU and admitting hospital (eg, patient diagnosed as a stroke mimic [eg, migraine, functional] on the MSU is assessed by the receiving hospital and is given a new diagnosis of stroke) the proportion of instances where the opportunity for acute treatment with intravenous thrombolysis was missed on the MSU will be calculated and compared between treatment arms. Treatment effects will be presented with respective 95% CIs. A detailed statistical analysis plan document will be finalized before the study data lock.

Patient and Stakeholder Involvement

This trial was developed through codesign by clinical stakeholders from all disciplines on the MSU. Additionally, a consumer advocate with lived experience and expertise in health care delivery was invited to review the study documents and provide feedback. After reviewing the study documents and discussing the study with the co–principal investigators, the consumer advocate supported the protocol in its current state and made no significant suggested changes.

Discussion

This trial will provide the first head‐to‐head data comparing a remote telehealth neurologist MSU model of care to the “traditional” onboard model of care. We hypothesize that a telemedicine model will provide substantially greater resource efficiency while continuing to provide safe, timely care. Should our hypotheses be substantiated, a paradigm shift toward a more resource‐efficient model of care would be likely, thereby facilitating broader deployment of MSUs, and expanding their use beyond more densely populated areas. Advantages include the potential for a single neurologist to staff multiple MSUs simultaneously via telemedicine or to staff an MSU from any location. With expanding numbers of MSUs around the world, including an increasing number in resource‐poor countries, this is a critical issue to be resolved by a well‐designed prospective trial.

This trial has several novel features. This study represents the first head‐to‐head trial of an onboard versus a telemedicine MSU model of care using a workflow that provides a “real‐world” model of telemedicine care. A substudy of the BEST‐MSU (Benefits of Stroke Treatment Using a Mobile Stroke Unit Compared to Standard Management) trial was designed to compare the agreement between the decision making of an onboard neurologist undertaking the primary evaluation with that of a telemedicine neurologist. 7 A similarly designed study from the Berlin MSU program also demonstrated decision‐making agreement between the onboard neurologist who performed the primary evaluation and the telemedicine neurologist who performed the second assessment. 9 However, in both studies, the telemedicine evaluation was not used as the primary assessment, and the telemedicine neurologist received clinical information from the patient or family member who had already provided a history to an onboard neurologist, with the telemedicine assessment typically undertaken after initial assessment and loading onto the MSU. These study designs introduced potential confounders into the real‐world evaluation of an MSU workflow with no neurologist on board, with the sole medical assessment being performed via telemedicine. In the present study, our workflow provides insights into the broader impact of telemedicine, including reduced staff numbers for patient extrication and additional time requirements of the remaining staff on board, including facilitating telemedicine and treatment delivery. In addition, we will report data on all patients assessed by the MSU rather than limiting our study population to patients receiving reperfusion therapy. Given that an MSU encounters a large proportion of patients without stroke, the inclusion of all patients will provide insights into the efficiency of the telemedicine model of care that are generalizable and reflect real‐world practice. The additional novel feature of this study will be the use of the DOOR approach to formulating the outcome. The ability to incorporate multiple outcome measures in a hierarchy of importance within the primary outcome allows an effective comparison of the 2 different models of care, incorporating patient‐centered priorities into the primary outcome. We also use adaptive methods for randomization, to ensure a balance between the number of patients assessed and the level of onboard nursing staff experience.

Limitations of this study include this being a single‐center study. Additionally, we will restrict our long‐term (90‐day) outcome assessment to participants receiving reperfusion therapies for ischemic stroke, namely, those who undergo intravenous thrombolysis onboard the MSU or who are transferred for EVT. Based on an anticipated 15% of participants receiving thrombolysis or being transferred for EVT, our study is underpowered to assess the impact on the clinical outcomes of reperfusion therapy. However, previous studies have demonstrated the interrater agreement between onboard and telemedicine neurologists, indicating that patient selection for reperfusion therapy is anticipated to be appropriate. Furthermore, timely provision of care will be evaluated as part of our primary outcome. While DOOR designs improve over the pitfalls of composite outcomes by explicitly reflecting the order of relative importance of the multiple outcome components, it is still “compensatory” by nature, and better performance on 1 component can mask worse performance on the other. For example, difficulty with an assessment may confound the time to thrombolysis. It is therefore important to emphasize that the resulting overall effect estimates need to be considered together with respective component effects to ensure that no such masking occurs.

The ultimate objective of this study is to investigate whether a telemedicine model of care on board the MSU offers greater resource efficiency for prehospital hyperacute stroke care delivery, when compared with a standard onboard neurologist model without compromising safety and timeliness of decision making. A telemedicine model of prehospital stroke care has the potential to facilitate greater deployment of MSUs across regional and remote settings worldwide, thereby providing greater access to timely, lifesaving stroke care.
