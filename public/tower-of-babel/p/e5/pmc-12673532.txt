Characterizing the motion management system accuracy on a 1.5T MR-Linac
Authors: William Donahue, Shu Xing, Lauren Smith, Paola Godoy Scripes, Eric Aliotta, Jim Mechalakos, Lakshmi Santanam, Neelam Tyagi, Seng Boh Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Motion management techniques have enabled drastic reductions in treatment volume allowing for dose escalation. The superior contrast of MRI combined with the ability to perform cine imaging during treatment enable surrogate-less tracking for gating. The purpose of this work is to commission motion management system with gating capabilities on the 1.5T MR-Linac and to characterize system performance.Methods:Linear accelerator stability during gating was measured for a wide range of duty cycles (16 % – 100 %). Motion tracking accuracy and gating latency were characterized for varying target sizes and breathing rates, and with and without adaptive motion prediction using a commercially available motion phantom. Clinical treatment plans were created for lung, prostate, pancreas, and liver sites, using varied gating strategies and tumor motion amplitudes. Film measurements were performed for each plan and compared to either the treatment planning system (TPS) or a reference film delivery. Finally, end-to-end testing was performed with the motion phantom and film to quantify the delivery accuracy of the system for exception and free-breathing gating strategies.Results:Beam characteristics were stable for all duty cycles analyzed, with all measurements within 0.6 % of the ungated baseline. Tracking accuracy showed a strong dependence on breathing rate. The system had difficulties tracking a 1 cm target due to through plane motion caused by the helical path of the phantom. Film comparisons for the clinical plans demonstrated average dose differences within 1.8 % and gamma passing rates (3 %/2 mm) >75 % within the 50 % isodose line. The cases with the worst gamma passing rates corresponded to exhale gating strategy where a systematic shift in dose was observed due to tumor motion interplay with the gating envelope. End-to-end testing showed excellent dose agreement (<3 % average dose difference) and a localization accuracy of <1 mm for targets with non-periodic motion and <2 mm for moving targets.Conclusions:The motion management system on the Elekta Unity provides advanced capabilities for treating moving targets. The results of this work support the ability of the system to deliver accurate radiotherapy plans with reasonable uncertainties. However, as demonstrated by exhale gating results, better understanding of the clinical impacts of these new functionalities may be warranted.

Introduction

Advancements in radiation treatment technologies have enabled improved outcomes through reduced margins, hypofractionation, and dose escalation [1–5]. Gating and motion management has contributed to this by reducing the volume of irradiation through the selective targeting of tumor motion [6,7]. This has historically relied on surface-based surrogates [8,9], fiducial tracking with on-board imaging [10,11], or implantable RF beacons [12] to determine when the beam should be on. These methods are limited in their utility due to the potential for inaccuracy or instability in the correlation between the surrogate and tumor position or the need for invasive and time-consuming implantation methods.

MR-LINACs can utilize their superior soft-tissue contrast to visualize the tumor directly, without surrogates or fiducials. When used with the cine imaging capabilities of the MRI, this allows for tracking the target position in real-time while the radiation beam is on. The original ViewRay MR-IGRT system (ViewRay Inc., Oakwood Village, OH, USA) used this mode of target tracking to compensate for the long treatment times of the Co-60 radiation source [13], which showed a system latency of approximately 400 ms. The ViewRay MRIdian (ViewRay Inc., Oakwood Village, OH, USA), a LINAC-based upgrade to the original ViewRay, had an improved beam off latency (319 ms) but even doubling the frame rate from 4 Hz to 8 Hz did not improve the overall system latency [14]. Elekta recently released their Comprehensive Motion Management (CMM) upgrade for the Unity 1.5T MR-Linac. This new upgrade included the ability to gate for respiratory motion using a predictive algorithm to reduce latency or unexpected motion of static targets without a predictive algorithm [15]. This new capability could enable more precise radiotherapy for commonly treated pathologies on the Unity, including lung, liver, and prostate. Previous work commissioning CMM investigated the performance for respiratory motion tracking primarily [15]. They demonstrated that the system was able to deliver accurate radiotherapy for regular and irregular breathing traces. Even with these results, it was not known if other treatment scenarios, such as small or lateral targets, that could lead to tracking and delivery inaccuracies.

The goal of this work is to commission the comprehensive motion management system on the Elekta Unity MR-Linac and to characterize system performance. We evaluated the treatment beam stability for a wide range of duty cycles. The MR tracking accuracy and gating latency were measured using a commercial 3D motion phantom. We delivered composite IMRT doses to film for simulated-patient plans with and without gating for each commissioned strategy and evaluated the agreement with the expected dose. Finally, we performed end-to-end (E2E) tests for gating strategies to evaluate the delivery accuracy when motion was included.

Methods

Unity motion management implementation

CMM uses alternating coronal and sagittal 2D cine images, captured at approximately 5 Hz, to localize and track a target structure. Keiper et al. [16] described the fundamental principles in their feasibility study. First, 2D reference images in the sagittal and coronal planes are extracted from the volumetric reference MRI-of-the day used for planning. These two planes pass through the centroid of the target structure and are warped to account for the system distortion, which is uncorrected in cine images. Second, a series of alternating sagittal and coronal cine images are collected for a fixed period of time. These “template” images are then registered to the reference image-of-the day to determine the rigid mapping between 2D tracking images and the 3D planning volume. This registration can be adjusted by the user and must be approved prior to treatment. Finally, during treatment, sagittal and coronal cine images are collected and rigidly registered to the template cine images in real time. The resulting 2D-2D registrations are combined with the template 2D-3D registration to determine the 3D vector describing the motion of the target with respect to the planning geometry. To compensate for the fact that the cine images only update two coordinates at a time, the missing coordinate is re-used from the previous frame. This results in an approximate 400 ms delay in the verification of the x and y directions, assuming 200 ms between images.

Gating is performed via one of four strategies that determines the 3D pre-beam imaging used for planning and tracking template generation, and whether a predictive algorithm is used to gate the beam. A summary of the imaging protocols for the MR of the day, whether the predictive algorithm is used, and cine image binning used in template generation is given in Table 1. Exception gating is used for static targets, such as prostate, and does not include the use of the predictive algorithm. Two free-breathing strategies, average position and exhale, use a predictive algorithm to inform the gating decision and compensate for system latency. The difference in the free-breathing strategies comes from the 3D imaging and template generation. Finally, a breath-hold strategy is available, which does not utilize the predictive algorithm. To ensure the patient is in a breath hold, the system incurs an additional beam-on delay.

CMM provides two ways for users to specify the acceptable range of motion. First, displacement gating, which triggers the beam when a target moves a fixed distance from its planned position, on each axis. This can be described as a bounding box on the centroid of the target structure. Second, envelope gating, which uses a contour to encompass an acceptable envelope of motion for the target structure contour. The user can adjust the acceptable overlap percentage of the target and envelope contours, with the default setting to disable treatment with <100 % overlap.

Finally, CMM provides a baseline shift planning mechanism to adjust MLC apertures based on changes in the target centroid over time. Since Unity couch does not move once the patient is positioned, this feature is used to compensate for drifts in the target position cause by phenomenon, such as bladder filling for prostate. To perform the plan adjustments, the tracking system sends the average target position over the past 60 frames (~12 s) to the Treatment Planning System (Monaco, Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden) and the centroid of the remaining fields in the plan is shifted using an aperture morphing algorithm. The entire process can be completed in 60 s to allow for rapid adjustments to small drifts in the target position. Because this process is based purely on a virtual couch shift, only the target and motion management structures are shifted in the MRI-of-the-day. Therefore, the algorithm does not correctly account for OAR doses or the effects of tissue deformation that occurred between the planning scan and time of the shift [17].

Beam stability with beam holds

APPM TG-142 recommends routine verification of machine performance with respect to beam holds used in respiratory gating using a single measurement [18]. These tests are typically performed in-situ, with the beam-holds being triggered by the gating system. The service functions on the Unity provides a convenient way to decouple the beam-hold generation and evaluate the beam stability without a moving phantom or surrogate to track.

Using service mode on the Unity, artificial beam holds were generated with user-specified on and off durations. We measured LINAC output and profile stability for 38 combinations of beam on and beam off times (Table 2). Linac output was measured with an Exradin A12MR Ion Chamber (Standard Imaging Inc., Middleton, Wisconsin, USA) at a depth of 5 cm in solid water for a 10 cm square field. An IC Profiler-MR (Sun Nuclear Inc., Melbourne, Florida, USA) was used to measure flatness and symmetry for a 20 cm square field. For both measurements, each configuration was repeated three times and the relative difference between deliveries with and without beam-holds were calculated. To estimate the linear accelerator and measurement setup reproducibility without beam-holds, 15 measurements for both the output and flatness and symmetry were measured consecutively and the standard deviation was taken.

Additionally, to assess the stability of the beam during normal treatment conditions, 8 IMRT fields were delivered to film using service mode. Film dosimetry was performed using a phantom (Quasar MRI4D Motion Phantom, Modus Medical Devices) with a film insert with no respiratory motion applied. Gafchromic EBT4 (Ashland Inc., Wilmington, Delaware, USA) film was used for this measurement [19]. Two different beam-holds were used during the film deliveries, a 5000 ms on/1000 ms off combination and a 100 ms beam off time with beam on times calculated to ensure a minimum of three beam holds were performed for each segment of the field with a range of 270 ms to 890 ms. This ensured that even segments with few MUs were gated more than once. Each film with beam-holds was compared to a film delivered without beam-holds using dose difference and gamma analysis with a 3 %/2 mm criteria and a 50 % threshold [20].

Tracking accuracy

Position and tracking accuracy were measured using a commercial motion phantom (Quasar MRI4D Motion Phantom, IBA Quasar, London, Ontario, Canada). The phantom includes multiple spherical target sizes that can be driven in a helical or 1D motion pattern. The motion phantom and inserts are shown in Fig. 1. The range of motion is 2 mm, 5 mm, and 38 mm for the Left-Right, Anterior-Posterior, and Superior-Inferior directions, respectively. The phantom software (IBA Quasar Programmable Respiratory Motion, v4.2.12, IBA Quasar, London, Ontario, Canada) includes a custom module to compare the phantom position traces to log files from the Unity that contain the coordinates determined using the cine images. Using this module, we compared the tracking accuracy over four different breathing rates: 8, 12, 16, and 20 breaths per minute (bpm) using a sinusoidal trace. We tracked 1 cm, 2 cm, and 3 cm spherical targets at the center of the MRI bore and the 3 cm sphere displaced 10 cm and 13 cm laterally from the center (Fig. 1d). We quantified the average, standard deviation, maximum, and 95th percentile errors in all three directions.

To independently verify tracking accuracy, MV images were simultaneously taken of the phantom during tracking using the Unity’s portal imager. MR locations were recorded from the console during imaging and then the relative displacement was calculated from the phantom reference position. The relative displacement on MV image was determined by manually extracting the position of the sphere center using ImageJ [21]. The relative displacements of the phantom were compared between the two measurement techniques and evaluated for agreement.

Gating latency

Gating latency was measured using the same commercial motion phantom. The phantom can directly measure the digital signal that triggers the electron gun in the Unity. Using the known position of the phantom, the user can specify the size of the gating window to be used. Latency was determined by comparing the electron gun signal to the time the target entered or left the prescribed gating window. Beam on and beam off latency were captured individually and analyzed separately.

When measuring the latency, we used a 3 cm spherical target and 1D motion. We delivered the beam until at least 100 samples for beam on or off time were measured. For exception gating (which does not use the prediction algorithm) we used an approximate square wave breathing pattern with 7 mm amplitude and 5 s between transitions. This waveform tests the full latency of the system because the transition between positions was faster than the imaging frequency (5 Hz). For free-breathing gating, sinusoidal patterns were used with 30 mm of peak-to-peak motion with breathing rates of 8 bpm, 12 bpm, and 16 bpm. We measured additional peak-to-peak motions of 20 mm and 12 mm at 12pbm. Once again, the target was positioned in the center of the MRI bore and 10 cm laterally. All measurements utilized the envelope gating trigger, as this matched our planned clinical practice for all sites. Template images were collected with the phantom in a static position. While this is not what would happen in a clinical case, this enables a more robust and reproducible definition of the starting phantom position. This is critical to the latency and position accuracy measurements as any manual shifts applied to the templates become a systematic offset. When performing the template image registration, shifts were set to zero to reduce the uncertainty introduced by the subjective alignment (and because no motion occurred between 3D imaging and template acquisition).

Dosimetric studies

Using the motion phantom with a film insert, dosimetric studies were performed for simulated clinical plans. The insert contained a 3 cm target located at the center of the 16.5 cm long by 6 cm wide insert (Fig. 1b). Simulation was performed on a Brilliance Big Bore RT scanner (Koninklijke Philips, Netherlands) in static and free-breathing (12 bpm) sinusoidal modes using our clinical 4D-CT protocol. Treatment planning was done for 8 different cases in the Monaco Treatment Planning System (v6.2, Elekta, Stockholm, Sweden). For the exception gating, two prostate cases were planned on a static phantom, with a film centrally located for measurement, to a total dose of 40 Gy in 5 fractions. Both free-breathing and exhale gating strategies were evaluated using two distinct motion scenarios and phantom insert positions: a 1 cm range of motion with the insert positioned centrally within the phantom, and a larger 3 cm range of motion with the insert placed laterally. Furthermore, treatment plans for pancreas and liver cancers were simulated on the same phantom to a total dose of 50 Gy in 5 fractions, and a lung cancer plan was created for a total dose of 66 Gy in 33 fractions. For each of these disease sites (pancreas, liver, and lung), both free-breathing average position gating and exhale gating strategies were used. Sinusoidal traces were used for the average position free-breathing and exhale gating deliveries. This created a rigorous test of exhale gating due to the short time spent in the exhale position.

During the delivery of the simulated plans, the full adaptive workflow was applied. Following the collection of the pre-beam MRI image for planning, the targets in the reference image and daily image were rigidly registered. Using the adapt-to-shape workflow, the gating envelope was generated by expanding the target structure by 3 mm in the motion direction (superior and inferior) and by 5 mm in the other directions to avoid triggering caused by tracking in accuracies. For exception gating, no motion was used during the 3D image and template acquisition. During the beam delivery, a symmetric square wave pattern was used with 6 mm shifts and 5 s beam-on windows, where movement alternated positively and negatively. For both the free-breathing average and exhale strategies, the planned motion amplitude was applied for 3D image and template acquisition. Additionally, the planned motion amplitude was applied during the treatment delivery stage.

Gafchromic EBT-4 films were used for composite doses less than 9 Gy [19], while Gafchromic EBT-XD film was used for higher doses [22,23]. Calibration films were collected for each delivery session at the machine to minimize uncertainties from film batch and development time effects. The films were converted to dose maps using the FilmQA Pro software (Ashland Inc., Wilmington, Delaware, USA). Analysis was performed using an in-house dose analysis tool looking at average dose difference and gamma analysis (3 %/2 mm) within the 50 % isodose line. The in-house software was previously validated using our Varian Linear Accelerators (Varian Medical Systems, Siemens Healthineers, Palo Alto, CA, USA) by importing measurements from MapCheck (Sun Nuclear Corporation, Melborne, Florida, USA), MapCheck SRS (Sun Nuclear Corporation, Melborne, Florida, USA), Matrixx Resolution (IBA Dosimetry, Schwarzenbruck, Germany), and evaluating calculated gamma value in the commercial and in-house software when comparing the measurements to the calculated TPS results. Using a global gamma analysis, all results of these comparisons were within 1 % independent of criteria.

Exception gating films were compared to the planned dose distributions from the TPS. Gated films for the free-breathing and exhale strategies were compared to films measured under expected reference conditions. This made registration of the doses straight-forward using pin pricks created by the film insert. For the free-breathing (average position) strategy, the treatment volume was based on an ITV which incorporated the entire range of motion of the target. Therefore, the target should never leave the gating envelope and match a film delivered for the same range of motion with no gating. To create the reference film for the free-breathing strategy, the plan was delivered with the gating system disabled. For the exhale strategy, the reference film was irradiated with the target at the full-exhale position.

Because we were using dose to film as the reference dose in most of our comparisons, running the optimizer for each online session could create differences in delivered dose, causing additional uncertainty. Therefore, for these cases, we forced the plan to use the original segments for delivery. This ensured that the MLCs, MUs and other treatment parameters remained constant between the deliveries.

End-To-End testing

End-to-end (E2E) testing is a critical component to evaluating not only the system performance but also the robustness of the workflow to errors. For this work, E2E testing was completed on the exception and free-breathing average gating strategies, the two strategies that are currently clinically in use at our institution. A new plan was generated on the phantom for prostate and pancreas treatments by treatment planning staff. Treatment delivery was performed by radiation therapists, with physicists performing their roles as well as standing in for physicians. For the prostate case, a ramp function with 5 mm inferior motion over 10 min was used to mimic drift due to bladder filling, with the motion starting as soon as the beam was turned on. Once the beam was gated due to the motion, a baseline shift plan was performed, and all motion was stopped for the rest of the delivery. For the pancreas case, the phantom used a continuous 1 cm peak-to-peak sinusoidal motion trace at 12 bpm with a 3 mm gating envelope. Film was used for the measurements as described in the previous section.

Our institutional criteria for E2E tests requirements are average dose difference less than 5 % and localization error within 1 mm. This was evaluated by first performing an alignment based on minimizing the dose difference metric to determine the isocenter location on the film. Then the position of the fiducial markers on the film relative to the isocenter were compared to those in the reference CT after the alignment with the MR image of the day. The 1 mm criteria was applied to the prostate case. The pancreas case was planned on the free-breathing average CT scan generated with our clinical pancreas protocol. Evaluating the CT image during planning revealed that motion artifacts due to the phantom motion caused the separation of the fiducial to be 3 mm greater in the scan than their physical position. To account for this additional uncertainty, we increased the localization criteria to 2.5 mm in the direction of motion for the pancreas case, i.e. 1 mm for delivery uncertainty to the original target and 1.5 cm for the deformation in the CT.

Results

Beam stability with beam holds

The results of the output, flatness, and symmetry stability testing are shown in Table 3, Table 4, and Table 5, respectively. The measured uncertainty in the output measurements was 0.1 % while the measured flatness and symmetry error was 0.12 %. The output constancy was well within the 2 % tolerance in TG-142, with most values being within 0.3 % (Table 3). For duty cycles <25 % the dose difference increased to a maximum of 0.59 %, with two notable exceptions. First, the 10 ms on and 10 ms off case resulted in a higher error compared to other points with a 50 % duty cycle. This is attributed to the linac underperforming in a fast-switching scenario. This type of beam hold frequency is not expected to occur during clinical use cases and can be ignored. Second, the 10 ms beam on with 100 ms beam off was always interrupted by an interlock and therefore deemed undeliverable. Comparing these results to the measurement uncertainty shows that anything about a 25 % duty cycle are the same as not being gated. The flatness and symmetry remained within 0.2 % for all tested beam-hold configurations (Tables 4 & 5). All flatness and symmetry is within 2 sigma, demonstrating the flatness and symmetry is unaffected by beam-holds. This is attributed to the fixed beamline configuration of the MR-Linac. Together, these results demonstrate that the LINAC was exceptionally stable when beam-holds were introduced.

Comparing the deliveries of IMRT fields with and without beam-holds, there was a maximum average dose difference of 1 cGy ± 1 cGy (<0.2 %) and a gamma pass-rate of >99 % for all fields analyzed. This demonstrates that the machine is stable during beam holds for fields with moving MLC.

Tracking accuracy

When analyzing tracking accuracy, we used the phantom software to analyse >1500 position samples for each combination of breathing rate, target location, and target size. For brevity, the data shown below represents the 3D vector magnitudes of the errors for each measurement condition. The results broken down by cartesian coordinate are provided in the Supplemental Files.

Fig. 2 shows the relationship of tracking error with target size and breathing rate. The data demonstrates a correlation between breathing rates and unsigned error. This dependence is caused by the tracking algorithm and the assumption that the coordinate perpendicular to each image can be reused from the last image to define the location in 3D. For normal breathing rates (8, 12, and 16 bpm) the mean error was less than 0.9 mm for 3 cm and 2 cm targets, while the 95th Percentile (P95 %) remained below 2.2 mm. This suggests that breathing rates should be considered during treatment when deciding the gating margins to use, if tumor coverage is the primary concern. The 1 cm target has average higher maximum and P95 % values of 4.75 mm ± 0.3 mm and 3 mm ± 0.4 mm respectively. This was caused by through-plane motion with respect to the coronal plane causing the tracking algorithm to fail. The tracking quality interlocks in the system would have prevented beam on during most of these deviations. However, this data shows that consideration should be given to the size and motion trajectory of small targets.

Fig. 3 plots the results of tracking a 3 cm diameter sphere at 0 cm, 10 cm, and 13 cm laterally from isocenter. Overall, this data shows the same trend with respect to breathing rates as seen in the target size measurements. However, this data shows that the tracking accuracy appears independent of position in the bore demonstrating the system should track central and lateral tumors equally as well. The mean and P95 % remained below 0.9 mm and 2.2 mm respectively.

The independent MR and MV position analysis is shown in Fig. 4. This data shows that the position accuracy seems reasonable for the S-I and A-P directions, with strong correlations between the position detected using the MR and MV imaging. However, the Left-Right motion has less correlation with the recorded MV positions. This is attributed to the limited range of motion of the phantom in this direction.

Gating latency

The gating latency measured for the exception gating strategy, i.e., with no predictive algorithm, had a P95 % value of 350 ms and a maximum error of 450 ms. Beam on and beam off latency were similar in magnitude. This is reasonable and consistent with the imaging time of 200 ms between frames and the additional processing time required for each frame.

For the free-breathing algorithms, the measured latency for our Unity is displayed in Table 6. The data shows that the latency is independent of all factors considered, except for the 12 mm range of motion. This smaller motion resulted in the target only leaving the gating envelope by 1 mm. This demonstrates a potential limitation in the tracking accuracy for small ranges of motion relative to the position.

Dosimetric studies

Exception gating was tested by comparing the TPS predicted dose to doses delivered under motion with gating. Fig. 5 shows the dose difference isodose plots for one of the two delivered cases. The mean dose difference within the 50 % isodose line was less than 9 cGy, with an average dose within the 50 % isodose being 723 cGy. This means that we were within 1.1 % dose difference for the deliveries. The Gamma analysis for both cases was >95 % for 3 %/2 mm criteria. It should be noted that hotspots were observed outside of the 50 % isodose line due to the beam latency, which continued irradiation after the target left the gating window.

For free-breathing gating, the measured dose with gating was compared to a film where gating was disabled while the phantom was in motion. Fig. 6 shows the dose differences between this non-gated film and the gated film with the expected motion for a liver plan (3 cm motion, 10 cm lateral) and a pancreas plan with 1 cm range of motion at isocenter for the planned ranges of motion. For the liver case, we see a hotspot that forms relative to the ungated film. This suggests that the gating system is improving the penumbra caused by the blurring of the dose, which is attributed to the predictive algorithm stopping the beam early due to a predicted excursion of the gating envelope. For the smaller amplitude pancreas case, we see similar behavior, but the effect is subdued to the reduced range of motion. Across all three delivered cases using this gating mode, the average dose difference was < 1.6 % with gamma passing rates above 89 %.

Exhale gated films were compared to a film with a static delivery instead of the TPS, this was done to simplify the alignment of the dose distributions using the fiducials built into the phantom. Fig. 7 shows the dose difference maps of lung and pancreas cases with exhale gating. The average dose difference was less than 1 % for all cases but there are systematic hotspots of >10 % at the superior edge of the 50 % isodose line and corresponding cold spots at the inferior edge. This resulted in poor gamma pass rates for the lung case, where the pass rate was 74 % (3 %/2 mm) although the other cases were >90 %.

These large dose differences are due to the activation of the beam before the tumor is in the planned treatment location. The reason for this early activation the gating envelope’s large size relative to the target; the beam turns on once the target is encompassed by the envelope, which is before it is in the planned position. The dose difference effect is dependent on the margin size. Similar effects have been observed by others when studying gating [24–30].

End-To-End testing

For the exception gating end-to-end test, the average dose difference was < 0.7 %. Localization accuracy was 0.7 mm. This passed our standard institutional criteria of 5 % dose difference and 1 mm accuracy. The cause of the lower gamma pass rate when comparing these results to the previous prostate deliveries was due to the linear motion used and baseline shift plan applied during the test.

The average dose difference for the free-breathing respiratory end-to-end was 3.1 % and the localization accuracy was < 1.4 mm, which is within our 2.5 mm criteria for a phantom under motion. This high level of inaccuracy attributed to the distortion from phantom motion in the CT scan, as discussed previously. While the average dose difference was less than the pass criteria of 5 %, an ion chamber was used to validate the dose to the target resulting in a dose difference of 0.6 %. This implies that the hot dose was due to an issue with the film.

Discussion and conclusions

We evaluated the target tracking and dosimetric performance of the Comprehensive Motion Management System on the Eletka Unity MR-Linac. We used a 3D motion phantom with sinusoidal or square wave traces to represent the target motion. Film dosimetry was performed to determine the influence of gating on delivered doses. Finally, we performed end-to-end tests for the free-breathing and exception gating modes to determine the dosimetric localization accuracy of the system. The major findings of this work were that the system was dosimetrically stable and able to track targets down to 2 cm with an accuracy of 2.2 mm in the 95th percentile. Additionally, exhale gating showed the most dosimetric differences because of its asymmetric motion through the gating envelope.

The implications of this study are that the CMM system meets the AAPM TG-76 definition of a real-time gating system [7]. We showed that for large motions in the sagittal and coronal planes, the gating system may experience tracking errors due to through-plane motion for targets smaller than 2 cm. The predictive algorithm compensated for latency in the respiratory motion gating, with the potential to gate earlier and hence improve tumor coverage and reduce OAR doses compared to an ungated field. This work also showed that the delivered dose for exhale gating may have a dosimetric shift that is dependent on the gating window size. While this shift has been demonstrated previously in other treatment and gating systems [24–30], our study adds additional evidence that this is a gating system independent phenomenon.

One study by Smith et al., has been published on the commissioning of the Elekta Unity with the new CMM system [15]. Both their work and ours reviewed the pilot versions of the Unity CMM upgrade. For tracking accuracy, Smith et al. studied just a centrally placed 3 cm target but used sinusoidal, cos4, and patient breathing traces. Their numbers for the sinusoidal trace were consistent with ours showing the reproducibility of the CMM implementation. We also studied other target locations and sizes expanding on this dataset. Our latency measurements were comparable and showed strong agreement for the cases that had similar delivery profiles. Dosimetric measurements in Smith et al. incorporated both an ion chamber and film for the respiratory average free-breathing mode. The film profiles were compared to theoretical profiles generated versus an in-house application using gamma analysis. Looking at the similar free breathing respiratory cases using a 3 %/2 mm gamma criteria, their results were an 98.4 % gamma pass rate compared to our 89 % minimum. This is primarily attributed to their use of global gamma analysis compared to our approach of using the average dose within the 50 % isodose line as the reference dose in the calculation. Additionally, these differences are amplified by our use of a film-to-film comparison in these cases, where inter-film variation can be up to 2 %. The agreement between our works strengthens the argument for the ability of CMM to deliver reasonable tracking accuracy.

It is also important to put this new system within context of other gating and treatment delivery systems. Chen et al. [31] quantified the gating latency of an optical-surface gating system and both an Elekta and Varian C-Arm LINA0 ms when using sinusoidal functions. The Varian LINAC showed an average gating latency of 300 ms ± 45 ms while the Elekta LINAC had a latency of 1600 ms ± 72 ms. For the Unity, we measured an average of 350 ms for the exception gating, which is comparable to the Varian measurements. This is attributed to the Unity having a gridded electron gun, unlike older Elekta LINA0 ms. Additionally, our measurements for the non-prediction corrected beam latency meet the TG-76 definition for real-time tracking of 500 ms with a maximum latency of 450 ms. Kim et al. [32] performed latency measurements on the ViewRay MRIdian system using the same phantom used in our study. The MRIdian has two different imaging frame rates (4 Hz and 8 Hz) and four different registration algorithms, including deformable registrations. The beam latency for the standard registration technique averaged 159 ms for the 4 Hz imaging frame rate when sinusoidal motion was applied. Our average latency was 50 ms with an average 95th percentile of 120 ms. While a higher frame rate of the Unity contributed to this difference, the bulk of the difference can be attributed to the predictive algorithm. Multiple studies have looked at the impact of the breathing trace and gating on dose distributions for the ViewRay MR-LINA0 ms [14,33,34]. Green et al. [13] measured point doses for static and gated deliveries in a motion phantom. Their maximum dose difference was 2.18 % with an average of 0.9 %. These point dose differences are similar to the average dose differences in our study (1 % – 1.6 %). Lamb et al. [34] calculated the gamma value (3 %/3 mm, 10 % threshold) for a cos6 waveform with 10 bpm to be 87.1 %. Our measured gamma pass rates (3 %/2 mm, 50 % threshold) were all above 89 % (89.2 % – 98 %). This improvement could be attributed to the increased latency observed on the original ViewRay system. Charters et al. [14] evaluated the MRIdian with cos6 functions to represent a full exhale scenario. For their measurements with 10 bpm they achieved an average gamma pass rate of 91 % when optimizing the film position to maximize the gamma pass rate. In our work, the exhale gating strategy resulted in gamma pass rates of 74 % – 97 %. This large difference can be explained by the difference in film alignment techniques between our works.

The major strength of this work was the wide spectrum of parameters studied with respect to the beam hold duration, duty cycle, breathing rates, and range of motion prior to the dosimetry deliveries. This allowed us to streamline the film dosimetry to use a single breathing pattern while inferring the impact based on the other results. A second strength was the use of eight different plans to evaluate the dosimetric accuracy of the deliveries of clinical plans. This allowed us to vary the CMM strategy or range of motion for some studies and observe the sensitivity of the results to clinically relevant choices. Finally, we performed end-to-end testing of both the exception and free-breathing gating system to evaluate the proposed clinical workflow’s ability to not only deliver an accurate dose but to deliver it with acceptable localization accuracy.

This work is not without limitations. First, we did not commission the breath-hold gating strategy as part of this work. However, we expect the performance of the system to be similar to the exception gating, albeit with a longer beam on latency due to the algorithm implementation. Second, we only used sinusoidal waveforms for breathing motion. While most literature uses realistic breathing traces in addition to sinusoids, we felt that for our test cases, the sinusoidal pattern would allow us to better identify the cause of any deviations due to the repetitive pattern. For the free-breathing gating, the symmetric waveform made it easier to compare doses. This waveform also functioned as a difficult test for exhale gating, because the target was entering and leaving the target site rather quickly, compared to a more realistic breathing pattern. Finally, this work did not focus on the clinical implementation aspects of the gating system. This includes margin selection, patient screening for tumor visibility on the cine images, template alignment procedures, limitations on baseline shift plans, and the selection of surrogate structures for tumors not visible on MRI. These details will be discussed in future works.

The CMM upgrade provides advanced motion management capabilities to the Elekta Unity. The positional accuracy is excellent with < 1.5 mm tracking 95 % of the time. The gating system minimized dosimetric errors when delivering treatments to a moving target. This new capability is synergistic with the adaptive workflow of the Unity by further reducing uncertainty in tumor position and dosimetric deliveries.
