Precise orbital parameters,masses, and parallax of the subgiant binary system 12 Persei: a combined spectroscopic–interferometric analysis
Authors: Ahmad A. Abushattal, Hatem Widyan, Maximiliano Dirk, Mashhoor A. Al-Wardat, Mai Al-Mashaqbeh, Rene A Mendez, Deshinta Arrova Dewi, Abdallah M Hussein, Enas M. Abu-Alrob
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, we determine the orbital parallax of 12 Persei from a combined spectroscopic and interferometric solution and compare it with those from Gaia and Hipparcos to assess their consistency. We refined previous astrometric measurements and utilised precise orbital parameters and mass ratios derived through MCMC data, adjusted with the Tokovinin dynamical method and least squares. This led to a solution that was statistically consistent with both historical and new observations. By combining the visual and spectroscopic solutions, we accurately fit the system’s mass components and orbital parallax, and recent data confirm these estimates. We derived the stars’ effective temperatures, radii, and luminosities through synthetic photometry relation techniques, further validating our model when compared with observational data. Physical parameters were obtained from modified orbital elements using the Tokovinin and Al-Wardat methods. We identified 12 Per as part of the subgiant stars group in analysing magnitudes, colour indices, and magnitude differences. Our study indicates that 12 Per consists of stars of spectral subclasses F6.5IV and G1IV in the subgiant phase, likely formed by fragmentation. This work provides an independent validation of the orbital parallax through agreement with Gaia astrometry, makes a significant contribution to understanding subgiant stars, and lays the groundwork for future higher-resolution observations and improved modeling techniques.

Introduction

In order to validate independently derived parallax determinations, an interdisciplinary approach is required that combines observational, dynamical, and spectrophotometric techniques. This work explores the subgiant binary system 12 Persei (HIP 12623) using such a combined methodology. By combining interferometric, spectroscopic, and photometric data, we aim to refine its orbital parameters, mass estimates, and parallax. Gaia Data Release 1 (DR1) gave us parallaxes for over 2 million stars on average with an uncertainty of around 0.3 mas for the bright ones1. However, DR1 contains systematic errors and a bias in the zero-point of the parallax. Gaia Data Release 2 (DR2) already contained parallaxes of more than 1.3 billion stars, with typical uncertainties of 0.04 mas for bright stars, but again with a systematic parallax zero-point bias of about −0.03 mas and other systematic errors2. The independent Gaia Early DR3 significantly improved the parallax—over 1.5 billion stars have their distances estimated. For bright sources like the Sun, typical uncertainties are decreased to 0.02 mas. EDR3 provides improved astrometric and photometric precision over the previous Gaia releases. In this context, Gaia astrometry provides a robust external reference against which the orbital parallax derived in this work can be assessed3,4. Comparing the results from Hipparcos new reduction (2007) and Gaia DR3 (2022), which are statistically consistent within uncertainties, offers insight into the consistency of current astrometric methods for this system. The difference of -0.45 ± 0.52 mas reflects measurement uncertainty rather than a significant discrepancy. Although this difference does not significantly affect the astrophysical conclusions drawn for the 12 Persei system, it remains useful for contextualizing known systematic effects reported in large-scale astrometric studies, rather than indicating a catalog-level inconsistency. Gaia DR1 and RAVE data, for example, are used in the Local Disc Model, illustrating the difficulties of obtaining precise astrometric measurements. As a result of subsequent analysis, such as that based on Gaia DR2, it was determined that parallax errors, previously identified using Hipparcos data, remain a significant obstacle to refining stellar distances and kinematic models in general stellar populations5–7. Using GALEX UV Unique Source Catalogue (GUVCAT), ultraviolet photometric data can be cross-matched with Gaia DR2 and includes uncertainty information. In the present work, Gaia astrometry is therefore used as an external benchmark to assess the consistency of the independently derived orbital parallax for 12 Persei, rather than to evaluate global improvements in astrometric precision8–12. In a study that used Gaia parallaxes to derive distances to embedded objects13, Hipparcos-based distances were compared in order to demonstrate improvements in the Gaia DR2 error model. Such studies can shed light on the progress and remaining challenges by comparing the parallax errors between Gaia and Hipparcos. The astrometry precision and accuracy have improved linearly from Hipparcos (1997) to Gaia EDR314–16. Compared to earlier releases, Gaia EDR3 astrometric data are extremely reliable and consistent with the refined calibration standards of ESA, as each release has been continually improved1,17. Binary stars have greatly enriched our understanding of the universe, from exoplanets to galactic structures4,18,19. Additionally, it is crucial for our understanding of stellar evolution. They are used to test theories of star formation and stellar evolution. Moreover, analyzing these systems allows us to determine their physical and geometrical parameters. Detailed studies of binary star systems are among the most reliable methods for determining stellar masses. It remains challenging to obtain accurate physical details for each part of visual close binary systems (VCBS), even with advanced techniques such as speckle interferometry and adaptive optics. Alternatively, photodynamic modelling can analyse eclipsing binary systems (e.g.,20–22). As a result, they can produce distinctive evolutionary paths, especially when mass is exchanged between stars23–25. The binary system allows for a variety of stellar interactions, including mass transfer, tidal locking, and common envelope evolution. Through observation and analysis of these systems, astronomers can track the evolution of compact objects, including Type Ia supernova progenitors, cataclysmic variables, and X-ray binaries. Binary systems are identified, and their orbital parameters are determined by the astrometric reflex motions generated by gravity interactions within the stellar components. When these motions are detectable, they provide a powerful means of identifying binary systems1,17,26,27. The spectroscopic and visual orbital solutions can contribute significantly to the understanding of stellar properties and calibrate astrometric parallaxes by Gaia and Hipparcos9,28–33. Using spectrophotometric observations and atmospheric modeling,34 introduced a novel method for estimating both components’ physical and geometrical characteristics in visual close binary systems. An accurate measurement of magnitude differences and reliable measurements of colour indices are necessary for this technique to succeed35. Speckle interferometry is typically used for this purpose. Numerous binary systems have been effectively analyzed using this method when combined with the dynamical modeling framework developed by Tokovinin36, and Al-wardat37–45. Astrometric missions, such as Hipparcos and Gaia, have significantly advanced our knowledge of stellar distances and dynamics. However, the differences in parallax measurements between these missions present notable challenges17,46.The parallax measurements reported by different astrometric missions are briefly discussed here, with further details provided in Section 2, where the archival data and system characteristics are fully examined. Although these measurements are statistically consistent within their uncertainties, small numerical differences correspond to variations in distance that can influence derived stellar luminosities and orbital parameters. This study therefore aims to validate the orbital and physical parameters of the binary star system 12 Per, by examining two complementary methodologies: the dynamical approach using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and the spectrophotometric approach using Al-Wardat’s method. By analysing data from Hipparcos and Gaia DR3, we assess the consistency and reliability of the resulting orbital and physical parameters derived using these independent techniques16,47–49.

Archival data and system characteristics

A variety of space-based astrometric catalogues have been used to characterize HIP 12623 (12 Persei). This includes Gaia DR250, Gaia EDR31, and the Hipparcos Catalog (ESA 1997), as well as the new Hipparcos reduction17. For the analysis presented here, we used these archival datasets. During our comparative study, we emphasize both Hipparcos and Gaia DR3 parallaxes, ensuring consistency and a seamless transition to the next section on methodology. The following sections discuss the dynamical and spectrophotometric approaches to data analysis. This system has four main parallaxes. According to Hipparcos and Tycho Catalogues, we find a parallax of 40.52 ± 1.25 mas, corresponding to a distance of 24.68 ± 0.76 pc. A Hipparcos New Astrometric Catalog17 gives 41.34 ± 0.43 mas (24.19 ± 0.25 pc). Gaia DR2 reports 42.39 ± 0.29 mas (23.59 ± 0.16 pc), while Gaia DR3 yields 41.79 ± 0.29 mas, corresponding to 23.92 ± 0.17 pc.1,50–52. The parallax measurements of both the Hipparcos and the Gaia DR3 missions are then converted to distances in parsecs. In this regard, it should be noted that the parallax measurements converted to distance are not used in the final computation of the system’s physical characteristics. The conversion of the parallax measurements to distance was only done to allow the easy comparison of the measurements from various sources. This has no bearing on the final computation of the system’s physical characteristics. A list of the cataloged parameters used as input for this analysis can be found in the Table 1. These data are retrieved from SIMBAD, Hipparcos, Tycho, Gaia DR2/EDR3, the Fourth Catalogue of Interferometric Measurements of Binary Stars, and the Ninth Catalog of the Spectroscopic Binary Orbits53,54. These data sets form the basis for deriving the orbital solution of the system. The observational data used in the study are explained in detail in Sect. Orbital parameters from visual orbit. The methodology approach, including dynamical models, spectroscopic, interferometric, and photometric observations and synthesis, is outlined in Sect. “Orbital parameters from mcmc combined solution”. The synthesis of photometric observations and the construction of spectral energy distributions of both components are outlined in Sect. “Parallax and masses using spectro-visual solutions”. The orbital solution parameters, with a discussion of the derived physical implications, are outlined in Sect. “Physical parameters”. Sect. “Synthetic photometry” outlines the evolutionary level of the system based on theoretical reference paths and isochrones. A comparison of dynamic, orbital, and spectrophotometric mass determinations is discussed in Sect. “Results and discussion”, with an emphasis on agreement among parallax determinations in Section 8. Orbital parameters from visual orbit

To extract the orbital solution and refine the system’s orbital elements, we employed Tokovinin’s dynamical approach, utilizing his ORBITX code36. Relative astrometric measurements, such as angular separations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}, in arcseconds) and position angles (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}, in degrees), collected with various high-resolution observational techniques, were compiled as part of the Fourth Catalogue of Interferometric Measurements of Binary Stars (INT4). In ORBITX, all position measurements are fitted with a weighted least-squares method, with weights equal to the square of the observed uncertainty. For the code, orbital elements are necessary, including the period P (in years), the eccentricity e, the semimajor axis a (in arcseconds), the orbital inclination i (in degrees), the argument of the periastron (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega$$\end{document}) in degrees, the ascending node (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document}) in degrees, and the passage of the periastron (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_0$$\end{document}). In the observational history of the system, 12 Persei was recognized as a double-lined spectroscopic binary for the first time by Campbell & Wright (1900) 82. The subsequent calculations of the early spectroscopic orbital elements were done by Colacevich (1935, 1941) 83,84 based on photographic radial velocity observations. The later separation of the system as a visual binary through speckle interferometry resulted in the first angular constraints on its semimajor axis and orbital inclination (McAlister 1978) 85. Further speckle observations were obtained later and summarized in Hartkopf et al. (1997). The later speckle observations were supplemented by a much-improved set of spectroscopic observations and a three-dimensional orbital solution by Barlow et al. (1998) 86 . The available radial velocity and interferometric observations were comprehensively summarized by Pourbaix (2000) 87. The rich observational basis is discussed in detail and critically evaluated by58. They further showed that long-baseline interferometric observations could significantly improve the orbital and physical characterization of 12 Persei. In 2006, Bagnuolo plotted the orbit and calculated the orbital parameters of the system using the 50 points observed before 200658. Fourteen additional interferometric measurements were retrieved from the Fourth Catalogue of Interferometric Measurements of Binary Stars (INT4) that were recorded after 2006, as reported in59–62. Fig. 1 shows the modified and old ones. A comparison between the original and the altered visual measurements used in the orbital solution. The original speckle interferometric measurements were made through autocorrelation analysis, which always leads to a 180\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} ambiguity in the position angle (McAlister et al. 1987) 88. In contrast, the bispectrum analysis applied in the more modern speckle interferometric measurements completely removes the 180\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} ambiguity. In order to ensure consistency, the position angles of the original measurements were shifted by 180\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ$$\end{document} where appropriate, based on the continuity in time and compatibility with the surrounding measurements. This strategy can be verified by the joint visual-spectroscopic MCMC solution. It must be pointed out that the interferometric measurements reported by Bagnuolo et al. (2006) were performed with the CHARA Array, whose baselines are about two orders of magnitude larger than those used in classical speckle interferometry. The CHARA observations were obtained using a single baseline and are based on resolved fringe packets that provide one-dimensional projected separations. These data were not published in a form that can be directly merged with the two-dimensional relative positions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho$$\end{document}) derived from speckle interferometry. Therefore, the CHARA measurements were not included in the present orbital solution. A comparison of the orbital solutions obtained in the two cases reveals that our orbit differs with respect to the solution obtained by Bagnuolo et al. (2006), particularly with respect to the semi-major axis and the inclination. In particular, the orbital inclination and parallax are different at the 4.4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document} and 2.1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document} levels, respectively, whereas the mass and parallax estimates are in good agreement. As shown in Fig. 1, the differences are largest near the periastron passage, where the sensitivity of speckle interferometry is limited, whereas long-baseline interferometry can still be effective. These findings are representative of the complementary capabilities and limitations of speckle and long-baseline interferometric measurements for the determination of close binary orbits.

Orbital parameters from MCMC combined solution

The Bayesian framework was used to compute a self-consistent astrometric and radial velocity solution. As part of this methodology, Bayesian inference has the following key strengths: (i) providing realistic confidence intervals for orbital parameters estimated, and (ii) producing posterior probability density functions (PDFs) for each orbital element and stellar mass. Initial parameter estimates used in the MCMC algorithm were taken from previous literature reports for efficient convergence. Using this method, sampling is initiated near the global minimum of the parameter space, which increases the probability of rapid and reliable convergence. There were two solutions: one that incorporated Gaia EDR3 parallax as a prior constraint and one that treated parallax as a free parameter. It ensures full internal consistency among the astrometric, spectroscopic, and orbital parameters by dynamically determining the parallax as part of the estimation process. The orbital parallax refers to the parallax value derived by combining the spectroscopic and visual orbital solutions of a binary system. In the spectroscopic solution, the projected semi-major axis in physical units \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(a \sin i)$$\end{document} and the orbital period are obtained. The visual solution independently provides the semi-major axis in angular units and the orbital inclination. By combining the linear scale of the orbit from spectroscopy with the angular scale from the visual solution, the orbital parallax can be directly derived. This method determines the distance to the system based solely on its orbital geometry and kinematics, without requiring trigonometric astrometric data from space-based missions such as Hipparcos or Gaia7,63,64. In this work, the likelihood functions assume a Gaussian observation model, and the priors are just uniform densities in a specific interval (e.g., the prior of inclination p(i) is 1 in [0, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document}) and 0 outside that range). In orbits with relatively good orbital coverage, the expected value approximately coincides with the maximum a posteriori (MAP)/maximum likelihood (ML),65,66. Table 3 exhibits the results of our two solutions, as well as the values reported in the Sixth Catalogue of Orbits of Visual Binary Stars maintained by the US Naval Observatory (hereafter Orb61), and the 9th Catalog of Spectroscopic Binary Orbits (hereafter SB92), for comparison purposes. For each solution, the upper row indicates the MAP value. In contrast, the lower row is the median of the distribution, with the upper quartile (Q75) and lower quartile (Q25) of the distribution as superscript and subscript, respectively. The same logic is applied to Table 4,where we present the results for the orbital parallax, masses, and mass ratio, along with the values of parallaxes reported by HIPPARCOS (the new reduction,67), and GAIA missions (DR3 values). For our no-prior solution, the orbit and radial velocity curves are presented in Fig. 2, while Fig. 3 displays the PDFs for the different orbital parameters. We also calculate the O-C residuals for our data, which are shown in Fig. 4. The figures for our prior solution, as well as the figures of pair distribution for both solutions and the data points used in this work, are available in Zendo. The good observational sampling in both astrometry and radial velocity implies that we can obtain a fairly tight orbital solution for this system, as indicated by the narrow inter-quartile ranges in Table 3. In particular, for the no-prior solution, the uncertainty in the period is negligible (less than 0.0008%, mostly due to the dense phase coverage in radial velocity). In contrast, the uncertainty in the semi-major axis is less than 1.4%. As a result, the final individual masses are determined to better than 3.5% for both primary and secondary (and, even a factor of two smaller than that for the fit with a prior in parallax). The adopted parallax is a critical parameter that enters into the final mass estimate. In Table 4 we show the Hipparcos (new reduction) parallax and the Gaia DR3 value. The Hipparcos and Gaia values seem consistent, within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\sigma$$\end{document} of the (larger) Hipparcos error. However, the F2 goodness-of-fit statistics of the Hipparcos solution is rather larger (8.42, a“good value” is considered to be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lesssim 3.0$$\end{document}3 which implies that the Hipparcos parallax could be somewhat uncertain. On the other hand, the RUWE parameter provides a good indicator of the reliability of the Gaia astrometry3. For well-behaved sources, where the single-star model is a good fit for the astrometric observations, RUWE is expected to be around 1.0. However, sources with RUWE significantly greater than 1.0 (e.g., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$> 1.4$$\end{document}) may have issues with the astrometric solution or may be indicative of a non-single source (and hence a biased parallax), which can aid in the detection of binaries or multiple systems68. For 12 Persei, the RUWE is indeed slightly elevated, at 2.75. However, there are no indications of detecting multiple peaks in the individual satellite scans (IPD_frac_multi_peak = 0). To asses the impact of an uncertainty in the parallax in the derived orbital solutions, we have carried out solutions with a and without a parallax prior, the results are shown in Table 3. We employed two primary methodologies to analyse 12 Per: the dynamical approach using MCMC and the spectrophotometric approach using Al-Wardat’s method. The MCMC method, being purely dynamical, provides orbital solutions based on astrometric and radial velocity data. In contrast, Al-Wardat’s method combines spectrophotometric data with atmospheric modelling to derive physical parameters. We used the MCMC code to derive orbital elements and masses, initialising with parameters from the literature and incorporating Hipparcos and Gaia DR3 parallaxes. This method allows for a self-consistent orbital solution with realistic confidence limits. Al-Wardat’s method integrates spectrophotometric data to model the atmospheres of binary components, providing effective temperature, radius, and luminosity. Conclusions

By validating the consistency between independently derived orbital parallaxes and space-based astrometric measurements, we achieved a self-consistent and robust characterization of the binary star system 12 Per by applying both observational and analytical techniques. Utilizing the interaction power of MCMC, we extracted the precise orbital parameters and mass ratios, thereby providing orbital parallaxes that are statistically consistent with both Hipparcos and Gaia data, as listed in Table 10. These parameters further developed the Tokovinin dynamical method by using least-squares adjustments of historical and new observational data. Visual and spectroscopic solutions have provided us with a precise estimation of the mass and inclination of the binary components, and have been validated with recent data. The method applied to derive physical parameters, such as effective temperature, radius, and luminosity, using synthetic photometry ensures the reliability of these parameters. The convergence of observational and synthetic data provided a detailed assessment of the accuracy of our estimations. We have stringent evidence that our model is robust, and future work should become more rigorous, applying it to a broader range with higher-resolution observations, state-of-the-art modeling, and a more consistent level of monitoring. In this study, we conducted a thorough investigation of the visual binary system 12 Per by applying two complementary analytical techniques: Tokovinin’s dynamical approach, used for solve the orbital motion and refine the system’s orbital parameters, and Al-Wardat’s method, aimed at deriving a complete set of physical characteristics. Al-Wardat’s technique utilizes observed magnitudes, color indices, and magnitude differences to construct synthetic spectral energy distributions (SEDs) for each stellar component. The results from this in-depth analysis classify 12 Per as belonging to the subgiant category. Using data from established photometric systems such as Johnson, Strömgren, and Tycho, we derived both combined and individual synthetic magnitudes and colour indices for the system. The integration of Al-Wardat’s and Tokovinin’s methodologies enabled us to estimate the dynamical and individual masses of the components by placing them on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram and comparing their positions to theoretical evolutionary tracks. These comparisons revealed strong consistency between the observed photometric data and the synthetic results generated through Al-Wardat’s framework. The estimated dynamical mass sum for the system falls within the range of 2.329 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.701,M_\odot$$\end{document}, while the mass sum obtained via Al-Wardat’s method ranges between 2.40 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.46 M_\odot$$\end{document}. Note that the dynamical mass from the visual + spectroscopic orbit fit (Tables 3+4) has a smaller range of 2.46 to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2.58, M_\odot$$\end{document} to than the dynamical values listed above. The small spread in the derived values likely stems from residual uncertainties in the orbital parameters, which could be reduced through future high-precision measurements of relative positions. Based on their placement on evolutionary tracks, the stellar components are identified as subgiant stars of spectral types F6.5 and G1, and are hypothesized to have formed via fragmentation. Given the similar metallicity, coeval age, and comparable evolutionary status of both components, the fragmentation represents the most plausible formation mechanism for the 12 Persi system. This is consistent with theoretical expectations and observational evidence for binaries of near-equal mass. These results make a significant contribution to the field, particularly in light of the scarcity of well-characterized subgiant binary systems in the current astronomical literature. Our comparative study of 12 Per using dynamical and spectrophotometric methods has provided valuable insights into the reliability of astrometric measurements. By focusing on Hipparcos and Gaia DR3 data, we demonstrated the complementary nature of these methods in validating parallax determinations. The MCMC approach offered precise orbital solutions, while Al-Wardat’s method provided detailed physical parameters through spectrophotometric analysis. The results highlight the importance of employing multiple methodologies for accurate stellar characterisation. Rather than revealing a conflict between space-based astrometric missions, this study demonstrates that, for a well-constrained binary system such as 12 Persei, orbital parallax determinations provide an independent and reliable validation of Gaia astrometry. Future research should continue to integrate these approaches, thereby enhancing our understanding of stellar evolution in the comparison of orbital solutions and cross-referencing contributions to the field of astrometry, providing a robust framework for the independent validation and benchmarking of parallax determinations in future studies.
