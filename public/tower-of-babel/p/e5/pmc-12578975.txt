A special latch in yeast mitofusin guarantees mitochondrial fusion by stabilizing self-assembly
Authors: Shu-Jing Huang, Dong-Fei Ma, Caiting Yu, Jing Li, Xinyu Tu, Zi Huang, Yuanbo Qi, Jun-Ying Ou, Jian-Xiong Feng, Bing Yu, Yu-Lu Cao, Jia-Xing Yue, Junjie Hu, Ming Li, Ying Lu, Liming Yan, Song Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe mitochondrion is a highly dynamic organelle, constantly undergoing fusion and fission, which are critical processes for the health of cells. Fusion of the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) is mediated by the mitofusins belonging to the dynamin superfamily of GTPases. Most eukaryotic organisms possess two cooperatively functioning mitofusins, but yeast has only one mitofusin (Fzo1). How Fzo1 solely catalyzes OMM fusion is unclear. Here, we present crystal structures of truncated Fzo1 (Fzo1IM) in different nucleotide-loading states and report a special mechanistic feature of Fzo1 through systematic functional studies. Differing from mammalian mitofusins, Fzo1 contains an extra latch bulge (LB) that is essential for the viability of yeast. Upon GTP loading, Fzo1IM dimerizes via the GTPase domain and prefers the closed conformation. This state is then locked by the subsequent trans interaction mediated by the LB of each protomer, so that Fzo1IM remains dimerized in the closed conformation even after GTP hydrolysis. This special mechanistic feature may be relevant to the previous observation that degradation of Fzo1 by the ubiquitin-proteasome system is required for mitochondrial fusion. Our study reveals how mitochondrial fusion in yeast is efficiently ensured with limited GTP consumption, which broadens current understanding of this fundamental biological process.Subject terms: Structural biology, Single-molecule biophysics, Mitochondria

Introduction

Mitochondria are highly dynamic organelles that constantly undergo fusion and fission1–4, the balance of which is important for their health and physiological functions5,6. As unicellular eukaryotes, yeast have varying mitochondrial volume and morphology during mitosis, mating, meiosis, and sporulation7–11. Mitochondrial fusion is essential for the maintenance and normal inheritance of mitochondrial DNA, which has been observed for budding yeast in both sexual and vegetative life cycles12–14. Yeast also confers mitochondrial fusion to adapt to different metabolic conditions15. For example, mitochondria form a branched tubular network in logarithmically growing yeast cells cultured on glucose-containing medium6,16,17. When transferred to non-fermentable carbon sources, such as glycerol or alcohol, the mitochondrial network becomes larger and more branched because of the increased demand for respiration18,19.

Outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) fusion is catalyzed by mitofusin, a multi-domain GTPase belonging to the dynamin superfamily20–22. Most metazoans possess two mitofusins (mitofusin-1/MFN1 and mitofusin-2/MFN2) that function cooperatively, and sometimes redundantly23–26. The OMM anchored mitofusins possess a cytosol-faced N-terminal GTPase domain (GD), two helical domains (HD1/2, or helical bundles, HB1/2), and a transmembrane domain (TM) between HD1 and HD223. Unlike metazoans, yeast have only one mitofusin called Fzo1 (fuzzy onion 1, or Fzo1p)27–29. Compared to mammalian mitofusins, Fzo1 is larger in size and has substantial sequence variation beyond the GTPase domain.

Previous structural studies have made great contributions to the understanding of mitofusin-mediated OMM fusion. Our groups solved structures of truncated human MFN1 and MFN2 for which HD2 and TM were removed (termed MFN1IM and MFN2IM, IM for ‘internally modified’) in different nucleotide loading states, and found that GTP-binding-dependent homo- and hetero-dimerization of the GDs and GTP-hydrolysis-coupled domain rearrangement are essential for mitochondrial tethering and eventual fusion30–33. Mitofusin homologs in bacteria (e.g., BDLP from N. punctiforme and IniA from M. smegmatis) lack TM, and thus are not strictly membrane-anchored34,35. Structures of full-length BDLP revealed GTP binding-dependent movement of HD2 and formation of helical oligomers34,36. A cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) study on isolated yeast mitochondria provided insightful structural information on the fusion process; the formation of a ‘fusion pore’ at the rim of docked OMMs is an essential step and likely mediated by Fzo137. In addition, the fusogenic action of Fzo1 is highly dependent on the crosstalk with other proteins, including the ubiquitination fusion degradation (UFD) pathway-related chaperone Cdc48 and fungi-specific UGO138–43. UGO1 protects Fzo1 from degradation by mediating Fzo1 cis dimerization44. After GTP hydrolysis, Fzo1 gets ubiquitylated via Mdm30 and then recognized by Cdc48, which eventually leads to complex segregation and promotes mitochondrial fusion43,44. However, due to the lack of a high-resolution structure, the detailed functional mechanism of Fzo1 during yeast mitochondrial fusion remains obscure.

In this study, we structurally characterized Saccharomyces cerevisiae Fzo1 and found a specific domain that is pivotal for mitochondrial fusion and the vitality of yeast cells. Biochemical and single-molecule biophysical analysis revealed that the newly discovered domain locks Fzo1 in the dimeric state and closed conformation even after GTP hydrolysis, ensuring the efficiency of mitochondrial tethering. Our data hint to how yeast manages to fulfill mitochondrial fusion with only one mitofusin homolog.

Results

Overall structure of truncated Fzo1

As full-length yeast Fzo1 proteins are insoluble, we generated a modified construct from S. cerevisiae Fzo1 based on the MFN1IM and MFN2IM constructs used in our previous studies, for which the non-conserved N-terminal disordered region (residues 1‒82) and the predicted helical domain 2 (HD2) and transmembrane helices (TM) were removed (Fig. 1a). This construct, hereafter termed Fzo1IM, was loaded with GTP when purified from Escherichia coli (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We determined a crystal structure of dimeric GTP-bound Fzo1IM at 2.7 Å (Table 1). Fzo1IM is composed of a GTPase domain (GD), helical domain 1 (HD1), and a bulging region (Fig. 1b). The GD consists of a central nine-stranded β-sheet (β1G‒β6G and β1’G‒β3’G) surrounded by seven ɑ-helices (ɑ1G‒ɑ5G, ɑ1’G and ɑ2’G) and two 310-helices (η1G and η2G) (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The overall folding of the Fzo1 GD is highly consistent with that of human MFN2, with the following exceptions: Fzo1 possesses an extra ɑ-helix (ɑ0G) between β1’G and β2’G compared to MFN2, and ~ 20 amino acid residues between β6G and ɑ5G of Fzo1, which corresponds to ɑ2’G of MFN2, are missing due to flexibility (Supplementary Fig. 1c). The so-called Hinge 2, namely residues Val186 and Lys459, links the GD and HD1, and the latter is formed by four bundled ɑ-helices (ɑ1H‒ɑ4H) with a massive internal hydrophobic network (Supplementary Fig. 1d).

Fzo1IM remains in a closed conformation analogous to GDP•BeF3¯-bound MFN1IM32, in which the GD intimately contacts the HD1. The GD-HD1 interface involves an extended salt bridge belt and a small hydrophobic cluster (Fig. 1c, d). Notably, Lys464, a key ubiquitylation site involved in the regulation of mitochondrial fusion45, forms a trilateral salt bridge with Asp335 and Arg182 as predicted in an earlier report43. In addition, HD1 residues Lys464 and Arg182 form interdomain hydrogen bonds with several main chain atoms from the GD (Fig. 1c). Though buried in the closed conformation of Fzo1IM, Lys464 is likely to be exposed at open conformation, as predicted from MFN1IM and MFN2IM structures (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Another ubiquitylation site, Lys398, is on the surface of Fzo1IM with no apparent contact with other residues (Supplementary Fig. 1f).

The GD interface of Fzo1IM dimer differs from that of mitofusin dimers

GTP hydrolysis by dynamin superfamily members relies on homo-dimerization via their GDs, which means that they are self-stimulated GTPases and do not require specific GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs). Therefore, it was unusual for Fzo1IM dimers to maintain a GTP-bound form during protein purification and crystallization processes (Supplementary Fig. 2a). GTP hydrolysis assays also indicated that Fzo1IM had negligible GTPase activity in solution (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Similarly, a bacterial mitofusin homolog, Mycolicibacterium smegmatis IniA, has been found to crystallize in a GTP-bound form35. The back-folded IniA Trunk domain, corresponding to the HD2, which was absent in our Fzo1IM, prevented GD dimerization. However, contacts between the GDs were present in GTP-bound Fzo1IM dimers (Fig. 1b). To understand why the GTPase is intrinsically suppressed here, we structurally analyzed the GTP coordination and GD dimerization of Fzo1.

G1‒G4 motifs are essential elements for GTPases to bind and hydrolyze GTP. In the GTP-bound Fzo1IM structure, most key residues are present at the canonical positions for coordinating the nucleotide and catalyzing the Mg2+ ion (Fig. 2a, b). An Fzo1-specific variant residue of the G4 motif, Lys370, forms a hydrogen bond with the C-6 carbonyl group of the guanine base. The Switch I conformation of Fzo1IM is consistent with that of the dimeric MFN1IM in the transition state of GTP hydrolysis but slightly different from those of post-hydrolysis GDP-bound MFN1IM and MFN2IM dimers (Supplementary Fig. 2c). By extensively washing with NaCl and ion-exchange chromatography, we managed to purify nucleotide-free Fzo1IM; unlike the GTP-loaded version, this form was monomeric in solution, as indicated by size-exclusion chromatography coupled with right-angle light scattering (SEC-RALS) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2d). Because Fzo1IM aggregated during isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), we measured the nucleotide-binding affinity for Fzo1IM via a fluorescence-based assay46. Conjugation of the N-methyl-3’-O-anthranoyl (mant) fluorescent group did not prevent GTP from triggering Fzo1IM dimerization (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f ). The dissociation constant (Kd) values for mant-GTP and mant-GDP were 5.6 and 51.8 μM, respectively (Fig. 2d).

Next, we investigated the GD dimeric (G) interface of Fzo1IM. The ~ 1100 Å2 G interface of Fzo1IM was comparable to those of MFN1 and MFN2 in terms of area31,33. Major trans contacts were found between Switch I and η1G/ɑ4G, and between ɑ2G and ɑ3G (Fig. 2e), whereas nucleotides were not directly involved. Notably, the central trans salt bridge pairs found in MFN1 (Glu209-Arg238) and MFN2 (Glu230-Arg259), which are pivotal for the GD-mediated dimerization and GTP hydrolysis31,33, are absent in the Fzo1IM dimer. Though the corresponding residues are conserved for Fzo1 (Glu344 and Lys371), they are not close enough to form a salt bridge (Supplementary Fig. 2g), leaving us an impression that the key hydrolysis machineries of this Fzo1IM dimer are not in optimal configurations.

The Fzo1-specific latch domain constitutes a dimeric interface

Between ɑ1H and ɑ2H of the HD1 lies a loop-rich bulging region (residues 126‒156) containing a short β-strand (β1B, residues 140‒142). β1B and adjacent residues of the bulging region mediate extensive intramolecular (cis) associations with the HD1. In particular, Val135, Leu136, Leu138, and Ile140 wedge into an extended hydrophobic valley formed by the HD1 residues (Fig. 3a), and a few hydrophilic interactions are also present (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, β1Bs from the two protomers of the Fzo1IM dimer form an antiparallel β-sheet (Fig. 3c). Other intermolecular (trans) contacts mediated by the bulging region comprise Gln145 forming two hydrogen bonds with Gln168/Ser171 from the HD1, as well as π-π stacking of bulging region residue Tyr146 and GD residue Met328 (Fig. 3d). Taken together, these trans contacts constitute a profound dimeric interface (~ 1400 Å2) that is even larger than the G interface. Based on the structural and functional characteristics of the bulging region presented here and subsequent studies, we termed it the ‘latch bulge’ (LB).

Based on the primary and tertiary structures of eukaryotic mitofusins, we found that the LB exists only in fungal Fzo proteins, but its length and predicted structure vary among different fungal species (Supplementary Fig. 3). Only the hydrophobic residues involved in the intramolecular contacts, as mentioned above, exhibit moderate conservation (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 4). Perhaps for this reason, the LB has not been investigated sufficiently since Fzo1 was first reported more than 25 years ago.

LB is critical for yeast mitochondrial fusion and vitality

To understand the biological function of this specific domain, we generated three Fzo1 constructs with different deletion versions in the LB and individually expressed them in a yeast strain depleted of endogenous FZO1. Of these constructs, LBΔ1 lacked the entire LB, whereas LBΔ2 and LBΔ3 had only their β1B and several adjacent residues replaced by artificial Gly/Ser linkers (Fig. 4b). These modifications regarding LB did not affect the exogenous expression and mitochondrial localization of Fzo1 (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b) First, we tested the rescue of these constructs to mitochondrial elongation. As indicated by mitochondria-targeted GFP, cells with exogenous expression of wild-type (WT) Fzo1 presented similar mitochondrial morphology as the native strain, but all three LBΔ constructs failed to restore the mitochondrial elongation of the fzo1Δ strain (Fig. 4c). Next, we assayed the rescue of yeast growth by LBΔ constructs on dextrose and glycerol media (YPD and YPG). Yeast cells defective in mitochondrial respiration were able to survive on YPD but not on YPG because glycerol is a non-fermentable carbon source19,47. As expected, though all tested groups showed normal growth on YPD, fzo1Δ cells expressing either of the three LBΔ constructs did not survive on YPG (Fig. 4d). These results suggest that the LB is critical for yeast mitochondrial fusion and vitality.

We then examined the biological importance of the intra- and inter-molecular interactions mediated by the LB as shown in Fig. 3. We generated five Fzo1 variants (V135D, L136D, L138D, I140D, and double mutant Q168A/D478A) at the intra-molecular interface, and two variants (double mutant Q145A/Y146A and triple mutant Q168A/S171A/M328A) at the inter-molecular interface. These variants were individually applied to the mitochondrial morphology assay and glycerol growth assay. Except for V135D, fzo1Δ yeast cells transfected with either of the other four intra-molecular interface variants exhibited fragmented mitochondria, and did not survive on YPG (Fig. 4e, f and Supplementary Fig. 5c), suggesting that the intra-molecular contact between the LB and the other area of Fzo1 is essential for its function. On the other hand, unlike the β1B-missing LBΔ constructs tested above, the Q145A/Y146A and Q168A/S171A/M328A variants did not affect the fusogenic activity of Fzo1 or cell growth on YPG, which means that β1B is the key element for the LB-mediated trans interaction in Fzo1 dimer.

LB deletion does not alter the G interface

Given the profound LB-mediated dimeric interface, as well as the difference between Fzo1 and MFN1/2 G-interfaces mentioned above (Supplementary Fig. 2g), we wondered whether the presence of the LB hinders the ‘proper’ crosstalk between the GDs that is required for stimulated GTP hydrolysis. To elucidate this issue, we solved two additional crystal structures of Fzo1IM with deletions in the LB, namely GTP-bound Fzo1IMLBΔ2 and GDP•BeF3¯-bound Fzo1IMLBΔ3, with resolutions at 2.8 Å and 2.1 Å, respectively (Table 1 and Fig. 5a, b). For these two nucleotide-bound constructs, which also form dimers in solution (Supplementary Fig. 6a), though the LB-mediated trans interactions are apparently absent, the overall folding and domain orientation are highly consistent with those of GTP-bound Fzo1IM dimer, with only an approximately 3 Å difference in the distance between the selected sites at the two distal HD1 ends (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 6b). Interestingly, the distal HD1 ends of the two protomers of the Fzo1IM dimers are much closer than those of the GDP•BeF3¯-bound MFN1IM dimer (Fig. 5c), and this structural difference is likely to be intrinsic and independent of the presence of the LB. The G interfaces shown in the three structural models are nearly identical except that a number of water molecules could be found along the G interface in GDP•BeF3¯-bound Fzo1IMLBΔ3 with a higher resolution, contributing to the trans interactions (Supplementary Fig. 6c). At the active site of the GD, the G1‒G4 motifs of Fzo1IM in the GTP-bound and GDP•BeF3¯-bound forms exhibited congruent local conformations (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). These structural details indicate that the residue contacts at the G interface found in GTP-bound Fzo1IM are not influenced by the LB-mediated trans interactions.

In accordance with GTP remaining intact in the Fzo1IMLBΔ2 structure, removal of the LB did not stimulate the GTPase activity of Fzo1IM (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Inspired by a previous study on Fzo128,47, we found that the supply of acetate boosted GTP turnover rates of Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ3, but not in a protein concentration-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 7b). For human MFN1 and MFN2, variance of a single amino acid residue (MFN1I108/MFN2T129) resulted in the vast difference in GTPase activity between the two proteins31,33. Similarly, a point mutation at the MFN1I108/MFN2T129-comparable site in Switch I, P219G, dramatically increased the GTP turnover rate of both Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ3 (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). In all cases, the GTP turnover of Fzo1IMLBΔ3 never exceeded that of Fzo1IM, confirming that the GTPase activity of Fzo1 is somewhat ‘restricted’ by the architecture of its own catalytic elements but not by the presence of the LB.

LB stabilizes Fzo1 dimer after GTP hydrolysis

Considering its indispensability in mitochondrial fusion and growth in yeast, it is important to understand why the LB is needed as an extra contributor to the dimerization of Fzo1. Therefore, we investigated the function of the LB at the molecular level. As mentioned earlier, nucleotide-free Fzo1IM was intrinsically monomeric in solution (Fig. 2c), implying that the LB-mediated trans interaction did not occur spontaneously without nucleotide. We also tested the dimerization efficiency of G interface mutants for both Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ3. Individual mutations Q217R, L218R, and N318A completely abolished the dimerization of Fzo1IMLBΔ3, as well as Fzo1IM. Interestingly, the Fzo1IMLBΔ(D216R) mutant reserved a minimal potential to dimerize in the presence of the hydrolysis-resistant GTP analog GTPγS or GDP•BeF3¯, but this potential was greatly expanded in Fzo1IM(D216R) (Supplementary Fig. 8a), suggesting that the LB provided extra affinity for dimerization.

We then introduced all four GD interface mutations (D216R, Q217R, L218R, and N318A) individually to Fzo1 and tested these mutants in cell-based assays. Consistent with the biochemical data, fzo1Δ yeast cells transfected with either of the mutants Q217R, L218R, and N318R exhibited fragmented mitochondria and did not survive on YPG, whereas the D216R mutant was able to restore the mitochondrial elongation and cell growth on YPG (Supplementary Fig. 8b‒d). These results indicate that, similar to MFNs, dimerization of the GD is essential for the fusogenic activity of Fzo1.

Next, we checked the dimerization status after Fzo1IM constructs completed the catalyzation of GTP hydrolysis. Proteins were incubated with GTP before SEC. As WT Fzo1IM or Fzo1IMLBΔ3 were not able to fully hydrolyze GTP and remained predominantly GTP-bound even after overnight incubation (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b), we used hyperactive (relative to WT) mutant P219G for the experiment. After a 2-hour incubation with GTP, Fzo1IM(P219G) was still dimerized, as indicated by SEC, and HPLC analysis confirmed that proteins collected from the dimer peak were exclusively GDP-loaded. In contrast, Fzo1IMLBΔ(P219G) became monomeric after GTP was hydrolyzed (Fig. 5d, e). These data reveal the role of the LB as a post-hydrolysis latch to maintain Fzo1 dimerization.

LB locks post-GTP-hydrolysis Fzo1 in a closed conformation

A common feature of dynamin superfamily members is a GTP hydrolysis-coupled domain-wide conformational change. For mitofusins, nucleotide-free and GDP-bound MFN1IM and MFN2IM both adopt an open conformation in which the HD1s of the MFN1IM and MFN2IM dimers stretch in the opposite directions30,31,33. In contrast, GDP•BeF3¯-bound MFN1IM remains in a closed conformation, and the HD1s of the dimer point in the same direction, which is supposed to be a crucial step for pulling the opposing membranes together32. For our structures of GTP-bound and GDP•BeF3¯-bound Fzo1IM constructs, only the closed conformation was observed. To understand whether Fzo1 also experiences a GTP hydrolysis-coupled conformational change and the role of LB in this process, we performed single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (smFRET) experiments on Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ3 in different nucleotide-loading states48. We generated cysteine-free Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ3 constructs in which the cysteines were replaced with other amino acid residues, and then introduced double-mutation D391C/S491C on these constructs for site-specific labeling with Cy3/Cy549 (Supplementary Fig. 10a). These mutants, termed Fzo1IMFRET and Fzo1IMLBΔ3FRET, remained active in GTP hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 10b) and exhibited satisfactory labeling efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 10c). For smFRET experiments on Fzo1IM constructs in nucleotide-free (apo) and GDP-bound forms, N-terminally His6-tagged proteins with Cy3/Cy5 labeling were immobilized and the FRET of the two fluorophores on each molecule recorded, which negatively correlated with the distance between the two labeled sites. Notably, the FRET measurements for the dimeric GTPγS-bound and GDP•BeF3¯-bound Fzo1IM constructs were designed as described previously50, to ensure that within all measured GD-mediated dimers, only one protomer carries fluorescent labels (Supplementary Fig. 10d).

First, we inspected the GD-HD1 relative movement of Fzo1IMFRET at a time resolution of 100 ms. Fzo1IMFRET displayed two steadily distinguished FRET states (~0.4 and ~0.8) that were similar to the equivalent regions of MxA, another member of the dynamin superfamily50 (Fig. 6a). These two FRET values represented the open (low-FRET) and closed (high-FRET) conformations, respectively (Fig. 6b). The low-FRET states were frequently observed for nucleotide-free (~ 65%) and GDP-bound (~ 50%) Fzo1IMFRET molecules, suggesting that Fzo1IMFRET is able to adopt the open conformation during GTP hydrolysis. In contrast, Fzo1IMFRET remained predominantly in the high-FRET closed conformation in the presence of GTPγS or GDP•BeF3¯, which was consistent with the crystal structures (Fig. 6a, c). Moreover, analysis of the transition density plots indicated that the open/closed conformational transitions of Fzo1IMFRET were frequent in the nucleotide-free and GDP-bound forms but rare in the GTPγS-bound and GDP•BeF3¯-bound forms (Fig. 6d). The dwell times of each state also supported the above conclusions, in which the high-FRET states in GTPγS- and GDP•BeF3¯-bound forms showed significantly longer dwell times (11.4 ± 1.3 s and 13.2 ± 1.5 s) than low-FRET states (1.2 ± 0.1 s and 0.8 ± 0.1 s), indicating relatively stable closed conformations with low transition rates into open conformations (Fig. 6e). In the same smFRET analyses of Fzo1IMLBΔ3FRET, however, we observed much higher portions of low-FRET open-conformation molecules and more frequent open/closed conformational transitions in the presence of GTPγS or GDP•BeF3¯, though it behaved similarly to Fzo1IMFRET in the nucleotide-free and GDP-bound forms (Fig. 6f‒i). These results reveal that Fzo1 undergoes a GTP-hydrolysis-dependent conformational change at the GD-HD1 region and LB locks Fzo1 in the closed conformation after the GD-mediated dimer is formed.

Discussion

In summary, we elucidated unique biochemical and structural features of Fzo1 required for catalyzing OMM fusion, including low intrinsic GTPase activity, guanine nucleotide-dependent domain-wide conformational dynamics, and the specific LB serving as an indispensable latch to lock Fzo1 molecules in the GD-mediated dimeric form and closed GD-HD1 conformation. These data address several questions remaining in the field of yeast OMM fusion and provide valuable insights to achieve a general understanding of the functional mechanism and evolution of mitofusins.

Unlike many other membrane fusion machineries in eukaryotic cells, mitofusins need to perform multiple tasks by themselves, including the tethering, docking, and merger of opposing membranes. As the first step, tethering is realized by GTP-dependent dimerization of mitofusin GD. Being the sole mitofusin in yeast, Fzo1 resembles human MFN2 over MFN1 in terms of overall topology, GTPase activity (in non-acetate conditions), affinity for guanine nucleotides, and maintenance of post-hydrolysis dimerization. Our previous study indicated that human MFN2IM has much stronger liposome tethering efficiency than MFN1IM because MFN2IM is able to remain dimeric after GTP is hydrolyzed33. In turn, stable GD-mediated dimerization prevents GDP/GTP exchange and renders MFN2 a weak GTPase, whereas MFN1 executes relatively quick dimerization-hydrolysis-dissociation cycles31,33,51. This mechanism of MFN2 may apply to Fzo1, suggesting fast GTP turnover not being required for mitochondrial fusion.

Despite the above similarities, some differences between Fzo1 and MFN2 should be noted. MFN2 maintains post-hydrolysis dimerization via the G interface. For Fzo1, the G interface is crucial for the initial dimerization upon GTP loading, but post-hydrolysis dimerization is actually maintained by the trans interaction mediated by LBs because we observed that Fzo1IMLBΔ3 dimers broke into monomers after GTP was hydrolyzed. More importantly, smFRET showed that, once the LB-mediated dimer was formed, Fzo1 was locked in the closed GD-HD1 conformation. As the open-to-closed conformational change in mitofusins is thought to pull the opposing membrane into close proximity23,52, such closed Fzo1 dimers likely account for the electron densities surrounding the docked OMMs seen in the cryo-ET study37. In this case, sustained LB-mediated Fzo1 dimers would be key machinery to hold the opposing OMMs in the docking state until the ‘fusion pore’ is formed to initiate the final membrane merger.

Although sustained Fzo1 dimers facilitate fusion initiation, their existence on post-fusion OMMs may not be favorable because clustered Fzo1 dimers fixed in a closed conformation restrict enlargement of the fusion pore and prevent re-use of Fzo1 molecules for the next round of OMM fusion. This may be an intriguing explanation for why active degradation of Fzo1 by the ubiquitin-proteasome system is critical for yeast mitochondrial fusion42–45,53 and hints to an efficient but probably ‘disposable’ character of Fzo1. We speculate that mitochondrial fusion in unicellular yeast must be fulfilled in a timely and GTP-saving manner to meet relatively simple and somewhat ‘scheduled’ metabolic needs during the life cycle. Similar examples can be seen with ER fusion in yeast cells, as the fusogen Sey1p quickly achieves dimer formation in the closed conformation upon nucleotide loading and likely sustains it during the fusion reaction54. In contrast, MFN2 lacks the LB and can adopt the open conformation in the post-hydrolysis dimer33, suggesting that the regulation and recycling of MFN2 would be more convenient, rendering MFN2 adaptive to different and more complex biological events in various cell types from metazoans.

Previous studies have suggested that Fzo1 forms cis dimers on the OMM44. We infer that the observed self-association is likely mediated by TM-based binding in a nucleotide-independent manner55 and LB interactions are not directly involved in these dimers, as nucleotide-free Fzo1IM is monomeric in solution. Moreover, in the AlphaFold-generated model56, the LB region is a loop with high flexibility and large structural differences from the LB observed in our structure, implying that the interaction between LBs is not instantly formed, but entails refolding of LBs following GD-mediated dimerization. Instead, the cis-dimerization is more likely to be mediated by the HD2, which is missing in our structure, as the MFN1 construct containing HD2 is a dimer in the nucleotide-free form31. We cannot exclude the possibility that GTP loading may also incur LB-mediated cis-dimerization of Fzo1, but these dimers would not be active anymore in catalyzing OMM fusion. Finally, it is interesting that the LB exhibits a diverse length and amino acid composition in Fzo1 from different fungal species. Future studies are needed to answer questions on whether they all function as S. cerevisiae Fzo1-like latches and/or interaction sites for other binding partners.

Our smFRET results have clearly supported the role of LB in locking the Fzo1 dimer in the closed conformation, but we still need to clarify two points regarding the smFRET data. The first point is that the FRET efficiency value measured for Fzo1IMFRET in the open conformation (~ 0.45) indicated a theoretical distance of ~ 62 Å between the FRET pairs, which differed from 72.9 Å as depicted in the model shown in Fig. 6b. However, the predicted open-conformation model of Fzo1IM shown here was not experimentally determined but derived from the crystal structure of nucleotide-free MFN2IM. Besides the experimental factors, including the protein-fluorophore linker57 and donor-acceptor relative orientations58, the above inconsistency of distance values implies that: just as in the closed conformation shown in Fig. 5c, the difference of GD-HD1 angle exists also between open-conformation Fzo1 and MFNs. The second point is that for the GTPγS-bound Fzo1IMFRET, the dwell time of the high-FRET state (11.4 s) was 9.5 times that of the low-FRET state (1.2 s), corresponding to a free energy difference of ~ 2.3 kBT, which is far less than the total zippering energy (up to ~ 65 kBT) during the formation of the SNARE complex for membrane fusion59. This huge discrepancy can be explained by the different working mechanisms of the Fzo1/MFNs and the SNARE complex. For Fzo1/MFNs, the relative movement of GD and HD1 is important for the tethering and docking of opposing membranes, and the final fusion step requires the participation of HD2 and probably also TM. Moreover, whereas a single copy of the SNARE complex is theoretically sufficient in catalyzing vesicle fusion, Fzo1/MFNs work in multiple copies to mediate OMM fusion23,37. Thus, the ~ 2.3 kBT energy for the conformational change at the GD-HD1 region of a single Fzo1 molecule is reasonable.

With the results and analyses above, we put forth an amended model for yeast OMM fusion with a key step of LB-mediated post-hydrolysis dimerization and conformational fixation of Fzo1 (Fig. 7). This model helps reconcile the previous controversy on whether GTP hydrolysis by Fzo1 occurs only once or in several rounds during yeast OMM fusion. Though we showed that the addition of hydrolysis-resistant GTPγS was able to render Fzo1IM in the dimeric state with a stable closed conformation, it is plausible that, for Fzo1, GTP acts more like a plug-in to trigger dimerization and domain rearrangement than as fuel for propelling the fast domain movement cycles required by fission dynamins.

Model of Fzo1-mediated OMM fusion.

Illustration of a modified working model of Fzo1-mediated OMM fusion in yeast based on the characterization of the LB. Upon loading of GTP, Fzo1 molecules form trans dimers to tether the opposing mitochondria. During GTP hydrolysis, Fzo1 undergoes an ‘open-to-closed’ conformational change to pull the tethered mitochondria into close proximity and promote membrane docking. Importantly, LBs lock Fzo1 into stable ‘closed’ dimers, even after GTP is hydrolyzed and the G interface is broken up, thereby holding the juxtaposed mitochondria into a docked state and facilitating their merger. Accomplishment of this step may need a large number Fzo1 trans dimers gathered at the contact site of the opposing OMMs, as depicted in an earlier cryo-ET study37. Possibly, the Fzo1 dimers may be degraded via the ubiquitin-proteasome system after the OMM fusion process is completed.

A limitation of this study, and also of our previous studies on MFNs, is the lack of structural information on HD2 and TM. These two domains may contribute to the oligomerization of Fzo1/MFNs and are critical to the final steps of OMM fusion. Furthermore, full-length structures of BDLP and IniA suggest that HD2 folds back to intimately contact the GD in certain nucleotide-loading states34,35. This interdomain contact, which may also exist in Fzo1/MFNs, prevents the dimerization via GDs and HD2 may thus directly affect the process of GTP-hydrolysis-mediated conformational change at the GD-HD1 region. On the other hand, unlike Fzo1/MFNs, IniA and BDLP possess no permanent transmembrane region. The HD2-backfolded conformation likely represents an auto-inhibitory state, which may be released by the loading of GTP and/or the insertion of Paddle/lipid-interacting (LI) loop into the membrane36. To which extent the constantly membrane-anchored Fzo1/MFNs inherit the above features of their bacterial ancestors are not clear. Further structural and biophysical studies on a full-length context are needed to fully reveal the working mechanism of Fzo1/MFNs and how they catalyze OMM fusion.

Methods

Cell lines

Yeast strain JSY2287 (MATa, leu2Δ1, his3Δ200, ura3-52, trp1Δ63, lys2Δ−202, fzo1Δ::HIS3 rho+, pRS416-FZO1) was kindly provided by J.M. Shaw (University of Utah).

Antibodies

The following antibodies were used in this study: DYKDDDDK Tag (D6W5B) Rabbit mAb (14793, CST,1:1000), GAPDH monoclonal antibody (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:1000), Anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (7074, CST, 1:5000), Anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (7076, CST, 1:5000).

Protein expression and purification

The engineered Fzo1 construct (Fzo1IM) containing residues 83–493 and 815–855 (residues 494–814 replaced by an ‘GSGSGSGGS’ linker) was cloned into a modified pET-28 vector. For Fzo1IMLBΔ2 and Fzo1IMLBΔ3, residues 140–154 and residues 140–156 were replaced by an ‘GGSGGGS’ linker and an ‘GSSGSS’ linker, respectively. All mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis. Unless specified, the Fzo1IMLBΔ3 construct was used in all functional experiments. Recombinant Fzo1IM proteins containing an N-terminal His6-tag followed by a cleavage site for PreScission protease (PSP) were expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells (Invitrogen). Transformed bacteria were cultured at 37 °C before induction with 0.1 mM isopropyl-1-thio-β-d-galactopyranoside (IPTG) at an OD600 nm of 0.6 and grown overnight at 16 °C in Terrific Broth medium. Cells expressing Fzo1IM were lysed in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 400 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 30 mM imidazole, 1 μM DNase I, 1 mM phenylmethanesulfonylfluoride (PMSF), and 2.5 mM β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) using a cell disruptor (JNBIO) and subjected to centrifugation at 40,000 × g for 1 h. The supernatant was filtered and applied to a Ni-NTA (first Ni-NTA) column (Cytiva) equilibrated with binding buffer 1 containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 400 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 30 mM imidazole, and 2.5 mM β-ME. After washed with binding buffer 1, proteins were eluted with elution buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 400 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 300 mM imidazole, and 2.5 mM β-ME. Eluted proteins were incubated with 20 μg glutathione S-transferase (GST)-fused PSP to remove the His6-tag and dialyzed overnight against binding buffer 2 containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 400 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, and 2.5 mM β-ME. After dialysis, PSP was removed using a GST column. The protein was re-applied to a second Ni-NTA column equilibrated with binding buffer 2. Binding buffer 1 was used to elute the proteins, which were subsequently loaded onto a Superdex 200 16/60 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with gel filtration buffer containing 20 mM TRIS (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). The proteins eluted in a discrete peak corresponding to a molecular mass of approximately 50 kDa. Cell lysis and protein purification were both performed at 4 °C. The selenomethionine (SeMet) derivative of Fzo1IM was expressed in E. coli Rosetta (DE3) cells in M9 minimal media. We added 100 mg lysine, 100 mg phenylalanine, 100 mg threonine, 50 mg isoleucine, 50 mg leucine, 50 mg valine, and 60 mg SeMet to 1 L of bacterial culture when the OD600 reached 0.5. The cell induction/collection and protein purification protocols for SeMet Fzo1IM were the same as for native Fzo1IM, DTT was used at a 5-fold concentration. To obtain nucleotide-free Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ constructs, 0.8–1 M NaCl was used in the lysis buffer, binding buffer 1/2, and elution buffer instead of 400 mM NaCl in the Ni-NTA affinity purification. For the constructs used for immobilization in the smFRET assays, the PSP cleavage and second Ni-NTA steps were omitted to preserve the His6-Tag. The resulting proteins were desalinated to 150 mM NaCl and then applied to ion exchange chromatography using a RESOURCE Q column (Cytiva) in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) containing 1 mM DTT with an NaCl gradient from 150 mM to 1 M. The nucleotide-free Fzo1IM or Fzo1IMLBΔ constructs were collected from the flowthrough and further purified by SEC using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) containing 1 mM DTT. For proteins used in crystallography, 150 mM KCl and 5 mM MgCl2 were added to the SEC buffer. For proteins used in biochemical and smFRET assays, 150 mM KOAc and 5 mM Mg(OAc)2 were added to the SEC buffer.

Protein crystallization

Fzo1IM or Fzo1IMLBΔ constructs were crystallized via hanging drop vapor diffusion by mixing equal volumes of protein (~ 10 mg ml−1) and reservoir solution. Crystals of GTP-bound SeMet Fzo1IM grew in 0.1 M Bis-Tris (pH 6.5), 0.1 M sodium chloride, and 1.5 M ammonium sulfate. Purified Fzo1IMLBΔ constructs were pre-incubated with the appropriate nucleotides at a 10-fold concentration relative to protein overnight at 4 °C before crystallization. Crystals of GTP-bound Fzo1IMLBΔ2 grew in 0.15 M cesium chloride and 17% PEG3350. Crystals of GDP•BeF3¯-bound Fzo1IMLBΔ3 were obtained from 0.1 M MES (pH 6.5) and 8% PEG6000 in the presence of 10-fold GDP, 10-fold BeSO4, and 100-fold NaF. All crystals grew at 4 °C for roughly 1 week before being flash-frozen in an extra 20% PEG200 or PEG400.

Structure determination

X-ray diffraction datasets were collected at beamlines BL17U, BL02U1, and BL10U2 of the Shanghai Radiation Facility (SSRF)60–62 and processed using the XDS suite63. Initial phases of the GTP-bound SeMet Fzo1IM structure were calculated and refined by single anomalous dispersion (SAD). Structures of Fzo1IMLBΔ2/3 were solved by molecular replacement (MR) with the coordinates of GTP-bound SeMet Fzo1IM as the search model. SAD and MR were carried out using Phaser64. Models were built using COOT65 and refined in Phenix66. Structural validation was carried out using MolProbity67. Structural illustrations were prepared using the PyMOL Molecular Graphic Systems (version 4.6, Schrödinger LLC; http://www.pymol.org/). The area of the G interface was calculated using the PISA server68.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae constructs

For endogenous expression of Fzo1, the coding region plus 500 bp of upstream and downstream sequences of Fzo1 or Fzo1LBΔ, together with an N-terminal 3 × Flag tag, was amplified and inserted into LEU2/CEN plasmid pRS315 at the BamHI/XbalI cloning sites. All recombinant plasmids were verified by DNA sequencing. Because the loss of mtDNA in fzo1Δ yeast is a non-revertible phenotype, yeast strains expressing the indicated Fzo1 constructs were generated through a plasmid shuffle strategy. JSY2287 was transformed with a plasmid encoding FZO1 under LEU selection (pRS315-FZO1). The transformation plates were replica plated, and the pRS416-FZO1 plasmid was removed after counter-selecting three times with 5-fluoroorotic acid.

Mitochondrial morphology assay

For the mitochondrial morphology assay, 1 ml of yeast cells at the exponential phase were collected by centrifugation, washed in PBS buffer, and resuspended in 4% polyformaldehyde (Biosharp, BL539A). After 20 min, cells were washed in PBS and applied to poly-D-lysine-coated (Sigma Aldrich, P1149) microscope culture dishes for incubation for 20 min at room temperature before being visualized under a super-resolution fluorescence microscope (3D-SIM, Nikon) equipped with an SR Apo total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) 100x objective and 488 nm laser lines. GFP N-terminally fused with the pre-sequence of subunit 9 (1‒69) of the F0 ATPase of Neurospora crassa was recombinantly overexpressed using the pFA6a-kanMX6 vector to specifically label yeast mitochondria.

Glycerol growth assay

Yeast strains were grown at 30 °C in YPD medium overnight and serially diluted 1:10 in YPD five times. The dilutions (3.5 μl) were spotted onto plates and grown for 2 days on YPD or 4 days on YPG at 30 °C.

SEC-RALS

A coupled RALS-refractive index detector (Malvern) was connected in line to an analytical gel filtration column (Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL, Cytiva) to determine the absolute molecular masses of the applied protein samples. For each experiment, 25 μM purified protein was incubated with or without the corresponding ligand at 250 μM overnight at 4 °C before being applied to the column equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM KOAc, and 5 mM Mg(OAc)2. Data were analyzed using the provided OMNISEC software. All experiments were repeated at least twice, and the data showed satisfying consistency.

Nucleotide binding assay

The binding affinities of Fzo1IM to guanine nucleotides were measured by a fluorescence-based assay. N-methyl-3’-O-anthranoyl (mant-)GTP or mant-GDP (1 μM) was incubated with increasing concentrations of Fzo1IM in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM KOAc, 5 mM Mg(OAc)2, and 1 mM DTT at 25 °C. The fluorescence intensity (λexc 355 nm, λem 448 nm) was recorded by a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader. GTP hydrolysis assay

GTP hydrolysis assays were performed at 37 °C for MFN1IM/MFN2IM and 30 °C for Fzo1IM as described previously31 in reaction buffers containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5) and 1 mM DTT plus either 150 mM KCl and 5 mM MgCl2 for results shown in Supplementary Figs. 2b and 7a, or 150 mM KOAc and 5 mM Mg(OAc)2 for results shown in Supplementary Fig. 7b and 7d. Reactions were initiated by the addition of protein to the final reaction solution. At different time points, reaction aliquots were 30-fold diluted in HPLC buffer containing 10 mM tetrabutylammonium bromide, 100 mM K2HPO4 (pH 6.5), 100 mM KH2PO4 (pH 6.5), and 7.5% acetonitrile and then quickly transferred into liquid nitrogen. Nucleotides in the samples were separated via a reversed-phase Hypersil ODS-2 C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, Thermo) with HPLC buffer as the running buffer; denatured proteins were blocked by a C18 guard column (Thermo). Nucleotides were detected by absorption at 254 nm and quantified by integration of the corresponding peaks. The GTP hydrolysis rates derived from a linear fit to the initial phase of the reaction (< 40% GTP hydrolyzed) were plotted against the protein concentrations. To measure the stimulated GTP turnover of Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ3, proteins at concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 μM were individually mixed with 2 mM GTP. For other experiments, 20 μM protein and 2 mM GTP were used. All experiments were repeated in triplicate.

Dimerization assay

The dimerization efficiency of Fzo1IM, Fzo1IMLBΔ, and mutations was tested by SEC using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM KOAc, 5 mM Mg(OAc)2, and 1 mM DTT. Before applying the sample to the column, 25 μM purified protein was incubated with or without corresponding nucleotides at a 10-fold concentration relative to protein overnight at 4 °C. To monitor the post-hydrolysis dimerization status, 25 μM protein was incubated with 1 mM GTP at 30 °C overnight for Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ, or 2 h for Fzo1IM(P219G) and Fzo1IMLBΔ(P219G), before being applied in SEC.

Dye labeling

The corresponding Fzo1IM and Fzo1IMLBΔ derivatives were labeled with Cy3- and Cy5-maleimide dyes69 (Cytiva) as donor and acceptor, respectively. For labeling, the protein was incubated with a 5-fold molar excess of respective dye for 2‒3 h at 4 °C in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM KOAc, 5 mM Mg(OAc)2, and 0.5 mM TCEP. The excess free dye was removed using Zeba spin desalting columns (Thermo Scientific).

Coverslip modification for single-molecule fluorescence experiments

The coverslips were placed in staining jars and cleaned with acetone and methanol in an ultrasonic bath for 15 min. After rinsing with deionized water, 1 M sodium hydroxide was added to treat the coverslips for 60 min. The treated coverslips were rinsed and dried thoroughly. A mixed solution of acetic acid, methanol, and (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (95:5:1, volume ratio) was added to the coverslips and incubated for 10 min. After washing and drying in nitrogen airflow, a mixture of mPEG-SVA and biotin-PEG-SVA (Mw: 5000) solution was added on the coverslips and incubated for 120 min before finally rinsing and drying. The coverslips were assembled into flow chambers for subsequent fluorescence imaging.

To make the PEG solution, mPEG-SVA and biotin-PEG-SVA powder were dissolved separately at a concentration of 0.1 mg μl−1 in the solution containing 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate and 0.6 M sodium sulfate. The mPEG-SVA and biotin-PEG-SVA solutions were mixed at a volume ratio of 99:1.

Single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis

The FRET experiments were carried out using TIRF microscopy. The samples were excited by a 532 nm laser beam at a power of 10 mW. The signals were split by a 647 nm dichroic mirror and passed through the bandpass filters, which were 575 ± 25 nm and 690 ± 30 nm, respectively. The two wavelength components were then collected by the electron-multiplying charge-coupled device (EMCCD).

To measure the FRET of Fzo1IMFRET or Fzo1IMLBΔ3FRET monomers, 0.01 mg ml−1 streptavidin was added to the flow chamber and incubated for 10 min. Excess streptavidin was removed by washing with buffer. The biotinylated His6-tag antibody was added at 0.01 mg ml−1 and incubated for 10 min. After washing with buffer, Fzo1IMFRET or Fzo1IMLBΔ3FRET monomers (Cy3 and Cy5-labeled, with N-terminal His6-tag) were added at 10 nM. The excess proteins in solution were removed by buffer, or 100 μM GDP as needed. The dual-channel images were recorded as films at 10 ms resolution until most of the fluorescence spots were photo-bleached.

For the FRET measurement on Fzo1IMFRET or Fzo1IMLBΔ3FRET dimers, 100 μM GTPγS or GDP⋅BeF3−, Cy3 and Cy5-labeled proteins (50 nM, without His6-tag), and non-labeled proteins (50 nM, with N-terminal His6-tag) were mixed and incubated for 30 min, diluted, and then added to the chamber after conjugation of the His6-tag antibody. GTPγS or GDP•BeF3¯ (100 μM) was added to remove the free proteins in solution before recording images. The FRET experiments were performed in a buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM KOAc, and 5 mM Mg(OAc)2.

To increase the photo-stability of the labeled fluorophores, oxygen scavengers were introduced during image recording. The concentrations of the components of the oxygen scavengers were 8 mg ml−1 glucose, 1 mg ml−1 glucose oxidase, 0.4 mg ml−1 catalase, and 0.25 mg ml−1 ( ± )-6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchromane-2-carboxylic acid (trolox).

The images of the Cy3 and Cy5 wavelength channels were aligned according to the positional relationship of the fluorescence spots in the two channels, depending on the instrument configuration. The fluorescence intensity of a single spot was derived from the integration of its fit with the Gaussian function. The colocalized Cy3 and Cy5 intensity profiles were used to calculate the FRET efficiency. FRET states were identified and used to build the histograms and transition density plots (TDPs). The distribution of the durations from the FRET traces were applied to exponential fitting to generate the dwell time data.

Statistics & reproducibility

GraphPad Prism8 was used to perform the statistical analysis and graphical display of data in Fig. 2d. Data presented were representatives of three biological replicates ± standard deviation (S.D.). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses.

The FRET histograms were built from the intensity traces in single-molecule FRET experiments, in which each experiment contains about 10 films. The intensity traces showing FRET signals were used for further analysis. The distinct FRET states were identified from the traces, and the mean value of the FRET efficiency of each state was used to build the frequency histogram.

For dwell time analysis, the duration from the start to the end of each state was calculated and built into a histogram. The number of analyzed films was chosen according to the samples of the minor states (> 100 samples for the minor states, like the low states of GTPγS and GDP•BeF3¯ forms of Fzo1IM, or GTPγS form of Fzo1IMLBΔ3). The histograms were then fitted with a single-exponential function (y = Ae-x/τ) to derive the values of decay constant τ, which represented the dwell times of the states.
