Reorganization of integration and segregation networks in brain-based visual impairment
Authors: Ibai Diez, Carla Troyas, Corinna M. Bauer, Jorge Sepulcre, Lotfi B. Merabet
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Changes in cerebral connectivity associated with visual impairment due to early neurological injury are not fully understood.•Cerebral visual impairment (CVI) is a brain-based visual disorder associated with damage and maldevelopment of visual pathways and areas.•Participants with CVI showed significantly reduced grey matter volume within the primary visual cortex and intraparietal sulcus (IPS).•Participants with CVI exhibited marked reorganization, including increased integration of visual connectivity to somatosensory and multimodal integration areas.•Link-level functional changes in CVI were also associated with key clinical outcomes related to visual function and development.

Introduction

Growing evidence suggests cerebral connectivity changes network organization by altering internally dense (segregation) and externally sparse (integration) modular topology in response to developmental and environmental experience (Cohen and D'Esposito, 2016, Diez and Sepulcre, 2018, Sporns, 2013, Wig, 2017). In the case of congenitally blind individuals, previous neuroimaging studies have demonstrated how the occipital (visual) cortex is functionally recruited to process non-visual information, including tactile and auditory, as well as language and memory ((Abboud and Cohen, 2019, Bedny et al., 2011, Burton et al., 2014, Butt et al., 2013, Deen et al., 2015, Liu et al., 2007, Striem-Amit et al., 2015, Watkins et al., 2012) see (Merabet and Pascual-Leone, 2010) for review). This suggests that the brain undergoes significant reorganization in response to early visual (ocular) deprivation. Resting-state functional connectivity (rsfc) analysis has revealed that congenital ocular blindness is associated with a generalized pattern of lowered functional connectivity between visual and non-visual areas of the brain (e.g. (Guerreiro et al., 2021)). It has been further postulated that multiple areas along the hierarchical axis of the human brain, from primary sensory regions to multisensory and association cortex, can be impacted in this reorganization process (Striem-Amit et al., 2015). Indeed, earlier work by our group (using rsfc analysis) revealed that neuroplastic changes resulting from ocular blindness were most prominent within multimodal and association cortices (Ortiz-Teran et al., 2017, Ortiz-Teran et al., 2016). While studies in the setting of ocular blindness have highlighted the importance of early visual experience with respect to modulating functional connectivity in the human brain, it remains to be determined how cerebral connectivity changes in association with early brain-based visual impairment.

Cerebral visual impairment (CVI) is an umbrella term for a heterogeneous brain-based visual disorder associated with early damage and maldevelopment of retrochiasmal visual processing structures and areas (Dutton and Lueck, 2015, Sakki et al., 2018). CVI is commonly associated with perinatal neurological injury leading to the malfunction of key visual processing pathways (Volpe, 2009), and common causes include hypoxic-ischemic injury, trauma, and infection, as well as genetic and metabolic disorders (Fazzi et al., 2007, Hoyt, 2003). The clinical profile of visual impairments in CVI is broad and can include reduced visual acuity and contrast sensitivity, visual field deficits, and ocular motor abnormalities (e.g., impaired fixation, saccades, and pursuit movements) (Fazzi et al., 2007, Philip and Dutton, 2014). In some individuals, higher-order visual processing deficits may represent their prominent manifestations, such as impaired object recognition, visuospatial processing, and attention. This is particularly evident in individuals who have visual acuity and visual field functioning at normal or near-normal levels (Boot et al., 2010, Dutton, 2013, Dutton et al., 2006). Finally, individuals with CVI often have co-occurring difficulties with learning, language, and visuomotor integration, highlighting the impact of early brain injury and visual impairment on multiple aspects of development, education, and independence (Chokron et al., 2021).

Given that how visual impairment associated with early neurological damage and maldevelopment of central visual pathways impacts cerebral connectivity and network organization remains unknown, we used a multimodal imaging and connectomic approach (based on graph theory analysis (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009)) to characterize network features and investigate potential differences in brain structure and functional connectivity in individuals with CVI compared to normally sighted controls. This strategy included 1) voxel-based morphometry (VBM) to analyze region-specific structural changes at the level of the cortex, 2) weighted degree functional connectivity analysis to evaluate the reorganization of brain circuits, and 3) link-level functional connectivity analysis to detect possible differences in network organization in CVI. Furthermore, given that the clinical profile of CVI is heterogeneous and complex, we also explored potential associations between network reorganization and two clinical outcomes of interest, namely, visual acuity (as an index of baseline visual function) and verbal IQ (as an index of language abilities and general cognitive development).

VBM analysis is useful in investigating focal differences in brain structure, given its ability to quantitatively assess region-specific changes in gray matter volume across cerebral regions (Ashburner and Friston, 2000, Whitwell, 2009). In contrast, rsfc analysis reflects patterns of connectivity derived from low-frequency oscillations from fMRI blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals in the absence of a particular task (Stevens and Spreng, 2014). A major advantage of this approach is the fact that brain network organization and interactions subserving specific functions can be characterized in a task-independent manner, thus avoiding potential confounds associated with differences in inter-individual abilities and performance, such as in task-based fMRI studies (Hallquist and Hillary, 2019). Regarding the coefficients that can be derived from rsfc analysis, weighted degree (or strength) provides valuable information regarding the prominence (or “influence”) of a particular node within a brain network (Sepulcre et al., 2012, Sporns, 2011) by revealing the extent to which a given voxel is functionally connected to other regions. Moreover, it also reveals the level of segregation (i.e., within functional network connectivity) and integration (i.e., intersecting links between distinct brain networks) if modular topology distinctions are considered (Achard et al., 2006, Buckner et al., 2009, Hagmann et al., 2008, Sepulcre et al., 2010). Accordingly, this approach can capture potentially complex changes in network reorganization as they are not expected to be compartmentalized in discrete areas but rather are dispersed hierarchically across multiple brain systems (Sepulcre et al., 2012). Finally, link-level functional connectivity analysis complements these previous approaches by characterizing the strength of specific links between brain regions (Guzman-Velez et al., 2022). Taken together, this combined strategy provides a comprehensive analysis to explore and compare potential structural and functional connectivity changes in the case of CVI, providing insight into how early brain-based visual impairment reshapes the connectomic landscape devoted to the segregation and integration of functional connectivity networks.

Methods

Study participants

Twelve individuals previously diagnosed with CVI (6 females, mean age 19.08 years ± 5.42 SD) and eighteen normally sighted controls with neurotypical development (12 females, mean age 22.55 years ± 4.73 SD) were recruited. A comparison of demographic factors revealed no statistically significant group differences with respect to age or sex (both p > 0.05). Demographic and clinical information regarding the CVI participants can be found in Table 1. Best corrected binocular visual acuity of the CVI participants ranged from 20/15 to 20/70 Snellen (−0.12 to 0.54 LogMAR equivalent), with the majority (8 out of 12 or 66.67 %) being within normal/near normal range. Four out of the 12 (or 33.33 %) were born prematurely (before 37 weeks). The mean Verbal IQ score for the CVI sample was 100.09 ± 29.83, with one participant considered below average (i.e., <70). Further details regarding the diagnosis and clinical assessment of CVI participants can be found in the supplemental materials section. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from all participants and a parent/legal guardian (in the case of a minor). The study was approved by the Investigative Review Board at the Massachusetts Eye and Ear in Boston, MA, USA, and carried out in accordance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) for experiments involving humans.

MRI data acquisition

Imaging data was acquired using a 3T Philips Ingenia Elition X scanner and a 32-channel phased-array head coil. For each subject, a high-resolution 3D T1-weighted magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo (MPRAGE) sequence was acquired with the following parameters: 1 mm isotropic voxels, 170 sagittal slices, acquisition matrix size = 240 × 240, repetition time (TR) = 6.5 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.9 ms. In addition, two 5 min, 15 sec whole-brain resting state scans were obtained with the following parameters: total number of volumes = 766, TE = 30 ms, and TR = 800 ms. Participants were instructed to lie still, stay awake, and “let their mind wander” while they fixated on a white dot on a black screen with dim ambient lighting. Head motion was restricted using of bi-temporal foam and medical tape placed across the chin. To attenuate scanner noise, sound-dampening over auricular headphones and earplugs were worn by all participants.

MRI preprocessing

Pre-processing was conducted using FMRIB Software Library v6.0.4 (FSL) and custom scripts in MATLAB (version 2017a). The anatomical T1 preprocessing pipeline included reorientation to right-posterior-inferior (RPI), alignment to anterior and posterior commissures, skull stripping, gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid segmentation, and computation of non-linear transformation between individual skull-stripped T1 and 2 mm resolution MNI 152 template images. The functional MRI preprocessing included slice time correction, reorientation to RPI, realigning functional volumes within runs with a rigid body transformation (6-parameter linear transformation), computation of the transformation between individual skull-stripped T1 and mean functional images, intensity normalization, removal of confounding factors from the data using linear regression including 12 motion-related covariates (rigid motion parameters and its derivatives), linear and quadratic terms, and five components each from the lateral ventricles and white matter. Global signal regression was not applied to prevent the introduction of artefactual correlations that were not originally present in the data (Douaud et al., 2007, Good et al., 2001, Smith et al., 2004). We also transformed resting-state data to MNI space (3 mm), which involved concatenating the transformation from functional to structural and from structural to MNI space. In addition, we performed spatial smoothing with an isotropic Gaussian kernel of 6 mm FWHM and band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz). Head motion was quantified using realignment parameters obtained during image preprocessing, which included 3 translation and 3 rotation estimates. All time points with a Jenkinson frame displacement greater than 0.5 mm were eliminated. The two resting-state runs were concatenated, and only subjects with at least 700 time points not exceeding the scrubbing head motion correction threshold were used in the analyses. Based on these criteria, 2 CVI and 1 sighted control subject were excluded from further analysis. For the later computation of the connectivity matrices, an equal number of time points (i.e., the first n = 700 time points not exceeding the motion threshold) per subject were selected. Inspecting the distributions of the correlations for possible noise contamination across all-time series and all subjects revealed no outliers.

Voxel-based morphometry analysis

For the study of voxel-based grey matter volume, the FSL Voxel-Based Morphometry (VMB) tool (Douaud et al., 2007, Good et al., 2001, Smith et al., 2004) was used based on acquired T1 scans. Previously derived individual subject grey matter partial volume estimates were registered to MNI 152 standard space using non-linear registration. Subsequently, the resulting images were averaged and flipped along the x-axis to create a left–right symmetric, study-specific grey matter template. Then, all native grey matter images were non-linearly registered to the study-specific template and modulated to correct for local expansion or contraction due to the non-linear component of the spatial transformation. Finally, an isotropic Gaussian kernel was used to smooth grey matter images with a σ = 3.

Between-group statistical analysis was performed using a mass univariate two-sample t-test using a general linear model (GLM). The comparison was adjusted for age and intracranial volume. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using a cluster-wise Monte Carlo simulation. This was performed using the 3dClustSim tool (AFNI software; https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/) with 10,000 iterations to evaluate the minimum cluster size required for a cluster to survive with an α = 0.05 and p value < 0.05. Clusters were defined as contiguous voxel groups. Data was also displayed as boxplots for the identified significant clusters. This allowed for the examination and detection of potential outliers or influential participants.

Weighted degree functional connectivity analysis (integration and segregation networks)

For each subject, voxel-level integration and segregation weighted degree values were computed (Guzman-Velez et al., 2022). First, 3 mm voxel-level functional connectivity matrices were determined for each subject. These were based on Pearson correlation coefficients for the time series of all pairs of cortical-subcortical gray matter voxels. A 36,098 voxels × 36,098 voxels association matrix was obtained for each participant, and negative values were removed. A 7-networks functional parcellation map (Yeo et al., 2011) was employed to classify connections as segregated or integrated. Segregated connections start and end at the same functional network, while integrated connections start and end at different networks. As with the VBM analysis, between-group statistical analysis was performed using a mass univariate two-sample-t-test using a GLM adjusting for age. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using cluster-wise Monte Carlo correction (3dClustSim tool; https://afni.nimh.nih.gov/; 10,000 iterations; alpha = 0.05 and p value < 0.05).

Link-level functional connectivity analysis

To deal with the computational and statistical demands of link-level functional connectivity analysis, data were down-sampled to 6 mm to perform voxel-level analyses. After down sampling the data, we recomputed the functional connectivity matrix using a Pearson correlation. A 5,237 voxel × 5,237 voxel matrix was used for subsequent analyses. As statistical analyses were performed at the link level, no threshold value was applied. We used a link-level whole-brain correction for multiple comparisons (adapting the Monte Carlo simulation method to link-level analysis) with clusters defined as groups of links instead of voxels (Diez et al., 2021). Ten thousand random networks were generated with the same smoothing properties as the obtained residuals, and only clusters whose size exceeded that obtained for a random network (with a two-tailed p value < 0.05) remained. Clusters were defined as links that connected contiguous voxel groups. As with the weighted degree analysis, 7-network functional parcellation allowed for the links to be classified as segregated or integrated based on whether they were connected to within- or between-network areas, respectively. For each of the seven resting-state networks, we quantified the proportion of the significant segregated and integrated connections among all the links surviving multiple comparisons.

Associations between link-level functional connectivity and clinical profile in CVI

We used two outcomes of interest to investigate putative associations between link-level functional connectivity and the clinical profile of the CVI group. First, best-corrected binocular visual acuity (LogMAR equivalent) served as an index of visual functioning (note that a higher LogMAR value is indicative of a lower visual acuity function). Second, verbal IQ score was used as an index for spoken language capabilities and overall level of development (see Supplemental materials section for further details regarding assessment).

The link-level connectivity strength values across the different brain areas were drawn from the previously computed correlation matrices. Using a GLM, we computed the association between link-level connectivity strength values with individual visual acuity and verbal IQ scores while adjusting for age. We used a link-level whole-brain correction for multiple comparisons (adapting the Monte Carlo simulation method to link-level analysis) with clusters defined as groups of links instead of voxels (Diez et al., 2021). Ten thousand random networks were generated with the same smoothing properties as the obtained residuals, and only clusters whose size exceeded that obtained for a random network with a two-tailed p value < 0.05 remained. Clusters were defined as links that connected contiguous voxel groups.

We investigated integration and segregation changes between the CVI and control groups (Fig. 2). This analysis revealed a significant increase in cortical integration connectivity in the CVI group, primarily localized within cortical areas devoted to multimodal/multisensory integration processing. Moreover, we observed that CVI subjects showed increased segregation in the frontal pole/superior frontal gyrus and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) areas, regions mainly associated with the default mode network (DMN). Link-level functional connectivity analysis

To complete our characterization of the underlying reorganization of functional connectivity, we performed a complementary analysis that detected the individual connections that significantly differed between CVI and control groups. First, we obtained all brain links displaying statistically significant differences between the CVI and control groups. We then plotted the target-source areas of increased and decreased links in the cortex and computed the within- and between-network link correspondence into 7 networks (using the Yeo et al. 2011 parcellation map) (Fig. 3). Of all the connections showing statistically significant differences between the CVI and control group, the majority (88 %) displayed an increase in connectivity strength (Fig. 3A). In comparison, only 12 % showed a decrease in connectivity strength Fig. 3B). In addition, most of the difference in connectivity strength between groups was between-network rather than within-network connections. Specifically, 84 % of the links showing a significant increase in strength for the CVI group connected two distinct networks. Of these, the greatest proportion was for connections linking the sensory-motor network with other networks, such as the medial visual network (10 %), limbic network (12 %), and ventral attention network (VAN) (9 %). Enhanced connectivity was also found between the medial visual and dorsal attention network (DAN) (9 %). The other 16 % of links showing an increase in strength in the CVI group were located within networks, with the largest percentage of these connections associated with the limbic (26 %) and the medial visual (30 %) networks. Similarly, most links with decreased connectivity in the CVI group were associated with between-network connections. Specifically, links connecting the medial visual network to other brain networks, such as the DMN (26 %), the limbic network (23 %), and the VAN (16 %) (Fig. 3B). It is worth noting that link-level analysis offers a distinct advantage over other approaches by revealing more granular reorganization patterns, even with overlapping results. Our analysis revealed that worsening binocular visual acuity (i.e., increasing LogMAR values) was positively correlated with distributed areas in PCC and primary motor areas, medial occipital region, right superior temporal sulcus, and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Visual acuity was negatively associated with somatosensory cortex, IPS, and dorsal ACC connectivity strength. Regarding verbal IQ, a positive correlation was found with link-level functional connectivity strength within the frontal/parietal operculum, insula, superior parietal lobule/posterior cingulate gyrus, somatosensory cortex, and left lateral occipital cortex. Discussion

Using multimodal imaging and graph theory-based connectomic analysis, we investigated region-specific alterations in grey matter volume and functional connectivity (including weighted degree and link-level analyses) associated with early brain-based visual impairment (specifically, in individuals with CVI). To our knowledge, this study represents the first reported characterization of changes in cortical morphometry and functional network topology in this clinical population. Furthermore, our results contribute to the growing literature (beyond studies from ocular blind subjects) highlighting the importance of early visual experience with respect to shaping the structure and functional connectivity of the human brain.

At a structural level, VBM analysis revealed that CVI subjects had significantly reduced grey matter volume within primary visual cortex and intraparietal sulcus (IPS) compared to normally sighted controls. In contrast, increases in grey matter volume were found in the frontal medial and insular cortical areas. Resting-state functional connectivity analysis revealed that CVI subjects also displayed patterns of aberrant segregation and integration within multisensory and default mode regions. Increased integration was observed in cortical areas devoted to multimodal processing (frontal/parietal operculum, dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, sensorimotor cortex, and temporal parietal junction). In contrast, increased segregation was observed in the frontal pole/superior frontal gyrus and posterior cingulate cortex areas related to the default mode network. Increases in both integration and segregation were seen in the right temporal pole and the posterior cingulate gyrus. While weighted degree analysis revealed a mixed pattern of increased integration and segregation, linked-level analysis showed that overall, the CVI group had greater differences in between-network connectivity strength than within-network connectivity strength. Specifically, enhanced functional connectivity was observed between primary and multimodal integration regions (e.g. between primary visual and somatosensory systems). Finally, we also observed that link-level functional connectivity changes were associated with clinical outcomes in CVI. Specifically, worsening binocular visual acuity (i.e., increasing LogMAR values) was positively correlated with posterior cingulate cortex, primary motor areas, medial occipital region, right superior temporal sulcus, and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and negatively correlated with somatosensory cortex, IPS, and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex connectivity strength. In contrast, higher verbal IQ scores were positively correlated with the frontal/parietal operculum, insula, superior parietal lobule/posterior cingulate gyrus, somatosensory cortex, and left lateral occipital cortex connectivity strength and negatively correlated in medial visual, inferior parietal regions, supramarginal and superior temporal regions.

Several studies have been carried out to characterize structural and functional connectivity changes in the setting of ocular-based visual impairment and blindness. Here, we propose that using novel connectomic approaches, investigating cortical volume and functional network changes in CVI can serve as a unique model to help uncover neuroplastic changes following early brain injury or maldevelopment of retrochiasmal visual processing structures and areas.

VBM analysis has previously been used to characterize widespread morphological changes in the case of ocular visual impairment and blindness. Specifically, a number of studies have reported that profound ocular blindness is associated with significant increases in cortical thickness (e.g. (Anurova et al., 2015, Bridge et al., 2009, Jiang et al., 2009, Park et al., 2009, Voss and Zatorre, 2012)) as well as reductions in grey matter volume within the occipital (i.e., calcarine) cortex (e.g., (Bridge et al., 2009, Lepore et al., 2010, Noppeney, 2007, Voss et al., 2014)) and areas implicated with higher-order visual processing when compared to normally sighted controls (Ptito et al., 2008). Furthermore, these changes appear to be related to the duration of blindness (Maller et al., 2016). At the same time, it is important to note that premature birth can be associated with certain causes of blindness (such as retinopathy of prematurity), and prematurity itself has also been associated with widespread structural changes in grey matter volume beyond the effect of ocular blindness (Wang et al., 2015). The comparative loss of grey matter volume observed in our CVI subjects within the occipital cortex and IPS would suggest atrophy within these areas implicated in early and higher-order stages of visual processing, respectively (Grefkes and Fink, 2005). Indeed, although most of our participants had binocular corrected visual acuity at or near normal levels, there was a positive association between increased LogMAR (i.e., worse acuity) and greater functional connectivity of the medial occipital cortex and lower connectivity of the IPS. This suggests that both volume reductions and aberrant functional connectivity of early visual regions may have differential effects on low- and high-order visual processing. Additional analyses in the future will help disentangle these potential contributions to impaired visual information processing and visuomotor integration, which are commonly present as part of the clinical profile of CVI (Fazzi et al., 2007). Comparatively speaking, the significance of observed increases in cortical grey matter volume within the frontal medial and insular cortices (areas implicated in social cognition (Amodio and Frith, 2006) and sensorimotor processing (Kortz and Lillehei, 2024), respectively) is unclear. While the precise neurobiological mechanism underlying these increases is unknown, one possibility is an increase in neuronal density in the supragranular layers that are implicated in cortico-cortical connections (Galakhova et al., 2022, Medalla and Barbas, 2006). Indeed, we also observed increased segregation (i.e., within network connectivity) in the left frontal cortex, which would support this hypothesis. Finally, it is possible that prematurity (relating to 4 out 12 of our participants; see also Supplemental Fig. 1) and possible concomitant ocular conditions (Smyser et al., 2019) may have also contributed to observed group differences in volume and connectivity.

Previous rsfc studies characterizing connectivity patterns following neonatal brain injury and prematurity (see (Smyser et al., 2019) and (Vo Van et al., 2022) for reviews on these topics) highlight the deleterious effects that early neurological injury and prematurity can have on brain network organization and maturation. Specifically, network development is generally characterized by an increasing transition from a local/segregated to an integrated topology during development, which can be disrupted by the presence of diffuse or widespread injury ((Smyser et al., 2019, Vo Van et al., 2022) see also (Cao et al., 2017) for further discussion). Developing neural networks are not only susceptible to injury, but also to the absence of sensory information. For instance, in the case of early onset and profound ocular blindness, rsfc analyses have revealed a generalized pattern of lowered functional connectivity between visual and non-visual areas of the brain compared to normally sighted controls (but see (Guerreiro et al., 2021) for a discussion on the effects of study design on observed changes in functional connectivity). For example, early work by Burton and colleagues (2014) demonstrated that early blind individuals showed reduced functional connectivity between visual and non-visual sensory cortical areas and markedly greater functional connectivity between the occipital visual cortex and cortical areas associated with memory and attention (Burton et al., 2014). Work by our group has also revealed that these neuroplastic changes in early ocular blindness were most prominent within multimodal and association cortices (Ortiz-Teran et al., 2017, Ortiz-Teran et al., 2016). A recent study by Tian et al. 2024 also highlights how visual experience influences the development of functional connectivity networks between visual and non-visual cortical areas of the brain. Specifically, by comparing rsfc in infants (average age from birth of 2.79 weeks) with congenital ocular blindness (mean age 44.23 years) and normally sighted adults (mean age 35.33 years), these authors found that extrastriate visual cortical areas in infants were more functionally similar to blind adults than normally sighted (blindfolded) adults, providing support for the crucial role of visual experience in establishing long-range (i.e. occipito-frontal) functional connectivity in the adult brain (Tian et al., 2024). By comparison, how networks develop in individuals with visual impairments as a result of early brain injury has been less well studied.

An emerging theme from recent rsfc studies has been the importance of the dynamic interplay between network segregation and integration across multiple specialized areas of the brain that support effective brain function (see (Sporns, 2013) for review). As mentioned previously, rsfc analysis can be helpful in determining the extent to which a given area of the brain is segregated (i.e., functionally connected to other regions of the same functional network) and integrated (i.e., participates in intersecting links between distinct brain networks (Achard et al., 2006, Buckner et al., 2009, Hagmann et al., 2008, Sepulcre et al., 2010)). Accumulating evidence demonstrates that network segregation changes across the lifespan and in relation to cognitive abilities and behaviors, and accordingly, can serve as an important biomarker for the integrity of large-scale brain network organization and function on a global scale (Cohen and D'Esposito, 2016, Wig, 2017). Put simply, maintaining a balance between segregation and integration (i.e., distinct yet interactive) brain systems is considered crucial, and any perturbations (e.g., through injury or disease) can be detrimental to information processing and global communication in the brain (Wig, 2017). Viewed in this context, the altered segregation and integration connectivity patterns we observed in our CVI group highlight the importance of this interplay and demonstrate the impact early injury and maldevelopment have in shaping these brain networks.

The large-scale alterations in functional connectivity in CVI observed in this study also mirror evidence of dramatic reductions in the structural integrity of key white matter pathways implicated in visual processing in this population. Using diffusion-based imaging and white matter tractography, previous work by our group and others has shown that white matter integrity is compromised along the ventral (inferior longitudinal fasciculus; ILF (Ortibus et al., 2012)) and dorsal (superior longitudinal fasciculus; SLF (Bauer et al., 2014) visual processing streams, as well as pathways implicated in multisensory and semantic language processing (inferior-fronto-occipital fasciculus; IFOF (Bauer and Merabet, 2024)). While it is important to realize that functional connectivity represents statistical associations as opposed to physical, structural connections between brain regions (Wig, 2017), these underlying changes in anatomical architecture resulting from early neurological injury and maldevelopment are likely to contribute to the widespread alterations observed in the functional reorganization of the brain in CVI.

While evidence of overall decreases in functional connectivity in CVI may be expected in the context of early neurological injury, the significance of observed (seemingly paradoxical) increases in connectivity is less clear. One interpretation is that enhanced (i.e., “hyper”) connectivity following brain injury represents a short-term adaptive mechanism to sustain network communication efficiency through network hubs corresponding to brain regions that are the most central and metabolically efficient, thereby mitigating potential behavioral deficits and neurometabolic demands (Hillary and Grafman, 2017). However, chronic hyperconnectivity can result in network vulnerability and lead to secondary pathological processes over time (Hillary and Grafman, 2017). Because our participants with CVI all had their initial brain insult at least 14 years before the time of scanning, it is possible that these observed patterns of increased functional connectivity are in the chronic stage and associated with secondary pathological processes rather than compensatory behavioral processes (as is commonly assumed). In other words, this potentially sustained increase in functional connectivity may reflect network reorganization underlying impaired visual perceptual functions that require communication integrated over distant brain regions.

Finally, it is important to recognize several study limitations. Notably, our initial conclusions should be interpreted with caution, given the relatively small sample size. The inclusion/exclusion criteria outlined for participation in this study likely bring about a certain level of selection bias (e.g., level of cognitive functioning and ability to lie still in the scanner for prolonged periods without sedation). The etiological heterogeneity and varying severity of CVI (including the effect of prematurity) likely contribute a high degree of inter-individual variability that further impacts the internal validity and external generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, this study was focused on characterizing putative differences in cortical network topology. However, it is important to recognize the potential interplay with aberrant development of subcortical and brainstem structures (e.g., thalamus, globus pallidus, superior colliculus) and white matter integrity (e.g. (Bauer and Merabet, 2024)). Finally, while rsfc-based analyses offer advantages by avoiding potential confounds associated with inter-individual differences in task performance and abilities (such as in task-based fMRI studies, (Hallquist and Hillary, 2019)), the danger remains of overinterpreting results based on reverse inference alone. Further studies are needed in larger samples (controlling for potential confounding effects such as prematurity, etiology, level of visual function as well as other developmental factors) and employing multimodal imaging approaches combined with broader clinical and behavioral data to uncover the complex interplay between the broad clinical profile of CVI and underlying structural and functional changes occurring in the brain.

Conclusion

These findings complement our understanding of how CVI (i.e., brain-based visual impairment due to early neurological injury and maldevelopment of retrochiasmal visual processing structures) is associated with alterations in the structural and functional architecture of the human brain. This multimodal imaging approach using connectomic analysis has the potential to provide a better understanding of the complex reorganization and plasticity of large-scale networks in CVI. While CVI is seen primarily as a visual impairment, the observation of broad network changes impacting visual and nonvisual areas helps to develop a better appreciation of the complex neurobiological basis of the full neurodevelopmental profile associated with this condition.
