A paradigm shift for health equity in implementation science
Authors: Dara Gordon, Simone Shahid, Shivani Chandra, Sumaya Mehelay, Ibukun-Oluwa Omolade Abejirinde, Nancy Clark, Bernadette Zakher, Bisma Imtiaz, Carolyn Steele Gray, Rosanra Yoon, James Shaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch and theory in the field of implementation science (IS) are increasingly prioritizing health equity. Although this shift is an important development for the field, IS has not yet systematically engaged with theories and methods of critical social science that help to clarify the challenges and opportunities of efforts to improve health equity. In our paper, we present a summary of recent contributions to dialogue on health equity in the field of IS and highlight the value of critical social science theory to advance research and action for social justice and equity in implementation research and practice.MethodsWe conducted a targeted literature review using citation tracking on Google Scholar. We purposefully selected 10 articles and inductively identified three categories of relevant articles within the literature that represent three distinct approaches to addressing gaps in health equity for IS research and practice.ResultsThe first category brings attention to the importance of health equity in IS but remains strongly situated in the commonly accepted, mostly positivist methods and concepts of IS. The second set of articles broadens the perspective on structural factors relevant to health equity in IS, encouraging innovations of focus that hold greater potential to impact upon the social determinants of health. The third and final category includes articles that center concepts from the critical social sciences, such as intersectionality, to interrogate taken-for-granted beliefs in the field of IS, aiming to advance IS through social justice.ConclusionsWe propose a learning pathway for IS researchers, practitioners, and educators that progresses from acknowledging the need for explicit emphasis on health equity in IS toward the integration of critical social theory to advance health equity for population groups marginalized by differential axes of inequities such as class, gender, ethnicity/race, (dis)abilities and others. Such a learning pathway points toward a paradigm shift, inviting deeper critical reflection about the roles that power and oppression play in shaping IS in research and practice. This paradigm shift would lead to a stronger emphasis in IS on mitigating systemic oppression and marginalization of population groups most affected by health and social inequalities and healthcare inequities.Keywords: Implementation science, Health equity, Power, Oppression, Social science

Introduction and background

Research and theory in the field of implementation science (IS) are increasingly incorporating health equity as a priority aim [1, 2]. This shift toward health equity is a promising development in the field, demonstrating critical reflection on the aims of IS as arising from a history that is biomedically oriented and that has not fully explored the complexity of the ways that social forces influence health and wellbeing [3]. As a field, IS aims explicitly to improve healthcare through supporting the adoption of evidence-based practice; however, with a scientific legacy that has tended to marginalize perspectives that depart from the dominant way of producing that evidence [4], the perspectives, needs, and experiences of marginalized communities have not been central to IS work [5]. In part because of this history, IS has been slow to engage with concepts from the critical social sciences that would otherwise clarify the processes and projects that stand to have the most positive impacts upon health equity. In our paper, we aim to summarize recent contributions to dialogue about health equity in the field of IS. This paper highlights the value of continuing to pursue deeper thinking with theories from the critical social sciences to strengthen action for social justice in implementation research and practice.

Implementation science is a scientific approach to understanding which strategies are most effective at introducing and sustaining evidence-based best practices in health care delivery and policy [6, 7]. In this way, IS attends to both (a) approaches to enhancing implementation, and (b) examining whether these approaches enable best practices to be implemented and sustained as intended. In efforts to address the health-related exclusions and harms experienced by structurally marginalized communities, implementation scientists have begun to focus on approaches to implementation research and practice that explicitly promote health equity. Health equity has been defined as, “the state in which everyone has a fair opportunity to attain their full health potential” [8], p. 433), and is shaped by systems of power and oppression that influence that nature of that opportunity. However, many of these emerging IS approaches are not grounded in social science concepts, like power and systemic oppression, that social justice scholars see as critical to understanding the root causes of health inequity [9–12]. Theory from critical social sciences clarifies how structures of power and oppression, including white supremacy, heterosexism, sexism, racism, ableism, classism and ageism operate in confluence at institutional, interpersonal and internalized levels [10, 13, 14]. Such systems of oppression are accelerated by economic and political systems, namely neoliberalism and capitalism, which prioritize market-based approaches and decreased social spending, and result in health inequities and marginalization [15]. Literature on health equity that incorporates these perspectives illustrates multiple roles of these systems of power and oppression in reinforcing health inequities and posing major challenges to efforts to achieve health equity [16, 17].

We view the world as animated by systems of power and oppression that generate determinants of health that are social, political, and commercial. Within the world of these determinants exist systems of health care and public health. We see that IS literature addressing health equity has often addressed systems of health care and public health, at times addressed the broader determinants of health, and has less frequently addressed the systems of power and oppression in which they are situated. In this paper, we aim to explore the way that IS literature has engaged with these different approaches to health equity and comment on implication for the field.

In their 2021 article, Snell-Rood et al. (2021) presented an important commentary about the lack of social science concepts such as power, inequality, and reflexivity in most IS theories, models and frameworks (TMF). They propose three broad areas of social science theory that are relevant to equity-oriented IS research and practice. These include: (1) reflexivity about colonialism, which foregrounds attention to the role of power in knowledge production and the ways in which research and interventions might perpetuate inequalities; (2) theory about intersecting structures of oppression, which explain the unequal burden of health disparities and encourage researchers to consider upstream causes of vulnerability; and (3) theories of power and governance, which encourage researchers to examine the social-political forces of the macro-level context crucial for implementation and sustainability [3]. We suggest that engagement with these and other concepts from critical social science is fundamental to effective implementation science that aims to enhance health equity. Inspired by the call for deeper engagement with critical social science theories from Snell-Rood et al. (2021), we critically examine equity-focused contributions to IS and call for stronger engagement with critical social theory to advance health equity in IS research and practice.

Methodology

Our research team is actively exploring a variety of approaches to equity-focused implementation science across several projects, focused especially on equity-enhancing practices in primary and community-based care. Inspired by our shared effort to apply concepts from critical social science to our implementation research projects, and based on our strong collective knowledge of equity in IS, we purposively selected 10 published articles that represent a breadth of contributions related to IS for health equity. Two members of our team, a doctoral student (XX) and Associate Professor (XX), led a targeted search process and undertook each of the following steps described. Responding to advocacy in the literature to avoid “empty, redundant, or pointless” systematic reviews of the literature [18], our intent was not to comprehensively summarize this literature. Such a comprehensive summary has already been partially accomplished, in which Gustafson et al. (2023) sought to describe the literature employing TMF to address ethnicity-related health inequities [19]. Instead, our aim was to critically examine (a) the primary recommendations and (b) underlying premises of IS literature that aims to strengthen the role of IS in advancing health equity. To do so, we identified exemplar articles in different categories of contributions to better understand the breadth of approaches to these issues in the field. In so doing, we sought to bring a critical social science perspective to bear on the IS literature, exploring whether and how core ideas from critical social theory might be used to enhance the rigor and utility of IS for health equity.

We identified the 10 articles of focus in our paper using citation tracking and a targeted search using Google Scholar. Beginning with the seminal contribution of Woodward et al. (2019) [20], we used citation tracking to identify a subset of papers aiming to make conceptual contributions to health equity in implementation science. We chose to begin with Woodward et al. (2019) because it is the first widely cited article in implementation science that explicitly proposed an equity-focused framework for the field. We completed the citation tracking process in two rounds. First, we assessed the titles and abstracts of the articles citing Woodward et al. (2019) on Google Scholar. We first searched these citations in January 2024, and checked again in October 2024 to see whether any new articles had been published during our analysis period. We identified one additional article in October 2024 for a total of 285 articles. At both time points we supplemented the citation tracking of Woodward et al. (2019) by searching the terms [“implementation science” AND (“health equity” OR “health inequities” OR “health disparities”], and reviewed titles of the first five pages of results, which offered no unique articles from our citation tracking process.

We selected as possible inclusions those articles that sought to make conceptual contributions to IS and health equity based on title and abstract, and then narrowed our working sample to those most directly aiming to advance the concepts and TMF on this topic (a total of 24 articles). We deemed as most directly relevant to our purposes those articles that explicitly sought to advance, add to, or propose TMFs in IS from the perspective of health equity. In this way, we were more interested in theory-focused and commentary articles than on empirical research articles. We then reviewed the articles that had cited these 24 articles and reviewed the reference lists of the 24 articles to further expand our total sample of potential articles, including only those aiming to make a similarly focused contribution. We ended up with a sample of 28 articles.

Next, we organized the articles into three categories that were inductively defined based on the way that health equity was described in the articles and the recommendations provided regarding how IS could best advance health equity. The same two members of the team read each included article with the intent to understand (a) the ways that health equity was conceptualized, (b) whether concepts from critical social science were present in the article, and (c) what the authors described as the main intent. The categories were then developed in an iterative process in which these two authors discussed the papers, the nature of the contributions they were making, and they ways in which they approached health equity. The categories were designed to represent the quality and conceptual depth of health equity and related ideas in each paper, based on the notion that increasing conceptual depth could be viewed as a spectrum.

The three categories we inductively developed were: (1) articles that encourage attention to topics related to health equity but do not aim to examine or address their underlying causes, (2) articles on IS that call for a structural-theoretical understanding of health equity, meaning that they draw on theory to point to features of social structure that shape health inequities, and (3) articles that engage in a deeper, critically reflexive inquiry into IS to generate advances in theoretically informed approaches to equity in IS. There were twelve papers in Category 1, eight papers in Category 2, and eight papers in Category 3 (See Table 1 for further details). From these three categories, we selected 10 exemplars to summarize in detail and profile in our analysis. We selected these 10 archetype papers by identifying those papers that represented clear engagement with core concepts related to that category, and were also highly cited. We selected three articles in each of the first two categories, and identified high consistency across the three articles included within each category. We chose to include four articles in the final category because of the diversity of ways in which these articles applied critical social theory to advance thought and practice in IS, which is a central motivation of our analysis. In our results section, we present a synthesis of insights regarding how articles framed their objectives, their core concepts, and the definition of health equity or inequity on which their effort to build theoretical knowledge in IS was based.

Reflexivity

We are a multidisciplinary group of researchers who represent a range of career stages, and we embody a multitude of diverse identities and lived experiences. Our social identities include settlers with ancestry from Europe, East Asia, and South Asia, newcomers from Europe and Africa, histories and lived experiences of being low-income and experiencing poverty, chronic illness, and caregiving responsibilities, with religious affiliations of Jewish, Muslim, Christian, and Hindu. We share our positionalities to offer insight into our social position as it relates to intersecting identities that embody privilege and/or oppression. While some of us identify with structurally marginalized groups, we acknowledge that as researchers who work in the fields of academia and healthcare, we are critiquing a space that has over time afforded us significant privilege and power. Central to these juxtapositions is our individual and collective commitment to advancing work that addresses structural oppression within our spheres of influence.

Results

We identified three categories of papers that make conceptual advances in the ways that IS TMF address health equity. Category 1 calls for explicit attention to health equity in implementation science, identifying that IS as a field has not historically centered the objective of enhancing health equity. The second category raises attention to the impact of broader social structures on health equity as a core consideration for IS. The third and final category of papers engages with critical social theory to strengthen the conceptual and practical resources of IS to support health equity through social justice. We describe each category in detail below, accompanied by Table 2.

3. Theories of power and governance.

The authors describe their conceptualization of health inequities as they describe the role of critical social theories:

“These theories advance the goal of health equity by assessing the structural factors that shape our research, the health and service systems in which we apply our insights, and the lives of the people meant to benefit from interventions. These structural factors explain not only what makes implementation successful but also why some people are so much sicker than others and why barriers to evidence-based intervention may be greater in certain delivery settings.”

This paper seeks to clarify the ways in which implementation science may sustain White supremacist ideas in community mental health. The paper explored the questions: What information is valued and considered evidence? How do power differentials within implementation research and practice manifest?

They sought to illustrate how a White supremacist structure undermines efforts to employ IS to enhance health equity.

Category 1: Calling for explicit attention to health equity in implementation science

The papers in this category were situated within a post-positivist epistemology that focused perspectives on a narrow set of causes of health inequities (e.g., educational and economic inequalities) and the ways that IS might enhance health equity. With respect to core concepts, papers in this category tended to focus on health care as opposed to health or wellbeing more generally and lacked strong criticism of accepted concepts and practices in IS. These papers had a propensity to define health inequities specifically in relation to inequities in access to or outcomes of health care services. Woodward et al. (2019) specifically refer to “health care disparities” and Baumann and Cabassa (2020) refer to “healthcare inequities” [21], linked to their focus on the quality and nature of services offered by healthcare organizations. Although Pyra et al. (2022) define health equity as “a state wherein everyone has a fair and just opportunity to be as healthy as possible” [22], the content of their article focused specifically on implementing accessible health care treatments for people living with HIV.

The frameworks in this first category resonate strongly with existing TMFs in IS, drawing attention to specific, measurable efforts to orient IS toward enhancing equity in health services. For example, Woodward et al. (2019) provided a compelling case for the importance of a multi-level framework to consider implementation issues that arise in health equity initiatives at the levels of patients, providers, the clinical encounter, and the healthcare system. They suggested that “attending to multilevel factors is essential to promote implementation because the uptake of treatments is greatly affected by multiple variables in complex healthcare systems” [20]. Baumann and Cabassa (2020) similarly acknowledge the importance of the “ecology” of the health care system [21] yet build on the relatively narrow framing of Proctor et al.’s (2009) implementation framework (i.e., with a narrow focus on a specific set of implementation activities and outcomes), “because of its simplicity, its clear depiction of distinct and inter-related processes and outcomes, and because it is used to inform the design and evaluation of implementation studies” p. 2). Papers in this category tended to acknowledge the broader set of influences that lead to healthcare disparities (i.e., the social determinants of health), but did not describe how power operates to mitigate health inequities and outcomes across macro, meso, and micro levels. In this way, these papers remained relatively focused on a defined set of implementation activities and proximal outcomes related to enhancing access to or quality of health care delivery.

Category 2: Calling for attention to social structures for health equity in implementation science

The three papers included in the second category of our findings represented a shift in perspective in three important respects. First, these papers generally acknowledge the nuanced influence of social structures and higher-level contexts in shaping health inequities, and demand that such influences be more explicitly incorporated into IS research. For example, Brownson et al. (2021) began their article by acknowledging that “there is growing urgency to tackle issues of equity and justice, which is driven by greater awareness of decades-long increases in income and wealth inequality and the visibility and impacts of structural racism and associated societal problems” [1], p. 1). By explicitly naming the issues of income inequality and structural racism, they drew more explicit attention to the forces shaping inequities in health and health care.

Second, these papers advocated that IS researchers and practitioners should choose interventions that effectively address the social structures that produce health inequities. This is an important departure from the first category of contributions on health equity in IS, as these articles advocated for deeper attention to the kinds of practices and policies that IS researchers spend their time supporting. As Baumann et al. (2023) argued, after acknowledging the role of systemic racism in producing health inequities, “we must identify ways to dismantle the effects of racism in our systems and implement more equitable policies and practices, including evidence that was informed by and reflects populations and settings impacted by racism” [23], p. 2). Authors in this second category of articles advocated that the evidence and practices selected for implementation is a central consideration for a meaningful equity-focused IS.

Third and finally, contributions in this category argued specifically for multi-sectorial interventions that include collaborators beyond the formal health care system. Kerkhoff et al. (2022) suggest that it is essential to work in participatory ways, engaging “those with relevant lived experiences and in conjunction with community partners and other stakeholders to identify which contextually specific barriers to accessing or receiving an intervention may be the most important ones to target” [24], p. 3). They clarify that such barriers might fall outside the health care system yet are nonetheless extremely important to address in IS projects. Building on a similar insight, Baumann et al. (2023) position cross-sector collaboration as a fundamental principle of equity-focused IS. These authors suggest that “we must focus our efforts on systemic perspectives and multisector settings that have the potential to address the underlying causes of these inequities” [23], p. 4). In these ways, contributions in this category reflected deeper thinking about the social and structural causes of health inequities in comparison to the first category, and the participatory, multi-sector approaches necessary to address them.

Category 3: Critical social theory for social justice in implementation science

Contributions in the third category we identified shared a common characteristic that distinguished them from those in the preceding categories: they all selected a theoretical concept from critical social sciences that offered more nuanced insights into health inequities, and explored the implications of that concept for IS in detail. A central premise of the articles in this category is that the effort to develop an adequate understanding of the root causes of inequities, and therefore strategies for their resolution, depends on a satisfactory understanding of the operations of power and oppression. For example, Etherington et al. (2020) suggest that a central recognition for bringing intersectionality theory to the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF) is that although “many TDF domains focus on the individual, individuals are impacted by systems and structures of power” [9], p. 5). Even more explicitly, Stanton et al. (2022) propose a typology of different types of power that influence the conduct of IS projects, shaping which kinds of evidence are selected for implementation and who gets to make decisions about IS processes. As a result, it is not only structural features of social reality that are viewed as impacting health inequities and forming an important focus of IS (as in Category 2), but also the concentrations of power that uphold those social structures and enable them to persist over time (emphasized in Category 3).

A fundamental insight linked to many critical social science conceptualizations of power is the concept of oppression. Oppression is used in different ways across the contributions in this category, but in general refers to disadvantage experienced by communities resulting from social structures that generate unwarranted challenges in accessing safety, opportunity, and well-being while conferring such advantages upon other groups [14, 16]. Sanchez et al. (2023) explicitly address a particular form of oppression in their contribution: white supremacy. White supremacy refers to a set of institutions and social systems that “center whiteness as the default human experience’ [25], p. 2), marginalizing ways of knowing and living that do not align with a white, Western, Euro-centric way of seeing the world. Sanchez et al. (2023) articulated that if IS researchers and practitioners are to deeply embrace health equity as a fundamental goal, then they must understand how white supremacy shapes accepted practices in IS and work to disrupt them in their IS projects.

Beyond the application of theoretical concepts from critical social sciences, these contributions tended to actively explore the dynamic nature of theoretical concepts and multiple views of the world that exist within critical social theory. Like IS, critical social theory is not a stable field with a single identity. Rather, for example, Snell-Rood et al. (2021) explored a variety of concepts from critical social theory that can be used to inform more sophisticated understandings of health equity for IS, such as anti-colonialism (i.e., resistance to social structures and practices that represent colonial power). Furthermore, Snell-Rood et al. (2021) highlighted the central importance of critical reflexivity, a skillful practice through which IS researchers and practitioners can learn to “thoughtfully analyze our own positions of power”, thereby “recognizing the limitations of our individual perspectives and Western biomedical frameworks while considering the potential that even well-intentioned research might alienate or do harm to its subjects” [3], p. 1619). Such self-honesty, although deeply challenging, was a fundamental element of contributions in this third category.

Discussion

In this paper, we have collated select publications that represent three distinct perspectives to conceptualizing and addressing health equity for research and practice in IS. Based on a targeted review of this body of work, we identified three categories of relevant articles. The first sought to draw attention to the importance of health equity in IS but remained strongly situated in the more positivist and biomedically-oriented methods and concepts of IS. The second sought to broaden the perspective on the structural influences relevant to health equity in IS, encouraging IS researchers and practitioners to think across sectors and target the social determinants of health. The third and final category included articles that centered concepts from the critical social theory to interrogate normative beliefs in the field of IS, aiming to make social justice more prominent in IS as an approach to achieving health equity. In our discussion section, we highlight opportunities for development in equity-oriented IS work, and build upon the contributions in Category 3 in our findings to discuss the need for a paradigm shift as a central tension in the field.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, many researchers shifted focus to exploring themes related to health equity in public health and health services [26]. Although the additional attention to health equity was seen as an important advancement, the growth in research output, competition for grant funding, and additional research burden placed on structurally marginalized communities was also identified as a salient point of tension and risk [11]. In their highly cited commentary on this phenomenon, which they referred to as “health equity tourism”, Lett et al. (2022) explained that attention to health equity from researchers untrained in the concepts and methodologies of health equity risks “polluting” the health equity knowledge base and generating inappropriate demands on structurally marginalized communities. These impacts then risk producing fragmentation in the evidence base and deepening the difficulties for researchers and advocates who seek to re-orient health care services, public health, and health policy to more meaningfully address health inequities. Through the analysis reported in our paper, we identify a similar risk in the field of IS: Where IS researchers engage with health equity-related issues only on a surface level, the field of IS risks contributing to the fragmentation and implementation challenges faced by practitioners and advocates working in the field.

However, our findings also provide important signals to advance theorizing in IS for health equity. The literature we reviewed illustrates a learning pathway for IS researchers and practitioners related to health equity, beginning with the acknowledgement that health equity deserves explicit focus in IS (Category 1), progressing to the recognition that health equity requires a broader focus on social structures across sectors (Category 2), and finally that more critical thinking is required for IS to make meaningful impacts on health equity (Category 3). We also see practical and popular contributions emerging in IS that encourage the development of expertise for IS and health equity in more meaningful ways. The special collection of the Stanford Social Innovation Review on equitable implementation in 2021 was an essential output for the field [27], which inspired practical tools such as the Collaborative for Implementation Practice’s (hosted by UNC School of Social Work) Equitable Implementation Guide [28]. These resources explicitly call attention to the need to dismantle structures of power and engage in critical reflexivity to advance the ways that IS can address health equity, representing promising practices to advance the field. We also acknowledge that the contributions in Category 1 and 2 are essential aspects of this pathway, introducing health equity in ways that align with the readiness of different actors in the IS ecosystem to engage with the issues raised in our paper.

Proposing that IS researchers and practitioners might progress through these stages as a learning process also implies an important tension in the field. Boulton et al. (2020) described the historical development of IS as a branch of evidence-based medicine (EBM), identifying that IS originally inherited a scientific paradigm represented by the so-called hierarchy of evidence [4]. Over time, IS has included social science methods that help to illuminate issues of context and process, such as qualitative research, but often subsumed those methods under the dominant paradigm of evidence-based medicine [29]. The paradox we see here is that the dominant paradigm at the foundation of IS has historically undervalued deeper critical reflection and other ways of knowing that stand to be more effective at conceptualizing and mitigating health inequities. Critical social science approaches, such as those reviewed in Category 3 in our findings, encourage a strong critique of the structures of power that position EBM as the sole, dominant conceptualization of knowledge. These critiques identify the ways that EBM and its post-positivist worldview marginalize the experiences and perspectives of non-White and non-European communities not central to the EBM paradigm [30]. In essence, insights from the papers in Category 3 in our findings set a path for a paradigm shift that is necessary for IS research and practice that aims to effectively enhance health equity.

A paradigm shift is necessary and possible in large-scale IS projects. For example, Todic et al. (2022) explicitly situated their efforts to implement a collection of equity-promoting initiatives at the University of Chicago Medicine in critical social science perspectives [31]. They translated critical reflections into practical efforts, such as building relationships as vehicles of change, which builds on critical insights, such as the importance of relational accountability for meaningful action to dismantle systems of power and oppression. These authors reflected upon the challenges of bringing a wide variety of actors in the broader health ecosystem along with them, acknowledging the difficulties of encouraging people to see innovative equity-focused strategies in new ways.

Calling for a paradigm shift in the way we propose demands attention not only to the way that IS practitioners and scholars think about and approach implementation but also to the broader nested environments in which these actors are trained, collaborate, identify projects, and carry out their work. In their article on the knowledge required for implementation and improvement in health care, Cribb et al. (2023) explained that “sociological analyses of expertise show how it does not simply reside ‘in the heads’ of specialist scientists or professionals but is embedded in material and institutional contexts and sets of practices, and across distributed networks of knowledge generation” [32] p. 735). We propose that efforts to advance health equity as a field of IS demand further reflection on this distributed set of contexts and practices, informing strategic projects to align resources on high-impact opportunities to make meaningful changes toward advancing health equity in IS. This approach comes with the explicit recognition that meaningful work on health equity does not align with “quick wins” but rather takes prolonged efforts to build good relationships that inform high-impact approaches to change. A limitation of this study is that we did not undertake a more systematic search strategy. While our approach was sound and appropriate for the aims of our paper and our focus on theoretical and commentary articles, a more systematic search might have yielded a larger set of papers related to IS and health equity. However, our processes for reviewing and selecting the 28 articles, which involved reviewing papers that had cited those in our sample and reviewing the reference lists of our sample articles, ensured a strong sample of highly relevant articles for our purposes.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have identified three distinct categories of contributions in IS for health equity and proposed a learning pathway that progresses from acknowledging the need for explicit emphasis on health equity in IS toward deeper critical reflection about power and oppression. Such a learning process implies a paradigm shift that runs counter to the historical foundations of IS as a field, as it demands attention to alternative ways of knowing that contradict the conventions of evidence-based medicine. Furthermore, this shift demands distributed efforts across the ecosystem of IS education, research, and practice, embracing critical theoretical approaches, like intersectionality, that guide deeper reflection on how power and privilege operate across individual and social structures. Toward this end we advocate for integration of critical social theory to efforts from IS aimed at promoting health equity in practice.
