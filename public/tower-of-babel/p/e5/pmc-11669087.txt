Vanadium-Based Cathodes for Aqueous Zinc-Ion Batteries: Mechanisms, Challenges, and Strategies
Authors: Kaiyue Zhu, Weishen Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ConspectusZinc-ion batteries (ZIBs) are highly promising for large-scale energy storage because of their safety, high energy/power density, low cost, and eco-friendliness. Vanadium-based compounds are attractive cathodes because of their versatile structures and multielectron redox processes (+5 to +3), leading to high capacity. Layered structures or 3-dimensional open tunnel frameworks allow easy movement of zinc-ions without breaking the structure apart, offering superior rate-performance. However, challenges such as dissolution and phase transformation hinder the long-term stability of vanadium-based cathodes in ZIBs. Although significant research has been dedicated to understanding the mechanisms and developing high-performance vanadium-based cathodes, uncertainties still exist regarding the critical mechanisms of energy storage and dissolution, the actual active phase and the specific optimization strategy. For example, it is unclear whether materials such as α-V2O5, VO2, and V2O3 serve as the active phase or undergo phase transformations during cycling. Additionally, the root cause of V-dissolution and the role of byproducts such as Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O in ZIBs are debated.In this account, we aim to outline a clear and comprehensive roadmap for V-based cathodes in ZIBs. On the basis of our studies, we analyzed intrinsic crystal structures and their correlation with performance to guide the design of V-based materials with high-capacity and high-stability for ZIBs. Then, we revealed the underlying mechanisms of energy storage and instability, enabling more effective design and optimization of V-based cathodes. After identifying the key challenges, we proposed effective design principles to achieve high cycling performance of V-based cathodes and outlined future development directions toward their practical application. Vanadium-based compounds include [VO4] tetrahedrons, [VO5] square pyramids, and [VO6] octahedra, which are connected through a cocorner, coedge and coplane. The [VO4] tetrahedron is inactive, and the [VO5] square pyramid is unstable in aqueous solutions because water attacks the exposed vanadium, whereas stable [VO6] octahedra are desirable because of their ability to reduce from +5 to +3 with minimal structural distortion. Therefore, high-performance vanadium-based oxides in ZIBs should maintain intact [VO6] octahedra while avoiding [VO4] tetrahedra or [VO5] square pyramids. The energy storage mechanism involves H2O/H+/Zn2+ coinsertion. The existence of interlayer water in V-based cathodes significantly improves the rate and cycling performance by expanding galleries, screening Zn2+ electrostatically via solvation, reducing ion diffusion energy barriers, and increasing layer flexibility. The insertion of H+/Zn2+ and the instability of V-based cathodes lead to the formation of byproducts such as basic zinc salts (i.e., Zn4SO4(OH)6·nH2O) and dead vanadium (Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O), whose reversibility strongly affects long-term stability. To increase the cycling stability of vanadium-based cathodes, strategies such as electrolyte modulation and coating have been proposed to decrease water attack on the surface of V-oxides, thereby affecting the formation of byproducts. Additionally, in situ electrochemical transformation, ion preintercalation, and ion exchange were explored to prepare intrinsically stable V-based cathodes with enhanced performance. Furthermore, future research should focus on revealing atomic-scale mechanisms through advanced in situ characterization and theoretical calculations, enhancing rate-performance by facilitating ion/electron diffusion, promoting cycling stability by developing highly stable cathodes and refining interface engineering, and scaling up vanadium-based cathodes for practical ZIB applications.

Introduction

The growing demand for clean energy has driven advancements in renewable energy technologies such as solar, wind, and tidal power. However, their intermittent nature necessitates efficient energy storage solutions.5,6 Although lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) are widely used, they face challenges in large-scale storage because of their safety concerns and high cost.7 Zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs), which utilize near-neutral aqueous electrolytes (ZnSO4) and abundant Zn, are promising alternatives.8−12 Despite their lower working voltages, Zn has high theoretical capacities (820 Ah/kg and 5851 Ah/L), enabling ZIBs to achieve superior energy density compared with other aqueous batteries, such as those based on Li/Na/K.13−15

Although ZIBs hold promise for large-scale energy storage, their progress has been impeded by the lack of durable cathode materials.16−18 These materials must enable rapid and reversible ion intercalation, which dictates the voltage, rate capability, and cycling stability of the battery. Various studies have explored ZIB cathodes, including manganese- and vanadium(V)-based compounds, Prussian blue analogs, and organic compounds.19 Vanadium-based oxides, in particular, offer multiple oxidation states (+3 to +5), allowing for efficient electron transfer and high theoretical capacity.20 The broad tunnel and layered structures of these materials facilitate zinc-ion movement, resulting in excellent rate performance and cycling stability. Despite their potential, V-based cathodes face challenges such as vanadium dissolution and phase transformation, limiting long-term cycling stability.21−23 Understanding the working principles of ZIBs and the evolution of V-based compounds during operation is crucial for overcoming these limitations and designing high-performance V-based cathodes.

Research on V-based materials as cathodes for ZIBs has yielded conflicting findings, complicating the commercial advancement of ZIBs. Uncertainties exist regarding whether materials such as α-V2O5, VO2, and V2O3 serve as the active phase or undergo phase transformations during cycling. Additionally, the role of byproducts such as Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O in aqueous ZIBs is debated. This account analyzes the intrinsic crystal structures derived from fundamental building blocks of V-based cathode materials and provides a summary of their charge storage and dissolution/instability mechanisms, challenges, and strategies for enhancing cycling stability (Figure 1). Insights are offered for further research directions in the field to aid in the commercialization of ZIBs.

Overview of vanadium-based cathodes for aqueous ZIBs.

The Building Blocks for V-Based Cathode Materials

V-based cathodes utilize vanadium–oxygen coordination polyhedra as building blocks ranging from tetrahedrons to square pyramids and octahedra, with oxidation states ranging from +5 to +3 (Figure 2a).24,25 These polyhedra are interconnected through corner, edge, and plane sharing, forming layered structures or open tunnel frameworks (Figure 2b-2c). Correlating the building blocks with the performances will help us design V-based materials with high capacity and stability.

Tetrahedron

In the V–O tetrahedron, vanadium has an oxidation state of +5, and attempting to alter this state leads to significant structural changes.24 That is, reversible redox reactions within pure tetrahedral frameworks are challenging. Consequently, Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O (ZOV), composed solely of [VO4] tetrahedra, exhibits minimal activity (∼2 mAh g–1) in ZIBs (Figure 3a).4

Square Pyramid

In the V–O square pyramid configuration, four oxygen atoms lie in a plane with a fifth double-bonded oxygen at the apex. For α-V2O5, these [VO5] square pyramids form layers with an interlayer distance of 4.37 Å.24 Such tightly stacked V2O5 sheets in α-V2O5 pose challenges for the intercalation and diffusion of zinc ions, thereby leading to the limited capacity (∼160 mAh g–1) of α-V2O5 as a ZIB cathode (Figure 3a).26 Moreover, soaking α-V2O5 in a ZnSO4 aqueous electrolyte for 20 days changed the color of the solution from colorless to pale yellow as the vanadium content increased from 0 to 62 ppm and the pH decreased from 4.6 to 3.2 (Figure 3b). This dissolution is due to water attacking the exposed vanadium in the square pyramid structure rather than the proton attacking. Regular Octahedron

In its +3 oxidation state, vanadium forms a regular octahedral configuration.24 In V2O3, each V3+ bonds with six equivalent O2– ions, resulting in corner-, edge-, and plane-sharing VO6 octahedra. Owing to the low valence of V3+, V2O3 cannot be reduced by Zn, making it inactive in ZIBs. However, V3+ ions are susceptible to oxidation in air, leading to slight V4+ or V5+ states in V2O3. Consequently, V2O3 has a very low capacity of 45 mAh g–1 in ZIBs (Figure 3a).28

Distorted Octahedron

The addition of extra oxygen converts the square pyramid into a distorted octahedron, which is typical for vanadium with oxidation states of +4 and +5.24 Tunnel-structured VO2, which is composed of distorted octahedra shared by edges and corners, allows ion insertion in aqueous ZIBs because its tunnel size is approximately 3.7 Å × 4.4 Å. As VO2 undergoes the sole redox reaction involving V4+ and V3+, its capacity in ZIBs is limited to ∼300 mAh g–1 (Figure 3a).29 Notably, VO2 is susceptible to oxidation upon air exposure and transforms into V2O5·nH2O (Figure 3d), as indicated by eq 3.3

Another example is layer-structured V2O5·nH2O analogs, which feature bilayered V2O5 frameworks connected by distorted octahedra that encapsulate solvent molecules or ions (e.g., Zn2+, Ca2+, Mg2+, NH4+, Li+, Na+, K+) within their oxide network as pillars.30−32 These materials demonstrate remarkable stability in ZIBs. For example, the electrolyte solution after immersion of Zn0.1V2O5·nH2O remains colorless, with the V concentration stabilizing at 2 ppm (Figure 3e), which is significantly lower than that of α-V2O5.3 As the oxidation state of vanadium in V2O5·nH2O changes from +5 to +3, the coordination polyhedra change from distorted to regular octahedra. Consequently, V2O5·nH2O analogs can achieve a capacity of up to 440 mAh g–1 (Figure 3a).

Distorted Octahedron + Square Pyramid

A2V6O16·nH2O (AVO, A = NH4+, Na+, K+) is a common ZIB cathode material, with stacked V6O16 layers hosting hydrated A ions.33 These layers contain both [VO5] square pyramids and [VO6] distorted octahedra, allowing AVO to offer a capacity of approximately 300 mAh g–1 (Figure 3a). This capacity is higher than that of α-V2O5 (a sole [VO5] square pyramid) but lower than that of V2O5·nH2O analogs ([VO6] distorted octahedra).1 Notably, its cycling stability is compromised due to dissolution caused by the [VO5] square pyramid (Figure 3f).

Overall, high-performance vanadium-based materials in ZIBs should maintain intact [VO6] octahedra while avoiding [VO4] tetrahedra or [VO5] square pyramids.

Mechanisms for V-Based Cathode Materials

Understanding the mechanisms of energy storage and instability clearly helps us propose efficient optimization strategies to increase the performance of V-based cathode in ZIBs.

Ionic Storage Mechanism of V-Based Cathodes

Initially, it was thought that only Zn2+ contributed to aqueous ZIB cycling, but H+ also participated because of its presence in electrolytes. Aqueous electrolytes exhibit a greater capacity than nonaqueous electrolytes (e.g., acetonitrile) for VO2 and V3O7·H2O cathodes, indicating the involvement of H+ alongside Zn2+.34,35 During discharge, H+ insertion results in the formation of basic zinc salts (BZSs) on the cathode surface. These BZSs serve a pH-buffering function, facilitating the abundant insertion of H+. The BZS composition depends on the electrolyte, such as Zn4(SO4)(OH)6·nH2O in ZnSO4 and Zn(OH)2 or Znx(OH)y(OTf)2x-y·nH2O (OTf– = CF3SO3–) in Zn(OTf)2.36 We utilized in situ transmission XRD for the first time to illustrate the reversible formation/decomposition of Zn(OH)2 (2θ = 13° and 19.5°) in VO2 electrodes during cycling, in addition to lattice expansion/contraction from Zn2+/H+ (Figure 4a).34 Additionally, we observed the emergence of nanoflakes on the electrode surface during discharge, which vanished upon charging (Figure 4b). STEM mapping also revealed the presence of Zn2+ in the discharged VO2 nanorods (Figure 4c). The process of H+-insertion/extraction causes pH fluctuations in the electrolyte, resulting in the reversible precipitation/dissolution of BZSs (such as Zn4(SO4)(OH)6·nH2O or Zn(OH)2) (eqs 4–7).4567

Ab initio molecular dynamics simulations further demonstrated the transport, adsorption, and surface redox processes for Zn2+ and H+ within the gallery of Zn0.1V2O5·nH2O (Figure 4d and 4e).3

The Vital Role of Interlayer Water

The gallery of Zn0.1V2O5·nH2O expands when immersed in a ZnSO4 aqueous solution and pure water. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed a d001 spacing of 10.14 Å with a single water layer, whereas the spacing increased to 13.0 Å with two more layers of water (Figure 5b). Along with H+/Zn2+ coinsertion, water participated in the charge–discharge processes, as shown schematically in Figure 5b. To study the role of interlayer water, we prepared hydrated NaCa0.6V6O16·3H2O and anhydrous NaCa0.6V6O16, which have the same [V6O16]2– layers but different interlayer water contents (Figure 5c-5e).2 Compared with NaCa0.6V6O16, NaCa0.6V6O16·3H2O has a larger interlayer spacing (8.1 Å vs 6.7 Å), which enhances the Zn2+ storage capacity by providing additional Zn2+ host sites (Figure 5d and 5e). Consequently, NaCa0.6V6O16·3H2O demonstrates superior Zn2+ storage performance compared with that of NaCa0.6V6O16, highlighting the importance of interlayer water in enhancing Zn2+ (de)intercalation (Figure 5g and 5h). This beneficial effect of interlayer water is also observed in other compounds, such as V2O5·nH2O, emphasizing its general role in facilitating ion diffusion.38,39

Instable Mechanism for V-Based Cathodes

Based on the characteristics of its building blocks, α-V2O5 exhibits amphoteric behavior, readily dissolves in both acidic and alkaline solutions and is even slightly soluble in water, forming a pale yellow solution.40 The dissolved vanadium species depend on the pH and vanadium-ion concentration (Figure 3c).41 When vanadium in V-based oxides is pentavalent, the soluble V5+ species remain consistent across different materials at a given pH and V5+ concentration. Therefore, α-V2O5, which is commonly used in ZIBs, was chosen as a representative material for studying the dissolution of V-based oxides. Figure 6a shows that vanadium ions exist as VO2+ in the Zn(NO3)2 electrolyte (pH: ∼ 2) due to proton attack (eq 9), whereas they are present in the form of [VO2(OH)2]− or [V10O26(OH)2]4– in the ZnSO4, Zn(OTf)2, ZnCl2, and Zn(OAc)2 electrolytes with a pH range of 4.2–5.4 due to water attack (eqs 1 and 2).49

Interestingly, in the ZnSO4 and Zn(NO3)2 electrolytes, the dissolved vanadium ions did not react with Zn2+. However, in Zn(OTf)2, ZnCl2, and Zn(OAc)2 solutions, they did, forming Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O (eq 10) with various morphologies (Figure 6b-6d). Since the vanadium ions are the same (Figure 3c), the discrepancy likely arises from the Zn2+ ions. This is likely due to the influence of the anions present in the solution, which strongly affect the solvation structures around zinc-ions.410

In ZnSO4, ZnCl2, Zn(OTf)2, Zn(OAc)2 and electrolytes, the average numbers of water molecules in the Zn2+ solvation shell are 5.78, 5.56, 5.39 and 5.28, respectively, while the Zn2+ solvation shell is occupied by 0.22 SO42–, 0.44 Cl–, 0.61 OTf–, and 0.72 OAc–.4

In addition to zinc ions, vanadium ions also react with BZSs formed during discharge in various zinc-based electrolytes (Figure 6e-6h, eq 11). Simplifying, Zn(OH)2 can be regarded as the primary component of the aforementioned BZS.11

Similar phenomena were noted for other vanadium oxides ((NH4)0.4V2O5·1.6H2O and (NH4)2V6O16·1.5H2O), indicating widespread occurrence akin to what is observed with the α-V2O5 electrode.4

Clearly, without adequate stability, the immersion of V-based oxides in zinc electrolytes can produce soluble V-species or insoluble Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O (Figure 7a). Although Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O does not directly form from the dissolution of V-based oxides in ZnSO4, it does during cycling due to the reaction between Zn4(OH)6SO4·4H2O (generated during H+-insertion) with soluble vanadium ions. In ZnCl2, Zn(OAc)2, and Zn(OTf)2 electrolytes, Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O forms during both immersion and cycling. The dissolution of V-based oxides induces the continuous transformation of soluble vanadium ions into Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O precipitates, fuelling further dissolution until depletion.42

Although slight oxidation/reduction of soluble V-ions occurs resulting from V-dissolution, these processes are highly irreversible, contributing only minimally to the overall capacity. Additionally, soluble V-ions could react with the Zn anode or BZS, forming dead-vanadium (Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O). Therefore, controlling the presence of soluble V-ions in ZIBs is essential.

Balancing the positive impacts of water on capacity and diffusion kinetics but its negative role in corrosion and optimizing water activity in the electrolyte is crucial for enhancing the performance of V-based cathodes.

Identification of Inactive Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O in ZIBs

The synthesis of Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O via hydrothermal methods at high temperatures (∼150 °C) often yields low-crystalline V2O5·nH2O analogs, complicating Zn2+ storage assessments.43,44 To address this, Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O was prepared at 60 °C, ensuring that its purity was devoid of V2O5·nH2O analogs, as XRD confirmed the absence of hydrated vanadium-based compounds (2θ range of 5–10°) (Figure 7b), and inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectroscopy (ICP–OES) confirmed a 3:2 Zn:V ratio.4,45 Electrochemical tests revealed a marginal capacity of ∼2 mAh g–1 in the ZIBs, indicating the inactivity of these materials in the ZIBs (Figure 7c). Its structure features [ZnO6] octahedral layers interspersed with [VO4] tetrahedra pillars. Unlike [VO6] distorted octahedra in hydrated vanadium oxides, which can readily undergo oxidation/reduction by octahedral distortion (V change between +5 and +3), [VO4] tetrahedra cannot undergo reduction in ZIBs.24

Figure 7d indicates that Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O dissolves at pH values below 3.6 but remains stable at higher pH values.4 Consequently, once formed, it persists in the battery during cycling or immersion, behaving as “dead vanadium”. Considering its lack of Zn2+-storage activity, the dissolution of vanadium-based cathodes and their transformation to Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O contribute to capacity decay during cycling, which needs to be effectively suppressed.

Critical Challenges Faced by V-Based Cathodes

V-based compounds show promise as cathode materials owing to their diverse structures and multielectron redox processes, offering a high capacity. However, their practical use is hampered by poor long-term cycling stability, which is attributed to the formation of irreversible byproducts stemming from H+ insertion and the intrinsic instability of V-based oxides.

The Irreversibility of Layered Zinc Hydroxides Arising from H+

Passerini et al. designed a cell for operando pH monitoring using a Zn anode and a Ca0.34V2O5·nH2O cathode.46Figure 8a shows a sharp pH increase during discharge (1.1 V → 0.9 V) due to H+ intercalation, which stabilized later (0.9 V → 0.2 V) from pH buffering by BZS. During charging (0.2 V → 1.55 V), the pH slightly decreases because H+ is released from the cathode without returning to the initial value. The Irreversibility of Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O Arising from V-Dissolution

In ZIBs with V-based cathodes, extended cycling often leads to the formation of Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O as a harmful byproduct (Figure 8c).4 Its formation is caused primarily by the dissolution of V-based cathodes. Additionally, we found that soluble vanadium-ions have a detrimental effect on the Zn anode. After the Zn foil was soaked in soluble vanadium ions, mossy Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O formed on the surface of the Zn anode, accompanied by a color of the solution from pale yellow to blue green (Figure 8d).4

From the building blocks, [VO5] square pyramids contribute to instability and susceptibility to water attack, causing structural distortion and dissolution (Figure 8e).26 Considering the inactive [VO4] tetrahedra, V-based oxides should preserve intact [VO6] octahedra and avoid [VO4] tetrahedra, [VO5] square pyramids, or oxygen-deficient [VO6] octahedra. Therefore, designing intrinsically stable V-based materials and minimizing water activity are crucial for reducing corrosion potential, thereby suppressing the generation of Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O byproducts.

Strategies for Enhancing the Stability of V-Based Cathodes

To increase the stability of V-based cathodes in ZIBs, several strategies include electrolyte modulation to improve the reversibility of BZSs and reduce material dissolution by lowering water activity. Surface modifications such as carbon and/or conductive polymer coatings mitigate water attack, whereas the intrinsic stability is increased by strategies such as in situ electrochemical activation, ion preintercalation, and ion exchange-induced phase transformation, thereby curbing V-dissolution.

Electrolyte Modulation

Water in ZIBs enhances the gallery spacing in vanadium oxide, promoting zinc-ion insertion and increasing the capacity.3,47,48 Protons from water also migrate quickly within the cathode, improving active site utilization. However, water can generate hydroxide ions during proton insertion, forming BZS byproducts. It also causes dissolution and phase transformation of V-based materials, thereby affecting long-term performance.

We propose the use of organic electrolytes with minimal water content to enable sufficient proton insertion while mitigating cathode damage.49 In a Zn(OTf)2 electrolyte with 8% H2O in acetonitrile (E-8% H2O), Figure 9a shows that Zn||(NH4)0.5V2O5·0.5H2O (NVO) achieved 475 mAh g–1 at 0.5 A g–1, with 92% retention after 100 cycles, demonstrating a high capacity as in aqueous electrolytes (E-100% H2O) and excellent stability as in organic electrolytes (E-0% H2O).

Reducing the water content minimized V-based cathode dissolution and improved the reversibility of BZS (Znx(OTf)y(OH)2x-y·nH2O), thereby increasing the stability. The optimal water content depends on the cathode mass loading.

Additionally, some hydrogel electrolytes can effectively reduce water activity, minimizing water-induced damage to V-based cathodes and suppressing the formation of Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O.47 Consequently, the adoption of suitable hydrogel electrolytes can significantly enhance the cycling performance of V-based cathodes.

Coating

To prevent V-based cathode dissolution, a carbon shell was uniformly coated on V2O3 nanorods via a polydopamine-assisted approach (Figure 10a and 10b), which significantly increased the electrical conductivity and stability compared with those of bare V2O3. The resulting V2O3@C nanorods exhibited a high capacity of 290 mA h g–1 at 1 A g–1, which was superior to that of V2O3 (Figure 10c). Similarly, conductive polyaniline and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) coatings could improve the electronic conductivity and structural integrity of V-based oxides.50

In Situ Electrochemical Transformation

The stability and capacity shortcomings observed for α-V2O5, VO2, and V2O3 make them unsuitable as primary active materials in ZIBs.26,28 However, they can serve as precursors for synthesizing high-performance materials via electrochemical activation.

When charging beyond 1.4 V vs Zn/Zn2+, V2O3 undergoes electrochemical oxidation to form high-performance V2O5·nH2O (eq 12, Figures 10d and 10e).28 However, it cannot revert to V2O3 when it is reduced. Consequently, in ZIBs utilizing a V2O3 cathode, the active component is the V2O5·nH2O phase rather than V2O3 itself.12

Similarly, pure VO2 undergoes electrochemical conversion into V2O5·nH2O at elevated anodic voltages (∼1.55 V vs Zn/Zn2+) (eq 13, Figures 10f-i).29,31 Upon activation, the discharge capacity of the cathode notably increases from 332 to 610 mAh g–1 due to the activated V5+/V3+ redox couple and enlarged gallery spacing.13

Another pertinent example requiring activation is α-V2O5. After soaking α-V2O5 in water, two broad XRD peaks appear at ∼7.1° and ∼26°, corresponding to bilayer V2O5·nH2O (Figure 11a).26 The SEM image in Figure 11b indicates a change in the morphology from nanoparticles to nanowires. Ion Preintercalation

Weak van der Waals forces between layers in hydrated V2O5·1.75H2O and V3O7·H2O can cause fragmentation during charge/discharge cycles, leading to material dissolution and structural compromise.31 To address this issue, ion preintercalation is used to strengthen layer interactions. For example, ions are inserted into a crystal structure before cycling, forming stable bonds with oxygen and acting as “pillars” to support the material during Zn2+ migration, thus preventing collapse.1,2,37

Bilayered V2O5·nH2O is a promising host for Zn2+ preintercalation. Zhu et al. synthesized Zn0.1V2O5·nH2O (ZnVO) via a hydrothermal method in a concentrated ZnSO4 solution (1 M). Zn2+ ions and structural water stabilize the layered structure as “pillars” and “adhesives” (Figure 12a).3 It exhibited excellent immersion stability (Figure 3e) and electrochemical performance: 463 mAh g–1 at 0.2 A g–1 and 240 mAh g–1 at 10 A g–1, with 93% and 88% capacity retention after 200 cycles at 0.2 A g–1 and 20000 cycles at 10 A g–1, respectively (Figures 12b and 12c), outperforming V2O5·nH2O.

In addition to bilayered V2O5·nH2O hosts, we first proposed the insertion of monovalent (Na+) and divalent (Ca2+) cations into the interlayers of [V6O16]2– layers to increase structural stability.2 We selected Na+ and Ca2+ because of their similar ionic radii (Na+: 1.02 Å, Ca2+: 1.00 Å) and favorable formation energies in the [V6O16]2– layer structure (Ca: – 10.08 eV, Na: – 4.27 eV). Consequently, NaCa0.6V6O16·3H2O delivers outstanding electrochemical performance for ZIBs (Figures 12d and 12e).

Conclusion and Perspective

V-based oxides show promise as cathodes for aqueous ZIBs because of their high capacity, but their cycling stability remains a challenge. We correlated the capacity and stability of these materials with their fundamental building blocks. By exploring the mechanisms of ion insertion/extraction and V dissolution, along with the impact of water, we highlight critical challenges facing V-based cathodes, such as the irreversibility of BZSs and Zn3(OH)2V2O7·2H2O. Strategies such as electrolyte modulation, interface coating, and structural enhancement are proposed to address these challenges. These insights pave the way for innovative electrolytes and cathode materials, advancing ZIB technology.

The following are some perspectives on the development directions for V-based cathodes in future ZIBs:To gain a deeper understanding of the ionic storage mechanism, it is imperative to accurately determine the quantities of inserted H+ and Zn2+. Enhanced methodologies, such as DFT calculations and advanced techniques, need to be developed to provide profound insights into the atomic- and molecular-level processes in ionic storage, encompassing ion diffusion paths and insertion sites. For example, when HRTEM is employed under high vacuum conditions, the structural water in hydrous V-based oxides tends to evaporate, resulting in the contraction of lattice fringes. Therefore, employing advanced techniques becomes essential for depicting alterations in the lattice spacing and atomic arrangements after discharge and charge.To achieve optimal performance in ZIBs, it is crucial to explore new materials, together with implementing surface and bulk modifications to existing cathode materials. The quest for novel cathode materials exhibiting high capacity, superior rate capability, elevated operating voltage, and extended cycle life should be prioritized in future research endeavors. Furthermore, attention should be given to regulating the electrolyte, separator, and cathode–electrolyte interfaces to increase the reversibility of BZSs and mitigate water-induced corrosion of cathodes. Given that many V-based oxides inherently exhibit low ionic/electronic conductivity, efforts should also be directed toward enhancing conductivity. This will enable the full utilization of active sites within the bulk, consequently enhancing the rate capability and ensuring user convenience.To facilitate the commercial utilization of V-based oxides in aqueous ZIBs, scaling up the production of these materials is imperative. Furthermore, advancements in electrode fabrication techniques are essential for achieving a higher cathode mass loading, ideally reaching 20 mg cm–2.
