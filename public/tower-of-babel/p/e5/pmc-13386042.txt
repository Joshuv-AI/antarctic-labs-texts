Diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound for predicting successful liberation from mechanical ventilation: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Osvaldo Simari Neto, Mauricio Silva de Jesus, Daniela Queiroz de Oliveira, Aline Mizusaki Imoto, Fábio Ferreira Amorim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVentilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction is increasingly recognised as an important complication of prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV) and a major contributor to difficult weaning and extubation failure. Although bedside diaphragmatic ultrasound has emerged as a useful tool for assessing diaphragm function, conventional indices such as diaphragm excursion and thickening fraction may be influenced by ventilatory mechanics and patient effort. Speckle-tracking ultrasound (STU), a deformation-based technique adapted from echocardiography, allows assessment of diaphragmatic strain and strain rate and may provide a more accurate evaluation of diaphragmatic contractility. However, the predictive utility of diaphragmatic STU for successful liberation from MV remains uncertain. This study aims to systematically review the available evidence on the predictive performance of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound for successful weaning from MV in adult patients.MethodsThis protocol follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and is registered in the PROSPERO database. A comprehensive search will be conducted in MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, LILACS/BVS and CENTRAL without restrictions on language or publication date. Observational studies and clinical trials evaluating diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound during the weaning process in adults receiving invasive MV for more than 24 hours will be eligible. Two reviewers will independently select studies, extract data and assess the risk of bias using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool for diagnostic and predictive accuracy studies and the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool for randomised trials. When feasible, meta-analysis will be conducted using Review Manager software. The certainty of the evidence will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)approach.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not applicable for this study since no original data will be collected. The results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication and conference presentations.Prospero registration numberCRD420261338352.

Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) is a cornerstone of care for critically ill patients and has significantly reduced mortality from acute respiratory failure since its widespread adoption in the 1950s.1 Nevertheless, MV is also associated with clinically important adverse effects, particularly when ventilatory support is prolonged, including ventilator-induced lung injury and ventilator-associated pneumonia. While early research and preventive strategies focused primarily on optimising ventilator settings to mitigate lung injury, recent attention has shifted to ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction (VIDD) as another major complication of prolonged ventilation.24 Approximately one quarter of critically ill patients require MV for more than 7 days, and VIDD has emerged as a major contributor to difficult and delayed weaning. Importantly, diaphragmatic dysfunction may persist even when patients appear clinically ready for liberation from ventilatory support, thereby increasing the risk of extubation failure. This finding is particularly concerning because extubation failure occurs in up to 30% of intubated patients even after a successful spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) and is associated with longer length of stay, aspiration pneumonia and increased mortality.5 6

Nowadays, bedside diaphragmatic ultrasound has increasingly emerged as a practical tool for evaluating diaphragmatic dysfunction.7 Commonly used measures include diaphragm thickness at end-inspiration and end-expiration (trophic assessment), excursion and thickening fraction, parameters intended to reflect muscle function.8 9 Although clinically valuable, these indices can be strongly influenced by respiratory system mechanics and ventilatory settings, including lung compliance, pleural and airway pressures, patient effort and preset support levels. Such factors may alter diaphragmatic displacement or thickening without proportional changes in true contractile force.10

An increasingly investigated alternative is diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound (STU), a deformation strain-based technique adapted from echocardiography that may provide a more refined assessment of muscle motion and contractility during assisted or spontaneous breathing.11 Conventional B-mode/M-mode approaches used to derive thickening fraction primarily capture deformation in a transverse plane, whereas speckle tracking can quantify deformation longitudinally along the direction of diaphragmatic fibre shortening, which more directly reflects active contraction.12 In addition to strain magnitude, STU can estimate deformation rate (strain rate) using dedicated software that tracks stable acoustic ‘speckle’ patterns frame-by-frame throughout the contraction–relaxation cycle, thereby offering a closer approximation of real contractile function.13

Evidence suggests that diaphragmatic STU provides a reliable assessment of diaphragm kinematics and function in healthy individuals, with performance that may be more robust than conventional ultrasound indices and that correlates with transdiaphragmatic pressure measurements and electromyographic activity.1416 More recently, studies in critically ill adults undergoing SBTs have found that diaphragm strain is an independent predictor of extubation success, aligning with established two-dimensional ultrasound measures and potentially improving decision-making when integrated with other clinical and physiological tools.11 12 17

Despite these promising findings, diaphragmatic STU remains insufficiently disseminated, and its routine clinical role in ventilator weaning has not been firmly established.18 The most consistent data come from healthy populations. In contrast, studies in critically ill patients are often small, pilot or observational in design, heterogeneous in methodology and lack standardisation and a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence.17 18

Given the established value of deformation-based imaging for functional assessment in echocardiography and the growing need for practical and accurate tools to detect ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction, a rigorous appraisal of the existing evidence is warranted. Besides, the predictive utility of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound for successful weaning and extubation remains unclear. Accordingly, the primary aim of this study is to assess the best available evidence regarding the predictive performance of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound for successful weaning from MV. Additional outcomes include predictive accuracy measures, the Weaning according to a New Definition (WIND) classification, weaning failure, intensive care unit length of stay, MV-free days and mortality.

Methods

Study design

This systematic review will be conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P).19 The protocol of this review is registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), maintained by the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination at the University of York, UK, under the number CRD420261338352. Because this review evaluates the predictive performance of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound, it will also follow the methodological recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses of Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies (PRISMA-DTA)) guidance for diagnostic and predictive test accuracy studies.20

The systematic review is planned to start in July 2026 and to be completed by October 2026. This period will include study selection, data extraction, risk-of-bias assessment, evidence synthesis and manuscript preparation.

The question guiding this study will be: What is the effectiveness of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound in predicting successful weaning from MV?

Inclusion criteria

Population of interest

All adult patients (≥18 years) receiving invasive MV for more than 24 hours, in whom diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound was performed during the weaning process to predict successful liberation from MV.

Intervention

We will include studies that assessed diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound (eg, diaphragmatic strain and/or strain rate) at any point during the weaning process (eg, prior to or during a spontaneous breathing trial, and/or prior to extubation). Patients may or may not have received noninvasive ventilation or high-flow nasal cannula after extubation.

Comparison/control

We will include studies that compared diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound with usual clinical weaning assessment and/or conventional diaphragm ultrasound measures, when applicable.

Outcome

We will include studies that reported at least one outcome related to weaning success, predictive accuracy of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound, WIND classification, weaning failure, ICU length of stay, MV-free days or mortality.

The primary outcome will be weaning success. For this review, weaning success will be defined according to the WIND framework as successful liberation from invasive MV, characterised by extubation without subsequent reintubation or death within 7 days or before ICU discharge, whichever occurs first. Patients discharged from the ICU before 7 days without reintubation will be considered to have achieved weaning success.21 For tracheostomised patients, weaning success will be defined as sustained spontaneous breathing without return to invasive MV or death within the same period, according to the definitions used in the included studies.

Because definitions of weaning success and weaning failure may vary across studies, alternative definitions will also be accepted when clearly reported by the original authors, including successful completion of a spontaneous breathing trial, extubation without reintubation within 48 or 72 hours, extubation without reintubation within 7 days or successful liberation from MV until ICU discharge. For harmonisation, the WIND-based definition using 7 days or ICU discharge will be considered the preferred definition for the primary analysis when sufficient data are available.21 Definitions based on shorter time windows, such as 48 or 72 hours, will be extracted separately and analysed at their original time points. These studies may be included in a quantitative synthesis only when outcome definitions and time windows are sufficiently comparable. Otherwise, findings will be summarised narratively or explored in subgroup or sensitivity analyses, depending on the outcome definition and follow-up period.

When reported, weaning outcomes will also be interpreted according to the WIND framework. The first separation attempt will be defined as the first spontaneous breathing trial, whether or not followed by extubation, or extubation without a previous spontaneous breathing trial, whether planned or unplanned. WIND categories will be extracted when available or classified when sufficient data are provided: no weaning, when no separation attempt is performed; short weaning, when weaning is completed within 24 hours after the first separation attempt; difficult weaning, when weaning is completed after more than 24 hours but within 7 days after the first separation attempt; and prolonged weaning, when weaning is not completed within 7 days after the first separation attempt.21

Predictive accuracy measures will include sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, likelihood ratios, diagnostic ORs, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and, when reported, proposed cut-off values.

Additional clinical outcomes will include weaning failure, ICU length of stay, MV-free days and mortality. Weaning failure will be defined as reintubation, return to invasive MV or death within 7 days after extubation or before ICU discharge, whichever occurs first. ICU length of stay will be defined as the number of days from ICU admission to ICU discharge. MV-free days will be defined according to the definition used in each included study and, when possible, standardised as the number of days alive and free from invasive MV within 28 days. Mortality will be extracted according to the time point reported in each study, including ICU, hospital, 28-day, 30-day or longer-term mortality.

Weaning success was prioritised as the primary outcome because it directly reflects the clinical objective of evaluating whether diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound can predict successful liberation from invasive MV. Predictive accuracy measures were included because they quantify the performance of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound as a predictive tool. WIND classification, weaning failure, ICU length of stay, MV-free days and mortality were considered additional outcomes because they represent clinically relevant consequences of the weaning process and may help contextualise the prognostic value of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound.

Clinical trial registries and grey literature

Grey literature will be searched using Google Scholar, with the first 200 records screened and sorted by relevance. Selection and data analysis

Selection of studies and evaluation of methodological quality

All references retrieved through the search strategy will be imported into the Rayyan platform for systematic reviews, where they will be used for duplicate removal, screening and study selection.

Study selection will be conducted independently by two reviewers (MSdJ and DQdO) using the Rayyan platform. Title and abstract screening will be performed independently by both reviewers. Any disagreements identified during this stage will first be resolved by discussion and, if consensus is not reached, by a third reviewer (OSN).

The full texts of potentially eligible studies will subsequently be independently assessed by the same two reviewers, with any further disagreements resolved by discussion or adjudication by the third reviewer.

Studies that do not meet the inclusion criteria will be excluded, and the reasons for exclusion will be documented. The study selection process will be presented using the PRISMA flow diagram.22

Inter-reviewer agreement will be assessed separately for title/abstract screening and full-text eligibility assessment using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, with percent agreement also reported. Kappa values will be interpreted as follows: <0.00, poor agreement; 0.00–0.20, slight agreement; 0.21–0.40, fair agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement.

Data extraction

Data extraction will be performed independently by two reviewers using a standardised form developed for this review. The form will be pilot-tested by the reviewers before full data extraction and refined if necessary. Discrepancies in the extracted data will be resolved through discussion and, when necessary, through adjudication by a third reviewer. Agreement during data extraction will be summarised descriptively, and any key unresolved discrepancies will be documented.

Summary of results and statistical analysis

Data on the outcomes of interest will be extracted as continuous or dichotomous variables, depending on how each outcome is reported in the included studies. When numerical data required for synthesis are missing, unclear or insufficiently reported, the corresponding authors will be contacted by email up to three times: an initial request followed by two reminders at 2 week intervals. If no response is received within 6 weeks after the initial contact, the data will be considered unavailable for quantitative synthesis.

When necessary and methodologically appropriate, data reported as medians and IQRs, or as ranges, will be converted to means and SD using established statistical methods. Missing SD may be derived from standard errors, CIs, p values or other available summary statistics when valid formulas are applicable. No outcome data will be imputed when the required information cannot be reliably derived from the published report or obtained from study authors. In such cases, the study will be included in the narrative synthesis but excluded from the corresponding quantitative analysis.

For dichotomous outcomes, if missing outcome data are reported and sufficient information is available, best-case and worst-case sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the potential influence of missing data on pooled estimates. If the extent or distribution of missing data is insufficiently reported, the potential impact will be discussed qualitatively.

Because diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound parameters may vary across studies, predefined harmonisation rules will be applied prior to synthesis. Diaphragmatic strain and strain rate will be extracted and analysed separately and will not be pooled with conventional diaphragm ultrasound measures, such as excursion or thickening fraction. Strain values reported as percentages or proportions will be standardised to percentage units when appropriate, and strain-rate values will be standardised to s⁻¹. When strain values are reported using different sign conventions, negative values representing shortening will be converted to absolute values so that higher values consistently indicate greater diaphragmatic deformation. If multiple speckle-tracking parameters are reported, priority will be given to the parameter used in the main predictive model of each study. If both hemidiaphragms are reported separately and no primary hemidiaphragm is specified, the right hemidiaphragm will be preferentially extracted to avoid double-counting; bilateral or averaged measurements will be used when reported as the primary study estimate. Differences in ultrasound software, acquisition protocol, respiratory condition during measurement, timing of assessment and hemidiaphragm evaluation will be considered potential sources of methodological heterogeneity and explored through subgroup or sensitivity analyses, according to whether the variable is prespecified as primary or exploratory. No universal cut-off value will be imposed a priori. Study-specific cut-off values and corresponding diagnostic accuracy estimates will be extracted. When studies report comparable thresholds and sufficient data are available, threshold-specific accuracy estimates may be pooled. When thresholds, units, software or measurement protocols are too heterogeneous for meaningful pooling, findings will be summarised narratively.

Quantitative synthesis will be considered when at least two studies are sufficiently comparable with respect to population, clinical condition, ultrasound technique, timing of assessment, outcome definition and reported effect measures. Meta-analyses will be conducted separately for clinically and methodologically homogeneous groups of studies. When multiple eligible estimates or time points are reported, the selection and grouping of estimates will follow the predefined rules described below to avoid double-counting. For observational studies, adjusted estimates will be preferred when available and when the adjustment strategy is judged clinically and methodologically appropriate; otherwise, unadjusted estimates will be used.

When meta-analysis is feasible, quantitative synthesis will be performed using Review Manager (RevMan), V.5.3 (The Cochrane Collaboration) and/or R statistical software. Because clinical and methodological heterogeneity is anticipated, random-effects models will be used as the primary approach. Fixed-effect models will be considered only in sensitivity analyses or when studies are judged to be clinically and methodologically homogeneous and statistical heterogeneity is negligible, defined as I² <25%, low between-study variance (τ²) and no important inconsistency in the direction or magnitude of effects. For random-effects meta-analyses, between-study variance (τ²) will be estimated and reported. The interpretation of pooled estimates will consider τ², I², the Cochran’s Q test, CI overlap and clinical and methodological diversity across studies. Pooled estimates will be presented with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and statistical significance will be set at p<0.05.

For continuous outcomes, mean differences with 95% CIs will be calculated when outcomes are measured using the same scale or unit. Standardised mean differences with 95% CIs will be used when similar constructs are assessed using different scales or measurement units. For dichotomous outcomes, relative risks with 95% CIs will be used when incidence or risk data are available; ORs with 95% CIs will be used when this measure is more appropriate to the study design or when only odds-based estimates are reported.

For predictive accuracy outcomes, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, positive and negative likelihood ratios, diagnostic ORs, areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve, proposed cut-off values and data required to reconstruct 2×2 contingency tables will be extracted when reported or derivable from the available data. When sufficient studies report paired measures of sensitivity and specificity or provide sufficient information to reconstruct 2×2 contingency tables, a diagnostic or predictive test-accuracy meta-analysis will be considered using hierarchical summary receiver operating characteristic models or bivariate random-effects models as appropriate. Likelihood ratios and diagnostic ORs may be summarised as complementary measures of test performance when they can be reliably extracted or calculated. Positive and negative predictive values will be interpreted with caution as they are influenced by disease prevalence or event probability in the study population. If the available data are insufficient or too heterogeneous with respect to thresholds, populations, ultrasound protocols, timing of assessment or outcome definitions, predictive accuracy findings will be summarised narratively.

To avoid double-counting, each study population will contribute only one estimate to each meta-analysis for a given outcome and time point. When multiple eligible estimates are reported from the same study, priority will be given to the estimate that is most consistent with the predefined outcome definition and the timing of assessment. If several time points are reported, estimates will be grouped into clinically meaningful periods and analysed separately when possible. When both hemidiaphragms or multiple speckle-tracking parameters are reported, the predefined harmonisation rules described above will be applied. For studies reporting clustered, paired or repeated measurements, effect estimates that appropriately account for within-patient correlation will be preferentially extracted. If such adjusted estimates are unavailable, only one estimate per patient or per predefined time point will be used for the primary analysis; additional correlated estimates may be explored narratively or in sensitivity analyses when appropriate.

Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed using Cochran’s Q test, the I² statistic and τ². I² values will be interpreted as low (<25%), moderate (25–50%), substantial (50–75%) or considerable (>75%) heterogeneity. The magnitude and direction of the effects, the overlap of CIs, clinical characteristics, methodological differences and the risk of bias will also be considered when interpreting heterogeneity. When substantial heterogeneity is identified, potential sources will be explored using prespecified primary subgroup analyses, exploratory subgroup analyses or sensitivity analyses, provided sufficient data are available.

If sufficient data are available, prespecified primary subgroup analyses will be performed according to clinically relevant variables, such as the type of respiratory failure, the timing of the ultrasound evaluation and the hemidiaphragm evaluated. Additional subgroup analyses based on the software platform, ventilatory mode, image processing methodology and other specific study characteristics will be considered exploratory and conducted only when sufficient data are available. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted by excluding studies judged to be at high risk of bias, studies using non-standard or poorly described ultrasound acquisition protocols, studies with non-comparable outcome definitions, studies requiring major data transformation and studies contributing extreme or highly influential effect estimates. Influence analyses may be performed by removing one study at a time when at least three studies are included in a given meta-analysis.

Meta-regression will be considered only when an adequate number of studies is available for a given outcome, generally at least 10 studies per covariate, and when covariates are clinically justified and prespecified. Candidate covariates will include timing of ultrasound assessment, ultrasound software, hemidiaphragm evaluated, type of speckle-tracking parameter, patient population, type of respiratory failure or clinical condition, cut-off strategy and risk of bias. Meta-regression findings will be interpreted as exploratory because of the expected limited number of eligible studies and potential ecological bias.

Publication bias or small-study effects will be assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test when at least 10 studies are available for a given outcome. If fewer than 10 studies are available, publication bias will be discussed qualitatively, considering study size, effect direction, selective reporting and the overall pattern of the available evidence.

If quantitative synthesis is not feasible because of clinical, methodological or statistical heterogeneity, findings will be summarised narratively. The narrative synthesis will be structured according to population characteristics, clinical condition or type of respiratory failure, ultrasound method, timing of assessment, outcome definition, direction and magnitude of the observed effects, consistency across studies and methodological quality or risk of bias. Tables will summarise key study characteristics, outcome definitions, effect estimates and main findings to support transparent comparison across studies.

Assessment of risk of bias

Risk of bias will be assessed according to the study design and methodological framework. Nonrandomised diagnostic and predictive accuracy studies will be evaluated using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool,23 with attention to the domains of patient selection, index test, reference standard and flow and timing. Randomised controlled trials, if included, will be assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool.24 Two reviewers will independently assess the risk of bias, and disagreements will be resolved by a third reviewer (OSN).

Studies will not be excluded solely based on risk-of-bias judgments. However, risk-of-bias assessments will be incorporated into the interpretation of findings, subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses and certainty-of-evidence judgments. When sufficient data are available, subgroup or sensitivity analyses will be performed by excluding studies judged to be at high risk of bias or with major concerns in key QUADAS-2 domains, particularly patient selection, conduct or interpretation of the index test and appropriateness of the reference standard. The influence of high-risk studies on pooled estimates will be evaluated when quantitative synthesis is feasible.

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The certainty of the evidence will be assessed according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach,25 which considers five domains: risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness and publication bias. For diagnostic or predictive accuracy outcomes, certainty will be assessed across these domains, taking into account the specific methodological features of diagnostic and predictive test accuracy studies.

Risk-of-bias judgments from QUADAS-2 or RoB 2 will inform the GRADE risk-of-bias domain. Evidence may be downgraded when most of the information comes from studies with a high risk of bias or major applicability concerns, especially when these limitations affect patient selection, ultrasound measurements, outcome definitions or follow-up.

Two reviewers (MSdJ and DQdO) will independently evaluate the certainty of the evidence for the included studies, and disagreements will be resolved by a third reviewer (OSN).

Ethics and dissemination

This review will be based exclusively on data from studies published in electronic databases. Because it involves synthesising previously published and publicly accessible information, it does not require approval from a research ethics committee. Nevertheless, ethical principles will be upheld throughout the review process, particularly regarding fidelity to the original authors, accurate reporting of findings and preservation of textual and intellectual integrity. The reviewers have no relationships with the authors of the included studies that could give rise to conflicts of interest.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Discussion

The role of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound in the weaning process has not yet been clearly established, despite growing interest in this technique. Preliminary studies suggest that strain-based ultrasound assessment may provide clinically relevant information about diaphragmatic mechanics during spontaneous breathing and assisted ventilation, potentially improving predictions of weaning and extubation outcomes.1112 1418 However, the available evidence remains fragmented and methodologically heterogeneous. Most published studies have involved relatively small samples, observational or pilot designs, and variability in patient selection, timing of assessment, ultrasound protocols, software used for strain analysis and definitions of weaning success or extubation failure. As a result, the current literature does not yet allow robust conclusions regarding the reproducibility, prognostic performance and clinical applicability of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound in routine critical care practice.26

In this context, speckle-tracking ultrasound may offer significant potential.26 27 Conventional diaphragm ultrasound parameters, such as excursion and thickening fraction, are useful bedside measures. However, their interpretation may be influenced by inspiratory effort, ventilator assistance, respiratory mechanics and technical factors related to image acquisition and measurement conditions.8 28 Conversely, speckle-tracking analysis evaluates longitudinal deformation in the direction of muscle fibre shortening. It can also quantify deformation rate, potentially providing a more direct assessment of diaphragmatic contractile behaviour.26 27 29 Experimental and physiologic studies have shown that strain and strain rate measurements correlate with transdiaphragmatic pressure and diaphragmatic electrical activity. In contrast, conventional thickening fraction may correlate poorly with pressure-based reference measures in mechanically ventilated patients.28 29 This feature is particularly relevant in critically ill patients, in whom apparent diaphragmatic movement does not always correspond to preserved muscle performance.12 29

Another potential contribution of this technique is its ability to complement existing clinical and physiological markers used in weaning assessment.26 27 The decision to extubate is usually based on a combination of respiratory, haemodynamic, neurological and gas-exchange parameters, as well as the spontaneous breathing trial. Nevertheless, extubation failure remains frequent even among patients who complete an SBT.4 Since diaphragmatic dysfunction may persist despite apparently favourable clinical criteria, a more refined assessment of diaphragm function could improve risk stratification in this setting.24 Recent studies suggest that diaphragmatic strain may be independently associated with weaning outcomes and may add discriminatory value when interpreted alongside conventional diaphragm ultrasound and other clinical tools. If confirmed, this would support the incorporation of speckle-tracking ultrasound into a more integrated and individualised approach to ventilator liberation.11 17 18 27

This systematic review is therefore expected to make relevant contributions by synthesising the currently available evidence on the use of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound as a predictor of successful liberation from MV. By summarising study characteristics, technical protocols, outcome definitions and reported associations, the review may help clarify whether the observed promise of this method is consistent across different clinical settings and patient populations. In addition, risk of bias and certainty of evidence evaluations may provide a more balanced interpretation of the literature, distinguishing preliminary enthusiasm from findings sufficiently robust to support clinical adoption. The review may also help identify important knowledge gaps for future research. In particular, it may highlight the need to standardise image acquisition protocols, the choice of hemidiaphragm, the respiratory phase during measurement, strain and strain-rate reporting, software processing methods and cut-off values associated with clinically meaningful outcomes. Likewise, the findings may reinforce the need for larger, prospective, multicentre studies to evaluate reproducibility, interobserver agreement, external validity and the incremental value relative to established weaning predictors. From a clinical perspective, a better understanding of the role of diaphragmatic speckle-tracking ultrasound may contribute to more accurate bedside assessment of diaphragmatic function, improve prediction of weaning outcomes and ultimately support safer and more individualised decisions regarding ventilator liberation.
