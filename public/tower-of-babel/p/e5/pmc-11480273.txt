Demographics, Disease Characteristics, and Time to Effective Treatment of Patients with Psoriasis in the Ghent PsoPlus Cohort of 2021
Authors: Evelyn Meulewaeter, Anke Eylenbosch, Evelien Verhaeghe, Rani Soenen, Jo Lambert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated skin disease with several comorbidities and a considerable influence on quality of life. Many patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis are undertreated and have a substantial disease duration before effective treatment is started. This study analyzed patient and disease characteristics and time to effective treatment of patients with psoriasis who consulted PsoPlus. It also examined whether a treat-to-target (T2T) approach, which is implemented in PsoPlus, has an impact on treatment choice and disease progression.MethodsThrough a single center, retrospective study, 170 patients in the PsoPlus dedicated clinic were compared at moment of enrollment in PsoPlus and at the last recorded consultation in 2021.ResultsMedian disease duration at the first PsoPlus consultation was 16.0 (interquartile range (IQR) 19.0) years. There was a significant difference in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) between the first and the last recorded PsoPlus consultation (PASI 6.0 (IQR 6.4) vs. 0.6 (IQR 2.6); DLQI 11 (IQR 11) vs. 2 (IQR 6); p < 0.001). A weak positive Spearman correlation (rs) was found between disease duration and PASI at the first PsoPlus consultation (rs = 0.175; p = 0.034), while a weak negative correlation (rs = − 0.2; p = 0.013) was found at the last registered PsoPlus consultation. Patients with a disease duration of more than 20 years had significantly more switches of treatment than those with a shorter disease duration (p < 0.001). Median time from psoriasis onset until PASI ≤ 2 was 16.0 years. Median time from the first PsoPlus consultation until PASI ≤ 2 was 7.0 months.ConclusionThe PsoPlus program with its T2T approach effectively improves clinical outcomes and quality of life for patients with psoriasis in a relatively short period, emphasizing the value of a structured, personalized treatment plan for long-term management.Keywords: Psoriasis, Treat to target, Demographics, Disease characteristics

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Many patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis are undertreated and have a substantial disease duration before effective treatment is started.PsoPlus is a specialized psoriasis consultation where patients are treated according to the principles of value-based health care and the treat-to-target (T2T) paradigm.This study analyzes the patient and disease characteristics of the patients enrolled in PsoPlus in 2021 and examines if the T2T approach has an impact on treatment choice and disease progression. What was learned from the study? Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) scores were significantly lower after a year of enrollment in PsoPlus.The specialized T2T approach has a positive impact on disease severity and quality of life.This result can be a driver for other dermatology departments and practices to utilize this approach as well.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated skin disease that affects about 3% of the Belgian population [1–3]. Approximately 20–30% of patients with psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis [4]. Other comorbidities associated with psoriasis are cardiometabolic diseases, psychological illnesses, and inflammatory bowel disease [2, 5, 6]. Knowing the important implications of this disease in the daily life of patients, adequate disease control is of utmost importance.

In the past decade, numerous new biologic treatments targeting proinflammatory cytokines in psoriasis have been developed. As a consequence of all those new treatment options, a 90% improvement (∆PASI 90 response) with respect to baseline Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI) is the new treatment goal in psoriasis [7]. Mahil et al. more recently stated that an absolute PASI ≤ 2 corresponds to a PASI 90 response and it forms a relevant disease end point for treat-to-target (T2T) approaches in psoriasis [8]. At this moment however, there is not one treatment that has proven to be effective in all patients with psoriasis. In 2016, the Belgian DISCOVER study revealed that nearly 40% of patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis are undertreated [9]. Furthermore, patients with psoriasis have a substantial disease duration before initiation of biologic treatment.

Since psoriasis is a chronic illness, patients require lifelong treatment. The efficacy and safety of systemic treatments is mainly investigated in randomized controlled trials. Although clinical studies give us a large insight into the efficacy and most frequent side effects of the developed drug, there is still a good chance that rare side effects or implications that occur as a result of long-lasting treatment are not being registered. An important confounding factor of those clinical studies is the very rigid selection of patients based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria [10, 11]. So, when choosing a treatment option for a specific patient with certain comorbidities, dermatologists still need to fall back on their own knowledge and expertise. Real-world studies and registries can help to build on this knowledge and guide the way to a more personalized treatment with the best benefit/risk ratio for the individual patient.

In 2012, a dedicated PsoPlus consultation was founded in Ghent University Hospital (UZ Ghent). Over the last few years, the format has continuously evolved, with a major change as of 2019 in which Value-Based Health Care (VHBC) was introduced. Prior to attending the PsoPlus clinic, patients are contacted via email 3 weeks beforehand to fill out questionnaires and carry out assignments such as blood and/or urine sampling (in the context of comorbidity screening). These requests are sent out online using PsoQuest, a custom-made General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)-compliant electronic patient platform. When patients attend the hospital, they are first seen by specialty-trained psoriasis nurses who perform the anamnesis and who are in constant contact with dermatologists. The nurses take time to address lifestyle issues and/or psychosocial problems and screen patients for comorbidities. Furthermore, they inquire about the patient’s knowledge, preferences, and beliefs about psoriasis and its treatment options to allow for shared decision-making later on. Afterward, patients are seen by a dermatologist specialized in psoriasis who does the clinical examination, commissions extra testing, assesses comorbidities, prescribes the appropriate treatment regimen, and summarizes the overall disease management approach. The dermatologist decides together with the patient on the ideal treatment, guided by the T2T paradigm. This paradigm consists of nine items which are reported on by both the patient and the dermatologist. These criteria encompass the 12-week response (depending on the type of systemic therapy), PASI 90 response, itch Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) ≤ 10 mm, daily functioning VAS ≤ 10 mm, Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) ≤ 1, comorbidity awareness, absence of adverse events of more than mild severity, full tolerability, and the absence of socially impairing or challenging lesions. If fewer than eight of the nine goals are met, the patient will have a follow-up appointment in 3 months. If eight or all nine goals are achieved, the patient's follow-up will be scheduled for 6 months. This consultation format was created to minimize the chances of overlooking certain aspects of psoriasis management, to provide integrated care and promote a more individualized approach for each patient with psoriasis [12, 13]. Additionally, as a result of the extensive documentation of every consultation, it gives the potential to retrospectively gather information about the demographics, disease course, comorbidities, and treatment course of the patients who enrolled in the PsoPlus consultation.

In this study, we analyze the patient and disease characteristics and overall treatment journey of patients with psoriasis who attended the dedicated PsoPlus consultation in 2021. More specifically, it is of our interest whether the more targeted approach of PsoPlus has an impact on treatment choice and disease progression.

Methods

Retrospective Study

Study Design

This single-center, retrospective study was conducted at the Department of Dermatology of Ghent University Hospital, Belgium. The study design was drafted according to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration of 1964, and its later amendments. The present study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Ghent University Hospital (reference number BC-09420) and all patients provided written informed consent before any of the study procedures were executed.

Patient Population and Eligibility Criteria

Patients were eligible for the study if they were diagnosed with any kind of psoriasis, were 18 years or older, and visited the PsoPlus consultation at Ghent University Hospital between January 1 and December 31, 2021.

Data Collection and Analysis

To visualize the impact of the PsoPlus format on the disease course, disease and treatment characteristics were extracted from the first consultation at time of enrollment in the PsoPlus trajectory and at the last recorded PsoPlus consultation in 2021. The following patient characteristics were collected: age, gender, ethnicity, age at onset of disease, presence of psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, mental health disorders, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, family history of psoriasis, disease duration at enrollment in PsoPlus, disease duration at start of first conventional systemic therapy, and disease duration at start of first biologic therapy. The treatment characteristics focused on systemic treatments before and during PsoPlus follow-up. The first date of use, the date when the therapy was stopped and the reason for discontinuation were extracted from the Electronic Patient File. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Ghent University Hospital [14, 15]. For each variable, descriptive statistics were performed. Frequencies and percentages were used for categoric variables and mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range for quantitative variables. On the basis of the result of normality tests, parametric or non-parametric tests were performed. For all analyses a two-sided 5% level of significance was considered significant. SPSS Statistics 28 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism (V.7.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) were used for the statistical data analysis. A power analysis did not apply for this retrospective study.

Results

In total, 170 patients were enrolled in this retrospective study.

Median age at the time of enrollment in PsoPlus was 47.5 [17.0; 77.0] years, while the median age at the last recorded PsoPlus consultation in 2021 was 49.5 [18.0; 77.0] years. Disease Characteristics

In this cohort, median age at disease onset was 25.0 [2.0–68.0] years, with a median disease duration of 16.0 [0.0; 56.0] years at the first PsoPlus consultation. The vast majority (97.1%) of patients were diagnosed with psoriasis vulgaris. In 22.9% and 20.0% of the subjects, the nails and the body folds were affected, respectively. In 7.6% palmoplantar psoriasis and in 4.7% psoriasis guttata were diagnosed. One patient presented with erythroderma caused by psoriasis and two patients had pustular psoriasis (Table 2).

Comorbidities

At enrollment in PsoPlus, 13.5% of patients were already diagnosed with psoriatic arthritis. Metabolic disorders and cardiovascular disorders were previously detected in 34.1% and 21.2% of patients, respectively. Mental health disorders were present in 26.5%. Almost 3% suffered from inflammatory bowel diseases (Fig. 3).

Comorbidities at the first and last recorded PsoPlus consultation

At the last registered PsoPlus consultation in 2021, 15.4% was diagnosed with psoriatic arthritis and 37.3% of patients had metabolic disorders. Almost one-quarter of patients had cardiovascular and mental health disorders. Inflammatory bowel disease was diagnosed in 3.6% of patients (Fig. 3).

Treatment Journey

In this cohort, 146 of the 170 patients (86.0%) were treated with a systemic therapy (conventional or biologic) at some point in time. Patients suffered from psoriasis for a median of 15 years [0.0; 47.0] before start of conventional systemic treatment and a median of 18 years [2.0; 50.0] before biologic treatment was commenced. Cyclosporine (35.6%) and methotrexate (33.6%) were most commonly used as first conventional systemic treatment, followed by acitretin in 11.6% of the patients. In the 97 patients who were treated with biologics, adalimumab was most commonly the first used agent (18.6%). An overview of first started systemic and biologic treatment can be seen in Fig. 4.

Treatment Shift from First to Last PsoPlus Consultation

At the beginning of the PsoPlus trajectory, most patients (65.3%) had a topical treatment (either in monotherapy or in combination with phototherapy or a systemic treatment). Five subjects received phototherapy. Twenty-one (12.4%) patients had a conventional systemic therapy. At the time of enrollment in PsoPlus, 23 (13.5%) patients were already on a biologic treatment. At the time of the last recorded PsoPlus consultation, 33.1% of the patients was using a topical treatment. Only two patients were treated with phototherapy. Thirty-six (21.1%) patients had a conventional systemic therapy, with cyclosporine and methotrexate as the most frequently prescribed conventional systemics. More than half (55.0%) of the patients were treated with biologics.

Disease Duration and Treatment Switches

The Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant difference (p < 0.001) in the number of treatment switches between groups of disease duration (< 10 years, 10–20 years, > 20 years). Patients with a disease duration of more than 20 years had significantly more switches of treatment than those with a shorter disease duration (Fig. 5).

Time Until Effective Treatment

In 81.3% of the subjects, a PASI ≤ 2 was reached during the PsoPlus follow-up. Median time from psoriasis onset until PASI ≤ 2 was 16 years [1.0; 51.0]. When only patients with PASI > 2 at the enrollment in PsoPlus are considered, median time from the first PsoPlus consultation until PASI ≤ 2 was 7 months [0.0; 80.0] (Fig. 6).

Probability of obtaining Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) ≤ 2 in patients with psoriasis

No significant difference in duration until PASI ≤ 2 was found between male and female subjects (p = 0.132), patients with or without overweight (p = 0.342), smokers, past-smokers and non-smokers (p = 0.675), and patients who were biologic naïve and those who had used at least one prior biologic (p = 0.147). No correlation was found between the time until PASI ≤ 2 and disease duration (p = 0.363).

Discussion

In the absence of a Belgian psoriasis registry, this retrospective study gives an insight into the demographics, disease characteristics, and disease journey of Belgian patients with psoriasis in follow-up in a dedicated PsoPlus consultation at UZ Ghent. Being aware of the existence of several psoriasis registries, such as PSOLAR, the Spanish Biobadaderm, the Dutch Bio-Capture, the Swedish PsoReg, the British-Irish BADBIR, the American CorEvitas psoriasis registry and so on, there is still added value from real-life Belgian data because of differences in health care organization and societal and environmental factors.

Median age at enrollment in PsoPlus was 47.5 years, which is comparable to the age of enrollment in several large registries [10, 16–21]. More than half of the patients in this study were overweight or obese. Similar findings were reported in BADBIR and PsoBest [18, 22]. The American CorEvitas Psoriasis Registry reported a higher percentage of patients that were overweight and obese [16]. This might be a reflection of the overall higher prevalence of overweight and obesity in the USA [23, 24]. A meta-analysis by Armstrong et al. showed that patients with psoriasis are at significantly higher odds of obesity [25]. With this in mind, it is important to note that a high BMI has a negative impact on response to biologic treatments [26, 27]. Several studies showed increased levels of multiple proinflammatory cytokines in the blood of patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis. Systemic elevations in these cytokines promote chronic subclinical inflammation that can cause tissue damage over time and is associated with comorbidities that are frequently seen in patients with psoriasis [28–30]. In 2018, a meta-analysis was conducted by Scotti et al. They concluded that psoriatic arthritis (PsA) was seen in about 20–30% of patients with psoriasis [4]. In our study, however, about 15% were diagnosed with PsA. Possible explanations for that difference might be the rather small study population or that patients with mild arthritis might not report their symptoms [4].

About one-third of patients had metabolic disorders and more than 20% had cardiovascular disorders. Girolomoni et al. hypothesized that early systemic treatment with the goal of complete clearance may not only improve control of the cutaneous symptoms but may also reduce comorbidity progression [31]. Our follow-up time is too short to show any result on progression in this cohort. The higher percentage of comorbidities observed at the final consultation in PsoPlus compared to enrollment likely reflects an increased detection rate due to more proactive screening efforts. This includes routine blood lipid screening, blood pressure measurement, and referrals to specialists such as rheumatologists for joint complaints and gastroenterologists for intestinal issues, ensuring that these comorbidities are identified and managed appropriately. While most comorbidities can be effectively managed with medication, they are often difficult to fully cure, which explains why their prevalence does not decrease post-treatment. An exception is mental health disorders, where we observed some patients achieving full remission after referral to a psychologist, which is reflected in the data. It would be expected that lifestyle interventions and referrals to dietitians would lead to a reduction in metabolic diseases. The fact that we do not see a decrease in metabolic conditions may be due to the follow-up period not being long enough to observe the effects, or it could indicate that more effort is needed to motivate patients to actively engage in these interventions.

While the median disease duration at the first PsoPlus consultation was 16 years, the vast majority of patients were already treated. Most of them had a topical therapy at enrollment. However, given the median PASI of 6 and median DLQI of 11, we conclude that a considerable proportion of patients was undertreated at start of the PsoPlus trajectory. This reflects the results of the Belgian DISCOVER study [9]. Grine et al. defined a multidimensional T2T outcome, with a total of four dimensions: disease control, well-being, burden of treatment, and beyond skin. This approach takes into account physical, mental, physician-reported, and patient-reported outcomes [32]. The Belgian REDISCOVER study found that these Belgian T2T guidelines were only achieved in 51% of patients [33]. By working with the T2T composite score, PsoPlus goes more rapidly to more efficacious treatment options with more than 70% of patients on systemic treatment (conventional or biologic) by the last PsoPlus consultation versus about a quarter of patients at enrollment. This is also reflected in the significant lower median PASI (0.6) and DLQI (2) measured during the last recorded PsoPlus consultation. Notably, PASI ≤ 2 was reached in a median time of 7 months after enrollment in PsoPlus. To put the number of patients treated with biologics into context, it is important to note that in Belgium, biologics for the treatment of psoriasis are only reimbursed if the patient has a PASI > 10 or a body surface area (BSA) involvement > 10%, despite prior treatments including adequate phototherapy, at least 3 months of methotrexate (15 mg/week), and at least 2 months of cyclosporine at a dose of 2.5mg/kg per day. When looking at the percentages of patients who are treated with a specific biologic, it is important to consider the date of market authorization of the biologic in question.

The importance of early intervention is supported by the positive correlation that is seen between disease duration and PASI at the first PsoPlus consultation. As previously suggested by various authors, early intervention may also contribute to reducing the cumulative life course impairment (CLCI) associated with psoriasis [34, 35]. A possible explanation for the negative correlation between disease duration and PASI at the last PsoPlus consultation could be the initiation of systemic treatment in patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis.

It is worth acknowledging that PsoPlus is situated in a tertiary care hospital setting, suggesting that the patients might have had prolonged disease duration upon initial consultation or might have been more resistant to therapy compared to patients at other facilities.

This study has several limitations. The sample of 170 patients from a single tertiary center may not represent all Belgian patients with psoriasis. The retrospective design introduces potential biases, and the specific Belgian reimbursement criteria for biologics may not apply to other countries, affecting the generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

This work illustrates the added value of real-world data and may lead to the formation of a Belgian, multicenter psoriasis registry. The study demonstrates that the PsoPlus program with its T2T approach effectively improves clinical outcomes and quality of life for patients with psoriasis in a relatively short period, emphasizing the value of a structured, personalized treatment plan for long-term management.
