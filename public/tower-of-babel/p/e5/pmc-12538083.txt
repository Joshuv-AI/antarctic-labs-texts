Tuberculosis in the Americas: a worsening crisis amid genomic surveillance gaps
Authors: Richard Steiner Salvato, Katharine S Walter, Ezio Tavora dos Santos-Filho, João Perdigão, Afrânio Lineu Kritski
Published: 2025

In 2023, tuberculosis (TB) returned as the leading infectious disease killer, after falling behind COVID-19 during the pandemic. An estimated 1.25 million lives were lost and 8.2 million new TB cases were reported worldwide, the highest number since the World Health Organization (WHO) began global TB monitoring in 1995.1 These facts reveal the failure to achieve the global commitment made in 2014 through the WHO's End TB Strategy, which set goals of reducing TB incidence by 90% and mortality by 95% by 2035. The WHO Region of the Americas has shown concerning trends. Between 2015 and 2023, TB incidence in the region increased by 20%, and mortality surged by 44%, the highest increases across all WHO regions. Several Latin American countries reported incidence rates increasing by 30% compared to 2015, a serious setback to the End TB Strategy.1

Despite being preventable and curable, TB remains a major health threat, driven by poverty, inequality, and political neglect. Contributing factors include limited primary health coverage, delayed diagnosis, lack of support to ensure treatment adherence, and rising drug resistance. New drugs, including bedaquiline, linezolid, and pretomanid, were recently introduced for rifampicin-resistant TB,2 after decades without significant innovation. However, the absence of robust surveillance systems and drug susceptibility testing to monitor resistance puts their long-term effectiveness at risk. This highlights the urgent need for new strategies and timely action to prevent infections and further loss of life.

The COVID-19 pandemic spurred investment in genomic surveillance and Next-Generation Sequencing globally, including many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Sequencing infrastructure has since been crucial for other viral epidemics, including recent dengue and Oropouche outbreaks across Latin America and the Caribbean.3,4 But, despite recent alarming TB trends, coordinated regional genomic surveillance programmes for the disease remain nonexistent. Such surveillance is essential to understanding transmission dynamics, identifying high-risk populations such as incarcerated populations, monitoring migration-driven gene flow, supporting molecular resistance diagnostics, and tracking drug-resistant strains. Regional genomic data could guide targeted public health interventions.5 Currently, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis) genomic surveillance is conducted by isolated efforts across the Americas. Even so, these initiatives have provided critical insights for improving disease control, including the emergence and spread of highly multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains,6 high MDR-TB transmission rates,7 spread of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) TB,8 and the role of prisons in disseminating TB into surrounding communities.9

Expanding TB genomic surveillance presents specific challenges compared to viral pathogens. Most genomic studies of M. tuberculosis rely on culturing the bacteria from patients' sputum to generate sufficient DNA for sequencing. Bacterial culture can take several weeks and requires laboratory infrastructure. Additionally, the M. tuberculosis genome is larger than viral genomes. However, alternatives have emerged, such as direct capture of M. tuberculosis DNA from complex clinical samples. In the past year, new methods have been developed for M. tuberculosis amplicon sequencing, enabling sequencing directly from clinical samples, using an approach widely applied for SARS-CoV-2 sequencing.10 But the true barriers are not technological; they are political and economic. Despite its burden, TB remains neglected. Brazil, one of the top 30 high-burden TB nations, despite having invested in infrastructure for genomic surveillance of other pathogens in recent years, lacks a national strategy for TB genomic surveillance. However, just as important and urgent as preparing for health threats driven by climate change is the fight against a disease that kills hundreds of thousands in the Americas each year and is worsening, disproportionately affecting the most vulnerable (Box 1).

Implementing genomic surveillance at scale requires a public and private coordinated and multisectoral action. International health organizations need to lead efforts to support the establishment of regional sequencing hubs and offer technical support to expand local capacity in LMICs. Equally important, strengthening local capacities to reach people with the disease and provide support to guarantee continuity of treatment is essential in this scenario. National health systems need to identify and fund reference laboratories, standardize sequencing protocols, and ensure that genomic data–and metadata–are integrated into public health information systems. The scientific community must generate empirical evidence to inform cost-effective sampling strategies and guide practical implementation. Crucially, policymakers and governments must commit to sustained funding and legislating policies that support integrated surveillance systems coordinated by public health priorities, in permanent coordination with civil society. Without urgent and comprehensive investment, the region will continue to fall further behind the commitments made a decade ago. The tools and technologies to confront it exist, and it is urgent that we advocate for their effective implementation now.
