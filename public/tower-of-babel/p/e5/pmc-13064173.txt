Perforated ulcer: patient education series understanding trauma and emergency surgery conditions
Authors: Katherine Marie Kelley, Shannon Marie Foster
Published: 2026

Background

Location

A perforated ulcer is a hole in the stomach or in the first part of the small intestine (duodenum).

Function

Ulcers start as small areas of irritation in the stomach and small bowel lining that over time can tear into a hole. These are often caused by an infection with a certain type of bacteria (Helicobacter pylori or H. pylori). Irritation can also be caused by non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or other medications such as steroids used regularly. The most common NSAIDs known to cause ulcers are ibuprofen, naproxen and aspirin. Ulcers can also happen when patients are very sick and in the intensive care unit from something else.

Symptoms

The patient may only have mild upper abdominal pain or pain that comes and goes when an ulcer first develops. An ulcer may feel like a burning feeling in the chest and stomach, sour taste in the mouth, nausea, or stomach cramps. These symptoms are more likely when the stomach is empty—in between meals—as food can coat and soothe the areas of irritation. If the ulcer makes a hole (perforates), the pain becomes severe and can spread to the entire abdomen. Many patients can say exactly when the pain got worse because it is sharp, sudden, and very different from the usual discomfort.

What happens in the hospital

Diagnosis

The story, symptoms, and physical examination will usually give clues to the diagnosis. X-rays or other imaging scans are often ordered when an ulcer is getting worse. Perforated ulcers can usually be diagnosed by how they look on imaging scans. Also, blood will be taken for laboratory studies which help to see how sick the body has become due to the perforation.

Medical (non-surgical) options

A perforated ulcer usually needs surgery. Doctors often find air outside the stomach on X-rays or CT scans, which is a sign of this problem. Sometimes, the body seals the hole on its own, and surgery is not needed. But most of the time, the hole keeps leaking, letting food and stomach acid spill into the belly. This can cause pain and serious infections.

How sick a person gets depends on how long the hole has been there, how much leaked out and if they have other health problems. Older people and those with weak immune systems can get even sicker. Some people get so sick that their blood pressure drops, and they need strong medicine to keep it up. They might also need a breathing tube and care in the intensive care unit.

No matter what, treatment always includes antibiotics and stopping food from passing through the stomach. This is done by not eating or by using a tube in the nose to drain the stomach. Ulcers also need treatment with antacids. While in the hospital, these are usually given through the veins, but they can also be given as pills. Sometimes, gastroenterologists or stomach doctors will need to take a look at the ulcer from inside the stomach using a special camera. This procedure is called an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) and involves a camera going in through the mouth into the intestines.

Surgical options

Surgery for a perforated ulcer can be done with either multiple small incisions (ie, minimally invasive or laparoscopically) or one large incision (ie, open or laparotomy). Laparoscopic surgery involves small incisions and cameras in order to perform the operation. Some surgeons will use a robot to help with the operation. Open surgery uses a vertical incision in the middle of the abdomen. For both operations, patients are asleep with a breathing tube in place during the operation under general anesthesia. During surgery, the hole is located and fixed. The hole is either sewn up with stitches or patched with a piece of tissue close to the ulcer, like omentum (a layer of fat inside the abdomen). Fixing the ulcer with an omental patch is the most common and may be called a Graham patch by the surgeon and providers. Sometimes, the hole is so large or in a location not easy to reach that it requires a portion of the stomach or intestines to be removed and then rebuilt. Tubes and drains may be placed in the abdomen, inside the stomach or inside the intestines to help with healing and prevent infections. Be sure to ask procedure details directly from the surgeon as the healing time and recovery plan is very different based on which combination of surgery, tubes, and drains are needed to fix the ulcer.

Care after treatment

A tube going through the nose down to the stomach (ie, nasogastric tube (NG) tube) is generally left in place for 3–5 days after surgery. An NG tube is used to suction out any stomach contents. This tube is not comfortable but is very important to let the hole in the intestines heal. Prior to the removal of the tube, an additional imaging X-ray or CT scan may be obtained to make sure the intestines have healed. Once the tube is removed, patients are usually eating and drinking. Once patients are eating normally, they can go home. This process typically takes a week from arrival at the hospital to going home but can sometimes take longer. Follow-up visits with other medical teams are often needed. These may include visits with a primary care provider, surgeons, or gastroenterologists to monitor the ulcer for any signs of healing and to prevent recurrence.

Possible complications

Sometimes, the hole takes longer to heal and is still leaking when imaging is obtained after surgery. This is generally managed with a drain to direct the leak outside the body. There will also be a longer period without eating. The drain may already have been left from surgery or may be placed during a separate procedure using CT scan pictures and a needle by the radiologist.

Other complications can happen just from being sick and in the hospital. A patient could develop an infection such as a urinary tract (bladder) infection or pneumonia in the lungs. A blood clot (DVT) may also develop, most often in the legs, from inactivity and swelling. One of the best ways to prevent both infections and clots is to get out of bed multiple times every day. All patients will also get injections to prevent blood clots if not taking oral blood thinning medications.

What happens next

Discharge location

Where a patient goes after being discharged from the hospital is based on a variety of factors, including any active medical issues and the overall status of the patient at the time of admission. This will depend on the patient’s health prior to admission and need for assistance. Physical therapists and a team of social work and case managers help us make the decision of where patients can go after leaving the hospital.

Wound care

Laparoscopic incisions are usually closed with dissolvable sutures under the skin and either glue or bandages on the outside that fall off on their own in 7–10 days. Larger incisions may also be closed with sutures under the skin but are often closed with staples. Most incisions can get wet 24–48 hours after surgery, but they should avoid being submerged underwater for the first few weeks, and this plan should be discussed with the surgical team.

Drain care

If a patient has a drain in the abdomen, the site will need to be kept clean, and the drain collection bulb or bag will need to be emptied regularly. In the hospital, this is done by the nurse, but at home, it can be done by the patient or a family member. When the drain is emptied, the fluid has to be measured and recorded to help the doctors know when it is safe to be removed. Before going home, the patient and/or their family members should be taught how to take care of the drain and how to monitor it.

Diet

While in the hospital, there is a period of not being allowed to eat, but by the time patients are discharged home, they can eat normally, usually with no new restrictions.

Medications after discharge

Patients tested and found to have H. pylori as the cause will need to take antibiotics after discharge for another 2–4 weeks. Additionally, they will be medications that reduce stomach acid levels. Patients who have had ulcers should avoid taking NSAIDs for the rest of their life.

Activity

After surgery, a patient should avoid heavy lifting, pushing, pulling, or straining for 2–6 weeks depending on the operation performed; otherwise, they can return to normal baseline activity.
