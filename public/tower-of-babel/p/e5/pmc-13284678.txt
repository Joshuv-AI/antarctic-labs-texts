Identification of patients receiving amyloid‐targeting therapies in observational studies using amyloid PET trajectories: Insights from LEADS
Authors: Renaud La Joie, Ganna Blazhenets, Piyush Maiti, Konstantinos Chiotis, Ani Eloyan, Kala Kirby, Dustin Hammers, Robert A. Koeppe, Sarah F. Ackley, Justin Robison, David N. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAs amyloid‐targeting therapies (ATTs) enter clinical care, observational cohorts must accurately ascertain ATT exposure. We developed an approach to flag mis/undocumented ATT in the Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study using longitudinal amyloid positron emission tomography (PET).METHODSWe analyzed 742 [18F]florbetaben PET from 270 participants with early‐onset Alzheimer's disease. Using PET acquired before US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of ATTs, we quantified Centiloid (CL) variability between two consecutive scans (PET “segments”) to determine unusual CL decline thresholds that were then applied to post‐FDA approval segments.RESULTSPre‐FDA approval segments increased by a median of 4.1 CL/year (whole cerebellum reference) and 3.6 CL/year (composite reference); unusual CL decline thresholds, defined as mean – 2 standard deviations of the pre‐approval distributions were −15.8 and −9.4 CL/year, respectively. When applied to segments acquired post‐FDA approval, extreme declines were observed in 59% to 78% of treated (n = 54) versus 4% to 8% of untreated segments (n = 344).DISCUSSIONLongitudinal amyloid PET analyses can help identify ATT exposure in observational studies.

BACKGROUND

As amyloid‐targeting therapies (ATTs) move from clinical trials to clinical practice, 1 , 2 real‐world evidence on their effects will increasingly be generated through clinical registries, electronic health records, and longitudinal observational studies. 3 , 4 These cohorts are valuable because they typically include deep phenotyping with standardized cognitive evaluations and multimodal biomarkers over longer durations than randomized clinical trials. 5 , 6 , 7 Yet observational studies must now reliably identify who is receiving ATT and when, which might be particularly challenging when ATT is managed in the clinic, outside the study.

Some cohorts may then exclude treated participants to preserve a natural‐history sample, whereas others may continue follow‐up and model ATT exposure as a subgroup or time‐varying covariate. Either way, accurate ascertainment of ATT initiation, dose, and discontinuation is essential: missing or incorrect treatment information can introduce substantial bias in estimates of clinical or biomarker progression. These risks are heightened in large multisite cohorts in which treatment‐related information is captured inconsistently across sites, or when ATT documentation lags behind data analysis.

The Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study (LEADS 8 ) is a multisite observational study initiated in 2018, before regulatory approval of ATTs by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA); Figure 1A. After FDA approval, LEADS investigators continued following participants who receive ATTs as part of routine clinical care and allowed participants already receiving ATT to enroll in LEADS. Importantly, ATT delivery is managed by treating clinicians who are not necessarily LEADS investigators and operate across multiple health systems. To monitor ATT use, LEADS sites have been asked to document ATT in the electronic data capture (EDC) system at each annual visit using a dedicated form. In parallel, the LEADS positron emission tomography (PET) core prospectively quantifies amyloid PET using standardized procedures. 9 Because approved ATTs markedly reduce amyloid PET signal, 10 longitudinal amyloid PET provides a biomarker‐based method to flag potential ATT exposure independent of EDC records. Over the last couple of years, prospective PET adjudication has identified a dozen participants whose amyloid PET trajectories suggested undocumented or misclassified ATT use, prompting verification with sites and correction of EDC records (Figure 1B).

The present article summarizes the procedures we have developed to improve ATT ascertainment in LEADS based on measured changes in Centiloids (CL), a global metric of amyloid PET. 11 Our approach relies on the assumption that in the natural history of Alzheimer's disease (AD), true amyloid accumulation follows a monotonic trajectory: it does not spontaneously decrease over time. However, change measured between two consecutive PETs reflects true accumulation (≈ 3 CL/year 12 , 13 ) plus measurement error due to test–retest variability and disease progression‐related artifacts (atrophy, changes in blood flow), occasionally producing small declines in CLs. We aimed to define a threshold for CL change that exceeds measurement error between consecutive scans and likely reflects treatment‐related amyloid clearance (TRAC 10 ). We derived these unusual CL decline thresholds using LEADS amyloid PET trajectories acquired prior to clinical availability of ATT and then evaluated their performance in data acquired after FDA approval.

METHODS

LEADS

LEADS is a multisite longitudinal study of early‐onset AD that was initiated in 2018 and has since recruited participants from 18 sites across the United States. 8 Briefly, LEADS enrolls cognitively unimpaired and cognitively impaired participants aged 40 to 64 years. Impaired participants must meet 2011 National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) criteria for mild cognitive impairment due to AD 14 or mild dementia due to AD 15 and have a global Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR 16 ) score ≤ 1 at screening. Because LEADS focuses on sporadic early‐onset AD (EOAD), individuals with pathogenic mutations associated with autosomal‐dominant AD or frontotemporal lobar degeneration are excluded. 17

All participants undergo amyloid PET at baseline, and impaired participants are classified as EOAD (amyloid PET‐positive) or early‐onset non‐AD (EOnonAD; amyloid PET‐negative) based on centralized quantitative‐aided visual interpretation. 9 As we focus on patients receiving ATT, we only included EOAD (amyloid‐positive) participants. Per study design, EOAD participants receive structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and amyloid PET at screening and at months 12, 24, and 36.

This study was approved by the Indiana University Institutional Review Board. Participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, US federal regulations, local state laws and regulations, and institutional policies.

Disease‐modifying therapies

Since the initiation of LEADS, participants have not been allowed to enroll or remain in the study if participating in a randomized clinical trial. However, as ATT received FDA approval, participants have been permitted to remain enrolled while receiving an approved ATT as part of their clinical care. Importantly, treatment decisions are made by participants’ treating clinicians and may occur outside LEADS sites. ATT exposure (agent, initiation date, discontinuation/interruption dates) is recorded at each annual visit in the LEADS EDC system using a dedicated anti‐amyloid therapy form implemented in July 2022.

Neuroimaging

Image harmonization, acquisition, and processing have been described in detail previously; they are harmonized with other large‐scale studies of aging and AD. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Briefly, we acquire T1‐weighted MRI on 3T scanners for volumetric analyses and to guide PET processing. Amyloid PET is acquired using [18F]florbetaben (20‐minute acquisition starting 90 minutes after ≈ 300 MBq injection). Images are reconstructed as four 5‐minute frames and uploaded to the Laboratory of Neuroimaging (LONI). PET scans then undergo centralized quality control and preprocessing.

Injected dose, timing of acquisition, attenuation correction, reconstruction parameters, and imaging artifacts are assessed by the PET core at the University of Michigan. Scans then undergo preprocessing including frame realignment, averaging, and differential smoothing to harmonize effective resolution to a ≈ 6 mm full width at half maximum. Preprocessed scans are then transferred to the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF) PET core through LONI.

At UCSF, MRIs are processed using FreeSurfer 7.1 to obtain a parcellation of the brain into regions of interest (ROIs) based on the Desikan–Killiany atlas. 22 FreeSurfer segmentations are visually inspected and manually edited when necessary. [18F]florbetaben images are co‐registered to the corresponding MRI to be aligned with FreeSurfer‐defined ROIs. Mean PET signal is extracted from a large neocortical target region (frontal, cingulate, parietal, and lateral temporal cortices) and two reference regions: the whole cerebellum and a composite region (whole cerebellum, brainstem, and eroded supratentorial white matter). Previous investigations have shown that the latter outperforms the whole cerebellum when measuring within‐subject longitudinal changes. 23 , 24 Two standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) values are calculated for each scan using the neocortical target region as the numerator and either reference as the denominator. SUVR values are converted to CL using reference‐specific SUVR‐to‐CL equations. 9 , 25

Statistical approaches

Participants and scan selection

Analyses were based on the LEADS PET core quarterly report finalized in December 2025. This report includes all [18F]florbetaben scans acquired through September 30, 2025 that were preprocessed, quality controlled, and quantified by December 2025 (n = 1156 scans from 653 individuals).

After excluding the unimpaired group (n = 96 individuals with one scan each), the EOnonAD group (n = 158 scans from 127 individuals), and EOAD participants with only one scan (n = 160), the final analytic sample included n = 742 [18F]florbetaben scans from 270 participants with EOAD: n = 124 had exactly two scans, n = 91 had exactly three scans, n = 54 had exactly four scans, and n = 1 had five scans.

Amyloid PET “segments”

In this article, a “segment” is defined as the interval between two consecutive amyloid PETs. Participants with n scans contributed n – 1 segments to the dataset; see Figure 2. Segments were first split based on acquisition date.

Approach to classify PET segments based on exposure to and timing of ATT. Each row represents a hypothetical patient to illustrate how different segments were assigned to the untreated versus treated category. ATT, amyloid‐targeting therapy; EDC, electronic data capture; LEADS, Longitudinal Early‐Onset Alzheimer's Disease Study; PET, positron emission tomography.

All segments ending prior to the FDA accelerated approval of aducanumab were assumed to be unaffected by ATT. They served as a gold standard to characterize within‐subject CL variability calculated with our pipeline from consecutive [18F]florbetaben scans in the natural history of EOAD.

Segments ending after July 2021 were considered potentially impacted by ATT, and the EDC anti‐amyloid form was used to classify them as treated or untreated. A segment was considered “untreated” if no ATT was recorded at any time between the two scans (Figure 2A–C). A segment was considered “treated” if any ATT was recorded between the two scans. This definition encompasses several situations (Figure 2D‐G): some segments reflect continuous treatment across the entire interval (Figure 2D,E), while others include treatment initiation or discontinuation between scans (Figure 2E‐G). One segment met the definition for “untreated” but occurred after ATT discontinuation (Figure 2G); it was excluded from analyses comparing treated versus untreated segments because it no longer represents a treatment‐naïve state. Last, some participants started ATT after their last available scan and therefore only contributed untreated segments to the current dataset (Figure 2H).

For each segment, we calculated the duration between the two PETs (“segment duration,” in days). For treated segments, we additionally extracted the number of days during which ATT exposure was recorded within that segment (“ATT days”) to quantify ATT exposure during the PET interval and test the hypothesis that longer exposure (higher “ATT days”) would be associated with greater CL decreases. If ATT was initiated before the first PET of the segment and not discontinued during the segment, then “ATT days” = “segment duration.” If ATT was initiated and/or terminated between the two scans, then “ATT days” < “segment duration.”

Note that EDC records used in this article were extracted in March 2026, after corrections prompted by prospective PET core quality control had been incorporated (see Figure 1B for examples).

Longitudinal amyloid PET analyses

For each segment, we calculated the annualized change in CLs between the two [18F]florbetaben scans using either reference region. We first described the distribution of annualized CL change before the FDA approval of aducanumab to isolate longitudinal PET segments without having to rely on the accuracy of EDC records. We derived unusual CL decline thresholds from pre‐approval segments as mean − 2 standard deviations (SD) of annualized change. We then evaluated these thresholds in the PET segments acquired after the FDA approval of ATTs to test their ability to identify ATT exposure as captured in the EDC. Agreement between classification of post‐approval segments as abnormal based on whole cerebellum versus composite reference region was quantified using Cohen's kappa. Additional analyses were conducted in treated segments to assess the relationship between either ATT exposure (measured as ATT days) or baseline CLs and change in CLs.

RESULTS

Dataset

The dataset included 742 [18F]florbetaben PET from 270 cognitively impaired participants with EOAD (amyloid PET‐positive at screening). At baseline, participants were 59 ± 4 years old and 53% (n = 143) were women. Mean Mini‐mental State Examination was 21.9 ± 4.9 and mean CDR Sum of Boxes score was 3.7 ± 1.8; 37% (n = 96/261 with available data) had a global CDR = 1.

Each participant had between two and five amyloid PETs, yielding 472 segments (mean duration = 445 ± 123 days). Figure 3 shows CL trajectories for all EOAD patients with at least two scans acquired between August 2018 and September 2025.

CL variability before and after ATT approval

Segments were split into two groups relative to US regulatory approval of ATT. Prior to aducanumab approval, n = 73 segments from 66 participants were completed (i.e., both scans acquired before July 2021; duration = 464 ± 112 days). In contrast, 399 segments from 237 participants were completed after ATT approval (duration = 442 ± 124 days), meaning that at least the second scan defining the segment was acquired after July 2021.

Regardless of reference region, substantial CL declines were observed in a subset of segments ending after ATT approval, especially after 2023 (Figure 3). Using the whole cerebellum reference, segments increased by a median of 4.1 CL/year prior to approval (Q1–Q3 = −0.1 to 13.8) and had a median change of 0.0 CL/year after approval (Q1–Q3 = −8.6 to 7.6). Using the composite reference region, segments increased by 3.6 CL/year before approval (Q1–Q3 = −0.1 to 7.6) and by 1.8 CL/year after approval (Q1–Q3 = −3.8 to 5.6).

Unusual CL decline thresholds, defined as mean − 2 SD of the annualized CL change observed in pre‐approval segments were −15.8 CL/year (whole cerebellum reference) and −9.4 CL/year (composite reference); see dashed lines in Figure 3.

Detecting ATT from longitudinal PET in post‐FDA approval segments

Of the 399 post‐approval segments, n = 54 (14%) were classified as treated per EDC, n = 344 as untreated per EDC, and n = 1 was excluded (see Methods and Figure 2G). Mean segment duration was 416 ± 102 days for treated segments and 447 ± 127 days for untreated post‐approval segments. Based on EDC records, most treated segments involved lecanemab (n = 43 segments; 34 participants), with others receiving aducanumab (n = 8 segments; 5 participants), donanemab (n = 1 segment), or switching from aducanumab to lecanemab within the same segment (n = 2 segments; 2 participants).

Annualized CL change differed markedly between untreated and treated segments (Figure 4A). Untreated segments showed modest increases (median change = 2.1 CL/year with whole cerebellum and 2.6 CL/year with composite reference), whereas treated segments showed pronounced declines (median change = –25.0 and –24.5 CL/year, respectively). Annualized changes derived from the two reference regions were strongly correlated (r = 0.88; Figure 4B). Even though statistical comparison of different monoclonal antibodies was not possible due to small numbers in non‐lecanemab groups, data are presented in Figure 4C–D.

Using the unusual CL decline threshold based on pre‐approval segments for whole cerebellum‐normalized quantification (−15.8 CL/year), 26/344 (8%) untreated segments showed abnormal amyloid PET decline compared to 32/54 (59%) treated segments (odds ratio = 17.8, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 9.1–34.9). Using composite reference‐based CL values, 13/344 (4%) untreated and 42/54 (78%) treated segments exceeded the −9.4 CL/year threshold (odds ratio = 89.1, 95% CI = 38.2–208.0).

Binary classification of post‐approval segments based on the two CL thresholds agreed for 369/398 (93%) post‐approval segments (κ = 0.70): 42 (11%) segments were classified as abnormal by both references, 327 (82%) as normal by both, 13 (3%) as abnormal only with the composite reference, and 16 (4%) as abnormal only with the whole cerebellum reference.

Post hoc analyses were conducted to investigate the misclassified segments. First, we analyzed treated segments to understand why some of them did not show substantial CL decline (22%–41% depending on the reference). As segments were classified as treated even when participants received ATT for a small portion of that time window (Figure 2), we hypothesized that treated segments showing no clear CL change were these segments with minimal ATT exposure. Figure 5A confirms that most segments with short ATT exposure (< 100 days) did not show significant CL decrease. However, several segments with substantial treatment exposure (> 300 days) did not show much amyloid PET decline (see right top corner of each scatter plot in Figure 5A). Additional investigation showed that most, but not all, of these segments had relatively low baseline CL, likely reflecting a floor effect (Figure 5B).

ATT days correspond to the number of days, during each segment, when each patient was considered to be receiving ATT based on the information available in the EDC. Last, we investigated the post‐approval segments labeled as untreated in the EDC that showed significant amyloid PET decline. Notably, two outlier segments showed major decline with a > 50 CL/year decline with whole cerebellum and > 20 CL/year with the composite reference. Repeated communication with the clinical site confirmed that the two corresponding participants never received ATT, and inspection of the PETs and records could not identify any major artifact in acquisition, reconstruction, or preprocessing that could account for such erroneous quantification. Inspection of the MRI did not show any particularly unusual anatomical changes (Figure S1 in supporting information) and re‐processing of the PET scans using an independent MRI‐free pipeline showed the same patterns of changes, emphasizing that the unusual CL trajectories were not a processing artifact (Table S1 in supporting information). For one participant, the identified outlier segment was the only available segment (Figure 6A). Note that this participant showed very rapid clinical progression between the two PET scans (CDR global score changed from 1 to 3 and Sum of Boxes score changed from 8 to 18), with substantial weight loss. The second participant underwent three scans; abnormal CL decrease was observed between the first two scans but CL values later increased, almost returning to baseline levels (Figure 6B). For both participants, the major CL drop reflected a global decrease in cerebral PET signal, including both gray and white matter, which explained why CL changes were more dramatic with the cerebellar than the composite reference (see outliers in Figure 4B). In contrast, true TRAC was mainly associated with decline of PET signal in the gray, not white, matter (see Figure 6C‐D), leading to CL changes that were comparable across reference regions (see red dots around the identity line in Figure 4B).

DISCUSSION

LEADS expanded from being an observational study of the natural history of EOAD to retaining and including patients receiving recently approved ATTs. This transition gives us a unique opportunity to study the impact of ATT on clinical and biomarker trajectories, but it comes with the need to rigorously monitor ATT in all participants. Although we started recording ATT exposure in the EDC in July 2022, our continuous analysis of amyloid PET led us to identify cases with missing or incorrect ATT information (Figure 1B), prompting us to establish standardized approaches to detect mis/unlabeled cases. Here, we described the development of a PET‐based procedure to prospectively flag ATT‐like trajectories and prompt verification of ATT records by leveraging the legacy LEADS PET dataset. Using segments (intervals between two consecutive amyloid PETs) completed before FDA approval of ATTs to estimate measurement variability associated with the natural disease history, we derived thresholds for annualized CL changes that are unlikely to occur without active amyloid removal. When applied to post‐approval segments, these unusual CL decline thresholds captured most segments recorded as treated (59%–78%) while only flagging a small proportion of segments recorded as untreated (4%–8%), supporting their utility as a high‐specificity quality‐control screen. As longitudinal amyloid PETs continue to be acquired in LEADS, extreme declines on amyloid PET will systematically trigger targeted queries to sites to investigate treatment status, clarify start/stop dates, and correct EDC entries. This PET‐based flag is intended to complement standard EDC records rather than replace clinical documentation.

In ATT‐naïve participants, longitudinal amyloid PET showed some variability reflecting measurement error, with some individuals showing slightly negative slopes in the absence of treatment. The recent TRAC framework 10 highlighted the need for a precise quantification of this variability to enable detection of PET changes that reliably reflect treatment effects. In LEADS, the magnitude of these non–ATT‐related CL declines was generally minor, apart from a couple of outliers.

Our PET‐based ATT detection approach had relatively low (59%–78%) sensitivity to detect segments during which participants received ATTs. Post hoc analyses highlighted that short exposure to ATT may not cross the unusual CL decline thresholds, leading to false negatives, that is, treated patients not showing clear amyloid PET decline. In addition, our approach cannot be used to flag ATT for patients who have low baseline amyloid PET levels, notably those that have already reached full TRAC, 10 reflecting a floor effect. Alternatively, it is possible that some of these false negative cases reflected limited biological response to ATT. However, it should be noted that our main analyses treated ATT exposure as a binary (yes/no) variable, limiting our ability to model dose–response relationships. Detailed information on titration changes and on potential interruption, restart, or discontinuation of infusions might help understand all the cases documented as treated who did not show clear CL decline. It should also be noted that ATT in LEADS predominantly reflected clinical use of lecanemab in the 2023 to 2025 time window. As newer ATTs with more rapid amyloid‐lowering effects become available, 26 substantial reductions in CL values may be detectable within a few weeks, enabling earlier and potentially more reliable detection of participants exposed to ATT.

Consistent with previous publications, 23 , 24 including supratentorial white matter in the reference region helped reduce longitudinal measurement variability, leading to a less negative threshold (−9.4 CL) compared to the whole cerebellar reference (−15.8 CL). The composite reference also yielded better detection of treated segments (78% vs. 59% sensitivity) without compromising on specificity (4% vs. 8%).

Importantly, the unusual CL decline thresholds we reported cannot be directly transferred to other settings because CL transformation harmonizes the dynamic range across tracers and pipelines, but it does not account for differences in measurement error/noise across PET quantification methods. 13 , 24 Thresholds we reported are specific to the tracer ([18F]florbetaben) and the standardized acquisition, reconstruction procedures, and processing pipeline used in LEADS. Different tracers, reference regions, reconstruction parameters, or image resolution may yield different levels of intrinsic variability, therefore requiring study‐specific threshold derivation. 27 , 28 It is also plausible that changes in radiotracer, time of acquisition, 29 scanner, or reconstruction parameters 28 , 30 , 31 between scans, which could likely happen in real‐world settings, could substantially increase measurement error.

Another limitation to the generalizability of our findings is that our study specifically enrolled patients with EOAD. Patients with late‐onset AD (LOAD) might show different levels of measurement error and/or treatment effects on amyloid PET, affecting the ability of our approach to flag unlabeled participants receiving ATT. Comparison of LEADS participants to patients from the older Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort revealed that patients with EOAD harbor greater amyloid load than patients with LOAD, 32 which might have an impact on accumulation rates and measurement error. In addition, LOAD is associated with more numerous co‐existing neuropathological diagnoses (i.e., mixed pathologies) than EOAD, 33 , 34 and one could hypothesize that these co‐pathologies, notably cerebrovascular lesions, impact TRAC. Future work combining LEADS and ADNI should test these hypotheses.

We acknowledge that longitudinal amyloid PET is not available in many real‐world datasets or in many observational studies of AD. In cohorts without repeated PET, similar quality‐control concepts may be explored using other biomarkers (e.g., plasma or cerebrospinal fluid measures associated with brain amyloid), but effect sizes and measurement error will differ substantially from PET, 10 , 35 , 36 and thresholds would need to be carefully developed and validated. The broader implication is that in this post ATT‐approval era, observational studies should anticipate some degree of treatment misclassification and consider analytic strategies that are robust for imperfect exposure ascertainment. 37 When ATT exposure is a key covariate, sensitivity analyses and quantitative bias analysis can help evaluate how misclassification might affect estimated associations with clinical or biomarker outcomes. 38 , 39 , 40 Prospective biomarker‐based flags, such as the method described here, aim to reduce misclassification at the source.

By sharing these early lessons from ATT use and tracking in LEADS, we aimed to help inform best practices for other longitudinal studies seeking to generate robust real‐world evidence on the clinical implementation, safety, and efficacy of ATT. Our proposed approach is not meant to replace careful clinical monitoring of ATT use but to add an independent layer of quality control to improve rigorous data collection in ongoing observational studies of AD that involve, or might involve, unlabeled participants undergoing ATT treatment.
