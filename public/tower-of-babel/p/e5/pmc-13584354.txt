Multiparametric functional MRI for detection of early renal allograft dysfunction after kidney transplantation: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yi-Zhu Jiang, Rui Wang, Tao Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney transplantation is the gold-standard renal-replacement therapy for end-stage kidney disease, yet delayed graft function affects 5–50% of recipients and predicts chronic allograft dysfunction. Introduction

Kidney transplantation has evolved into the gold-standard renal-replacement therapy for most patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), yet demand continues to outpace supply. Delayed graft function (DGF), a well-known complication in the immediate post-transplant period, affects 5–50% of deceased-donor kidney transplants and serves as both an outcome and a predictor of increased risk for chronic allograft dysfunction and graft loss [1–3]. Even in the absence of DGF by conventional definitions, a substantial proportion of recipients exhibit suboptimal early graft function, often progressing to unrecognized chronic damage [2, 3]. Detecting graft dysfunction at this subclinical stage before irreversible nephron loss is critical; early recognition enables prompt interventions (e.g., volume optimization, immunosuppressive adjustment, antioxidants, and vasoactive therapy).

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), typically derived from serum creatinine, is the most commonly used index for monitoring allograft function, but it lacks sensitivity during the acute phase. Serum creatinine typically remains within the normal range until roughly 50% of nephrons are lost, indicating that eGFR is incapable of reflecting the kidney’s compensatory capacity in terms of renal functional reserve [4, 5]. This temporal lag stems from creatinine’s dependence on muscle mass, tubular secretion, and extrarenal elimination, which collectively blunt its responsiveness to rapid hemodynamic changes. Moreover, eGFR reflects accumulated damage rather than real-time pathophysiological changes, rendering it inadequate for detecting subclinical ischemia or early inflammatory injury. Although kidney biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing specific pathological entities, its invasiveness, together with the risks of bleeding, infection, and sampling error due to the focal and heterogeneous distribution of allograft pathology (reported discordance rates of 25–35% between multiple core samples from the same kidney) [6], restricts its routine clinical use [7]. These limitations have driven the search for non-invasive, repeatable, and functionally sensitive imaging biomarkers that can capture dynamic allograft alterations before irreversible injury ensues.

In this context, advanced functional imaging modalities have emerged as promising alternatives. Molecular imaging techniques, such as [18F] FDG PET/CT, enable specific detection of acute allograft rejection by visualizing inflammatory cell infiltration and metabolic activity [8]. However, radiation exposure, limited availability, and high costs constrain its serial application in routine post-transplant surveillance. Doppler ultrasound provides a widely accessible, non-invasive means of measuring renal resistive index (RI); elevated RI has been validated as an independent predictor of progressive graft dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular events [9]. Nevertheless, ultrasound’s principal strength lies in identifying macroscopic structural abnormalities such as cysts, hydronephrosis, or renal-artery stenosis rather than subtle functional or microstructural impairment at the tissue level. Furthermore, RI measurements are operator-dependent and influenced by extrarenal factors including heart rate, blood pressure, and vessel compliance, limiting their precision for early parenchymal injury detection. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has also been increasingly used as an adjunct to conventional ultrasound for renal allograft assessment, enabling real-time visualization of microvascular perfusion without ionizing radiation or nephrotoxic contrast exposure. It is useful for evaluating vascular complications, focal infarction, cortical necrosis, post-biopsy complications, and perfusion abnormalities related to acute tubular injury or rejection. However, its clinical application remains limited by acoustic-window dependency, operator experience, off-label use in some regions, and insufficient standardization of quantitative perfusion parameters [10–12].

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) addresses these gaps by offering a radiation-free, non-invasive platform that simultaneously interrogates allograft perfusion, oxygenation, and microstructure without nephrotoxic contrast agents [13]. Unlike ultrasound, which assesses macrovascular hemodynamics indirectly, fMRI enables direct, quantifiable mapping of tissue-level pathophysiology through multiple complementary sequences: arterial spin labeling (ASL) quantifies renal blood flow by magnetically labeling arterial water protons; blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) imaging assesses regional oxygenation through deoxyhemoglobin-induced susceptibility effects (R2*); diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) captures water-molecule diffusion reflecting cellular density and tubular integrity (apparent diffusion coefficient, ADC); and intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) MRI applies biexponential fitting to disentangle microvascular perfusion (pseudodiffusion coefficient D*, perfusion fraction f) from pure molecular diffusion (D) [14]. Quantitative T1 and T2 mapping have attracted increasing attention for assessing structural injury in renal allografts. T1 mapping provides voxel-wise relaxation measurements that may reflect interstitial expansion, inflammation, edema, and fibrosis, and recent studies have shown its association with renal function and histopathological injury in long-term transplant recipients [15–17]. T1-based approaches have also been explored for early detection of acute kidney injury after solid-organ transplantation and for reproducible assessment of stable renal allografts, supporting their potential use in serial graft monitoring [18, 19]. Moreover, multiparametric renal MRI studies suggest that relaxometry parameters can contribute to the diagnosis and prognosis of renal transplant dysfunction [19], while T1ρ mapping has shown preliminary value for evaluating renal allograft fibrosis [20].These sequences are now readily available on contemporary clinical MRI systems, positioning multiparametric fMRI as a comprehensive tool for detecting early allograft dysfunction.

Despite this conceptual promise, the clinical translation of fMRI in kidney transplantation remains hindered by substantial uncertainty regarding its real-world diagnostic performance. Published evidence exhibits marked heterogeneity across technical, clinical, and analytical dimensions: (i) technical parameters – including magnetic field strength (1.5 T vs. 3.0 T, with reported variability in absolute RBF quantification), b-value selection for diffusion sequences (ranging from 3 to 16 b-values, 0–1000 s/mm²), ASL labeling schemes (pulsed vs. pseudocontinuous with varying post-labeling delays), and post-processing algorithms (single-compartment vs. two-compartment models, ROI delineation methods) vary widely across institutions, directly affecting parameter quantification and inter-study comparability [21]; (ii) population definitions - eGFR thresholds for “dysfunction” range from 45 to 60 mL/min/1.73 m², post-transplant timing spans days to years with divergent pathophysiological mechanisms, and reference standards differ (biopsy-proven pathology vs. eGFR-based classification vs. composite outcomes), complicating cross-study synthesis; and (iii) diagnostic accuracy - reported AUCs for ASL-derived cortical RBF range from 0.72 to 0.94 across studies, while BOLD-derived R2* shows conflicting associations with acute rejection (decreased R2* in some studies, unchanged or increased in others) and poor discrimination of acute tubular necrosis. These inconsistencies, compounded by small single-center sample sizes and lack of external validation, preclude definitive conclusions about optimal fMRI protocols, reference ranges, or clinical implementation strategies. Crucially, no prior quantitative synthesis has examined these sources of heterogeneity, estimated pooled diagnostic performance, or evaluated parameter–eGFR correlations across the post-transplant continuum [22–28].

To address these critical knowledge gaps, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis with three specific objectives: [1] to quantify the pooled diagnostic performance of ASL, BOLD, DWI, and IVIM MRI for detecting post-transplant renal dysfunction; [2] to examine how technical factors (magnetic field strength, b-values), timing of assessment, and baseline renal function modify fMRI performance through subgroup and meta-regression analyses; and [3] to evaluate correlations between fMRI parameters and eGFR as a step toward establishing their clinical validity.

Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [29]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (registration number CRD42024580736).

Search strategy and data extraction

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, and MEDLINE from inception to June 18, 2025, to identify studies evaluating fMRI for detecting renal dysfunction after kidney transplantation. The search was restricted to studies published in English or Chinese. Dissertations, institutional reports, unpublished clinical trial results, technical reports, and white papers were not systematically searched. Conference abstracts or proceedings identified through database searches were screened but were excluded if they did not provide sufficient original quantitative fMRI data for extraction and meta-analysis. No age restriction was applied during study screening. Studies including kidney transplant recipients of any age were considered eligible if they met the predefined imaging and outcome criteria. However, no pediatric-specific or predominantly pediatric cohort was identified among the included studies. The search terms and history are provided in supplementary materials. Articles were exported to EndNote 20, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text assessment against inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by discussion until consensus was achieved.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: [1] evaluated post-transplant complications in kidney transplant recipients; [2] employed non-invasive fMRI techniques (ASL, BOLD, DWI, or IVIM); and [3] used either (a) pathological confirmation from biopsy or surgery, or (b) documented eGFR as the reference standard.

PICO framework: Population - kidney-transplant recipients with graft dysfunction; Intervention - fMRI (any combination of ASL, BOLD, DWI, or IVIM MRI); Comparator - recipients without graft dysfunction; Outcome - demonstrably different measured values (RBF, R2*, ADC, D, D*, f).

Exclusion criteria

Studies were excluded if they were reviews, meta-analyses, animal studies, conference abstracts, guidelines, commentaries, or editorials; or lacked available original fMRI measurement data or usable data (e.g., study groups not classified according to eGFR or specific pathological types).

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data were extracted in a standardized format including: (a) study characteristics (authors, region, publication year, study design, time span); (b) clinical and demographic characteristics (age, gender, grouping criteria, renal function indicators, pathological information, data collection timing); (c) technical characteristics (MRI sequences, magnetic field strength, b-values); (d) methods used to estimate renal function (MDRD or CKD-EPI equations); and (e) fMRI data (RBF, R2*, ADC, D, D*, and f). For diagnostic studies, the QUADAS-2 tool was used to assess study quality [30]. Two reviewers independently performed data extraction and quality assessment, resolving discrepancies through discussion with a third reviewer.

Data analysis

For ease of analysis, fMRI data for cases with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² were combined. fMRI parameters were used as effect sizes, with mean differences (MD) and standard deviations (SD) calculated using the Cochrane Handbook. Results were presented in forest plots. The Higgins I² statistic was used to measure heterogeneity. For studies with heterogeneity ≥ 50%, random-effects models were applied to balance the weights. Subgroup analyses were conducted by magnetic field strength (1.5 T vs. 3.0 T), post-transplant time (< 3 months, < 12 months), and baseline creatinine levels (< 210, ≥ 210 µmol/L). Sensitivity analyses were also performed. Fisher’s Z transformation was applied to convert correlation coefficients to Z-values. All analyses were performed using RevMan (version 5.3), with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 11,046 articles were retrieved, of which 3,141 were excluded due to duplication, 7,348 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria, and 12 articles were unavailable in full text. We retrieved and reviewed 545 full texts, of which 28 articles were included in the statistical analysis (Fig. 1) [31–58]. The studies were conducted in China, Egypt, Switzerland, Germany, India, the United States, Turkey, Italy, South Korea, and Serbia, published between 2003 and 2025. A total of 1,956 cases were included: 11 studies used 1.5 T, 17 studies used 3.0 T; 9 studies used DWI, 5 studies used IVIM, 9 studies used ASL, and 11 studies used BOLD imaging (Table 1). ASL

Cortical RBF was significantly higher in recipients with preserved allograft function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) compared to those with dysfunction (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) (MD 98.48 mL·min⁻¹·100 g⁻¹, 95% CI 68.66-128.31; P < 0.00001), with moderate between-study heterogeneity (I²=57%) (Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis by magnetic field strength did not significantly reduce heterogeneity or alter effect estimates. Sensitivity analysis excluding Wang2018 eliminated heterogeneity (I²=0%), likely attributable to that study’s more stringent grouping criteria (Supplementary Figures S1–S2). Medullary and total RBF were not meta-analyzed due to insufficient data.

Forest plot of the cortical RBF for high versus low categories of eGFR. (RBF: renal blood flow; eGFR: estimated glomerular filtration rate)

BOLD

Cortical and medullary R2* were significantly lower in recipients with acute rejection compared to those with stable graft function (cortical MD − 1.71, 95% CI − 2.65 to − 0.76, P < 0.0001; medullary MD − 5.77, 95% CI − 7.16 to − 4.38, P < 0.00001), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (cortical I²=77%, medullary I²=86%) (Figs. 3 and 4). Subgroup analyses by magnetic field strength, post-transplant time (< 3 months vs. ≥3 months), and baseline creatinine levels (< 210 vs. ≥210 µmol/L) did not significantly reduce heterogeneity. Sensitivity analysis identified Xiao2012 as an outlier, though no specific methodological differences were apparent. For acute tubular necrosis (ATN), neither cortical nor medullary R2* demonstrated significant differences compared to stable function (Supplementary Figures S3–S6).

DWI

Cortical and medullary ADC values were significantly higher in recipients with preserved allograft function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) compared to those with dysfunction (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) (cortical P = 0.007; medullary P = 0.001), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (cortical I²=96%, medullary I²=94%). Subgroup analysis by b-values was not performed due to insufficient data. ADC values showed similar discriminatory performance for acute rejection versus ATN, with moderate heterogeneity (ADC I²=57%, medullary ADC I²=72%) (Supplementary Figures S7–S13). The extreme heterogeneity in overall ADC comparisons precludes definitive conclusions regarding optimal b-value selection or diagnostic thresholds.

IVIM

All IVIM-derived parameters demonstrated significant differences between preserved (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) and impaired allograft function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²). Cortical and medullary D values exhibited the strongest discriminatory power (cortical MD 0.14 × 10⁻³ mm²/s, 95% CI 0.12–0.16; medullary MD 0.16 × 10⁻³ mm²/s, 95% CI 0.11–0.20; both P < 0.00001) with minimal heterogeneity (cortical I²=0%, medullary I²=0%) (Figs. 5 and 6). D* and f values showed significant differences but with substantial heterogeneity (cortical D* I²=98%, medullary D* I²=96%; cortical f I²=95%, medullary f I²=18%) (Figs. 7, 8, 9, and 10). Sensitivity analysis excluding El-Ksas2024 resolved heterogeneity for select parameters, which may be attributed to methodological differences such as ROI delineation strategy, inclusion of mixed post-transplant stages, and variations in b-value distribution, potentially influencing perfusion-related IVIM measurements (Supplementary Figures S14–S16). These findings indicate that IVIM-D is the most robust IVIM parameter for detecting allograft dysfunction, independent of perfusion confounding.

Correlation with eGFR

Cortical RBF demonstrated a moderate positive correlation with eGFR (Fisher’s Z-transformed r = 0.58, 95% CI 0.52–0.63; Z = 6.64, P < 0.00001) (Fig. 11). This association suggests that perfusion impairment precedes filtration decline, supporting the utility of RBF as an early functional biomarker. Correlation data for other fMRI parameters with eGFR were insufficient for meta-analysis.

Discussion

Principal findings

In this first systematic review and comprehensive meta-analysis of 28 studies comprising 1,956 kidney transplant recipients, we demonstrate that multiparametric functional MRI, particularly ASL-derived cortical RBF and IVIM-derived D values, exhibits good diagnostic accuracy for identifying early allograft dysfunction. Three findings warrant emphasis. First, cortical RBF discriminated preserved from impaired renal function (eGFR ≥ 60 vs. <60 mL/min/1.73 m²; MD 98.48, 95% CI 68.66-128.31) and correlated with global filtration (r = 0.58), suggesting perfusion imaging captures functional decline earlier than conventional biomarkers. Second, IVIM-derived D values showed robust discriminatory power (cortical MD 0.14 × 10⁻³ mm²/s, 95% CI 0.12–0.16; medullary MD 0.16 × 10⁻³ mm²/s, 95% CI 0.11–0.20) with minimal heterogeneity (I²=0%). Third, BOLD identified acute rejection (lower cortical and medullary R2*) but not tubular necrosis, indicating specificity for inflammatory rather than ischemic injury. Notwithstanding these promising signals, substantial inter-study heterogeneity (I²=0–98%) underscores the urgent need for standardized protocols before clinical implementation.

Comparison with existing literature

Prior narrative reviews have summarized fMRI technical principles and pilot studies in kidney transplantation [13, 14], but none has quantitatively synthesized diagnostic performance or examined sources of heterogeneity. Our pooled estimates address this gap, revealing wider accuracy ranges than individual studies suggest. For example, while individual ASL studies reported AUCs 0.73–0.94 [52, 53, 59–61], our meta-analytic approach incorporating diverse populations and protocols indicates more conservative real-world performance. Notably, a 2025 prospective study of ASL combined with DTI demonstrated similar diagnostic accuracy but highlighted substantial inter-scanner variability [28], reinforcing our finding that technical standardization remains a prerequisite for clinical translation.

Integrated interpretation of multiparametric fMRI

Rather than viewing ASL, BOLD, and IVIM as isolated tests, our results support their integration as complementary probes of distinct pathophysiological processes. ASL-quantified cortical RBF reflects macrovascular perfusion and microvascular integrity; the observed 99.5 mL·min⁻¹·100 g⁻¹ reduction in graft dysfunction and correlation with eGFR (r = 0.58) suggest reduced perfusion precedes filtration decline, consistent with “perfusion-first” models of kidney injury [62, 63]. IVIM-derived D values, representing pure molecular diffusion after excluding microvascular perfusion effects, exhibited the strongest discriminatory power with minimal heterogeneity (I²=0%), likely capturing tubular cell swelling and interstitial edema—early reversible changes that may be invisible to conventional functional markers.

DWI and IVIM provide complementary diffusion-related information, but with distinct biophysical interpretations. DWI measures Brownian motion using mono-exponential fitting; we found significant differences in ADC values between preserved and impaired function, though extreme heterogeneity (I²=94–96%) precluded definitive conclusions. This variability likely stems from divergent b-value protocols - higher b-values (> 200 s/mm²) predominantly reflect molecular diffusion, whereas lower b-values capture perfusion effects [64, 65]. IVIM addresses this limitation by biexponentially decomposing diffusion and perfusion components. In our meta-analysis, IVIM-derived D values exhibited the strongest discriminatory power with minimal heterogeneity, while D* and f values showed substantial variability (I²=18–98%). Sensitivity analysis excluding El-Ksas2024 resolved heterogeneity for select parameters. This may be attributable to divergent post-processing methodologies. These findings indicate that IVIM-D is the most robust diffusion parameter for detecting allograft dysfunction, independent of perfusion confounding.

The contrast between BOLD’s sensitivity for acute rejection (inflammatory hyperemia with relative hypoxia) and insensitivity for ATN (uniform ischemia without metabolic gradient) highlights that R2* reflects oxygen extraction mismatch rather than absolute tissue oxygenation [66, 67]. Renal blood volume fraction and tubular volume fraction may confound deoxyhemoglobin detection in glomerular hyperfiltration, vascular fibrosis, hypoperfusion, anemia, and post-renal obstruction [67]. Therefore, BOLD cannot distinguish oxygenation changes caused by pathological changes from those attributed to confounding factors.

Together, these parameters enable a “functional biopsy”: ASL assesses delivery, BOLD assesses consumption-demand balance, and IVIM assesses structural integrity—simultaneously and non-invasively. Recent integration of IVIM or ASL with T1 mapping for long-term transplant survivors further supports multiparametric approaches over single-sequence assessment [34, 59, 60].

Clinical implications

The clinical value of multiparametric fMRI lies in the complementary biological information provided by individual parameters. ASL-derived RBF is most relevant for early perfusion surveillance, particularly in recipients at high risk of delayed graft function, donor-derived ischemic injury, or unexplained early functional decline. Because ASL quantifies tissue perfusion without exogenous contrast, reduced cortical RBF may reflect impaired arterial inflow, microvascular rarefaction, or insufficient cortical perfusion before these changes are fully captured by filtration-based markers [68]. Therefore, ASL may help identify patients who require closer biochemical monitoring, hemodynamic optimization, adjustment of immunosuppression, or targeted biopsy when graft dysfunction remains clinically ambiguous [69].

BOLD-derived R2* provides a different layer of information by reflecting the balance between oxygen delivery, oxygen consumption, and deoxyhemoglobin concentration. This feature may be clinically relevant in suspected acute rejection, where inflammatory infiltration, endothelial injury, and microvascular dysfunction can disturb tissue oxygenation [70, 71]. However, R2* is influenced by factors such as anemia, hydration status, blood volume fraction, fibrosis, and urinary obstruction [67]. Thus, BOLD findings should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical manifestations, laboratory markers, Doppler or CEUS findings, and biopsy results when available, especially when differentiating rejection from ischemic or obstructive injury [72].

Diffusion-based imaging may be particularly useful for evaluating parenchymal microstructural injury. Conventional DWI-derived ADC reflects the combined effects of molecular diffusion and microvascular perfusion, and is therefore sensitive to tubular injury, interstitial edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and fibrosis, but its interpretation is affected by b-value selection and acquisition protocols. IVIM further separates diffusion-related and perfusion-related components, allowing D to more specifically represent pure molecular diffusion and parenchymal integrity [73]. Clinically, IVIM-D may be useful for repeated monitoring of dynamic tissue changes after transplantation or therapeutic intervention: an increase in D may suggest recovery from cellular swelling or interstitial edema, whereas a persistent decrease in D may indicate ongoing microstructural restriction related to inflammation, tubular injury, or fibrotic progression [74]. In contrast, D* and f may provide additional perfusion-related information, but their broader clinical application still requires better-standardized acquisition and post-processing methods.

Limitations

Several limitations temper these conclusions. First, residual confounding from unmeasured technical factors likely persists; we could not extract detailed pulse sequence parameters (labeling duration, echo times, parallel imaging factors) or post-processing workflows from published reports. Second, reference standard heterogeneity – including biopsy timing (protocol biopsy vs. for-cause biopsy), eGFR equations (MDRD vs. CKD-EPI), and outcome definitions - introduced measurement bias we could not quantify. Third, publication bias toward positive results may inflate diagnostic accuracy estimates; funnel plots were underpowered given < 10 studies for most comparisons. Fourth, external validity is uncertain—included studies were predominantly single-center studies from Asian and European populations, with limited representation of African ancestry or pediatric recipients. Fifth, not all studies provided diagnostic data (sensitivity, specificity, cutoff values) or specific eGFR calculation equations, magnetic field strengths, or data collection timing; we could not analyze follow-up outcomes, pathological scores, combined MRI performance, or adverse prognostic outcomes (e.g., thrombotic microangiopathy). Sixth, optimal diagnostic thresholds remain undefined; we report pooled mean differences rather than sensitivity/specificity at specific cutoffs, precluding immediate clinical implementation. Other techniques (T1 mapping, DTI, DKI, MRE) were not included due to insufficient data. Finally, because the included studies were predominantly adult cohorts and pediatric-specific data were not separately reported, the applicability of our findings to pediatric kidney transplant recipients remains uncertain. Although biopsy-confirmed diagnoses such as AR, ATN, and CAN were extracted and summarized, most studies did not provide detailed Banff scores, quantitative fibrosis measures, or correlation coefficients with individual MRI parameters. Consequently, a meta-analysis of MRI–histopathology correlations could not be performed, and the relationships are reported descriptively.

Future directions

Three research priorities emerge. First, prospective multicenter validation using harmonized protocols (aligned with PARENCHIMA consensus recommendations [22–25]) should establish field strength-specific reference ranges and test-retest reliability, directly addressing the heterogeneity sources identified herein. Second, integration of artificial intelligence for automated ROI segmentation and radiomic feature extraction [75–78], as piloted in recent studies, may reduce may reduce operator dependence and enhance reproducibility, mitigating a major source of inter-study variability. Third, head-to-head comparisons of fMRI with emerging biomarkers (donor-derived cell-free DNA, urinary CXCL9) and machine learning-enhanced ultrasound would clarify incremental value and cost-effectiveness, informing clinical adoption strategies. Finally, building upon the observed correlation between early perfusion impairment and subsequent functional decline, interventional trials randomizing high-risk recipients to fMRI-guided versus standard care management could ultimately demonstrate impact on hard endpoints (graft survival, eGFR slope).

Conclusion

Multiparametric fMRI offers a promising, non-invasive window into allograft perfusion, oxygenation, and microstructure, with ASL and IVIM showing particular diagnostic utility. However, substantial heterogeneity across studies mandates standardized acquisition and analysis protocols before clinical adoption. Realizing fMRI’s potential as a “functional biopsy” will require prospective validation, cost-effectiveness analyses, and demonstration of impact on patient-centered outcomes.
