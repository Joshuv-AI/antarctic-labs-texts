A Natural LTR Retrotransposon Insertion in the Promoter of GhNAC140‐Dt Boosts Cotton Lint Yield
Authors: Yujia Yu, Xiaoguang Shang, Haitang Wang, Lijie Zhu, Xu Han, Qingfei He, Weixi Li, Yonglin Tan, Guozhong Zhu, Wangzhen Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTransposable elements (TEs) are fundamental drivers of crop evolution and domestication. Whereas the underlying mechanisms of TE‐mediated gene activation remain poorly understood. Lint percentage is an important yield component in cotton. Here, we report a retrotransposon insertion in the promoter of GhNAC140‐Dt, a secondary wall NAC encoding gene, to promote the lint production by elevating its expression. We confirm that a 60 bp core cis‐regulatory module within the TE's LTR (long terminal repeat) specifically recruits the transcription factor GhMYB46 and increases downstream genes' expression. GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression activates the expressions of secondary cell wall development related genes, including GhCESA4‐Dt, GhCESA4‐At, and GhCOBL9‐At, promotes cellulose deposition, and enhances lint percentage. This retrotransposon insertion massively emerges on the domestication transition from Gossypium hirsutum races to cultivated cotton accessions, with > 80% fixation in modern cultivars. This work deepens our understanding of TE‐mediated gene activation; it also provides direct molecular evidence for “transposon‐driven yield evolution” in crop domestication.

Introduction

Transposable elements (TEs) are DNA elements that are able to move and amplify within the host genome (Jangam et al. 2017). Initially thought to be “junk and selfish DNA” (Doolittle and Sapienza 1980; Orgel and Crick 1980), TEs have now been shown to play a pivotal role in facilitating gene amplification, genomic diversification, and evolutionary innovation across species via genetic and epigenetic mechanisms (Rebollo et al. 2012; Oliver et al. 2013). TEs are classified into two major categories: Class I retrotransposons and Class II DNA transposons. Retrotransposons primarily encompass long terminal repeat (LTR) elements, DIRS‐like (Dictyostelium intermediate repeat sequence) elements, Penelope‐like elements (PLEs), autonomous elements including long interspersed nuclear elements (LINEs), and non‐autonomous short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs). These elements transpose via a “copy‐and‐paste” mechanism utilising RNA intermediates during DNA replication. Class II DNA transposons mediate transposition through DNA intermediates (Wicker et al. 2007). In plants, the most abundant class of TEs is the LTR retrotransposons, contributing up to 70% of the plant genome size, as reported in wheat (Sabot et al. 2005), maize (Schnable 2009), or sugar pine (Stevens et al. 2016). These elements exhibit a distinctive structural hallmark characterised by two flanking LTRs, which typically contain open reading frames (ORFs) encoding viral‐like particle structural proteins, aspartic proteases, reverse transcriptase, RNase H, and integrases (Vitte and Panaud 2005). The two major superfamilies of LTR retrotransposons, Gypsy and Copia, are distinguished by differences in the sequential arrangement of reverse transcriptase and integrase.

The LTRs flanking their internal protein domains contain promoter‐like cis‐regulatory motifs, which are deemed to control transcription of the internal mobile elements. However, the long terminal repeats (LTRs) of these retrotransposons contain regulatory sequences that can be co‐opted by the host genome to generate novel promoters or enhancers. When such sequences insert near genes, they have the potential to confer new expression patterns, thereby directly linking the mobility of transposable elements to phenotypic variation. A previous study showed that the LTR‐GUS fusions were regulated by microbial elicitors and abiotic stresses (Pouteau et al. 1994; Mhiri et al. 1997), demonstrating the transcriptional regulation of cis‐regulatory motifs in LTRs on nearby genes. A hypothesis has been raised that the gene promoters actively co‐opted with the regulatory motifs in the LTR to fulfil the developmental or environmental regulation. An example on how LTR can be co‐opted is illustrated by the Copia‐LTR retrotransposon Tcs1 which is inserted upstream of a gene encoding Ruby, a MYB transcriptional activator of anthocyanin production in blood orange (Butelli et al. 2012). Both insertion of the full Tcs1 retrotransposon or the solo LTR in the promoter region results in high levels of Ruby expression and blood flesh. Likewise, a “sleeping” R gene allele (Pit) in rice was waken up due to the Ty1‐Copia retrotransposon insertion in its promoter, resulting in high resistance to rice blast of the cultivars bearing the TE (Hayashi and Yoshida 2009). Insertion of Hopscotch retrotransposon in the regulatory region of teosinte branched1 (tb1) gene acts as an enhancer, strengthening apical dominance, a pivotal trait in maize domestication (Studer et al. 2011). Beyond providing linear sequences, TEs significantly influence the epigenetic landscape and three‐dimensional (3D) architecture of the genome. Their insertion can recruit chromatin‐modifying complexes or disrupt pre‐existing chromatin boundaries. A seminal study on the murine endogenous retrovirus MusD demonstrated that its insertion can lead to the formation of an ectopic chromatin loop, effectively placing a developmental gene under the control of a novel enhancer and resulting in severe limb malformations. This underscores how TEs can hijack local genomic regulatory networks by altering higher‐order chromatin structure (Juliane et al. 2025). Furthermore, host defence mechanisms, such as those mediated by the m6A reader YTHDC1, can recruit repressive complexes like SETDB1 to deposit the H3K9me3 mark at TE loci, leading to heterochromatin formation and the concomitant silencing of neighbouring genes (Liu et al. 2021). This interplay between TE activity and epigenetic silencing represents a crucial aspect of gene regulation. The cumulative effect of multiple TE insertions can lead to large‐scale rewiring of transcriptional networks over evolutionary and domestication timescales. Genome‐wide studies have revealed statistically significant associations between LTR retrotransposon insertions and key domestication traits, such as grain width in rice and fruit weight in tomato, indicating that TE activity is a recurrent mechanism in crop evolution (Akakpo et al. 2020; Alseekh et al. 2020). This process frequently involves the co‐option (or exaptation) of TE‐derived regulatory motifs, whereby gene expression comes under the control of environmental or developmental signals embedded within the TEs. For instance, the Copia‐LTR retrotransposon ONSEN carries a heat‐responsive element, and its insertion can confer heat‐inducible expression upon nearby genes (Pietzenuk et al. 2016). This mechanism facilitates rapid adaptation by linking existing genes to novel regulatory cues provided by TEs. Consequently, a deeper elucidation of the precise molecular mechanisms by which TEs regulate gene function during crop evolution and domestication is not only fundamental to understanding the origins of phenotypic diversity but also provides revolutionary concepts and tools for the directional improvement of crop traits through molecular breeding by design.

Cotton is a globally significant cash crop that serves dual agricultural purposes as the world's primary source of natural textile fibre and an important provider of vegetable oil and protein from its seeds. The allotetraploid upland cotton (AADD), which accounts for over 90% of the global cotton planting area, originated from an interspecific hybridization between diploid A genome ( Gossypium herbaceum or Gossypium arboreum , AA) and D genome (Gossypium raimondii, DD) approximately 1–2 million years ago (Paterson et al. 2012; Huang et al. 2020). Following polyploidization, rapid and substantial reprogramming in the hybrid tetraploid genome reshapes chromosome structure, genetic, and gene regulatory networks, exhibiting profound and broad effects on phenotypic diversity (Chen et al. 2020). Genomic studies demonstrated that TEs in the tetraploid cotton genome, most of which are retrotransposons (over 90%), play a crucial role in evolution and domestication (Wang et al. 2022). A number of studies have demonstrated the regulatory roles of TEs, especially LTR‐retrotransposons, on cotton phenotype so far (Wang et al. 2016). Ding et al. (2015) demonstrated that the insertion of GBRE‐1, a Ty1/Copia‐type retrotransposon, into the HD1 (homeodomain leucine zipper 1) gene within the At subgenome leads to a glabrous stem phenotype in tetraploid cotton. Notably, the LTR sequences of GBRE‐1 harboured abundant transcriptional regulation cis‐elements. Guan et al. (2026) showed that an LTR‐retrotransposon insertion in the first exon of GhDYA (dark‐yellow anther, which encodes phytoene synthase) caused loss of function and light‐yellow anther colour in upland cotton. Liu et al. (2025) constructed a cotton transposon‐related variations (TRVs) map using 10 Gossypium genomes and identified 128 genes as resources for improving cotton fibre quality. The study demonstrates that TRVs are a key driver of genome evolution and diversity in cotton. Huang et al. (2024) revealed that cotton centromeres are primarily composed of long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposon fragments and identified a tripartite regulatory module, miR2947–MuTC01–LEC2b. This transposon‐derived siRNA regulatory network controls embryonic morphogenesis. Tian et al. (2025) discovered that TEs have differential effects on transcriptional regulation; DNA transposon insertions often suppress transcription, whereas LTR retrotransposons can enhance it by providing transcription factor binding sites, a finding supported by integrated analysis of direct RNA sequencing, polysome profiling sequencing (Polysome Profiling‐seq), and small RNA sequencing data.

NAC proteins, which are initially identified in NAM (No Apical Meristem) (Souer et al. 1996), ATAF1/2 (Arabidopsis), and CUC2 (Cup‐shaped Cotyledon2) (Aida et al. 1997), are characterised by a highly conserved N‐terminal DNA‐binding domain and a variable C‐terminal transcriptional activation/repression domain (Xie et al. 2000). The NAC gene family represents one of the largest plant‐specific transcription factor families, playing pivotal roles in diverse developmental processes and stress responses (Ernst et al. 2004). Several NAC transcription factors have been identified as key regulators in secondary cell wall (SCW) development, and thus named SWNs (secondary wall NACs). The regulation of SCW formation is governed by a complex three‐tier transcriptional network in which SWNs locate at the top tier and activate the entire SCW biosynthetic program (Zhong et al. 2010). In cotton, upregulation of GhFSN1, a SWN encoding gene, results in an increase in fibre SCW thickness but a decrease in fibre length via elevating the expression of a suite of fibre secondary cell wall‐associated genes, including GhKNL1, GhMYBL1, GhGUT1, GhDUF231L1, and GhIRX12 (Zhang, Huang, et al. 2018). Another study demonstrated that simultaneously silencing two SWN encoding genes in cotton, GhSND1 (secondary wall‐associated NAC domain protein1) and GhNST1 (NAC secondary wall thickening promoting factor1), significantly downregulated the expression of SCW biosynthesis‐related genes, including GhCESA4, GhCESA7, and GhCESA8 (Fang et al. 2020). MYB transcription factors, such as MYB46 and MYB83, act as the secondary tier switches in the three‐tier transcriptional network (Hussey et al. 2013). Notably, MYBs not only regulate the expression of downstream genes in the third tier but also directly control the transcription of upstream SWN genes. As exemplified, MtMYB4 has been shown to inhibit the expression of the SWN encoding gene MtSND1, forming a negative feedback regulatory pathway (Wang et al. 2011). Although SWNs are promising genes for crop breeding, their roles and mechanisms in plant development, for example the cotton fibre development which determines the fibre yield and quality, remain largely unknown.

Here, we characterise an LTR‐Copia retrotransposon insertion event in the promoter region of GhNAC140‐Dt gene, thereby enhancing its expression. Association studies using the molecular marker which detects the presence/absence of the TE insertion event in a natural cotton population consisting of 225 G. hirsutum accessions showed that the TE insertion obviously elevates cotton yield via increasing lint percentage and lint index. We confirmed that the solo LTR sequence of the Copia‐retrotransposon could significantly promote the transcription of downstream genes. The presence of MYB binding sites within the LTR sequence and its ability to recruit the transcriptional activator GhMYB46 account for the transcriptional activation capability of this LTR sequence. Constitutive overexpression of GhNAC140‐Dt in cotton increases lint percentage while resulting in shorter and thicker fibres due to excessive cellulose accumulation in fibres. Moreover, we demonstrate that GhNAC140‐Dt directly binds to promoters of GhCESA4‐Dt and GhCESA4‐At, cellulose synthase encoding genes, and GhCOBL9‐At, a cobra‐like protein encoding gene, to activate their transcription. Population genomics analysis reveals that this TE insertion massively appears in the semi‐wild G. hirsutum races, especially in G. hirsutum races latifolium and punctatum, and is further selectively retained during the cultivated G. hirsutum domestication process. While this TE insertion is few in cultivated G. barbadense accessions and absent in three wild cotton relatives ( G. tomentosum , G. mustelinum, and G. darwinii ) and putative tetraploid cotton species donors ( G. arboreum or G. herbaceum and G. raimondii). We also observe that this TE insertion event occurs at high frequency in cultivated cotton accessions, marking it as a molecular signature of artificial selection for higher lint percentage to serve yield improvement during the upland cotton domestication process. This study advances our understanding of retrotransposon‐mediated regulation of gene expression in plants and elucidates the dual role of GhNAC140‐Dt in coordinating cell wall biogenesis and agronomic yield traits.

Results

GhNAC140‐Dt Plays an Essential Role in Fibre Development and Yield in Cotton

In a previous study, we conducted a genome‐wide identification of NAC transcription factors in cotton and found GhNAC140 is predominantly expressed during cotton fibre development (Fang et al. 2020). Here, we confirmed the expression pattern of GhNAC140 in the genetic standard accession TM‐1 of G. hirsutum using RT‐qPCR. GhNAC140 expression peaked in 15‐25 days post‐anthesis (DPA) fibres, coinciding with the secondary cell wall (SCW) thickening stage (Figure 1a), indicating its essential role in SCW formation.

Given the tetraploid G. hirsutum includes At and Dt subgenomes, we further developed the subgenome‐specific single nucleotide polymorphism primers of GhNAC140 to analyse the expression patterns of the At and Dt subgenome homeologs of GhNAC140 (Figure S1). Both RT‐qPCR and transcriptome data revealed significant differential expression between these homeologs, with GhNAC140‐Dt being the predominantly expressed gene (Figure 1b). Cloning and sequencing analyses of the GhNAC140‐At and GhNAC140‐Dt promoter in G. hirsutum acc. TM‐1 identified a 5108 bp insertion in the GhNAC140‐Dt promoter, located 1634 bp upstream of the translation initiation codon (Figure 1c). Online program of RepeatMasker (http://www.repeatmasker.org/) analysis showed that the insertion sequence as a Ty1 Copia‐type retrotransposon (Table S1). Next, we examined whether this retrotransposon insertion in GhNAC140‐Dt promoter is a common event across G. hirsutum accessions. A molecular marker, with the forward primer annealing to the retrotransposon terminus and a reverse primer binding at position −1491 bp in the promoter, was developed to specifically detect the insertion event (Figure 1c). By screening a natural population of 225 upland cotton accessions (Song et al. 2021), the result showed the retrotransposon insertion in 181 accessions and its absence in 44 accessions (Figure S2). Thus, GhNAC140‐Dt gene has two distinct haplotypes in the G. hirsutum population, and the haplotype with or without the retrotransposon insertion was termed GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 and GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 , respectively. Next, using phenotypic data from 15 environmental conditions of the 225 accessions grown across multiple locations in different years, we compared the cotton yield and fibre quality traits associated with GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 and GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 . We found that the retrotransposon insertion event (Hap1) was significantly associated with increased lint percentage (LP) and lint index with 8 environments (Figure 1d,e). Moreover, seed index between GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 and GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 showed difference in two environments (Figure S3a), boll weight in three environments (Figure S3b). Also, fibre length, strength and micronaire showed difference in only one environment or no obvious difference in tested 15 environments (Figure S3c–e). These results indicate that GhNAC140‐Dt plays an important role in lint percentage formation, a vital yield component in cotton.

The Natural LTR Insertion Elevates the Promoter Activity

Given the profound impact of the retrotransposon insertion on GhNAC140‐Dt expression, we attempted to explore its effect on promoter activity. The promoter sequences of two GhNAC140‐Dt haplotypes, one is a 6977 bp fragment containing the retrotransposon (Hap1) designated P6977 and the other is a 1634 bp fragment lacking the retrotransposon (Hap2) designated P1634, and the promoter sequence of an 1869 bp fragment of GhNAC140‐At designated P1869, were cloned, respectively. These three fragments were fused upstream of the GUS reporter gene to generate P6977::GUS, P1634::GUS and P1869::GUS constructs, respectively (Figure 2a). Using a transient transformation system in 20 DPA cotton fibres (Shang et al. 2022), alongside negative and positive controls, we assessed the promoter activity of these three fragments. Both histochemical GUS staining and enzymatic activity assays revealed significantly stronger GUS expression in fibres infiltrated with Agrobacterium tumefaciens carrying P6977::GUS compared to those carrying P1634::GUS and P1869::GUS (Figure 2b,c), with no obvious difference between P1634::GUS and P1869::GUS. These results indicated that the retrotransposon insertion in the promoter of GhNAC140‐Dt enhances downstream gene expression.

According to previous reports (Bennetzen 2000), Ty1 Copia‐retrotransposon contained long terminal repeats (LTRs) at each end. Through analysing the retrotransposon sequences, we found that the 459 bp sequences at each end of the retrotransposon showed high sequence identity (with only two nucleotide polymorphisms) and were designated LTR1 (5′ terminus) and LTR2 (3′ terminus) (Figure 2d). Also, ORFs encoding transposition enzymes (gag, pr, int., rt., rh) were identified within the retrotransposon's central region. As previously reported, LTRs frequently harbour cis‐regulatory elements (Butelli et al. 2012; Hayashi and Yoshida 2009; Studer et al. 2011; Feschotte 2008). To compare their promoter activity, we constructed LTR1‐mini35S::GUS and LTR2‐mini35S::GUS vectors. Histochemical staining showed strong and indistinguishable promoter activity for both LTRs (Figure 2e).

We next compared the promoter‐enhancing capacity of the full‐length retrotransposon versus a solo LTR. The native 1634 bp GhNAC140‐Dt promoter fragment (pF1), the 459 bp LTR2 plus pF1, and the full‐length 5108 bp retrotransposon plus pF1 were each fused to GUS (Figure 2f). Both LTR2 and the full‐length retrotransposon significantly elevated pF1‐driven GUS transcription, with no significant difference observed between them, demonstrating that solo LTR2 is sufficient to confer high promoter activity. To delineate the functional region within LTR2, we divided its 459 bp sequence into four overlapping 150 bp fragments (LTR2‐1 to LTR2‐4) with the lengths of 47 bp, 58 bp, and 36 bp overlapped between LTR2‐1 and LTR2‐2, LTR2‐2 and LTR2‐3, LTR2‐3 and LTR2‐4, respectively (Figure 2g). Each fragment was fused to mini35S::GUS. Only LTR2‐2 robustly activated GUS expression, while fragments LTR2‐1, LTR2‐3, and LTR2‐4 exhibited activity comparable to the negative control (Figure 2h). Consequently, we identified a 150 bp enhancer within LTR2‐2 responsible for the retrotransposon's transcriptional activation function, facilitating subsequent functional studies.

LTR2‐2 Specifically Recruits GhMYB46 to Elevate Downstream Gene Expression

We investigated the mechanism by which the 150 bp sequence of LTR2‐2 promotes downstream gene expression. Initial screening for potential upstream trans‐acting factors binding this sequence was employed the yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) method. However, the 150 bp fragment exhibited strong autonomous activation activity in yeast cells, preventing the identification of interacting proteins. In spite of this, we collected a list of reported transcription factors (TFs) implicated in secondary cell wall development during cotton fibre development based on a comprehensive literature review of the past decade, as candidate interactors, including GhMYBL1 (Sun et al. 2015), WLIM1a (Han et al. 2016), GhMYB7 (Huang et al. 2016), GhXLIM6 (Li et al. 2018), GhFSN1 (Zhang, Huang, et al. 2018), GhbHLH18 (Gao et al. 2019), GhMYB46 (Huang et al. 2019), GhTCP4 (Wang et al. 2019), GhFSN5 (Sun et al. 2020), GhKNL1 (Gong et al. 2014), GhERF108 (Wang et al. 2023), and GhMYB52‐Like (Ding et al. 2021), to detect the binding with the 150 bp LTR sequence.

Interaction verification was performed using the dual‐luciferase (LUC) reporter system. Open reading frames (ORFs) of the candidate TFs were cloned downstream of the constitutive 35S promoter to generate effector constructs (Figure 3a). The 150 bp LTR2‐2 sequence was fused upstream of the mini35S::LUC reporter gene. The reporter construct also included the REN (Renilla luciferase) gene as an internal control. Co‐transformation of the LTR2‐2 reporter with effectors encoding GhMYBL1, GhMYB7, GhFSN1, GhMYB46, GhFSN5, or GhKNL1 into N. benthamiana leaves resulted in a significantly elevated LUC/REN ratio. In contrast, co‐transformation with the GhMYB52‐Like effector significantly reduced this ratio (Figure 3a), indicating that LTR2‐2 recruits these seven TFs (six activators and one potential repressor). Transcriptome data for G. hirsutum tissues confirmed high expression levels of these seven TFs in cotton fibres (Figure S4). To determine whether these TFs represent novel regulators specifically recruited by LTR2‐2, or are also recruited by the native GhNAC140‐Dt promoter, we fused the original 1634 bp GhNAC140‐Dt promoter sequence upstream of LUC to construct a comparative reporter. This reporter was co‐transformed with effectors encoding the seven candidate TFs. Dual‐LUC assays revealed that GhMYB46 failed to bind the 1634 bp GhNAC140‐Dt promoter sequence, while the other six TFs demonstrated binding activity (Figure 3b). This demonstrates that GhMYB46 is specifically recruited by LTR2‐2.

To investigate which of the seven transcription factors plays a more critical role in driving the activity of the downstream gene, the previously constructed 1634 bp GhNAC140‐Dt promoter fragment (pF1) and 459 bp LTR2 plus pF1 fusion GUS vector, along with effectors coding seven TFs, were co‐infiltrated into N. benthamiana leaves. The GUS activity was subsequently measured to assess the transcriptional regulatory effects. According to the GUS assays, an obvious difference in GUS activity was observed between the two reporters, 1634 bp GhNAC140‐Dt promoter fragment (pF1) and 459 bp LTR2 plus pF1, in the absence of any effector (CK panel in Figure 3c). The GUS activity of the LTR2 plus pF1 reporter was 25.23% higher than that of the pF1 reporter. The most pronounced difference in GUS activity between two reporters occurred under the influence of GhMYB46 protein, reaching 88.06%. Under the influence of other transcription factors, the differences in GUS activity were 31.29%, 24.19%, 20.83%, 11.64%, and −1.63%, respectively (Figure 3c). These results indicate that, compared to proteins that can be recruited by both 459 bp LTR2 and 1634 bp promoter of GhNAC140‐Dt, the GhMYB46 protein, specifically recruited by LTR2, plays a more critical role in driving the expression of the downstream gene.

To delineate the GhMYB46 binding site within the 150 bp LTR2‐2 sequence, we subdivided it into three 60 bp fragments (LTR2‐2‐1, LTR2‐2‐2, and LTR2‐2‐3). Dual‐LUC assays showed that GhMYB46 specifically binds to the LTR2‐2‐1 region (Figure 3d). This specific interaction was further confirmed using yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays (Figure 3e). Additionally, electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) were performed to assess direct GhMYB46 binding in vitro. Results demonstrated that GhMYB46 protein bound specifically to the biotin‐labelled LTR2‐2‐1 probe, but not to three mutated probes (Figure 3f). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the LTR2‐2‐1 sequence specifically recruits the transcription factor GhMYB46, thereby elevating the expression of downstream genes.

Overexpression of GhNAC140‐Dt Increases Lint Yield

Subcellular localization confirmed that GhNAC140‐Dt is a nuclear protein, as evidenced by the co‐localization of GhNAC140‐Dt‐enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) green fluorescent signals with the red signals of the mCherry‐fused nuclear marker histone 2B (H2B) (Figure 4a). To investigate the functional role of GhNAC140‐Dt in cotton fibre development, we generated transgenic cotton lines overexpressing or suppressing the gene, using constructs driven by the constitutive 35S promoter. Nine independent overexpression (OE) lines and three RNA interference (RNAi) lines were obtained. Homozygous GhNAC140‐Dt OE lines and GhNAC140‐Dt RNAi lines were confirmed at the T3 generation by PCR‐based genotyping. RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed significant upregulation of GhNAC140‐Dt expression in six OE lines and downregulation in all three RNAi lines (Figure S5). Thus, we selected two OE lines (OE‐43, OE‐94) and two RNAi lines (R‐34, R‐52), which exhibited higher and lower transcript levels respectively, for further analysis (Figure 4b).

We first evaluated the impact on the cotton yield trait of lint percentage and lint index. Both the lint percentage and lint index were significantly increased in GhNAC140‐Dt OE lines compared to wild‐type (WT) plants, whereas GhNAC140‐Dt‐RNAi lines showed an obvious decrease (Figure 4c,d). Given that GhNAC140‐Dt is a NAC transcription factor implicated in secondary cell wall development, we detected the cell wall thickness of cotton fibres. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of fibres at 20 DPA and maturation demonstrated that GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression increased secondary cell wall thickness, whereas RNAi‐mediated suppression resulted in thinner cell walls (Figure 4e,f). Furthermore, we quantified the fibre cellulose content at 20 DPA and maturation. GhNAC140‐Dt OE lines exhibited significantly higher cellulose content than WT, while GhNAC140‐Dt‐RNAi lines showed reduced content (Figure 4g,h). In addition, the seed index showed no significant difference between transgenic lines and WT (Figure S6a), demonstrating a role for GhNAC140‐Dt in regulating cotton fibre yield without affecting seed development.

Furthermore, we detected the fibre quality traits of the GhNAC140‐Dt transgenic cotton lines. Results revealed that GhNAC140‐Dt OE lines produced significantly shorter fibres, whereas RNAi lines yielded longer fibres compared to the WT (Figure S6b,c). Moreover, fibre strength was significantly reduced in GhNAC140‐Dt OE lines but increased in RNAi lines (Figure S6d). This finding appears to contradict the conventional view that secondary cell wall thickening generally leads to enhanced fibre strength. Previous studies have indicated that fibre strength is not solely determined by cell wall thickness but is also closely associated with fibre structural features, such as the twist degree and microfibril arrangement in mature fibres (Wu et al. 2025). Therefore, we further examined the twist degree and microfibril arrangement in mature cotton fibres. The results revealed that the twist degree of mature fibres was significantly reduced in the overexpression line relative to WT, whereas it was markedly increased in the RNAi line (Figure 5a). Concurrently, the cellulose microfibril in the overexpression line exhibited a more dispersed and disordered arrangement compared to WT. In contrast, the RNAi line showed finer and denser packing of microfibrils (Figure 5b). Thus, although GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression increased cell wall thickness, the disordered microfibril alignment and a decreased fibre twist degree in GhNAC140‐Dt OE fibres resulted in a net reduction in fibre strength.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that overexpression of GhNAC140‐Dt increases lint percentage by enhancing secondary cell wall thickness, at the expense of fibre quality due to its persistent overexpression during fibre development in the artificially produced OE lines.

GhNAC140‐Dt Modulates Cell Wall Organisation and Biogenesis by Directly Regulating GhCESA4‐At/Dt and GhCOBL9‐At Expression

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which GhNAC140‐Dt modulates cotton fibre development, we first analysed transcriptomes from 20 DPA fibres of GhNAC140‐Dt transgenic lines and WT plants. Clustering analysis of the transcriptome data demonstrated that the three replicates of each sample grouped together, confirming the consistency among biological replicates (Figure S7). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using thresholds of |log2 (fold change)| > 1 and adjusted p‐value < 0.05. Compared to WT, 2190 and 663 upregulated DEGs were identified in OE‐43 and OE‐94 lines, respectively, yielding 457 common upregulated DEGs across both overexpression lines (Figure 6a, Table S2). Similarly, 379 and 647 downregulated DEGs were detected in R‐34 and R‐52 RNAi lines, respectively, with 206 common downregulated DEGs (Figure 6c, Table S3). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the top 20 enriched terms for common upregulated DEGs in GhNAC140‐Dt OE lines were predominantly associated with cell wall organisation and biosynthesis (Figure 6b, Table S4). Common downregulated DEGs in GhNAC140‐Dt RNAi lines similarly showed enrichment for external cell wall organisation and biogenesis terms (Figure 6d, Table S5). Complementarily, we performed in vitro DNA affinity purification sequencing (DAP‐seq) to identify genome‐wide direct targets of GhNAC140‐Dt, following established protocols (Bartlett et al. 2017). After filtering nonspecific binding peaks against control samples, 142 350 peaks were enriched in GhNAC140‐Dt pulldowns, with 13 572 high‐confidence binding peaks localised within 2 kb promoter regions upstream of candidate target genes (Figure 6e, Table S6). GO enrichment of these target genes identified plant‐type secondary cell wall biogenesis and cell wall organisation or biogenesis as top categories (Figure 6f, Table S7). These omics data collectively indicate that GhNAC140‐Dt regulates cotton fibre development through modulation of cell wall processes.

Next, we integrated multi‐omics data to identify candidate downstream genes regulated by GhNAC140‐Dt. Three common genes (GH_D07G2262, GH_A07G2317, and GH_A08G0578) were identified through integration of the upregulated DEGs in OE lines, the downregulated DEGs in RNAi lines, and genes associated with cell wall biosynthesis or organisation from DAP‐sequencing analysis (Figure 6g). Interestingly, GH_D07G2262 and GH_A07G2317 encode homologous genes of CESA4 (cellulose synthase 4) from the Dt and At subgenomes of tetraploid cotton, respectively, designated GhCESA4‐Dt and GhCESA4‐At. GH_A08G0578 encodes a previously characterised cobra‐like 9 protein (GhCOBL9‐At) (He et al. 2024). Transcriptome data confirmed that these three genes were upregulated in GhNAC140‐Dt OE lines and downregulated in RNAi lines (Figure 6g). To gain insight into the expression patterns of the three candidate genes, we analysed previously published transcriptome data from the G. hirsutum acc. TM‐1 (Hu et al. 2019). The expression patterns of these genes in cotton tissues were also highly similar to that of GhNAC140‐Dt (Figure 6h–j).

RT‐qPCR confirmed significant upregulation of GhCESA4‐At/Dt and GhCOBL9‐At in OE lines and downregulation in RNAi lines (Figure 7a,b). To validate direct regulation, we performed dual‐luciferase assays in N. benthamiana. Promoters of GhCESA4‐Dt, GhCESA4‐At, and GhCOBL9‐At were positioned upstream of LUC to generate reporters, while GhNAC140‐Dt was driven by the 35S promoter as the effector (Figure 7c,d). The REN gene contained in the reporter construct was used as the internal control. Compared to the empty reporter control, the LUC/REN ratio was significantly higher when co‐transformed the tested reporters and effector (Figure 7c,d), demonstrating transcriptional activation by GhNAC140‐Dt on these promoters. Putative binding sites (PBS) were predicted within target promoters using DAP‐seq data (Figure 7e). Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) with biotin‐labelled 50 bp PBS probes (PBS‐GhCESA4‐Dt, PBS‐GhCESA4‐At, PBS‐GhCOBL9‐At) confirmed specific GhNAC140‐Dt binding, with competition observed using unlabeled probes (Figure 7f). Yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) assays provided further validation. Yeast strains co‐transformed with pGADT7‐GhNAC140‐Dt and pAbAi‐PBS‐GhCESA4‐Dt grew robustly on selective medium containing 200 ng/mL Aureobasidin A (AbA), whereas strains containing the empty pGADT7 vector did not (Figure 7g). Identical results were obtained for the PBS‐GhCESA4‐At and PBS‐GhCOBL9‐At sequences (Figure 7g). Collectively, these results demonstrate that GhNAC140‐Dt directly binds to specific promoter elements within GhCESA4‐Dt, GhCESA4‐At, and GhCOBL9‐At to regulate their expression.

Evolutionary Analysis and Breeding Utilisation of the Retrotransposon Insertion in GhNAC140‐Dt Promoter

As shown in Figure 1c, two haplotypes of GhNAC140‐Dt exist within upland cotton accessions, defined by the presence (GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 ) or absence (GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 ) of a retrotransposon insertion. To investigate the evolutionary origin of these haplotypes, we utilised the molecular marker described in Figure 1c to detect the different cotton species and accessions (Table S8–S10). Results revealed that the putative tetraploid cotton genome donor, G. arboreum (AA) or G. herbaceum (AA) and G. raimondii (DD), as well as three wild allotetraploid cotton (AADD) ( G. tomentosum (AD)3, G. mustelinum (AD)4, and G. darwinii (AD)5), exclusively possess GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 without retrotransposon insertion (Figure 8a, Figure S8 and Table S8). We further assessed the insertion frequency across the seven G. hirsutum races, including yucatanense, punctatum, palmeri, latifolium, marie‐galante, morrilli, and richmondi (Hutchinson 1951). The insertion was absent in G. hirsutum race yucatanense and G. hirsutum race palmeri. However, it was detected at increasing frequencies of 5% in G. hirsutum race morrilli and G. hirsutum race marie‐galante rising to 35% in G. hirsutum race punctatum and 45% in G. hirsutum race latifolium (Figure 8a, Figure S8 and Table S8). Among these races, G. hirsutum race yucatanense represents a wild form considered the most ancestral, while G. hirsutum race latifolium is the progenitor from which modern cultivated upland cotton was derived (Lubbers and Chee 2009). Collectively, these findings suggest the retrotransposon insertion within the GhNAC140‐Dt promoter originated from the evolution of G. hirsutum race to cultivated accession. Next, we assessed whether these haplotypes underwent artificial selection during cotton domestication. Among 614 tested G. hirsutum cultivars/accessions, 71.01% harboured GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 , a frequency significantly higher than that observed in G. hirsutum races (Figure 8a, Figure S8 and Table S9). In addition, we screened a set of G. barbadense natural population with 269 accession and found only 1.86% accessions harbouring GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 (Figure 8a, Figure S8 and Table S10). These results indicate the retrotransposon insertion overwhelmingly exists in G. hirsutum during domestication.

Next, we conducted selective sweep analysis using the available genomic data from 34 semi‐wild accessions, 42 landraces, and 292 modern varieties. For the genetic differentiation index (Fst) analysis, we conducted pairwise comparisons among the semi‐wild accessions, landraces, and modern variety groups. The results revealed high Fst values between the semi‐wild group and both the landrace and modern variety groups on chromosome Dt‐12 around the LTR retrotransposon insertion site (Figure S9a,b). Interestingly, the Fst between landraces and modern varieties was low at this locus (Figure S9c). For the nucleotide diversity (π) analysis, we conducted the analysis in the semi‐wild accessions, landraces, and modern variety groups respectively. The results showed that the π value around the LTR insertion site was lower in the modern variety group compared to the landrace and semi‐wild groups (Figure S9d). This pattern indicates a continued reduction of diversity through more intense artificial selection during modern breeding from landraces following the initial domestication from semi‐wild to landraces. Collectively, these results provide direct population genetics evidence that the genomic region harbouring LTR retrotransposon insertion has undergone selective sweeps, supporting its role as a target of artificial selection during cotton domestication and improvement.

We further analysed the retrotransposon insertion of 305 upland cotton commercial cultivars released in four major cotton‐producing regions in China: the Yangtze River Region (YZR), Yellow River Region (YRR), Northern Specific Early Maturation Region (NSER), and Northwest Inland Region (NIR), with introduced founders as controls (Table S9). Historical records indicate that early‐maturing cultivars bred in NSER predominantly derive from ‘King's Cotton’, an early‐maturing germplasm introduced from the United States (Wang and Wang 1982). Molecular marker analysis confirmed that ‘King's Cotton’ carries the GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 allele, explaining high frequency of GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 in NSER varieties (Figure 8b). Early planted cotton varieties in China were derived from a few germplasm sources, primarily from the United States with minor contributions from other countries and regions (Chen and Du 2006). Haplotype analysis of 23 landraces revealed approximately 60% carried the favourable GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 allele (Table S9). This frequency is significantly lower than the > 80% observed in modern Chinese varieties in major cotton‐producing regions (Figure 8b, Figure S8). Furthermore, we analysed the retrotransposon insertion of 305 upland cotton commercial cultivars based on their released years in China and found that the proportion of GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 remained largely consistent across different breeding years (Figure 8c). These results indicate that this superior GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 haplotype has been artificially selected and retained during upland cotton breeding programs in China following its important contribution on high lint percentage and fibre yield.

Discussion

TEs are fundamental drivers of plant evolution and agronomic trait innovation, yet their underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms remain largely unexplored. Here, we characterise a Copia‐type retrotransposon insertion within the GhNAC140‐Dt promoter as a key genetic determinant of cotton lint yield (Figure 9). We demonstrate that a functional 60 bp cis‐regulatory module in the LTR recruits the transcription factor GhMYB46 to activate downstream GhNAC140‐Dt expression, representing the first definitive characterisation of a TE‐derived transcriptional switch mechanism in crops. Transgenic validation confirmed that GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression significantly elevates the expression levels of GhCESA4‐At/Dt and GhCOBL9‐At to thicken the cell wall of cotton fibres and ultimately enhance lint percentage. Evolutionary analyses further revealed that this insertion event originated during the domestication transition from semi‐wild G. hirsutum races to cultivated accessions, establishing it as a molecular fossil of artificial selection for yield improvement. Our work uncovers a TE‐mediated gene regulatory paradigm for crop improvement, illustrating how evolutionary “genomic scars” can be harnessed for agricultural innovation.

Transposons, as mobile genetic elements within the genome, can profoundly modify gene expression levels by inserting into regulatory regions such as promoters, enhancers, or untranslated regions (Bonchev and Parisod 2013). Although large‐scale studies demonstrate that TE insertions frequently induce transcriptional silencing via repressive epigenetic modifications (Sigman and Slotkin 2016; Zhang, Lang, and Zhu 2018), they also act as critical drivers of gene upregulation. For example, a retrotransposon insertion within the OsUBC12 promoter enhances transcription and low‐temperature germination capability in japonica rice (Zhang et al. 2024). While the regulatory impact of LTR retrotransposons on downstream gene expression has not been extensively studied, motif‐centric analyses of LTR sequences remain underexplored. In this study, we elucidate how retrotransposons modulate expression of the cotton gene GhNAC140‐Dt. We first observed that a TE insertion in the promoter of GhNAC140‐Dt significantly elevates its expression relative to its At subgenome homologue GhNAC140‐At, which lacks this insertion (Figure 1b,c). This transposon‐mediated upregulation was further confirmed using GUS staining (Figure 2a,c). Subsequent motif analysis of the 5108 bp LTR insertion identified transcription factor binding sites within a critical 60 bp LTR sub‐region, LTR2‐2‐1. We demonstrate that this sequence recruits the transcription factor GhMYB46, which binds to the LTR but not to the original GhNAC140‐Dt promoter (Figure 3a–c). Notably, the GhMYB46 binding site in LTR2‐2‐1 does not match any documented MYB protein binding motif, indicating that LTR insertions create novel, uncharacterized cis‐regulatory elements. The introduction of these novel cis‐regulatory elements signifies that the transposable elements play a pivotal role in the expansion of gene regulatory networks and thereby drive functional evolution across species. Moreover, sequence alignment of the 60 bp LTR sub‐region across Hap1 cotton accessions revealed no SNP or InDel sequence polymorphisms within this module. This finding indicates that the 60 bp cis‐regulatory sequence is conserved among accessions that harbour the retrotransposon, which indicates its functional importance as a regulatory element that was likely fixed during selection. This work pioneers a mechanism‐centric approach to investigate retrotransposon‐mediated gene regulation through LTR‐encoded motifs. Given the agronomic importance of lint percentage in cotton breeding, these LTR insertion sites and their associated motifs may serve as valuable molecular markers for selecting high‐lint‐yield cultivars.

Generally, the cell wall formation is generally correlated with fibre quality traits. However, in our population analysis, we found that the LTR insertion was significantly associated with increased lint percentage and lint index, but not with fibre quality traits across multiple environments (Figure 1d,e and Figure S3). This suggests that the natural variation of LTR insertion in the GhNAC140‐Dt promoter primarily affects yield‐related traits rather than quality traits under field conditions. It is well‐known that cotton fibre quality traits are complex quantitative traits, and their final phenotypic value is influenced by multiple genes as well as environmental factors. The LTR insertion in the promoter can enhance the expression of GhNAC140‐Dt, but such an effect may not be strong enough to induce subtle changes in cell wall formation that could influence fibre quality traits. Furthermore, the impact of different genetic backgrounds and environmental conditions on the formation of fibre quality traits may ultimately result in no statistically detectable changes in the population analysis.

Lint percentage in cotton exhibits a positive correlation with cell wall thickness in cotton fibres (Gao et al. 2021). Cellulose biosynthesis, a key determinant of SCW thickness, critically influences both fibre development and lint percentage. In this study, we demonstrate that GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression promotes cellulose deposition in the fibre SCW, significantly increasing both SCW thickness and lint percentage (Figure 4c–h). Furthermore, GhNAC140‐Dt positively regulates direct downstream targets GhCESA4‐At/Dt and GhCOBL9‐At (Figure 7c–g). Interestingly, overexpression of GhNAC140‐Dt leads to thicker cell walls but reduced fibre strength, whereas its suppression results in thinner walls yet increased strength. Previous studies have also established that GhCesA4 and GhCOBL9 are known positive regulators of fibre strength (Wen et al. 2022; He et al. 2024), which appears contradictory. Indeed, the determinants of cotton fibre strength are complex, involving not only cell wall thickness but also cell wall composition, the twist degree of mature fibres, and the deposition pattern of cellulose microfibrils. Several studies have demonstrated that fibre strength can be enhanced through optimization of cell wall structure without changes in thickness or even with thinner walls. A notable example is the difference between Sea Island cotton and Upland cotton. Mature fibres of Sea Island cotton exhibit superior strength despite having a thinner cell wall compared to Upland cotton. This is attributed to two key structural advantages in Sea Island cotton: an increased fibre twist degree which demonstrated as more spirals per unit fibre length and a finer, denser packing of cellulose microfibrils in the fibres (Wu et al. 2025). Transgenic evidence further illustrates this principle. Overexpression of a NAC transcription factor GbNTL9 increases fibre strength without altering cell wall thickness, primarily by making the accumulation and arrangement of cellulose microfibrils on the fibre surface more uniform, neat, and compact (Wu et al. 2025). Overexpression of the UBX domain‐containing protein GhUBX leads to thinner cell walls but significantly higher fibre strength, largely due to an increased fibre twist degree (Zang et al. 2021). Similar phenotypes that thinner cell walls but increased fibre strength have been also reported for overexpression of GhWLIM1a and GbEXPATR genes (Han et al. 2016; Li et al. 2016). These findings collectively indicate that cell wall thickness is not always positively correlated with fibre strength. In this study, the twist degree of mature fibres was significantly reduced in GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression lines but markedly increased in RNAi lines compared to the wild‐type control (Figure 5a). Furthermore, fibres from overexpression lines exhibited a more dispersed and disordered arrangement of cellulose microfibrils, whereas RNAi fibres displayed finer and denser packing of microfibrils (Figure 5b). Thus, we propose that the abnormally high expression of GhNAC140‐Dt in the overexpression lines causes excessive and dysregulated activation of its downstream targets, including the cellulose biosynthesis machinery. This leads to disordered cellulose deposition, manifested as disorganised microfibril alignment and a reduced fibre twist degree, which ultimately compromises the structural integrity and strength of the fibre. Although GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression thickens the cell wall, the cotton fibre strength reduced due to disordered structure of the cell wall and decreased twist degree of mature fibres. These results illustrate a typical trade‐off between cotton yield and quality. CRISPR‐mediated coordinated editing of multi‐gene networks, which enables simultaneous modulation of functionally linked genes to optimise cellulose deposition while mitigating trade‐offs between fibre strength and lint percentage, holds significant promise for future simultaneous improvement of cotton fibre yield and quality.

Upland cotton ( G. hirsutum ) derives its agronomic traits from functional divergence and synergistic interactions between its At and Dt subgenomes. Within the Dt subgenome, two distinct haplotypes of the GhNAC140‐Dt promoter (GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 and GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 ) have been identified across cultivars, differentiated primarily by a retrotransposon insertion specific to the Dt subgenome (Figure 1c). Molecular marker‐assisted analysis of these haplotypes revealed that both putative tetraploid cotton genome donors ( G. arboreum or G. herbaceum and G. raimondii) and three wild tetraploid species ( G. darwinii , G. mustelinum, and G. tomentosum ) exclusively harbour the GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 allele (Figure 8a). Analysis of seven G. hirsutum semi‐wild races demonstrated that paleo‐landraces (yucatanense and palmeri) retained the ancestral GhNAC140‐Dt Hap2 state (Figure 8a). In contrast, the latifolium race, phylogenetically closest to modern cultivars, exhibited the highest GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 frequency. Notably, only five GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 were detected across 269 G. barbadense accessions (Figure 8a), further substantiating the independent domestication origins of G. barbadense and G. hirsutum (Hu et al. 2019). Population genotyping of 614 indigenous G. hirsutum accessions confirmed strong human‐mediated selection for GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 during cotton domestication and breeding (Figure 8a), and phenotyping of natural populations established GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 as the superior allele for enhancing lint percentage and lint index (Figure 1d,e). Transgenic validation further demonstrated that GhNAC140‐Dt expression positively regulates lint percentage. The predominance of GhNAC140‐Dt Hap1 in commercial cultivars reflects decades of breeding prioritisation for lint yield improvement by enhancing lint percentage.

Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

A total of 614 G. hirsutum cultivars/accessions and 269 G. barbadense accessions were collected from different regions of China and major cotton‐growing countries around the world. All the cultivars/accessions from these countries, including China, the former Soviet Union, the United States, Egypt, Sudan, and Albania, have all been maintained by self‐pollination at the Engineering Research Center of the Ministry of Education for Cotton Germplasm Enhancement and Application of Nanjing Agricultural University. The above‐mentioned cotton accessions, plus GhNAC140‐Dt transgenic cotton lines and WT, were planted in the field with normal field management at the Wanjiang Cotton Experimental Station of Nanjing Agricultural University (118° E, 32° N) for sampling and phenotyping investigation. Cotton flowers were tagged on the day of anthesis and labelled as 0 day post anthesis (DPA) at flowering day. Fibres at various developmental stages were carefully sampled and frozen in liquid N2 immediately, then stored at −70°C until use. Nicotiana benthamiana plants used for subcellular localization and interaction analysis were grown in the greenhouse at Nanjing Agricultural University (16 h: 8 h, light:dark, 28°C: 25°C).

The leaves of diploid G. arboreum and G. raimondii, three wild allotetraploid cotton, G. tomentosum , G. mustelinum, and G. darwinii , and 94 G. hirsutum semi‐domesticated accessions (involved in seven races) were collected from the Wild Cotton Resources Garden of Institute of Cotton Research, The Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, for DNA isolation. The information of plant materials used in this study is provided as Tables S8–S10.

Plasmid Construction and Plant Transformation

To make the overexpression construct, the open reading frame (ORF) of GhNAC140‐Dt was cloned into a pCAMBIA2301 vector, at the BamH I and Kpn I restriction sites downstream of 35S promoter to generate the GhNAC140‐Dt overexpression vector. In the construction of the RNA interference (RNAi) vector, the first intron (453 bp) of GhNAC140‐Dt gene was inserted into a pKANNIBAL vector. GhNAC140‐Dt RNAi construct was produced by inserting a 453 bp specific sequence of GhNAC140‐Dt gene into the pKANNIBAL vector to create an inverted repeat transgene. These plasmids were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain LBA4404. The A. tumefaciens ‐mediated cotton transformation was conducted following the method as previously reported (Li et al. 2009). The transgenic lines underwent continuous self‐pollination to obtain homozygous lines in the greenhouse at Nanjing Agricultural University. The presence of the transgene in the T0 cotton plants was examined by PCR amplification. The primers used are detailed in Table S11.

Subcellular Localization

The coding sequence of GhNAC140‐Dt was cloned upstream of eGFP to generate pBIN‐35S::GhNAC140‐Dt‐eGFP4 construct with Kpn I and BamH I restriction enzyme sites. The primers used are listed in Table S11. Then, the constructs were subsequently transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. The pBIN‐35S::eGFP4 vector or recombinant vector was transiently co‐expressed with a mCherry labelled plasma nuclear‐localised AtHTB2‐mCherry in leaves following the infiltration method (Song et al. 2021). After injection for 3 days, the fluorescence signal was observed and photographed using the Zeiss LSM780 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany). The GFP signals were detected using a confocal laser scanning microscope at the specific excitation and emission wavelengths (eGFP, 488 and 507 nm; mCherry, 587 and 610 nm).

RNA Extraction and RT‐qPCR Analysis

Total RNAs were isolated from different tissues using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China). Total RNA was reversely transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript RT SuperMix (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Inc., Nanjing, China) for subsequent analysis. The primers used are listed in Table S11. The Cotton Histone 3 (AF024716) acted as a reference gene for normalization (Shang et al. 2015). The Roche 480 PCR System was used for RT‐qPCR with AceQ SYBR Green Master (Vazyme Inc., China).

Microscopy of Cotton Fibres

Cotton fibres (20 DPA) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in phosphate buffer solution (PBS, 0.1 M, pH 7.4). These fibres were resin‐embedded in resin SPI812. Treated fibre samples (cross sections) were examined and photographed under a transmission electron microscope (TEM) HT7800 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Cell wall thickness was measured using IMAGE J software (Collins 2007). The twist degree and microfibril arrangement of mature fibres were observed and photographed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) Regulus 8100 (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Three biological replicates were analysed for each sample.

Determination of Cellulose Contents in Cotton Fibres

Cotton fibres (20 DPA and mature) were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. The powders were washed sequentially three times with 80% ethanol, 1:1 (v/v) methanol/chloroform, and twice with acetone. The resulting residues (alcohol‐insoluble residues, AIRs) were followed by treatment with type‐I porcine a‐amylase (Sigma) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to eliminate starch. The dried AIRs were subsequently treated with UPdegraff reagent (HOAc:H2O:HNO3, 8:2:1, v/v/v) in boiling water for 30 min, sequentially resuspended using distilled water and acetone, and washed twice. After drying, 0.5 mL distilled water was added and thoroughly mixed; 0.75 mL concentrated sulfuric acid was slowly added in an ice water bath, and incubation was conducted for 1 h. The samples were centrifuged at 9600 g for 3 min, and the supernatant was collected for analysis. The crystalline cellulose content was quantified using the anthrone assay (Han et al. 2016).

Fibre Yield and Quality Assessment

Approximately 25 mature cotton bolls were collected from comparable middle sections of fruit branches in the field. After drying, the fibres were ginned from the cotton seeds. Lint percentage is the proportion of fibre (lint) mass relative to the total mass of seed cotton (fibre plus seeds). Boll weight is the average mass of seed cotton (fibre plus seeds) of each boll. Lint index is the absolute weight of fibre (lint) produced per 100 seeds. Seed index is the absolute weight of 100 cotton seeds. Then, the mature fibre quality traits, including fibre length, strength, and micronaire were measured using a high‐volume fibre test system (Premier HFT 9000, Premier Evolvics Pvt. Ltd., Coimbatore, India) at the Center of Cotton Fibre Quality Inspection and Testing, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs (Anyang, Henan Province, China). Three biological replicates were used for each sample.

Transcriptome Sequencing and Data Analysis

The 20 DPA fibres of GhNAC140‐Dt transgenic lines and wild type ( G. hirsutum acc. W0) were collected for RNA‐seq analysis. Total RNA samples were extracted and used for constructing sequencing libraries. The sequencing process was conducted using the Illumina HiSeq2000 platform. The clean sequencing reads were mapped to the genome of G. hirsutum acc. TM‐1 using Hisat2 software (Kim et al. 2015). The number of matched reads aligning to the TM‐1 genome was determined using HTSeq software and then imported into DESEQ R package (version 1.18.0) for analysis of differentially expressed genes (fold change [FC] > 2, p < 0.01) (Anders and Huber 2010). The gene expression values were subjected to normalisation using the fragments per kilobase per million fragments (FPKM) values analysis method. GO analysis for DEGs in the biological process was conducted using the AgriGO software (Du et al. 2010). All samples contained three biological replicates.

Yeast One‐Hybrid Assay

The Matchmaker Gold Yeast One‐Hybrid Library Screening System (Clontech) was employed to assess the interaction between GhMYB46 and the selected retrotransposon sequence, GhNAC140‐Dt and the promoters of downstream genes, respectively. The primers used are listed in Table S11. The plasmids were transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae Y1H Gold strain to generate bait‐reporter yeast strains and subsequently cultured on a selective SD medium lacking Ura/Leu. The cultures were further used to test for the minimal inhibitory concentration of Aureobasidin A (AbA). This experimental setup was conducted using the MATCHMAKER One‐Hybrid System (Clonetech, USA).

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay

The 1167 bp coding sequence of GhNAC140‐Dt was cloned and inserted into the pGEX‐4 T‐2 vector which was transformed into E. coli strain BL21 (DE3). E. coli cells were incubated with 0.5 mM isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) overnight at 16°C and collected by centrifugation. The GhNAC140‐Dt protein was purified using GST protein purification magnetic beads (Cat#M2320, Solarbio, China). A Lightshift Chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Thermo, USA) was utilised according to the manufacturer's protocol for the electrophoretic mobility shift assay according to a previously described method (Hou et al. 2014). The primers used are listed in Table S11.

Transient Expression Assays in N. benthamiana Leaves

To analyze the promoter activity of short sequences, the mini35S promoter was inserted into the pGreenII0800‐LUC vector to construct a transformed pGreenII0800‐LUC vector. The different promoter region sections of GhNAC140‐Dt were amplified and inserted into the transformed pGreenII0800‐LUC vector with Kpn I/Hind III sites, used as the reporters, while the full‐length ORF of different genes was cloned into the pBI121 vector with BamH I/Sac I sites, used as the effector. In addition, the 1679 bp promoter region of GhNAC140‐Dt was amplified and inserted into the pGreenII0800‐LUC vector with Kpn I/BamH I sites as the reporter. All constructs were verified via sequencing and transformed into A. tumefaciens (GV3101‐pSoup).

A. tumefaciens ‐mediated transformation of N. benthamiana leaf cells used a mixture of cells carrying the effector and reporter at a 3:1 ratio. N. benthamiana leaves infected with reporter and original effector were used as the negative control. The infiltrated N. benthamiana plants were incubated dark for 16 h and then were moved to normal growth conditions for 2 days (16 h: 8 h, light:dark, 28°C: 25°C). The dual‐luciferase assay was performed in N. benthamiana leaves following a previously established protocol (Fang et al. 2023). The ratio of LUC/REN represents the promoter activity. Three replicates were used for each transient expression assay.

For the β‐glucuronidase (GUS) assay, different promoter sections of mini35S::GUS with Hind III and BamH I restriction enzyme sites and different genes of 35S::Gene vectors were constructed and used as reporter and effector, respectively. A. tumefaciens ‐mediated transformation of N. benthamiana leaf cells was same as described in LUC assay. N. benthamiana leaves infected with reporter of 35S::GUS only were used as the positive control, whereas leaves infected with various reporters were used as the negative control. The infiltrated N. benthamiana plants were incubated in the dark for 16 h and then were moved to normal growth conditions for 2 days (16 h: 8 h, light:dark, 28°C: 25°C). GUS staining of the N. benthamiana leaves was performed following the histochemical method described previously (Jefferson et al. 1987). The determination of GUS enzyme activity was performed using GUS enzyme activity kit (Cat# SL7161‐50 t, Coolaber, China).

DAP‐Seq Analysis

DNA Affinity Purification Sequencing (DAP‐Seq) is an in vitro technique employed for the identification of DNA binding motifs targeted by target protein. The DAP‐seq method is implemented based on the previously established scheme (Bartlett et al. 2017). Its core principle involves the in vitro synthesis of the target protein using a cell‐free expression system, followed by its incubation with a library of fragmented genomic DNA for affinity‐based capture. The CDS of GhNAC140‐Dt was cloned into a pUC18‐Halo vector. Subsequently, the protein‐DNA complexes are purified via affinity chromatography. The bound DNA fragments, recovered by elution, are then subjected to high‐throughput sequencing. Primers used are listed in Table S11. Motif identification of sequences in 100 bp of upstream and downstream of the peaks determined using the MEME‐ChIP analysis tool in MEME Suite (Bailey et al. 2015).

Transient Transformation System for Gene Characterisation

The different promoter regions of GhNAC140‐Dt were amplified and inserted into the pBI121 vector with Hind III / Xba I sites. All constructs were verified via sequencing and transformed into A. tumefaciens (LBA4404). A. tumefaciens liquid cultures were grown at 28°C overnight in YEP medium containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin and 50 μg/mL rifampicin until the OD600 reached between 1.4 and 1.8. Cells were collected by centrifugation (4000 rpm, 10 min) and re‐suspended to an OD600 0.8–1.0 in transformation solution: 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium, 165 μM acetosyringone (AS) and 3% (w/v) sucrose. The transformation solution was degassed using a diaphragm‐type dry vacuum pump under 0.07 Mpa pressure for 3 min before use. The ovule used from 20 DPA cotton bolls cleaned with 70% ethanol on the epidermis. The ovules were completely immersed in the transformation solution and treated with a diaphragm‐type dry vacuum pump under 0.07 Mpa pressure for 2 min before adding 0.01% (v/v) Tween 20. Samples were shaken at 25°C in dark for 3 h (120 rpm). After the co‐cultivation, the samples were washed over five times with sterile ddH2O, for 2 min each time. Then samples were transferred onto 1/2 MS medium, pH 5.8, 0.3% (w/v) agarose that contained kanamycin 50 μg/mL, and rifampicin 50 μg/mL and kept under long‐day conditions (16 h/8 h light/dark photoperiod) and 26/20°C day/night temperatures for 1 day. The samples were washed three times with sterile ddH2O after greenhouse cultivation. GUS staining of the N. benthamiana leaves was performed following the histochemical method described previously (Zhang, Lang, and Zhu 2018). The determination of GUS enzyme activity was performed using GUS enzyme activity kit (Cat# SL7161‐50 t, Coolaber, China).

Selective Sweep Analysis

Vcftools were used to calculate population fixation statistics (Fst) with a sliding window of 20 kb and step size of 5 kb. Nucleotide diversity was calculated using vcftools with a 40 kb sliding window based on genotypes in different groups separately (Danecek et al. 2011).

Statistical Analysis

All values represented the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed by one‐way ANOVA (the p‐values are provided in the Source data) or two‐tailed Student's t‐test (the p‐values are provided in the figures). * represents significant differences at p < 0.05 level and ** represents significant differences at p < 0.01 level, respectively.
