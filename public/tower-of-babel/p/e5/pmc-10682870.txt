Incidence, mortality, and predictors of acute kidney injury in patients with heart failure: a systematic review
Authors: Song‐Chao Ru, Shu‐Bin Lv, Zhi‐Juan Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute kidney injury (AKI) is common in patients with heart failure (HF), but studies have been inconsistent about the incidence of AKI in patients with HF. We conducted a meta‐analysis to examine the incidence of AKI and its impact on mortality in patients with HF. We also looked at inpatient variables that could predict the development of AKI to identify potential risk factors, so that these can be used as a starting point for intervention and prevention in this group. The Embase, Medline, PubMed, Cochrane libraries, and Web of Science databases were used for searching articles from the inception of the database to October 2022. The EndNote software was used for screening. Meta‐analysis was performed using Stata 16.0 software to combine effect sizes. A total of 37 studies were included. Of all the 3 533 583 patients with HF, 774 887 had AKI, with a pooled incidence of 33% [95% confidence interval (CI): 32–35%]. The incidence rate of AKI in acute HF and chronic HF was 36% (95% CI: 31–40%) and 30% (95% CI: 24–35%), respectively. Eleven studies found that AKI patients had higher in‐hospital mortality than non‐AKI patients [risk ratio (RR): 3.65; 95% CI: 3.04–4.39, P < 0.001]. Mortality was assessed in five studies, and it was found that mortality remained high at 1‐year follow‐up after onset of AKI (RR: 1.85, 95% CI: 1.54–2.22, P < 0.001). Fifteen admission variables were included and analysed in 13 studies. The combined results showed that diabetes, hypertension, history of chronic kidney disease, chronic HF systolic, age, N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide, creatinine > 1.0 mg/dL, index estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, blood urea nitrogen > 24 mg/dL, intravenous dobutamine, and serum albumin were predictor factors for HF patients with AKI (P < 0.05). In this meta‐analysis, AKI occurred in approximately 33% of HF patients during hospitalization and the risk of dying in the hospital was tripled. Even during 1‐year long‐term follow‐up, the risk of death remained high, and multiple inpatient variables showed that HF patients tended to have AKI. Early intervention and treatment are important to reduce the incidence of AKI and improve the prognosis.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a common event in the natural disease history of patients with heart failure (HF). The specific mechanism for this remains unclear, and possible explanations are as follows: Acute heart failure (AHF) is characterized by weakened left ventricular systolic function and insufficient cardiac output. This triggers compensatory mechanisms such as the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system, sympathetic nervous system, and other local mediators. These compensatory mechanisms interact to maintain fluid volume. An imbalance between these compensatory mechanisms will lead to inadequate cardiac output and capacity overloading. In addition, decreased renal perfusion, coupled with nephrotoxic drugs and excessive diuresis, ultimately leads to AKI in these patients.

Over the past two decades, there have been significant changes in the definition of AKI. Various studies have employed different criteria to define and classify AKI. According to the RIFLE criteria formulated by the Acute Dialysis Quality Initiative (ADQI) (2002), 1 AKI can be divided into five stages: renal injury risk, renal impairment, renal failure, renal function loss, and end‐stage kidney disease. However, this classification does not consider factors that may affect serum creatinine (SCr) levels, such as age, gender, and race. The AKI Network criteria (Acute Kidney Injury Network, commonly known as AKIN (2005), 2 emphasizes a time window of 48 h for diagnosing AKI and proposes that an absolute increase in SCr levels of >26.4 μmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) can be used for diagnosis. This AKI Network criteria improves the sensitivity of diagnosis and is more beneficial for the early diagnosis and treatment of AKI. The new international definition of AKI proposed by the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes in 2012 (commonly known as KDIGO), 3 that is, an increase in SCr levels of 0.3 mg/dL (consistent with AKIN) within 48 h, or an increase in SCr levels of 250% from baseline within 7 days, or a urine output decrease (urine volume <0.5 mL/(kg/h)) persisting for 6 h. This definition is also applicable to paediatric and contrast‐induced nephropathy patients. Additionally, the guidelines provide clear staging of AKI based on the degree of creatinine elevation, extent of urine output reduction, and duration, making the KDIGO guidelines widely used in recent studies.

Although all definitions of AKI appear to be similar in nature, differences in AKI definitions are one of the most important factors contributing to the heterogeneity in the reported incidence of AKI in patients with HF. Although many studies have found a high incidence of AKI and a large impact on outcomes in HF patients, data pooled from these studies are inadequate. Therefore, we collected data from the available observational studies and performed this meta‐analysis to determine the incidence of AKI in HF patients and its impact on patient survival. In order to figure out which patients with HF are at risk of AKI, in the determination, we included inpatient variables that may lead to AKI. In this way, this can provide a basis for reference in early intervention.

Methods

This study was registered at inplasy.com (2022‐12‐24), and the registration number is INPLASY2022120095.

Criteria for inclusion

We selected cohort studies (prospective or retrospective) as the study type for this meta‐analysis. Patients in the study were adults (≥18 years old) who had been diagnosed with HF and showed enough signs of AKI to meet the diagnostic criteria. The number of patients with HF and AKI, and predictive variables or mortality were reported after adjusting for confounders. The mortality outcome measures included all‐cause mortality during hospitalization and the 1‐year follow‐up. We excluded comments, case reports, animal experiments, conferences, letters, or articles with only abstracts. We excluded studies if they did not have data on AKI incidence and mortality, and predictors, or if data were insufficient for analysis. We did not set limits on the language or geographical distribution of the published studies, and we included full manuscripts.

Data retrieval strategy

All methods used in this meta‐analysis met the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) criteria 4 for cohort studies. Experienced library staff helped us with a comprehensive retrieval of studies from the Embase, Medline, PubMed, Cochrane libraries, and Web of Science databases. We identified studies from the time of inception of the database to October 2022, with the subject headings ‘Heart Failure’ and ‘Acute Kidney Injury’. We have provided detailed retrieval strategies for each database in the supporting information.

Two investigators (S. R., Author 1 and S. L., Author 2.) independently screened the literature, extracted the data, and cross‐checked them. Disagreements were resolved by a discussion or a consultation with a third party (Z. L., Author 3.). In the first round of screening, we reviewed titles and abstracts to eliminate articles that did not seem to be related to AKI and HF. We then read the full text to determine if the article should or should not be included. Wherever needed, we contacted the authors of the original study by mail or telephone to obtain additional information that was not documented or was important for the study.

Quality evaluation

Two investigators (S. R. and S. L.) independently used the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale 5 to assess the quality of each study selected for the analysis. This scale has eight items covering three categories (cohort selection, inter‐group comparability, and outcome measurement). The full scores of the three categories are 4, 2, and 3 points, respectively. For each item, one point is scored if the study meets the criteria (except for comparability of the study groups); two points are scored if the study controlled for age, gender, or other confounders. We defined literature as high‐quality if it had a score of 7 or more than 7 points, and as low quality if it had a score of less than 6 points. Any discrepancies in findings were resolved by author consensus through joint assessment of the concerned articles.

Data extraction

Two investigators (S. R. and S. L.) used a self‐developed spreadsheet in Excel format to collate data. Data collected from each study included authors, year of publication, location, type of study design, type of HF, the definition of AKI, follow‐up period, and various effect measures. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved by referring to the original text and by consensus among the authors.

Statistical analysis

We included the number of people with HF and AKI from each study. We performed meta‐analysis using Stata 16.0 software to calculate the incidence of AKI and the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). From the literature, we extracted data on the number of deaths at different time points to obtain the risk ratio (RR) and 95% CI of mortality between AKI patients and non‐AKI patients, or the hazard ratio (HR) and the corresponding 95% CI at different time points. We selected predictors of AKI after adjusting for confounders, and their ORs and 95% CIs were combined. In each study, data were weighted according to sample size. The heterogeneity of the studies was assessed according to I 2 and Cochran's Q test. 6 If I 2 < 50% and Q test's P value was >0.05, the fixed effects model (FEM) was used for analysis; if I 2 ≥ 50% and Q test's P value was <0.05, the random effects model (REM) was used. We used sensitivity analyses on the source of heterogeneity or focused subgroup analysis in studies with high heterogeneity. Funnel plot and Egger's test were used to analyse the publication bias of studies with ≥10 research studies, and P < 0.1 was considered statistically significant; the Z‐test was used to determine the correlation between the incidence of AKI, mortality, and related predictors of each AKI, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Incidence of acute kidney injury

Of the 3 533 583 HF patients included in the 37 studies, 774 887 developed AKI, with the pooled data being significantly heterogeneous (I 2 = 99.7%, P = 0.000), with a pooled incidence of 33% (95% CI: 32–35%, P < 0.001). AHF and chronic heart failure (CHF) were analysed in subgroups and combined by random‐effects model, and the incidence of AKI was 36% (95% CI: 31–40%) in AHF and 30% (95% CI: 24–35%) in CHF (Figure 2 ). Subgroup analysis was conducted on different AKI diagnostic criteria in 30 studies on AHF, and the results still exhibited substantial heterogeneity. When using a random‐effects model, the estimated incidence rates of AKI were as follows: 34% (95% CI: 31–36%) for KDIGO diagnostic criteria, 43% (95% CI: 29–56%) for RIFLE diagnostic criteria, and 27% (95% CI: 21–33%) for AKIN diagnostic criteria (Figure 3 ). Association between acute kidney injury and all‐cause mortality in heart failure patients

Eleven articles 8 , 9 , 11 , 13 , 17 , 22 , 26 , 29 , 31 , 37 , 41 reported all‐cause mortality in AHF patients with and without AKI while they were hospitalized. The difference was statistically significant with no heterogeneity in the results of each study (I 2 = 0.0%, P = 0.897), and an FEM was used for combined analysis. Meta‐analysis showed that AHF patients who developed AKI had a higher risk of death than non‐AKI patients (RR, 3.65; 95% CI: 3.04–4.39, P < 0.001) (Figure 4 ). Publication bias

The funnel plot results showed that the 11 pieces of literature on all‐cause mortality during hospitalization were basically symmetrical in the middle line. Egger's test (t = 2.33, P = 0.045) revealed that there was publication bias (Figure 6 ).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding one study from the 11 literature sources regarding the risk of in‐hospital mortality associated with AKI in patients with AHF. The results showed that there was no significant difference between each pooled effect size after excluding a single study and the total pooled RR value, indicating that the findings were reliable (Figure 7 ).

Risk factors associated with the development of acute kidney injury

Eighty predictive variables were included in 13 studies on multivariate risk factors linked with AKI. However, some predictive factors had only one study available or were not adjusted for confounding factors; thus, a meta‐analysis was not conducted. Hence, using Stata 16.0 software, we integrated a total of 15 risk factors. The risk factors were categorized as follows: (1) as per general factors: age, 14 , 17 , 29 , 43 gender (male) 14 , 29 , 30 ; (2) based on disease factors: diabetes (yes vs. no), 8 , 16 , 29 , 30 hypertension (yes vs. no), 16 , 29 history of chronic kidney disease (yes vs. no), 14 , 30 , 36 CHF systolic (systolic vs. diastolic) 30 , 33 ; (3) based on objective indicator factors: LVEF, 12 , 36 N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide, 14 , 16 , 29 creatinine, 12 , 24 , 29 , 33 creatinine >1.0 mg/day (yes vs. no), 19 , 30 , 35 index estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (yes vs. no), 8 , 32 index eGFR, 16 , 32 blood urea nitrogen 19 , 35 ; (4) based on therapeutic factors: intravenous dobutamine (yes vs. no) 19 , 35 ; (5) protective factors: serum albumin. 14 , 24 , 29 , 32 The meta‐analysis revealed that 10 of the influencing factors were predictor factors (P < 0.05). Sensitivity analysis

We combined the risk factors and size effect using Stata 16.0 software and compared the OR and 95% CI indexes. Most of the results showed I 2 < 50% and P Het > 0.05, suggesting no significant heterogeneity. For the articles with more than three studies, sensitivity analysis was performed by removing one study in turn. The results showed that there was no significant difference between each pooled effect size after excluding a single study and the total pooled OR, indicating that the meta‐analysis results were stable and credible. There was significant heterogeneity in the results (I 2 = 82.3%, P Het = 0.001) after combining the four risk factor analyses on SCr (OR, 1.03; 95% CI: 0.98–1.08; P = 0.238). Sensitivity analysis revealed that the results of studies with ‘Ge, H. X’ were highly heterogeneous. After excluding the study that contributed to the heterogeneity, we found moderate heterogeneity (I 2 = 64.3%, P Het = 0.061) when these studies were excluded, and a REM was used to combine the data (OR, 1.15; 95% CI: 0.89–1.49; P = 0.282). The results were not statistically significant. Careful analysis of this study did not determine the cause of heterogeneity, but the consistent conclusions of the sensitivity analysis indicated the stability of the results, suggesting that creatinine did not influence the occurrence of AKI in patients with HF.

Among the clinical factors, there was moderate heterogeneity after combining factors for variable left ventricular ejection fraction (I 2 = 58.8%, p Het = 0.119). Sensitivity analysis could not be carried out due to insufficient raw data, so the REM was used for data combination (OR, 1.00; 95% CI: 0.94–1.06; P = 0.887). The results were not statistically significant.

The results showed that age (OR, 1.02; 95% CI: 1.01–1.03, P < 0.001), diabetes (OR, 1.70; 95% CI: 1.37–2.11, P < 0.001), hypertension (OR, 1.81; 95% CI: 1.19–2.74, P = 0.005), chronic kidney disease (OR, 3.63; 95% CI: 2.49–5.31, P < 0.001), CHF systolic (OR, 2.53; 95% CI: 1.43–4.49, P < 0.001), N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (OR, 1.24; 95% CI: 1.13–1.37, P < 0.001), creatinine >1.0 mg/dL (OR, 1.76; 95% CI: 1.14–2.71, P = 0.011), glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (OR, 1.64; 95% CI: 1.23–2.18, P < 0.001), blood urea nitrogen >24 mg/dL (OR, 2.67; 95% CI: 1.64–4.34, P < 0.001), and serum albumin (OR, 0.96; 95% CI: 0.94–0.98, P < 0.001) were predictive factors for the occurrence of AKI in patients with heart failure, and the differences were statistically significant.

Discussion

The incidence of AKI in patients with HF has been shown to be as high as 27–45%, 44 , 45 , 46 which is substantially higher than the incidence of AKI in hospitalized general patients (3.2–9.6%), 47 showing that HF may increase the incidence of AKI in hospitalized patients. Our systematic review and meta‐analysis involved 37 studies, and we found a pooled incidence of AKI of 33% in patients with HF. The high incidence of AKI in patients with HF may be due to multiple reasons. First, patients with HF are more likely to have reduced renal perfusion due to systolic or diastolic dysfunction. Second, a considerable number of patients may have had underlying CHF, a condition associated with chronic renal impairment. Third, potential nephrotoxic medications, such as angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, are used to treat HF patients.

The results of this study showed significant heterogeneity in the incidence rates, which may be attributed to the inherent heterogeneity of the population of patients with heart failure. This population includes individuals with AHF, CHF, STEMI‐related cardiogenic shock, and other conditions. The sample size of the included literature in this study varies greatly. The majority of them are retrospective studies, and the quality of causal demonstration is relatively weak in such studies. Different diagnostic criteria such as RIFLE, AKIN, and KGIGO were used for AKI patients in various studies included in our analysis, which might have resulted in selection bias. Wide variations in study subjects and their significant exposure factors, and confounders like unadjusted medication and renal function could have potentially impacted the accuracy of AKI incidence.

More and more studies have established that renal impairment is common in patients with HF and that renal impairment is linked to adverse outcomes. In contrast, renal disease is independently related to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and death. 48 Cardiorenal syndrome 49 refers to the clinical manifestation of interactions between the heart and the kidney. In cardiorenal syndrome, patients with HF develop renal dysfunction via a number of interaction pathways between the heart and kidney. Studies have shown that even mild, reversible AKI continues to be a risk for tissue and organ damage, and severe AKI leads to irreversible decreased renal function and end‐stage renal failure. 50 The proposed biological mechanism also supports a potential causal pathway. The investigators hypothesized that renal impairment might upregulate the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, enhance basal sympathetic discharge, increase pro‐inflammatory factors, exacerbate underlying anaemia, and worsen left ventricular hypertrophy and myocyte contractility, leading to impaired capacity load, pump failure, and death. In this study, we found that in patients with HF, AKI not only predicted a worse in‐hospital prognosis, but it also more than tripled the risk of in‐hospital mortality; the risk of death in these surviving inpatients remained high during the 1‐year follow‐up. However, because the included studies had very limited data on mortality in different AKI stage subgroups, we were unable to perform a combined mortality analysis based on the severity of AKI.

In this study, we chose risk factors using multivariate logistic regression analysis only, which lessened the influence of confounders to a certain extent. A systematic evaluation was performed in strict accordance with evidence‐based methods. The pooled data showed low heterogeneity among studies. Studies show that AKI is more likely to occur in elderly people with HF, who also have a history of chronic kidney disease, hypertension, diabetes, systolic CHF, and other diseases. High expressions of blood urea nitrogen and SCr, and low expression of eGFR, indicate a weaker baseline renal function at the time of admission and a higher risk of AKI after HF. Brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) markers are also positively associated with the development of AKI in HF patients. A meta‐analysis study 51 showed that BNP increased the risk of renal deterioration. Another large‐scale study 52 also showed that BNP tended to worsen renal function, and the mechanism causing renal deterioration was hypotension. 53 This study also found that the decreased expression of serum albumin in people with HF might be one of the main reasons why the risk of AKI was higher. Serum albumin has free sulfhydryl groups that can react directly with oxygen and nitrogen compounds in plasma. These groups help protect endothelial cells and prevent oxidation, and they may also help prevent kidney injury. Lastly, dobutamine injections administered in HF patients after admission also increase the risk of AKI. This may be because high‐dose dobutamine injections stimulate α‐adrenergic receptors, resulting in increased peripheral vascular resistance, renal vasoconstriction, and decreased renal blood flow and urine volume. Persistent hypoperfusion and hyperfiltration will result in renal tissue ischemia, hypoxia, renal cell necrosis, apoptosis, and, eventually, dysfunction.

As a result of the aforesaid findings, clinicians should closely monitor the expression of serum blood urea nitrogen, sSCr, eGFR, BNP, and albuminuria in elderly patients with underlying diseases during hospitalization, particularly in patients with HF. If the expression of this index level is abnormal, relevant interventions should be implemented to decrease the risk of concurrent acute renal injury and help them recover.

Although this study's findings are generally trustworthy, nonetheless, they include the following limitations: Firstly, a large number of conference proceedings and abstracts were excluded in this study, resulting in incomplete data inclusion, which may introduce publication bias. Secondly, due to limited data on subgroup analysis of mortality rates based on heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, heart failure with mid‐range ejection fraction, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, or disease severity based on New York Heart Association classification and different stages of AKI, it was not possible to analyse the mortality rates according to the severity of heart failure or AKI. Thirdly, for some predictive factors related to AKI in heart failure, only two studies were included in the meta‐analysis, which may affect the accuracy and reliability of the results. Additionally, the criteria for defining certain predictive factors varied among studies or were based on single‐study results, which prevented their combination for analysis, potentially leading to incomplete results. Therefore, in future research, better‐designed prospective cohort studies with large sample sizes are required to further demonstrate the relationship between HF and AKI.

In conclusion, we found in this meta‐analysis that AKI was common in patients with HF, and it increased the risk of dying in hospital. The risk of dying was high in the 1‐year follow‐up. Currently, as there is no effective treatment for AKI, early detection and prevention are crucial to its treatment. Early intervention for high‐risk patients and identification of their associated risk factors are particularly important for enhancing AKI prevention, treatment levels, and prognosis in patients with HF.
