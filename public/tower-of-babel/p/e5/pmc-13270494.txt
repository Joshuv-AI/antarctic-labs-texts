Late Recurrences Beyond Five Years in Salivary Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma: Implications for Long‐Term Surveillance

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSalivary adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) is a rare head and neck malignancy with limited surveillance guidelines. ACC disease patterns are quite variable and unpredictable. Herein, we present an institutional retrospective review of salivary adenoid cystic carcinoma and discuss patterns and predictors of recurrence.Study DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingSingle Quaternary Care Referral Center.MethodsWe analyzed 102 adult patients with salivary gland adenoid cystic carcinoma between 1988 and 2019. Patient and tumor‐related characteristics are summarized, and recurrence, survival, and metastatic site data are described.ResultsMultivariable analysis showed that greater than 30% solid pathology was independently predictive of recurrence (HR 5.32, CI 2.23‐12.66). Advanced stage disease (stages III and IV) was also predictive of recurrence (HR 3.77, CI 1.57‐9.03). Nearly one‐third of all patients experienced distant recurrence. Among patients experiencing distant recurrence, 29% were diagnosed with distant recurrence more than 5 years post‐treatment.ConclusionThese data support long‐term surveillance and clinical follow‐up for patients with ACC.Keywords: adenoid cystic carcinoma, salivary tumors

Salivary adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC) is a rare malignancy that accounts for approximately 10% of salivary gland tumors and 1% of all head and neck malignancies with an incidence of 4.5 cases per 100,000 individuals. The clinical behavior of salivary gland ACC can be variable and the rare nature of this malignancy obviates robust data with long‐term clinical outcomes and patterns.

Salivary gland ACC is commonly treated with surgical excision often followed by pathology‐driven adjuvant treatment. Recurrence rates for both local and distant disease can be quite high and can occur many years after treatment. 1 Despite this, there are no standardized posttreatment surveillance guidelines for salivary gland ACC. 2 Real‐world practices often group salivary adenoid cystic carcinoma with other salivary gland malignancies. Currently, the American Society for Clinical Oncology Guideline on Salivary Gland Malignancy Management recommends follow‐up surveillance imaging of the primary site with contrast CT or MRI along with chest CT every 6‐12 months for the first 2 years after initial treatment. During years 3 to 5, annual follow‐up imaging may be offered and beyond 5 years yearly exam and chest CT may be offered especially in cases of high‐grade histology or other poor prognostic clinicopathologic features. 2

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines for Head and Neck Cancers acknowledge that surveillance is challenging and there are no consensus guidelines on the frequency and modality of routine posttreatment imaging, especially after 5 years posttreatment. NCCN Guidelines are published after discussion with a multidisciplinary panel of experts from member institutions, however, no follow‐up guidelines specific to ACC are provided.

In this study, we retrospectively review the disease patterns and survival trends among a cohort of patients with ACC treated at a single quaternary referral institution with robust clinical follow‐up. The objective is to assess prognostic clinical characteristics and characterize long‐term outcomes to better inform surveillance recommendations.

Methods

Study Population

This was a retrospective cohort study of patients diagnosed with salivary gland ACC between 1988 and 2019 at the University of Michigan Rogel Cancer Center. Adults over 18 years of age with a confirmed histopathologic diagnosis of adenoid cystic carcinoma were included. This study was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (IRBMED HUM00178209). Patients were identified from University of Michigan DataDirect. Demographic data such as sex, age at diagnosis, race/ethnicity, and smoking history as well as disease‐related variables such as stage at diagnosis based on year of diagnosis, pathologic grade (tubular and cribriform pattern alone, less than 30% solid component, 30% or more solid component), and treatment modality were manually abstracted from the medical records. Our institutional practice is that typically all ACC patients are sent for adjuvant radiation, especially patients with T2 or higher disease, close or positive margins, or perineural involvement (PNI). Typical radiation fields include the postoperative bed treated to 60 to 66 Gy with the area of named nerves typically treated to 54 to 66 Gy all in 30 fractions. Elective nodal areas are not typically treated unless clinically involved. Recurrence, survival, and metastatic disease site data were also collected. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was recurrence‐free survival (RFS). RFS was defined as the time from date of initial biopsy to date of diagnosis of recurrent or metastatic disease at any site. The anatomic location of recurrence was determined by review of radiology and pathology reports. Patients who were diagnosed with recurrent disease were separated into groups defined by locoregional recurrence only, distant metastatic recurrence only, or both. Secondary outcomes included overall survival and sites of distant metastatic disease.

Statistical Analysis

Univariate and multivariate regression analysis was performed to identify factors predictive of disease recurrence. Variables in the univariate logistic analysis including age, stage, grade, perineural invasion, and treatment with primary surgery and adjuvant radiation were chosen a priori. Kaplan‐Meier and log‐rank tests were performed to measure the effect of individual covariates on RFS. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of RFS. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (IBM).

RFS

Thirty‐nine patients (38%) were diagnosed with recurrent disease. Among these 13 patients (13%) had locoregional recurrences, 31 patients (30%) had distant recurrences, and 5 patients (5%) had both locoregional and distant recurrences. To avoid double‐counting, patients with both recurrence types were included in both individual categories (n = 13 and n = 31) but counted only once for the overall total (n = 39). Figure 1A shows the cumulative incidence of locoregional recurrence and Figure 1B shows the cumulative incidence of distant recurrence. In the overall cohort, 51% of patients who experienced any disease recurrence recurred within 36 months of initial diagnosis. Among patients experiencing locoregional recurrence, 62% of patients recurred within 36 months of initial diagnosis (Figure 1A). The longest interval for locoregional recurrence was 13 years after initial diagnosis. Among patients experiencing a distant metastatic recurrence, 45% of patients recurred within 36 months of initial diagnosis, and 29% were diagnosed with recurrence more than 5 years posttreatment (Figure 1B). Among patients with delayed distant recurrence greater than 5 years posttreatment, management approaches included surgical resection such as wedge resection for isolated pulmonary metastases, systemic therapies such as lenvatinib or carboplatin/paclitaxel, clinical trial participation, and hospice care. The longest interval for distant metastatic recurrence was 18 years after initial diagnosis. The median RFS was 127 months (SE 22 months) (Figure 2). Univariate predictors of any recurrence included late‐stage disease (stages III, IV) compared to early‐stage disease (stages I, II), 30% or more solid component compared to no solid component on histopathology (Table 2), and treatment with combined primary surgery and adjuvant radiation compared to other. Patients with late‐stage disease at time of diagnosis had a two‐fold greater risk of developing recurrence compared to patients with early‐stage disease (HR 2.19, 95% CI [1.02‐4.68], P = .044). Additionally, patients with more than 30% component in their disease had a 5‐fold greater rate of developing recurrence compared to patients without any solid component in their disease (HR 5.06, 95% CI [2.30‐11.13], P < .001). The presence of perineural invasion (PNI) was not predictive of recurrence risk (P = .524). Of 92 patients with known PNI status, 75 patients were positive for PNI and 17 were negative. Of 17 patients without PNI, 7 received radiation while 10 did not. Of 75 patients with PNI, 65 received radiation while 10 did not. Furthermore, treatment with primary surgery followed by adjuvant radiation (n = 69) decreased the rate of developing recurrence significantly compared to individuals undergoing any other treatment strategies (n = 27, Table 2). Although our institutional practice is for most ACC patients undergoing primary surgery to receive adjuvant radiation, there are instances in which this exact paradigm was not completed due to patient preference or other clinical considerations. On multivariable analysis, independent predictors of disease recurrence included late‐stage disease (HR, 3.91; 95% CI, 1.63‐9.37), disease with 30% or higher solid component (HR, 6.23; 95% CI 2.56‐15.19), and treatment with primary surgery followed by adjuvant radiation (HR, 0.40; 95% CI 0.18‐0.90) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this large study of salivary gland ACC, we highlight factors predictive of recurrence patterns to guide development of individualized surveillance schedules. Our findings demonstrate that the risk of recurrence in salivary gland ACC persists for many years after treatment. Staging at the time of diagnosis and histopathologic grade are the strongest predictors of recurrence. Treatment with both primary surgery and adjuvant radiation reduces recurrence risk compared to other treatment modalities or sequences. Perineural invasion did not predict recurrence risk. Lung, bone, and brain are the most common sites of distant disease recurrence.

Many characteristics of our cohort are consistent with the published literature. For example, it is well‐known that ACC exhibits a female predominance. 3 Furthermore, rates of perineural invasion can be upwards of 60% (occurring in 82% of our cohort). 4 , 5 Previously published prognostic factors for recurrence include perineural invasion, advanced tumor stage, and higher histological grade with increased solid component. 6 , 7 While the presence of perineural invasion was not significantly related to recurrence in our cohort, we confirm that late‐stage disease and higher histopathologic grade, especially tumors with greater than 30% solid component, solid tumors, did portend increased recurrence risk in both univariate and multivariate models. As the majority of patients with positive PNI received radiation, it is likely that the routine use of radiation to electively treat the base of skull masked the predictive value of PNI in our cohort.

Our data suggest that recurrence can occur many years after a disease‐free interval, up to 18 years in this cohort. However, nearly 30% of distant recurrences occurred after 5 years. Van Weert et al showed that 91% of distant metastases developed in the first 5 years after presentation; however, Rapidis et al showed that nearly 55% of distant metastases developed between 5 and 10 years after initial treatment. While the natural history of salivary gland ACC is varied, the trend towards later onset of distant disease suggests that longer‐term follow‐up with cross‐sectional radiographic monitoring is necessary.

Optimal surveillance strategies can be influenced by factors including probability of disease recurrence, efficiency of surveillance strategy in detecting recurrence, impact of delayed detection, and cost‐effectiveness. As an example, sensitivity analysis in soft tissue sarcoma showed that for lower‐risk patients with distant recurrence risk less than 33%, chest X‐ray may be preferred while for higher‐risk patients with distant recurrence risk greater than 33%, chest CT may be the optimal strategy. 8 In primary cutaneous melanoma, expert opinion surveys suggested that around 30% 5‐year risk of recurrence was the threshold identified for recommending cross‐sectional imaging. 9 While there is no defined universal setpoint, our data showing nearly 30% of patients developing distant metastases after first 5 years after presentation suggests value in long‐term surveillance with cross‐sectional imaging. Though there is no specific data in this regard, we suggest monitoring with CT chest, abdomen, pelvis every 6 months for 10 years after initial diagnosis with follow up imaging afterwards as clinically indicated based on symptoms.

In our subset of patients with distant metastatic disease, 10% had brain metastases. This rate of brain metastases is consistent with that reported by Rapidis and colleagues. 10 Though lung distant metastatic disease is more common both in previously published studies as well as in our cohort, 1 our data show that brain involvement can occur. Surveillance and management strategies should be tailored to patient symptoms and risk profiles.

When comparing patients receiving upfront surgery and adjuvant radiation with those treated otherwise (ie, no adjuvant radiation often due to patient preference or chemotherapy and radiation), there was a benefit in RFS. This is consistent with the published literature where there is clinical benefit to adjuvant radiation and is also consistent with the ASCO guidelines where adjuvant radiation after primary surgery is recommended.13

The retrospective nature of this study represents a key limitation. This study also spans patients over thirty years making it challenging to control for changes in staging systems and advancements in surgical and radiation approaches. Furthermore, as a quaternary care referral center, this study of patients at the University of Michigan also includes many who received portions of their care at other sites thus making it challenging to abstract complete data at times. However, given the overall paucity of large‐scale studies on salivary gland ACC, the addition of our institutional experience can help refine individualized prediction models and personalize surveillance schedules and treatment strategies for patients with this rare disease. Nonetheless, prospective, future studies are needed to explore whether clinical exam versus imaging is sufficient and to understand the benefit of surveillance to overall survival to avoid possibility of lead time bias.

Future study of salivary gland ACC molecular subsets is also an evolving frontier and may better inform surveillance strategies. Examining the correlation between characteristics such as NOTCH mutational status or p63 status with recurrence patterns and clinical behavior of disease may further define the highest‐risk patient subset and aid clinicians in refining treatment and surveillance recommendations.
