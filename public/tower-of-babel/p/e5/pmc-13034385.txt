Effects of transcranial magnetic stimulation on cortical network modulation and functional recovery in patients with chronic ankle injury following surgery: study protocol for randomised controlled trial
Authors: Rao Xu, Jingjun Hu, Xuhao Cao, Yuchao He, Qinglei Wang, Jun Zhu, Cui Yu, Yuanyue Chen, Chuan Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSingle-modality motor rehabilitation is often insufficient to address the complex functional restoration needs of patients with chronic ankle injuries following surgery. Research indicates that non-invasive neuromodulation techniques, such as intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS), may enhance motor recovery by increasing cortical excitability and facilitating neural network reorganisation. Compared with exercise therapy alone, this study aims to explore the rehabilitation efficacy of combining iTBS with exercise therapy in patients with chronic ankle injuries following surgery. It also investigates the associated cortical network modulation, providing scientific evidence to optimise rehabilitation strategies for these patients.Methods and analysisThis study is a prospective, multicentre, randomised controlled trial with single-blind assessment. A total of 28 patients with chronic ankle injuries following surgery were enrolled and randomly assigned to either the experimental group or the control group according to centre-stratified randomisation. The experimental group will receive iTBS combined with exercise therapy, while the control group will receive sham iTBS combined with exercise therapy. Both groups will undergo one session per day, 5 days per week, for a total of 3 weeks. The primary outcome measure will be the American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society Ankle-Hindfoot Score (AOFAS). Secondary outcome measures will include isokinetic muscle strength assessment of the ankle dorsiflexor and plantarflexor muscles, Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores, ankle joint range of motion (ROM) and the 10-Metre Walk Test. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) will also be used to assess cortical functional activity and neurophysiological changes. All outcome measures will be collected at baseline prior to enrolment, post-intervention at week 3, and at follow-up at week 6. Primary outcomes analyses will use group-by-time comparisons to examine between-group differences in functional and neurophysiological measures.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Province (Suqian) Hospital (Application No. 2025-SR-0333). All participants will provide written informed consent before enrolment. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals and scientific conferences.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500110905.Protocol versionV1.0.

Introduction

Ankle injuries are a common and widespread traumatic issue in clinical practice and have become a significant global public health concern.1 In the USA alone, about 25 000 people seek medical care each day for ankle-related issues, placing a substantial burden on healthcare systems and socioeconomic resources.2 Although surgical procedures like open reduction and internal fixation can restore precise anatomical alignment, they do not guarantee full functional recovery. Approximately 20%–30% of patients continue to experience different levels of pain, stiffness or functional impairment within 6–12 months postoperatively.3 4 Prolonged immobilisation and delayed weight-bearing following surgery frequently result in secondary changes such as muscle disuse atrophy, joint adhesions and stiffness. These complications collectively compromise lower limb functional recovery, impact quality of life and delay return-to-work.5 Furthermore, poor postoperative functional recovery is associated with patient factors (advanced age, obesity and diabetes), lifestyle habits (smoking), postoperative complications and fracture complexity.3 6 Among these, delayed rehabilitation intervention is considered one of the important modifiable factors influencing postoperative recovery.5 Delayed initiation of rehabilitation may allow initially reversible functional impairments to progress into chronic deficits. This progression is often accompanied by secondary peripheral changes, including fibrotic scar formation, altered neural sensitivity and persistent joint stiffness, which increase the complexity and difficulty of functional recovery.7

Rehabilitation after ankle injuries following surgery typically includes joint mobilisation, balance training and muscle strengthening to improve periarticular muscle strength and proprioceptive function.8 However, for individuals who have entered the chronic stage, peripheral training alone offers limited benefit for restoring gait stability and complex motor control.9 Evidence indicates that chronic musculoskeletal disorders are often accompanied by alterations in central nervous system plasticity, involving key regions such as the sensorimotor cortex, mesolimbic system, prefrontal areas and brainstem.7 Another study reported that patients with chronic ankle instability show significant structural and functional abnormalities in several sensorimotor-related cortical regions, including the precentral gyrus, supplementary motor area, postcentral gyrus, middle frontal gyrus and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex.10 Collectively, these findings suggest a bidirectional interaction between peripheral dysfunction and the central sensorimotor network. Persistent abnormal sensory input may contribute to cortical dysfunction and altered motor control patterns, while cortical imbalance may, in turn, exacerbate peripheral impairment.11 12 Despite these observations, systematic investigations of combined central–peripheral intervention approaches in patients who have entered the chronic phase after ankle injury surgery remain scarce.13 14

Non-invasive brain stimulation has been shown to regulate cortical excitability and neural plasticity.7 Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) induces electric currents in cortical neurons through electromagnetic induction. By altering membrane potentials and neurotransmitter release, TMS can modulate and assess brain network function.15 Intermittent theta-burst stimulation (iTBS) is a high-frequency TMS protocol that can induce long-term potentiation effects within a short administration time, which enhances cortical excitability and supports reorganisation of cortical networks.16 Findings from stroke rehabilitation studies indicate that iTBS combined with peripheral therapies can improve motor function and may also help restore functional balance between the cerebral hemispheres.17 18

This study will employ multimodal neurophysiological assessments, including functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and TMS. fNIRS measures changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin to characterise cortical activation and functional connectivity patterns.19 It is non-invasive, has high temporal resolution and allows real-time monitoring. These properties make it suitable for evaluating cortical function during task performance in patients following foot and ankle surgery.20 TMS reflects cortical functional states at different levels. The resting motor threshold (RMT) reflects global cortical excitability. Motor evoked potential (MEP) quantifies corticospinal conduction efficiency. The cortical silent period (CSP) represents inhibitory cortical circuit activity. Interhemispheric inhibition (IHI) reflects reciprocal inhibition and functional balance between the bilateral primary motor cortices.21 These indicators collectively form a comprehensive framework for evaluating cortical excitability and functional equilibrium, offering objective indices for quantifying the effects of neuromodulation on cortical functional reorganisation.

This study adopts a multi-level assessment framework based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF). At the body structures and functions level, fNIRS and TMS will be used to evaluate cortical haemodynamics and neurophysiological excitability. Pain intensity, muscle strength and ankle joint range of motion will also be assessed to characterise both central neural activity and peripheral motor system function. At the activities and participation level, walking performance tests and ankle functional scales will be used to assess lower limb motor execution and daily functional abilities, thereby indicating how central modulation is reflected in practical functional activities. This study aims to investigate the modulatory effects of iTBS on cortical networks in patients with chronic ankle injuries following surgery and to examine its association with motor function recovery. We hypothesise that iTBS combined with exercise therapy will lead to greater improvements in motor function compared with peripheral rehabilitation alone. The intervention is expected to be accompanied by increased excitability of the ipsilesional motor cortex, improved interhemispheric inhibitory balance, enhanced corticospinal conduction and optimised functional connectivity within motor-related cortical networks. Improvements in motor function are also expected to correlate with these neurophysiological changes, thereby further supporting the central modulatory role of iTBS in chronic-phase rehabilitation.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

This study is a prospective, double-centre, randomised controlled trial. A total of 28 patients with chronic ankle injury following surgery will be enrolled and randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (iTBS combined with exercise therapy) or the control group (sham stimulation combined with exercise therapy). Randomisation will be stratified by study centre to ensure balanced allocation across sites. The intervention will be administered once daily, 5 days per week, for 3 weeks. Primary outcomes will be assessed at baseline prior to enrolment (T0), post-intervention at week 3 (T1) and at follow-up at week 6 (T2). Study setting

This study will be conducted at Jiangsu Province (Suqian) Hospital and Yancheng Dexin Hospital. All assessments and interventions will be performed in the rehabilitation departments of both hospitals by physical therapists and research team members who have received standardised training. Uniform protocols will be followed to ensure consistency of interventions and comparability of data across centres. Participant recruitment will follow routine clinical pathways at both hospitals. The research team will screen patients according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and eligible individuals will be scheduled for assessments and interventions.

Randomisation and blinding

This study will use a centre-stratified block randomisation method. An independent statistician will generate random sequences for each centre using SPSS 26.0. These sequences will be encrypted and securely stored. At each centre, study coordinators will allocate participants after they have completed baseline assessments and met the eligibility criteria. Allocation will follow a predetermined sequence concealed in opaque, sealed envelopes to ensure both concealment and traceability. Participants, outcome assessors and data analysts will be blinded to group allocation. To evaluate the effectiveness of participant blinding, a post-intervention blinding assessment will be conducted at the end of the intervention period. Participants will be asked to indicate whether they believe they received active iTBS, sham stimulation or are uncertain. The results of this assessment will be used to describe the success of blinding and will not be treated as an outcome measure. In the event of a serious adverse event during the study, the principal investigator will determine whether emergency unblinding is necessary and will document the reason and management. All participants will be unblinded at the end of the trial to verify group allocation and ensure data integrity. The entire randomisation and allocation process will be supervised by independent personnel to maintain scientific rigour and protocol compliance.

Eligibility criteria

Patients meeting all of the following criteria will be evaluated for inclusion in this trial: ⑴ unilateral ankle injury treated surgically; ⑵ age between 18 and 65 years; ⑶ postoperative duration of ≥3 months; ⑷ no history of other trauma; ⑸ ability to provide written informed consent and willingness to comply with all assessments and interventions.

Exclusion criteria

Patients meeting any of the following conditions will be excluded from this trial: ⑴ presence of metallic implants in the head or neck, cardiac pacemakers, or other contraindications to TMS, including a history of epilepsy, intracranial pathology or severe psychiatric disorders; ⑵ severe dysfunction of major organs (cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic or renal) that limits ability to tolerate rehabilitation training; ⑶ pregnancy or breastfeeding; ⑷ cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders that prevent effective communication; ⑸ inability to tolerate rehabilitation training due to severe pain; ⑹ other musculoskeletal injuries or neurological disorders that may affect lower-limb function; ⑺ concomitant peripheral nerve injury; ⑻ serious postoperative complications that may affect functional recovery, such as infection, hardware loosening or non-union.

Interventions

This study established an experimental group (iTBS combined with exercise therapy) and a control group (sham stimulation combined with exercise therapy), and both groups will be given training for 3 weeks. All interventions will be administered by physical therapists and research team members who have undergone standardised training and will follow standardised operating procedures.

iTBS combined with exercise therapy (experimental group)

iTBS will be applied to the contralateral M1 corresponding to the affected limb at 80% of active motor threshold (AMT). The protocol will deliver 600 pulses per session, consisting of bursts at 50 Hz repeated at 5 Hz, administered in 2 s trains with 8 s intervals for 20 cycles (total stimulation time: 192 s).23 The intervention will be provided once daily, 5 days per week, over 3 weeks. Adverse events will be monitored during each session and immediately after treatment.

Sham stimulation combined with exercise therapy (control group)

In the control group, participants will receive 192 s of sham stimulation before each exercise therapy session. A custom sham coil will be used, with stimulation parameters, session duration and target location identical to those of real iTBS, producing only acoustic output without generating a magnetic field. This approach aims to improve the scientific validity and interpretability of comparisons between interventions.

Exercise therapy

Exercise therapy will be initiated within 5 min after completion of each iTBS or sham stimulation session.24 25 The exercise programme will follow a standardised, protocol-based rehabilitation framework, including joint mobilisation, strengthening exercises, stability training, proprioceptive training and balance training.26 27 Exercise intensity and progression will be individually adjusted according to each participant’s functional capacity and tolerance, while the intervention content and progression principles will be applied consistently across groups. All interventions will be delivered by trained and experienced physical therapists. Each session will last 40 min, conducted once daily, 5 days per week, for a total duration of 3 weeks.

Outcome measures

Table 1 shows the assessment measures and time points. Motor function assessments, fNIRS and TMS will be conducted at baseline (T0), post-intervention at week 3 (T1) and follow-up at week 6 (T2), with demographic data collected at baseline. To control for individual variability and minimise the impact of external factors on the outcomes, participants will be instructed to get adequate sleep the night before each assessment. They will also be asked to avoid alcohol, caffeine and other substances that may influence neural excitability for 24 hours before stimulation. All assessments will be performed by the same trained physical therapist following standardised procedures to guarantee reliability and reproducibility.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this trial is ankle-foot motor function, assessed using the American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS). AOFAS is a widely validated, standardised assessment tool for evaluating ankle function.27 It includes both patient-reported and clinician-assessed components and covers nine items related to pain, function and alignment. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating less pain and better functional status. Based on previous studies, an 8-point change has been identified as the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), signifying a meaningful improvement in the ankle function.28 29 This provides a comprehensive measure of ankle recovery.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes will comprise clinical functional assessments and neurophysiological measures, aiming to characterise changes in musculoskeletal performance, pain, mobility and cortical function associated with the intervention.

Clinical secondary outcomes will include isokinetic muscle strength, pain intensity, ankle joint range of motion (ROM) and walking performance. Muscle strength will be assessed using an isokinetic dynamometer (Prima isokinetic dynamometer series, Easytech, Italy). Maximal isometric torque of the ankle dorsiflexor and plantarflexor muscles will be recorded in an isometric position.30 Participants will sit with their trunk and thigh secured by straps to reduce compensatory movements. The foot will be fixed on a pedal to keep the ankle in a neutral position (0°), ensuring proper alignment between the ankle joint axis and the dynamometer’s rotation axis. The lever arm will be fixed at 30°. After entering basic information into the system and completing limb gravity and device weight calibration, participants will perform two low-intensity warm-up contractions to familiarise themselves with the movement and rhythm. During the formal test, participants will perform a 5 s maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVC) in response to a verbal cue, followed by a 30 s rest interval. Each direction will be repeated three times, and the highest peak torque of the three trials will be recorded as the result for that limb. Testing will be conducted first on the unaffected side, then on the affected side.

Pain intensity will be quantified using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS).31 Ankle range of motion will be measured with a goniometer to assess active, pain-free dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion and eversion. The highest value of three trials will be used for analysis.32 Walking ability will be evaluated with the 10-Metre Walk Test (10MWT), recording the average walking speed (m/s) at each participant’s maximum safe pace.33

Neurophysiological secondary outcomes will include cortical haemodynamic responses assessed by fNIRS and corticospinal and interhemispheric excitability assessed using TMS.

fNIRS assessment will be conducted using a portable continuous-wave system (Nirsmart-3000B series, Danyang Huichuang Medical Co., Ltd., China). The device records optical signals at 730 nm and 850 nm with a sampling rate of 11 Hz and detects haemodynamic changes 2.5–3 cm beneath the scalp, enabling real-time monitoring of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO2) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) concentrations. Within the region of interest, 18 light sources and 16 detectors are arranged symmetrically, forming 42 channels, of which 39 are valid. The optode spacing is 30 mm, providing coverage of the frontal and bilateral motor cortical areas. Based on the standard Brodmann cortical map, the channels will be assigned to ten regions of interest (ROIs): left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (LDLPFC), right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (RDLPFC), left premotor cortex (LPMC), right premotor cortex (RPMC), left supplementary motor area (LSMA), right supplementary motor area (RSMA), left primary motor cortex (LM1), right primary motor cortex (RM1), left primary somatosensory cortex (LS1) and right primary somatosensory cortex (RS1).34 35 The channel configuration and cortical mapping are presented in figure 2.

Schematic illustration of fNIRS channel configuration and cortical region assignment. fNIRS, functional near-infrared spectroscopy. CH, channel; DLPFC, dorsolateral prefrontal cortex; M1, primary motor cortex; PMC, premotor cortex; SMA, supplementary motor area; S1, primary somatosensory cortex.

fNIRS data will be collected continuously during resting and task states. Participant positioning and ankle fixation will be consistent with the isokinetic strength assessment described above. All recordings will be conducted in a quiet environment with stable lighting to minimise external interference and head motion artefacts. During resting-state acquisition, participants will maintain the same position with eyes closed, remain relaxed and avoid voluntary contractions or body movements. A continuous 10-min resting-state recording will be acquired to analyse baseline functional connectivity and cortical activation. Task-state acquisition will follow a block design (figure 3), starting with a 30 s baseline, followed by alternating periods of 15 s isometric ankle dorsiflexion and 30 s rest. This sequence will be repeated six times, lasting a total of 5 min.36 The isometric ankle dorsiflexion task will be synchronised with the isokinetic device’s task programme.37 Contraction intensity will be maintained at 30% of each participant’s MVC and monitored using real-time device feedback to ensure accuracy.38 39 If a contraction does not reach the target intensity or exhibits obvious compensatory movements, that block will be considered invalid. Each participant must have at least four valid blocks to be included in the analysis.40 Head motion will be quantified using signal-based motion detection during preprocessing, and contaminated segments exceeding predefined thresholds will be excluded.

During TMS assessment (MagNeuro R230, Nanjing Vishee Medical Co., Ltd., China), participants will be seated comfortably. Two surface electrodes will be placed over the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle belly at the proximal third of the lower leg, with an inter-electrode distance of approximately 2 cm. A ground electrode will be positioned superior to the lateral malleolus. EMG signals will be amplified, band-pass filtered at 20–2000 Hz and sampled at 2000 Hz. During testing, participants will wear a positioning cap designed according to the international 10–20 electroencephalography (EEG) system, and their head will be stabilised to ensure accurate localisation of the stimulation site. A 110 mm double-cone coil will be used for magnetic stimulation.41 The coil will be positioned over the M1 hotspot corresponding to the TA of the affected limb, on the contralateral primary motor cortex.42 It will be placed tangentially to the scalp with the handle oriented perpendicular to the surface. Single-pulse stimulation will be delivered at 1 Hz (one pulse every 5 s). The motor hotspot will be defined as the scalp site that elicits the largest and most consistent MEPs in the target muscle at the lowest stimulation intensity.43 The coil will be moved along the medial border of the M1 region in 0.5 cm increments, with no more than 10 stimuli delivered at each site. A location will be identified as the motor hotspot when at least three stable MEPs are induced in five consecutive stimuli; otherwise, the coil will be advanced to the next adjacent site. RMT will be defined as the lowest stimulation intensity capable of eliciting MEPs ≥50 μV in at least 5 of 10 consecutive stimuli.44 MEPs will be recorded by delivering 10 single pulses at 120% of the RMT, with an interstimulus interval of 5 s. After artefact removal, the mean peak-to-peak MEP amplitude will be calculated to evaluate corticospinal excitability.44 CSP will be assessed while the tibialis anterior is maintained at 30% of MVC. Single-pulse stimulation at 120% of RMT will be delivered, with five stimuli per intensity and a 5 s interstimulus interval. The duration of electromyography (EMG) silence following the MEP will be recorded to reflect motor cortical inhibitory function.45 IHI will be measured using a paired-coil conditioning-test paradigm. A conditioning stimulus (CS) at 110% of RMT will be applied over one M1, followed by a test stimulus (TS) at 120% RMT over the contralateral M1. The interstimulus interval (ISI) will be set at 10 ms. The magnitude of interhemispheric inhibition will be determined by comparing the MEP amplitude elicited under the CS–TS condition with that elicited by the TS alone.46

Sample size and power considerations

Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1. The analysis was based on a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) design, with statistical power set at 0.90 and a two-tailed α level of 0.05. In the absence of prior randomised controlled trials evaluating iTBS in this population, the effect size was informed by a previously published rehabilitation study in postoperative ankle–foot patients, which reported a Cohen’s d of 0.73 for functional improvement. This value was adopted as a pragmatic and conservative estimate of clinically meaningful change in ankle–foot motor function, rather than as a direct comparison of intervention mechanisms.47 Under these assumptions, the required total sample size was calculated to be 22 participants. Allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of 20%, the final planned sample size was increased to 28 participants, with 14 allocated to the experimental group and 14 to the control group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses will be performed using SPSS software V.26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, USA). Continuous variables will be assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance. Normally distributed data will be presented as mean±SD, and non-normally distributed data as median (IQR). The primary outcome (AOFAS) will be analysed using a mixed-design ANOVA with group (iTBS vs sham) as the between-subject factor and time (baseline, post-intervention at week 3, and follow-up at week 6) as the within-subject factor, including the group×time interaction. Significant interactions will be further explored using post hoc pairwise comparisons with appropriate adjustment for multiple testing. The primary hypothesis will be tested by comparing between-group differences in change from baseline (T0) to post-intervention (T1). Effect estimates will be reported as mean differences with 95% CIs and two-sided p values. In addition, the proportion of participants achieving the prespecified MCID of 8 points in AOFAS will be calculated to support the interpretation of clinically meaningful improvement. The follow-up at week 6 (T2) will be analysed as a secondary outcome to assess the durability of treatment effects. Secondary outcomes, including clinical and neurophysiological measures derived from fNIRS and TMS, will be analysed using the same group-by-time analytical framework to characterise functional and cortical changes over time.

For repeated-measures analyses, sphericity will be assessed using Mauchly’s test, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction will be applied when violations are detected. Significant interactions will be explored using post hoc simple-effects analyses with adjustment for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction or false discovery rate control. The primary analysis will follow the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomised participants. A per-protocol analysis will be conducted as a sensitivity analysis. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption, with imputation models incorporating group allocation, time point, baseline outcome values, and relevant demographic and clinical variables. Estimates from imputed datasets will be pooled using Rubin’s rules. Statistical significance will be defined as p<0.05 for a two-tailed probability.

fNIRS data processing will be conducted using NirSpark (Danyang Huichuang Medical Technology Co., Ltd., China) for initial preprocessing, followed by further analyses with the Homer2 toolbox in MATLAB (MATrix LABoratory) 2013b (MathWorks, USA). The raw light-intensity signals will first be converted into optical density. Motion artefacts will then be identified (tMotion=0.5 s, tMask=1.0 s, standard deviation threshold=15.0, amplitude threshold=1.0) and corrected using a spline interpolation algorithm. A band-pass filter (0.01–0.1 Hz) will be applied to remove physiological noise. The filtered optical density data will subsequently be converted into HbO₂ concentrations using the modified Beer–Lambert law. For resting-state analysis, Pearson correlation coefficients will be calculated across all channel pairs based on HbO2 signals and Fisher’s Z transformation will be applied to derive functional connectivity strength. For task-based analysis, a general linear model will be used to estimate cortical activation β-values, and graph-theoretical measures will be applied to characterise cortical network properties. Bonferroni correction will be performed when comparing multiple brain regions.

TMS data will be preprocessed and normalised using MATLAB R2022b (MathWorks, USA). Outliers will be identified through manual inspection and confirmed by statistical checks before removal. To reduce inter-individual variability, RMT, CSP and IHI will be expressed as percentages, whereas MEP amplitudes will be normalised to stimulation intensity. All neurophysiological measures will be analysed alongside behavioural and fNIRS parameters within a unified statistical framework to examine associations between neurophysiological changes and motor recovery. Relationships between variables will be evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. Statistical analyses and significance thresholds will follow the procedures previously described.

Safety measurements

According to current international TMS safety consensus guidelines, participants with metallic implants will be screened in accordance with established safety guidelines for transcranial magnetic stimulation.44 48 Individuals with metallic or electronic implants located in the head or neck, or in proximity to the stimulation coil, will be excluded. Orthopaedic metallic implants located distal to the stimulation site (eg, internal fixation devices in the foot or ankle) may be permitted,49 provided that the implant is non-ferromagnetic, securely fixed and anatomically remote from the stimulation area. Given the rapid spatial decay of the TMS-induced magnetic field, such implants are generally considered to pose low risk.50 Implant type and anatomical location will be documented and reviewed prior to enrolment, and participants will be excluded if implant safety cannot be clearly verified. Safety will be closely monitored and documented throughout the entire treatment period.

Potential adverse events include rare but serious seizures and vasovagal responses such as vasodepressor syncope. Mild and transient events may also occur, including local discomfort, headache, muscle twitching or fatigue.51 To minimise risk, the study will strictly follow international TMS safety guidelines, recommended stimulation dose limits and standardised participant screening procedures. All stimulation will be conducted by physicians trained in TMS safety.44 Vital signs and subjective responses will be continuously monitored, and structured assessments will be performed before and after each intervention. The study team has established a comprehensive emergency response plan, with appropriate resuscitation equipment and referral procedures in place. In the event of any adverse event, appropriate medical interventions will be administered immediately. Event severity, management and outcomes will be meticulously documented and reported promptly to the ethics committee and relevant regulatory authorities.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention

Adherence to the stimulation protocol and rehabilitation sessions will be monitored throughout the study. Attendance will be documented for each treatment session, including session completion and any protocol deviations. To support adherence, participants will receive scheduled reminders and standardised instructions before each visit. All iTBS and sham stimulation procedures will be administered by certified therapists to ensure intervention fidelity.

Data management

All study data will be meticulously recorded in case report forms (CRFs) and independently entered into an Excel database by two researchers, who will perform cross-checks to ensure accuracy. Each participant will be assigned a unique identifier to enable anonymised data management and protect confidentiality. Physical documents will be securely stored in locked cabinets. Electronic data will be saved on password-protected computers. Access will be limited to the principal investigator and authorised study personnel. The data will be used solely for research purposes. Ethical approval and safety oversight are provided by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Suqian First People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University. The committee will periodically review study progress and retain the authority to suspend the trial in the event of serious adverse events. All data management procedures will adhere strictly to established standards for medical research data security and participant privacy protection.

Ethics and dissemination statement

This study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangsu Province (Suqian) Hospital (Application Number: 2025-SR-0333) and registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: ChiCTR2500110905). All participants will receive detailed information about the study and provide written informed consent before enrolment (see online supplemental file 1). Any amendments to the protocol during the trial will be promptly reported to the ethics committee and updated on the trial registry. The study findings will be disseminated through publication in peer-reviewed journals and presentations at scientific conferences.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that chronic-phase patients following peripheral injury may exhibit persistent adaptations in the central nervous system.13 14 These changes include decreased sensorimotor cortical activity and reduced interregional functional connectivity. Such alterations may limit neurofunctional reorganisation induced by peripheral training.52 Importantly, cortical plasticity persists for months to years post-injury.53 It can be modulated through rhythmic stimulation to reactivate relevant neural circuits.54

Building on this rationale, the present study targets patients with chronic ankle injuries following surgery. A ‘central–peripheral’ synergistic rehabilitation strategy will be implemented by combining iTBS with functional exercise training. Modulation of cortical excitability and sensorimotor network connectivity may facilitate motor command transmission and sensorimotor integration, potentially contributing to more efficient functional task execution during rehabilitation training. This approach aims to assess the potential augmentative effect of non-invasive central stimulation on rehabilitation outcomes in chronic-phase patients.55 Neurophysiological and functional outcomes will be jointly measured and analysed. Examining the temporal correspondence between neural modulation and functional performance may help clarify how cortical-level changes are associated with observed functional improvements, without implying a direct causal relationship. This allows for the evaluation of the consistency between neural modulation and functional improvement. It also provides a methodological basis for elucidating intervention mechanisms and supports the optimisation of individualised neuromodulation strategies.

Although this study has innovative aspects, several limitations exist. These include a small sample size, a relatively short intervention period, a limited follow-up duration and individual variability in cortical responses. In addition, participants may perceive subtle differences between real and sham iTBS despite blinding. Future studies could integrate brain imaging navigation systems or real-time EEG feedback. This would allow optimisation of stimulation parameters and individualised protocols. Overall, this study combines neuromodulation with functional training. It establishes a top-down central regulation and bottom-up peripheral feedback interaction. This strategy may facilitate comprehensive optimisation of the brain–limb control network. It has the potential to improve local motor function and offers a novel approach for the rehabilitation of patients with chronic ankle injuries following surgery.
