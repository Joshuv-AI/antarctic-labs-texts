High Chlorine Concentrations in an Unconventional Oil and Gas Development Region and Impacts on Atmospheric Chemistry
Authors: Catherine
G Masoud, Mrinali Modi, Nirvan Bhattacharyya, Leif G Jahn, Kristi N McPherson, Pearl Abue, Kanan Patel, David T Allen, Lea Hildebrandt Ruiz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrowth in unconventional oil and gas development (UOGD) in the United States has increased airborne emissions, raising environmental and human health concerns. To assess the potential impacts on air quality, we deployed instrumentation in Karnes City, Texas, a rural area in the middle of the Eagle Ford Shale. We measured several episodes of elevated Cl2 levels, reaching maximum hourly averages of 800 ppt, the highest inland Cl2 concentration reported to date. Concentrations peak during the day, suggesting a strong local source (given the short photolysis lifetime of Cl2) and/or a photoinitiated production mechanism. Well preproduction activity near the measurement site is a plausible source of these high Cl2 levels via direct emission and photoactive chemistry. ClNO2 is also observed, but it peaks overnight, consistent with well-known nocturnal formation processes. Observations of organochlorines in the gas and particle phases reflect the contribution of chlorine chemistry to the formation of secondary pollutants in the area. Box modeling results suggest that the formation of ozone at this location is influenced by chlorine chemistry. These results suggest that UOGD can be an important source of reactive chlorine in the atmosphere, impacting radical budgets and the formation of secondary pollutants in these regions.Keywords: Cl2, unconventional oil and gas development, chlorine chemistry, secondary pollutants

Introduction

The advancement of horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing technology has led to an increase in unconventional oil and gas development (UOGD) in the United States.1 The Eagle Ford Shale, one of the most active gas shale plays in the world, has seen rapid growth in production over the last two decades, increasing gas and oil production by approximately an order of magnitude from 2008 to 2022.2 These increases in production have resulted in increased emissions associated with UOGD and concerns regarding human health impacts as several studies have highlighted the detrimental health impacts of residing in proximity to UOGD activity.3–9

UOGD directly emits a range of air pollutants, including light alkanes (especially methane), single-ring and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, hydrogen sulfide, particulate matter (PM), and black carbon,10 among others. While these species and their photochemical products (oxidized species) have been the focus of air quality measurements in UOGD regions, other potential pollutants such as chlorinated species have not been considered in detail. Likely sources of chlorine in UOGD regions include hydrochloric acid added to fracturing fluid as biocide, molecular chlorine or other chlorinated biocides added to fracturing fluid or produced water retention ponds, and highly concentrated formation water which mixes with produced water during hydraulic fracturing operations.11–13

Reactive chlorine species such as molecular chlorine (Cl2) and nitryl chloride (ClNO2) photolyze in the atmosphere to form chlorine radical (Cl), a potent atmospheric oxidant that impacts air quality by oxidizing VOCs, contributing to the formation of ozone and secondary organic aerosol, and affecting radical budgets.14–21 Cl radical oxidizes a wide range of VOCs, often more rapidly than hydroxyl radical (OH),22–24 and forms a different set of products than other oxidants.19,25 These include organochlorine species (generally formed when Cl adds to a double bond), which may have substantial health impacts. Furthermore, observations and models show that Cl radicals alter OH and O3 concentrations.26–29

Various species and processes have been recognized to contribute to atmospheric concentrations of reactive chlorine species. Chloride-rich sea spray was one of the first aerosol types to be recognized as contributing to reactive chlorine budgets through aqueous-phase and heterogeneous reactions between chloride and other atmospheric oxidants or NOx species.14 In recent years, a wide variety of chloride-rich aerosol types, including biomass-burning aerosols,30,31 playa dusts,32 and road salt,33 have been observed to contribute to atmospheric chlorine radical budgets through similar reactions with atmospheric oxidants and NOx. Photochemical production of Cl2 has also been posited in several regions based on observed diel trends in Cl2 concentrations that peak during daytime periods of high solar flux.34–36 Photolysis of particulate nitrate within acidic aerosols has been proposed as a pathway to form Cl2 in one of these environments.36 The recent growth in the number of environments and aerosols where production of Cl radical precursors has been observed suggests that potential formation processes may remain undiscovered.

In the present work, we describe measurements of chlorinated compounds performed in Karnes City: a rural town located at the center of the Eagle Ford Shale oil and gas production region in southeastern Texas. We investigate potential sources of high Cl2 emissions and the impacts of the resultant chlorine chemistry on secondary pollutant formation.

Methods

Campaign and Trailer Setup

The Eagle Ford Shale is an oil and gas shale play that extends over a 20,000-square-mile area of the Western Gulf Coast Basin.2 We conducted a 7 week field campaign in Karnes City, Texas, a town in the Eagle Ford Shale with 8852 drilled wells producing 93 million barrels of oil and 306 billion cubic feet of gas in 2021.37 The instrumentation trailer was colocated with the Continuous Ambient Monitoring Station [CAMS 1070, EPA Site 482551070; coordinates (28.8804400, −97.8880650°)] at Karnes City, operated and maintained by the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ). We retrieved meteorological parameters such as wind speed and direction, along with hourly concentrations of selected alkanes and VOCs [measured from the TCEQ Automated Gas Chromatograph (Auto GC)], as reported by TCEQ.38 A noteworthy feature of Karnes City is its location within the Eagle Ford Shale wet gas/condensate window, with a high density of oil wells to its north and gas wells to its south (several well sites are within 5 miles of the measurement site), and its proximity to a major metropolitan area (50 miles southeast of San Antonio, TX).39

The instrumentation trailer was a 30 ft trailer containing a suite of instruments including a cavity attenuated phase shift NO2 analyzer (CAPS-NO2, Environment S.A AS32M), a chemiluminescence NO/NOx analyzer (Teledyne Model 200E), a photometric ozone analyzer (Teledyne Model 400E), an Aerosol Chemical Speciation Monitor (ACSM, Aerodyne Research Inc.),40 a Scanning Electrical Mobility Spectrometer (SEMS, Brechtel Model 2002),41 a Chlorine-Oxidative Flow Reactor (Cl-OFR, Aerodyne Research Inc.),42 a Vocus 2R Chemical Ionization Time of Flight Mass Spectrometer (Vocus-2R CI-ToF-MS, Aerodyne Research Inc.),43 and a Filter Inlet for Gases and Aerosols mounted onto a High-Resolution Time of Flight Chemical Ionization Mass Spectrometer (FIGAERO-CIMS, Aerodyne Research Inc.).44,45 We recorded temperature and relative humidity (RH) using three HOBO UX100-003 data loggers, which were placed inside, directly outside, and approximately 3 m away from the instrumentation trailer. Due to gaps in available relative humidity and temperature data, reanalysis model results from the High-Resolution Rapid Refresh model46,47 were utilized and are further described and compared to available on-site measurements in the Supporting Information (Section S4 and Figure S9). This article focuses on data from the FIGAERO-CIMS; other forthcoming articles will summarize results from other instrumentation.

FIGAERO-CIMS

We used a High-Resolution Time of Flight Chemical Ionization Mass Spectrometer (CIMS) in iodide (I−) mode to measure slightly to highly oxidized organic species, chlorinated (or halogenated) species, and nitrogenated species.44 The FIGAERO-CIMS was operational for 3 phases of the campaign: March 20 –March 27, April 17 –April 28, April 29 –May 5, 2021, hereafter referred to as phase 1, phase 2, and phase 3, respectively. CIMS data were unavailable for the periods between these phases. For the first phase of the campaign, ultrahigh-purity nitrogen gas (UHP N2) was passed through a bubbler filled with deionized water and then through a methyl iodide (CH3I) permeation tube, after which the resulting humidified reagent gas stream was passed through a Po-210 source to generate I− and (H2O)I− ions. The water bubbler was bypassed for the second and third phases of the campaign due to temporary issues with water purity, so the reagent gas stream was not actively humidified during those phases, which can alter ion-adduct formation behavior and clustering.44,48 The iodide ions meet the gas-phase analyte (M) stream in the ion-molecule reaction region of the CIMS, forming M[I−] adducts. These adducts then transfer to the time-of-flight (ToF) chamber in the CIMS where their time-of-flight is related to their exact mass-to-charge ratio (m/z).

We measured the molecular composition and desorption behavior of species in the particle phase using a FIGAERO inlet which was mounted to the CIMS. The gas sampling lines were made of perfluoroalkoxy polymer and were approximately 2.4 m long with an internal diameter of 3.9 mm. Gases were sampled at a total flow rate of 2.5 LPM. The lines were made long enough to reasonably avoid the influence of air flow from the trailer while minimizing the impact of sampling line artifacts from semivolatile or reactive compounds. The particle sampling lines were made of cleaned 316 stainless steel (McMaster-Carr), were colocated with gas sampling lines, and were of the same 2.4 m length with an internal diameter of 4.5 mm. Particles were collected at 5 LPM onto a 1.0 μm poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE) filter (Pall Corporation) during online gas-phase measurement. The particle collection period varied from 1 to 2 h for this campaign. After the particle collection period, the filter with the deposited particles was moved to align with the HR-ToF-CIMS inlet via an actuator. Particles were desorbed by passing heated UHP N2 gas through the PTFE filter to volatilize compounds from the sampled particles. The thermal desorption routine comprised 5 min of room temperature desorption, 25 min of a temperature ramp from 25 to 200 °C, 15 min of filter soaking at 200 °C, and 15 min of cooling back to 25 °C.

We recorded the instrument background signals in two ways: for the gas-phase background, we placed a carbon filter (Whatman) in line with the inlet for a period of 10 min and utilized the resulting signal as the gas-phase background signal. For the particle-phase background, the particle PTFE filter underwent a thermal desorption cycle without prior collection of particles (i.e., a “blank” filter desorption), and we used the resulting signal as the particle-phase background signal.

We analyzed the FIGAERO-CIMS data using Tofware version 3.2.3 (Tofwerk, AG) in Igor Pro version 8 (Wavemetrics Inc.). Three calibrants (I−, (H2O)I− , and I3−) defined the conversion of ion time-of-flight to mass-to-charge (m/z) with a mass scale calibration error less than 5 ppm. Subsequently, exact m/z values recorded for the sampled chemical species enabled the assignment of elemental formulas to species. We report ions consistent with iodide adducts of sampled species and use these for subsequent analyses. We normalize signals by the reagent ion (I−) signal to account for changes in the instrument sensitivity.

Due to technical difficulties with the operation of the CIMS, the three measurement phases had different voltage settings, chosen to optimize sensitivity to sampled species and the mass resolution of the acquired data. Between phases 1 and 2, the preamplifier failed and was replaced, which necessitated retuning of instrument voltages to achieve the desired instrument response. During phase 2, a voltage in the short-segmented quadrupole region was not set correctly, resulting in another voltage change and tuning adjustments between phases 2 and 3. Due to a hardware change (installation of a new preamplifier) between phases 1 and 2, postcalibration for sensitivity during phase 1 was not possible. For phases 2 and 3, we conducted a series of multipoint (4+) Cl2 and ClNO2 calibrations in the laboratory after the completion of the campaign for each voltage setting at different relative humidity values. Postcalibrations of this type have been previously utilized for Cl2 and ClNO2 in the literature.49,50 In both of our calibrations, the analyte of interest was injected into a 10 m3 Teflon chamber flushed with clean, dry (<1% RH) air and prehumidified to the desired level of humidification (<1, 25, and 50%) via clean air flow through a bubbler of deionized water. Cl2 was directly injected into the chamber from a gas cylinder (100.3 ppm of Cl2 in N2, Airgas). ClNO2 was reactively produced by the reaction of Cl2 with an aqueous solution of sodium nitrite as in prior studies25,51 and injected into a chamber. We fit a line to each multipoint calibration, obtaining a sensitivity (cps ppb–1) at each humidity level (Figure S1). We then fit a power law function (eq S1) to the measured sensitivity vs absolute humidity curves (Figure S1) to generate humidity-dependent instrument sensitivity functions. This power law function results in smaller sensitivity changes at higher humidities, consistent with measured sensitivities in prior literature.44,48,52 We estimate an uncertainty of ±30% for the calculated sensitivities.

Atmospheric Box Modeling

To understand the impact of high chlorine concentrations on ozone, secondary OH formation, and subsequent atmospheric reactivity, typical ambient conditions at the measurement site were simulated using the box modeling framework within the California Statewide Air Pollution Research Center (SAPRC) software system.53 The ambient gas-phase chemistry was modeled using the Carbon Bond 6 revision 5 mechanism (CB6r5) with an added chlorine chemistry.54,55 The box modeling framework mimics atmospheric variability by performing single-cell ambient simulations with time-varying solar intensities corresponding to the geographical location and time of the year. It takes into account the temperature, humidity, and initial pollutant concentrations, as described in more detail in the Supporting Information (Section S2).

We simulated three scenarios to estimate the impact of Cl2 emissions on ozone and secondary OH formation: (case 1) a base case scenario with no Cl2 emissions; (case 2) moderate Cl2 emissions during the day, resulting in an average Cl2 concentration of 150 ppt; and (case 3) high Cl2 emissions during the day, resulting in an average Cl2 concentration of 800 ppt (Figure S3). We chose Cl2 emissions to be higher during the day than at night to simulate the measured diurnal cycle of Cl2, which showed higher concentrations during the day despite Cl2 photolysis.

Results and Discussion

Chlorine and Nitryl Chloride Measurements

We measured elevated levels of molecular chlorine (Cl2) and nitryl chloride (ClNO2) during several periods of the campaign. The highest concentrations were consistently measured in phase 2 from 04/17 to 04/26, with more consistently high levels measured from 04/17 to 04/22 and sporadically elevated levels measured from 04/25 to 04/26, as seen in Figure 1. The highest Cl2 concentrations were observed during the daytime, while ClNO2 concentrations peaked overnight during the high Cl2 period, as shown in the inset diurnal plot in Figure 1. Of the 8 days with elevated Cl2 measurements, four had the daily Cl2 maximum occur in the early afternoon (12–2 pm), two had the daily maximum in the late afternoon (4–6 pm), and two had the daily maximum later in the day (8–11 pm). Each day during this period (with the exception of 04/21) also experienced the daily minimum in the early morning hours of 4–8 am. High daytime Cl2 concentrations indicate either a strong local source, given the short photolysis lifetime of Cl2 (∼7 min based on our model and 10 min in previous work in the Arctic56) or a photoinitiated production mechanism. Phase 2 and 3 average Cl2 concentrations were 141 and 14 ppt, respectively. The high variability and intermittent nature of the high Cl2 concentrations are suggestive of a temporary or episodic emission source. The concentrations of Cl2 and ClNO2 appear to anticorrelate with each other during certain measurement periods (04/19–04/22) but show no correlation during other periods, which may point to these species originating from differing sources and/or production pathways.

Concentrations of Cl2 [ppt] (left axis) and ClNO2 [ppt] (right axis) for phases 2 and 3 of the measurement campaign. The inset shows the average diurnal cycle during this high Cl2 period from 4/17 to 5/5, with shading indicating ± one standard deviation of the diurnal average. Sunrise and sunset occurred at approximately 7 am and 8 pm (hours 7 and 20), respectively, over this period.

In this study, the highest measured hourly averaged Cl2 concentration was 800 ppt for 1 h on 04/24. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the highest concentrations of molecular chlorine recorded in the troposphere to date. It is second only to a recent measurement from Hong Kong (1 ppb of Cl2) made near a polluted coastal site.36Table S2 presents several studies which measured atmospheric Cl2 and ClNO2 levels and provides information on the median and maximum concentrations, measurement locations, and potential sources when available. Concentrations of ClNO2 measured in the Karnes City were consistently lower than Cl2 concentrations and peaked at different times. This indicates that N2O5/ClNO2 is not a major driver in volatilizing particle-phase chloride or in generating reactive chlorine species in the gas phase. NOX levels ranged from less than 2 to 25 ppb during the campaign (Figure S6) and were reasonably correlated with ClNO2 but not correlated with the behavior of Cl2. Without a clear local source of high ClNO2 concentrations, the formation of Cl2 from ClNO2 reactive uptake57 is also unlikely. Based on the diel trends and lack of correlation with ClNO2 and NOX, we posit that a photoactive source is more likely, as suggested in other studies which observe similar periods of high Cl2 and low ClNO2.34,36 In addition, studies of Arctic snowpack halogen chemistry58 have also observed similar high Cl2 and low ClNO2 periods, through behaviors that are dependent on temperatures and surfaces and are not relevant in this study.

Potential Sources of Molecular Chlorine

To understand the potential origin of elevated chlorine concentrations, we utilized publicly available data on wind speed and wind direction from the colocated TCEQ site (EPA Site 482551070)59 in tandem with data from the Texas Railroad Commission and images from Google Earth. Several sites near our measurement location may have emitted chlorine and/or its precursors, including several retention ponds that hold flowback and/or produced water (Kotara Unit well pad in the NE direction, Retention Pond A in the ENE direction, Retention Pond B in the SE direction, and a local hospital in the SSE direction), as seen in Figure S4. Active well sites are an important potential source of chlorine, given that produced water which is released from hydraulic fracturing activity can be up to 10 times more concentrated in chloride salts than seawater,60 providing a means of generating highly concentrated chloride-bearing aerosols and surface water. Depending on the stage of the well production, the produced water may be primarily composed of fracturing fluid or natural formation water.11,61 In any stage of the well’s lifetime, biocides can be added to the produced water that is stored underground or in surface retention ponds like those in Figure S4.12 These biocides often include chlorinated compounds due to their biocidal functionality, some of which are classified as potential carcinogens (e.g., tetrachloroethylene, dichloromethane, dichloropropene, bromodichloromethane, chloroform, benzyl chloride, etc.).62

To further narrow the potential original source, we utilize a combination of wind roses (Figure S5) and Cl2 pollutant roses (Figure 2) to determine the relevant wind direction during high concentration periods. For the duration of the campaign (and more specifically for phase 2), the wind was primarily blowing from the northeast and southeast directions (Figure S5). For one of the high Cl2 periods (04/19–04/21, shown in Figure 2), the highest concentrations of Cl2 (500+ ppt) coincided with the wind blowing from the east, southeast, and southern directions. Cl2 concentrations in the range of 200–400 ppt coincided with the wind blowing from the northeast direction. We used online data published by the Texas Railroad Commission63 on oil and gas production activity (in BBL oil/month and MCF gas/month) for active well sites near the measurement site. Table S1 shows oil production activity for 11 well sites that are located in the northeast (NE), east (E), southeast (SE), and south (S) directions relative to the measurement site. The most notable of these sites includes the Kotara Unit – A site (no. 1) in the NE direction, which shows oil production activity increasing from 13 BBL oil in March 2021 to ∼100,000 BBL oil in April 2021, suggestive of well stimulation activity or potential refracturing activity sometime in April 2021, concurrent with the elevated Cl2 concentrations during phase 2 of the campaign. Other notable sites are the Karnes City SE Unit (#6) and the Moy South (#9) sites, which also recorded a ramp-up in oil and gas production rates from February to March 2021 and March to April 2021, respectively. Of note, Cl2 concentrations do not correlate with the observed alkane concentrations (Figure S7), consistent with the Cl2 sources related to hydraulic fracturing and flowback water rather than oil and gas production.

Average pollutant roses for 3 days of consistently elevated Cl2 concentrations during phase 2 (04/19–04/21). Colors indicate the concentrations of Cl2 observed and the average wind direction during the observation, while the total distance from the center of a particular wind direction wedge indicates the fraction of observations that originated from that direction.

Another potential site of interest noted in Figure S4 is a local hospital located 2 miles south of the measurement trailer. Hospitals may have on-site medical waste incinerators that are known to release chlorinated compounds such as HCl at up to 1000 ppm under uncontrolled conditions.64 HCl is classified as a hazardous air pollutant under the Clean Air Act65 and may be a potential precursor for molecular chlorine formation. Although the hospital may be one of the sources of chlorinated species in this area, we posit that it is not the primary source of Cl2 for three reasons. First, the wind back – trajectory analysis (Figure S5) discussed above for the high Cl2 periods does not show good overlap with the hospital location. Second, HCl emitted from medical waste combustion primarily originates from polyvinyl chloride (PVC, a common plastic) combustion, which is expected to be a regular component of medical waste that is therefore inconsistent with the sporadic nature of elevated Cl2 concentrations over the measurement campaign (i.e., 8 days over a 7 week campaign). Third, we do not observe conditions conducive to rapid HCl uptake and conversion to Cl2.20,57,66 Specifically, the ACSM (which measures bulk nonrefractory PM1 aerosol composition) did not measure high levels of particulate chloride in association with high Cl2 episodes, nor did it measure elevated concentrations of species such as ammonium that are necessary to drive high HCl uptake.67,68 The ACSM is limited to measuring PM1 and does not measure refractory species, such as sodium chloride. Both sodium chloride aerosols and particles larger than 1 μm may contribute to Cl2 formation but would be undetected in this study.

To summarize, an assessment of wind direction shows air masses to the site passing over a nearby well, the timing of local well activity suggests that hydraulic fracturing or well stimulation using chloride-rich waters coincided with the high Cl2 period, and the diel pattern of observed Cl2 suggests a photoactive process to form Cl2. These observations together point toward a photoinitiated process involving the chloride-rich water used during hydraulic fracturing and/or well stimulation as the observed source of Cl2. Based on the present measurements, it is not clear whether this occurs in surface waters (such as holding ponds or storage tanks) or in aerosols (such as droplets or aerosols generated during fluid usage and pumping). This mechanism would be consistent with studies36,69 that have shown that photoactive processes can drive inorganic halogen formation in chloride-containing aerosols of different compositions. However, due to the variable and proprietary water compositions, we are unable to point to a specific photoactive agent that may initiate Cl2 formation. Hydraulic fracturing and well-stimulation processes, therefore, may impact the availability of chlorine radical precursors in oil and gas production regions depending on the composition of hydraulic fracturing and produced waters and potentially fluid storage practices.

Impacts of Chlorine Chemistry on the Measured Pollutant Mixture

In addition to molecular chlorine and nitryl chloride, we observed a variety of organic species in the gas and particle phases, including oxygenated organics (compounds with the molecular formula CxHyOz, abbreviated as CHO); chlorinated organics (CxHyCliOz, abbreviated as CHClO); and nitrogen-containing organics (CxHyNiOz, abbreviated as CHNO). Specifically, we observed organochlorines including C2H3ClO2, C3H5ClO2, C4H5ClO3, and C6H9ClO5, which were previously measured from environmental chamber reactions of alkanes with chlorine,70 and C4H7ClO3, C5H9ClO2, C5H9ClO3, C5H9ClO4, C5H7ClO3, C5H11ClO3, C5H8Cl2O3, and C5H10Cl2O2, which were measured from the reactions of isoprene with chlorine under varying levels of NOx.16,18 As seen in Figures 3 and S8, almost all of the species show similar behavior during phases 2 and 3 of the campaign, where their gas-phase concentrations generally only increase around 7 AM (sunrise), peak around 11 AM to 3 PM, and begin to decline around 6 PM (as solar radiance declines before sunset at 8 PM). The particle phase (FIGAERO) data are shown in the shaded gray areas in Figures 3 and S8. Particle-phase signals will generally follow the programed temperature ramp as discussed in Section 2.2, with signals increasing as the temperature increases and as the compounds desorb off the filter and volatilize, followed by a period of signal decrease as the compounds’ loading on the filter decreases and as the filter cools down. Most species appear in both the gas and particle phases at varying levels, except for C3H5ClO2, which is only observed in the gas phase, and C5H8Cl2O3 (a dichlorinated organic), which is only observed in the particle phase. Other notable organochlorines that were observed at higher intensities include C2H5ClO2, C3H7ClO2, C6H5ClO (which has a formula consistent with chlorophenol), C6H12Cl2O, C7H9ClO5, and C8H11ClO2. The presence of various organochlorines is consistent with the chlorine radicals reacting with (and adding to) VOCs present in the atmosphere. The variation in organochlorine signals with time of day (specifically the increase in signal during daytime) seen in Figure 3 suggests that photolysis of chlorinated species, including molecular chlorine, is necessary to produce chlorine radicals, which initiate the formation of organochlorines from VOCs. Chlorine radicals may initiate oxidation of VOCs via hydrogen abstraction (to form oxygenated organics) or chlorine addition (to form organochlorines), and further atmospheric processing of organochlorines may result in the loss of the Cl moiety from the molecule.71 Thus, our observations cannot be used to evaluate the extent of Cl- versus OH-initiated reactions, but they do provide evidence for the relevance of chlorine chemistry in this ambient environment.

The top and middle panels in Figure 4 show that the highest chlorine concentrations occur during the periods when the wind is blowing from the northeast or southeast directions. To understand the impact of Cl2 levels on the resulting distribution of pollutants measured by the CIMS, we studied several 1 h gas sampling periods during similar diurnal periods for all three phases of the campaign (identical periods may not have been chosen due to FIGAERO desorption cycles occurring over different periods each day). As seen in Figure 5, the CHO group made up 70 to 84% of the total identified gas phase signal measured by the CIMS. Of note, this treatment assumes a uniform sensitivity to all measured and identified species. Within the CHO group, the largest contributors were compounds with 1–5 carbon atoms, followed by compounds with 6–10 carbon atoms, which is consistent with lighter alkanes dominating the VOC mixture in oil and gas production regions.72 We observed elevated levels of inorganic chlorine gases, including Cl2, HCl, ClNO2, HOCl, and ClONO2 during phase 2 of the campaign and notably lower levels during phases 1 and 3. During phase 2, gas-phase organochlorine (CXHYCliOZ) contributions do not shift along with Cl2 levels (i.e., the organochlorine fraction remains fairly constant as Cl2 concentrations go from 185 to 6 ppt on Apr-22 1 PM to Apr-23 1 AM). This is consistent with longer persistence of these organochlorine species in the atmosphere than the highly reactive Cl2, as has been observed in prior work.73

Potential Impacts of Chlorine on Ozone and Secondary Pollutant Formation

Figure 6A shows a comparison of modeled OH and Cl concentrations for three box modeling scenarios: (case 1) base case scenario with no Cl2 emissions; (case 2) moderate Cl2 emissions during the day, resulting in an average Cl2 concentration of 150 ppt; and (case 3) high Cl2 emissions during the day, resulting in an average Cl2 concentration of 800 ppt (Figure S3). OH radical formation begins relatively early in the presence of Cl2 emissions (case 3 and case 2), in comparison to case 1 with no Cl2 emissions. This suggests the formation of secondary OH radicals in cases 2 and 3 from the Cl radical–initiated chemistry. Additionally, the OH radical concentrations are higher throughout the day for cases 2 and 3 than for case 1, indicating an overall increase in OH and Cl reactivity in the atmosphere in the presence of Cl radicals.

Modeled and observed concentrations from April 20, 2021, of (A) hydroxyl and chlorine radicals and (B) ozone. Case 1 is the modeled “no Cl2 emission case”, while cases 2 and 3 have moderate to high Cl2 emissions.

Figure 6B shows a comparison of the modeled ozone for all three scenarios with the observed ozone concentrations. The rise in modeled ozone concentrations in the morning in all three scenarios is consistent with the observations on April 20, 2021. The qualitative behavior of ozone observed is very similar to the modeled ozone in case (3), i.e., the ozone concentrations rise steeply during the early morning hours, followed by steady behavior during the afternoon. We see a slow decline in the evening, and then finally ozone becomes constant throughout the night. Quantitatively, the model overestimates the magnitude of the ozone formed in cases (2) and (3) but underestimates the ozone in case (1). The overestimation of ozone by the box-model simulations could be due to the simplicity of the VOC mixture chosen in the model runs. Additionally, the presence of high Cl2 emissions in case (3) creates a more reactive environment, leading to early ozone formation. However, later in the day, excess Cl radicals react with ozone, resulting in the depletion of some of the ozone formed. Consequently, during the latter part of the day, we observe lower ozone concentrations in case (3) compared with those in case (2).

The observed ozone during the daytime is almost two to three times higher than the modeled ozone in case (1). However, early morning growth in ozone, as observed in the measurements, is seen in both cases (2) and (3), where Cl2 emissions are simulated. This is consistent with the presence of Cl2 emissions and their subsequent chemistry leading to an increase in ozone formation. While some measurements have shown tropospheric depletion of ozone by chlorine and other halogens,74 in polluted urban environments, ozone enhancement by chlorine radicals has been previously observed in early morning air masses.29,75,76

The presence of elevated levels of chlorine (up to 800 ppt) in a VOC-rich environment in this oil and gas production region has serious implications for secondary pollutant production. Previous studies have shown that chlorine initiates reactions that form secondary organic aerosol at yields higher than reactions initiated by more commonly studied atmospheric oxidants such as OH, O3, and NO3.21 In addition to elevating levels of PM, chlorine has the potential to enhance ozone mixing ratios, as illustrated here and in prior work.76 Some organochlorines are known to be toxic, and PM and ozone are criteria pollutants with known negative impacts on human health, highlighting the importance of understanding the impact of atmospheric chlorine levels on the formation of harmful secondary products.
