Breathless and Beyond: Anterior Mediastinal Malignant Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumor as a Rare Neurofibromatosis Type 1 Manifestation
Authors: Muhammad Hassan Raza, Hamza Asif, Sadia Anjum, Zaira Shahid, Moosa Mubarika, Abdulsattar Anjum, Hassan Abdullah Shakeel, Rejina Chhetri, Muneeb Khawar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMalignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) can rarely present as anterior mediastinal masses in patients with neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), causing respiratory symptoms and diagnostic challenges. Multidisciplinary evaluation, including biopsy and imaging, is essential for diagnosis, with surgical resection as the primary treatment. Early recognition and vigilant monitoring in NF1 patients are crucial for prompt intervention and improved outcomes.Keywords: anterior mediastinal mass, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, neurofibromatosis type 1, soft tissue sarcoma

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), historically referred to as von Recklinghausen disease, is an autosomal dominant neurocutaneous disorder with a global prevalence estimated at 1 in 3000 to 1 in 6000 individuals [1]. Caused by mutations in the NF1 gene located on chromosome 17q11.2, this condition disrupts the production of neurofibromin, a tumor suppressor protein, leading to a wide array of clinical manifestations across multiple systems [2]. NF1 is characterized by dermatologic features such as café‐au‐lait macules, axillary and inguinal freckling (Crowe sign), and cutaneous neurofibromas, alongside neurological, ophthalmologic, and orthopedic complications, including optic gliomas, Lisch nodules, and skeletal dysplasias [1, 3]. The diagnostic criteria for NF1, established by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), require the presence of at least two of seven clinical features, including a first‐degree relative with NF1, to confirm the diagnosis [4].

One of the most severe complications of NF1 is its predisposition to malignancies, notably malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs), previously termed neurofibrosarcomas or malignant schwannomas. MPNSTs are aggressive soft tissue sarcomas arising from peripheral nerve sheaths, with an incidence of 8%–13% in NF1 patients compared to 0.001% in the general population [2, 5]. Anterior mediastinal MPNSTs are exceedingly rare, with only isolated case reports in the recent literature, often leading to delayed diagnosis due to overlap with common mediastinal pathologies like thymomas or lymphomas. These tumors typically manifest as rapidly enlarging, painful masses, often accompanied by localized neurological deficits, and are most commonly located in the extremities, trunk, or head and neck regions [6]. Early detection is paramount, as complete surgical resection with clear margins remains the only curative treatment, while delayed diagnosis significantly worsens prognosis [2, 7]. Adjuvant therapies, such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy, are often employed for unresectable or metastatic disease, though their efficacy is limited [8].

MPNSTs in NF1 patients are associated with a poorer prognosis compared to sporadic cases, with 5‐year survival rates ranging from 15% to 50% depending on tumor size, grade, and surgical resectability [9]. The rarity of MPNSTs presenting as mediastinal masses, particularly in the anterior mediastinum, adds a layer of diagnostic complexity, as such presentations are sparsely reported in the literature [10]. Mediastinal MPNSTs may compress vital structures, leading to symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, or hoarseness, which can mimic other thoracic pathologies, thus delaying diagnosis [11]. The histopathological diagnosis of MPNST relies on features such as spindle cell morphology, hypercellularity, and immunohistochemical markers like S100 and β‐III‐spectrin, though these are not universally present [6, 12]. Imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET), play a critical role in delineating tumor extent and guiding surgical planning [7].

This case report describes a rare presentation of an anterior mediastinal MPNST in a 19‐year‐old female with NF1, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and clinical implications of such an uncommon manifestation. By presenting this case, we aim to contribute to the limited literature on mediastinal MPNSTs and underscore the importance of vigilant monitoring for malignant transformation in NF1 patients.

Case Presentation

Patient Information and Clinical History

The patient was a 19‐year‐old female of South Asian ethnicity residing in an urban area of Pakistan, with no history of occupational exposure. She was a non‐smoker, did not consume alcohol, and followed a standard diet. Her body mass index was 20.5 kg/m2. She presented with progressive shortness of breath and chest discomfort.

She had a known history of café‐au‐lait macules since early childhood and a positive family history of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) in her father. She presented to the emergency department with progressively worsening dyspnea over 3 weeks. Initially exertional, her dyspnea progressed to occur at rest. This was associated with dull, left‐sided chest pain and a dry cough. She denied hemoptysis, fever, weight loss, night sweats, recent travel, or known tuberculosis exposure. There was no history of palpitations, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, syncope, or lower limb swelling. Her symptoms did not improve with over‐the‐counter medications.

She had no prior hospitalizations or surgeries and reported no known drug allergies.

Clinical Examination

On examination, the patient was alert but visibly anxious and using accessory muscles of respiration. Her temperature was 37.1°C, heart rate 116 beats per minute, blood pressure 104/66 mmHg, respiratory rate 28 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation was 89% on room air, improving to 94% with 4 L/min of supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula.

Respiratory examination revealed asymmetric chest expansion with reduced movement on the left side. Percussion was dull throughout the left hemithorax, and auscultation demonstrated absent breath sounds over the entire left lung field. The trachea was deviated to the right. Cutaneous examination showed six café‐au‐lait macules larger than 1.5 cm, multiple cutaneous neurofibromas over the trunk and upper limbs, and axillary and inguinal freckling consistent with Crowe sign. Cardiovascular and neurological examinations were unremarkable.

Laboratory Investigations

Initial laboratory investigations revealed a complete blood count within normal limits. Inflammatory markers were elevated, with a C‐reactive protein level of 9.8 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 52 mm/h. Lactate dehydrogenase was elevated at 520 U/L. Liver and renal function tests were within normal limits, and screening tests for HIV and tuberculosis were negative. The elevated LDH and inflammatory markers raised suspicion for an underlying malignant process.

Imaging Findings

Chest radiography demonstrated complete opacification of the left hemithorax with tracheal deviation to the right and loss of the left cardiac silhouette (Figure 1).

Subsequent contrast‐enhanced computed tomography of the chest revealed a large, well‐defined, heterogeneously enhancing mass occupying the anterior mediastinum, measuring approximately 11 × 9 × 7 cm. The mass displaced adjacent mediastinal structures and caused near‐complete collapse of the left lung. No pleural effusion, rib destruction, or significant lymphadenopathy was identified (Figure 2).

Histopathology and Diagnosis

A CT‐guided tru‐cut biopsy of the mediastinal mass showed densely cellular fascicles of monotonous spindle cells with hyperchromatic nuclei and frequent mitotic figures. Areas of hypercellularity, pleomorphism, and necrosis were present. Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated focal positivity for S100 protein, strong positivity for β‐III‐spectrin, and a Ki‐67 proliferation index of approximately 40%, consistent with a high‐grade malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor.

Given the clinical findings, the patient was further evaluated for neurofibromatosis type 1. Genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic NF1 gene mutation. Based on National Institutes of Health diagnostic criteria, she fulfilled multiple diagnostic features, including café‐au‐lait macules, axillary and inguinal freckling, multiple cutaneous neurofibromas, a positive family history, and the presence of an MPNST.

A whole‐body PET‐CT scan showed no evidence of distant metastatic disease. The final diagnosis of anterior mediastinal malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor in a patient with neurofibromatosis type 1 was established.

Management and Follow‐Up

The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary tumor board meeting. Surgical resection via median sternotomy was planned with the goal of achieving negative margins. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy was deferred due to acute respiratory compromise. Adjuvant radiotherapy (50 Gy in 25 fractions) was planned postoperatively because of high‐grade tumor features.

Acute management included supplemental oxygen therapy and close monitoring in the intensive care unit to stabilize respiratory distress prior to definitive treatment. The patient was also referred for genetic counseling and scheduled for regular follow‐up for surveillance of recurrence and metastasis.

At six‐month follow‐up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no evidence of disease recurrence on repeat computed tomography imaging. Ongoing surveillance was planned at three‐month intervals.

Conclusion

Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST) can present as rare anterior mediastinal masses. Frequent follow‐up, history and physical examination, and imaging studies can detect these tumors early for surgical excision, as chemotherapy and radiation are of limited use in the case of advanced MPNST stage. For clinicians in neurology, oncology, and radiology, this underscores the value of multidisciplinary tumor boards in managing rare NF1 complications. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for MPNST in NF1 patients with atypical symptoms, advocating for prompt imaging and biopsy to enable timely multidisciplinary management. Follow‐up at 6 months post‐diagnosis revealed stable disease on imaging, with the patient undergoing regular surveillance every 3 months via CT scans and clinical exams to monitor for recurrence or metastasis.

Discussion

Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST), also known as neurofibrosarcomas, neurogenic sarcomas, and malignant schwannomas, comprise approximately 2% of sarcomas. The median age for NF1‐associated MPNST is between 20 and 40 years. They present as enlarging masses in the nerve roots of the extremities and pelvis, particularly in the sciatic nerve. Up to 50% of patients present with metastatic disease at the time of diagnosis [2]. The most frequent sites of metastasis are the lungs, liver, brain, bones, soft tissues, regional lymph nodes, and retroperitoneum [13].

Imaging studies like CT, MRI, and PET scans establish the diagnosis. MRI is the most useful tool in assessing the extent of the tumor for surgical planning. FDG‐PET has been shown to differentiate between benign neurofibromas and MPNST with relatively high specificity [14]. However, a biopsy with histopathological analysis is required for a definitive diagnosis. Histopathology generally shows monotonous spindle cells arranged in intersecting fascicles, hypercellularity, and pleomorphism. There is also an association of the S100 protein and MPNST, but it is not present in all cases [6]. β‐III‐spectrin immunostaining has also shown promising results to be used in the diagnosis of high‐grade MPNST [12].

Recent case reports further illustrate the diverse presentations of NF1‐associated peripheral nerve sheath tumors. For instance, Gaydarski et al. [15] described a giant atypical neurofibroma of the calf in a patient with NF1, highlighting diagnostic challenges due to rapid growth and atypical location, similar to the anterior mediastinal involvement in our case. Such reports emphasize the need for vigilant monitoring and comprehensive imaging in NF1 patients to detect unusual tumor manifestations early. Anterior mediastinal MPNST in NF1 is rarely reported in the literature, with challenges including compression of vital structures leading to respiratory failure and diagnostic overlap with thymic or lymphoproliferative disorders [16]. Recent cases, such as Hayashi et al. describe rapidly growing MPNSTs in NF1 requiring urgent intervention, mirroring our patient's presentation and emphasizing the need for early biopsy to differentiate from benign entities [16]. Management hurdles include surgical accessibility and high recurrence risk, as noted in consensus guidelines [17].

Surgical resection with clear margins is the treatment of choice. Gross total resection and surgical margin status are statistically significant contributors to the overall survival of patients in NF1 [7]. Adjuvant radiotherapy and chemotherapy can be used for metastatic disease and to prevent local recurrence. MPNST generally has a poor prognosis, with a 5‐year survival ranging from 15% to 50%. MPNST associated with NF1 has a poor prognosis as compared to sporadic MPNST [18]. Large tumor size at time of presentation (> 5 cm) and high tumor grade are the worst prognostic factors [9]. Other contributing factors reported include local recurrence, surgical margin status, heterologous rhabdomyoblastic differentiation, and truncal location.

Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST) contribute to 5% of thoracic neoplasms. Though uncommon, they arise in the posterior mediastinum with variable presentations depending on the structures involved [11, 19, 20]. However, they can present rarely in the anterior mediastinum as well, which results in delayed diagnosis and poor outcomes. These tumors can arise from the vagus, recurrent laryngeal, and phrenic nerves and present as hoarseness of voice, dysphagia, palpitations, bradycardia, and dyspnea on exertion [10]. Therefore, strong clinical suspicion of MPNST should be considered in patients presenting with these symptoms who are known cases of NF1.
