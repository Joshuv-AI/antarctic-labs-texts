An experimental study to estimate the early postmortem interval based on the degradation of lncRNAs in rat brain tissue
Authors: Haibo Gao, Siyu Yang, Jie Gao, Siqi Zhang, Li Qin, Meng Huang, Hua Wu, Qun Tang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
To study the degradation of lncRNAs in EPMI in rat brain tissue, this study provides a new direction for the estimation of EPMI. LncRNA high-throughput sequencing was performed on the brain tissues of hemorrhagic shock model rats at 0 h and 24 h, and the target lncRNAs were screened. Samples at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 18 and 24 h after death were collected, and miRNA-9 and miRNA-125b were used as reference genes. The relative expression levels of lncRNAs at each PMI were detected by RT–qPCR, and a functional model involving lncRNAs and EPMI was established. Samples were collected at 6, 9, 15, and 21 h after death for functional model verification. The expression of several lncRNAs decreased with the prolongation of EPMI, and the mathematical model established by several lncRNA indices exhibited good fit. The verification results of the multi-index joint function model are significantly better than those of the single-index function model, and the established model is more practical. There is a linear relationship between lncRNAs and EPMI, and the multi-index function model is significantly better than the single-index function model, which is important for EPMI inference in forensic pathology practice.

Introduction

The concept of time of death refers to the interval that has passed after an individual's death, specifically the duration between the discovery and examination of a deceased body and the actual occurrence of death. This period, commonly referred to as the postmortem interval (PMI), can be further divided into two categories: early postmortem interval (EPMI) and late postmortem interval (LPMI). Ultimately, the decomposition process leads to the transition into a bony skeleton postmortem interval. In forensic practice, the EPMI holds greater significance than the LPMI1. Estimating the EPMI has always posed a challenge in forensic research.

Traditional approaches for estimating the EPMI primarily rely on monitoring early corpses phenomenon changes, which include examining corpse temperature, assessing cadaveric ecchymoses, and observing cadaveric rigidity2. Other methods include evaluating gastric content digestion and bladder urine volume to infer the time of death. However, it is important to acknowledge the influence of various external factors, such as temperature, humidity, and microbial activity, as well as internal factors such as age, sex, and physiological state prior to death. These factors can potentially introduce inaccuracies when estimating the PMI based solely on postmortem phenomena. Therefore, there is a need for more precise methods for estimating the PMI within the field of forensic medicine.

With continuous advancements in science and technology, several methods have emerged as important approaches for estimating the PMI. These include the analysis of the biochemical composition of body fluids, metabolomics, imaging techniques, and nucleic acid degradation patterns.

Notably, changes in chemical composition levels in body fluids such as serum, vitreous humor, aqueous humor, cerebrospinal fluid, and pericardial fluid have been extensively studied for PMI estimation3–5. However, the current research in this field mainly focuses on identifying patterns of variation in relation to PMI, without conducting comprehensive function fitting, testing, and verification. Some studies have shown that the concentration of potassium ions ([K+]) in the vitreous increases nonlinearly with the PMI. However, it is important to consider that this increase is also influenced by factors such as age, environmental temperature, long-term mental stress, and elevated alcohol levels at the time of death6. Additionally, the ability of organs, tissues, and cells to maintain certain functional activities or respond to external stimuli for a short period of time after death is referred to as a supravital reaction. In 2016, Larpkrajang et al. established a regression equation linking the pupil diameter with the estimated PMI based on changes observed in the pupil after death7. However, this equation is only valid within an 8-h time frame and further research is needed to address the limitations related to time windows. In summary, the development of science and technology has provided valuable techniques and methods for estimating the PMI. However, current research primarily focuses on identifying patterns of variation without thoroughly examining functional relationships, conducting rigorous testing, or establishing verification protocols. Future research should explore the complex interplay of factors influencing PMI estimation, including nonlinear changes in potassium ion levels and supravital reactions. Efforts should also be made to address the limitations associated with time windows in equations used for PMI estimation.

In recent years, the utilization of RNA degradation patterns has emerged as a prominent research topic, particularly in the estimation of PMI. A growing body of evidence has demonstrated that the degradation characteristics of RNA can be utilized to estimate the PMI8. Notably, in 1994, Sibile Pardue reported a decrease in the expression of heat shock protein 70 (hsp70) in the cerebellum and cerebral cortex after 24 h compared to 0 h, marking the first indication of messenger RNA (mRNA) degradation in brain tissue following death9. Subsequently, in 2002, Hiroshi Inoue et al. explored the potential of using mRNA as an indicator for PMI estimation and reported a strong linear relationship between glyceraldehyde-3-phase dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and the PMI10. Several other brain tissue indicators, including β-actin11, HAF mRNA12, and RPL3213, have been proposed in subsequent studies, but a universally recognized target RNA has yet to be identified.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a group of noncoding RNAs that exceed 200 nucleotides in length and possess structural similarities to mRNAs. Although the number of investigations of lncRNAs is expanding across diverse fields, their potential utility in estimating the PMI remains unclear. In a study conducted by KRAU et al. in 2015, it was reported that most lncRNAs in human brain tissue undergo downregulation after death, thereby establishing a basis for utilizing lncRNAs in PMI estimation14. Some lncRNAs may demonstrate faster and more sensitive changes in EPMI than mRNAs, making them potential indicators for estimating EPMI15. Nevertheless, further experimental research is required to validate the use of lncRNAs for EPMI estimation.

Hemorrhagic shock-related deaths are frequent in forensic practice16. In this study, we utilized a rat model of hemorrhagic shock death17 and employed high-throughput sequencing techniques to screen and identify potential RNA indicators. Subsequently, we established a functional model and validated its effectiveness by calculating the error rate. By exploring the degradation patterns of lncRNAs in brain tissue, we aimed to identify specific lncRNA indicators for EPMI. These findings have important implications for advancing the field of forensic PMI estimation.

To validate the effectiveness of these function models, we calculated the time difference, error rate, average time difference, and average error rate see S3. The outcomes of the validation demonstrate that both the average time difference and average error rate of the function models are notably superior to those of the univariate linear function model. Among the various functions considered, one particular function was found to yield the smallest average time difference. This function can be described as y = -5.8139a + 3.3551 g + 3.3241 s + 17.1035, with a resulting average time difference of 1.86 h. Notably, this value is significantly lower than the average time difference of 3.17 h obtained from a univariate linear function. Furthermore, a function yielding the lowest average error rate was identified as y = -3.621a + 2.279 m + 2.858 g + 15.632, which resulted in an average error rate of 20.50%. This value is significantly lower than the 29.40% average error rate obtained from a univariate linear function. Importantly, all of these findings demonstrate a clear superiority of the investigated functions over univariate linear function models. It is essential to acknowledge that, similar to univariate linear function models, an increase in the R2 value of the function model does not necessarily correspond to a decrease in the average time difference or average error rate.

In summary, our findings highlight the superiority of the seven multivariate linear function models over the single indicator function model. The validation results obtained for these function models showcase a remarkable improvement in both the average time difference and average error rate compared to the univariate linear function model. It is worth noting that a high R2 value does not guarantee the lowest error rate in estimating the time of death.

Discussion

The estimation of EPMI is of great importance in forensic investigations and plays a crucial role in forensic appraisal. In recent years, RNA analysis has gained increasing prominence in the study of postmortem interval estimation, as the degradation patterns of RNA can be used to estimate the time since death. Among the various RNA types, mRNAs have received the most extensive research attention19–21.rRNA is another type of RNA commonly used in the estimation of PMI. However, most studies have shown that rRNA undergoes minimal changes within EPMI, making it more stable than mRNA. Consequently, it is not suitable for estimating EPMI22,23. MicroRNAs have predominantly been studied as internal reference markers. Due to their unique structure and length, miRNAs can remain stable without significant degradation for a certain period after death24–28. Research on circular RNA (circRNA) is still in its early stages. Some researchers have suggested that circ-AFF1 can serve as an internal reference index for skeletal muscle and liver tissues26,27. Others have proposed that certain circRNAs exhibit regular and stable changes in degradation, allowing for the estimation of the time since death based on their degradation rate29. The highest rate of degradation typically occurs within 24 h29,30.

In conclusion, RNA analysis, particularly mRNA analysis, shows promise in improving the estimation of EPMI. MiRNAs , rRNA and circRNAs also hold potential as internal reference markers. Further research is needed to enhance the accuracy and reliability of these methods.

The use of lncRNAs for estimating the PMI is still in its early stages of development. In 2022, Mona M. Ali et al. reported a significant negative correlation between the expression of lncFAO RNA and PMI in rat skin tissue 24 h after death, suggesting that it may be a reliable indicator for PMI estimation31. This findings provide strong evidence for the potential use of lncRNAs in determining the time of death.

In our study, we aimed to estimate EPMI using lncRNAs extracted from rat brain tissue. We performed high-throughput sequencing and fitted a functional model based on the obtained data. Subsequently, we validated the function model and calculated the time difference and error rate. Our experimental results demonstrated that lncRNAs can indeed be used for estimating EPMI. The high-throughput sequencing results revealed that certain lncRNAs exhibited degradation 24 h after death, which aligns with our expectations. Furthermore, the RT‒qPCR results indicated that the Cq values of some indicators increased gradually with increasing PMI, and this trend became more pronounced over time. Among the nine univariate linear function models fitted, four exhibited statistical significance (P < 0.05). Additionally, we applied seven multivariate linear function models, which demonstrated significantly improved fitting results compared to the univariate linear function model. The multivariate linear function models exhibited a significantly reduced error rate, consistent with the findings of Wang et al.1. Interestingly, we observed no significant relationship between the R2 value of the function and the time difference or error rate of the validation results.

In conclusion, our study provides promising evidence for the use of lncRNAs in estimating EPMI. We demonstrated that certain lncRNAs exhibit degradation after death, and their expression levels can be used as indicators for estimating the PMI. The multivariate linear function models yielded better fitting results and reduced error rates compared to the univariate linear function models. These findings highlight the potential benefits of utilizing multivariate functions in PMI estimation.

Another important concern in using RNA for the estimation of PMI is the selection of internal reference genes. Traditional reference genes such as GAPDH and β-actin are not stable enough after death10, and most existing reference biomarkers are miRNAs. Studies conducted on brain tissue have identified miRNAs, particularly miR-9 and miR-125b, as stable internal reference markers for PMI estimation in postmortem rat and human brain tissues12,24. Therefore, in this study, we utilized miRNA-9 and miRNA-125b as internal parameters. Currently, the primary indicator used for PMI estimation is mRNA, but its use is not consistent with common reverse transcription and RT‒qPCR reagents. Consequently, existing research uses separate systems for mRNA and miRNA analysis, which introduces instability in the influencing factors. Thus, the identification of new internal reference biomarkers is crucial for improving the application of PMI estimation in forensic practice.

However, this study has several limitations. First, it has been shown that the combined use of multiple tissues provides more accurate PMI estimation19. In this study, we focused only on lncRNAs in brain tissue. In the future, we intend to investigate the use of multiple tissue-specific lncRNAs to estimate the EPMI and create joint models. Second, our research was conducted under a single temperature condition, which may limit its applicability in forensic practice. Further studies could explore the effects of multiple temperatures on PMI estimation. Finally, due to limitations in human tissue sources and ethical considerations, the lncRNAs used in this study were measured in rats. However, future research plans include conducting experiments on human tissue samples.

The application of lncRNA in forensic PMI inference practice requires the development of more convenient and efficient methods. In this regard, point-of-care testing (POCT) of nucleic acids technology is expected to play a vital role. POCT enables the timely and efficient detection of specific sequences without the need for complicated laboratory instruments. This technology offers numerous benefits, including portability, ease of operation, high sensitivity, high specificity, and rapid detection32. As a result, POCT has been successfully employed in the field of biomedical diagnosis and has contributed significantly to disease diagnosis, treatment monitoring, prevention of recurrence, and mitigation of serious infectious disease threats33. This study also presents a promising avenue for utilizing RNA-based techniques in inferring PMI. Additionally, it showcases novel methodologies and approaches for estimating the time of death in forensic investigations. Moreover, it provides valuable insights that will guide our future research endeavors in this field.

Overall, by addressing these limitations and conducting further research, we aim to enhance the accuracy and utility of PMI estimation in forensic practice.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals and grouping

A total of 54 specific pathogen-free (SPF) grade Sprague–Dawley (SD) male rats, aged 7–8 weeks and weighing 200–250 g, were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (LL2023041202).

The rats were divided into three groups: high-throughput sequencing, functional model establishment, and model validation. The high-throughput sequencing group consisted of two subgroups: 0-h and 24-h time points, with two rats in each subgroup, resulting in a total of four rats. The functional model was established at various time points—0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h—with six rats in each time point group, for a total of 42 rats. The model validation group included four time point subgroups: subgroups—6, 9, 15, and 21 h—with two rats in each subgroup, for a total of eight rats.

Model preparation and sampling

After one week of adaptive feeding, the rats were weighed, and anaesthesia was induced via intraperitoneal injection of 0.1 mL of 3% pentobarbital sodium per 100 g body weight. To establish a rat model of hemorrhagic shock death, we followed the operating procedure described in the literature17. Following the modelling, the collected samples were maintained in an environment with a temperature of 25 ± 3 °C and a humidity of 50 ± 10%. At 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 h after death, the cerebral cortex was carefully excised and the tissue samples were immediately placed in EP tubes containing RNA Later reagent (Solarbio, China) and kept at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the samples were transferred to a -80 °C freezer for further experimental procedures.

High-throughput sequencing and indicator screening

After total RNA extraction, ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was depleted and the remaining RNA was fragmented to construct a strand-specific sequencing library. The library underwent quality inspection and was sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform, generating paired-end reads of 2*150 base pairs. The raw data obtained from sequencing were subjected to filtering using Cutadapt to obtain clean data by discarding unqualified sequences. Annotation information in species reference databases was statistically analysed. Furthermore, the clean data was aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene location information were analysed. Based on the HISAT2 comparison results, transcripts were reconstructed using Stringtie, and the expression levels of all genes in each sample were quantified. The gene expression levels were primarily measured using fragments per kilobase million (FPKM) values. Subsequently, data analysis was performed on the gene expression levels. Gene expression differences were identified using a volcano plot, which visually depicts the number of significantly upregulated and downregulated genes. A positive correlation was observed between lncRNA concentrations measured by FPKM. Among the significantly downregulated genes, any lncRNAs with significant differences within the same group were excluded. The remaining lncRNAs were ranked based on large intergroup differences and small intragroup differences, and the top lncRNAs were selected.

RNA extraction

RNA was extracted from cerebral cortex using the RNAsimple Total RNA Kit (Tianjin, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 20–100 mg of cerebral cortex tissue was added to a 1.5 mL enzyme-free Eppendorf (Ep) tube. The tissue samples were cut into small pieces, and 1 mL of RZ decomposition solution was added. Grinding beads were also added, and the samples were thoroughly ground under low-temperature conditions. Following grinding, the mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 5 min. Then, 200 μL of chloroform was added, and the mixture was vigorously shaken for 15 s. After 3 min of standing at room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant (400 μL) was carefully transferred to a new 1.5 mL enzyme-free Ep tube. Next, 200 μL of anhydrous ethanol was added, and the mixture was thoroughly mixed before being transferred to an adsorption column (CR3). The column and collection tube were then centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 30 s, and the liquid in the collection tube was discarded. Subsequently, 500 μL of deproteinized RD solution was added to the adsorption column CR3, which was centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 30 s. The liquid in the collection tube was discarded. The adsorption column was further treated with 500 μL of rinsing solution RW, allowed to stand at room temperature for 2 min, and then centrifuged at 4 °C and 12,000 rpm for 30 s. This step was repeated once to ensure thorough rinsing. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 2 min, any remaining liquid was discarded, and the adsorption column CR3 was opened to air-dry completely at room temperature. The dry adsorption column CR3 was then transferred to a new 1.5 mL enzyme-free Ep tube, and 50 μL of enzyme-free water was added. The mixture was allowed to stand for 2 min before being centrifuged at 4 °C for 2 min at 12,000 rpm. The adsorption column was discarded, leaving the extracted RNA solution at the bottom of the enzyme-free Ep tube.

RNA concentration and quality testing

The extracted RNA solution was subjected to analysis in order to determine its concentration and quality. This was carried out using a NanoDrop One Ultramicro UV visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Prior to analysis, the spectrophotometer was calibrated using 2 μL of enzyme-free water. Subsequently, 2 μL of the RNA sample was evaluated. The A260:280 ratio of the RNA fell within the range of 1.8 to 2.2, indicating high purity. The RNA was subsequently subjected to reverse transcription experiments.

Reverse transcription

Reverse transcription was performed using the Mastercycler nexus GX2 (Eppendorf, Germany) instrument. For the reverse transcription of lncRNAs, the NovoScript® Plus All-in-one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (gDNA Purge) kit (Novoprotein, China) was used. Following the kit instructions, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA for the lncRNAs. The reverse transcription protocol consisted of incubating at 50 °C for 15 min, followed by a quick denaturation step at 85 °C for 5 s.

To reverse transcribe the microRNAs (miRNAs), the NovoScript® miRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis and SYBR qPCR Kit (Novoprotein, China) was used. According to the kit instructions, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA for the miRNAs. The reverse transcription protocol consisted of incubation at 39 °C for 60 min, followed by a final denaturation step at 85 °C for 5 min.

The resulting cDNA from the reverse transcription reactions was diluted by a factor of 10, and stored at − 80 °C for further use in RT‒qPCR experiments.

RT–qPCR

RT‒qPCR was performed using a CFX Connect instrument (Bio-Rad, USA) to analyse the expression levels of lncRNAs and miRNAs. The lncRNA primers were designed based on BLAST searches conducted at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The primers for miRNA-9 and miRNA-125b were obtained from previous studies34 and are listed in Table 1. All primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

For lncRNA analysis, a NovoScript® SYBR High Sensitivity qPCR SuperMix (Novoprotein, China) kit was used. A 20 μL reaction system was prepared, including 3 replicates for each sample. Each well contained 1 μL of cDNA, 0.4 μL of 10 μM forward primer, 0.4 μL of 10 μM reverse primer, 10 μL of 2 × NovoScript® SYBR High Sensitivity qPCR SuperMix Solution, and 8.2 μL of RNase-free water.

To analyse miRNA expression, a NovoSplit® MiRNA First Strand cDNA Synthesis and SYBR qPCR Kit (Novoprotein, China) were used. Similarly, a 20 μL reaction system was prepared, including 3 replicates for each sample. Each well contained 1 μL of cDNA, 0.4 μL of 10 μM forward primer, 0.4 μL of Universal miRNA primer R, 10 μL of 2 × miRNA RT Reaction Mix solution, and 8.2 μL of RNase-free water.

The following RT‒qPCR conditions were used: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, 40 cycles of denaturation and annealing, and melting curve analysis.

Data analysis

This study focuses on determining the validity of the data obtained from complex holes by calculating the difference in Cq values. It is essential that the difference in Cq values between three complex holes is less than 1 to ensure that the data are reliable. The average Cq value is then used as valid data.

To calculate the ΔCq value, the formula used was Δ Cq = Cq (target gene)–Cq (average value of internal reference genes). The average values of the internal reference genes were obtained by calculating the mean Cq values for miRNA-9 and miRNA-125b.

To establish a mathematical model, R4.2.3 was employed, utilizing the average ΔCq value. The quality of the model was evaluated using adjusted R2 and P values. A higher adjusted R2 value close to 1 indicates that the model effectively explains the variance in the response. A P-value less than 0.05 implies that the assumption of a zero coefficient is not supported. GraphPad Prism 8 was utilized to construct a functional model diagram.

In the validation experiment, the function model is used to calculate the validation PMI by substituting ΔCq values. If the calculated validation PMI is negative, the corresponding data are discarded. The time difference and error rate for each sample were then calculated. Subsequently, the average time difference and average error rate of the corresponding PMI for each function model were obtained. Finally, the average time difference and average error rate for each function model were calculated. The formula is as follows:

Time difference =|Verified PMI–Actual PMI|;

Error rate = Time difference/Actual PMI.
