Integrating RV–PA coupling and geometry-based indices into the TRI-SCORE improves prognostic assessment in tricuspid regurgitation
Authors: Raffaella Mistrulli, Monika Beles, Elayne Kelen De Oliveira, Rosie Vrenozaj, Sara Gharehdaghi, Emanuele Barbato, Pasquale Paolisso, Jerrold Spapen, Martin Penicka, Marc Vanderheyden, Liesbeth Rosseel, Frank V Praet, Hadewich Hermans, Guy Van Camp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTricuspid regurgitation (TR) is a progressive and underdiagnosed condition associated with poor prognosis. Although the TRI-SCORE is a validated risk model for patients undergoing tricuspid valve surgery, it does not incorporate echocardiographic measures of right ventricular (RV) function or RV–pulmonary artery coupling, which are increasingly recognised as major prognostic determinants.ObjectivesTo evaluate whether integration of simple, widely available echocardiographic indices—tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE)/pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP), TAPSE/RV end-diastolic area (RVAD) and TAPSE/RV end-systolic area (RVAS)—improves the prognostic performance of the TRI-SCORE in patients with moderate-to-severe TR.MethodsWe retrospectively included 93 patients with severe TR, including functional, mixed and primary/Cardiac Implantable Electronic Device (CIED)-related aetiologies treated with medical therapy, surgery or transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality; the secondary endpoint was a composite of mortality or heart failure hospitalisation. Multivariable Cox regression models were used to assess the incremental predictive value of each TAPSE-derived ratio beyond the TRI-SCORE. Optimal thresholds were derived using spline regression and maximally selected rank statistics.ResultsLower TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS values were independently associated with worse outcomes. Each parameter significantly enhanced risk discrimination when added to the TRI-SCORE (C-index improved from 0.614 to 0.710 for TAPSE/PASP, 0.685 for TAPSE/RVAD and 0.696 for TAPSE/RVAS; all p<0.001). Prognostically relevant cut-offs were identified at 0.44, 0.80 and 1.3, respectively.ConclusionsSimple echocardiographic indices of RV function provide substantial incremental prognostic value when combined with the TRI-SCORE and may improve risk stratification and timing of intervention in isolated TR.Keywords: Echocardiography, Tricuspid Valve Insufficiency, Heart Valve Prosthesis

Introduction

Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is a frequent and increasingly recognised valvular disorder, associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, particularly when left untreated.1 Despite its clinical impact, isolated severe TR remains largely undertreated: surgical correction is seldom performed, and most patients are managed conservatively with diuretics, often due to the perceived benignity of the condition, the high perioperative risk, and the uncertain long-term benefit of intervention.2 Recent guidelines from major cardiology societies recommend tricuspid valve surgery in symptomatic patients or in those with significant right ventricular (RV) dilatation in the absence of severe RV dysfunction and severe pulmonary hypertension.3 However, these recommendations are based on limited evidence, and the optimal timing and modality of intervention remain debated. The advent of transcatheter techniques has broadened therapeutic options, offering a less invasive alternative to surgery.4 Nonetheless, trials such as TRILUMINATE5 have raised concerns by showing no significant impact on mortality or heart failure (HF) hospitalisation at 1 year compared with medical therapy alone. A critical challenge in managing TR lies in its insidious progression. Delayed intervention, once RV remodelling or end-organ dysfunction (eg, hepatic or renal impairment) is established, is associated with poor outcomes, including a reported in-hospital mortality of 10% following isolated tricuspid surgery.6 7 Thus, accurate risk stratification is essential to guide clinical decision-making and to select patients who may benefit from earlier and more aggressive therapy. To address this, the TRI-SCORE was developed and validated as a prognostic tool integrating eight clinical, biochemical and echocardiographic variables to predict in-hospital mortality after isolated tricuspid valve surgery. By staging patients across a spectrum of risk, the TRI-SCORE has demonstrated utility in identifying those who derive the most benefit from surgical or transcatheter intervention versus conservative management.8 9 However, while the TRI-SCORE incorporates basic indices of RV function, it does not account for more advanced or geometry-sensitive echocardiographic measures. Conventional parameters such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), though widely used, primarily reflect longitudinal contraction and may underestimate dysfunction in dilated or pressure-overloaded RVs, where transverse motion becomes increasingly relevant. In this context, indexed parameters such as TAPSE/RV end-diastolic area (RVAD) and TAPSE/RV end-systolic area (RVAS) have been proposed to better account for RV size and remodelling.10 Similarly, the TAPSE/pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP) ratio has emerged as a reliable marker of RV–pulmonary artery coupling, correlating with clinical outcomes in various cardiac conditions.11 Given the prognostic implications of RV dysfunction and the limitations of current risk models, the present study aimed to explore whether the inclusion of advanced echocardiographic markers of RV performance—namely TAPSE/RVAD, TAPSE/RVAS and TAPSE/PASP—could enhance the predictive accuracy of the TRI-SCORE in patients with moderate to severe functional TR. By refining risk stratification, this approach may help identify patients who could benefit from timely and targeted intervention.

Methods

Study population

We conducted a retrospective observational study including consecutive patients with moderate-to-severe isolated TR who underwent clinical evaluation and transthoracic echocardiography between 2010 and June 2024 at our institution. Patients were included if they received medical therapy, surgical intervention or percutaneous edge-to-edge repair (TriClip or Pascal). Patients were excluded if they had RV outflow tract or pulmonary valve obstruction, inadequate image quality precluding quantitative assessment of RV function, or an insufficient TR Doppler signal for reliable estimation of PASP. Patients who declined participation in retrospective research were also excluded. Clinical history, baseline characteristics, vital signs, laboratory values and outcomes (including mortality and hospital readmissions) were collected from electronic medical records. Baseline laboratory tests were considered valid if obtained within 12 months prior to the index echocardiogram.

Echocardiographic assessment

All transthoracic echocardiographic examinations were performed with patients in the left lateral decubitus position using either a Vivid S70 or E95 ultrasound system (General Electric Vingmed Ultrasound, Milwaukee, WI, USA) or a Philips EPIQ 7 system (Philips Healthcare, Andover, MA, USA). Data were acquired using 3.5 MHz or M5S transducers and digitally stored in cine-loop format. Offline analysis was performed using EchoPAC software (V.204, GE Vingmed Ultrasound) or IntelliSpace CardioVascular (Philips). TR severity was graded according to a multiparametric approach, in line with current American and European guidelines.12 13 When clinically indicated, transoesophageal echocardiography was performed to provide a more accurate assessment of TR severity and leaflet morphology. A dedicated apical RV-focused view was used for RV functional analysis. TAPSE was measured in M-mode at the lateral tricuspid annulus. Peak systolic myocardial velocity (RV S′) at the lateral tricuspid annulus was assessed using pulsed-wave tissue Doppler imaging. RVDA and RVSA were measured to calculate fractional area change (FAC) as [(RVDA−RVSA)/RVDA]×100. Global longitudinal strain (GLS) of the RV (RV GLS) and free-wall longitudinal strain of the RV (RVFWLS) were obtained using speckle-tracking echocardiography in apical views optimised for each ventricle. PASP was estimated from the peak velocity of the TR jet using the modified Bernoulli equation, with estimated right atrial pressure based on inferior vena cava diameter and collapsibility. In addition to conventional parameters, we calculated the ratios of TAPSE to PASP (TAPSE/PASP), TAPSE to RVAD (TAPSE/RVAD) and TAPSE to RVAS (TAPSE/RVAS) as surrogates of RV–pulmonary arterial coupling and size-adjusted RV systolic function.

TRI-SCORE calculation

The TRI-SCORE is a risk model recently proposed and derived from a large cohort of patients undergoing isolated tricuspid valve surgery. It includes eight items: age ≥70 years (1 point), New York Heart Association functional class III to IV (1 point), right-sided HF signs (2 points), daily dose of furosemide ≥125 mg (2 points), glomerular filtration rate <30 mL/min (2 points), elevated total bilirubin (2 points), left ventricular ejection fraction <60% (1 point) and moderate/severe RV dysfunction (1 point).5 6 For the purpose of the present study, patients with complete data for TRI-SCORE calculation were included. The TRI-SCORE was computed at baseline (prior to intervention or during conservative management) (online supplemental figure 1).

Study endpoints

The aim of the study was to investigate whether the integration of advanced echocardiographic indices of RV performance could improve the prognostic accuracy of the TRI-SCORE in patients with moderate to severe TR. Accordingly, we assessed the predictive performance of the TRI-SCORE in combination with each of these advanced echocardiographic parameters for:

All-cause mortality (primary clinical outcome).

All-cause mortality (primary clinical outcome).

A composite outcome including all-cause mortality and HF hospitalisation.

A composite outcome including all-cause mortality and HF hospitalisation.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised as median (IQR) for continuous variables and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann-Whitney U test) for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables.

Univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate the association of clinical and echocardiographic parameters with outcomes. Variables with p<0.05 in univariable analysis were considered for inclusion in multivariable Cox models. Prior to model inclusion, correlation between the TRI-SCORE and each TAPSE-derived ratio was assessed to exclude multicollinearity.

Multivariable Cox models were built to test the incremental prognostic value of TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS beyond the TRI-SCORE. Model performance was compared using the concordance index (C-index), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and likelihood ratio test.

To identify optimal cut-off values for TAPSE/PASP and TAPSE/RVAD, restricted cubic spline curves were generated by plotting the log10-transformed variable against HRs from univariate Cox models. Variables were then dichotomised based on the point where HR exceeded 1. Corresponding Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for illustrative purposes.

All analyses were performed using R software (V.4.3.1), with statistical significance set at a two-sided p<0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 104 consecutive patients with significant TR were initially screened. 11 patients were excluded because of incomplete echocardiographic datasets precluding the calculation of one or more study variables, leaving 93 patients for the final analysis. The study cohort included patients managed conservatively, surgically or with percutaneous edge-to-edge repair. The median age was 78 (72–83) years, and 39.8% were male. Functional TR was the predominant aetiology (78.2%), followed by mixed (14.0%) and primary/Cardiac Implantable Electronic Device (CIED)-related TR (12.9%). Among patients with functional TR, 16.2% were classified as atrial type and 83.8% as ventricular type. Regarding treatment strategy, 33.3% received medical therapy alone, 31.2% underwent surgery and 35.5% were treated with transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. Over a median follow-up of 42.2 months (20.5–67.0), 33 patients (35.5%) died and 36 (38.7%) experienced the composite endpoint of all-cause mortality or HF hospitalisation.

Clinical characteristics according to outcomes

In unadjusted comparisons, non-survivors were older (80.0 (75.0–84.0) vs 76.0 (70.8–82.3) years, p=0.048) and had worse renal function (41.0 (26.0–50.0) vs 50.5 (40.0–65.8) mL/min/1.73 m², p=0.003) compared with survivors. Baseline TRI-SCORE values were significantly higher among patients who died (4.0 (3.0–6.0) vs 2.0 (1.0–4.0), p=0.001). Similarly, patients experiencing the composite endpoint had lower renal function and higher TRI-SCORE values compared with those free from events. The distribution of TR mechanisms and treatment strategies was similar across outcome groups (table 1).

The independent prognostic significance of these variables was subsequently assessed using Cox regression analyses. In univariate Cox models, all TAPSE-derived ratios were significantly associated with all-cause mortality, with HRs of 0.006 (95% CI 0.0004 to 0.106) for TAPSE/PASP, 0.087 (95% CI 0.022 to 0.341) for TAPSE/RVAD and 0.223 (95% CI 0.093 to 0.537) for TAPSE/RVAS (all p<0.001) (table 3). The best-performing cut-offs were 0.44 for TAPSE/PASP, 0.80 for TAPSE/RVAD and 1.30 for TAPSE/RVAS, which were used for dichotomised exploratory stratification (figure 1). Other echocardiographic parameters, including FAC, RVFWLS, RVGLS and RVFWLS/PASP, were not significantly associated with outcomes. Similarly, each one-point increase in the baseline TRI-SCORE was associated with a 22% higher risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.40, p=0.005; concordance index 0.608).

Model comparison and variable selection

To assess the incremental prognostic value of each index beyond the TRI-SCORE, we tested multivariable Cox models combining the TRI-SCORE with each parameter. Before inclusion in the multivariable Cox models, potential collinearity between TRI-SCORE and TAPSE-derived parameters was assessed. Correlation coefficients were low (r=–0.15 for TAPSE/PASP; r=–0.17 for TAPSE/RVAD; r=–0.19 for TAPSE/RVAS), indicating minimal redundancy and allowing for joint modelling (online supplemental figure 2). The addition of TAPSE/PASP improved the model’s discrimination, increasing the concordance index from 0.614 to 0.710 and reducing AIC from 239.5 to 226.8 (p<0.001). Similarly, the TRI-SCORE+TAPSE/RVAD model showed improved performance (C-index 0.685, AIC 224.7, p<0.001). The model incorporating TAPSE/RVAS also significantly enhanced predictive accuracy (C-index 0.696, AIC 226.4, p<0.001), supporting its prognostic utility. Direct comparison between the best-performing models confirmed that TRI-SCORE+TAPSE/PASP and TRI-SCORE+TAPSE/RVAD offered similar predictive accuracy (C-index 0.710 vs 0.685; AIC 226.8 vs 224.7), while TRI-SCORE+TAPSE/RVAS performed comparably (C-index 0.696; AIC 226.4) (table 4). The incremental predictive contribution of these indices beyond the baseline TRI-SCORE is illustrated in online supplemental figure 3. Importantly, these findings were consistent for both all-cause mortality and the composite endpoint. For survival analyses, patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups according to the median of the predicted risk scores derived from the Cox models: the high-risk group included individuals with a predicted log hazard (linear predictor) above the median, while the low-risk group comprised those at or below the median (figure 2). Additionally, model-predicted survival was visualised across representative TRI-SCORE values (2, 5 and 8), stratified by TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS tertiles. As shown in figure 3, lower TAPSE-derived ratio values were consistently associated with worse survival, regardless of baseline clinical risk.

Discussion

In our study with severe isolated TR undergoing surgical, percutaneous or medical management, we demonstrated that advanced echocardiographic indices of RV function—TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS—are powerful predictors of both all-cause mortality and a composite endpoint of death or HF hospitalisation. When integrated into the established TRI-SCORE, these indices significantly enhanced the model’s prognostic accuracy, offering a refined stratification of patient risk.

Our findings confirm the clinical utility of the TRI-SCORE as a predictor of in-hospital and mid-term outcomes in patients with severe TR. Although TRI-SCORE was originally developed to predict in-hospital mortality after isolated tricuspid valve surgery, subsequent studies have demonstrated its prognostic value across transcatheter tricuspid interventions, supporting its broader role as a marker of disease severity and clinical risk.14 However, we also show that the incorporation of geometry-adjusted or coupling-sensitive RV indices enhances its prognostic accuracy. This suggests that a more refined characterisation of RV performance may be essential for improving risk stratification in this complex population.

Notably, while TAPSE is already included as a dichotomous marker of moderate-to-severe dysfunction in the original TRI-SCORE, our data highlight that quantitative, ratio-based measures such as TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS capture prognostically relevant nuances that may be overlooked by conventional parameters. In multivariable analysis, all three indices retained independent prognostic value, with comparable improvements in model discrimination (C-index 0.710 for TAPSE/PASP, 0.696 for TAPSE/RVAD and 0.696 for TAPSE/RVAS).

These results align with the broader literature highlighting the importance of RV–PA coupling in HF and valvular heart disease. TAPSE/PASP, a non-invasive surrogate of RV–PA coupling, reflects the ability of the RV to adapt contractility in response to afterload and has shown consistent prognostic value across a wide spectrum of cardiovascular conditions, including pulmonary hypertension, HFrEF, HFpEF and mitral regurgitation.15,17 The physiological rationale for TAPSE/PASP is compelling. As RV pressure or volume overload progresses, uncoupling of RV contractility and afterload occurs, impairing RV efficiency and ultimately leading to failure.

In TR, where both pressure and volume overload may coexist, TAPSE/PASP appears to serve as a final common indicator of maladaptive RV remodelling. 18

Furthermore, TAPSE/RVAD although less studied than TAPSE/PASP, may provide complementary information by incorporating RV chamber size, thereby accounting for both longitudinal contractility and geometric remodelling. This is particularly relevant in TR, where RV dilatation is often prominent due to chronic volume overload. Indeed, our results suggest that TAPSE/RVAD performed similarly to TAPSE/PASP, offering a viable alternative in cases where PASP estimation is unreliable, which is often the case in severe TR.

Importantly, our findings provide a pragmatic alternative to more technically demanding echocardiographic techniques. A previous study proposed the TRIO-RV score, which incorporates RV free-wall strain (RVFWS) and RVFWS/PASP into the TRIO risk model, demonstrating improved prognostic stratification.19 While these findings are conceptually aligned with ours, strain-based parameters require high-quality image acquisition and accurate endocardial border tracking, which may be challenging in elderly patients with advanced TR and suboptimal acoustic windows.20 This may partly explain why RV strain parameters were not significantly associated with outcomes in our cohort, particularly given the relatively small sample size and limited number of events. In contrast, TAPSE, PASP and RV dimensions are routinely obtainable during standard echocardiographic examinations, making indices such as TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS more feasible, reproducible and readily applicable in everyday clinical practice. The updated 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines on the Management of Valvular Heart Disease emphasise the need for early intervention in TR, ideally before irreversible RV remodelling or end-organ dysfunction develops. These guidelines call for a comprehensive evaluation of RV size and function when assessing candidacy for tricuspid valve procedures.3 However, in routine clinical practice, RV function is often assessed only qualitatively or semi-quantitatively, limiting the ability to detect early dysfunction.21

Risk stratification in TR is particularly challenging due to the heterogeneity of the disease and the frequent presence of confounding factors such as age-related deconditioning and multimorbidity. In the present cohort, functional TR represented the predominant mechanism (78.2%), whereas mixed and primary/CIED-related forms accounted for a relatively small proportion of patients. Although no significant differences in TR aetiology were observed between patients with and without adverse outcomes, the limited number of non-functional cases precluded meaningful subgroup analyses. Future studies with larger cohorts will be needed to determine whether the prognostic performance of TAPSE-derived indices varies according to the underlying TR mechanism. In this context, a structured approach that combines a validated clinical risk score, such as the TRI-SCORE, with quantitative, physiology-based echocardiographic indices may enhance diagnostic clarity and guide patient-centred decision-making.22 Recent evidence from the TRILUMINATE trial (extended follow-up) and the TRI-FR study demonstrated that transcatheter edge-to-edge repair leads to sustained reductions in TR severity and improvements in symptoms and quality of life. Notably, clinical benefit was most evident in patients with more preserved RV function, underscoring the importance of timely referral and intervention.23 24

Given that TR often progresses silently and reaches advanced stages before clinical recognition, the integration of advanced echocardiographic indices of RV function—such as coupling-sensitive or geometry-adjusted parameters—into routine evaluation may enhance risk stratification.25 When used alongside established tools like the TRI-SCORE, these indices may facilitate earlier identification of high-risk patients and support more tailored therapeutic strategies.

Limitations

This was a single-centre, retrospective cohort with a modest sample size, which limits generalisability and precludes causal inference. Management strategies were heterogeneous, including medical therapy, surgery and transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, and residual confounding by indication cannot be excluded. Because patients receiving different treatment modalities were analysed together, a potential influence of treatment strategy on outcomes cannot be ruled out. However, treatment-specific subgroup analyses were not feasible owing to the limited sample size and the relatively small number of events within each treatment group. Furthermore, the cut-off values identified for TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Prospective multicentre studies with external validation are needed to confirm these thresholds, validate the incremental prognostic value of the proposed integrated models, and further investigate their performance across different treatment strategies.

Conclusion

In patients with moderate-to-severe isolated TR, simple and widely obtainable measures of right-ventricular performance—TAPSE/PASP, TAPSE/RVAD and TAPSE/RVAS—added substantial prognostic information beyond the TRI-SCORE. Incorporating these indices improved model discrimination for all-cause mortality and a composite of death or heart-failure hospitalisation, and yielded practical clinical thresholds (TAPSE/PASP≈0.44, TAPSE/RVAD≈0.80, TAPSE/RVAS≈1.30) for risk stratification. These data support a pragmatic, echocardiography-first approach that pairs a validated clinical score with quantitative RV indices to identify high-risk patients earlier and to guide timely referral for intervention.
