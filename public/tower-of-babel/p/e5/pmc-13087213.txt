Identifying time-lag effects of temperature and precipitation on vegetation growth variation in the lower Yellow River of east China
Authors: Xinying Lu, Yan Xiao, Yifang Duan, Nianci Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Addressing the backdrop of global climatic changes and focusing on how vegetation reacts to these shifts,this article acting a imperative significant.This article investigates the administrative jurisdictions in the eastern of lower of the Yellow River.It selects MODIS NDVI remote sensing data spanning from 2001 to 2021 and employs different analysis methods including simple correlation, partial correlation, and multiple regression to quantitatively assess the response of NDVI during the vegetation growth season to climate variations, analyzing it on a grid by grid basis within the research region. Studies have found that: (1) the NDVI’s responsiveness to temperature and precipitation during the peak growth season exhibited a certain delay; A temperature response lag showed one or two months, while a precipitation response lag showed one or three months.(2) Partial correlation analysis demonstrated that NDVI was negatively correlated with temperature, while NDVI was positively correlated precipitation. Moreover, during the growing season, NDVI across various vegetation types revealed a stronger association with precipitation compared to temperature.(3) The cultivated vegetation, coniferous forest, and swamp vegetation exhibit significant responses to climatic changes.(4) When accounting for time-lag effects, it has increased by 128.98% for the capacity of climate variables to interpret vegetation dynamics compared to simultaneous assessments.

Introduction

Vegetation indicates a considerable sensitivity to changes in climate and serves as a central component of terrestrial ecosystems1–3. Investigating the interaction among vegetation with climatic factors has emerged as a vital field of research, particularly amid global climatic changes, characterized by warming and rising humidity. Recent studies indicate that the reaction of vegetation to climatic changes involve significant spatio-temporal shifts, with phenological traits closely connected to these climatic changes. Various climate factors act the considerable role in the variations observed in vegetation growth, with particular emphasis placed on temperature and precipitation, which have garnered extensive attention. Although numerous research outcomes from diverse regions reveal that the trends in vegetation growth and their acts to climatic factors vary across both temporal and spatial dimensions4, the relationships with temperature and precipitation exhibit varying degrees of positive and negative correlations. Additionally, it has been established as a critical factor influencing vegetation alterations5. Furthermore, as the research progresses, factors such as sunshine duration6,7, relative humidity8,9 and evapotranspiration10,11 are increasingly recognized in studies concerning how vegetation responds to climate change. A significant amount of paper has shifted the focus on the simultaneous reactions of vegetation to climatic changes, with many studies also addressing the time-lag effects in vegetation growth relative to climatic changes12,13. The duration of vegetation’s response to diverse climate elements varies, and the response patterns among various vegetation types to climate conditions are inconsistent14,15. Numerous findings across various regions suggest that incorporating the hysteresis effect enhances the capacity of climate factors to explain vegetation growth16,17 The exploration of the lag in vegetation responses to climate change has become a significant focus of studies, particularly concerning a pressing issues of global climate change impact on vegetation.

As the advancement of satellite remote sensing technology, products like (NDVI) NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) time series data derived from common sensors such as MODIS, SPOT, and AVHRR have yielded significant insights into the spatio-temporal dynamics of vegetation across multiple scales. Among these products, MODIS NDVI is notably effective for capturing vegetation states and processes owing to its suitable accuracy and broad dynamic range. Research into vegetation distribution and phenological responses of vegetation to global climate change has been widely applied across differing spatial scales, including intercontinental, regional, and watershed levels18,19. To ensure thorough and refined analysis outcomes, NDVI data alongside meteorological product data structured as raster files can be examined utilizing a grid by grid calculation approach. A variety of methodologies are accessible for analyzing the interactions between climate and vegetation, applicable based on specific scenarios, including time series harmonic analysis20, partial correlation analysis21, regression analysis22, multiple correlation analysis23 and other correlation computation techniques. This article uses correlation coefficient method and regression analysis method as key techniques to probe response characteristics and sensitivity of vegetation to various climate related factors changes by map algebra calculation.

The Yellow River Basin runs a imperative role as an ecological barrier zone in China, with its ecological preservation and sustainable development being key strategic priorities for the nation. Below Taohuayu in Zhengzhou, located in Henan province, the Yellow River flows into the downstream area, known for its significant grain production and referred to as "River hanging over the ground" due to the large quantities of sand present. A study on Vegetation Phenology Responses to climatic changes in the Lower Yellow River Basin seeks to quantitatively assess the correlation between vegetation and climate during the peak growth season, which holds substantial practical importance. The interplay between vegetation and climate elements from the same or preceding periods markedly affects how climatic elements influence plant growth, providing valuable insights for improving agricultural production and development in the lower Yellow River basin. There is a notable variation in the distribution of diverse vegetation types within the basin, leading to diverse responses to climate change. Identifying how climate impacts vegetation during the growing season can aid in the ecological and environmental management efforts in this region.

This research employs techniques such as partial correlation analysis, regression analysis, and lagged correlation coefficient models to quantitatively evaluate the response time of vegetation NDVI to temperature and precipitation in nine cities across Shandong section of the lower Yellow River basin, analyzing data on a grid basis. Subsequently, the changes in vegetation attributes are examined from both temporal and spatial perspectives to investigate how different types of vegetation appear varying responses to climatic factors. Not only the scientific basis did this paper provides for ecological environment protection and sustainable development of vegetation resources in the lower Yellow River basin, but also helps to understand the seasonal variation of vegetation in the research zone.

Data and methodology

Overview of the research zone

The lower Yellow River’s reaches extend between latitudes 34°N—38°N, and longitudes 113°E—119°E, stretching from the Peach Blossom Valley’s starting point in Zhengzhou to the river’s mouth, with an overall distance of 878 km from the western mountains to the eastern plain. This total region of the Yellow River basin, which includes internal flow zone, measures 795,000 km2, with the lower Yellow River basin comprising 3% of this overall region, which is equivalent to 23,000 km2. Characterized by flat terrain, the lower Yellow River region lies in the temperate monsoon climate zone. The rainfall throughout the whole year exceeds 600 mm, primarily occurring from June to September. During the summertime in the lower Yellow River basin, high temperatures and increased rainfall are observed, while winter brings cooler temperatures and diminished precipitation, with an average temperature over many years ranging from 12.8 to 13.9 °C24. This article focuses on nine prefecture-level cities within Shandong segment of the lower Yellow River basin (Heze, Liaocheng, Jining,Tai’an, Jinan, Dezhou, Binzhou, Zibo, and Dongying). The research zone spans the North China Plain and is relatively economically developed. Within this region, vegetation is classified into eight distinct types: cultivated vegetation, swamp vegetation, scrubs, grasslands, meadows, coniferous forests, broadleaf forests, and other vegetation types25. The proportion of cultivated vegetation constitutes 85.95% of the total area, broadleaf forests comprise 3.55%, coniferous forests make up 2.73%, while the remaining types including shrubs, meadows, grasslands, and others account for 8.02% of the entire region. Cultivated vegetation occupies the largest area, with scrubs being the smallest category, and the “other” category is primarily composed of water bodies with minimal vegetation; therefore, it is excluded from this analysis. In this study, administrative boundaries are used as spatial units primarily to align with the spatial scale of available long-term climate and remote sensing data and to facilitate regional comparisons across consistent geographic units. Although ecological processes operate independently of political borders, these units provide a practical framework for summarizing large-scale patterns of vegetation–climate relationships, especially in regions where finer-scale land-cover or management data are unavailable. Our analysis focuses on climatic drivers of NDVI dynamics rather than local management effects; therefore, the use of administrative units is considered appropriate for assessing broad-scale lagged responses to temperature and precipitation.

Basic data

The foundational data employed in this article consists of meteorological, NDVI data, vegetation type data, and information pertaining to the research region. NDVI data is sourced from the National Science and Technology Resource Sharing Service Platform, commonly referred to as the National Earth System Science Data Center (10.12078/2024072901). The platform provides NDVI dataset (1 km resolution) across China. This dataset is created by synthesizing, mosaicking, and clipping the monthly maximum values from the MODIS MOD13A2 dataset26. In conjunction with the climate data time frame, this research focused on the 1 km monthly NDVI data spanning from 2001 to 2021, generating multi-year averages at each monthly interval based on the vegetation growth season that lasts from May to September, with adjustments made to extend one month (from April to August), two months (from March to July), and three months (from February to June). The spatial resolution of vegetation type dataset in the study area is 1km, which is derived from the《1:1,000,000 China Vegetation Atlas》 (edited by Academician Xueyu Hou, a vegetation ecologist)(10.12072/ncdc.nieer.db2978.2023) provided by the Data Center for Resources, Environment and Science under the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The vegetation type raster data relevant to this zone was acquired through extraction and projection conversion. Temperature and precipitation information was drawn from the monthly dataset on temperature and precipitation in China, which has a 1 km resolution (1901–2021), compiled by Shouzhang Peng et al. in NetCDF (.nc) format, and is accessible via the National Qinghai-Tibet Plateau Data Center platform (10.12041/geodata.192891852410344.verl.db).

Methods

Firstly, the format conversion, extraction, projection and mask processing of NDVI data and meteorological data were carried out, and the 1KM NDVI data in the research zone were transformed into grid points, and the one-to-one correspondence among NDVI points with temperature and precipitation was established according to the spatial location. Secondly, the vegetation lag time under the influence of various meteorological factors was calculated by using the lag correlation analysis model combined with vegetation NDVI (may to September) and the meteorological data of the corresponding months, and the lag time of per vegetation type in the research region was statistically analyzed. Thirdly, using NDVI and various climate factor data based on lag effects, a partial correlation analysis model is applied grid by grid to obtain the spatial distribution of partial correlations among various meteorological factors. Fourthly, according to the multiple regression analysis model, combined with NDVI and meteorological data, the spatial distribution of each meteorological factor’s explanatory ability to vegetation with and without lag effect was obtained respectively. Finally, the the effect of climate factors on vegetation growth and spatial distribution rules were obtained. The specific technical pathway is illustrated in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Technical roadmap.

Lag time calculation method

The existing research results show that climatic changes had an influence on vegetation phenology, and vegetation will have a delayed response to climate change22,27–29. Based on the research scale and climate zone, this article set the maximum time span of exploration lag within 3 months. The lag time is calculated according to the lag correlation coefficient method, the basic principle is to calculate correlation coefficient between the NDVI of the vegetation growth season and meteorological factors from the same era, one month in advance, to n-1 months in advance .The lag correlation coefficient of meteorological elements within n-1 months15,that is, the difference between the time of the meteorological element data corresponding to the maximum lag correlation coefficient and the corresponding calculated NDVI time, is regarded as the specific climate factor of the vegetation lag time growing season.

Based on ArcGIS map algebra calculation, NDVI values for the vegetation growth season (From May to September) from 2001 to 2021 were obtained grid by grid, as well as the average monthly temperature and precipitation for months n-1 were deduced. Partial correlation analysis

Partial correlation analysis involves examining the impact of one variable on another while accounting for the effects of additional variables within a complex system featuring numerous variables28. In the context of the multivariable system containing vegetation NDVI, temperature, precipitation, and time lag effects discussed in this thesis, the influences of precipitation and temperature are controlled. This allows for an investigation into the correlation between NDVI and an alternative climatic factor during the period when vegetation experiences significant growth, with consideration given to time lag effects. This approach aims to elucidate the importance of various climate factors on NDVI. Utilizing NDVI data gathered throughout the growing season, in conjunction with averaged temperature and precipitation data calculated from February to June, March to July, April to August, and May to September spanning the years 2001 to 2021, the partial correlation coefficient was determined using the map calculator in ArcGIS, as outlined in formula (2).

Multiple regression analysis

The multiple regression model is adept at analyzing the linear relationship between a single dependent variable and various independent variables22. For this investigation, monthly NDVI data from 2001 to 2021, along with the corresponding temperature and precipitation data for the same period or the lagging months, were employed. By applying the method of multiple linear regression analysis, the determination coefficient R2 for the derived model was calculated grid by grid, reflecting the explanatory capacity of climate factors regarding changes in NDVI. Analysis of time-lag effect of NDVI on climate elements

Spatial differences of time-lag effects

The distribution in space concerning the lag time of vegetation’s response to temperature fluctuations is illustrated in Fig. 3. In the vegetation growth season of the research region, the NDVI lag time related to temperature changes ranges from 1 to 3 months. The overall average lag time for NDVI’s response to temperature variation across all grids in the examined region is 1.733 months. Contribution power of climate elements to NDVI changes in vegetation growth season

To quantitatively assess the degree to which changes in climate factors influence the vegetation growth season’s NDVI, this article conducted a grid by grid multiple regression analysis using NDVI, temperature, and precipitation data in accordance with formula (3). The resulting regression coefficient, R2, serves as the determining coefficient that expresses the overall explanatory power of climatic changes on vegetation growth, and spatial visualization was performed (Fig. 10). Under two conditions—without considering the lag effect (Fig. 10a) and with the lag effect (Fig. 10b)—significant differences were observed in the explanatory capacity of temperature and precipitation changes on vegetation NDVI. Without accounting for the lag effect, the maximum value of R2 reached 0.7, with areas exhibiting R2 values above 0.4 accounting for 34.66% of the total research zone, primarily located in parts of Jining City, Jinan City, and Zibo City in the eastern portion of the research region. Discussion

Time-lag effect of vegetation NDVI to climate change during the season in which vegetation grows rapidly

During the growing season in the lower Yellow River Basin, the NDVI demonstrates a general upward trend from 2000 to 2021, aligning with the findings of Xie Han[28]and Fu Hanpei34.Many studies have indicated that vegetation growth exhibits a lag effect in response to climate change22,35,36, with the duration of this effect being variable. Even under optimal climatic conditions, vegetation does not respond instantaneously: its growth involves a complex and gradual progression37. The delay in vegetation’s reaction to climatic factors is intertwined with alterations in soil temperature, organic matter levels, and nutrient release, along with the time required for water to reach the root systems of plants38. Key factors limiting vegetation growth include precipitation and temperature39. Changes in temperature will gradually lead to shifts in soil temperature and the processes of nutrient cycling, consequently affecting vegetation40. The temporal response of vegetation to temperature variations is primarily determined by several factors, including physiological adaptations, soil characteristics, altitude, and others41. In contrast, vegetation’s response to precipitation is influenced by the time lag from the occurrence of precipitation and water’s arrival at the roots42. Although vegetation growth does not react immediately to precipitation, it is influenced by actual soil moisture levels, and there exists a time gap between precipitation events and water absorption by plant roots35,41. Therefore, because diverse vegetation types exhibit varying capacities for absorbing and transforming soil nutrients and water, the response of NDVI on temperature and precipitation during the peak growing season differ significantly among vegetation types22,43.

This article was revealed that cultivated vegetation had the longest average lag time (1.780 months), followed by meadow (1.586 months), broadleaf forests (1.500 months), swamp vegetation (1.488 months), shrubs (1.453 months), and grasslands (1.270 months). In comparison, coniferous forests exhibited the shortest response time to temperature fluctuations (1.244 months). The extensive management practices surrounding cultivated vegetation, including soil, fertilization, and water, potentially reduce its sensitivity to natural temperature changes.The lag effect observed in various vegetation types appears somewhat different from findings in other research, primarily due to their relatively minor share within the region under investigation.

Research into the delayed response of vegetation NDVI to precipitation revealed that swamp vegetation exhibited the longest average lag time of 2.0007 months. This was followed by coniferous forests with an average of 1.956 months, grasslands at 1.779 months, meadows at 1.740 months, broad-leaved forests at 1.551 months, and shrubs at 1.380 months. Conversely, cultivated vegetation demonstrated the shortest average lag time of 1.333 months. The long lag time associated with swamp vegetation can be attributed to several factors: swamps are capable of storing water and managing climate conditions, which may mitigate the impacts of natural elements on climate variations. In addition, the complex response of swamp vegetation to changes in precipitation is influenced by a range of biological and abiotic factors.

In this paper, NDVI displayed varying lag times in response to climatic changes of temperature and precipitation. Generally, lag-time for temperature ranged from 1 to 2 months, whereas for precipitation, it typically spanned 1 to 3 months. The observed differences in lag time between the two factors may stem from several influences. For instance, under conditions of high temperatures and low precipitation—specifically during droughts—the indirect effects of drought on vegetation moisture36 can lead to differing rection times of NDVI to temperature and precipitation within the examined area. Research has suggested that the extent of vegetation’s vulnerability to drought is contingent upon multiple factors, including the intensity, duration, and peak severity of drought occurrences44. Comprehensive studies are necessary to explore these aspects thoroughly.

Partial correlation level of vegetation NDVI with temperature and precipitation

Partial correlation analysis is widely utilized to research the association between vegetation activity and climatic variables45,46. In this thesis, a partial correlation analysis was utilized to isolate one factor (either temperature or precipitation) and examine the influence of the other factor (precipitation or temperature) on vegetation growth. According to Fig. 6, partial correlation index of vegetation NDVI and changes in precipitation throughout the growing season reveals that 90% of the research region displays a positive correlation, with 87.07% exhibiting a moderate positive correlation. In contrast, partial correlation index involving vegetation NDVI and temperature changes during the growing season indicates a negative correlation in 82.60% of the research region, and a weak negative correlation in 52.05% of the entire region. In summary, the predominant vegetative growth in the research area reveals a positive correlation with precipitation and a negative correlation with temperature. This finding is generally in agreement with the research conducted by Zhang Yingjie47 and Tao Jieyi48.

From broader global studies, significant distinctions are evident in the relationships among various types of vegetation and climate. However, research focused on smaller and medium-sized areas indicates that the correlation between diverse vegetation types and climatic factors exhibits varying degrees of consistency. The case study region in this article is relatively small, leading to a consistent pattern in partial correlation levels (Figs. 8, 9). There is minimal variation in the partial correlation results concerning temperature and precipitation across the 8 diverse vegetation types found within the research zone. Except for the weak negative correlation associated with cultivated vegetation and temperature, the majority of the other 7 vegetation types exhibited extremely weak correlation levels with temperature, suggesting that temperature has a negligible impact on the NDVI during the vegetation growth season in the lower Yellow River basin.The variation in partial correlation levels among eight distinct vegetation types and precipitation is generally stable, with notable peaks observed at moderate positive correlation levels.

The explanatory power of climatric changes on vegetation NDVI

Our study employs a multiple regression analysis framework to illustrate the combined explanatory influence of climate change on vegetation development within the designated research area, utilizing the coefficient of determination (R2) to quantitatively evaluate the level to which climate affects vegetation growth in the lower Yellow River basin during peak growing season. Both with and without accounting for lag effects, the analysis revealed a robust explanatory capacity of climate change concerning vegetation NDVI during the growing season (indicated by a high R2) in the eastern part of the research region, demonstrating consistency within the multiple regression results. In this investigation, temperature and precipitation were chosen as the independent variables, and the R2 value for NDVI of vegetation during the growing season in the research area exceeded 0.4, differing somewhat from earlier research employing various multiple regression techniques. This discrepancy arises because the number of independent variables considerably influences the coefficient of determination49, thereby affecting the capacity to explain climate impacts on vegetation during the rapid growth season. Nevertheless, akin to earlier findings, the coefficients of determination in multiple regression analyses showed significant enhancement after factoring in the lag effects of vegetation NDVI on climatic element during the growing season. There exists a lag effect resulting from temperature and precipitation variations impacting vegetation during this period in the lower Yellow River basin. Consequently, the time-lag effect must be involved in studying the response of vegetation to climate change22.

Limitations

Regarding the time-lag effect of vegetation on climate, this article conducted its analysis on a monthly scale, which may lead to inaccuracies in differentiating lag time within a month. research should focus more on investigating time lags and cumulative effects across various temporal scales50,51, especially at shorter intervals in the future.

This thesis employed correlation analysis to assess the relationship between climate and vegetation, overlooking their potentially nonlinear interactions. In applying the multiple linear regression technique to identify the significance of diverse elements influencing vegetation changes52,53, it must be noted that this approach relies on the assumption of a linear relationship between the dependent and independent variables. Therefore, it is crucial to address the nonlinear impacts of various elements on vegetation changes before employing multiple linear regression methods.

Two types factors affecting vegetation growth significantly. The first category comprises climate-related elements, such as temperature and precipitation, which possess certain characteristics associated with vegetation growth and create necessary conditions for its development. The second type of influence arises from human activities and natural disturbances, which include urban development, land use changes, forest management, agricultural irrigation, landslides, and wildfires, among others54–59. Human interventions, such as afforestation, can directly enhance vegetation coverage60, while overgrazing, agricultural expansion, and unsustainable resource use linked to urban growth may contribute to browning trends in susceptible regions61,62. Since our research primarily concentrated on the correlation between vegetation NDVI growth during the rapid growth season and climatic factors, we did not include non-biological factors, and thus, we did not perform a quantitative analysis regarding the influences of natural disturbances and human activities on regional vegetation growth.

We acknowledge that the 1 km resolution of both NDVI and vegetation-type data likely results in mixed pixels, limiting the reliability of vegetation-type-specific lag effects reported here.Beside,We used a static vegetation map due to data limitations; although some change may have occurred, major land-cover transitions are limited in our region, making the map a reasonable approximation. We acknowledge that unaccounted vegetation dynamics could introduce uncertainty.

It is important to recognize that human activities, such as urban development, farming practices, and policy implementations, are having an increasing impact on the growth of vegetation63. Therefore, further studies are necessary to investigate how additional factors influence changes in vegetation.

Conclusion

This article analyzes MODIS NDVI data alongside grid parameters for temperature and precipitation throughout the growing season across nine cities situated in the Shandong section in the lower Yellow River, spanning from 2001 to 2021. Utilizing the ArcGIS platform, the lag times related to vegetation’s response to climate variability during the significantly rapid growth period were assessed on a grid basis. The study clarified the explanatory capabilities of temperature and precipitation regarding NDVI in relation to vegetation growth, considering both scenarios of lag and non-lag, while also examining how various types of vegetation respond differently to climate alterations.

From the examination of lag effects, it was found that notable variations exist in the lag times of NDVI for response to climatic changes ( temperature and precipitation) for the growing season within the researched zone, with significant differences in the spatial distribution of these lag times. The lag times associated with NDVI in response to temperature during the vegetation growth season predominantly range from 1 to 2 months, comprising 94.83% of the entire research region. In the western and southern sectors of the area, the lag is typically 1 month, while the eastern and coastal parts experience a lag of 2 months. In terms of precipitation, the principal lag times during the vegetation growth season are primarily 1 and 3 months, representing 99.93% of the total region. Excluding the southeastern mountainous and hilly regions, where a lag of 3 months is noted, the majority of other sections (covering 79.90% of the research area) experience a 1-month lag.

Significant variations exist in how different types of vegetation respond to climate change within the research area. The reactions of cultivated crops, coniferous forests, and wetland vegetation to climate shifts are notably pronounced. Cultivated vegetation shows the longest delayed response to temperature fluctuations, averaging 1.780 months, while its response to precipitation changes is the quickest, at 1.244 months. In contrast, coniferous forests exhibit the shortest response time to temperature changes, averaging 1.333 months, and their response to precipitation changes has the second longest lag time among the vegetation types examined, at 1.956 months. Swamp vegetation, on the other hand, has the longest average lag time in response to precipitation shifts, recorded at 2.007 months. An analysis of the lag times across all grid points in the research region reveals that the standard deviation for temperature and precipitation response times for each vegetation type exceeds 0.3, with the standard deviation for precipitation lag time typically being greater than that for temperature lag time..

NDVI value during the growing season in the research region shows a negative relationship with temperature and a positive relationship with precipitation. partial correlation index of vegetation NDVI with temperature changes over the growing season indicates a negative correlation in the vast majority of regions, comprising 82.60% of the entire zone, predominantly found in the western and southern sections of the research zone. Conversely, the spatial distribution of the degree of partial correlation concerning precipitation changes demonstrates an overall positive correlation, affecting 99.47% of the region, predominantly throughout the expansive regions of the research zone.

In comparison to temperature, the NDVI across various vegetation types in the research zone revealed a more robust correlation with precipitation during the peak growth season. The partial correlation degree of NDVI and temperature for all vegetation types is typically weak or even very weak. Specifically, the NDVI of cultivated vegetation exhibits a slight negative correlation with temperature, whereas the majority of other vegetation types reflect an extremely weak association. Conversely, the partial correlation between NDVI and precipitation across all vegetation types remains fairly consistent, demonstrating an overall moderate positive correlation.

When compared to the same time frame, and accounting for lag conditions, the influence of climate change on vegetation during the growing season has heightened by 97% in the research region, with an average enhancement of 128.98%. Whether considering lag or not, the coefficient of determination (R2) displays a similar spatial distribution pattern, highest in the eastern part of the research zone and lower in the central zone. Among the various vegetation types examined, under lag conditions, climate change shows the most significant increase in explanatory power for meadow growth (260.8%), while the incremental growth for coniferous forest is the least pronounced at 74.8%.
