Association between a diabetes-predictive index and diabetes-related kidney disease in U.S. adults: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Haonan Wu, Zheng Nan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between Zhejiang University (ZJU) index and diabetic kidney disease (DKD) in U.S. adults.MethodsWe selected 13,549 U.S. adults aged 20 years or older from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2009–2020. The ZJU index is a composite score calculated by summing fasting plasma glucose, body mass index, triglycerides, and three times the alanine aminotransferase to aspartate aminotransferase ratio, with an additional 2 points for female subjects. DKD was defined by the presence of confirmed diabetes combined with a urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/g or an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2. We employed weighted multivariable logistic regression and sensitivity analyses to investigate the association between the ZJU index and DKD.ResultsThe ZJU index is positively associated with DKD in U.S. adults. In adjusted model, the ZJU index showed a positive association with DKD (OR: 1.13, 95 % CI: 1.10, 1.16).ConclusionsThe findings suggest ZJU index's potential utility as a candidate metabolic marker associated with DKD. The ZJU index may serve as an auxiliary biomarker for DKD screening. However, due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, we cannot infer causal relationship between ZJU index and DKD.Keywords: ZJU index, DKD, Diabetic kidney disease, NHANES, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a common complication of diabetes and a leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) (Bae et al., 2021). DKD may cause changes in kidney structure, including glomerular and tubular basement membrane thickening and the development of glomerulosclerosis (Yang et al., 2024). Clinically, it manifests as progressively increasing proteinuria, elevated blood pressure, and a continuous decline in the glomerular filtration rate (Lin et al., 2018). With the rising diabetic population, the prevalence of DKD is also increasing sharply. By 2045, the number of people with diabetes is projected to reach 783 million, of whom 30 %–40 % are expected to develop DKD (Li et al., 2023a; Sun et al., 2022). When DKD progresses to ESRD, patients require dialysis or kidney transplantation (Trink et al., 2023).

The Zhejiang University (ZJU) index is an emerging metabolic index composed of multiple indicators including fasting plasma glucose (FPG), body mass index (BMI), triglycerides (TG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (Wang et al., 2015). The ZJU index demonstrates predictive utility for diabetes mellitus (DM) (Wu et al., 2024), sarcopenia (Hao et al., 2024), and cholelithiasis (Xie et al., 2024).

Early identification of DKD is crucial. Currently, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) are common risk indicators for DKD (Chang et al., 2024). However, these biomarkers have limitations. Abnormal UACR may be influenced by urinary tract infections or heart failure, which can lead to elevated urinary microalbumin levels (Lu et al., 2025). While eGFR values exhibit age-dependent decline independent of pathology (Lees and Shlipak, 2022). Therefore, it is imperative to develop a complementary biomarker to facilitate DKD screening. The ZJU index serves as a composite marker reflecting patients' glycemic status, lipid profile, and obesity level, indicating trends of metabolic dysregulation. The ZJU index was initially developed to predict non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) (Wang et al., 2015). Metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) represents an updated terminology for NAFLD (Chen et al., 2023). Emerging research demonstrates a strong association between MAFLD and DKD, with both conditions sharing multiple metabolic dysfunction characteristics including obesity, hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance (Bae and Lee, 2023). However, no studies have confirmed the association between the ZJU index and DKD. To address the gap, we utilized National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data to investigate the relationship between the ZJU index and DKD among U.S. adults.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

The dataset of this study was obtained from the NHANES. NHANES is a biennial cross-sectional survey of adults and children conducted by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The survey employs a stratified, multistage probability sampling method to collect nationally representative data through questionnaires, laboratory tests, and physical examinations, providing comprehensive information on participants' demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, health conditions, and nutritional status. The study was conducted in compliance with the guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and all procedures involving human participants were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board. All participants provided their written informed consent to take part in the research.

This study utilized data from five cycles of the NHANES database (2009–2020), initially including a total of 55,999 participants. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals aged <20 years (n = 23,501); (2) those with missing data of ZJU index (n = 18,806); (3) participants lacking estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) measurements (n = 143). After applying these criteria, 13,549 individuals were ultimately included in the study. The enrollment flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

The Zhejiang University index is a composite score calculated by summing fasting plasma glucose, body mass index, triglycerides, and three times the alanine aminotransferase to aspartate aminotransferase ratio, with an additional 2 points for female subjects.

Measurement of ZJU index

Fasting plasma glucose (FPG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and triglyceride (TG) levels were all measured from fasting blood samples in NHANES laboratory data. FPG concentration was assessed via the hexokinase-mediated reaction on the Roche Modular P, Roche/Hitachi Cobas C 501, or Roche Cobas C311 chemistry analyzers. ALT activity in serum or plasma was measured using the kinetic rate method on the DxC800 or Roche Cobas 6000 (c501 module) analyzers. AST activity in serum or plasma was measured using the enzyme rate method on the DxC800 or the kinetic rate method on the Roche Cobas 6000 (c501 module) analyzer. TG concentration in serum or plasma was determined using the fixed-time endpoint method on the DxC800 or the enzymatic endpoint method on the Roche/Hitachi Cobas 6000 Chemistry Analyzer. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).

ZJU index = FPG (mmol/L) + TG (mmol/L) + BMI (kg/m2) +3 × ALT (U/L) / AST (U/L) ratio (+2, if female (Wu et al., 2024). The ZJU index is a composite metabolic score calculated by summing FPG (mmol/L), TG (mmol/L), BMI (kg/m2), and three times the ALT to AST ratio (U/L), with an additional 2 points for female subjects. Analyses were performed with the ZJU index treated as a continuous measure and as categorical quartiles (Q1-Q4).

Definition of DKD

The diagnostic criteria for DM included: self-reported diabetes, FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5 %, using insulin or antidiabetic drugs. DKD was defined by the presence of confirmed DM combined with a single UACR ≥30 mg/g or an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Tan et al., 2025). Due to the cross-sectional nature of our study design, we were unable to account for temporal factors. The UACR was calculated as the ratio of urinary albumin to urinary creatinine. The eGFR was calculated using the CKD Epidemiology Creatinine Equation based on serum creatinine, age, gender, and race/ethnicity. Urinary albumin was measured by fluorescent immunoassay. Urinary creatinine was measured using the enzymatic method on the Roche/Hitachi Modular P or Roche/Hitachi Cobas 6000 chemistry analyzers. Serum creatinine was measured using the kinetic Jaffe assay on DxC800 or the enzymatic method on Roche Cobas 6000 (c501 module) analyzer.

Assessment of covariates

Our study accounted for various potential covariates that might affect the association between ZJU index and DKD, including age, race/ethnicity, gender, marital status, education level, poverty income ratio (PIR), smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT), serum uric acid (SUA), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and waist circumference (WC) (Xie et al., 2024). Participants were stratified by age into three groups: 20–39 years, 40–59 years, and ≥ 60 years. Race/ethnicity was categorized into five groups: Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other race/ethnicity. Gender was classified as male and female. Marital status was categorized into three groups: married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, and never married. Educational attainment was classified into three levels: highly educated, moderately educated and poorly educated. The PIR was divided into three ranges: < 1.3, 1.3–3.5, and ≥ 3.5. Smoking history was defined as “smoker” (≥ 100 cigarettes in lifetime) or “never smoker” (< 100 lifetime cigarettes) based on self-reported data. Alcohol consumption was categorized as “drinker” (any alcohol intake in the past 12 months) or “non-drinker” (no alcohol consumption during the same period). Hypertension status was based on self-reported diagnosis. CVD was identified through affirmative responses to a series of questions assessing a history of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, or stroke” (Zhan et al., 2023). GGT, SUA, HDL—C, and LDL-C were obtained from laboratory data, whereas WC was derived from physical examinations.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared across quartile groups stratified by the ZJU index (Q1-Q4). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (IQR), while categorical variables as n (%). Group comparisons employed ANOVA analysis, Kruskal-Wallis H test or Chi-squared test.

A multivariate single imputation method for missing data based on iterative imputer was implemented, using a Bayesian Ridge model as the estimator at each step of the round-robin-imputation (Van Buuren and Groothuis-Oudshoorn, 2011). Weighted multiple logistic regression analysis was employed to explore the association between ZJU index and DKD. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for gender, age, race/ethnicity, education level, marital status, PIR, smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, CVD, GGT, SUA, HDL-C, LDL-C, and WC.

Moreover, to provide a comprehensive analysis, we performed sensitivity analyses by excluding variables with missing data, adding covariates, and conducting receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses.

All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.2.2; R foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Free Statistics software (Version 2.1.1; Beijing, China). Two-tailed tests were employed throughout the study, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

The baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. The mean age was 49.8 years. Among participants, 51.9 % were female, 39.3 % were Non-Hispanic White, 54.5 % were highly educated, 60 % were married or living with a partner, and over 70 % reported alcohol consumption. Regarding socioeconomic status, 37.7 % had a PIR within the moderate range (from 1.3 to 3.5). More than half (56.6 %) were non-smokers, and 9.9 % had a history of CVD. The mean levels of HDL-C and LDL-C were 1.4 mmol/L and 2.9 mmol/L. The average SUA level was 325 umol/L, with a mean WC of 99.7 cm. The mean GGT level was 20 U/L. Participants with ZJU index in the lowest quartile had a DKD proportion of 1.9 % and a hypertension proportion of 20.5 %, whereas the highest quartile exhibited 14.4 % and 52.7 %, respectively. Higher ZJU index quartiles were associated with lower HDL-C levels and elevated SUA, WC, and GGT levels.

Association between ZJU index and DKD

We employed multivariable logistic regression analysis to examine the association between the ZJU index and DKD, with results presented in Table 2. In Model 1, we found that each unit increase in the ZJU index was associated with a 1.09-fold higher probability of developing DKD (OR = 1.09, 95 % CI: 1.08, 1.10). In Model 2, the OR for the relationship between the ZJU index and DKD was 1.13 (95 % CI: 1.10, 1.16).

Second, we extended the adjustments of Model 2 by incorporating additional covariates including physical activity and dietary intake variables (total energy, protein, carbohydrates, and total fat) in Model 3. The weighted multivariable logistic regression analysis in Model 3 demonstrated that the ZJU index remained significantly associated with DKD, with an adjusted OR of 1.18 (95 % CI: 1.15, 1.22). Although the OR values showed some variation, the positive association between the ZJU index and DKD remained persistent.

The ZJU index exhibited an AUC of 0.72 (95 % CI: 0.71, 0.74) for DKD risk assessment. Discussion

In this study, we identified a significant positive correlation between the ZJU index and DKD in U.S. adults. However, given the cross-sectional design, we cannot infer causal relationship between ZJU index and DKD.

Traditional biomarkers such as HbA1c, eGFR, and UACR (Tan et al., 2025) are well-established, straightforward tools for diagnosing DKD. Nevertheless, these markers face practical challenges: UACR is unsuitable for anuric/oliguric patients and involves time-consuming procedures; HbA1c fails to accurately reflect true glycemic status in patients with significant glucose fluctuations. (When averaged, both hyperglycemic and hypoglycemic episodes may result in a HbA1c level that appears nearly normal) (Martorina and Tavares, 2023); Additionally, in patients with chronic kidney disease accompanied by anemia, HbA1c levels may be falsely lowered (Martin et al., 2020); While isolated use of eGFR or UACR requires exclusion of other causes of renal dysfunction, with some cases even necessitating renal biopsy for definitive diagnosis (Tong et al., 2020). The ZJU index was specifically designed for early risk screening tool rather than disease diagnosis. We recognize that DKD is fundamentally a disorder of glucose-lipid metabolism, and its risk assessment should extend beyond glycemic parameters (like HbA1c) to incorporate key metabolic factors such as lipids and obesity. The ZJU index integrates readily available indicators—FPG, TG, BMI, and liver function (ALT/AST)—to provide rapid and convenient DKD risk stratification for populations not yet undergoing renal function or urinary testing. Critically, ZJU index does not replace existing diagnostic criteria but serves as a complementary tool to identify high-risk individuals and prompt further investigation.

The ZJU Index, a novel composite metric comprising FPG, BMI, TG, ALT, and AST, has emerged as an indicator for assessing insulin resistance (IR) and disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism. A cross-sectional study demonstrated a positive correlation between TG levels and DKD (OR = 2.447, 95 % CI: 1.648, 3.633) (Li et al., 2023b). A study found that among patients with T2DM, overweight and obese individuals had a higher likelihood of developing DKD compared to those with underweight or normal body weight (Man et al., 2018). Another study revealed BMI as an independent risk factor of DKD (OR = 1.052, 95 % CI: 1.058, 1.090) (Shi et al., 2023). Research has confirmed that the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a reliable marker of IR, is associated with an increased risk of DKD (Jiang and Lai, 2024). Additionally, a research found a U-shaped relationship between ALT levels and DKD (Bi et al., 2024). Although these simple indicators have demonstrated predictive capability for DKD risk, our ROC analysis revealed that the ZJU index outperformed BMI, TG, ALT, and AST in predicting DKD, with only FPG showing better predictive performance. Dysregulation of glucose and lipid metabolism is one of the pathogenic mechanisms of DKD (Jung and Yoo, 2022). The ZJU index, as a composite metric integrating glucose-lipid metabolism, obesity, and liver enzyme levels, provides a more comprehensive assessment of metabolic status compared to FPG or the TyG index. While Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) is a widely used surrogate marker for insulin resistance, its requirement for insulin measurement limits applicability in insulin-treated patients (Sun et al., 2025).

Disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses constitute key pathogenic mechanisms in DKD (Jung and Yoo, 2022). Hyperglycemia promotes the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and AGE-mediated accumulation of AGE receptors (RAGE) induces oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby triggering DKD (Wu et al., 2021). Obesity may promote DKD through mechanisms including insulin resistance, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction (Shi et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2021). Elevated hepatic enzyme levels are increasingly recognized as a manifestation of underlying endocrine-metabolic dysregulation (Tian et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2022). Disordered lipid homeostasis and ectopic lipid accumulation demonstrate significant correlations with DKD progression (Song et al., 2019). Hyperlipidemia can induce glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, ultimately leading to renal impairment (Wei et al., 2022). Our study has several notable strengths. First, we are the first to demonstrate a positive association between the ZJU index and DKD. Second, sensitivity analyses were conducted to provide comprehensive findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the cross-sectional design, our study can only establish an association inference rather than a causal relationship between the ZJU index and DKD. Second, despite adjustment for numerous potential covariates in our analyses, we cannot rule out residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as medication use. Third, due to the cross-sectional design, our definition of DKD was limited to using a single UACR/eGFR value in patients with DM, which could not account for temporal factors. Relying solely on a single-timepoint UACR/eGFR measurement may lead to misclassification. Fourth, the exclusion of cases with substantial missing exposure data resulted in reduced sample size, potentially affecting statistical power. Finally, due to racial variations, the ZJU index requires external validation in prospective cohorts or international samples before clinical implementation.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates a significant positive correlation between the ZJU index and DKD among the U.S. adults. These findings suggest ZJU index's potential utility as a candidate metabolic marker associated with DKD. The ZJU index may serve as an auxiliary biomarker for DKD screening. However, the cross-sectional design cannot establish causal relationships. Future longitudinal studies are needed to validate these findings and explore potential mechanisms.
