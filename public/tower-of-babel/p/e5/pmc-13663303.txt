Seroprevalence, associated risk factors, and molecular detection of Brucella spp. in cattle, sheep, goats and humans in Mvomero district, Morogoro, Tanzania
Authors: Emmanuel P Lita, Ernatus M Mkupasi, Erneo B Ochi, Gerald Misinzo, Henriette van Heerden, Robab Katani, Jacques Godfroid, Coletha Mathew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBrucellosis is a zoonotic disease of public health and economic importance worldwide. A cross-sectional study using simple random and purposive sampling techniques was conducted from May 2024 to October 2024 in agro-pastoral areas of Mvomero district, Morogoro Region. The aim of this study was to determine the seroprevalence of brucellosis, associated risk factors, and characterize the circulating Brucella spp., in livestock and humans.MethodsA total of 918 serum samples were collected from Mvomero district, comprising cattle (n = 339), sheep (n = 164), goats (n = 303), and humans (n = 112). Animal samples were obtained from 18 herds with mixed livestock species, while human samples were collected from four health facilities. All samples were screened for Brucella-specific antibodies using the Rose Bengal plate test (RBPT), and positive samples were subjected to competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (c-ELISA). Genomic DNA was extracted from the heparin-coated blood samples corresponding to the same samples that tested positive by c-ELISA for confirmation by using real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). A herd was considered Brucella seropositive if at least one animal tested positive on c-ELISA. A structured questionnaire was used to collect information on potential individual and herd-level risk factors. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was carried out using a backward elimination procedure to identify potential risk factors, following a univariate analysis, using a p-value cut-off of 0.25 from the univariable analysis to select candidate variables for a multivariable model.ResultsAt the individual level, screening by RBPT revealed seropositivity rates of 9.1% (31/339) in cattle, 4.3% (7/164) in sheep, 5.6% (17/303) in goats, and 10.7% (12/112) in humans. Subsequent results by c-ELISA demonstrated lower seroprevalence rates of 6.5% (22/339) in cattle, 1.2% (2/164) in sheep, and 2.0% (6/303) in goats. Human seroprevalence by c-ELISA was 4.5% (5/112), with all positive cases originating from a single health facility. At the herd level, the overall seroprevalence was 55.5% (10/18) based on c-ELISA results. Out of the 35 c-ELISA positive samples, 21/35 (60.0%) tested positive for the Brucella genus detection using the IS711 gene. The Brucella species-specific assay detected 17/35 (48.6%) samples as B. abortus. There was no B. melitensis detected in this study. Risk factor analysis in sheep, goats, and humans yielded no significant associations in the univariable analyses. However, multivariable logistic regression analysis in cattle identified two significant risk factors: prior history of abortion (p < 0.001, OR = 12.7, 95% CI: 3.88–41.8) and retained placenta (p = 0.023, OR = 32.5, 95% CI: 1.602–660.8).ConclusionsBrucella abortus was the predominant circulating species in cattle, goats and humans. The significant association between brucellosis seropositivity and reproductive complications in cattle (abortion and retained placenta) underscores its economic impact on livestock productivity. We recommend serological screening of livestock and vaccination programmes in cattle and strengthened collaboration between veterinary and public health sectors through a One Health approach.Keywords: Tanzania, Morogoro, Brucellosis, Brucella. abortus, Seroprevalence, Risk factors, Humans, Cattle, Sheep, Goats, Real-time PCR

Introduction

Brucellosis is a bacterial disease of animals, especially domesticated livestock, caused by various Brucella species [1]. The disease occurs worldwide, causing substantial economic losses in animal production and a potentially debilitating chronic infection in humans [2]. Brucellosis is endemic in Asian and African countries [3]. However, it is well controlled in developed countries because of routine screening of livestock and implementation of vaccination programmes [4].

There are 12 Brucella spp., of which four, namely B. melitensis, B. suis, B. abortus and B. canis., are of public health importance [5]. Brucellosis in cattle is usually caused by B. abortus, less frequently by B. melitensis and occasionally by B. suis [6]. In sheep and goats, infection is caused mainly by B. melitensis. Whereas in domestic pigs, infection with Brucella is due to B. suis biovar 1–3 [6].

Disease transmission to humans occurs through direct contact with infected materials (particularly aborted foetuses and placentas) via skin breaks [7] or indirectly through consumption of contaminated raw animal products (especially unpasteurised milk and dairy products) or inhalation of the pathogen [1]. Human brucellosis cases are invariably linked to the presence of Brucella spp. in animal populations with which people have direct or indirect contact [8]. In livestock, intra-herd transmission occurs through direct contamination with aborted foetuses or discharged placental tissues and fluids, whereas inter-herd infection occurs through the introduction of an infected animal from infected herds [9].

Cross-species transmission of some Brucella species, irrespective of their specific host, can occur, making it a significant concern for both animal and public health as it complicates control and diagnosis [10].

The clinical features of brucellosis in humans are non-specific and include recurring (undulant) fever, night sweats, headache, malaise, sleeplessness, arthritis, muscle pain, and arthralgias [8]. In contrast, the disease in livestock is characterised by one or more of the following signs, abortion, retained placenta, orchitis, epididymitis, and, rarely, arthritis [6].

Some of the risk factors associated with the transmission of brucellosis in humans include but not limited to assisting animals during parturition [11] and consumption of raw milk and meat and contact with animals, their products, and discharges [12]. In animals, some of the reported individual risk factors include age and history of abortion [13].

Brucellosis is listed among the top six priority diseases due to the threats and burdens posed by this zoonotic disease in Tanzania [14]. Studies conducted on brucellosis in various regions, including Morogoro, Mwanza, Arusha, Kagera, Mbeya, Dodoma, Kilimanjaro, Mara, and Kigoma regions, have reported varying individual-level seroprevalence rates ranging from 0.0% to 33.3% [12, 13, 15–18] and from 0.9% to 30.8% [18–23] in animals and humans, respectively. A herd-level seroprevalence of 2.2–77.7% has been reported in cattle herds [22, 24]. As compared to cattle and humans, there is limited information regarding brucellosis in sheep and goats in Tanzania.

The laboratory diagnosis of brucellosis is mainly based on serological and molecular testing, as isolation rates of the fastidious etiologic agent from blood or tissues are time-consuming, hazardous, and not sensitive, and their outcome depends on individual laboratory practices [7, 25, 26]. Serological tests are inexpensive and simple, and could be rapid, although they are susceptible to false positive reactions resulting from exposure to cross-reacting microorganisms [27]. Nucleic acid amplification assays combine exquisite sensitivity, specificity, and safety and enable direct detection of pathogens [5]. The use of PCR to identify Brucella DNA at genus, species, and even biovar levels has become extended to improve diagnostic tests [28].

Prevention and control of brucellosis require surveillance, animal control, and increased use of brucellosis vaccines for animals at risk [29]. In animals, the B. abortus Strain (S19) and the RB51 vaccines are used for the immunization of cattle, and B. melitensis Rev1 for sheep and goats [30]. Although cross-species transmission poses significant implications for the control of brucellosis, the S19 vaccine could play a role in preventing B. melitensis outbreaks in cattle in areas where this pathogen is prevalent in small ruminants [31].

The B. melitensis Rev-1 vaccine for the vaccination of small ruminants has not been validated for use in cattle [32].

This study aimed to determine the seroprevalence of brucellosis, identify associated risk factors, and characterize the circulating Brucella species in Mvomero District, Morogoro Region, Tanzania. The findings will inform evidence-based policy decisions for implementing effective control strategies in the Morogoro Region.

Materials and methods

Study area

This study was conducted in Mvomero District, one of the six districts of the Morogoro region (Fig. 1). The district is located at latitudes − 6.235617 and 38.693748, and it is bordered to the north by the Tanga region, to the northeast by the Pwani region, to the east and southeast by Morogoro Rural District and Morogoro Urban District, and to the west by Kilosa District. The predominant livestock species are cattle, sheep, and goats, which are kept and managed under an extensive system in mixed herds. The main livelihood activities engaged in by the local inhabitants are crops and livestock production. Additionally, health facilities (dispensaries and health centres) located in the selected wards were included for human sampling in the study.

Study design and subjects

A cross-sectional study was conducted from May 2024 to October 2024. Three wards, Dakawa, Melela, and Mvomero, were purposively selected due to a huge livestock population and the presence of both pastoral and agropastoral activities [33]. A structured questionnaire was used to collect information on potential individual-level risk factors.

The study subjects comprised cattle, sheep, goats, and humans of different ages and sexes. Animals less than six months of age and humans less than 14 years old were excluded from the study. The exclusion is because of the possible interference of maternal antibodies with serology test results in animals under 6 months, which often causes false positive results for infection. Humans under 14 years old were excluded from the study because of ethical issues relating to drawing of blood, consent, and cooperation. In humans, the study targeted patients attending the health services during the time of sampling at the health facilities (dispensaries and health centres) proximate to the selected wards.

Sampling methods and sample size determination

A simple random technique was used for sampling of cattle sheep and goats. Where as in humans, the sampling was purposive. The District Veterinary Officer (DVO) and the respective ward livestock officers identified and selected the herds based on availability of pastoral and agropastoral activities, communal grazing and mixed farming.

Sample size for each species was calculated according to the formula described by Thrusfield [34], using a 95% confidence interval at 5% desired absolute precision.

The calculation was performed based on an expected seroprevalence of brucellosis at 30.8% [22], 5.7% [35], and 13.7% [36] for cattle, sheep, and goats, respectively.

Based on the above formula, 328 cattle, 83 sheep, and 182 goats were needed to be collected. However, a total of 339 cattle, 164 sheep, and 303 goats were sampled to increase the precision of the estimates.

In humans, an expected seroprevalence of 7.7% [20] was used to calculate the sample size. Based on the above formula, a total of 110 samples was needed. However, a total 112 samples were collected.

Blood sample collection and handling

For cattle, seven millilitres (7 mL) of blood were collected aseptically from the jugular vein using both plain and a heparin-coated vacutainer tube (BD Vacutainer®, Becton Dickinson, USA). For sheep and goats, 5 mL of blood were collected aseptically from the jugular vein using the same type of tubes. The blood samples were transported in a cooler box maintained at 4–8 °C to the laboratory at Sokoine University of Agriculture for laboratory processing. In the laboratory, the blood sample in the plain vacutainer were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm (approximately 1,500 × g) for 5 min to separate sera. The harvested sera were aliquoted into labelled 2 mL cryovial tubes.

In human’s subjects, a qualified laboratory technician in the respective wards collected 5 mL of venous blood following standard venipuncture protocols after an informed consent of the participant. Serum separation followed the same protocol as described for animal samples. All sera were stored at -20 °C until analysis.

All the blood samples collected in heparin-coated vacutainer tubes were stored − 80 °C until DNA extraction.

Serological tests

Rose Bengal plate test

All sera were screened for Brucella-specific antibodies using RBPT according to the procedure described by [6]. Briefly, 30 µL of serum was added to an equal amount of RBPT antigen on a glass slide at room temperature and mixed. The slide was then agitated for 4 min. The RBPT antigen, positive and negative controls were obtained from the Animal and Plant Health Agency (APHA), New Haw, Addlestone, Surrey, England. The samples were considered Rose Bengal positive; any agglutination was observed.

C-ELISA

All samples found positive from the RBPT were further subjected to the c-ELISA kit (Boehringer Ingelheim SVANOVA®, Uppsala, Sweden) for confirmation. The procedure was performed following the SVANOVIR kit manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were run in duplicates with positive and negative controls. Briefly, 5 µL of sample/control was diluted with 45 µL of sample dilution buffer inside a well coated with Brucella abortus lipopolysaccharide. Then, 50 µL of mouse antibody solution was added to the wells. The wells were then incubated for 30 min before washing and adding 100 µL conjugate solution. After another 30 min incubation, a second wash step was performed, followed by the addition of 100 µL of substrate solution to the wells. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of stop solution. The optical densities were measured at 450 nm, and the percent inhibition value was calculated to determine the positivity of the samples. Samples with a 30% or above percent inhibition value were considered c-ELISA positive.

Genomic DNA extraction from the whole blood

A total of 35 genomic DNA samples were extracted from heparin-coated blood samples corresponding to the same samples that tested positive by c-ELISA: cattle (n = 22), sheep (n = 2), goats (n = 6), and humans (n = 5). The extraction was done using the Quick-gDNA™ Blood MiniPrep (Zymo Research, USA) kit following manufacturer’s guidelines. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed by using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Biochrom LTD, Cambridge, England) at 260 nm and 280 nm. The extracted DNA was stored at -80 °C until further analysis. DNA extraction from samples was done.

Brucella genus-specific DNA identification

The extracted DNA was subjected to real-time PCR for Brucella spp. specific targeting IS711 using primers (Forward: 5’-GCTTGAAGCTTGCGGACAGT-3’, Reverse: 5’-GGCCTACCGCTGCGAAT-3’, IS711-FAM probe: 5’-AAGCCAACACCCGGCCATTATGGT). The IS711-based qPCR assay is a specific, sensitive, efficient, and reproducible method for rapidly detecting the genus Brucella [37]. The qPCR assay was run in a LightCycler® Nano real-time PCR (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) apparatus using a final volume of 20 µL, containing 2Χ PerfeCTa® qPCR mix low ROX™ 10 µL, primer/probe 1 µL, nuclease-free water 6.5 µL, and DNA 2.5 µL. The test was performed using the following condition: initial activation at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 35 cycles. Each cycle comprised denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s followed by annealing and extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Brucella. abortus S19 was used as a positive control. In the negative control, nuclease-free water was used instead of DNA. Samples with a cycle threshold (Ct) of ≤ 35 were considered positive and subjected to further Brucella speciation assays. The test was performed at the Department of Veterinary Tropical Diseases, Faculty of Veterinary Science, University of Pretoria, Onderstepoort, South Africa.

Brucella genus-specific and species-specific DNA detection

All positive samples at the genus Brucella IS711 gene amplification were further subjected to speciation using qPCR assay as described [38]. The assay used species-specific primers for B. abortus (Forward: 5’-GCACACTCACCTTCCACAACAA-3’, Reverse: 5’-CCCCGTTCTGCACCAGACT-3’, Brucella abortus FAM probe: FAM-5’-TGGAACGACCTTTGCAGGCGAGATC-3’) and B. melitensis - (Forward: 5’-TCGCATCGGCAGTTTCAA-3’, Reverse: 5’-CCAGCTTTTGGCCTTTTC-3’, Brucella melitensis – HEX probe: 5’-CCTCGGCATGGCCCGCAA-3’).

The test was done in a 20 µL reaction, containing 2Χ PerfeCTa® qPCR mix low ROX™ 10 µL, B. abortus primer 0.6 µL, B. melitensis primer 0.6 µL, B. abortus probe (FAM) 0.4 µL, B. meltensis probe (HEX) 0.4 µL, nuclease-free water 5.5 µL, and DNA 2.5 µL. The test was performed with the following PCR condition: initial activation at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 35 cycles. Each cycle comprised denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s followed by annealing and extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Brucella abortus and B. melitensis positive controls, negative control, and templates were loaded in duplicates. Brucella. abortus S19 and B. melitensis were used as positive controls. In the negative control, nuclease-free water was used instead of DNA. The thresholds were set using the auto-baseline and threshold feature in LightCycler® Nano real-time PCR (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) apparatus. Any sample with a cycle threshold (Ct) of ≤ 35 on the speciation assay was considered positive.

Questionnaire administration

A structured questionnaire was used to collect information regarding potential risk factors associated with brucellosis. The questionnaire was prepared in English and translated to Kiswahili for easy understanding and administration. The questionnaire was pretested to ensure clarity and validity. The information collected from the individual animal species included age, sex, prior history of abortion, history of retained placenta, reproductive status, and parity level. Demographic information of the patients from the respective health facilities was recorded.

Data management and statistical analysis

The study used both qualitative and quantitative methods of data collection and analysis. Data were analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25 [39]. Descriptive statistics were run to obtain frequencies and percentages. The seroprevalence was calculated as the number of animals or humans testing positive by c-ELISA, divided by the total number of animals or humans tested. Univariable analysis was computed to identify the association between potential risk factors and Brucella seropositivity using a 95% confidence interval (CI) at (p < 0.05).

A structured questionnaire was used to collect information on potential individual and herd-levels risk factors. A multivariate logistic regression analysis was carried out using a backward elimination procedure to identify potential risk factors, following a univariate analysis, using a p-value cut-off of 0.25 from the univariable analysis to select candidate variables for a multivariable model. The investigated risk factors for livestock included age, sex, species, prior abortion history, retained placenta, parity, and reproductive status. A backward stepwise (Wald) model was used, and the validity of the test was assessed by computing the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit [40].

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the study subjects

A total of 806 animals, comprising cattle (n = 339), sheep (n = 164), and goats (n = 303) were screened for Brucella-specific antibodies. The samples were derived from 18 herds selected from three wards in Melela (n = 4), Mvomero (n = 10), and Dakawa (n = 4). The study included a higher number of female cattle 37.3% (301/806), sheep 20.3% (133/806), and goats 32.0% (257/806) compared to males. In cattle, most animals belong to age category “> 4 years old” 72.6% (246/339), while in sheep and goats age category “> one year old” 84.2% (393/46) predominated.

A total of 112 humans, males 24 (21.4%) and females 88 (78.6%) participated in the study. The majority of the participants were agro-pastoralists 67.9% (76/112). Age category “25–34 years old” 30.4% (34/112) were the majority, followed by the age category “18–24 years old”, 29.5% 933/112). The majority of participants were derived from Melela Ward 42.9% (48/112).

Brucella seroprevalence in cattle, sheep, and goats

A total of 806 sera comprising cattle (n = 339), sheep (n = 164), and goats (n = 303) were screened using both RBPT and c-ELISA. Based on c-ELISA results, the seroprevalence was highest in cattle at 6.5% (22/339: 95% CI: 4.1–9.7), followed by goats at 1.9% (6/303: 95% CI: 0.7–4.3%) and sheep at 1.2% (2/164; 95% CI: 0.15–4.3%) as shown in Table 1. The difference in seroprevalence among the three species was statistically significant (ꭓ2 = 14.32, p < 0.001).

Brucella seroprevalence in humans

Out of the 112 human serum samples tested for Brucella-specific antibodies, 24 (21.4%) were males and 88 (78.6%) females. The samples were collected from four health facilities in the district: Melela Hospital (n = 48, 42.9%), Mvomero Hospital (n = 25, 22.3%), Zenith Health Centre (n = 20, 17.9%), and Dakawa Dispensary (n = 19, 17.0%).

Based on c-ELISA test, the overall seroprevalence of human brucellosis was 4.5% (5/112; 96% CI: 1.5–10.1).

Analysis by gender showed a higher seroprevalence in females (4.5%, 4/88; 95% CI: 0.63–1.54) compared to males (4.2%, 1/24; 95% CI: 0.178–6.4), though this difference was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.709). All positive cases were detected in human samples from Melela Hospital, representing a facility-specific seroprevalence of 10.4% (5/48; 95% CI: 3.5–22.7%). Out of the 35 genomic DNA tested, 21/35(60.0%) were positive for the Brucella genus identification using IS711 gene. Discussion

This study revealed a seroprevalence of 6.5%, 1.2%, 1.9%, and 4.5% by c-ELISA in cattle, sheep, goats, and humans, respectively. Brucella abortus was identified as the predominant Brucella species circulating in cattle, goats, and humans. There was no Brucella DNA detected in the sheep blood. Notably, all human-positive cases were recorded in a single health facility. The majority of the participants in Melela Ward are pastoralists who are linked to animals, and this linkage might increase their likelihood of exposure to the livestock harbouring Brucella bacteria. Moreover, there is a Saturday cattle, sheep, and goat livestock market where roast meat, also referred to locally as nyama choma is sold. If improperly prepared, such meat could also spread brucellosis.

All the herds (100%, n = 4) were positive for brucellosis. Of note, infected animals shed bacteria for life through milk and during deliveries, posing a risk of human infection. As such, human disease is disproportionately high in areas with a high prevalence of animal brucellosis, as is the case in Melela Ward [41].

The RBPT is widely used as a sensitive screening test, and c-ELISA as highly specific reduces false positives from cross-reacting bacteria [6]. In humans, the RBPT is found to be highly sensitive and specific in the sera of persons with no contact with Brucella attending the health services presenting with Acute Febrile Illness (AFI) [42].

In this study, qPCR was used to confirm the seropositive Brucella samples, as it offers direct, sensitive, specific, and quantifiable detection of the pathogen, improving the accuracy of diagnosis and helping to differentiate between active and past infections [5].

The observed cattle seroprevalence (6.5%) aligns with previous findings from agro-pastoral areas of the Morogoro region [43] and the Katavi-Rukwa ecosystem [44]. The seroprevalence was higher in cattle compared to sheep and goats. Several factors may explain the higher seroprevalence in cattle compared to small ruminants, including the predominance of cattle in the study area, their longer herd retention periods, and their susceptibility to multiple Brucella species [6].

The low seroprevalence in goats (1.9%) is consistent with previous reports from the Morogoro region [45] and the Katavi-Rukwa ecosystem, Tanzania [44]. This consistency across studies suggests stable transmission patterns in small ruminants in these regions. No B. melitensis has been detected in our study. The low seroprevalence and the absence of isolation of B. abortus in sheep, reinforce the suggestion made that sheep are less sensitive to B. abortus infections compared to goats.

The human seroprevalence of 4.5% exceeds rates reported in the Katavi-Rukwa ecosystem [44] and Kilimanjaro region [24], despite all studies employing hospital-based sampling. However, our findings show lower seroprevalence compared to studies in Ngara and Kibondo districts [46], possibly due to methodological differences. While our study used the c-ELISA test, previous studies relied on RBPT, which may yield false positives due to cross-reactions with other bacteria [6, 28].

A notably higher seroprevalence (33.3%) based on c-ELISA was previously reported in the Mvomero district, Morogoro region [13]. These disparities might be attributed to differences in sample size, sampling technique, spatial distribution, and temporal factors.

The qPCR assay identified B. abortus as the dominant, if not the exclusive, Brucella spp. circulating in cattle, goats, and humans in the Mvomero district. Previous studies have reported B. abortus in Tanzania [35, 45, 47, 48].

Brucella abortus was detected more in cattle on 10/22 (45.5%) than in goats. This could be due to the predominance of cattle in the study area, their longer herd retention periods, and their susceptibility to multiple Brucella species [6]. The presence of B. abortus in goats in this study strongly suggests a cross-species transmission of the pathogen from cattle to goats. The study confirms previous findings that indicated the presence of B. abortus in goats in the Morogoro region [45]. This finding could pose a significant implication for the control of brucellosis, as the S19 vaccine is used for the vaccination of cattle against B. abortus and is not registered for use in goats, and although there is potential for cross-species protection, its effectiveness and safety in this species have not been sufficiently documented [6]. Our reported herd-level seroprevalence exceeds findings from Serengeti and Kagera [20, 21], but falls below rates reported in agro-pastoral areas of Morogoro [23] and Kasulu District, Kigoma region [22]. These variations likely reflect differences in herd size, management system, stocking density, and species composition [49, 50].

Brucella abortus was detected in 33.3% (5/18) of the sampled herds. This is critical in the epidemiology of brucellosis, as B. abortus can possibly spill over to sheep, goats, and humans under certain conditions, with the potential for significant health and economic impacts. The existence of more than one sero-reactor animal species in most villages/herds and the association of increased livestock species composition with seropositivity may add more credence to the possibility of cross-species transmission with Brucella infections [51].

The study revealed a significant association of prior history of abortion with Brucella seropositivity in cattle. A similar finding has been reported in the agro-pastoral areas of Tanzania [43] and in the Jimma zone, Ethiopia [52]. The predilection sites for B. abortus include the supramammary gland, lumber lymph node, and spleen tissue [53]. This is due to the presence of erythritol sugar that stimulates the growth of B. abortus. Invasion of the gravid uterus by this organism results in severe ulcerative endometritis and eventually abortion [9].

Similarly, a number of studies from different countries reported a significant association of prior history of abortion with Brucella seropositivity [54–56].

The study also found a significant association of retained placenta in cows with Brucella seropositivity. This finding is consistent with a number of studies that reported a significant association of retained placenta with Brucella seropositivity in cows [57–59]. Retention of the placenta is a common sequela to abortion caused by brucellosis [9].

Limitation of the study

This study is limited by its geographic scope, as data were collected from a single district, which may affect the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. This study was limited by a relatively small sample size and sparse data in certain categories, which may have led to unstable model estimates and wide confidence intervals observed for some odd ratios. Increasing the sample size in future studies would improve the statistical power and reduce variability. Despite these limitations, this study gives an insight into the seroprevalence, risk factors and circulating Brucella species in Mvomero district, Morogoro, Tanzania.

Conclusions and recommendations

The study demonstrated the persistent presence of brucellosis across multiple species in the Mvomero district, Morogoro region, with notable variations in seroprevalence. The results of the investigation showed that B. abortus is the dominant species circulating in cattle and possibly spilling to goats and humans. The investigation identified two significant risk factors for Brucella seropositivity in cattle: prior history of abortion and retained placenta. The detection of human cases, particularly their concentration in one health facility, underscores the ongoing public health significance of this zoonotic disease.

Based on these findings, we recommend the implementation of comprehensive control strategies that will involve targeted vaccination of cattle in the high-risk areas, enhanced biosecurity levels at the herd, and raising public awareness about brucellosis. Vaccination in goats is problematic because of the absence of validated data on the efficacy of S19 or Rev.1 vaccination against B. abortus infection, notwithstanding that vaccination can only be recommended if the role of goats in the intra- and interspecies transmission of B. abortus infection can be properly documented. Future research should investigate species-specific transmission dynamics and characterise the circulating Brucella strains.
