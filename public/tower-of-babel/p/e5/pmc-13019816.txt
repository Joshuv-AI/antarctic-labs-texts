The MeHPriC project in Lagos, Nigeria: a mixed methods study on policy, governance, and sustainability of mental health integration
Authors: Abiodun O Adewuya, Bolanle Ola, Seye Abimbola, Jibril Abdulmalik, Olabisi E Oladipo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe sustainability of integrating mental health into primary health care (PHC) in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is uncertain, often failing beyond pilot stages. This study examines the policy and governance factors shaping the sustainability of the Mental Health in Primary Care (MeHPriC) project in Lagos, Nigeria, a state-led MNS integration initiative.MethodsThis qualitative study combined a systematic document review with 15 in-depth interviews with state policymakers and MeHPriC managers. Guided by the Integrated Sustainability Framework (ISF) and Supporting the Use of Research Evidence (SURE) framework, we explored how policy, financing, and coordination influence sustainability. Data were analysed thematically and triangulated to map implementation pathways.FindingsLagos State demonstrated strong formal commitment via policies, task-shifting guidelines, and a mental health law. However, sustainability was constrained by implementation gaps, weak supervision, inconsistent budget execution, and high workforce turnover. Leadership enabled early momentum, but coordination was limited and informal. Community engagement diminished post-investment. Findings suggest sustainability depends on enforcement, ownership, and embedded financing and accountability mechanisms, not just policy presence.InterpretationSustainable mental health integration requires more than scaling clinical protocols; it demands structural alignment across policy, leadership, financing, and community domains. The MeHPriC case underscores the importance of treating sustainability as a deliberate, systemic priority from the outset. Policymakers in LMICs must institutionalise governance and financing pathways to ensure long-term resilience.FundingThis study was funded by the UK Medical Research Council (MRC) under the Global Health Systems Research programme [Grant number: MR/T021845/1].Keywords: Mental health integration, Sustainability, Primary health care, Nigeria, Task-shifting, Implementation science

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Embase, Global Health, and African Journals Online (AJOL) for research published between 1 January 2000 and November 2025, without language restrictions. We used search terms including “mental health”, “primary health care”, “integration”, “sustainability”, “scale-up”, “task-shifting”, “governance”, and “low- and middle-income countries” (LMICs) or “sub-Saharan Africa”. We identified a substantial body of evidence, including systematic reviews and randomised trials, demonstrating that task-shifting models (such as WHO's mhGAP) effectively improve detection and clinical outcomes in the short term. However, the quality of evidence regarding long-term sustainability remains limited, with most studies being short-term pilot evaluations prone to reporting bias. While recent governance-focused research (for example, outputs from the EMERALD consortium) has documented systemic barriers such as weak accountability mechanisms and fragmented coordination, there remains a paucity of empirical work systematically analysing the political, bureaucratic, and financial mechanisms that drive programme erosion in scaled initiatives within African megacity contexts. Current evidence focuses predominantly on service delivery metrics and short-term effectiveness, with insufficient attention to how upstream governance structures and budget execution processes influence the durability of mental health integration.

Added value of this study

This study moves beyond the evaluation of clinical outcomes to provide a systems-level analysis of the Mental Health in Primary Care (MeHPriC) project, a scaled, state-led initiative in Lagos, Nigeria. By triangulating data from a systematic review of policy documents, budgets, and legislation with in-depth interviews of state policymakers and programme managers, we document the specific mechanisms of programme erosion that occur after donor funding declines. Uniquely, this study empirically maps the “implementation gap” between “symbolic policy”, characterised by the successful passage of laws and creation of budget lines, and the “operational reality” of weak enforcement, inconsistent budget execution, and supervision collapse. It identifies how reliance on personalised political champions and the failure to institutionalise community governance created a “sustainability decay loop”, where workforce attrition and reduced service quality mutually reinforced political deprioritisation. We demonstrate that, without institutionalised stewardship, donor-driven gains are rapidly eroded by systemic fragility rather than technical failure.

Implications of all the available evidence

Sustainable integration of mental health into primary care in LMICs cannot be secured through legislation, clinical training, or time-bound donor pilots alone. Evidence from this study suggests that sustainability must be treated as an upstream design principle involving specific bureaucratic reforms rather than ad-hoc advocacy. Crucially, this requires the creation of permanent budget execution pathways to ensure allocated funds are released, the institutionalisation of supervision independent of donor cycles, and the establishment of career progression structures to retain the primary care workforce. Furthermore, the findings imply that without formalised community co-production and accountability mechanisms, integration initiatives lack the resilience to withstand political transitions and fiscal shocks. Policymakers must shift focus from implementation science to systems transformation, ensuring mental health is structurally embedded in the political economy of the health system to prevent the recurrence of the pilot-to-decay cycle.

Introduction

Mental and behavioral disorders account for approximately 14% of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide, with Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bearing nearly 20% of this burden despite profound underinvestment in mental health infrastructure and services.1,2 This burden encompasses both severe mental illnesses (such as psychotic disorders and severe mood disorders) and common mental health conditions (including depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders).3 In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), over 85% of individuals with severe mental illness receive no formal care, while treatment gaps for common mental disorders often exceed 90%.4,5 Although often attributed to human resource limitations, this care deficit reflects broader structural exclusions of mental health from national health priorities, financing systems, and policy frameworks.6

To address these gaps, the World Health Organization (WHO) has consistently advocated for integrating mental health services into primary health care (PHC). Through its Mental Health Gap Action Programme (mhGAP), WHO promotes task-shifting and task-sharing approaches that equip non-specialist providers in PHC settings to identify and manage common mental, neurological, and substance use disorders.7 Task-shifting involves delegating clinical responsibilities from specialists to trained non-specialists, whereas task-sharing emphasizes collaborative care models in which different cadres work together within their competencies.8,9 Although both strategies aim to expand service access in resource-constrained settings, task-sharing has emerged as the more sustainable approach, fostering mutual learning and reducing hierarchical dependencies.9 By embedding mental health services into routine care delivery, integration strategies aim to increase access, reduce stigma, and foster more sustainable models of care.

However, despite its promise, this model has faced considerable implementation challenges. Systematic reviews of mental health integration in LMICs document recurrent barriers including inadequate supervision of task-shifted workers, insufficient mental health training in pre-service education, weak referral linkages between primary and specialist care, and service discontinuation when external funding ends.10,11 Evidence from Ethiopia, South Africa, Uganda, and India reveals a consistent pattern: pilot-phase effectiveness often diminishes during scale-up as supervision weakens, trained staff turnover increases, and political attention shifts to other priorities.10,12 Two key phenomena signal this fragility: “voltage drop,” referring to diminished effectiveness of interventions in routine settings, and “programme drift,” describing gradual departure from standardized protocols.13 These challenges are not merely technical but reflect structural deficits in governance, inconsistent financing, limited institutional commitment, and inadequate accountability mechanisms.14

The MeHPriC initiative must be understood within the broader trajectory of Global Mental Health (GMH) advocacy and investment that gathered momentum in the early 2010s. Following influential calls for mental health system strengthening in LMICs, notably the Lancet Series on Global Mental Health (2007, 2011) and WHO's Mental Health Action Plan (2013–2020), donor investments and policy attention to mental health integration increased substantially in sub-Saharan Africa.15,16 Major research consortia including PRIME (Programme for Improving Mental Health Care) and EMERALD (Emerging Mental Health Systems in Low- and Middle-Income Countries) piloted integration models across multiple African countries, demonstrating both feasibility and early effectiveness.6,12 Lagos State's adoption of task-shifting approaches and PHC integration reflected this international momentum, adapting global frameworks to local governance structures. Yet as Patel and colleagues observed, the GMH movement has struggled to address the “implementation cliff”: the gap between demonstrating effectiveness in controlled settings and achieving sustained integration within routine government systems.16

Recognition has grown in recent years that policy frameworks and governance structures fundamentally shape implementation outcomes in mental health integration. Important contributions from the EMERALD consortium and related governance studies have documented systemic barriers including weak accountability mechanisms, fragmented coordination across administrative levels, and limited political prioritization.17, 18, 19 These studies established that governance challenges extend beyond technical implementation to encompass institutional power dynamics, resource allocation processes, and bureaucratic structures. However, significant gaps remain in understanding how these governance dynamics unfold during the critical post-implementation phase when donor support diminishes and programmes transition to government stewardship. Specifically, we lack detailed empirical accounts of the mechanisms through which formal policy commitments like legislation, budgetary allocations, task-shifting mandates, either translate into sustained operational practice or erode into symbolic gestures disconnected from facility-level realities. The disconnect between “policy-as-written” and “policy-as-practiced” in scaled integration initiatives remains insufficiently examined, particularly regarding how political economy shapes the implementation-to-sustainability transition.20

Lagos State, Nigeria, provides a particularly salient setting in which to examine these issues. With over 20 million residents, Lagos exemplifies the demographic and infrastructural pressures confronting African megacities: rapid urbanization straining health system capacity, population density concentrating mental health burden while fragmenting social support structures, complex multi-level governance involving state and local government authorities with often unclear jurisdictional boundaries, and large urban-poor populations with limited formal health insurance coverage.21,22 These conditions create both urgency for mental health service expansion and formidable implementation challenges.

In response to growing mental health burden, Lagos State launched the Mental Health in Primary Care (MeHPriC) project in 2013, initially across 10 PHCs with a focus on mobile-supported depression management, expanding to 57 flagship PHCs by 2017.23,24 MeHPriC focused specifically on common mental health conditions (primarily depression, anxiety disorders, psychosis, epilepsy, and substance use disorders), that could be effectively managed at PHC level using the mhGAP intervention guidelines.7 This strategic focus reflected both the high prevalence of these conditions and the feasibility of training non-specialist health workers to deliver evidence-based care. The intervention employed a hybrid task-shifting/task-sharing model, supported by enabling policies including a dedicated mental health desk within the State Ministry of Health, a State Mental Health Advisory Committee, and a comprehensive state mental health law. However, sustainability challenges have begun to emerge. Recent evaluations document declining protocol adherence at facility level, deterioration of supervision systems, and persistent gaps in budget execution.25 These patterns mirror documented experiences across similar LMIC mental health integration initiatives, where initial gains eroded through voltage drop and programme drift.13,26

In this context, questions arise regarding sustainability's structural underpinnings: Have policy commitments translated into institutionalized practices? Are task-shifting mandates operationally embedded or reliant on transient leadership? Do budget allocations reflect genuine prioritization? This study adopts a systems perspective to interrogate the governance and policy environment in which mental health integration occurs. This study aims to qualitatively map the policy and governance context influencing the sustainability of the MeHPriC project in Lagos State. By triangulating policy documents and stakeholder interviews, we develop pathways linking formal policy instruments to sustainable outcomes, advancing implementation science in LMIC contexts.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a qualitative case study design, constituting the first phase of a broader mixed-methods feasibility project examining the sustainability of mental health integration in Lagos State, Nigeria.27 The case study approach was selected for its capacity to examine complex, context-dependent phenomena in depth, particularly where boundaries between the phenomenon of interest (mental health integration sustainability) and its context (Lagos State health system and governance structures) are not clearly evident.27 This initial phase focused on mapping the policy and governance architecture underpinning the MeHPriC project, a flagship initiative that aimed to scale up mental health services across PHCs using task-shifting and the mhGAP intervention guidelines.7 The qualitative design was deliberately chosen to explore the complex, non-linear, and often opaque relationships between policy intent, institutional dynamics, and sustainability outcomes.

The study was conducted in Lagos State, the most populous and urbanised subnational unit in Nigeria, with strong political commitment and programmatic ambition for mental health making the state an analytically rich context for examining the interface between formal policy mechanisms and programme sustainability.

Theoretical framework

The study was informed by two complementary frameworks: the Integrated Sustainability Framework (ISF) and the Supporting the Use of Research Evidence (SURE) framework (Fig. 1). ISF conceptualizes sustainability as emergent from multiple domains: outer context (political commitment, socio-legal conditions), inner context (organizational leadership, culture), intervention characteristics, and implementation processes.13 SURE identifies barriers and facilitators across recipients, providers, stakeholders, health system constraints, and socio-political factors.28 Together, these frameworks enabled multi-level exploration of how policy decisions and governance structures influence MeHPriC durability.

Study components

The study employed two interconnected components: (1) a systematic document review and (2) semi-structured in-depth interviews with purposively selected stakeholders, designed to generate complementary insights.

Document review

The document review encompassed policy, legislative, administrative, and programmatic materials related to mental health governance in Lagos State (2012–2022). Documents were obtained through three purposive routes: formal requests to Lagos State Ministry of Health for mental health policy documents, budgets, and administrative circulars; access to MeHPriC programme office materials including training manuals, supervision protocols, and monitoring reports; and systematic searches of publicly accessible repositories including Lagos State Official Gazette, Federal Ministry of Health policy database, and WHO publications using search terms: “Lagos mental health,” “MeHPriC,” “primary health care integration,” and “task-shifting Nigeria.”

Two researchers (BO, OEO) independently screened documents using pre-defined inclusion criteria: published within 2012–2022, directly pertaining to mental health service delivery or PHC integration in Lagos State, and originating from recognized institutional actors. Initial screening yielded 47 documents; after applying criteria and removing duplicates, 28 documents were retained for analysis Key documents included: (1) National instruments: Nigeria's National Mental Health Policy,29 Nigeria WHO-AIMS report,30 and federal task-shifting guidelines31; (2) State-level frameworks: Lagos State Mental Health Policy,32 Lagos State Mental Health Law,33 and Lagos State Task-Shifting Policy34; (3) Budgetary documents: Lagos State Ministry of Health annual budgets (2017–2021)35 and budget execution reports; and (4) Programme materials: MeHPriC intervention manuals, training guides, supervision protocols, and monitoring frameworks.

A thematic content analysis was conducted using hybrid coding combining deductive codes from ISF and SURE frameworks (e.g., “task-shifting policy,” “funding mechanisms,” “governance structures”) with inductive codes emerging through close reading (e.g., “symbolic policy,” “informal coordination,” “implementation gaps”). Two researchers independently reviewed all documents, applying a common coding template. Key data were extracted into summary tables, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions. See Supplementary Appendix 1 for detailed document sampling methodology.

Participant sampling and interviews

We conducted 15 semi-structured in-depth interviews with purposively selected stakeholders who had direct involvement in mental health policy development or MeHPriC implementation. Participants were identified through a structured stakeholder mapping exercise proceeding in three steps: (1) reviewing MeHPriC programme documentation and organizational charts to identify formal roles with direct involvement in mental health governance or programme implementation; (2) consulting with the MeHPriC steering committee to identify additional stakeholders who played significant roles in policy development, resource allocation, or operational oversight; (3) mapping stakeholders into two primary strata: policymakers (n = 10) and programme managers (n = 5), to ensure representation of both strategic decision-making and operational implementation perspectives.

The sample was stratified into: (1) State policymakers (n = 10): senior officials from Lagos State Ministry of Health, Mental Health Advisory Committee members, health budget planners, and legal experts involved in drafting the state mental health law; and (2) Programme managers (n = 5): MeHPriC project coordinators, PHC supervisors, and clinical leads responsible for task-shifting oversight, training, and service delivery at facility level. Eligibility criteria included at least one year of involvement in mental health governance or programme coordination in Lagos. Snowball sampling identified additional informants with specialized knowledge. Table 1 presents complete participant characteristics. The participants are majorly from the Yoruba ethnic group in Nigeria.

Interviews were conducted September–November 2022 by two trained researchers (AOA, BO) with expertise in health systems and mental health, lasting 60–90 min, in private offices or via secure online platforms. All interviews were audio-recorded with informed consent, transcribed verbatim, and translated when required. Transcripts were anonymized with alphanumeric codes.

The interview guide was theory-informed, drawing from ISF and SURE domains, exploring six key areas: (1) policy development and prioritisation, (2) task-shifting implementation, (3) institutional leadership and coordination, (4) financing and budget execution, (5) intersectoral collaboration, and (6) challenges and enablers of sustainability. The interview guide was piloted with three participants not included in the final sample: one recently retired Ministry of Health official with mental health policy experience, one current PHC manager not involved in MeHPriC, and one mental health advocacy NGO representative. Based on pilot feedback, three key refinements were made: (1) simplified terminology around “governance” and “sustainability” to avoid academic jargon; (2) reordered questions to begin with participants' professional trajectories before moving to analytical questions; (3) added specific probes about budget processes and workforce retention after pilot participants indicated these were critical issues. See Supplementary Appendix 1 for pilot process details and full interview guide.

Data analysis and interpretation

Interview transcripts and policy documents were subjected to reflexive thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's approach,36,37 tailored for health systems research contexts.38 Analysis proceeded through six overlapping phases:

Phase 1: familiarisation

The research team (AOA, BO, SA) read all transcripts and key policy documents multiple times, noting initial observations in analytic memos.

Phase 2: generating codes

We developed hybrid coding combining deductive codes from ISF and SURE domains (e.g., “leadership,” “financing,” “political will”) with inductive codes emerging from participants' narratives. Two researchers (SA, OEO) independently coded five transcripts using NVivo 12, then met to compare coding decisions and refine definitions. Inductive codes proved crucial for capturing concepts not fully described by a priori frameworks, such as “symbolic policy” (policies existing on paper but not in practice) and “resource precarity” (perception of funding instability).

Phase 3: generating themes

Codes were systematically clustered into potential themes using data matrices examining patterns within and across stakeholder groups (policymakers vs. programme managers). This comparison was generative; for example, policymakers often cited “political commitment” as an achievement while programme managers highlighted “budget execution failures,” leading to the “symbolic policy vs. operational reality” theme.

Phase 4: reviewing themes

Each potential theme was tested against raw data in multiple review rounds. Thematic maps were created to visualize relationships between themes. We actively searched for negative cases or data contradicting emerging themes. For instance, accounts of two facilities that sustained supervision were explored, revealing success was due to informal personal relationships, which reinforced the theme of “weak institutionalised procedures.”

Phase 5: defining themes

Final themes were defined through detailed descriptions capturing their scope and nuance, representing core patterned “stories” about sustainability present in the data.

Phase 6: triangulation

Interview-derived themes were systematically triangulated with documentary evidence. Timeline diagrams mapped when policies were enacted against when participants described their effects. Budget utilization reports showing 67–78% absorption rates corroborated participants' accounts of execution failures; policy enactment dates from official gazettes mapped against participants' descriptions confirmed delayed facility-level awareness; organizational charts showed formal supervision structures that participants described as non-functional.

Ensuring analytical rigour

Several strategies enhanced trustworthiness of the analysis.

Credibility

Data triangulation (interviews and documents) and analyst triangulation (AOA, BO, SA, JA, OEO). Member checking with three participants (two policymakers, one programme manager) confirmed core interpretations while adding important nuances.

Dependability

Comprehensive audit trail maintained including evolving codebook, 47 analytic memos, thematic maps, and decision logs.

Transferability

We provide thick description (defined following Geertz39 as detailed, contextually rich accounts that convey not merely observable behaviors but the structures of meaning and interpretation surrounding them), of the Lagos State context, the MeHPriC project, and stakeholder perspectives.39,40 Rather than claiming statistical generalizability, thick description enables analytical transferability: readers can evaluate which elements of our findings resonate with their contexts and which may require adaptation.

Reflexivity

The team combined insider knowledge of MeHPriC (AOA, BO, OEO) with external expertise in implementation science and health systems (SA, JA). We maintained reflexive journals to document assumptions and potential biases. For example, as researchers involved in MeHPriC's design, AOA, BO and OEO had to consciously challenge assumptions about the programme's successes and remain open to critical feedback. This insider/outsider perspective was a strength, allowing access to key stakeholders while using external lenses to maintain analytical distance. See Supplementary Appendix 2 for detailed reflexivity procedures. This study's reporting adheres to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Supplementary Appendix 3).

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Lagos State University Teaching Hospital Health Research Ethics Committee (LREC/06/10/1443, approved 15 March 2022). All participants provided written informed consent after receiving detailed information about the study's purpose, data use, confidentiality safeguards, and their right to withdraw at any point. Separate consent was obtained for audio-recording. To ensure confidentiality, all transcripts were de-identified, and audio files were stored in encrypted, password-protected systems accessible only to authorised team members. Data protection protocols adhered to international standards and local regulatory frameworks, including the Declaration of Helsinki and Nigerian guidelines for health research.

Theme 1: policy architecture: the gap between symbolic commitment and operational reality

Formal frameworks and their promise

Documentary analysis confirmed Lagos State's robust formal commitment to mental health integration (Table 4). The Nigeria National Mental Health Policy (2013) explicitly mandates mental health integration into PHC.29 This directive found concrete expression in Lagos through the State Mental Health Policy (2015),32 which identifies task-shifting as a core implementation strategy. The Lagos State Mental Health Law (2018)33 provides legal backing for these commitments, establishing rights to community-based mental health care. Task-shifting received further legitimation through the Lagos State Task-Shifting/Task-Sharing Policy (2017),34 which explicitly authorised nurses and community health officers to provide mental health services following structured training. MeHPriC's clinical protocols and manuals aligned closely with these policy provisions.

The lived experience of policy implementation

Yet interviews revealed that this policy architecture existed largely as aspiration rather than operational guidance for many implementers. When asked about the mental health law, a facility supervisor with four years' involvement in MeHPriC responded:

“I've heard about the policy, but we've never seen a copy here. Everything we do is from the training manual. Maybe the big offices have it, but at the health centre level, we don't see those documents. What we know is what the supervisors taught us during training, and we keep referring to the manual when we're not sure what to do with a patient.”

(Programme Manager #04)

This disconnect between policy existence and policy awareness was recurrent. A programme manager elaborated on how policy documents functioned, or failed to function, in practice:

“The policies are there, yes. Sometimes when we have meetings at the Ministry, they mention the mental health law … But here's the problem: these documents don't come with implementation guides. It's one thing to say 'mental health should be integrated'. It's another thing entirely to tell us how to do it when we don't have drugs, when the trained nurse gets transferred, when the supervisor hasn't come in six months. The policy doesn't address those realities. So we improvise.”

(Programme Manager #02)

This improvisation, or “informal workaround,” emerged as a critical finding, suggesting day-to-day services were sustained less by formal policy guidance than by individual initiative and informal problem-solving in the face of systemic gaps.

Symbolic policy adoption and its consequences

This gap points toward what several policymakers themselves acknowledged as “symbolic policy adoption.” A senior Ministry official involved in drafting the mental health law offered a candid reflection:

“I'll be honest with you … part of it was genuinely about creating a framework for better services. But part of it was also political. Mental health had become a talking point internationally, and Lagos wants to be seen as progressive. So we got the law passed, and that was a victory. But passing a law and implementing a law are very different things. Implementation requires resources … Those are harder fights, and they're less visible … So the law exists, and that's important symbolically, but its operational impact has been limited.”

(Policymaker #09)

Documentary analysis revealed this disconnect: while policies articulated mental health as a priority, budget execution data showed consistent deprioritization, with mental health losing in resource competition during fiscal constraints.

Theme 2: leadership, authority, and the fragility of political will

Champions and their catalytic role

Political leadership emerged as perhaps the most consistently cited enabler of MeHPriC's initial expansion. Across stakeholder groups, participants credited specific individuals, particularly the State Commissioner for Health during MeHPriC's scale-up phase, with creating space for mental health within a crowded policy agenda. A policymaker involved in programme design reflected:

“The Commissioner was a true champion … He personally attended some of the early training sessions, he brought it up in cabinet meetings, he made sure we got budget allocations. Without that top-level support, MeHPriC would never have expanded beyond a small pilot.”

(Policymaker #03)

The mental health desk: focal point or symbolic gesture?

The establishment of a dedicated mental health desk within the Ministry of Health represented another manifestation of political commitment. Yet interviews revealed ambivalence about whether this structure possessed sufficient authority to drive sustainable integration. A policymaker coordinator offered a nuanced assessment:

“On paper, the mental health desk coordinates all mental health activities in the state. In practice, the authority of the mental health desk officer is limited. He don't control budgets, He can't hire or transfer staff … He coordinates, convene meetings and advocate. Those things matter, but they're not the same as having real institutional power.”

(Policymaker #01)

Multiple participants noted that the desk officer role, while symbolically important, overestimated what a single position could accomplish without authority to compel action across PHC boards, local government departments, and budget offices, resulting in fragmented implementation.

When champions leave: the vulnerability of personalised leadership

The most striking theme in leadership discussions concerned the fragility of political commitment tied to individual champions rather than institutionalised structures. Multiple participants described how MeHPriC's momentum shifted when key leaders transitioned out of their roles. A programme manager offered a vivid account:

“When the Permanent Secretary who championed MeHPriC moved on, things changed almost immediately. Not that the new PS (Permanent Secretary) was against mental health, but it wasn't his passion project and COVID came … Suddenly, meetings got cancelled … budget releases that used to happen relatively smoothly became more difficult, the sense of urgency diminished. It made us realise how much we'd been depending on one person's commitment rather than on systemic structures that would outlast any individual.”

(Programme Manager #03)

Theme 3: financing mechanisms and their systematic failures

The paradox of growing allocations and shrinking access

Budget documents (Table 4) revealed ostensibly positive trends: absolute allocations for mental health in Lagos State increased significantly. Yet when contextualized within stakeholder narratives, this growth appeared less impressive. An official in the Ministry of Health explained:

“Yes, the mental health budget has grown … But you have to look at what that money is allocated for. The bulk of it goes to constructing a psychiatric hospital … The amount allocated specifically for PHC-level mental health integration is much smaller, maybe 25 to 30 percent of the total.”

(Policymaker #01)

Budget execution: where allocations go to die

Beyond allocation imbalances, interviews revealed that even designated funds frequently failed to reach intended programmes due to implementation bottlenecks. Budget execution reports (Table 4) corroborated this pattern, showing mental health allocations utilized at rates between 67% and 78% across fiscal years. A programme manager described the practical impact:

“It's incredibly frustrating. You plan activities based on the budget you've been allocated … Then you wait for the funds to be released … And you wait … Sometimes the release comes so late that you've missed the window for the activity....”

(Programme Manager #02)

A policymaker involved in budget administration acknowledged these systemic failures as rooted in political economy:

“The problem isn't usually bad faith. It's bureaucracy … Mental health programmes often lack the political clout to push their requests to the front of the queue. More visible priorities, like maternal health campaigns or outbreak response, get expedited. Mental health waits its turn.”

(Policymaker #01)

The double bind of donor dependency

Several participants described how external donor support, while enabling MeHPriC's initial expansion, created dependencies that undermined long-term sustainability. A programme coordinator reflected:

“In the beginning, we had donor partners who supported everything: training materials, transport for supervision … That allowed us to demonstrate what was possible … But it also meant we never developed sustainable internal systems. When donor funding tapered off, suddenly we realised we didn't have Ministry processes in place … It's like learning to walk with crutches and then having the crutches taken away before your leg has healed.”

(Programme Manager #03)

External partners had funded refresher training and supervision tools, but these were not absorbed into routine health system financing. Policymakers acknowledged that donor funding enabled rapid action by bypassing bureaucracy rather than reforming it, creating dependencies that proved unsustainable when external support ended.

Theme 4: implementation fidelity: the slow erosion of programme quality

Training: from comprehensive to sporadic

Initial MeHPriC training was described as transformative. However, this investment was not sustained. Programme documents indicated that while initial training was widespread, refresher training became increasingly irregular after 2018, eventually ceasing as funding tightened and other priorities emerged.

Supervision: the collapse of accountability mechanisms

Supervision represented a critical pillar in MeHPriC's design, intended to ensure protocol fidelity and provide mentorship. Programme documents from MeHPriC's pilot phase (2013–2015) showed structured supervision: monthly facility visits by trained supervisors, case reviews, skills refreshers, and feedback loops (Table 4). By 2020, however, this system had largely eroded, with data indicating only 60% of facilities received any supervision visit that year. A facility coordinator described the experience:

“For the first two years, supervisors came regularly … They would review our patient files, observe consultations, give feedback … But then the visits became less frequent. Three months … six months … Last year, we had only one supervision visit … When you're on your own like that, you lose confidence.”

(Programme Manager #05)

A policymaker reflected that even when resourced, supervision lacked accountability mechanisms; if supervisors didn't visit facilities, there were no consequences, creating a structure without systemic enforcement.

Workforce turnover: the exodus of institutional knowledge

Perhaps no theme emerged as powerfully as the devastating impact of health workforce turnover. This manifested as emigration of trained workers (“brain drain”), internal transfers, and retirements without systematic knowledge transfer. A programme manager provided quantitative sense of scale:

“I recently did an informal audit … Out of 87 people [trained in 2013–2015] I could track … only 31 are still working in the same facilities … That's less than 40%. About 25 have emigrated, mostly to the UK, Canada … We invested heavily in training these people, and the majority of that investment has walked out the door.”

(Programme Manager #02)

A facility supervisor explained the impact:

“When we lose a trained staff member, the replacement usually has no mental health background … I've seen facilities where three different nurses have rotated through the mental health role in as many years, each one learning from scratch, each one leaving before they've really become competent. That's not a sustainable model.”

(Programme Manager #05)

Policymakers acknowledged that the task-shifting design failed to anticipate workforce mobility or build retention incentives and career pathways, leaving staff with no professional advancement in mental health roles.

Protocol drift: from standardisation to improvisation

The erosion of training, supervision, and workforce stability manifested clinically as “protocol drift.” A programme coordinator described observing this:

“What I saw concerned me. The health worker … wasn't following the protocols. She was screening for depression using her own set of questions rather than the PHQ-9 … When I asked her about it … she said, 'I don't remember exactly what the training said, so I do what makes sense … ' That's dangerous. Protocols exist for a reason.”

(Programme Manager #03)

Yet some participants framed this drift not as deviation but as “necessary adaptation” to resource constraints. A PHC nurse offered a defence of flexibility:

“The protocols assume you have all the medications available … patients can come back every two weeks … time for lengthy consultations. In reality, we're often missing medications, patients can't afford frequent visits … So yes, I adapt … Am I following the protocol exactly? No. Am I abandoning patients? Also no. I'm doing what's possible with what I have. Maybe that's drift, but it's drift toward reality.”

(PHC Nurse, cited by Programme Manager #05)

Theme 5: community engagement: from active partnership to passive service delivery

Early mobilisation and its impact

MeHPriC's initial phase included deliberate community engagement strategies to reduce stigma and generate demand. A policymaker who coordinated these efforts described the approach:

“We understood that you can't just put mental health services in a health centre and expect people to show up … We partnered with religious leaders including pastors, imams … We organised community health days … That groundwork was essential.”

(Policymaker #06)

A civil society representative confirmed the “real partnership” of this early phase:

“In the beginning … They asked us, 'What do communities need? What are the barriers?' … We had input. We felt ownership. Community health committees were given roles in monitoring service quality … That partnership approach made a huge difference.”

(Civil Society Representative, MHAC)

The withering of community voice

However, this active engagement proved unsustainable as donor support declined. A programme coordinator acknowledged the decline:

“Community engagement was one of the first things to suffer when resources got tight … When you're making difficult choices … maintaining clinical services takes precedence over community outreach. You can't do mental health care without clinicians and medications. You can, apparently, do it without active community engagement, at least in the short term.”

(Programme Manager #02)

Programme documents showed community engagement line items present in donor-funded project budgets (2013–2017) but absent from state budgets thereafter (Table 4). Community representatives described becoming “passive observers” rather than “active partners,” with no channels to raise service concerns after initial engagement structures were dismantled.

A policymaker identified this as a critical missed opportunity for building “community-based sustainability”:

“One of our biggest strategic mistakes was treating community engagement as an input rather than an outcome … Communities should become co-owners and co-managers of services, creating social sustainability that outlasts donor funding or government commitment … We didn't fully seize that opportunity.”

(Policymaker #04)

Triangulation of findings across data sources

Interview-derived themes were systematically triangulated with documentary evidence, revealing consistent patterns of disconnect between formal commitments and operational realities across all five themes.

Policy architecture

While the Lagos State Mental Health Law (2018) was formally enacted and published in the Official Gazette, no accompanying implementation circulars or facility-level dissemination guidelines appeared in Ministry records. Policy enactment dates mapped against participants' timelines confirmed substantial delays between formal adoption and facility-level awareness, often exceeding 12–18 months.

Leadership

Organizational charts confirmed the mental health desk position within Ministry structure, but the desk officer's terms of reference revealed no budgetary authority, no supervisory powers, and no enforcement mechanisms; corroborating participants' descriptions of “symbolic authority” without institutional power.

Financing

Budget documents provided striking triangulation. Lagos State health budgets (2017–2021) showed mental health allocations totaling ₦487 million. However, Budget execution reports revealed utilization rates of the mental health allocations of the Lagos State health budgets (2017–2021) at 71%, with a systematic 29% execution gap. Furthermore, 73% of allocations were designated for psychiatric hospital infrastructure, with only 27% for PHC-level integration: corroborating participants' “bulk funding to tertiary” accounts.

Implementation fidelity

Programme documents confirmed systematic erosion. Supervision logs showed facilities receiving average 8.3 visits annually (2014–2015) declining to 1.4 visits (2020–2021): an 83% reduction. Training records documented initial training of 412 health workers (2013–2016), but refresher training records showed only 37 participants (2018), 12 (2019), and zero (2020–2021). Workforce tracking confirmed “brain drain”: only 156 of 412 initially trained staff (38%) remained in the same facilities by 2022, with documented emigration (23%), transfers (22%), and resignations (18%).

Community engagement

Programme budgets revealed donor-funded allocations of ₦18.2 million annually for community mobilization (2013–2017, document #26), vs. zero dedicated state budget line items post-2018. Community health committee meetings declined from 47 (2014) to 2 (2018–2021 combined).

Cross-cutting pattern

Triangulation revealed consistent temporal erosion across all domains post-2018 when programmes transitioned from donor support to government stewardship, providing documentary validation for participants' “sustainability decay loop” characterization.

Systemic interdependencies: the sustainability decay loop

Participants emphasized that these themes were not isolated but formed interconnected systems where failures amplified one another. A senior policymaker with comprehensive programme oversight synthesized the dynamic:

“It's not one thing that failed … it's a vicious cycle. Budget execution failures meant supervision couldn't happen. No supervision meant quality declined. Declining quality demoralized staff, accelerating turnover. Turnover further eroded quality. Communities noticed, stopped coming. Lower utilization made mental health easier to deprioritize, restarting the cycle.”

(Policymaker #06)

Discussion

The experience of the MeHPriC project provides a granular case study of the complexities facing mental health integration in LMIC settings. Our findings move beyond a simple list of barriers to suggest that sustainability is not a final stage, but rather a dynamic process contingent on the resilience of health systems. The data reveal a programme caught between ambitious formal policy and fragile operational reality, where sustainability was ultimately undermined by systemic, interconnected failures in governance, financing, and workforce structures.

Sustainability as systemic resilience, not just longevity

Our findings challenge conventional framings of sustainability as mere programme continuation. MeHPriC achieved several markers of conventional success: it scaled beyond a pilot, formal policies were enacted, and services continue to be offered. Yet stakeholders consistently described the programme as precarious, with “sense of urgency diminished” and services maintained through “informal workarounds.” This paradox suggests sustainability is better conceptualized as systemic resilience: the capacity of health systems to absorb shocks, adapt to changing conditions, and maintain core functions.41

MeHPriC's design, which participants noted was optimized for rapid scale-up via donor support, proved to have low resilience. The programme was vulnerable to predictable shocks: when external funding ended, core functions like supervision and training, which had not been institutionalized, collapsed.42,43 Similarly, the system's reliance on personalized leadership from individual champions made it fragile, unable to absorb the shock of routine political transitions.44 This aligns with Shelton et al.'s distinction between “sustainability as maintenance” (keeping programmes alive) and “sustainability as evolution” (maintaining core functions and quality).13 MeHPriC achieved the former but not the latter.

Power, authority, and symbolic policy: the political economy of prioritisation

A central contribution of this study is empirical documentation of the gap between policy-as-written and policy-as-practiced. Our findings align with theories of symbolic politics, which posit that policies can be enacted primarily for their signaling function.45 As one policymaker candidly stated, passing the mental health law was “political” and made Lagos appear “progressive,” but its “operational impact has been limited.” The law functioned as symbolic achievement to satisfy international advocacy and political narratives, but was not equipped with enforcement mechanisms or implementation resources to drive change at facility level.46 This “symbolic implementation” has profound consequences: it creates a facade of progress while leaving underlying structural barriers like fragmented governance, misaligned budgets, and weak accountability, intact.

The structural translation gap

The disconnect between policy documents and facility-level awareness points to a fundamental challenge in how policies are operationalized across governmental levels in Nigeria's federal system. State-level policies require active translation into implementation guidelines, budget allocations, and administrative directives by local government health departments and PHC boards: a process depending on political prioritization, bureaucratic capacity, and inter-governmental coordination. As one manager observed, policies “don't come with implementation guides,” reflecting a governance gap wherein policy formulation at state level is not systematically accompanied by operational tools needed for facility-level enactment.

This reflects what decentralization scholars term “unfunded mandates”: higher government levels create policy directives without ensuring lower levels possess resources, authority, or technical guidance to implement them.47,48 In Nigeria's health system, where PHCs fall under local government administration while state ministries formulate policy, this structural disjuncture is acute. Without explicit mandates for policy dissemination, training of facility managers in policy interpretation, and accountability mechanisms for non-compliance, even well-crafted policies risk remaining “beautiful documents” that “sit on shelves.” Sustainable integration thus requires not merely policy enactment but institutional mechanisms ensuring policies cascade through governance levels with accompanying resources and accountability.

Leadership as double-edged sword: catalysis and risk

The prominence of individual leadership in MeHPriC's trajectory reveals a central paradox. Stakeholders consistently emphasized that personal support of senior government officials catalyzed the programme's expansion, enabling it to circumvent entrenched administrative inertia. This reflects wider evidence that charismatic or adaptive leaders can trigger rapid policy uptake and mobilize collective action during periods of uncertainty.49 Yet the same personalized authority carried significant systemic risk. As one manager observed, the programme's momentum “changed almost immediately” after champions exited, illustrating vulnerability of reforms that depend on individual agency rather than institutionalized stewardship.

These findings suggest that although strong leadership is widely recognized as an enabler of health system change, its benefits may be short-lived when it substitutes for, rather than strengthens, organizational processes.50,51 Sustainable leadership demands more than advocacy; it requires embedding programme functions into routine bureaucratic mechanisms so they persist independently of any single actor. MeHPriC's trajectory illustrates failure to make this critical shift from charismatic mobilization to institutional consolidation.

Financing, fidelity, and workforce: the political economy of capacity

Our findings reveal that operational capacity is not a neutral technical issue; it is a product of political economy.20 The financing problem was not merely one of allocation, but of execution and priority. The “allocation-absorption gap,” where only 71% of funds were utilized on average, represents systemic failure. As one policymaker explained, this is not just bureaucracy; it is about “political clout.” Programmes for “more visible priorities” get funds expedited, while mental health “waits its turn.”

The devastating workforce turnover (“brain drain”) was not just an economic phenomenon. It was, as a policymaker noted, failure to create “career progression pathways.” Mental health, especially in PHC, is seen as low “prestige” and a potential “career dead-end.”52 The task-shifting policy, while clinically innovative, failed because it did not address underlying professional and social hierarchies that devalue mental health work. Participants described “protocol drift” that is better understood through sustainment-related modifications literature. Faced with chronic constraints, frontline workers adapted protocols in ways that one nurse termed “drift toward reality.” Such adaptations are not inherently problematic; flexibility is essential when programmes encounter complex real-world contexts.53 The core failure lay in the “collapse of accountability mechanisms,” particularly supervision, which is the critical institutional space for distinguishing harmful drift from necessary adaptation.54

Community engagement: from co-production to passive service delivery

The erosion of community engagement represents a strategic failure with long-term consequences for sustainability. One policymaker characterized the problem as “treating community engagement as an input rather than an outcome,” underscoring profound misalignment between programme intentions and implementation. In its early phase, MeHPriC adopted a model of “real partnership” in which community members “felt ownership,” consistent with participatory development traditions that view co-production and local stewardship as essential drivers of social sustainability.55,56

As donor funds diminished, however, community engagement became one of the “first things to suffer,” as respondents put it, shifting the role of communities from “active partners” to “passive observers.” This transition, often rationalized by managers as necessary to prioritize “clinical services,” reveals an enduring biomedical bias that treats community mobilization as peripheral rather than intrinsic to effective and sustainable mental health systems. Research on health system governance consistently shows that when community structures are sidelined, accountability weakens and programmes lose relational infrastructure needed to weather political or financial shocks.57

Why sustainable mechanisms were not maintained

The question of why sustainable, embedded mechanisms for community involvement were not maintained despite their evident value points to a critical design failure. Several low-cost, potentially sustainable approaches existed but were not institutionalized: formalizing community health committees with explicit mental health monitoring roles and voting authority in facility governance; training community health volunteers as peer navigators who could sustain demand generation without requiring external funding; and establishing community scorecards or participatory monitoring mechanisms maintaining accountability even as programme staff changed.

The failure to institutionalize such mechanisms reflects a broader conceptual limitation: MeHPriC treated community engagement primarily as an input (mobilization activities to facilitate programme uptake) rather than as a governance structure that could anchor sustainability. This framing led to community activities being budgeted as “soft” expenditures that could be cut when resources tightened, rather than as core institutional infrastructure. Had community governance platforms been established with formal authority, for instance, community health committees with mandated representation in PHC board meetings and authority to escalate service complaints, they might have created enduring accountability mechanisms less dependent on programme budgets. This represents not merely a resource constraint but a missed opportunity to build the “social infrastructure” of sustainability that could have outlasted donor funding and political transitions.58,59

Systemic interdependencies and cascade failures

A central insight is that sustainability did not collapse due to a single discrete weakness but through interaction of multiple vulnerabilities that reinforced one another in what respondents described as a “vicious cycle.” These themes function as a network of interdependent failures in which breakdown of one subsystem amplifies fragility in another. This dynamic reflects complex systems theory, which emphasizes that health systems experience cascade failures when interlinked components deteriorate simultaneously, producing non-linear declines in performance.60,61

Our analysis proposes a “sustainability decay loop” (Fig. 2): political marginalization of mental health contributes to chronic failures in budget execution, a well-documented pattern in low-visibility health priorities.62 These fiscal gaps defund critical supervisory and training structures, removing institutional scaffolding needed to sustain clinical quality. The absence of supportive supervision and credible career pathways accelerates workforce attrition, echoing broader evidence that weak organizational support fuels turnover in low-resource health systems.63 As trained staff leave, programme fidelity erodes, generating protocol drift and declining service quality. Reduced quality undermines community trust and service uptake, which feeds back into political perceptions that mental health does not command public demand, reinforcing budgetary deprioritization and completing the cycle.

Similar dynamics have been documented in Ethiopia's PRIME district, South Africa's EMERALD sites, and Uganda's task-shifting programmes, suggesting these are systemic LMIC patterns rather than Lagos-specific phenomena.10,17 However, Lagos's urban context created distinct workforce mobility challenges: trained staff had abundant emigration opportunities and alternative employment options absent in rural settings where most integration pilots occurred. This urban “brain drain” may constitute a distinct sustainability threat requiring targeted retention strategies. Breaking such cycles requires more than single intervention points; it necessitates coordinated, multi-level strategies that simultaneously strengthen governance, financing, workforce, and community engagement.64

Based on this systems-level analysis, several implications emerge for policymakers in LMICs:

Study limitations

The focus on policymakers and programme managers excludes crucial perspectives of frontline workers, service users, and community members, which will be explored in subsequent research phases. The single-site focus limits statistical generalizability, though as a case study, transferability rests on “thick description” rather than representativeness.40 However, our findings parallel documented patterns in Ethiopia, South Africa, and Uganda where supervision erosion, workforce attrition, and budget execution failures similarly undermined sustainability, suggesting systemic rather than Lagos-specific dynamics.10,17 The cross-sectional design captures dynamics at one time point; longitudinal research could test our decay loop model's predictive validity.

Reflexivity and positionality require acknowledgment. As researchers (AOA, BO) involved in MeHPriC's design, we brought deep insider knowledge essential for access and understanding the programme's history. However, this created risk of bias. We actively managed this through analyst triangulation with external implementation scientists (SA, JA), reflexive journaling, and member checking. This process forced us to confront “uncomfortable realities” of the gap between initial ambitions and the programme's current precariousness, strengthening the analysis's critical distance and credibility.

Conclusion: from implementation science to systems transformation

The MeHPriC project in Lagos State offers sobering lessons. It demonstrates that technical feasibility, political will, and formal policy are necessary but profoundly insufficient for sustaining mental health integration. The MeHPriC case demonstrates that even programmes achieving substantial scale: operating across 57 facilities with formal policy backing and state-level governance structures, remain vulnerable to erosion when sustainability mechanisms are not institutionalized from inception. Scale and formal commitment are necessary but insufficient; what proves decisive is whether programmes are structurally grafted onto health system “core DNA” through embedded financing, institutionalized supervision, formalized community governance, and workforce retention mechanisms that outlast individual champions and donor cycles.

This study's findings, particularly the identification of “vicious cycles” and “cascade failures,” suggest that breaking this cycle requires moving from implementation science (which asks, “how do we implement this programme?”) to transformational systems science (which asks, “how must the system be re-architected for this programme to endure?”). This requires fundamental shifts: from projects to persistence, from charismatic leadership to resilient institutions, and from symbolic policy to the difficult, unglamorous work of structural reform.

The question for Lagos, and for the global mental health community, is not whether integration can work (MeHPriC proved it can), but whether we are willing to commit to the deep, systemic, and political work required to make it last. There are reasons for cautious optimism. Nigeria's National Health Act establishes mechanisms for more stable financing. Growing recognition within global mental health discourse of sustainability challenges suggests the field is learning from these failures. Yet optimism must be tempered with realism as the challenges documented are deeply rooted in political economy and bureaucratic structures resistant to rapid change. MeHPriC's story is unfinished. What we have provided is detailed documentation of how integration succeeded initially and why it struggled to endure: an evidence base that Lagos policymakers, and their counterparts across Africa, can use to write better next chapters.
