[18F]FDG WBC labelled PET/CT for the diagnosis of infectious disease: initial experience in a tertiary centre
Authors: Christel Hermann Kamani, Julien Costes, Maxime Panchaud, Kilian Casagrande, Giorgio Treglia, Mario Jreige, Marie Nicod Lalonde, Niklaus Schaefer, Judith Delage, John O Prior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLabelling WBC with [18F]FDG may be an alternative for accurate infection diagnosis, combining the excellent sensitivity of [18F]FDG with the excellent specificity of WBC-labelled SPECT/CT. We aim to investigate the feasibility, safety and accuracy of [18F]FDG-labelled WBC in patients with suspected infectious disease.ResultsScans were performed at 131 ± 13 min and 201 ± 16 min after [18F]FDG-labelled WBC administration. 14 identified lesions were follow-up. [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT yielded a sensitivity / specificity of 82% / 100%. False negative lesions were localized in axial and proximal skeleton. SUVmax@2h and SUVmax@3h for infected lesions were 10.4 ± 8.8 and 13.9 ± 12.3. aSUV and rSUV values for infected lesions were 3.5 ± 4.3 and 34.2 ± 29.1 Radiolabelling efficiency was 74.8 ± 19.4% (84.9 ± 5.34% vs. 57.9 ± 22.3%, p < 0.025 in diabetic vs. non-diabetic). The dose to the operator were 39.3 ± 11.8 µSv for the axial body and 69.1 ± 26.2 µSv for the extremities.ConclusionsAssessing the dynamic of [18F]FDG-labelled WBC is feasible and yielded a very good diagnostic performance, however with some limitation for lesion of the axial skeleton and the reticuloendothelial system. Moreover, the procedure is operator and patient safe. Improvement in shielding during radiolabelling as well as further investigations with larger population are still needed.Keywords: [18F]FDG-labelled WBC, PET/CT, Infectious disease, Diagnostic performance

Background

Infectious diseases remain one of the most frequent cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Delay in the diagnosis will results in a delay in the initiation of a specific treatment with a negative impact on the clinical course. Therefore, early and precise diagnosis and localization of the infection source as well as its extension is essential for the clinical management of the patient. Accurate localization of the infection site will guide the biopsy procedure to allow for the specific use of antibiotics after identification of the pathogen. For this purpose, anatomical imaging tools including echography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computer tomography (CT) have a significant role in many clinical settings such as in joint or soft infections [2], in native or prosthetic cardiovascular infection [3]. However, the use of these non-invasive imaging modalities can be limited in some clinical setting. Indeed, MRI is less accurate in detecting diabetic foot infection in comparison to nuclear imaging tools [4]. The sensitivity of echocardiography is limited by suspicion of prosthetic valve endocarditis or implantable cardiovascular devices due to inherent technical limitations [3]. The presence of metallic prosthesis may also altered the diagnostic performance of MRI and CT by inducing metallic artifacts [5].

Fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) is a radiolabeled glucose analogue, which enters the cell via the glucose transporters. Once entry, [18F]FDG is phosphorylated to [18F]FDG-6-phosphate by hexokinase, and trapped into the cells since it cannot be further metabolized [6]. Based on the increased glycolytic activity of the inflammatory cells involved in the infection and inflammatory processes, especially neutrophils and macrophages, [18F]FDG PET/CT has shown its utility in the diagnosis and management of various infectious and inflammatory diseases [3, 7]. However, [18F]FDG PET/CT can be limited in the differentiation between sterile inflammation and inflammation caused by infection, since the cells involved in these highlighted processes increase glycolytic avidity in the same extend [7, 8]. In this setting, 99mTc-HMPAO labelled white blood cells (WBC) scintigraphy showed an improved specificity in the diagnosis prosthetic cardiovascular or osteoarticular infections [9, 10]. However, scintigraphy imaging has limited sensitivity and spatial resolution. Labelling WBC with [18F]FDG could be seen as a promising investigation, allowing for combining the high resolution and sensitivity of PET/CT imaging with the improved specificity of WBC labelling [11]. Different methods of WBCs radiolabeling have been described [12–14] but no standardized interpretation criteria have been validated. Moreover, due to the short half-life [18F]FDG in comparison to agents of WBCs scintigraphy, new criteria interpretation based on the kinetic of the [18F]FDG uptake and the raise of the standardized uptake value (SUV) at the inflammatory site at 2 different times could be evaluated to differentiate sterile and infectious inflammation. The aim of this study was to assess the feasibility of [18F]FDG-labelled WBC for diagnosis of cardiovascular, soft tissue or osteoarticular infection, using new diagnostic criteria based on the uptake dynamic of [18F]FDG over time.

Materials and methods

Patients’ characteristics

Seven acutely ill patients hospitalized at our tertiary center were included in the study. All patients underwent an exhaustive clinical and biochemical work-up including inflammatory markers such as leucocytes subpopulations, C-reactive protein and sedimentation rate. The inclusion criteria were: (1) suspected or documented bacterial infection, (2) peripheral WBC ≥ 2G/L, (3) sedimentation rate > 5 mm, (4) no anti-inflammatory treatment 24 h before the blood collection. Exclusion criteria were: (1) pregnancy, (2) uncontrolled diabetes (glycemia > 11 mmol/L), (3) final activity of labelled WBCs < 150 MBq. Patients were asked to fast at least 12 h before the blood sample. Patients with oral antidiabetics or insulin were asked to pause the treatment for 6 h before the blood collection. The study protocol was approved by the local ethics committee (CER-VD #2018-01513) and ageneral consent form was approved by all patients participating in this study, who agreed retrospective use of their images and data for clinical research.

Blood samples handling

(Labeling, washing steps,…)

Each procedure was carried out according to a standard procedure. The entire radiolabeling process was carried out under a laminar flow with a Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) class A, placed in a class C room.

From 40 mL to 120 mL of fresh venous blood were collected from each participant in the morning of the PET scan into syringes containing 12% of citric acid (CA) for anticoagulation and mixed with 28% of hydroxymethylamidon for selective red blood cell sedimentation. 20 mL of additional blood was collected with heparin 25,000 UI/mL and centrifuged for 15 min (min) at 650 g to obtain cell-free plasma. After 45 to 90 min of sedimentation, the supernatant was collected in aseptic conditions and centrifuged for 10 min at 150 g. The cells were suspended with 0.5 mL of NaCl 0.9% and incubated with 800 ± 150 MBq of [18F]FDG during 20 min at 37 °C with gentle mix every 5 min. Free cell plasma was added to the labelled suspension to be centrifuged for 10 min at 160 g. Activities in the supernatant and the cell pellet were measured to determine the radiolabeling efficiency. Then, cells were resuspended with 3 mL of free plasma cell to be injected.

The radiolabeling yield was calculated by dividing the final activity in the cell suspension by the initial activity. The labeling efficiency was calculated by dividing the activity in the cells suspension by the total activity obtained at the end of labeling (activity in cells suspension + activity in supernatant).

Cell viability

The cell viability was determined by the trypan blue exclusion assay. 50 µL of the cell suspension were diluted into 180 µL of NaCl 0.9%. 20 µL of this solution were introduced in a vial containing 80 µL of trypan blue solution. The solution was mixed and 20 µL were loaded onto a haemocytometer. Viable and nonviable cells were then counted by microscopy.

Stability of radiolabeling

[18F]FDG release from the WBCs was evaluated at different times. 0.2 mL aliquots of the final cell suspension were kept and stored at 37 °C. The suspensions were centrifuged at 160 g during 10 min and the activity in the supernatants and cells pellets were measured at 2 h and 3 h. The [18F]FDG rates contained in the suspension were calculated at both times.

Doses to operators

The doses to operators were monitored during the whole process of radiolabeling. Measurements were performed on extremities and whole body.

Imaging protocol

The [18F]FDG-labelled WBCs were administered via a 19-gauge intravenous dwelling catheter. About 2 h and 3 h after intravenous injection of 253 ± 18 MBq [18F]FDG-labelled WBC, non-contrast whole body CT, and when indicated, non-contrast cardiac CT (n = 2) for attenuation correction were acquired, followed by acquisition of whole-body static images (from the skull to the feet) ± gated cardiac static images (n = 2; for 10 ± 1 min) on a hybrid Silicon Photomultiplier (SiPM) PET/CT scanner (60-slice Biograph Vision 600 digital hybrid PET/CT, Knoxville, TN, USA). Images were reconstructed from the list-mode acquisition using time-of-flight and point-spread function information combined with ordered-subset expectation maximization iterative reconstruction into one static image, as routinely performed in our PET laboratory.

Imaging analysis

Two experienced nuclear medicine physicians reviewed all [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT images, including images without CT attenuation correction to assess for possible artefacts. One trained medical student assessed the radiotracer activity in healthy organs of all included patients (liver, myocardium, spleen, aorta, brain, lungs, kidneys, colon vertebra and bones). [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT images were interpreted visually and semi-quantitatively. Each identified lesion was assessed in regard of the image quality (sharpness and the degree of noise of the image). For patient with suspicion of cardiac device infection, the physiological myocardial tracer uptake was assessed visually as well as semi-quantitatively, using the ratio between myocardial SUVmax of the septum wall to the SUVmean of the intraventricular cavity (normal value: less than 1). For each lesion identified, SUVmax was determined at 2 h (SUVmax@2h) and 3 h (SUVmax@3h). Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean values ± 1 standard deviation (SD) by normal distribution or median ± 25–75% Interquartile range (IQR) when distribution was not normal. Comparison between groups was performed using pairwise t-test for parametric or Wilcoxon signed rank test for non-parametric variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (version 29.0.0.1, Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, International Business Machines, Inc., Armonk, New York).

Results

Patient’s characteristics

7 males and no female were included in the study. The mean age was 66 ± 8 years (range from 53 to 76 years). Patients received an injection of 253 ± 18 MBq of [18F]FDG labelled WBC. The first PET/CT was performed at 131 ± 13 min and the second PET at 201 ± 16 min after administration of [18F]FDG labelled WBC. The delta time between the two PET scans was 70 ± 13 min. The glucose level before first PET was 7.6 ± 3.2 mmol/l (range from 4.6 to 9.4). The mean age of the cardiovascular devices (n = 3) was 72 ± 42 months. All patients except 2 underwent prior imaging test, namely 4 had a [18F]FDG PET-CT and 1 had a [99mTc]-labeled-WBC scintigraphy.

Imaging results

Measurements on suspected lesions

A total of 14 lesions were assessed on [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT based on the clinical suspicion of infection. For those 14 lesions with clinical confirmation or exclusion of the infection, [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT was found true positive in 9 lesions (65%), true negative in 3 lesions (21%), false positive in no lesion (0%) and false negative in 2 lesions (14%). The false negative results were all located at the level of the skeletal axe. One bone lesion at the level of the feet was correctly identified. [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT yielded a sensitivity and specificity of 82% and 100% respectively.

The median SUVmax@2h and SUVmax@3h values for infected lesions were 10.4 ± 8.8 and 13.9 ± 12.3 respectively. The mean aSUV and rSUV values for infected lesions were 3.5 ± 4.3 g/mL and 34 ± 29% respectively.

In patient 7, [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT accurately rule-in an infection of the pacemaker lead as diagnosed by the transoesophageal echocardiography, and which was not suspected in the previous performed [18F]FDG PET/CT (see Fig. 2). In patient 5, the [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT showed similar findings in comparison to [99mTc] SPECT/CT. In patient 2, [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT showed a matched focal uptake adjacent to the esophagus in comparison to [18F]FDG PET/CT, even if less extended. There was no significant tracer uptake of the aorta aneurysm wall in [18F]FDG-labelled WBC PET/CT, in contrary to [18F]FDG PET/CT (see Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our study showed the feasibility and the operator and patient safety of a [18F]FDG labeled WBC PET/CT protocol for the assessment of patients with suspicion of cardiovascular, soft tissues or osteoarticular infection. More, we investigated new diagnostic criteria based on the [18F]FDG-labelled WBC kinetic over time. In our study, [18F]FDG-labelled WBC yielded a sensitivity and specificity of 83% and 100% respectively.

Radiolabeling

The radiolabeling of WBCs was made according to previously described methods with [99mTc]HMPAO [15] except for the anticoagulant. Indeed, we used citric acid as anticoagulant instead of ACD (citric acid-dextrose) because of the dextrose which can disrupt the radiolabeling. The mean duration of the procedure was 3.5 ± 0.5 h, which implies a high initial activity due to the short half-life of [18F] (800 MBq minimum). The radiolabeling efficiency obtained was similar to previously described methods [74.8 ± 19.4% vs. 75.0 ± 21% and13 vs. 72.0 ± 8%12]). However, it was lower than those obtained with [99mTc]HMPAO, which were always between 75% and 85% [16, 17]. It is important to point out that the uptake of [99mTc]HMPAO is specific to leucocytes with 77 ± 8.0% bound to granulocytes and 17 ± 8.0% to mononuclear cells [18] whereas [18F]FDG binds to every cells expressing the glucose transporters, such as erythrocytes, and leucocytes. The mean free [18F]FDG rate at the end of radiolabeling was low, indicating a high radiolabeling efficiency. The radiochemical yields and the radiolabeling efficiency both exhibit high standard deviations. Indeed, in a few cases, we found low values for these parameters in diabetic patients or when the volume of collected blood was less than 80 mL. The correlation between the quality of radiolabeling and either the collected blood volume or glycemia should be assessed with a larger cohort of patients. The mean cell viability obtained in this study was 99.4 ± 0.7%, which was comparable to those reported on previous studies (98%)12, with no difference between diabetic and non-diabetic patients. We assessed the stability of the uptake of [18F]FDG by WBC over time (2 h and 3 h). We found respectively 15.1 ± 6.0% and 19.8 ± 4.4% of free [18F]FDG, which is higher than [99mTc]HMPAO [18] with 10% of release at 2 h. Some previous in-vitro studies found that the elution of [18F]FDG from WBCs was more important when extracellular concentration of glucose was important. 9% was released within 60 min when there was no glucose, whereas 27% of free [18F]FDG was found with 1 mg/mL of glucose [14]. This finding suggests that the in-vivo stability of the [18F]FDG uptake would be lower in diabetic patients in comparison to non-diabetic, highlighting the importance of blood glucose managing before scanning [19]. In our study, we could not compare the stability between both group due to limited residual activity at the end of the radiolabeling process in diabetic patients, as more volume was required for the radiotracer injection. This point would need to be address in further studies. Furthermore, the hypothetically improved stability of [18F]FDG-labelling with granulocytes, after elimination of lymphocytes, still needs to be investigated [18]. Regarding the operator safety, the important doses to the operators, as given in Table 7, could be explained by the absence of adequate shielding of the laminar flux and the waste, despite the use of lead-shielded protections for the syringes and vials. These results highlighted the importance of optimization of the radiopharmaceutical work environment for the clinical implementation of the procedure, including suitable leaded shield for vials and wastes. Nevertheless, in this small population, we demonstrate the feasibility, the efficiency and the operator safety of this procedure.

Clinical values of [18F]FDG labeled WBC PET/CT

The dynamic of recruitment and colonization of infected sites through WBC is a gradual process over several hours. This principle is fundamental in the determination of the imaging timepoint for radiolabeled WBC scintigraphy (1 h, 3–4 h, and 20–24 h) by suspicion of infection [20]. Previous investigations have proposed delayed imaging acquisition at 3 h for [18F]FDG-labeled WBC PET/CT13,21,22 in order to improve the target to background contrast. However, the dynamic of the WBC migration, as assessed by radiolabeled WBC SPECT/CT, could not be assessed with this acquisition protocol. In our study, we assess the tracer kinetic by calculation of the aSUV and rSUV from suspected lesions and healthy organs. Interestingly, all infected lesions, except lesion 14, showed an increase in [18F]FDG labeled WBC accumulation over time, as expressed by positive aSUV and rSUV values. In contrary, all lesions without infection highlighted negative aSUV and rSUV values. By direct comparison between healthy organs and infected lesions, SUVmax@2h and SUVmax@3h of infected lesion were significantly higher than SUVmax@2h of lung, mediastinum and gastrointestinal tract, whereas rSUV of infected lesion was significantly higher than rSUV of aorta, lung, mediastinum, liver, spleen and kidney. Our results are consistent with previous studies showing a physiologic distribution of [18F]FDG labelled WBC in reticuloendothelial system (liver, spleen and bone marrow) [13, 23]. This biodistribution is very similar to those found with [111In-oxinate] [24] and [99mTc]-HMPAO labelled leucocytes [25]. This physiological distribution is a limitation in the detection of axial skeleton lesion as well as lesion of the reticuloendothelial system. Indeed, the increase SUV measurements cannot be used to diagnose an infection of the vertebrae, since it corresponds to the normal kinetic of the [18F]FDG labeled WBC for this organ. Moreover, due to the increased background uptake, no focal lesion could be accurately identified. This limitation is less valid in distal extremities, where an infection of the Lisfranc could be accurately diagnosed [13]. Even if there was a reduced accumulation of [18F]FDG-labeled WBC over time in liver and spleen, the absolute tracer concentration in these organs remains high at 3 h post injection, which limits the identification of focal suspected lesion. Nevertheless, this novel metric could contribute to better discriminate infected lesion from non-infected lesion in organs others than those of the reticuloendothelial system. An advantage of [18F]FDG-labeled WBC PET/CT over [18F]FDG PET/CT pertains to the specific cellular elements contributing to the observed signal. While [18F]FDG PET/CT will highlights diverse inflammatory cell types, [18F]FDG-labeled WBC PET/CT will primarily reveals the presence of migrated WBC due to the infection [22]. An exemplary case within our patient series effectively highlighted this discrepancy (patient 3), with a false positive [18F]FDG, true negative [18F]FDG-labeled WBC lesion due to the use of surgical glue, known to cause a persistent sterile inflammation [21]. Another benefit of using [18F]FDG-labeled WBC PET/CT for cardiac imaging is that a specific dietary preparation over 24 to 72 h before PET/CT is no more mandatory, as recommended for [18F]FDG PET/CT8. Indeed, no relevant myocardial radiotracer uptake was detected in nearly all our patients. Therefore, patients could undergo PET/CT examination at an earlier timepoint with a more comfortable patient experience.

Despite the promising results of our study, it is limited by its retrospective nature, the small number of patients and the absence of female inclusion. The impact of diabetes on the radiolabeling stability also needs to be further investigated in a larger population. Finally, the impact of specific shielding measures in the radiopharmaceutical environment on operator safety also needs to be investigated to ensure the clinical implementation of this protocol.

Conclusions

[18F]FDG labeled leucocytes PET/CT is feasible, accurate, operator and patient safe for the detection of infectious disease. Further studies with larger number of participants and different clinical settings are still needed for the clinical implementation of this new diagnostic imaging method.
