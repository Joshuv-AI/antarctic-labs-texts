Dynamic bio-psychologic profiles: Tracking burnout and recovery in surgical trainees
Authors: Lauren Barron, Allison Weiderhold, Kevin G Aubol, Joshua Blair, Tait Shanafelt, Ronald Cotton, Ravi Ghanta, Marc Moon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBurnout affects 67% to 95% of surgical trainees and attrition approaches 3% per year, making both among the highest rates across all specialties. The discrepancy between burnout and attrition suggests potential reversibility and common strengths that foster retention. Most burnout studies utilize variable-centered analyses that average individuals’ trajectories obscuring distinct patterns of burnout and recovery. The lack of granularity hinders identification of interventions that address specific needs of individuals. This work explored physiologic markers of stress and burnout in surgical trainees to generate bio-psychologic profiles and potential profile-specific targets for intervention.MethodsPhysiologic markers, program characteristics, and burnout assessments were obtained from surgical residents over a 3-month period. Correlations, linear mixed models, and unsupervised clustering were used to explore the relationships among these variables.ResultsWe evaluated 1470 nights of sleep and 179 burnout assessments across 29 surgical residents. On average, subjects contributed 56 (interquartile range, 38-71) nights of sleep and 6 (interquartile range, 5-7) burnout assessments. High levels of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization were reported in 41.3% and 39.7% of surveys, respectively, and low levels of personal accomplishment in 41.9%. Greater bedtime variability over the prior 7 days was associated with lower personal accomplishment (P = .004) and was more pronounced earlier in training (P = .001). Trainees clustered into 6 hypothesized profiles with an average of 1.05 transitions between profiles per person.ConclusionsLongitudinal bio-psychologic profiling in surgical trainees is feasible. Residents appear to transition between profiles, suggesting that targeted interventions tailored to profile-specific drivers warrant validation in larger cohorts.Key Words: resident education, burnout, well-being, recovery

Heterogeneous drivers and buffers in burnout across bio-psychologic profiles.

Central Message

Longitudinal bio-psychologic profiling in residents is feasible. Residents transition between profiles, supporting future validation of targeted interventions tailored to profile-specific drivers.

Perspective

Burnout is often treated as a uniform condition in surgical trainees. This work identified novel profiles with differing patterns of physiologic strain, sleep disruption, and interaction with work environments, demonstrating that the drivers of burnout are not uniform. These findings support the need for targeted, profile-based approaches to burnout recovery and mitigation.

Burnout is common in surgical training with most trainees and half of practicing surgeons reporting moderate-to-severe symptoms.1, 2, 3, 4 The consequences of burnout in resident physicians are widespread and deeply disruptive. At the individual level, burnout is linked to increased cardiovascular mortality, metabolic disease, depression, and suicidal ideation in a population where suicide is the leading cause of death.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 At the occupational level, burnout has been linked to a decrease in cognitive performance, increased work-related injuries, and is the strongest predictor of attrition in surgeons.10, 11, 12, 13 Burnout is also associated with negative consequences for patients, with studies showing twice the incidence of medical errors, unprofessional behavior, and decreased patient satisfaction.14

Despite the high prevalence and severe consequences of burnout in this population, no single intervention has demonstrated consistent, clinically meaningful benefit across resident physicians, likely reflecting underlying heterogeneity in its drivers.15,16 This may be further compounded by the lack of longitudinal data on burnout trajectories at the individual level because most prior studies have relied on variable-centered approaches that report cross-sectional, population-level trends and linear associations.16 These approaches rely on the assumption that the individuals in the sample group are fundamentally similar and that findings can be generalized to the population.17 The most consistent finding in burnout is its heterogeneity in both population and manifestation. Accordingly, Leiter and Maslach18 describe a person-centered approach where individuals can be grouped into profiles utilizing shared symptom patterns. Unsupervised clustering methods help identify naturally occurring subgroups within a population without requiring prespecified categories. Similar to the informal groupings that can be found in any physician's lounge, individuals tend to congregate with others most like themselves forming distinct tables without any external direction.

Although person-centered analyses provide an avenue for progress, the individual-level data they require is rare in physician cohorts. This paucity of data constrains efforts to translate burnout heterogeneity into actionable profiles and limits our understanding of how burnout evolves over time within an individual physician. Prospective data from other professions have been captured and demonstrate that burnout fluctuates over time.19, 20, 21, 22 This dynamic may be reflected in surgical trainees: Although 70% to 95% of surgical residents report moderate-to-severe burnout, only 3.3% leave training annually, suggesting subsequent recovery from burnout and offsetting strengths that foster retention.1,2,22, 23, 24 The mechanisms of recovery and how to identify individualized targets remain poorly understood. Building on prior person-centered approaches that group individuals based on shared symptom patterns, this work incorporates physiologic measures into profile derivation. Heart rate variability (HRV) is a measure of the beat-to-beat variability between QRS complexes and reflects the dynamic balance of sympathetic and parasympathetic physiologic input. Prior research has demonstrated associations between decreased HRV (parasympathetic tone) and burnout.3,23 These findings are inconsistent across cohorts likely reflecting the heterogeneity of burnout itself as well as the limitations in established methodologies for studying it and the variety of drivers behind it.

In addition to physiologic markers, work environment was subsequently examined at the profile level to identify modifiable drivers of profile membership, such as scheduling patterns and sense of community that offer potential targets for profile-specific intervention. The objective of this research was to explore the relationship between physiologic markers, burnout, and work environment in surgical trainees to assess whether person-centered analysis can identify distinct bio-psychologic profiles and potential targets for intervention.

Methods

Design and Population

Baylor College of Medicine's institutional review board approved our clinical research protocol (H-53600) during May 2024. Written consent was obtained for data use from subjects before enrollment. This was a single-institution, multicampus, prospective, longitudinal cohort study. The population consisted of surgical residents in accredited general or integrated thoracic programs. Those younger than age 18 years, pregnant or breastfeeding, or with preexisting cardiac conditions that may confound wearable-derived metrics were excluded. There were 29 residents in total, with 10 women (34%) and 19 men (66%). Race and ethnicities included White (n = 16 [55%]), Hispanic/Latino (n = 5 [17%]), Black (n = 4 [14%]), and Asian (n = 4 [14%]). The majority were general surgery residents (n = 26 [90%]) and 3 (10%) were integrated thoracic residents. Marital status was distributed evenly with 11 (38%) married, 9 (31%) single, and 9 (31%) with a significant other but not married.

Data Collection

After providing written informed consent, enrolled participants received the Maslach Burnout Inventory - Human Services Survey (MBI), Areas of Worklife Survey (AWS), and program logistical questions at enrollment. For 10 weeks, subjects received MBI and program logistical questions every 2 weeks through an approved app. Physiologic data were captured utilizing wearable devices. The subjects were enrolled and monitored in waves with the first period between June to September and the second from October to January of the same academic year. Each wave spanned a 10-week per-participant monitoring window. This design was intended to span both seasonal windows of the academic year rather than a single block.

Measures

Burnout was assessed using the MBI, a 22-item survey scored on a 7-point Likert scale designed to measure burnout on 3 dimensions: Emotional Exhaustion (EE) (9 questions, score range = 0-54, high ≥27), Depersonalization (DP) (5 questions, range = 0-30, high ≥10), and Personal Accomplishment (PA) (8 questions, range = 0-48, low ≤34).24 Composite burnout strain scoring, an exploratory multipart score, was used to capture the multidimensional nature of burnout without losing the clinically relevant signal at the subscale level. This approach was intentionally designed not to simplify burnout into a single dimension, but to quantify how close each subject was to meeting the established thresholds for any burnout dimension. Given the absence of validated domain weighting and the MBI developers' explicit recommendation against composite scoring, this metric treats proximity to severe symptomatology as equivalent regardless of dimension to preserve subscale-level deviation as opposed to summing raw scores. This ensures that trainees at risk through any combination of subscale elevations are not systematically excluded by single-threshold classification. Formula: (EE – 27) + (DP – 10) + (33 – PA) where strongly negative scores are below established thresholds for severe burnout, strongly positive values have met criteria for burnout in at least 1 domain, scores near 0 indicate risk for moderate levels of burnout as defined by the MBI-Human Services Survey manual, and negative scores are protective.24 To assess the relationship between an individual and the training environment, the AWS was utilized.25 Lower scores indicate greater perceived mismatch between the individual and the training environment. Program logistics (call schedule, rotation, or location change) were collected from the trainees biweekly. Nightly biometrics (HRV, heart rate [HR], and sleep metrics) were collected passively via Oura Ring (Gen 4; Oura Health).26 Although Oura Ring has demonstrated comparable accuracy to medical-grade sleep and electrocardiogram studies, it is not a diagnostic tool and was used to characterize physiologic trends and identify potential signals of strain or recovery rather than diagnose sleep or cardiovascular disorders.

Statistical Analysis

Spearman rank correlations were calculated between MBI scores, postgraduate year (PGY), sleep, and physiologic variables. Mixed-effects linear regression models were utilized to evaluate associations between bedtime variability and burnout outcomes (EE, DP, and PA) with random intercepts for participants. Models included fixed effects for bedtime variability (both previous night and 7-day average), PGY, and their interaction, with random intercepts for participants. For transition analysis, only participants with more than 3 observations were included (N = 19). Exploratory unsupervised cluster analysis (K-means) was used to group resident-observations into bio-psychologic profiles. The clustering input comprised the 3 MBI subscales (EE, DP, and PA), 4 physiologic features (resting HR, HRV, total sleep duration, and 7-day sleep-duration variability), bedtime variability, and PGY year. Features were standardized to z scores before clustering so that no single metric dominated the distance calculation. The number of profiles was selected by silhouette score, which evaluates cluster separation, with a prespecified maximum to try and limit profiles populated by only a handful of observations (Table E1). Profiles were then ordered along a continuum from protected to high-strain using the composite burnout strain score described above, and were labeled descriptively (Protected-Stable, Protected-Compensated, and so on) for interpretation. Full feature lists, normalization parameters, silhouette curves, and stability checks are in Appendix E1. Feature-by-feature contributions to each profile are quantified in Table 1, and Figure 1.

Results

Subject Characteristics

We evaluated 1470 nights of sleep and 179 burnout assessments across these 29 surgical residents. On average, subjects contributed 56 (interquartile range, 38-71) nights of sleep and 6 (interquartile range, 5-7) burnout assessments. There were high levels of burnout, with 41.3% reporting high EE, 39.7% reporting high DP, and 41.9% reporting low PA. This was consistent across all training levels.

To determine whether the time of year affected levels of burnout, we tested whether the levels differed by wave. A linear mixed-effects model (wave as a fixed effect, resident as a random intercept), showed that EE (mean difference, 6.8; 95% CI, –0.6 to 14.1; P = .07), DP (mean difference, 3.5; 95% CI, –1.0 to 8.1; P = .13), and PA (P = .52) were not different between waves. Additionally, the distribution of profiles across waves did not differ significantly (χ2 = 1.7; P = .43) (Table E2).

Variable-centered analysis

Consistent with prior variable-centered analyses, EE and DP were strongly correlated, as were measures of sleep architecture and variability, whereas HR and HRV were strongly inversely correlated, indicating that our data reproduce established population-level relationships (Figure E1). Mixed-effects linear models showed that senior residents had more consistent bedtimes compared with junior residents (r = −0.51; P < .001) and higher bedtime variability was associated with lower PA scores, especially during the early PGYs (main effect P = .004; interaction with PGY P = .001). This relationship was only present when variability was assessed using a 7-day rolling average and was not detectable with single-night measurements preceding MBI assessment, suggesting that longitudinal data may be required to identify meaningful associations between sleep patterns and burnout.

To further evaluate modifiable contributors to sleep variability, regression analysis demonstrated that in-house call and night float were associated with significantly increased bedtime variability compared with no call (+129 and + 146 minutes, respectively; both P values < .001), whereas home call was not significantly different from no call. These findings suggest that clinical scheduling was a primary driver of increased bedtime variability in this cohort, with overnight in-hospital duty (in-house call and night float) accounting for the largest displacements in bedtime. We did not collect self-reported sleep complaint, insomnia symptoms, or chronotype, so nonclinical contributors to residual bedtime variability cannot be fully excluded. The concentration of increased variability within overnight in-hospital call types is consistent with a predominantly clinical-schedule driver.

Person-centered analysis

Unsupervised cluster analysis identified 6 bio-psychologic profiles with individuals transitioning between profiles an average of 1.05 times (Figure 1 and Table 1). The 6 profiles separated along 3 intuitive axes: composite burnout strain (how close the resident sat to the established MBI cutoffs), physiologic stability (whether sleep duration and bedtime were consistent or disrupted across nights), and work-environment alignment as captured by the AWS (Figure 2). The Protected profiles sat well below established burnout thresholds in the individual burnout domains (ie, EE, DP, and low PA). They differed in physiology: the Protected-Stable group (n = 8 participants with 18 observations) had consistent sleep duration and intermediate HRV, whereas the Protected-Compensated (n = 6 participants with 12 observations) group likely maintained low burnout scores by sleeping longer despite emerging bedtime variability. The Vulnerable profiles remained near established burnout thresholds on 1 or more dimensions and split on physiology and AWS domains: Vulnerable-Disrupted (n = 5 participants with 10 observations) carried high bedtime variability and short sleep, whereas Vulnerable-Stable (n = 2 participants with 9 observations) retained stable sleep but reported the most pronounced person-job mismatch across multiple AWS domains. The Burnout profiles exceeded the threshold for more than 1 dimension of burnout and similar to the vulnerable profiles seemed to divide on the driver: Burnout-Disrupted (n = 7 with 9 observations) had high bedtime variability and low sleep duration consistent with an acute physiologic load, whereas Burnout-Stable (n = 8 with 13 observations) had relatively preserved physiology paired with worse work-environment alignment, consistent with a more chronic or value-fit driven pattern. To further explore whether these profiles represent stable states or dynamic responses, we examined transitions between profiles over time. A transition was defined as a change in cluster assignment between 2 consecutive biweekly MBI time points for the same participant. On average, individuals spent 70% of observed time in a single dominant profile and profiles differed in persistence. Burnout-Disrupted was the least persistent, with individual members spending only 35% of their observed time in this profile, and Vulnerable-Stable was the most persistent, with its members spending 88% of observed time within this profile. The transition analysis included residents with >2 longitudinal MBI-plus-physiology assessments (n = 19), comprising 52 consecutive within-resident assessment pairs. Across these, residents made 20 profile transitions in total (mean, 1.05 per resident). Of the 20 transitions, 7 (35%) crossed the boundary between low/no burnout and high/moderate burnout profiles, and 14 (70%) involved a change in the 3-tier grouping (Protected/Vulnerable/Burnout). Durability of transition: Of the 7 boundary-crossing transitions, 5 had a subsequent assessment. Three subjects persisted in their new grouping at the next assessment, 1 reverted to their previous grouping, 1 moved to a new grouping. Across all 20 transitions, 6 persisted, 3 reverted, and 4 moved again at the next assessment. (Figure 3).

Divergence between variable- and person-centered findings

In variable-centered analysis EE and DP were strongly correlated (r = 0.62; P < .001), but this relationship was not uniform across profiles in person-centered analysis. Although both burnout profiles demonstrated similarly high EE, the DP scores diverged with Burnout-Stable demonstrating only borderline DP despite the highest EE (Table 1). Accordingly, comparisons across profiles with similar burnout severity revealed differences in physiologic and environmental characteristics, suggesting that burnout scores alone cannot define a homogeneous group (Figure 1).

Discussion

In this work, person-centered analysis integrating burnout measures, physiologic data, and work environment delineated novel bio-psychologic profiles among surgical trainees. Although burnout scores were similar across some profiles, physiologic and environmental patterns differed, suggesting that comparable burnout scores may arise from fundamentally different sources and thus require fundamentally different interventions.

Traditional variable-centered analysis finds relationships between variables and assumes that individuals uniformly express these variables within a population. Our work utilized a person-centered approach to group individuals into profiles based on shared patterns of burnout metrics and physiologic data. Although variable-centered analyses demonstrated expected correlations, for example between EE and DP, these relationships were not consistently preserved across profiles.18 Further illustrating that population-level associations may not generalize to all individuals, prior work from our group found that, despite established links between burnout and poorer knowledge-based performance, certain trainee cohorts maintain preserved performance even in the setting of high burnout.3,27 Systematic reviews report that both individual and organizational-level interventions yield variable and inconsistent improvements with significant heterogeneity across studies.16,28, 29, 30 Person-centered analysis supplies a framework to help align interventions with the specific physiologic and environmental drivers within each profile. This approach may be complementary to general system level approaches and also help identify protective factors, as profiles with similar physiologic strain reported differing burnout levels in the context of more or less favorable work environments (Figure 1).

The primary utility of a bio-psychologic approach is not identifying trainees with burnout but identifying modifiable aspects of training through which strain develops. In our cohort, profiles with similar levels of burnout appeared to separate along 2 actionable axes: disrupted recovery reflected by wearable-derived sleep patterns and diminished work-environment support reflected by lower AWS domain scores. The profiles also create driver-specific groupings that when contextualized with program logistical data are able to generate testable hypotheses about barriers to recovery and potential sources of reserve. As an example, the 2 disrupted profiles were characterized by increased bedtime variability and reduced sleep duration. Integration of program-level logistical data showed that individuals in disrupted profiles spent 89% to 100% of the observed time assigned to some form of call duty, with a disproportionate share consisting of types requiring overnight in-hospital duty (24-hour in-house call or night float). The Protected-Stable profile metrics further support the hypothesis that bedtime variability is a source of strain and is connected to overnight in-house duty. Individuals in this profile averaged total sleep similar to that of the disrupted profiles but reported excellent AWS scores and very little bedtime variability. This profile spent the least amount of time on call, and the majority of the call did not require the trainee to sleep away from home. This information suggests that sleep regularity and a work environment fit may confer more benefit than simply increasing sleep duration.

Although the sleep patterns are similar in the disrupted profiles, it is unlikely that the disruption results from a single uniform driver. Rather, these patterns are best interpreted as downstream markers of multiple overlapping contributors including systems level barriers and individual level pressures. Considering this, a multimodal approach that includes both systems and individual-level interventions aimed at the same target may offer a more reliable result.

Applying this to the trainees in disrupted profiles, potential interventions aimed at reducing bedtime variability could include analyzing the call structure to guide design modifications that avoid consecutive months of overnight or away-from-home call. Additionally, educating trainees on the established guidelines for shift workers and supporting their implementation by applying these sleep science principles when scheduling mandatory activities, providing alternatives for residents who are in-house or on night float.31, 32, 33, 34, 35 Additionally, these 2 night-shift heavy profiles also reported some of the worst sense of community, creating a clear target for initiatives directed at increasing reserve by improving community such as structured opportunities for relationship building during night shifts (eg, group midnight meals or postshift breakfasts).

In contrast to the disrupted profiles, the stable profiles in the vulnerable and burnout groups demonstrated relatively lower AWS domain scores, indicating person-job mismatch within the work environment. This finding suggests a direction for targeted, program-level interventions, including objective, profile-based screening. For example, querying trainees with professional values misalignment similar to our Vulnerable-Stable profile may offer opportunities to shift professional efforts to areas that trainees find more meaningful. Work from Shanafelt and colleagues36,37 showed that physicians who spent <20% of their time on their most meaningful professional activity were 2.7 times more likely to experience burnout, a relationship that has been corroborated in studies examining related constructs including satisfaction, meaning, and commitment. In our cohort, this was a small, more senior group with very little physiologic strain, suggesting that lack of opportunity rather than time prevented spending more time in a professionally fulfilling activity.

This study represents an early intentional step toward the development of bio-psychologic profiles in surgical trainees, and its design features define a clear agenda for future work. The small sample size, single institution, and narrow specialty mix reflect the proof-of concept nature of this work. Replication across multiple institutions, career levels, and specialties will increase the generalizability of the findings.

Sample size constraints, while expected, sharpen several findings and highlight future direction. The Vulnerable-Stable profile contained only 2 participants and 9 observations, which precludes treating it as definitive profile. However, the pattern of the profile was striking with its persistence (88% of subject's observed within the profile), low physiologic strain, and pronounced person-job mismatch across multiple AWS domains. Together, this pattern is consistent with the career-fit phenomenon described by Shanafelt and colleagues and present it as a case-series observation that requires replication in larger cohorts.36 Similarly, HRV findings for each profile should be treated as hypothesis-generating with the expectation that adequately powered studies will move findings from signal to characterization.

The exploratory, conceptually informed approach was appropriate for this initial phase of study and helps establish the foundation for more formal methods. Future work should apply latent profile analysis, consistent with the person-centered methods described by Leiter and Maslach18 to evaluate whether similar bio-psychologic profiles emerge in larger cohorts. This model-based approach offers the additional advantages of probability-based membership and fit statistics. Following independent cohort validation, extension of observed time would permit inquiry into longitudinal outcomes such as attrition, academic performance, and attending transitions.

Profile transitions represent another high-value subject. The current biweekly sampling interval and sample size limit interpretation of single-time point crossings, which may represent within-person measurement variation or boundary instability rather than meaningful profile transitions. Denser sampling designs would be required in future work to home in on this aspect of burnout and recovery.

The single baseline measurement of AWS effectively anchors profile characteristics but does not support analysis of individual transitions and overall trajectory. Repeated AWS administration in future studies will allow characterization of the role person-job fit plays in profile membership, transitions, and trajectory. Finally, future work should capture self-reported sleep complaints, insomnia symptoms, and chronotype to determine if residual variation may be explained by aspects beyond call structure and may offer alternative opportunity for intervention.

Advancing this work will require equal attention to confidentiality and the ethical infrastructure that the use of individualized bio-psychologic data in training demands. The stigma surrounding burnout and the power differential between trainees and those with programmatic influence and authority, renders individual-level data particularly sensitive. The current study was designed with this in mind; multiple layers of anonymity embedded in the protocol necessarily limit the use the data for individual-level interventions. This was an intentional trade-off. Connecting data to individuals would require meaningful institutional protections such that participants have no reasonable fear of reprisal. Real-time use of individualized data could be trialed in an attending population with peer-level coaches or in a separate but similar program such as neurosurgery, accepting that the results may not be generalizable to general surgery trainees. Ultimately, the populations most likely to yield meaningful answers are those under the greatest strain. The complexity of the ethical dimensions mirror the complexity of burnout itself and understanding it with the precision this framework offers requires protecting those whose candor makes understanding possible and whose well-being the entire field should be working to protect.

Conclusions

Although the burnout syndrome has a consistent constellation of symptoms, it does not manifest as a singular experience and may have differing physiologic and environmental drivers. Person-centered analysis offers a framework to move beyond generic population-level approaches and may inform tailored interventions. Recurring patterns of individual profiles on certain rotations may also provide human factors insights that enhance the influence of system-level approaches to improve the practice and training environment. Further work should focus on more clearly defining these profiles in larger cohorts and testing driver-specific interventions at the organization level and tailored interventions for individual trainees based on specific profiles.

Statistical Analysis

Associations among Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) scores, postgraduate year (PGY), sleep, and physiologic variables were evaluated using Spearman rank correlation coefficients. For correlative analysis, a total of 78 variable pairs were tested. P values were calculated using Pearson correlation testing, and CIs were derived using Fisher z-transformation. Mixed-effects linear regression models included fixed effects for bedtime variability (both previous night and 7-day rolling average), PGY, and their interaction, with random intercepts for participants.

Unsupervised K-means cluster analysis was performed to identify bio-psychologic phenotypes during burnout and recovery. Thirteen features, each standardized to a mean of 0 and SD of 1 (Table E3) were used as clustering inputs: The 3 MBI subscales (Emotional Exhaustion, Depersonalization, and Personal Accomplishment), PGY, and 9 physiologic/behavioral features derived from wearable data (bedtime variability, 7-day sleep-duration variability, total sleep duration, resting heart rate, heart-rate variability, deep-sleep duration, rapid eye movement sleep duration, average bedtime hour, and 7-day heart-rate-variability trend). A 7-cluster K-means solution (random_state = 42, n_init = 10) was fit and clusters with a single observation were then removed, yielding 6 profiles. Profiles were ordered along a Protected to high-strain continuum using a composite clinical-burnout strain score. Per-observation stability was quantified via bootstrap resampling (Table E4).

Profile transitions were analyzed among the 19 participants with more than 1 matched MBI-plus-physiology time point, comprising 52 within-resident pairs. A transition was defined as a change in profile assignment between 2 consecutive biweekly time points for the same participant. Residents made 20 transitions in total (mean, 1.05 per resident); of these, 7 (35%) crossed the boundary between low/no-burnout and high/moderate-burnout profiles and 14 (70%) involved a change in the 3-tier grouping (Protected/Vulnerable/Burnout). Most transitions were single-time-point movements. Across all 20 transitions, 6 persisted, 3 reverted back to previous clusters, and 4 moved to new clusters again.
