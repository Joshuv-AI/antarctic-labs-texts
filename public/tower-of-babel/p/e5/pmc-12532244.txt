Replantation of Avulsed Tooth: A Case Report and Review
Authors: Amit Bihari Lall, Mayank Singhal, Lokesh Chandra, Nidhi Gupta, Deekksha Baghel, Ahana Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAn avulsed tooth refers to a tooth that has been completely dislodged from its socket in the jaw, usually as a result of trauma or an accident. This is considered a dental emergency, and immediate action can significantly improve the chances of saving the tooth. Replantation plays a critical role in managing an avulsed tooth and aims to restore its function, esthetics, and maintain the integrity of the dental arch. Successful replantation depends on quick action, appropriate handling of the tooth, and proper postreplantation care. A male patient aged 7 years presented with a traumatic dental injury sustained to the anterior maxillary region due to an accidental fall. Avulsed tooth 21 was discovered during clinical examination; it was replanted in accordance with the International Association of Dental Traumatology's (IADT) recommendations and secured in position with splinting. Conventional root canal therapy was initiated 2 weeks postreplantation. The root canal treatment was completed 4 weeks postreplantation followed by removal of the splint. Introduction

A significant impact to the teeth and surrounding tissues can lead to dentoalveolar trauma, potentially resulting in complex outcomes such as the avulsion of a tooth from its socket. Dental injuries are most frequently observed in children between the ages of 7 and 11.1

The complete disengagement of a tooth from its alveolus is referred to as an avulsion. It is also known as exarticulation, and it frequently involves the maxillary teeth. Sports injuries, height falls, and automobile accidents that directly stress the teeth and periodontal ligaments are the most common causes of tooth avulsion.2

An avulsed tooth's root surface becomes extremely vulnerable to contamination from debris, soil, microorganisms, etc., if the extraoral dry period lasts more than 60 minutes. This contaminated root surface could lead to infection or difficulties during replantation. The prognosis of avulsed teeth following replantation is influenced by several factors, including the duration between the avulsion and replantation, the type of storage medium utilized, the stage of root development, the timing of root canal treatment (whether conducted prior to or after replantation), the interval at which root canal treatment is performed (immediately or after 1 week), and the surface treatment of the tooth prior to replantation.3

The major objective of avulsion treatment is to save the tooth or keep it functioning in order to prevent ankylosis, root resorption, and to allow the alveolar ridge to grow normally. Replanting the avulsed tooth as soon as possible after an avulsion injury is the best course of action. The positive prognosis in the treatment of an avulsion injury is influenced by timely intervention, considerable therapy plans, and appropriate emergency management. Previously, extraoral root canal therapy was recommended prior to replantation. However, to minimize the extraoral time, the current guidelines recommend root canal therapy be performed intraorally. Along with observation of the tooth every month to assess the vitality, mobility, ankylosis, and resorption.2

This study emphasizes the careful protocols followed in the successful replantation of an avulsed maxillary central incisor, despite a prolonged extraoral period of 90 minutes. The procedure was in accordance with the treatment guidelines outlined by the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT) and includes a detailed review analysis of available treatment options and pertinent findings.

Clinical Case

A 7-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department with an alleged history of fall, resulting in trauma to the anterior maxillary region. The patient preserved the avulsed tooth in a handkerchief and reported to the emergency unit approximately one and a half hours after the incident of trauma. The patient had no significant medical history and remained alert and responsive throughout the examination process.

The extraoral examination revealed swelling over the upper lip with no soft tissue injury. On intraoral examination, the left maxillary central incisor 21 was missing with a bleeding alveolar socket of 21 and no associated injuries to the gingiva. On palpation of the maxillary anterior segment, the dentoalveolar fracture was ruled out. The avulsed tooth had an intact crown and root with an open apex. The case was diagnosed as an Ellis Class V fracture of 21.

The patient was informed, and consent was taken, informing him about the potential complications associated with the replantation of an avulsed tooth that had experienced an extraoral duration of approximately 90 minutes, including inflammatory resorption, replacement resorption/ankylosis, and tooth discoloration.

The root surface was carefully rinsed with normal saline to remove any adhered debris. The avulsed tooth was subsequently immersed in gentamicin for 20 minutes. Throughout the procedure, the tooth was handled exclusively by the crown to avoid contact with the root surface. The tooth was then repositioned and replanted using a figure-of-eight wiring splint (Flowchart 1 and Fig. 1).

The patient was recalled on the next day to be reviewed on an outpatient basis, on examination the tooth was found to have grade I mobility. The stability of the replanted tooth was then reinforced by a composite based splint using 26-gauge orthodontic wire secured with light-cure flowable composite resin. At the middle third level of the crown, the labial surfaces of the maxillary anterior teeth were spot etched for 20 seconds using 37% phosphoric acid, followed by rinsing and air drying. After that, the bonding agent was applied to the prepared surfaces and polymerized using light-curing for 20 seconds. Flowable composite material was placed on all six maxillary anterior teeth, and the orthodontic wire was secured in position by sequentially curing each tooth for 20 seconds (Fig. 2).

(A) Etching prior to composite splint application, (B) Composite splint application, (C) IOPA of 11, 21 after composite splint application

The patient was prescribed oral analgesics and antibiotics for 5 days. Postoperative instructions were provided to adhere to a soft diet for two weeks, use a soft-bristled toothbrush, and perform oral hygiene maintenance with a 0.12% chlorhexidine mouth rinse. The patient was strictly advised to avoid biting from maxillary anterior teeth. The patient was scheduled for a follow-up after 2 weeks, during which conventional root canal therapy was initiated on the replanted tooth in the Department of Pediatric Dentistry.

After 2 weeks the splints were removed and tooth was assessed, no mobility or discrepancy in occlusion was observed. The replanted tooth's clinical examination during the 1-month follow-up appointment revealed normal physiologic mobility and normal probing sulcular depth. Radiographic evaluation demonstrated a normal periapical region with no evidence of periapical radiolucency. The lamina dura appeared intact, the periodontal ligament (PDL) space around tooth 21 was uniform, and no indications suggestive of resorption were observed.

The root canal treatment (RCT) of tooth 21 was performed. A collagen plug was placed to achieve an apical seal of the tooth, corresponding to the extent of root formation. Following biomechanical preparation (BMP), obturation was completed using biodentine and gutta-percha (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Usually following trauma, an avulsed tooth falls to the ground. A tooth that has been out of its socket for more than 60 minutes is susceptible to contamination from dirt, grime, microbes, and human interaction thus making surface treatment, cleaning, and disinfection crucial. The goal of surface therapy is to eliminate nonviable PDL fibers, as they may serve as an infection source and cause inflammatory resorption. Additionally, the nonviable periodontal tissue may stimulate the socket's undifferentiated mesenchymal cells to differentiate into osteoblasts, which would result in replacement resorption, or ankylosis. Therefore, an avulsed tooth surface therapy is crucial to the success of delayed replantation.3

The patient's overall health, the root's maturity, storage media, and extraoral duration are the variables that impact the effectiveness of replantation; the latter two are the most important (Table 1). Milk is the most widely used and advised storage medium for maintaining an avulsed tooth because it is readily available, has a pH that is adequate, and contains the right growth factors, nutrients, and osmolarity. Additionally, epithelial growth factor (EGF), which is found in milk, also encourages the growth and regeneration of Malassez's epithelial cell rest.2

It is best to either immediately transplant an avulsed tooth or to keep it in a physiological medium, such as saline, for a little amount of time before doing so. Avulsed teeth can also be kept longer in saline solution, milk, saliva, ViaSpan, Eagle's medium, Hank's balanced salt solution (HBSS), propolis, and other media before being replanted.4

The effectiveness of replanting an avulsed tooth depends on the reattachment of periodontal tissue and pulpal healing, both of which are favored by splinting. The tooth should be kept in its natural position without causing more trauma, orthodontic forces, or gingival irritation; aspiration and ingestion should be prevented; and the splint should permit proper dental hygiene. According to IADT standards (2020), a splint should be semirigid to permit the tooth's natural movement. Prolonged splinting should be avoided to avoid replacement resorption, which would cause the replanted avulsed tooth to ankylose.5

In the present case series, clinical and radiographic outcomes of immature permanent teeth with subject to trauma were treated with the help of a collagen plug and biodentine. Biodentine exhibits excellent marginal integrity due to its capacity to form hydroxyapatite crystals, which enhances the seal at the interface between the material and dentin.6 Thus in the current case report biodentine was used as it is highly effective in root canal treatment for teeth with an immature apex due to its bioactive properties, promoting apical closure by stimulating odontoblastic activity and forming a calcified barrier (Gandolfi et al., 2014).7 Its superior sealing ability prevents bacterial leakage, ensuring long-term success. Additionally, its biocompatibility supports tissue healing and reduces inflammatory reactions (Zanini et al., 2012).8 Biodentine's mechanical strength and durability make it suitable for clinical use in such cases. Factors such as inter-radicular radiolucency, periodontal ligament widening, or procedural errors affect the radio visibility of biodentine thus making it difficult to locate in the radiograph.

A significant challenge in cases involving a wide-open apex is the requirement to confine the material strictly to the apical region. To address this issue, an apical barrier was established using a collagen sponge. Excessive extrusion of material may lead to the persistence of inflammation. This ongoing inflammatory process can hinder or even prevent tissue repair.9

Preoperatively

Examine the avulsed tooth and gently shake it in the storage media to get debris off its surface. The surface can also be rinsed with normal saline. While taking the patient's history, perform a clinical and radiographic examination. To prepare the tooth for replantation, place or leave the tooth in a storage medium.

Intraoperatively

Give local anesthesia, ideally without the use of a vasoconstrictor. Use sterile saline to irrigate the socket. Check the socket of the alveolus. Use the appropriate tool to realign the fractured segment if the socket wall is fractured. Slowly replace the tooth while applying a little digital pressure. Make sure the transplanted tooth is positioned correctly using both clinical and radiographic methods. For 2 weeks, use a passive, flexible wire up to 0.016” (0.4 mm) in diameter to stabilize the tooth. Keep the bonding chemicals and composite away from the proximal regions and gingival tissues. As an alternative, a flexible splint can be made with nylon fishing line (0.13–0.25 mm) and bonded to the teeth using composite. A more firm splint is recommended in cases with concomitant alveolar or jawbone fractures, and it should be left in situ for approximately 4 weeks. If there are any gingival lacerations, suture them. When replanting immature teeth in young adults and children, the objective is revascularization of the pulp area, which can result in more root development and maturation. It is important to balance the likelihood of revascularization with the danger of external infection-related (inflammatory) root resorption.

Postoperatively

In young adults and children, this resorption happens quite quickly. As soon as pulp necrosis and infection are detected, apexification, pulp revascularization, or root canal therapy should be started if spontaneous revascularization does not take place. Give antibiotics systemically, verify the tetanus status, and give advice for recovery after surgery and follow-up on regular intervals.

The longest extraoral dry time was observed in the study by Coste et al.22 associated with a 50% survival rate in a follow-up period of 5.5 years whereas the shortest extraoral dry time was observed in the study by Walter et al.11 associated with good outcome in terms of survival rate of replanted tooth. Sonoda et al.13 performed a case where there was no extraoral dry time as the tooth was placed by the patient in the alveolar socket, most satisfactory outcomes were obtained with no complications till complete follow-up period of 3 months. The most common surface treatments included the use of normal saline, Hanks' balanced solution and acidulated phosphate fluoride (APF) gel application. Parthasarathy et al.,23 performed scraping of PDL fibers of the avulsed tooth which was found to be associated with loss of labial cortical plate. No significant association between splinting method and outcome was found, while the most preferred splinting method was application of composite splint. There was a study by Widyastuti et al.,24 in which arch bar fixation was used for splinting which obtained satisfactory results. A strong significance was found between the outcomes and follow-up period, the shortest follow-up period was found in a study by Saputra et al.,4 where no complications were observed till complete follow-up period of 1 month. The longest follow-up period was found in the study by Gul et al.,26 where follow-up period ranged from 7 months to 23 years, teeth which had greater extraoral dry time showed high risk of ankylosis. Similar findings were observed in the studies of Kinoshita et al.,10 Müller et al.,18 Coste et al.,22 where the follow-up period was 1 year 6 months–5 years 10 months, 3.2 years, 7 months–5.5 years, respectively.

Owing to a lack of research, some elements of tooth replantation have not been evaluated like revascularization of the pulp space in a tooth that has been reimplanted, ideal splint kinds and duration in relation to pulp and periodontal healing, the impact of using a local anesthetic that contains vasoconstrictors on healing, the impact of systemic and topical antibiotics on root resorption and healing, intracanal corticosteroids' impact on root resorption and healing, and the alveolar crest's long-term growth or establishment after decoronation and replantation, restoring normal function is impacted by periodontal regeneration, after tooth replantation, periodontal healing occurs.

Future trends in replantation of avulsed teeth are driven by advancements in regenerative medicine, materials science, and digital health technologies. Use of stem cells from dental pulp or other sources may be used to regenerate PDL tissues that promote reattachment of the tooth. Laser use for reducing microbial load and promoting tissue healing during replantation is one of the latest advances. These innovations aim to enhance the long-term success of replantation, minimize complications, and make emergency care more effective and accessible.

Conclusion

The replantation of an avulsed tooth is a crucial procedure that requires timely and appropriate management to ensure optimal outcomes. Success largely depends on factors such as the time elapsed since the avulsion, the condition of the periodontal ligament, and the storage medium used for the tooth before replantation. Immediate replantation or the use of a suitable storage medium can significantly improve the prognosis. Long-term success also hinges on proper follow-up care, including monitoring for potential complications such as root resorption or ankylosis. By adhering to best practices and employing multidisciplinary collaboration when needed, dental professionals can maximize the chances of restoring both function and esthetics in patients with avulsed teeth.
