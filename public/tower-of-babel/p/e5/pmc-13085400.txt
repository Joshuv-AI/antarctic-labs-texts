De-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of clusterin modulates neuropathology and memory deficits in an Alzheimer’s disease mouse model
Authors: Xiaoping Peng, Chaoyue Ouyang, Fubin Ma, Yu Liu, Lv Luo, Jiawei Xing, Sifan Feng, Li Li, Jiahao Zhang, Jiaying Wang, Helei Cheng, Tianying Zhang, Feng Chen, Yan Wang, Lili Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive, irreversible neurodegenerative disorder with no effective cure, posing a major clinical challenge. Lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib) is a recently identified post-translational modification found across diverse species and is involved in key metabolic processes associated with the regulation of protein function. However, its role in AD remains poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the involvement of Khib in AD pathogenesis as well as the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsWe compared lysine acylation profiles between 10-month-old APP/PS1 transgenic mice and age-matched wild-type (WT) controls using western blot. Quantitative Khib proteomics was employed to identify differentially modified proteins and specific Khib sites relevant to AD. To examine functional effects, we introduced an adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector expressing clusterin (CLU) K436R—a mutation that mimics Khib deficiency—into the bilateral lateral ventricles of 8-month-old WT and APP/PS1 mice via stereotactic injection. Additionally, C8-D1A astrocytic cells were transduced with lentivirus expressing CLU K436R. Co-immunoprecipitation was used to analyze the interaction between CLU and Aβ.ResultsKhib levels were significantly elevated in the brains of 10-month-old APP/PS1 mice. Proteomic analysis identified CLU as a Khib-modified protein, with K436 as the primary modification site. CLU K436 Khib levels were significantly upregulated in APP/PS1 mice. Notably, the loss of Khib at this site in CLU K436R mutant APP/PS1 mice led to marked suppressions in the number of β-amyloid plaques, gliosis, and neuroinflammation, along with improved memory performance in vivo. In vitro, CLU K436R expression in C8-D1A astrocytes promoted glial activation and cell viability. Mechanistically, the K436R mutation influenced the CLU–Aβ interaction, thereby modulating Aβ metabolism.ConclusionsOur findings underscore a critical role for Khib in AD pathophysiology and reveal that Khib modification of CLU at K436 influences amyloid pathology, glial responses, and neuroinflammation. These results provide novel insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying AD and suggest that targeting Khib at CLU K436 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02787-2.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, affecting over 6.9 million Americans and approximately 5.5 million people globally [1, 2]. Its hallmark pathological features include the extracellular deposition of β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles formed by hyperphosphorylated tau protein, and widespread neuronal and synaptic loss, culminating in cognitive decline [3]. Clinically, AD manifests as memory impairment accompanied by varying degrees of cognitive dysfunction [4]. Emerging evidence highlights a critical role for glial cell proliferation—particularly the accumulation of activated microglia and reactive astrocytes—in AD pathogenesis [5, 6]. Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as being not only a consequence of neurodegeneration but also a driver of disease progression [7, 8]. Despite decades of research, the underlying mechanisms of AD remain incompletely understood, and no effective pharmacological treatments exist that can halt or reverse its course. Consequently, elucidating the pathogenesis of AD and identifying novel therapeutic targets therefore remain pressing objectives.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) are chemical alterations that occur after protein synthesis, modulating protein activity, localization, folding, and interactions. Among these, lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib), first identified in 2014 on eukaryotic histones, is a novel acylation type with regulatory functions in chromatin dynamics and cellular processes [9, 10]. Khib modifications have been implicated in glucose metabolism and glycolysis [11–13], as well as in cancer metastasis and metabolic remodeling in cardiac hypertrophy models [14, 15]. Although multiple lysine acylation modifications have recently been associated with AD, the specific contribution of Khib remains unclear.

Clusterin (CLU), also known as apolipoprotein J (ApoJ), is a multifunctional glycoprotein highly expressed in mammalian tissues, particularly in the brain [16, 17]. Secreted CLU (sCLU) functions as a molecular chaperone in diverse physiological processes, including spermatogenesis, the clearance of misfolded proteins, lipid metabolism, complement inhibition, and apoptosis [18–21]. Genome-wide association studies have linked CLU gene variants to increased AD susceptibility [22]. Elevated CLU levels have been observed in specific brain regions and the cerebrospinal fluid of AD patients [23]. CLU is a component of Aβ plaques and the vascular deposits characteristic of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), where it interacts with Aβ and influences its aggregation, deposition, and clearance [24–27]. Notably, this interaction exerts dual effects—both protective and pathogenic—during AD progression. While histone modifications have been shown to regulate CLU expression in models of colon cancer and hepatitis D virus infection [28–30], the role of Khib modification on CLU and the mechanisms underlying its involvement in AD remain poorly defined.

In this study, we found that the levels of Khib modification at lysine 436 (K436) of CLU are elevated in the brain tissue of APP/PS1 mice. Astrocyte-specific overexpression of a CLU K436 mutant in these mice resulted in a supression in the Aβ plaque burden, decreased glial activation, attenuated neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity, and improved cognitive function and memory. In vitro, the lentiviral-mediated expression of the CLU K436 mutant suppressed astrocyte activation and neuroinflammatory responses in C8-D1A astrocytes, thus, enhancing cell viability. Furthermore, Khib modification at K436 influenced the interaction between CLU and Aβ, suggesting that CLU plays a functional role in AD pathogenesis. These findings identify CLU K436hib as both a novel regulatory mechanism and a potential therapeutic target in AD.

Methods

Animals

Seven-month-old male APP/PS1 transgenic mice (B6C3-Tg(APPswe, PSEN1dE9)85Dbo/Mmjax; Stock No. 004462) and their wild-type (WT) littermates were obtained from Jiangsu GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 22 ± 1 °C with a 12 h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to autoclaved food and water. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangdong Medical University (Approval No. GDMU-2023-000108).

and were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, 2011).

AAV-GFP /AAV-CLU/AAV-CLU mut viral production

The CLU overexpression vector (HBAAV2/9-GFAP-mCLU-3xFlag-EGFP), the CLU-K436R mutant vector (HBAAV2/9-GFAP-mClu mut-3xFlag-EGFP), and the eGFP control vector (HBAAV2/9-GFAP-EGFP) were designed, validated, and synthesized by Hanheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The K436R mutation was introduced by substituting lysine with arginine at position 436 in the full-length murine CLU sequence.

Construction of lentiviral vectors and cell infection

The lentiviral constructs used for in vitro studies included the eGFP control vector (pHBLV-CMV-ZsGreen-T2A-Puro), the CLU overexpression vector (pHBLV-CMV-mCLU-3xFlag-ZsGreen-T2A-Puro), and the CLU-K436R mutant vector (pHBLV-CMV-mClu-mut-3xFlag-ZsGreen-T2A-Puro), all obtained from Hanheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). C8-D1A astrocytes were grown to 30%–50% confluence at the time of transduction. After a 2 h preconditioning with serum-free Dulbecco’s minimal essential medium (DMEM), lentiviral particles (multiplicity of infection: 5–10) were applied for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with complete DMEM containing 1.5 µg/mL puromycin. Transduction efficiency (> 80%) was confirmed by ZsGreen fluorescence at 48 h post-infection. Stable polyclonal lines were selected via 72 h of puromycin treatment. Flag-tagged proteins were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag magnetic beads, and Khib at CLU K436 was assessed by immunoblotting.

Plasmids construction and cell transfection

Plasmid preparation: All expression plasmids were synthesized and validated by Yubo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China), including the wild-type CLU (NM_013492) and the K436R mutant (AAG→CGT substitution). Details are provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Transfection: (1) Cell seeding: Cells were grown to 80%–90% confluence at transfection. Antibiotic-free medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) was used unless otherwise specified. (2) Complex formation: Plasmid DNA (1 µg/µL) and Lipofectamine 3000 were each diluted in Opti-MEM, incubated separately for 5 min at room temperature (RT), mixed, and incubated for 20 min. (3) Transfection: The medium was replaced with serum-free Opti-MEM, and the complexes were added dropwise. The plates were gently rocked to ensure even distribution. After 6–8 h, the medium was replaced with complete growth medium. (4) Harvesting: Cells were harvested at 24–48 h post-transfection for subsequent assays. Transfection efficiency was validated by western blot and co-immunoprecipitation.

Plasmid DNA extraction

A single colony was inoculated in 5 mL of Luria Bertani (LB) broth containing antibiotics and cultured at 37 °C for 8–12 h with shaking (250 rpm). Following plasmid verification, 1 mL of this culture was transferred to 400 mL of fresh LB with antibiotics and incubated for 12–16 h under the same conditions. The cells were centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 10 min (4 °C) and resuspended in 40 mL of ice-cold STE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 100 mM NaCl, pH 8.0). The suspension was divided into two tubes and centrifuged at 6,000 × g for 5 min (4 °C), and the resulting pellets were gently resuspended in 3 mL of Solution I (50 mM glucose, 25 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), followed by the addition of 6 mL of freshly prepared Solution II (0.2 N NaOH, 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS]) and gentle mixing. Then, 4.5 mL of chilled Solution III (3 M potassium acetate, pH 5.2) was added. After mixing and centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min (4 °C), the supernatant was collected. An equal volume of isopropanol was added, and the solution was incubated at − 20 °C for ≥ 20 min. DNA was pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min (4 °C), washed with 70% ethanol, and air-dried. The DNA was dissolved in 5 mL of TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0), mixed with 5 mL of 5 M LiCl, and incubated at − 20 °C for 5–10 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min (4 °C), the supernatant was collected and subjected to another round of isopropanol precipitation. DNA was dissolved in 2 mL of Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer and treated with RNase A (final concentration 50 µg/mL) at 37 °C overnight. Phenol-chloroform extraction was performed twice, followed by precipitation with a 0.1 volume of 3 M sodium acetate (pH 5.2) and 2 volumes of ethanol. The DNA pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, air-dried, and stored in TE buffer at − 20 °C. The DNA concentration was measured by spectrophotometry before use.

Intracerebroventricular injections

Mice were anesthetized via the intraperitoneal injection of tribromoethanol (200 µL/10 g body weight). The surgical site was prepared by removing the hair using depilatory cream, followed by a skin incision. Following immobilization in a stereotaxic apparatus, a midline scalp incision was made, and the skull was drilled to a depth of 1 mm at coordinates targeting the bilateral lateral ventricles (AP: −0.2 mm; DV: −2.4 mm; ML: ±1.0 mm relative to Bregma). Viral vectors (1.5 µL per hemisphere) were injected at a rate of 0.1 µL/min. After injection, the needle was held in place for 10 min and then slowly withdrawn to prevent backflow. Respiratory function was closely monitored throughout to prevent asphyxia due to the prone position and stereotaxic clamping. Post-surgery, mice were placed on a warming pad and monitored until full recovery from anesthesia. Hyaluronic acid gel was topically.

applied to the incision site to promote wound healing.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The NOR test was conducted as described [31, 32]. On day 1, mice were habituated to an empty square arena (50 × 50 cm) for 8 min while locomotor activity—movement speed (cm/s) and total distance traveled (cm)—was recorded. On day 2 (24 h later), two identical objects were placed diagonally in the arena, and the mice were allowed to explore for 10 min. On day 3, one familiar object was replaced with a novel one, and mice underwent a second 10-min exploration session. Exploratory behavior, defined as nose contacts or proximity within 2–3 cm of the novel object, was recorded using an overhead camera. All sessions were video-recorded and analyzed in a double-blind manner using SuperMaze software.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

The MWM test was performed as previously reported [33]. The setup consisted of a circular water tank (120 cm diameter, water temperature 22 ± 1 °C) divided into four quadrants, with a hidden escape platform (10 cm diameter) submerged 1 cm below the surface. The protocol included a training phase (days 1–5) and a probe test (day 6).

On day 0, an acclimatization session was conducted in which mice were allowed to swim freely in the pool for 120 s. From days 1 to 5, the mice underwent four daily trials (90 s each) with varied starting positions, and escape latency (time to reach the platform) was recorded. On day 6, a probe trial was performed to assess memory retention. The platform was removed, and mice were released from the quadrant opposite the original platform location. Over 90 s, the number of crossings of the initial platform location and the time spent and distance traveled in the target quadrant were recorded. All trials were conducted between 12:00 and 18:00 h. To minimize stress, mice were handled gently, and all testing was conducted in a genotype-blinded, double-blind manner. Movement was tracked with a computerized system, and analysis was performed using Morris software.

Y-maze test

The Y-maze test was conducted following established protocols [34]. The Y-maze test comprised two components: spontaneous alternation and novel arm exploration. The apparatus consisted of three identical black arms (50 cm long, 10 cm wide, 30 cm high) arranged at 120° angles and connected via a central zone. The maze was elevated 50 cm above the floor. On day 1, mice were placed in a designated start arm and allowed to freely explore the maze for 8 min. Entry sequences and total arm entries were recorded. The spontaneous alternation rate was calculated as follows: Alternation rate (%) = [number of alternations / (total entries − 2)] × 100. The maze was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. On days 2–3, one arm was blocked to create a novel arm paradigm. Mice were allowed to explore the remaining two arms for 10 min. Subsequently, the novel arm was unblocked, and mice were allowed to freely explore for 6 min. The number of entries and time spent in each arm were recorded.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Hippocampal and cortical tissues were homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitors, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected for analysis of IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β1), and soluble Aβ1–40 and Aβ1–42 levels. Pellets were dissolved in 100% formic acid and neutralized with 10× Tris buffer (pH 7.5) to assess insoluble Aβ1–40 and Aβ1–42. Total protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The levels of soluble and insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 were quantified using ELISA kits (IBL, Japan, Code No. 27713 and Code No. 27711). The levels of the cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β1 were determined using the respective ELISA kits (EK0411, EK0527, and EK0515, respectively; BOSTER, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using an Epoch microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA).

Oligo-Aβ42 preparation

Unlabeled and Rhodamine-labeled Aβ42 peptides were purchased from QYAOBIO. To prepare oligomeric Aβ42 (100 µM), 5 mg of synthetic Aβ42 peptide was dissolved in 1.1 mL of ice-cold 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP) to obtain a 1 mM solution. The solution was incubated at RT for 60 min to ensure complete dissolution, chilled on ice for 10 min and left overnight in a fume hood to evaporate the HFIP, resulting in a transparent peptide film. The dried film was resuspended in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) by vigorous pipetting and vortexing for 30 s. This DMSO stock was diluted to 100 µM using ice-cold, phenol red-free F12 medium (Gibco). The solution was sonicated for 10 min and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. Following centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant—containing oligomeric Aβ42—was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for future use.

HEK293T cell, C8-D1A, and Neuro-2a cell culture procedures

HEK293T, C8-D1A, and Neuro-2a cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Immunofluorescence

For brain tissue sections, mice were deeply anesthetized with tribromoethanol (250 mg/kg, i.p.) following behavioral assessments and transcardially perfused with 50 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). The brains were dissected and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h, and then sequentially transferred to 20% and 30% sucrose in PBS, each for ~ 24 h, until the tissues were fully equilibrated. Samples were embedded in OCT compound (Tissue-Tek), flash-frozen, and coronally sectioned into 15-µm slices at − 20 °C using a cryostat (Leica CM1950). The sections were mounted on Superfrost Plus slides (Thermo Scientific), air-dried for 1 h at RT, and stored at − 80 °C. Before immunostaining, the slides were warmed to RT and circled with a hydrophobic barrier pen. Sections were washed three times in PBS (10 min each) with gentle agitation, and then blocked with 2% BSA and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer were applied, and the samples were incubated overnight (16–18 h) at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-NeuN (Abcam ab177487, 1:500), anti-GFAP (Abcam ab53554, 1:200), anti-Iba1 (Wako 019-19741, 1:200), anti-CLU (R&D AF2747, 1:100), anti-APP (Abcam ab32136, 1:100), anti-LAMP1 (DSHB H4A3, 1:20), and mouse anti-β-amyloid specific for Aβ42 (CST #14974, 1:200). After three PBS-T washes (0.1% Tween-20, 10 min each wash), species-matched secondary antibodies (1:1000; donkey anti-rabbit DyLight 594 [ab96893], donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 647 [ab150135]; both from Abcam) were applied, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. After three more PBS washes, the samples were counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL in PBS) for 3 min at RT, washed again with PBS (3 × 10 min), and mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher), left to stand at RT for 24 h in the dark, and stored at 4 °C. Images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope and the data was analysed using ImageJ. For quantification, every 5th section throughout the entire hippocampus and cortex was selected, resulting in a total of 6 sections per animal that were analyzed. Positive staining was highlighted by applying a minimal threshold within the manually traced regions of interest (ROIs). This same threshold value was applied to all images. For cells, confocal dishes were pre-coated with 0.1 mg/mL poly-L-lysine overnight and then washed three times with PBS. Cells were then seeded onto the coated dishes. After fixation in 4% PFA and permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100, nonspecific binding was blocked with 2% BSA. Primary antibodies diluted in antibody diluent were added, and the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies were added dropwise, and nuclei were mounted with DAPI-containing antifade mounting medium. To confirm antibody specificity, isotype controls were prepared using IgG at concentrations equivalent to the primary antibodies. Images were obtained on an Olympus FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope using a 10× objective lens. Scale bars are indicated in all figure legend. Detailed antibody information is provided in Supplemental Table 2.

Western blot

Cultured cells and mouse brain tissues (cortex and hippocampus) were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer and incubated for 40 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified against a standard curve. Equal amounts of protein (50 µg per lane) were separated by 10% or 12.5% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes by wet transfer (4 °C, 3 h) using buffer containing 25 mM Tris-HCl, 192 mM glycine, and 20% methanol. After blocking in 5% skimmed in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at RT, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies under gentle agitation. Following three 10-min washes in TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT and washed again with TBST (3 × 10 min). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using chemiluminescent substrate and imaged with a C600 system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to β-actin or GAPDH. Data represent means from at least three independent experiments. The primary antibodies used were anti-NeuN (1:2000, Abcam), anti-GFAP (1:1000, Abcam), anti-Iba-1 (1:200, Wako), anti-CLU (1:500, R&D Systems), anti-APP (1:1000, Abcam), anti-PSD95 (1:500, CST), anti-synaptophysin (1:500, Proteintech), anti-BACE1 (1:1000, Abcam), anti-PS1 (1:1000, Abcam), and anti-α-tubulin (1:2000, Abcam). Detailed antibody information is shown in Supplemental Table 2.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP).

Brain tissue or cultured astrocytes were lysed in a buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma, 4693116001), phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and P0013 (Beyotime) for 30 min on ice. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. Total protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay kit. A 50-µL aliquot of each lysate was retained as the input control. The remaining lysates were subjected to Co-IP or IP assays. For Co-IP/IP, protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime) were incubated with either anti-Flag or anti-CLU antibodies at RT for 1–2 h and washed three times with washing buffer. Antibody-conjugated beads were then added to the protein lysates, followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the beads were washed five times and then boiled at 95 °C for 5 min in 1× loading buffer to release the bound proteins. The resulting supernatants were collected for subsequent western blot analysis. Band intensities were quantified using a C600 multifunctional molecular imaging system (AZURE, USA).

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed with 1000 ng of RNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit (AG Biotechnology, China), which includes genomic DNA removal components, in a 20 µL reaction volume. For quantification of mRNA expression, the resulting cDNA was amplified by real-time quantitative PCR using SYBR Green Pro Taq Master Mix (AG) on an ABI Q6 system. GAPDH was used as the internal control. All samples were run in triplicate. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table 3.

CCK8 assays

Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime). C8-DIA cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well and treated or not with 1 µg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were washed with PBS, and 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent and 100 µL of DMEM were added to each well. After a 2-h incubation at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax 190 microplate reader.

Cell death assay

Cells were stained using 1 µL/mL LiveDye and 1 µL/mL NucleiDye (Abbkine). After two gentle PBS washes, the cells were incubated in staining solution for 30 min at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Following two additional washes, the cells were imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope, where LiveDye and NucleiDye identified live and dead cells, respectively. To quantify dead cells, all single-channel images were processed by applying a fixed threshold for binarization followed by particle analysis [35]. Representative images closest to the mean quantitative results were selected for presentation.

Generation of the polyclonal rabbit anti-2-hydroxyisobutyryl CLU (K436) antibody

A rabbit polyclonal antibody specific to CLU Khib-K436 was generated by PTM Biolab Co., Ltd. The immunogen comprised two synthetic peptides corresponding to CLU sequences containing K436 modified by 2-hydroxybutyrylation (TVAE-(Khib)-ALQEYRRKC and VAE-(Khib)-ALQEYRRC). A non-modified peptide (TVAE-K-ALQEYRRKC) was also synthesized and validated by mass spectrometry. New Zealand White rabbits were immunized in a pathogen-free facility. Antisera were affinity-purified using protein A columns and validated by ELISA, dot blot, and western blot.

Dot blot analysis

Protein samples were spotted on a nitrocellulose membrane and air-dried for 10 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA at RT for 1 h and washed five times with TBST (5–10 min per wash). The membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed again with TBST, and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent and visualized with the AMERSHAM ImageQuant 800 system.

LC-MS/MS analysis of protein 2-hydroxybutyrylation

Protein extraction

Frozen brain tissues (− 80 °C) were lysed by ultrasonication in a 4-fold volume of lysis buffer containing 8 M urea, 1% protease inhibitor, 1% phosphatase inhibitor, 3 µM trichostatin A (TSA), and 50 mM nicotinamide (NAM). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay.

Trypsin digestion

Protein concentrations across all samples were equalized with lysis buffer, and 20% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was gradually added. Samples were vortexed and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h to precipitate proteins. After centrifugation at 4,500 × g for 5 min, the supernatants were discarded, and the precipitates were washed 2–3 times with pre-cooled acetone. After drying, the pellets were resuspended in 200 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB), sonicated to disperse aggregates, and digested overnight with trypsin at a 1:50 (enzyme: protein, w/w) ratio. For reduction and alkylation, dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to a final concentration of 5 mM, followed by incubation at 56 °C for 30 min. Iodoacetamide (IAA) was then added to a final concentration of 11 mM, and the samples were incubated in the dark at RT for 15 min.

Peptide fractionation and immunoaffinity enrichment

To analyze the Khib proteome and enrich for modified peptides, peptides were dissolved in IP buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 0.5% NP-40, pH 8.0). The supernatant was transferred to pre-washed antibody resin (PTM804, Hangzhou Jingjie Biotechnology Co., PTM Bio), placed on a rotary shaker at 4 °C, gently agitated, and incubated overnight. Following incubation, the resin was washed four times with IP buffer and twice with ddH₂O. Bound peptides were eluted three times with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, and the eluates were collected and vacuum freeze-dried. The desalted peptides, processed according to C18 ZipTip instructions, were then vacuum freeze-dried for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Peptides were separated using a NanoElute ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system following reconstitution in mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile in water). Mobile phase B consisted of 98% acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution program was 6% to 22% B over 18 min, 22% to 30% over 4 min, 30% to 80% over 4 min, and maintained at 80% for the final 4 min. The flow rate was 450 nL/min. Separated peptides were ionized via a capillary ion source and introduced into a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer. The ion source voltage was set at 1.6 kV. Both precursor and fragment ions were analyzed using time-of-flight (TOF) detection in data-independent acquisition parallel accumulation–serial fragmentation (dia-PASEF) mode. The primary MS scan range was set to 100–1700 m/z, followed by eight PASEF acquisitions. Secondary MS scans ranged from 400 to 1200 m/z with a 25 m/z isolation window.

Database search

The resulting MS/MS data were processed using the MaxQuant search engine (v.1.6.15.0). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the Mus musculus UniProt database (17,045 sequences) concatenated with a reverse decoy database to calculate the false discovery rate (FDR) arising from random matches, with Trypsin/P selected as the digestion enzyme, allowing up to four missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while variable modifications included methionine oxidation, N-terminal protein acetylation, and lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation. The FDR was controlled at 1% for protein, peptide, and PSM identifications. To ensure high data quality, additional filtering required at least one unique peptide per protein, maintaining precursor and protein FDRs at 1%.

Cellular RNA extraction using Trizol

For RNA extraction, the cell culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were lysed with 500 µL of Trizol for 5 min, pipetted, and transferred to a 1.5-mL RNase-free tube. After adding 100 µL of chloroform, the mixture was vigorously inverted for 15–30 s, incubated on ice for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was transferred to a fresh tube and mixed with an equal volume of isopropanol. Following a 15-min incubation at RT, the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. A visible RNA pellet was washed twice with 75% ethanol, centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and air-dried for 10 min. The RNA was dissolved in DEPC-treated water. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and reverse transcription was performed immediately to prevent transcript degradation.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA was employed. Statistical significance was denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001. Sample sizes and the number of replicates are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Systematic proteomics analysis of Khib modifications in APP/PS1 mice

To investigate the role of PTMs in AD, we employed a panel of pan-specific antibodies targeting five recently characterized lysine acylation marks to profile PTM patterns in 10-month-old WT and APP/PS1 transgenic AD model mice. Brain tissue lysates were subjected to immunoblot analysis using antibodies against acetylation, succinylation, crotonylation, malonylation, and 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation. Among these, only lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib) showed a significant increase in APP/PS1 mice compared to WT controls, while the levels of other modifications remained unchanged (Fig. S1A–E). Based on this observation, subsequent investigations focused on characterizing Khib modifications.

To characterize the global landscape of lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib) in AD, we performed systematic, quantitative Khib proteomic profiling using brain samples from WT controls and APP/PS1 transgenic AD model mice aged 10 months (n = 3 per group; Fig. 1A). Quality assessment based on Pearson correlation coefficients (PCCs) and relative standard deviations (RSDs) demonstrated high reproducibility among replicates (Fig. S2B, C). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation between AD (Group A) and WT (Group C) samples (Fig. S2A), indicating that the Khib signatures were significantly different between these groups. Quality control (QC) metrics confirmed the reliability of the dataset. Assessments of peptide length distribution (Fig. S2D, E), protein coverage (Fig. S2F), molecular weight distribution (Fig. S2G), and peptide counts (Fig. S2H) all fell within expected ranges. Proteomic analysis identified 2,258 proteins containing 9,881 Khib sites, each with a localization probability > 0.75 and a FDR of 1% (Fig. 1B). Differentially expressed Khib sites (DEKSs) and proteins (DEKPs) were defined by a fold change > 1.5 or < 0.667 and p < 0.05. This yielded 84 DEKSs and 80 DEKPs distinguishing WT from AD brain samples. Specifically, 60 Khib sites were upregulated and 24 were downregulated, corresponding to 56 and 24 proteins, respectively (Fig. 1C and Table S4). These findings indicated that there is a pronounced trend toward Khib upregulation in the AD brain. Heatmap analysis highlighted marked differences in the Khib profile between the AD and WT groups (Fig. 1D). To elucidate the functional implications of Khib modifications, we performed a Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEKPs. The results indicated that there is a significant enrichment in biological processes related to amyloid regulation, particularly regarding the formation of amyloid fibrils (Fig. 1E). Molecular function analysis further revealed enrichment in chaperone binding and amyloid protein binding (Fig. 1F). Subcellular localization of DEKPs was analyzed using bioinformatics tools (Fig. 1G). Additionally, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network constructed using the STRING database identified functional interactions among 37 DEKPs, highlighting candidates involved in AD pathogenesis. Notably, the network revealed potential interactions between CLU and APP, of which are implicated in AD progression (Fig. 1H).

Identification of DEKPs and verification of CLU Khib at the K436 site in WT and APP/PS1 mice

Given the predominant increase in Khib modification observed in the brains of AD model mice, our study focused on proteins exhibiting elevated Khib levels. To identify functionally relevant Khib sites, we generated a volcano plot quantifying Khib-modified peptides, highlighting DEKPs and sites (DEKSs) (Fig. 2A). Among the DEKPs, CLU, a molecular chaperone, drew particular attention given that it has been implicated in the regulation and binding of amyloid-β, a central component in AD pathogenesis. The detected CLU bands ranged from 35 to 60 kDa, aligning with the 30–80 kDa range typical of Khib-modified proteins. These observations prompted us to select CLU for further investigation. To validate Khib modification of CLU in AD, we compared CLU Khib levels between AD model mice and WT controls. Immunoprecipitation using an anti-CLU antibody followed by immunoblotting with a pan-Khib antibody revealed that the levels of Khib-modified CLU were significantly elevated in the brains of APP/PS1 mice, with a prominent band detected at ~ 35 kDa (Fig. 2B). This result corroborated our proteomic findings, indicating that Khib modification of CLU is increased in AD. Surprisingly, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that CLU was associated with a wide array of biological processes and molecular functions (Fig. S3A, B). To further explore potential regulatory sites, we analyzed the amino acid sequences flanking the Khib residues using iceLogo and Motif-X, and identified two conserved modification motifs: lysine 53 (K53) within the XXXEKXXX motif and lysine 436 (K436) within XXXVKXXX (Fig. 2C, E). Analysis of MS spectra confirmed the presence of Khib modifications at both sites (Fig. 2D), though the fold change at K436 was greater, suggesting that this site undergoes greater Khib enrichment. Cross-species conservation analysis revealed that K436 is highly conserved from mouse to human, which indicates that it may possess significant functional importance (Fig. 2F). To determine the regulatory impact of these sites, we generated CLU mutants in which K53 or K436 was substituted with arginine (K53R, K436R), mimicking the de-Khib state. HEK-293T cells were transfected with FLAG-tagged wild-type (WT) or mutant CLU constructs. Immunoblotting results showed that the K436R mutation led to a markedly reduced Khib level compared to K53R, identifying K436 as a predominant modification site (Fig. 2G). To confirm site specificity, we generated an antibody targeting CLU Khib at K436. Dot blot assays confirmed that this antibody selectively recognized the Khib-modified K436 peptide rather than its unmodified counterpart (Fig. 2H), indicating that the antibody possesses high specificity. Western blot analysis using this antibody further validated that Khib levels were reduced in cells expressing the K436R mutant (Fig. 2I). Finally, immunohistochemistry revealed CLU Khib at K436 was increased in the cortex and hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice compared with that in WT controls (Fig. 2J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CLU undergoes Khib modification at lysine 436 in AD model mice and highlight its potential role in AD-related pathophysiology.

De-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of the CLU K436 site attenuated astrogliosis, neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity while improving viability in vitro

LPS is commonly used to establish in vitro neuroinflammatory models with mouse astrocytes (C8-D1A) [36–38]. Given that astrocytes are the predominant source of CLU in the brain, we selected C8-D1A cells for this study. To investigate the functional impact of CLU Khib on astrocytes, site-specific mutations were introduced into CLU. C8-D1A cells were infected with lentiviral (LV) vectors overexpressing either CLU WT or a K436R mutant (non-Khib mimetic). First, CLU mRNA levels were assessed via PCR. The results confirmed that CLU expression was significantly upregulated in cells transduced with either CLU WT or CLU K436R relative to that in cells harboring vector controls, with no difference between the two CLU constructs (Fig. 3A). Next, the CLU Khib level was evaluated by immunoprecipitation and western blot analysis. The Khib signal was markedly reduced in cells expressing the CLU K436R mutant compared to those expressing WT CLU, confirming that mimicry of the de-Khib state was successful (Fig. 3B). To assess the antineuroinflammatory effect of the CLU K436R mutant following LPS-induced astrocyte activation, we stimulated C8-D1A cells with LPS for 24 h and measured the mRNA levels of proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β) and neurotoxic astrocyte markers (C3, Ggta1, Ligp1, Serping1 [A1 marker], and S100a [A2 marker]) by qPCR. LPS treatment significantly increased the expression of IL-6, C3, and Ggta1. Notably, compared to CLU WT, CLU K436R suppressed astrocyte reactivity, as evidenced by the observed reduction in C3 and Ggta1 expression (Fig. 3C, D), and attenuated the LPS-induced upregulation of IL-6 (Fig. 3E). The expression levels of Ligp1, TNF-α, Serping1, and S100a were not significantly altered by LPS treatment (Fig. S4A–D). High levels of either CLU WT or CLU K436R had no significant effect on basal cell viability (Fig. 3F). However, in the presence of LPS, cells expressing CLU K436R exhibited significantly higher viability than those expressing CLU WT or the vector control (Fig. 3G). To determine whether CLU K436R could mitigate astrocyte-mediated neurotoxicity, we applied astrocyte-conditioned medium (ACM) from vector, vector-LPS, CLU-WT-LPS, or CLU-K436R-LPS groups to mouse Neuro-2a cell cultures. Exposure to ACM from CLU-K436R-LPS cells resulted in significantly less neuronal death compared with ACM from CLU-WT-LPS cells (Fig. 3H, I), indicating that de-Khib at CLU K436 suppresses reactive astrocytes neurotoxicity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation at CLU K436 may confers neuroprotection by reducing astrocyte activation, neuroinflammation, and neurotoxicity while enhancing cell viability in vitro.

De-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of the CLU K436 site ameliorated learning and memory deficits in vivo

To evaluate the neuroprotective effects of CLU Khib modification at K436 in vivo, we substituted lysine 436 with arginine (K436R) to mimic the de-Khib state. For brain-wide astrocyte-specific expression, AAV vectors encoding murine CLU WT, CLU K436R, or a null control were bilaterally injected intracerebroventricularly into 8-month-old APP/PS1 mice and their WT littermates. Astrocyte-specific expression was driven by the glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) promoter. Two months after viral transduction, we conducted behavioral testing followed by postmortem analyses (Fig. 4A, Fig. S5). Intracerebroventricular (ICV) administration of AAV resulted in high astrocyte-specific transduction efficiency across the brain. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed robust colocalization of GFP with GFAP-positive astrocytes, with no detectable expression in microglia or neurons in either the hippocampus or cortex, confirming the cell-type specificity of the vector (Fig. 4B, C). As expected, western blot analysis showed a marked increase in CLU expression in both CLU WT and CLU K436R mutant mice relative to GFP-only APP/PS1 controls (Fig. 4D). Furthermore, the Khib level at CLU K436 was significantly reduced in APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice compared with that in APP/PS1-CLU mice (Fig. 4E). To directly assess the functional impact of CLU Khib at K436 on cognition, we conducted behavioral assessments. Spatial learning and memory were evaluated using the MWM test. During training (days 1–5), APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice showed significantly shorter escape latencies than APP/PS1-GFP mice (Fig. 4F, S6A), indicating that the K436R mutation enhances spatial learning. In the probe trial (day 6), where the platform was removed, APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice exhibited a higher number of crossings of the previous platform location (Fig. 4H) and spent more time in the target quadrant than APP/PS1-GFP mice (Fig. 4I); no differences in swimming speed were observed across groups (Fig. 4G). These findings suggested that de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation at CLU K436 enhances memory performance without affecting motor function. These results further demonstrate that de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of CLU at K436 alleviates cognitive deficits in middle-aged APP/PS1 mice, highlighting its therapeutic potential for modulating astrocyte reactivity and neurodegeneration.

To further evaluate hippocampus-dependent spatial working memory and recognition memory in mice, we conducted Y-maze and novel object recognition (NOR) tests. The Y-maze test revealed that the CLU K436 mutation significantly alleviated spatial memory impairments without affecting locomotor activity. This was evidenced by the increased rate of spontaneous alternation, a comparable number of total arm entries, and the higher number of entries into the novel arm in APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice relative to APP/PS1-GFP and APP/PS1-CLU mice (Fig. 4J–L, S6B). In the NOR test, while no differences in total distance traveled were observed across groups on day 1, APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice spent significantly more time in the central area compared with APP/PS1-GFP and APP/PS1-CLU mice (Fig. 4M, N, Fig. S6C), suggesting that CLU K436 de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation influences anxiety-related behavior but not general locomotion. However, there were no significant differences in object exploration preference among the groups during the recognition test (Fig. S6D–E), indicating that spatial memory was unaffected. These findings provide strong evidence that de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation at the CLU K436 site may serve as a potential therapeutic target to counteract cognitive and memory deficits in vivo.

De-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of the CLU K436 site suppressed Aβ generation in vivo

To investigate the role and therapeutic relevance of Khib modification at CLU K436 in AD pathogenesis, we evaluated Aβ pathology 2 months post-microinjection. Baseline Aβ levels were established in uninjected 8-month-old APP/PS1 mice. The plaque burden in the cortex and hippocampus of 8- and 10-month-old APP/PS1 mice was quantified using an anti-Aβ42 antibody. APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice exhibited a significantly lower Aβ burden than either APP/PS1-CLU or APP/PS1-10 M mice. No significant difference in Aβ deposition was observed between APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice and APP/PS1-8 M mice, though there was a trend toward a slight increase, indicating that CLU K436R suppressed the progression of amyloid pathology rather than leading to the clearance of existing deposits. This was also corroborated by a decrease in the number of plaques in both brain regions (Fig. 5A–E). Immunostaining with X04, which specifically labels fibrillar Aβ, further confirmed these results, showing significantly less amyloid accumulation in the cortex and hippocampus of APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice (Fig. S7A, B). To further explore how CLU K436 de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation modulates Aβ accumulation, we quantified soluble and insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 levels by ELISA. In the hippocampus, APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice had significantly lower levels of soluble Aβ40, soluble Aβ42, and insoluble Aβ42 than APP/PS1-CLU mice (Fig. S8A–D). Similarly, soluble Aβ40 levels were lower in the cortex, although no significant differences were found for soluble Aβ42 or insoluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 in this region (Fig. S8E–H). To probe the underlying mechanisms, we assessed the expression levels of key proteins involved in Aβ generation and synthesis. Surprisingly, APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice exhibited significant downregulation of amyloid precursor protein (APP), β-secretase 1 (BACE1), and presenilin-1 (PS1) in whole-brain lysates relative to both APP/PS1-CLU and APP/PS1-GFP mice (Fig. 5F). However, no significant differences were observed in the levels of insulin-degrading enzyme (IDE) and neprilysin (NEP), both major Aβ-degrading enzymes (Fig. 5G). Immunofluorescence analysis further confirmed that APP levels were reduced in the cortex and hippocampus of APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice (Fig. S7C–F). As APP/PS1 mice are transgenic models that inherently overexpress APP and PS1, it was necessary to further examine whether high levels of also influence the expression of APP, BACE1, and PS1 in a WT background. We found that CLU-K436R expression did not significantly alter the protein levels of endogenous APP, BACE1, or PS1 in the brains of 10-month-old WT mice (Fig. S9A, B). Collectively, these data suggested that while CLU K436 de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation reduces the expression of APP, PS1, and BACE1, thereby suppressing Aβ production in vivo, it may not affect its clearance.

De-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of the CLU K436 site mitigated astrocytes and microglia activation in vivo

Given the known association between gliosis and amyloid pathology, we next examined how CLU K436 de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation affects glial activation in APP/PS1 mice. Western blot analysis revealed significantly decreased expression of GFAP and IBA-1—markers of astrocytes and microglia, respectively—in the brains of APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice compared to APP/PS1-CLU controls (Fig. 6A–C). Histological staining with GFAP and IBA-1 further demonstrated that gliosis was reduced in both the cortex and hippocampus. Specifically, APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice exhibited a significantly lower number of IBA1⁺ microglia surrounding amyloid plaques in the hippocampus compared to APP/PS1-CLU mice (Fig. 6D, F, G). Similarly, the IBA1⁺ cell number and area were markedly reduced in the cortex of APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice (Fig. 6E, I, J). However, when normalized to the extent of fibrillar Aβ deposition, these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 6H, K). Likewise, the number of GFAP⁺ astrocytes around plaques was significantly reduced in both the hippocampus and cortex of APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice (Fig. 6L–O, Q, R). However, GFAP signal intensity normalized to fibrillar amyloid did not differ between groups (Fig. 6P, S). These results suggested that CLU K436 de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation attenuates glial activation—both astrocytic and microglial—in a manner associated with reduced Aβ deposition.

Given that the de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of CLU influences glial cell activation, we investigated the effects of CLU K436 Khib modification on neuronal and synaptic integrity. Western blot analysis revealed no significant differences in the expression levels of synaptophysin and PSD95 between groups. Similar results were observed for NeuN expression, as shown by both western blot and immunoblot analyses (Fig. S10A–D, E–H). These findings indicate that Khib modification at CLU K436 may modulates glial activation without affecting the expression of key neuronal and synaptic markers.

De-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of the CLU K436 site ameliorated neuroinflammation and reduced amyloid-related neurotoxicity

We next assessed whether de-Khib at CLU K436 influences neuroinflammation. Protein levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, TGF-β1, and IL-6 were quantified in hippocampal and cortical tissues using ELISA. De-Khib at K436 significantly reduced the levels of these cytokines in the hippocampus compared to APP/PS1-CLU mice (Fig. 7A–C). Similar reductions were observed in cortical tissues (Fig. 7D–F), suggesting that de-Khib at this site attenuates neuroinflammatory responses.

Neuritic dystrophy, marked by abnormal swelling of dendrites and axons, is commonly found around fibrillar plaques [39]. To evaluate the role of CLU K436 de-Khib in neuritic pathology, we performed histological analyses using LAMP1 immunolabeling to visualize dystrophic neurites adjacent to X-34-positive fibrillar amyloid plaques. APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice exhibited a marked reduction in LAMP1-positive regions in both the hippocampus and cortex compared to APP/PS1-CLU controls, corresponding to with an overall decrease in the plaque burden (Fig. 7G–I, K–M). Additionally, both the average size of LAMP1-positive halos and the total LAMP1-labeled area were significantly reduced in K436R mice (Fig. 7J, N). Together, these results demonstrated that de-Khib at CLU K436 may alleviate neuroinflammation and amyloid-induced neurotoxicity in vivo.

Khib modification of CLU at K436 may influences the interaction between CLU and Aβ

PTMs critically regulate protein behavior, including subcellular localization and protein–protein interactions, thereby shaping cellular function in both health and disease [40]. In this study, we demonstrated that mimicking de-Khib at CLU K436 (via K436R mutation) mitigates Aβ plaque formation and related neuropathology. Next, we sought to identify the underlying mechanism. CLU is known to colocalize with Aβ in both AD model mice and human AD brains [24], prompting the hypothesis that de-Khib at K436 may reduce CLU–Aβ interaction and thereby influence Aβ accumulation. To test this, we assessed CLU–Aβ interactions using Co-IP and immunoblotting. Immunofluorescence revealed that CLU and Aβ levels were higher in APP/PS1 mice than in WT controls, with strong colocalization of CLU and Aβ plaques (Fig. 8A), consistent with previous reports. In contrast, APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice exhibited reduced CLU–Aβ interaction as shown by Co-IP (Fig. 8B). Correspondingly, CLU–Aβ42 colocalization was significantly decreased in LV-CLU K436R-infected C8-D1A cells compared with that in LV-CLU WT–infected cells (Fig. 8C, D).

To determine whether Khib modification affects CLU subcellular localization, we undertook a confocal microscopic analysis of C8-D1A cells expressing CLU WT or CLU K436R. Both forms were predominantly localized in the cytoplasm with minor nuclear distribution, and no significant differences were observed between the groups (Fig. S11). These findings suggested that Khib modification at K436 does not alter CLU subcellular localization. Collectively, our results indicated that de-Khib at CLU K436 may reduce CLU interaction with Aβ without affecting its intracellular distribution.

Discussion

This study provides the first in vivo evidence that lysine Khib modifications, a recently identified PTM, plays a critical functional role in AD. We identified CLU as a key Khib-modified protein in APP/PS1 mice and pinpointed K436 as its major modification site. Strikingly, the loss of Khib at this site (CLU K436R mutation) conferred potent neuroprotective effects, which can suppress amyloid pathology, mitigate neuroinflammation, and rescue cognitive deficits in late-stage AD model mice. The underlying mechanism may involve reducing the interaction between CLU and Aβ (Fig. 8E). These findings establish CLU K436hib as a central regulatory node in AD pathogenesis and a promising therapeutic target for the condition.

Protein PTMs refer to covalent alterations of amino acid residues or the addition of functional groups. These modifications critically expand protein functional diversity by modulating properties such as molecular interactions, subcellular localization, enzymatic activity, and stability [40–42]. Emerging evidence has implicated several PTMs—such as lysine acetylation [43], succinylation [44], lactylation [45], and β-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb)—in the pathology of AD [46]. Notably, among the five novel PTM types examined in our study, lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib) showed substantial upregulation in AD model mice. However, its biological role and regulatory mechanisms in AD remain poorly understood. Proteomic analysis of brain tissues from WT and APP/PS1 transgenic mice revealed significant Khib involvement in AD. We identified 2,258 Khib-modified proteins and 9,881 modification sites, of which 80 proteins and 84 sites were differentially modified in APP/PS1 mice. This comprehensive Khib proteomic dataset potentially provides valuable insights into the pathogenic mechanisms of AD and other neurodegenerative diseases. Among the differentially modified proteins, CLU emerged as a key candidate, exhibiting significantly increased Khib levels in AD model mice. Given its established role in AD pathogenesis, CLU was selected as the focus of our investigation. Although CLU is known to be involved in AD, the specific pattern and functional consequences of its Khib modification were previously uncharacterized. We identified lysine 436 (K436) as the primary Khib site on CLU and demonstrated that this modification plays a critical role in AD pathology. Khib is a reversible modification regulated by lysine acyltransferases (KATs) and deacylases (KDACs). Enzymes such as P300, Tip60, and CobB have been identified as Khib-modifying enzymes, while HDAC2 and HDAC3 function as demodifying enzymes [11, 47, 48]. Further research is required to elucidate the regulatory network of protein acylation and deacylation associated with Khib. Furthermore, an important consideration arising from our study is the potential for crosstalk between Khib and other post-translational modifications at K436. It has recently been shown that lysine residues are common substrates for a range of modifications, including acetylation, succinylation, and ubiquitination, which can compete for the same site and lead to distinct, often opposing, functional outcomes [49, 50]. Therefore, it is plausible that the chaperone activity of CLU is regulated via the dynamic equilibrium of modifying enzymes at K436. The increase in Khib observed in APP/PS1 mice may reflect a compensatory cellular mechanism that either outcompetes a degradation signal, such as ubiquitination, or counteracts an inhibitory modification, such as acetylation, thereby stabilizing CLU and enhancing its neuroprotective function. Hence, it is essential to explore the potential for crosstalk among different PTM at the K436 site, which could provide insights into how CLU modifications are involved in the pathological mechanisms of AD.

CLU, a molecular chaperone, interacts with Aβ, influencing its aggregation, fibrillization, and clearance [26]. Recent studies suggest that the relative CLU/Aβ ratio is a critical determinant of Aβ aggregation dynamics, where a higher ratio promotes Aβ solubility, while a lower ratio facilitates Aβ deposition [51]. It has also been proposed that CLU upregulation contributes to AD risk by inhibiting Aβ degradation and promoting its production, potentially through a histone-CLU-Aβ axis [27]. Other findings indicate that CLU expression accelerates Aβ deposition in the early stages of AD in mouse models [52]. Consistent with these reports, our study showed that APP/PS1 mice expressing CLU with a K436 mutation (APP/PS1-CLUK436R) had reduced levels of Aβ and Aβ-associated proteins compared to APP/PS1-CLU mice. Interestingly, APP/PS1-CLU mice exhibited higher Aβ expression than APP/PS1 controls. These findings prompted us to explore how the CLU K436 mutation influences Aβ metabolism. CLU is consistently co-localized with Aβ in both AD model mice and human brain tissue [25]. Prior studies have shown that CLU can stabilize Aβ in soluble complexes that slowly precipitate, possibly intensifying neurotoxicity in the brains of AD model mice [53]. Thus, we hypothesized that Khib at CLU K436 modulates its interaction with Aβ, potentially affecting Aβ expression. To confirm this hypothesis, our immunoprecipitation analysis revealed that a diminished CLU-Aβ interaction was diminished in APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice relative to that in APP/PS1-CLU mice.

Moreover, we found the protein levels of APP, BACE1, and PS1 were reduced in 10-month-old APP/PS1 mice expressing CLU-K436R. As these transgenes are under the control of exogenous promoters, this effect is unlikely to be transcriptional. We propose several post-transcriptional mechanisms. First, CLU-K436R, acting as an enhanced molecular chaperone, may facilitate the degradation of these proteins via proteasomal or autophagic pathways [19, 51, 54]. Second, the potent anti-inflammatory effect of CLU-K436R likely leads to the suppression of stress-responsive pathways known to upregulate BACE1 expression and modulate APP metabolism [55–59]. Finally, the substantial reduction in Aβ burden may disrupt a pathogenic feedback loop, leading to a downstream normalization of the entire APP processing machinery. However, this effect was not observed in matched WT mice. This pathology-dependent regulation suggests that the downregulatory effect observed in APP/PS1 mice is not a general property of CLU-K436R but is specifically elicited within the pathological context of AD. Additionally, this effect is potentially likely dependent on the presence of Aβ pathology or associated neuroinflammation, which could create a cellular environment where this regulatory mechanism is engaged. Notably, no significant difference in the protein levels of IDE and NEP was observed between the APP/PS1-CLU and APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice. This suggests that CLU-K436R exerts its potent anti-amyloid effects primarily by suppressing Aβ production via reductions in APP, BACE1, and PS1 levels, with little to no impact on Aβ clearance pathways. Beyond that, we found that Aβ burden in APP/PS1-CLU K436R mice at 10 months was comparable to pre-injection levels at 8 months. This indicates that the marked reduction observed relative to control mice at 10 months is primarily attributable to the potent inhibition of new Aβ accumulation during the intervention period, rather than the clearance of plaques present at the time of treatment. This intervention suppressed the progression of amyloid pathology over a critical two-month period in the APP/PS1 model, demonstrating a highly significant disease-modifying effect. Collectively, we propose that the K436R mutation alters the molecular chaperone function of CLU, thereby weakening its pathological interaction with Aβ. This altered interaction, particularly in a disease context, ultimately destabilizes the amyloidogenic machinery, leading to a coordinated downregulation of APP, BACE1, and PS1 levels. This reduces Aβ production to suppress amyloidogenesis, rather than enhancing its clearance, and consequently diminishes Aβ accumulation.

In contrast to our findings, a recent study reported that CLU overexpression in astrocytes of APP/PS1 mice reduced the Aβ aggregate burden and ameliorated amyloid-induced neurotoxicity and gliosis [60]. Several factors may account for this discrepancy: (1) The timing of intervention: In Wojtas’s study, AAVs were administered via two protocols—bilateral intracerebroventricular injection at postnatal day 2 (P2) in both APP/PS1 and WT mice, and intracerebroventricular injection at 5 months of age in APP/PS1 mice, with analysis at 8 months. In our study, however, AAV was injected bilaterally into the lateral ventricles of 8-month-old APP/PS1 mice, with expression sustained until approximately 10 months of age. (2) We specifically modulated a PTM site, likely altering CLU function, rather than simply increasing its abundance. These observations suggest a dual, stage-dependent role for CLU: in early disease, its chaperone activity may promote Aβ clearance or sequester toxic species. However, in established pathology, Khib modification at K436 may lock CLU into a pathological state that facilitates Aβ aggregation and/or stabilizes the amyloidogenic machinery. Our K436R mutation may therefore reverse this late-stage maladaptive function. Moreover, we found that the apparent discrepancy between the pronounced reduction in Aβ plaque burden observed by IHC and the more modest decrease in total insoluble Aβ quantified by ELISA. Methodologically, IHC is highly sensitive to the morphology and compactness of amyloid deposits, whereas ELISA measures the total biochemical pool of denaturant-extractable Aβ, including smaller aggregates that may be less visible by IHC. Biologically, We propose that CLU K436R actively remodels Aβ pathology. Beyond merely lowering Aβ levels, it drives the disaggregation of IHC-visible plaques into more diffuse forms.

Recent studies have shown that Aβ plaques induce significant changes in microglia and astrocytes [61–63], with both Aβ oligomers and fibrils capable of activating these glial cells [64, 65]. In our study, Khib-associated mutations led to a marked reduction in Aβ accumulation. However, given that the number of microglia and astrocytes surrounding Aβ plaques remained unchanged, we hypothesize that the CLU K436R mutation does not affects neither astrocyte responsiveness to plaques nor microglial migration—at least not in terms of morphology. Instead, changes in astrocyte reactivity may be driven by the overall reduction in plaque burden. Interestingly, our data indicate that Khib modification at CLU K436 primarily influences glial cells rather than neurons. The neuronal alterations observed in vivo may reflect an early-to-intermediate disease stage, where activated glia exert neuroprotective roles or neurons engage compensatory mechanisms, potentially delaying overt neurodegeneration.

Beyond Aβ aggregation, AD is characterized by neuroinflammation, which plays a central role in disease initiation and progression [66]. Studies have shown that CLU dysregulation is linked to increased levels of neuroinflammatory markers, potentially exacerbating AD pathology [67]. Aligning with this, our ELISA results revealed that CLU K436R mice exhibited reduced inflammatory gene expression, while CLU expressing mice showed elevated cytokine levels. These findings suggested that the K436R mutation attenuates chemokine production and may mitigate AD pathogenesis. It remains unclear whether such effects are directly mediated by the K436R mutation or are secondary to reduced Aβ pathology, warranting further investigation.

Despite the importance of our findings, this study has several limitations. First, our current findings are based on a late-stage disease model (8 and 10-month-old APP/PS1 mice) with pre-existing amyloid pathology. Within this context, we could not distinguish whether Khib is a causative agent in Aβ deposition or a responsive element to it, and determine if CLU K436R can promote clearance when administered at stages of less severe pathology. Second, we did not elucidate the precise molecular steps from the K436R mutation to the downregulation of APP/BACE1/PS1. Third, potential crosstalk between Khib and other PTMs such as acetylation or ubiquitination at K436 could create a dynamic regulatory code influencing CLU function, which remains unexplored. Moreover, the precise mechanism by which CLU K436hib regulates its interaction with Aβ, whether through structural changes or altered binding affinity, remains unresolved. Finally, in this study, the detection of Khib in human AD brain tissue was not performed, resulting in a lack of validation using human Alzheimer’s disease samples.

Further research is necessary to delineate the precise role of Khib modi fication in AD. Future directions include: (1) Earlier time-point interventions, along with the analysis of APP/PS1 mice at different age stages and exogenously Aβ-induced models, will be used to determine whether CLU K436R can also promote Aβ clearance and to define the causal role of Khib. (2) Using structural biology and biophysical approaches to decipher how Khib alters CLU conformation and Aβ binding. Specifically, employing molecular docking simulations to predict and analyze the CLU-Aβ interaction, circular dichroism to assess secondary structure, and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) to quantitatively compare the binding kinetics and affinity of site-specifically modified CLU variants for Aβ. (3) Profile the full PTM landscape at K436 under different pathological conditions to understand potential competitive modifications. (4) To explore Khib in human AD brain samples, which will helpful to provide a potential therapeutic target for clinical intervention.

Conclusions

In conclusion, by identifying and functionally validating CLU K436 as a critical Khib site, we have unveiled a novel PTM-mediated regulatory layer in AD. Targeting this mechanism offers a promising treatment strategy for suppressing AD disease progression.
