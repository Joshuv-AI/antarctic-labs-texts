Machine-learning-assisted continuous flow synthesis of clonidine
Authors: Cloudius R Sagandira, Sinazo Nqeketo, Alexander Pomberger, Daniel Wigh, Christophe Len
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClonidine•HCl is a clinically important imidazoline derivative whose manufacturing routes have seen little innovation since their development in the mid-to-late 20th century. Its widespread use as an antihypertensive and neuroactive agent underscores the need for more efficient, modern production strategies. Herein, we report the first continuous flow strategy for the synthesis of clonidine. By integrating telescoped multistep sequences, inline liquid-liquid workup, advanced solid handling, and machine learning, we developed an intensified process that delivers clonidine•HCl in only 24 min total residence time across 5 continuous transformations, achieving an overall yield of 54%. This approach significantly outperforms conventional batch protocols, which typically require more than 18 h. The platform enables efficient and precisely controlled isothiocyanate formation, thiourea hydrolysis, S-methylation, and imidazoline cyclisation, while reducing intermediate isolations, minimising reagent handling, and eliminating hazardous materials associated with legacy routes. These advances provide a scalable, data-driven manufacturing solution and establish a foundation for intensified production of clonidine and related APIs under continuous flow conditions.Subject terms: Green chemistry, Chemical engineering, Flow chemistry

Introduction

The pharmaceutical industry is grappling with unprecedented pressure to meet escalating global health demands in an era defined by complex and multifaceted challenges. Factors such as the high cost of medicines, persistent quality control issues, climate change-related disruptions and recurring supply chain shortages have created a resource-constrained environment that demands innovative solutions1–3. These challenges are compounded by the need to ensure equitable access to affordable, high-quality medications while maintaining compliance with stringent regulatory standards and addressing environmental sustainability concerns1,2,4–6. In this context, continuous flow manufacturing has emerged as a transformative technology, offering a robust framework for rapidly scaling production capacity, enhancing supply chain resilience and driving more efficient, sustainable and cost-effective manufacturing processes for pharmaceuticals7–16.

The adoption of continuous flow chemistry in industry has been strongly supported by global regulatory authorities7,13,14, including the United States Food and Drug Administration17, the European Medicines Agency18 and Japan’s Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency12. These agencies recognise the advantages of flow systems in improving process control, reducing variability and enhancing product quality. Importantly, continuous flow platforms enable precise management of reaction parameters, which aligns with the industry’s drive toward more reliable, safer and environmentally sustainable API manufacturing.

In parallel with advances in process intensification, the integration of automation and machine learning into continuous flow chemistry has significantly accelerated the development of efficient and high-performance synthetic processes19–21. Machine learning enables the extraction of predictive insight from complex experimental data and facilitates efficient navigation of high-dimensional reaction spaces that are otherwise challenging to interrogate using conventional approaches22

Within this paradigm, Bayesian optimisation has emerged as a particularly powerful strategy for reaction discovery and optimisation by coupling probabilistic surrogate modelling with sequential experimental design19,23,24. Numerous studies spanning diverse reaction classes have demonstrated that Bayesian optimisation, especially methods based on Gaussian process models, can markedly improve the identification of optimal reaction conditions while substantially reducing experimental effort19,20,23,24.

At its core, Bayesian optimisation addresses the efficient optimisation of expensive-to-evaluate objective functions. A probabilistic surrogate model is trained on accumulated experimental data to generate both predicted performance and associated uncertainty across the parameter space19,23,24. An acquisition function then guides the selection of subsequent experiments by balancing the exploitation of promising regions with the exploration of uncertain ones. Through iterative cycles of experimentation and model updating, optimal conditions are rapidly identified using far fewer experiments than exhaustive screening or one-variable-at-a-time methods19,22–25.

This approach is particularly well suited to continuous flow platforms, where automated control of reaction parameters, precise reagent delivery and rapid inline or online analysis enable the generation of high-quality datasets required for robust model training19,23. Bayesian optimisation has enabled the development of self-optimising flow reactors, uncovered complex nonlinear reaction landscapes and facilitated multi-objective optimisation involving trade-offs between yield, impurity profiles, space-time yield and sustainability metrics19,24,26. Across flow-based platforms, from nanomole-scale high-throughput screening to challenging bond-forming, redox and electrochemical transformations, these strategies have delivered substantial reductions in labour, reagent consumption and process development time19,23,24,27. Collectively, these advances highlight a deep and mutually reinforcing relationship between continuous flow chemistry and data-driven optimisation. The intrinsic reproducibility and precise control of flow platforms enable the consistent generation of high-quality experimental data, which is essential for building reliable and informative surrogate models23,27. Bayesian optimisation, in turn, exploits these data to prioritise the most informative experiments, systematically accelerating convergence toward optimal operating conditions while minimising experimental cost and resource use19,23,24. This integrated approach establishes a powerful and generalisable paradigm for the modernisation of pharmaceutical manufacturing, offering a practical route to robust, scalable and sustainable processes for legacy APIs that have historically resisted innovation.

Clonidine•HCl 1, an imidazoline derivative and mesomeric compound, is a centrally acting α₂-adrenergic agonist widely used as a hypotensive agent in the treatment of hypertension (Fig. 1)28,29. Beyond its antihypertensive applications, clonidine is also employed in the management of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, particularly in paediatric and adult populations, where it helps mitigate impulsivity, hyperactivity and inattention by modulating noradrenergic pathways30–32. Additionally, clonidine has off-label uses, including the treatment of opioid withdrawal, insomnia and certain pain conditions, underscoring its versatility in clinical practice33–35.

Despite the long‑established clinical importance of clonidine•HCl 1, its synthetic routes have undergone very little meaningful innovation since their development during the drug’s discovery and early process‑development period in the 1960s and through the 1980s36–40. Most reported methods depend on conventional batch processing and employ hazardous reagents, such as thiophosgene37,41. As a result, these routes are associated with significant safety concerns, long reaction times, difficult workups and poor scalability. The synthetic approaches described in the literature, largely developed by Boehringer Ingelheim28 together with other contributors, vary in their starting materials, reaction sequences and operating conditions, yet all rely exclusively on traditional batch technology36–38. Beyond that early period, the synthesis of clonidine•HCl 1 has not undergone any notable modernisation or improvement.

A common feature of these routes is the use of 2,6-dichloroaniline 4 as a key precursor40. One of the earliest and most frequently cited methods, developed by Boehringer Ingelheim during the initial process development of the drug, involves converting 2,6-dichloroaniline to 2,6-dichlorophenyl isothiocyanate using thiophosgene. This intermediate is then reacted with ethylenediamine to form the corresponding thiourea37,41. The thiourea subsequently undergoes cyclization in the presence of MeI and heat to form the characteristic imidazoline ring of clonidine37,41. Although this sequence is synthetically effective, it suffers from major limitations. The use of toxic thiophosgene and the generation of methanethiol byproduct pose serious safety and environmental hazards, and the overall efficiency is limited by constraints inherent to batch processing.

To address these inefficiencies, we investigated continuous-flow technology together with Bayesian-driven optimisation to develop an improved and scalable synthetic route to clonidine•HCl 1, building on the batch process outlined in Fig. 140,42,43. The formation of the thiourea intermediate 6 represents a critical step in the synthetic route, facilitating subsequent transformations leading to the target compound, clonidine•HCl 1. Continuous flow technology enables precise control of reaction parameters such as temperature, residence time and reagent stoichiometry13–16,44–46. This enables improved reaction efficiency, reduced formation of side products and the potential to telescope sequential steps. In addition, the integration of Bayesian optimisation with continuous flow synthesis19,21, supported by a growing body of evidence demonstrating its effectiveness, offers a powerful and data‑driven approach to the streamlined manufacture of active pharmaceutical ingredients. Herein, we present the first continuous flow synthetic route to clonidine•HCl 1, representing a significant advancement over established batch‑based methodologies.

Results and discussion

Flow synthesis of benzoyl thiourea 5

Following the batch protocol in Fig. 1 to prepare analytical standards, the synthetic route to clonidine•HCl 1 was initiated. In this sequence, benzoyl thiourea 5 is the first isolable intermediate, formed via a two‑step process beginning with the generation of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 from benzoyl chloride 2 and NH4SCN. Accordingly, establishing a robust continuous‑flow method for producing benzoyl thiourea 5 was a critical starting point for the overall flow synthesis.

Synthesis and optimisation of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 in a coil reactor

The synthesis of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 constitutes the first step in the overall route. To translate this transformation into a continuous setting, it was essential to first understand the limitations inherent to the batch protocol. In the batch process, benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 is formed through the reaction of benzoyl chloride 2 within acetone. During this batch synthesis work, a major operational challenge was identified. The reaction produces NH4Cl as an inorganic byproduct, and its formation leads to the accumulation of solid material within the reaction mixture.

The precipitation of NH4Cl presents a significant barrier to implementing this transformation under continuous flow conditions47,48. Solid formation in a flow system can result in deposition within tubing or reactor channels, ultimately causing clogging, pressure fluctuations and possible system failure. This issue is not unique to this system; rather, it reflects a broader challenge associated with heterogeneous by-product formation during flow processing. Effective solid handling remains one of the key bottlenecks in continuous-flow manufacturing. Therefore, overcoming this bottleneck required reconsideration of both reagent handling and reactor configuration. Although substantial advances have been made in recent years to enable the controlled processing of heterogeneous mixtures and suspensions47,48, solid formation continues to impose important constraints on reactor design and process robustness.

Direct translation of the batch synthesis to a continuous-flow synthesis necessitated targeted modifications to both the reaction process and the system configuration. To ensure compatibility with flow operation, it was essential to incorporate appropriate solid-management strategies available within our laboratory. These requirements informed the development of an adapted protocol for the continuous flow generation of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3.

Guided by these considerations, an adapted continuous flow design was implemented to ensure controlled operation despite in situ solid formation. Benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 was generated by reacting benzoyl chloride 2 (0.20 M) with NH4SCN (0.22 M) in acetone under continuous flow conditions (Fig. 2). The reaction was carried out in a coil reactor (1.58 mm OD, 0.76 mm ID) equipped with a peristaltic back-pressure regulator (7 bar) to maintain stable flow and prevent obstruction from the NH4Cl precipitate formed in situ during the transformation. The effects of temperature and residence time were evaluated, with reaction performance assessed by HPLC determination of the conversion of benzoyl chloride 2, quantified using a calibrated analytical standard curve. With stable operation established, the next objective was to define the optimal conditions for this transformation through data-driven optimisation.

Optimisation of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 formation was performed using Bayesian optimisation with temperature (25–70 °C) and residence time (1–5 min) as variables. Nine experiments were performed, comprising an initial Sobol-sampled set followed by sequential optimisation steps guided by Gaussian process model updates, balancing exploration and exploitation.

The contour and response-surface plots demonstrated that conversion is strongly temperature-dependent, increasing from roughly 60–70% at 25 °C to above 95% at temperatures exceeding 45 °C across most residence times (Fig. 3). In contrast, residence time shows a comparatively minor effect beyond approximately 2.5 min, as further increases do not lead to significant improvements in conversion. This observation is corroborated by the parameter-importance and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analyses, which identify temperature and the temperature–time interaction as the dominant contributors to reaction performance (Fig. 3b, c).

a 3D response-surface plot illustrating the combined effect of temperature and residence time on conversion. b Parameter-importance analysis highlighting the relative contributions of temperature, residence time and their interaction to reaction performance. c SHAP contribution plot for the best‑performing experiment, quantifying the individual influence of temperature and residence time on conversion.

The Bayesian model identified an optimal operating region of 55–70 °C and 1–3 min, which was confirmed experimentally, with the highest conversion (~100%) achieved at the upper end of this temperature range with a short residence time. Notably, the optimal balance between conversion and process efficiency was achieved at 70 °C and a residence time of 1 min, delivering near‑quantitative conversion and a throughput of 19.4 g h−1 of product in solution. These results show that the reaction is kinetically driven and that higher temperatures mitigate any mass-transfer limitations associated with the in situ formation of NH4Cl. Robust flow performance throughout optimisation also confirmed the effectiveness of the implemented solid-handling strategy, enabled by the use of a peristaltic back-pressure regulator and the relatively wide internal channel of the coil reactor. Overall, Bayesian optimisation enabled rapid identification of a high-efficiency and reliable continuous-flow operating space for the formation of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3.

These findings collectively defined a robust operating window; however, concentration effects remained an important factor to consider. Under the optimised conditions of 1 min residence time at 70 °C, increasing the reaction concentration to 0.5 M led to excessive formation of NH4Cl, which exceeded the solid-handling capacity of the reactor system. Consequently, a concentration of 0.2 M was selected to ensure reliable operation. At this concentration, benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 was collected continuously for 30 min without any reactor blockage, demonstrating robust and stable performance with a productivity of 9.7 g of product in solution over this time period. The reaction output was subsequently filtered to remove the in situ-formed NH4Cl, providing a homogeneous solution of 3 (600 mL) suitable for direct use in the downstream continuous-flow synthesis of benzoyl thiourea 5. Having established reliable conditions for generating benzoyl isothiocyanate 3, we next examined its downstream conversion to benzoyl thiourea 5 under continuous flow.

Optimisation was conducted using temperature (70–100 °C) and residence time (5–20 min) as variables across fourteen experiments, comprising an initial Sobol-sampled set followed by sequential Bayesian optimisation steps guided by iterative model updates. Reaction performance was evaluated based on the HPLC yield of benzoyl thiourea 5, quantified using a calibrated analytical standard curve.

The Bayesian optimisation 3D response-surface plot (Fig. 5a) shows that yield increases strongly with both temperature and residence time. Moderate yields of approximately 70–80% are observed at 70 °C and short residence times of 5–8 min, while increasing the temperature to 90–100 °C results in yields exceeding 90% across most conditions. Near-quantitative yields of ≥98% are achieved at temperatures close to 100 °C combined with residence times of 15–20 min, defining a robust high-yield operating plateau.

The most time‑efficient high‑performance conditions were identified at 100 °C with a residence time of 16 min, delivering benzoyl thiourea 5 in 99.2% yield. These conditions were selected as the optimal operating point, corresponding to a throughput of 1.2 g h−1 and highlighting the trade-off between reaction time and productivity. Parameter-importance analysis (Fig. 5b) confirms temperature as the dominant factor governing reaction performance, with residence time and the temperature-time interaction contributing to a lesser extent. This is supported by the SHAP analysis (Fig. 5c), which shows that, relative to a baseline yield of 92.2%, residence time and temperature provide positive contributions of +4.6% and +2.5%, respectively, resulting in a predicted yield of 99.3% under optimal conditions.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that benzoyl thiourea 5 formation is primarily temperature-driven and enhanced by sufficient residence time, while highlighting the effectiveness of Bayesian optimisation in rapidly defining a robust, high-yielding operating window. With optimal conditions established, the process was subsequently advanced toward telescoping to improve throughput and minimise intermediate handling.

Telescoped synthesis of benzoyl thiourea 5 in a coil reactor

Having established optimal conditions for each individual step, a telescoped continuous-flow process was developed to integrate both transformations (Fig. 6). Developing a telescoped configuration required balancing the differing kinetic demands of each reaction while ensuring smooth handling of solids throughout the system. To accommodate the residence-time requirements of each step, a compromise was adopted that ensured efficient performance across and seamless integration. In the first reactor, benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 was formed in a 20 mL coil held at 40 °C with a residence time of 12.3 min. Although shorter residence times were sufficient during single-step optimisation, this extended residence time was selected to align with the downstream requirement for the second reaction. The crude outflow, containing suspended NH4Cl, was passed directly into a 52 mL coil reactor held at 100 °C, where it was treated with dichloroaniline 4 for 16 min residence time to afford benzoyl thiourea 5 in 97% yield by HPLC.

Telescoped continuous flow synthesis of benzoyl thiourea 5 from 2.

Multistep flow synthesis of benzoyl thiourea 5 from benzoyl chloride 2 via isothiocyanate 3 in situ formation and consumption.

The telescoped system operated continuously for 2 h without any evidence of blockage, demonstrating robust performance under heterogeneous conditions. The product stream was collected and purified to afford benzoyl thiourea 5 in 92% isolated yield (5.8 g), providing material suitable for the subsequent step in the synthesis. Stable operation throughout the sequence was supported by the use of a 7-bar peristaltic back-pressure regulator, which prevented acetone from boiling at elevated temperature and ensured reliable handling of the suspended NH4Cl. In addition, the relatively wide internal channel of the coil reactors helped maintain uninterrupted flow by minimising particle accumulation. Together, these features enabled smooth and efficient telescoping of the two reaction steps under continuous-flow conditions. While the telescoped system provided a robust and scalable route to benzoyl thiourea 5, further intensification required addressing the root cause of NH4Cl precipitation at higher concentrations.

An alternative NH4Cl-free continuous flow route to benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 in a packed-column reactor

To explicitly circumvent solid-handling limitations associated with NH4Cl precipitation, an alternative strategy based on reagent immobilisation was investigated. While the coil-reactor system could tolerate NH4Cl formation at 0.2 M, operation at higher concentrations was constrained by excessive salt precipitation and increased clogging risk. As an alternative to managing solids in suspension, thiocyanate ions (SCN−) were immobilised on the anion-exchange resin Ambersep 900 in its hydroxide form to generate a thiocyanate-functionalised packed bed (Ambersep 900-SCN). This approach enabled controlled delivery of SCN⁻ under continuous-flow conditions while completely eliminating NH4Cl formation in the liquid phase. Ambersep 900 has a maximum operating temperature of 60 °C due to the thermal sensitivity of its polystyrene backbone; however, elevated temperatures were not required, as the formation of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 proceeds efficiently at ambient temperature. Consequently, the resin-based packed-column reactor provided a thermally compatible and operationally robust alternative flow configuration that eliminated salt-handling challenges inherent to the conventional NH4SCN route.

The Ambersep 900-SCN packed-bed reactor was prepared by loading Ambersep 900-OH resin (7.2 g) into a glass column reactor (10 mm ID × 100 mm), as shown in Fig. 7a. NH4SCN (1 M) was pumped through the column at 1 mL min−1 to facilitate ion exchange at 25 °C, replacing hydroxide ions on the resin with thiocyanate (SCN⁻). The progress of immobilisation was monitored in real time using inline IR spectroscopy, specifically by tracking the thiocyanate C-N stretching band at 2057 cm−1 (Fig. 7b). At the beginning of the process, the peak area of the C-N stretch in the effluent was low, reflecting rapid uptake of SCN⁻ by the resin. As ion-exchange sites became progressively saturated, the SCN⁻ peak area increased. After approximately 30 min, the signal reached a stable plateau (Fig. 7c), indicating that the resin had achieved full exchange capacity and no further SCN⁻ uptake was occurring. This distinct plateau provided a reliable spectroscopic endpoint confirming complete functionalisation of the packed-bed reactor. Under the operating conditions employed, the amount of NH4SCN required to fully convert the resin corresponded to 3.53 equiv. (2.28 g), ensuring reproducible preparation of Ambersep 900-SCN for subsequent continuous-flow synthesis. With the immobilised SCN⁻ bed prepared and fully characterised, its reactivity under continuous flow conditions was next assessed.

Complete conversion to benzoyl isothiocyanate 3 was achieved at residence times between 2 and 8.3 min (flow rates of 0.38–1.6 mL min−1), demonstrating the high reactivity of the immobilised thiocyanate system (Table 1, entries 1–3). Increasing the flow rate to 3.2 mL min−1 reduced the residence time to 1 min and resulted in a modest decrease in conversion to 93%, indicating the upper throughput limit under these conditions (Table 1, entry 4). When the benzoyl chloride 2 concentration was increased to 0.5 M (Table 1, entry 5), full conversion was again observed at a calculated 2 min residence time after the initial 2.8 min lag required to reach steady state. However, prolonged operation beyond approximately 3.8 min led to a gradual decline in conversion, suggesting early breakthrough of SCN− and defining the practical operating limit at higher substrate loadings. Overall, these results show that the Ambersep 900-SCN packed-bed reactor provides an efficient and reliable solid-supported platform for the continuous-flow synthesis of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3, effectively avoiding NH4Cl-related handling challenges while clearly defining the applicable concentration boundaries for robust operation. The solid-supported nature of the thiocyanate source simplifies the reaction workup, as the resin remains confined within the reactor, and the effluent contains the desired product in MeTHF. These observations define the operational boundaries of the packed-bed approach and highlight its advantages relative to the NH4SCN solution-based system.

Although reagent immobilisation effectively eliminates NH4Cl precipitation at a moderate scale, further process intensification, particularly at higher concentrations, will require reactor technologies capable of dynamically managing solids. Dynamic mixing reactors such as agitated cell reactors, agitated tubular reactors and spinning disc reactors offer enhanced shear, improved suspension stability and increased resistance to fouling, making them well-suited for precipitating systems operated at elevated throughputs48. Adoption of these intensified reactor architectures, therefore, represents a logical next step toward higher-capacity continuous production of benzoyl isothiocyanate 3.

Continuous flow synthesis and optimisation of thiourea 6

With the continuous-flow synthesis of benzoyl thiourea 5 successfully established, the next objective was to extend this approach to the preparation of thiourea 6, a key intermediate in the overall synthesis of clonidine•HCl 1. In this transformation, the amide bond in benzoyl thiourea 5 undergoes selective hydrolysis under basic conditions, effecting cleavage of the benzoyl group and yielding thiourea 6.

To develop an efficient continuous-flow process for this step, benzoyl thiourea 5 was subjected to hydrolysis with NaOH (aq.) in a stainless-steel flow reactor (5 mL, 1 mm ID) (Fig. 9). The use of a stainless-steel reactor was crucial, as it permitted operation at elevated temperatures and pressures, conditions necessary to facilitate hydrolysis in MeTHF while maintaining reactor integrity and operational safety. Due to the limited solubility of benzoyl thiourea 5 in MeTHF, the starting concentration was restricted to 0.25 M to avoid precipitation and subsequent risk of clogging during continuous operation.

Continuous flow synthesis of thiourea 6.

Flow hydrolysis of benzoyl thiourea 5 to thiourea 6 using NaOH.

A data-driven optimisation study examined the influence of temperature (50–150 °C), residence time (1–15 min) and NaOH equiv. (1–5% w/v) on thiourea 6 formation. Eleven experiments were conducted, comprising an initial Sobol-sampled set followed by sequential Bayesian optimisation steps guided by iterative model updates. Reaction performance was quantified by HPLC using a calibrated analytical standard curve of thiourea 6. The 3D response surface (Fig. 10a) showed that temperature had the strongest impact on reaction efficiency. Yields increased progressively from approximately 85–90% at temperatures near 60–80 °C to values above 98% at temperatures approaching 140–150 °C. This high level of conversion was achieved even at short residence times, indicating that hydrolysis is strongly thermally activated under these conditions.

The contour plot (Fig. 10b) highlighted a broad region of near-quantitative yield at temperatures above 130 °C, where residence times of less than 2 min were sufficient to reach greater than 99% yield. Residence time showed a secondary effect. Once the temperature exceeded approximately 120 °C, extending the residence time beyond 2 min produced only marginal improvements. The parameter-importance analysis (Fig. 10c) shows that reaction performance is dominated by temperature, with both the linear temperature term and the squared temperature term contributing substantially to the model. The prominence of the temperature2 term indicates pronounced curvature in the response, consistent with the presence of an optimal temperature regime rather than a simple linear dependence. Residence time plays a secondary role, while NaOH % (aq.) alone contributes minimally within the investigated range. Interaction terms involving temperature, particularly temperature-time, further highlight that the effects of residence time and base concentration are strongly temperature-dependent. Overall, these results demonstrate that the hydrolysis is primarily controlled by temperature, with significant nonlinear behaviour shaping the optimal operating window. Further insight was obtained from a SHAP feature-contribution analysis, which quantified how each experimental factor contributed to the observed high-yield outcomes. For the optimal experiment, temperature at 145 °C contributed strongly and positively toward the final yield with a SHAP value of +8.07. NaOH at 4.4% w/v also made a positive contribution with a SHAP value of +2.40. Residence time of 1 min contributed slightly in the negative direction with a SHAP value of −0.95, confirming that longer residence times are not required once sufficiently high temperatures are applied. The combined SHAP contributions converged on a predicted yield of 99.8%, in excellent agreement with the experimental result.

Together, these results define a clear operational window for the continuous flow hydrolysis of benzoyl thiourea 5. High temperatures combined with short residence times produce a rapid and quantitative yield to thiourea 6. The stainless-steel reactor allowed safe operation under these intensified conditions and enabled a compact and high-throughput process. This provides a reliable and efficient route to thiourea 6 that is compatible with downstream continuous flow processing in the overall synthesis of clonidine•HCl 1.

To complete the process, inline workup was incorporated using a liquid–liquid separator to continuously isolate the aqueous and organic phases (Fig. 11). Under the optimised conditions of 145 °C, 1 min residence time, and 4.4% w/v NaOH, continuous operation for 1 h after a 15 min steady‑state period afforded a throughput of 8.28 g h−1 of product in solution, confirming stable and sustained process performance. The reaction effluent, containing thiourea 6 and sodium benzoate 10 in MeTHF and water, was directed through a 5 mL PTFE coil cooled in an ice bath for cooling to enhance phase differentiation before entering the inline separator. The cooled stream was then separated, and the aqueous phase was collected directly into a 500 mL CSTR maintained at 0 °C. HPLC analysis confirmed that no thiourea 6 remained in the organic phase. After the collection period was completed, HCl (5% w/v) was added to adjust the pH to ~8, promoting selective precipitation of thiourea 6 while sodium benzoate 10 remained in solution. The resulting solid was isolated by offline filtration and dried under vacuum to provide thiourea 6 in 93% yield, corresponding to 7.7 g of product with 99% purity.

In summary, the combination of data-driven optimisation, intensified flow conditions, and integrated inline workup enabled a robust, high-yielding and scalable continuous flow synthesis of thiourea 6. This step completes a key transformation in the preparation of clonidine•HCl 1 and demonstrates the efficiency and practicality of the developed flow platform.

Continuous flow synthesis of S-methylisothiourea•HI 7

Following the successful continuous flow synthesis and isolation of thiourea 6, attention turned to developing an efficient flow process for its S-methylation to afford S-methylisothiourea•HI 7. Thiourea 6 (0.5 M) was reacted with MeI in a 4.5 mL glass flow reactor, enabling rapid S-alkylation under controlled conditions (Fig. 12). Bayesian optimisation was used to identify the key parameters governing reactivity and to define an optimal operating window. The effects of temperature (70–150 °C), residence time (0.5–10 min) and methyl iodide loading (1–2 equiv.) were evaluated across eleven experiments, comprising six Sobol-initialised runs followed by five sequential optimisation steps. Reaction performance was quantified by HPLC using a calibrated standard of S-methylisothiourea•HI 7.

Continuous flow synthesis and optimisation of S-methylisothiourea•HI 7.

S-methylation of thiourea 6 in continuous flow using MeI in a 4.5 mL glass flow reactor.

The 3D response surface generated during optimisation (Fig. 13a) revealed a strong dependence of reaction performance on temperature. The surface shows that yields increase sharply as the temperature is raised toward approximately 120 °C, forming a pronounced high-yield plateau. Residence time also contributes to improved performance, but to a lesser extent. Experimental data points plotted on the surface confirm that maximum yields occur at short residence times combined with elevated temperatures. The corresponding contour plot (Fig. 13b) also confirms that temperature is the primary driver of reaction efficiency, with yields increasing sharply as the temperature approaches approximately 120 °C. Residence time has a secondary effect, improving yields only modestly once temperatures are sufficiently high. The data indicate a well-defined optimal region where short residence times of around 1 min combined with temperatures between roughly 115 and 125 °C, consistently deliver yields above 95%. Outside this region, operating at lower or excessively high temperatures, or with unnecessarily long residence times, led to a clear decline in performance. This confirms that a combination of high temperature and short contact time is critical for achieving optimal conversion under continuous flow conditions. The progressive decrease in yield observed beyond 125 °C across all investigated residence times and pressures is likely due to MeI boiling within the reactor system, reducing its effective availability and consequently limiting product formation.

The parameter-importance analysis (Fig. 13c) shows that reaction performance is primarily governed by temperature, with both the linear temperature term and the squared temperature term contributing strongly to the model. The significant contribution of the temperature2 term indicates pronounced non-linear behaviour, consistent with the presence of an optimal temperature regime rather than a simple linear dependence. Residence time and MeI equivalents make smaller individual contributions, while interaction terms involving temperature, particularly temperature-residence time and temperature-MeI equivalents, are also significant, demonstrating that the influence of these variables is strongly temperature-dependent. In contrast, higher-order terms involving residence time and MeI alone contribute minimally. Overall, these results confirm that S-methylation efficiency under continuous-flow conditions is dominated by temperature, with non-linear temperature effects and temperature-mediated interactions defining the optimal operating window.

The SHAP analysis (Fig. 13d) shows that temperature is the dominant factor driving high yields, providing the largest positive contribution to the predicted outcome. A residence time of 1 min also has a strong positive influence, indicating that the reaction benefits from short contact times under suitable thermal conditions. The use of MeI (1.7 eq.) contributes to a lesser but still meaningful extent. When combined, these factors elevate the predicted yield from the model baseline to nearly quantitative levels, closely matching the experimentally observed value. These trends confirm that high temperature, short residence time and modest excess methyl iodide work synergistically to maximise S-methylation efficiency under continuous flow conditions.

Collectively, these results define a well‑established operational window for the continuous‑flow synthesis of S-methylisothiourea•HI 7. The reaction is primarily temperature‑driven, with optimal performance achieved at approximately 120 °C and a residence time of 1 min, requiring only a modest excess of MeI (1.7 equiv.). Under these conditions, S‑methylation proceeds rapidly and efficiently, rendering the transformation well‑suited to continuous processing and delivering a throughput of 24.1 g h−1. Using the optimised conditions, the system was operated continuously for 30 min following a 10 min steady-state period, delivering S-methylisothiourea•HI 7 in 97% isolated yield (11.9 g) after concentration. The combination of Bayesian optimisation, efficient heat transfer within the small-volume reactor, and short residence times enabled a compact, high-throughput and reproducible synthetic step.

In summary, the integrated optimisation framework allowed rapid identification of the key kinetic drivers governing this transformation and established robust conditions for the continuous-flow S-methylation of thiourea 6. The resulting process affords S-methylisothiourea•HI 7 in high yield under intensified yet mild flow conditions, further strengthening the overall scalability and reliability of the continuous-flow synthesis of clonidine•HCl 1.

Continuous flow synthesis of clonidine•HCl 1

With a robust and scalable continuous-flow route to S-methylisothiourea•HI 7 established, the next stage of the synthesis focused on its cyclisation with ethylenediamine 8 to construct the imidazoline core under continuous-flow conditions, affording clonidine•HI 9, which was subsequently converted to clonidine•HCl 1 during workup (Fig. 14). In batch, this transformation was performed under neat conditions, where ethylenediamine 8 served as both solvent and reagent. For the flow studies, both neat conditions and solvent-assisted conditions were evaluated. 2-ethoxyethanol was employed to enhance the solubility of the reagents, while neat conditions were also explored to assess the effect of high amine equivalents. In each case, the reaction mixture was pumped through a 10 mL stainless steel coil reactor (1 mm ID) capable of operating at elevated temperatures and pressures (Fig. 14). The effects of ethylenediamine equivalents, residence time and temperature on reaction performance were systematically investigated using a one-factor-at-a-time optimisation approach. (Table 2).

A systematic investigation of the cyclisation of S-methyl-isothiourea•HI 7 with ethylenediamine 8 under continuous-flow conditions revealed clear relationships between temperature, ethylenediamine equivalents, residence time and product selectivity (Fig. 14 and Table 2). Under standard conditions, 2-ethoxyethanol was employed as a solvent to maintain high solubility and flowability at 1 M, ensuring reliable reagent delivery and homogeneous reaction mixtures. In contrast, neat conditions were explored at 0.5 M, with ethylenediamine serving as both reagent and solvent, enabling access to very high nucleophile concentrations. Yields of clonidine•HI 9 were determined by LC analysis based on LC-MS peak areas at 210 nm relative to all other detected impurities in the chromatogram.

At 150 °C (Table 2, entries 1–2), the reaction exhibited only moderate conversion (65–75%), affording clonidine•HI 9 in 40–50% yield even at extended residence times. Increasing the temperature to 185 °C led to a marked enhancement in reactivity, delivering high to complete conversion across entries 3–10, thereby establishing elevated temperature as a critical requirement for efficient imidazoline ring closure. Ethylenediamine equivalents had a pronounced effect on product selectivity. At 185 °C and a residence time of 30 min (Table 2, entries 3–5), increasing the equivalents of ethylenediamine from 1.5 to 3 maintained full conversion but resulted in a progressive decrease in clonidine•HI 9 yield from 65 to 42%, accompanied by a corresponding increase in by-product formation. These results indicate that excessive ethylenediamine promotes competing pathways that reduce product selectivity despite complete substrate consumption.

Residence time was also a critical determinant of reaction performance. At 185 °C and 2 equivalents of ethylenediamine (Table 2, entries 4 and 6), shortening the residence time from 30 to 15 min increased the yield of clonidine•HI 9 from 63 to 79% while substantially suppressing the formation of undesired by-products. Even at a residence time of 5 min (Table 2, entry 7), the reaction remained highly efficient, affording clonidine•HI 9 in 82% yield. These observations indicate that shorter residence times limit secondary reactions and thereby enhance product selectivity.

Neat reactions (Table 2, entries 8–10), in which ethylenediamine served as both reagent and solvent, followed the same general trends: elevated temperature (185 °C) and reduced residence time favoured clonidine•HI 9 formation, although substantial impurity formation persisted under conditions of extreme amine excess. Notably, lowering the temperature to 150 °C at 75 equivalents of ethylenediamine (Table 2, entry 10) afforded a high yield of 87%, revealing an alternative selectivity regime in which very high nucleophile concentration compensates for reduced thermal input when residence time is appropriately controlled.

Overall, these results highlight the need to balance thermal activation, nucleophile concentration and reaction residence time. High temperature drives conversion; however, excessive ethylenediamine or extended residence times promote the formation of impurities. Under the conditions investigated, the highest performance for the final cyclisation step was obtained when ethylenediamine was used as both solvent and reagent. Operating under neat conditions at 150 °C with 75 equivalents of ethylenediamine and a residence time of 5 min afforded the highest observed yield while minimising impurity formation.

It should be noted that optimisation of this transformation was more limited in scope than the Bayesian-driven studies applied to earlier steps, and the conditions identified here represent the best conditions evaluated rather than a fully global optimum; nevertheless, this operating point provided robust and reproducible performance. Under these best conditions, the reaction was run continuously for 15 min, with product collection initiated after a 15 min delay to ensure steady state operation. The effluent was concentrated, basified, and subjected to purification to isolate the clonidine free base, which was subsequently converted to clonidine•HCl 1 in 69% yield (2.7 g).

A comparison with earlier batch studies clearly illustrates the advantages of continuous flow for this cyclisation. Van Dort and coworkers40 generated the imidazoline ring of the clonidine analogue p-iodoclonidine by reacting the corresponding S-methyl isothiouronium salt with ethylenediamine in a sealed steel vessel. Their process required 18 h at 150 °C and afforded only 7% yield. This low efficiency mirrors the decline in selectivity observed in the present work when residence times are extended, where prolonged heating promotes competitive cleavage to the aniline fragment. Continuous flow avoids these limitations by providing rapid and tightly controlled thermal input. Efficient heat and mass transfer enables fast cyclisation at elevated temperature while preventing the extended thermal exposure that drives decomposition in batch. As a result, high conversion and high selectivity are achieved within minutes rather than hours.

Overall, the comparison demonstrates that continuous flow offers a markedly more efficient and selective route to the imidazoline core. Precise control of residence time and access to high nucleophile concentrations deliver a robust and scalable process that aligns with modern principles of efficiency and sustainability in chemical manufacturing.

Conclusion

This work presents the first continuous flow synthesis of clonidine and demonstrates how modern flow technologies combined with data-driven optimisation can fundamentally reshape a legacy pharmaceutical process. By demonstrating 5 transformations under continuous conditions supported by telescoping, inline workup and effective solid management, we achieved reliable production of clonidine•HCl in 54% overall yield with a total residence time of only 24 min, representing a substantial improvement over conventional batch methods that require >18 h.

Critically, the continuous platform addresses long-standing challenges in the historical route, including hazardous reagents, lengthy reaction times, extensive manual handling and poor yields. Bayesian optimisation played a central role in rapidly identifying high-performing operating conditions across multiple reaction types. It enabled efficient mapping of reaction behaviour, revealed previously unrecognised trends in the interactions between temperature, residence time and stoichiometry, and allowed optimal conditions to be reached with minimal experimental effort.

Nevertheless, the formation of by-products during the final cyclisation highlights an opportunity for further selectivity control. Future studies leveraging inline analytics, dynamic optimisation, or alternative reactor architectures may reduce impurities load and further elevate process efficiency.

Overall, this study delivers a robust and efficient continuous manufacturing blueprint for clonidine, providing a modern data-driven solution that significantly improves speed, selectivity and process efficiency. The work establishes a strong foundation for intensified continuous production of clonidine and related APIs and offers a compelling model for the modernisation of mature pharmaceutical syntheses and the reinforcement of resilient and sustainable supply chains.
