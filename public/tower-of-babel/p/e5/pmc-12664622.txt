Angiography-Derived Versus Coronary Guidewire-Derived Index of Microcirculatory Resistance in Patients With Ischemia With Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries
Authors: Ethan Loftspring, Peter Zhang, William Beaty, Anaïs Hausvater, Amanda Joa, Kenneth L. Harkin, Claudia Serrano-Gomez, Ayman Farid, Lindsay Elbaum, Jeffrey S. Berger, Harmony R. Reynolds, Nathaniel R. Smilowitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary function testing (CFT) during invasive coronary angiography is guideline-recommended to identify coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) in patients with ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries (INOCA). Wire-free, angiography-derived index of microcirculatory resistance (IMRangio) measurements have been developed to simplify assessment for CMD. We assessed the validity of IMRangio compared with the conventional index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) in a cohort of individuals with INOCA.MethodsThis single-center observational study prospectively enrolled adults aged >18 years who were referred for clinically indicated invasive coronary angiography for the evaluation of chest discomfort or an anginal equivalent and had no coronary artery lesions with ≥50% stenosis. Participants underwent invasive CFT using bolus thermodilution techniques to determine IMR and coronary flow reserve (CFR) in the left anterior descending artery. IMRangio was calculated retrospectively, blinded to CFT results. CMD was defined by IMR ≥25 or CFR <2.5.ResultsAmong 122 enrolled participants with INOCA (median age 62 years, 77% women), the median IMR in the left anterior descending artery was 20 (13-27), CFR was 3.95 (2.5-4.7), and 54 participants (44%) had CMD. IMRangio did not correlate with IMR (r = 0.1; P = .14). Mean IMRangio was not different among patients with and without IMR ≥25 (P = .21). IMRangio ≥25 had a sensitivity of 44% and a specificity of 53% in identifying participants with CMD. In sensitivity analyses among participants with cineangiography at 10 to 15 frames per second (r = 0.37; P = .02) and limited to participants with both optimal angiographic view and frame rates ≥10 (r = 0.48; P = .03), we observed weak positive correlations between IMRangio and IMR.ConclusionsIn patients with INOCA, correlations between IMRangio and IMR were poor. IMRangio appears unreliable for identifying CMD in patients with INOCA.

Introduction

Ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) is characterized by angina or evidence of myocardial ischemia in patients without flow-limiting epicardial coronary artery disease (CAD). Approximately 50% of individuals with chest pain undergoing coronary angiography have INOCA, with a prevalence that is disproportionately higher in women.1,2 INOCA is associated with persistent angina, reduced quality of life, recurrent hospitalizations, and adverse cardiovascular events.3, 4, 5

Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is a key mechanism of INOCA.6,7 Invasive coronary function testing (CFT) using guidewire-based assessments of coronary pressure and flow can be used to identify patients with CMD. CMD is typically diagnosed based on an impaired coronary flow reserve (CFR) or an elevated index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR).8,9 When used to guide medical therapy, invasive CFT is associated with improved symptoms and quality of life in patients with INOCA.10 Unfortunately, invasive CFT requires placement of a coronary guidewire in the vessel of interest and administration of agents to elicit hyperemia, which prolongs procedure times, increases cost, and carries a small risk of iatrogenic complications. These limitations have slowed CFT adoption in clinical practice.

Angiography-derived IMR (IMRangio) is an alternative, less invasive approach that has been proposed to diagnose CMD. IMRangio uses images from the traditional cineangiography to determine vessel characteristics, estimate coronary flow, and calculate microcirculatory resistance. This approach could potentially obviate the need for guidewire-based CFT. Few studies have systematically explored relationships between IMRangio and invasive IMR in stable patients with INOCA. We sought to assess the validity of IMRangio compared with the conventional, wire-based IMR in a real-world cohort of individuals with INOCA.

Materials and methods

Participants

We prospectively enrolled adults aged >18 years referred for clinically indicated invasive coronary angiography for the evaluation of chest pain or an anginal equivalent at NYU Langone Health and Bellevue Hospital Centers (ClinicalTrials.gov; NCT03537586). Patients with obstructive CAD, defined as >50% diameter stenoses of any major epicardial coronary artery, were excluded. Patient characteristics including demographics, comorbidities, and laboratory data were extracted from the electronic medical record. This study was approved by the NYU School of Medicine Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Invasive CFT

Invasive CFT was performed using bolus coronary thermodilution techniques.11 A combined coronary temperature and pressure sensing wire (PressureWire X, Abbott) was positioned in the mid to distal left anterior descending artery (LAD), >60 mm from the ostium of the vessel. Bolus injections of 3 cc of room temperature saline were administered in triplicate to assess coronary transit times as an indicator of coronary flow. After a 2-minute infusion of intravenous adenosine at 140 μg/kg per minute to establish hyperemia, coronary transit time measurements were repeated using bolus thermodilution techniques. IMR was calculated as the product of the distal coronary pressure and the mean transit time at peak hyperemia, with IMR ≥25 considered abnormal. CFR was calculated as the ratio of resting to hyperemic mean transit times, with CFR <2.5 considered to be abnormal. CMD was defined as wire-based IMR ≥25 and/or CFR <2.5.

Angiographic measures of microcirculatory dysfunction

Angiography-derived IMR was derived from conventional coronary angiography acquired in the standard projections prior to administration of adenosine. Cineangiography frame rates were not prespecified for IMRangio analyses and were recorded between 7.5 and 30 frames per second (fps), as per institutional default settings or operator preference. Images were analyzed using the Medis QFR Software v2.0 (Medis Medical Imaging) using a previously validated formula (Supplemental Table S1). Two angiographic projections, ideally separated by ≥25° angulation and without significant vessel overlap, were required for the calculation of IMRangio. Angiographic views with cranial angulation were preferentially selected for all IMRangio analyses of the LAD to minimize vessel foreshortening. In participants without optimal cranial angiographic views for IMRangio analysis, caudal projections with right anterior oblique angulations were preferentially selected. For each angiogram analyzed, the origin of the LAD was labeled as the proximal point. The length of the segment analyzed was chosen to be 52.5 ± 2.5 mm. Mean arterial pressures were recorded from invasively measured aortic pressures at the time of coronary angiography when available or procedural noninvasive blood pressures. Frame counts were manually selected. The initial frame was determined as the first frame in the series with any dye opacification of the proximal LAD. The final frame was the first frame with any contrast opacification at the distal point of the analysis region. IMRangio was measured by 2 physicians who were blinded to the results of invasive CFT. Angiography-derived fractional flow reserve (quantitative flow ratio [QFR]) was also determined for all vessels analyzed.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables are presented as proportions and compared using the χ2 test. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and compared using the t test, whereas skewed continuous variables are reported as median (IQR) and are compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Normally distributed continuous variables were evaluated using Pearson correlations, and nonnormally distributed continuous variables were evaluated using Spearman correlations. Differences in IMRangio were compared among participants with and without IMR ≥25. Receiver operating characteristic curves were generated, and Youden indices were calculated to determine optimal IMRangio thresholds for IMR ≥25. We also evaluated the performance of IMRangio to identify CMD (defined by IMR ≥25 or CFR <2.5).

Sensitivity analyses

We performed a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the performance of IMRangio after excluding individuals with suboptimal angiographic views, defined by significant vessel overlap in all available series, absence of 2 cranially angulated angiographic views, or with otherwise suitable angiographic projections separated by <25° angulation. We also evaluated IMRangio in a subset of participants with cineangiography frame rates of 10 or 15 fps. Lastly, we assessed the correlation of IMRangio and IMR in participants' optimal views and frame rates ≥10 fps.

IMRangio analysis

The median QFR was 0.99 (IQR, 0.98-1). QFR values exceeded 0.8 in 99% of participants, indicating nonflow-limiting epicardial CAD, with 97% diagnostic concordance between QFR and fractional flow reserve measurements for nonobstructive CAD. The median angiographically-derived flow velocity was 19.3 mm/s. The median IMRangio measured in the LAD was 24.2 (IQR, 18.8-30.2) (Figure 1B). Out of 122 participants, 64 (52%) had optimal angiographic views for IMRangio analysis. After excluding individuals with suboptimal views, the median IMRangio was 25.2 (IQR, 21.7-29) with an angiographically-derived median flow velocity of 19 mm/s. 32 participants (26%) had cineangiography frame rates of 10 or 15 fps; in this subgroup, the mean IMRangio was 27.4 ± 11.8 and the mean angiographically-derived flow velocity was 18.9 mm/s. In the subset of participants with optimal views and angiography acquired at ≥10 fps (n = 21), the median IMRangio was 27.7 (IQR, 22-35.5) and the median angiographically-derived flow velocity was 15.9 mm/s.

Relationship between IMRangio and wire-based IMR

Overall, we observed no correlation between IMRangio and wire-based IMR (r = 0.10; P = .14) as shown in the Central Illustration, Panel A. A Bland-Altman Plot, shown in Figure 2, demonstrated low systemic bias (mean difference between IMR and IMRangio, 5.1). IMRangio ≥25 was associated with a sensitivity of 51% and a specificity of 57% to detect wire-based IMR ≥25 (Figure 3A). Complete diagnostic test characteristics of IMRangio to identify abnormal wire-based IMR are shown in Table 2. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for IMRangio to identify individuals with invasive wire-based IMR ≥25 was 0.57 (95% CI, 0.46-0.68). Using a threshold based on the Youden index, IMRangio ≥21.9 was associated with a sensitivity of 73% and a specificity of 44% to detect wire-based IMR ≥25 (Figure 4A). Diagnostic utility of IMRangio to identify CMD

Angiography-derived IMR did not identify individuals with CMD as determined by invasive wire-based CFT. IMRangio was associated with a sensitivity of 44% and specificity of 53% for predicting CMD (Table 2). There was no difference in IMRangio among participants with CMD (defined as either an abnormal invasive wire-based IMR or an abnormal invasive wire-based CFR) compared to those without CMD (P = .31; Figure 6). Sensitivity analyses

In a sensitivity analysis excluding cases with suboptimal angiographic views, no correlation between IMRangio and wire-based IMR was observed (r = 0.17; P = .10, Figure 8A). When the cohort was stratified by normal or abnormal wire-based IMR, there was no difference in IMRangio (P = .10; Figure 5B). In this sensitivity analysis, the AUC for IMRangio to identify individuals with invasive wire-based IMR ≥25 was 0.63 (95% CI, 0.49-0.77), with a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 37% at an optimal dichotomous threshold value of 21.9 (Figure 4B). In a second sensitivity analysis including a subset of participants with cineangiography acquired at 10 or 15 fps, there was a weak positive correlation between IMRangio and IMR (r = 0.37, P = .02; Figure 8B). Participants with an abnormal IMR had a higher IMRangio (IMRangio = 31.4) than participants with normal IMR (IMRangio = 24.2) (P = .04; Figure 5C). However, the AUC for IMRangio to identify participants with IMR ≥25 was only 0.66 (95% CI, 0.47-0.85). At an optimal dichotomous threshold of 21.4, IMRangio had a 93% sensitivity and a 50% specificity to identify IMR ≥25 (Figure 4C). In a third sensitivity analysis including only participants with both optimal angiographic views and frame rates ≥10 fps, IMRangio correlated moderately with IMR (r = 0.48, P = .03; Figure 8C). Further, in this subset, participants with abnormal IMR did not have a significantly higher IMRangio than participants with normal IMR (P = .39; Figure 5D). The AUC for IMRangio to identify abnormal IMR was 0.56 (95% CI, 0.31-0.82). Discussion

In a large, real-world cohort of individuals with INOCA who underwent invasive wire-based CFT using bolus thermodilution measures of coronary flow in the LAD, retrospective calculation of IMRangio did not reliably correlate with wire-based IMR. Our findings were consistent after excluding individuals with suboptimal angiographic projections for IMRangio analysis, although we observed a moderate correlation between IMRangio and IMR in 2 sensitivity analyses: one including only participants with cineangiography obtained at 10 or 15 fps and a second including only participants with both optimal views and cineangiography ≥10 fps. Overall, IMRangio provided no diagnostic utility when used as a surrogate for a final diagnosis of CMD determined by invasive CFT. These findings question the real-world clinical utility of IMRangio in individuals with INOCA.

The results from the current analysis are perhaps not surprising based on the poor performance of angiographic measures of coronary flow as surrogates for invasive CFT. We and others reported poor correlations between TIMI frame counts and microcirculatory resistance measured with bolus thermodilution or intracoronary Doppler techniques.12,13 Further, visually estimated coronary slow flow, defined by ≥3 cardiac cycles for contrast to opacify the distal vessels, was not associated with abnormal IMR under resting conditions.14 Future studies should assess the performance of hyperemic IMRangio and invasive IMR in patients with ANOCA.

In contrast to our findings in stable chest pain patients with INOCA, several studies report strong correlations between IMRangio and IMR in patients presenting with acute myocardial infarction.15, 16, 17 In an analysis of 340 individuals with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction using a different analytic software, robust correlations between IMRangio and IMR were observed. In that analysis, IMRangio >40 correlated with infarct size, cardiac mortality, and heart failure readmission at long-term follow-up.12 Similarly, in a separate study of patients with non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction using a different analytic software, individuals with elevated IMRangio had a higher incidence of major adverse cardiac events at 2 years than those with lower IMRangio values.18 In a meta-analysis of patients presenting with ACS undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, IMRangio was strongly associated with IMR and increased risk of major adverse cardiac events.19 Substantial damage to the microcirculation in myocardial territories supplied by infarct vessels may account for the superior sensitivity of IMRangio in the setting of acute myocardial infarction versus INOCA.

In patients with INOCA, the literature evaluating the use of IMRangio to predict wire-based IMR and CMD is more limited. A study by Mejía-Rentería et al20 enrolled 104 patients with INOCA and reported a strong correlation between Medis IMRangio and wire-based IMR. A subgroup analysis in a study by Scarsini et al21 that included patients without obstructive coronary arteries demonstrated a significantly higher IMRangio in patients with abnormal IMR when compared to individuals with a normal IMR. In a third study of patients with INOCA who underwent PET scans to localize areas of decreased perfusion, IMRangio was elevated in vessels with decreased perfusion.22

Several formulas have been proposed for estimating IMR using angiography, and each analytical software applies slight variations of these methods. In general, the calculation incorporates mean aortic pressure and contrast-derived flow velocity, typically estimated using the user-defined vessel length and the number of frames required for contrast to traverse that segment. Commonly used formulas are provided in Supplemental Table S1. These methodological differences may partially explain variability across studies.

In this analysis, correlations between IMRangio and invasive IMR were too weak to be a reliable clinical diagnostic tool to diagnose CMD. However, in participants with optimal angiography and in those with higher frame rates, IMRangio demonstrated high (>90%) sensitivity but poor specificity to identify abnormal wire-based IMR. Based on this observation, IMRangio could be used as a screening tool to identify patients who might warrant confirmatory invasive CFT.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a post hoc analysis of a prospective study, and IMRangio analyses were not planned at the time of angiography. Hence, in some cases, angiographic views were suboptimal for QFR analyses, limited by angulation, vessel overlap, or both, which may have led to inaccuracies in 3-dimensional vessel reconstruction. However, our findings were consistent in sensitivity analyses excluding participants with suboptimal angiographic views. Second, cineangiography was obtained at frame rates determined as per the interventional cardiologist, and in many cases were lower than the recommended 10 fps. At lower frame rates, small differences in frame counts may be magnified, potentially impacting the performance of IMRangio. Still, the lower frame rates used in the current study are consistent with modern radiation reduction strategies implemented in clinical practice. Only a subset of participants met the software company’s recommended criteria both for frame rates and optimal angiographic views. Still, even among ideal angiograms for analysis, correlations between IMRangio and wire-based IMR were poor. Third, nitroglycerin was not systematically administered at the time of initial diagnostic angiography, although, this is assumed to have a modest impact on the findings as QFR was ≥0.8 in all measured vessels. Nitroglycerin was systemically administered prior to CFT, and it is possible that the differential effects of nitroglycerin at the level of the microcirculation may impact the performance of IMRangio analyses. Still, even if the performance of IMRangio is substantially better under idealized conditions, the vulnerability of this measure to minor changes in clinical practice raises questions about its reliability and clinical relevance. Prospective studies of patients with optimal coronary angiography (15 fps frame rates, adequate angulations, and minimal vessel overlap) are needed to assess the diagnostic accuracy of IMRangio in patients with INOCA. Fourth, a single analytical software was used to calculate IMRangio; the diagnostic performance of other angiographic IMR measures (Supplemental Table S1) was not evaluated. Finally, we only assessed correlations between IMRangio and IMR measured in the LAD. LAD territory coronary flow is typically assumed to be representative of the entire coronary circulation, and consequently, CFT is usually performed in the LAD in routine clinical practice. However, relationships between IMRangio and IMR measured in the right coronary artery and left circumflex branches are unknown.

Conclusion

In this observational study of individuals with INOCA referred for CFT, correlations between IMRangio and invasive IMR were poor. Although less invasive approaches to CMD diagnosis are of great interest, IMRangio appears to be insufficient to identify CMD in individuals with INOCA in real-world practice. Prospective studies with optimal coronary angiography are needed to confirm the diagnostic accuracy of IMRangio in INOCA.
