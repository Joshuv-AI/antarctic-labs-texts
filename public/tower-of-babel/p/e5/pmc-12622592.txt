Tea intake and risk of allergic diseases: A Mendelian randomization study based on a European population
Authors: Xinlai Ma, Yang Zhang, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Some observational studies have shown that tea has some anti-allergic effects. Nevertheless, the correlation that was observed remains uncertain. We conducted a large sample two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) study to establish the causal link between genetically predicted tea intake and allergic conditions like asthma, allergic rhinitis, chronic cough, eczema, and atopic dermatitis. In a meta-analysis of genome-wide association data from the UK Biobank, single nucleotide polymorphisms linked to tea intake were found among 447,485 individuals of European descent. Genetic information for asthma, allergic rhinitis, eczema, and atopic dermatitis was obtained from the European Bioinformatics Institute, while data for chronic cough was collected from the UK Biobank. A magnetic resonance study was carried out to investigate how certain single nucleotide polymorphisms are linked to the susceptibility of the 5 aforementioned allergic conditions. We mainly use inverse variance weighting (IVW) for initial screening. To assess multiple validity, the MR-Egger regression test was employed, while the presence of heterogeneity was examined using the Cochran Q test. To ensure the reliability of the findings, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted. IVW discovered proof of a notable cause-and-effect relationship between consuming tea and the heightened likelihood of eczema (OR = 1.009; 95% CI = 1.002–1.016; P = .016), along with a shielding element against atopic dermatitis (OR = 0.638; 95% CI = 0.456–0.895; P = .009). No diversity or multiple effects were found. This association remained strong after adjusting for potential mediators, including eosinophil counts, mood factors, and intake of other beverages. Moreover, the IVW technique in this MR analysis did not find any indication of a causal link between drinking tea and asthma, allergic rhinitis, and chronic cough (all P > .05). This research discovered that consuming tea was linked to a lower likelihood of developing atopic dermatitis but a higher likelihood of developing eczema. Additionally, the research discovered that drinking tea did not have any connection to asthma, allergic rhinitis, or chronic cough.

1. Introduction

The prevalence of atopic diseases such as allergic asthma, hay fever and eczema has increased in several regions of the world over the past few years.[1] The occurrence of asthma, rhinitis, and eczema, 3 common noncommunicable diseases caused by allergies, varies greatly among and within countries.[2] In addition, chronic cough of any etiology reflects a hypersensitivity reaction characterized by a cough usually triggered by low-level thermal, mechanical, or chemical exposures.[3] Over the past few years, many countries have worked to prevent or treat allergic diseases, but these efforts have not yet been able to achieve the desired goals. Given that therapeutic options to address etiology are still limited, the development and implementation of sustained and effective preventive strategies are essential for the management of allergic diseases.

Tea, the 2nd most consumed beverage globally after water, contains a variety of biologically active compounds such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and theanine.[4,5] Tea’s growing global influence is due in part to its widely recognized health benefits, such as anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, anti-allergy, blood pressure-lowering, anti-obesity, and many more.[6] Laboratory experiments and research on animals suggest that tea polyphenols could have significant impacts on the development of various chronic illnesses, especially heart disease and cancer.[7] Research on animals also showed that the polyphenols found in tea are important in preventing allergic skin responses. Additionally, epigallocatechin gallate, a significant polyphenol in tea, can decrease reactions to contact hypersensitivity.[8] Studies have shown that flavonoids reduce allergic reactions by blocking the release of histamine from basophils, the production of IL-4 and IL-13, and the activation of CD40 ligands.[9] In addition, l-theanine may attenuate airway inflammation in asthma by modulating oxidative stress-responsive NF-κB pathways, thereby exerting some anti-allergic effects.[10] Yet, many research projects examining the anti-allergic properties of tea are currently in the observational or experimental phase, and standard epidemiological inquiries could be influenced by various factors and reverse causation, potentially resulting in a biased assessment of the connection between tea intake and health advantages. Hence, it remains uncertain if the connection between consuming tea and the likelihood of allergic disease is a result of a direct cause.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an advanced method for clarifying potential bias in observational studies.[11] The approach skillfully utilizes big data provided from genome-wide association (GWAS) to reveal causal relationships between specific exposures and outcomes by using single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs).[12] A MR analysis can be compared with a “natural” randomized controlled trial in which the assignment of genetic alleles affecting exposure effectively eliminates unobserved confounders in order to improve causal inferences.[13] This research delved into the possible cause-and-effect connection between genetically forecasted tea intake habits and allergic conditions within a MR framework, utilizing openly accessible GWAS summary information.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and data sources

Examining the potential causal connection between tea intake and allergic diseases, we conducted a two-sample MR study utilizing combined GWAS data. Only unrelated individuals of European descent were included in all GWAS investigations. Due to the lack of relevant data from other populations, this study included only the sum of genetic variants associated with tea intake in the UK biobank, involving 447,485 people of European descent living in the UK. This GWAS adjusted for gender, genotyping array and the first 10 principal components, and considered correlation and stratification. Data on habitual tea consumption were obtained through a questionnaire that addressed the following question: “How many cups of tea (both black and green) do you drink per day?’ According to the survey, the mean of tea intake was 3.51 with a standard deviation of 2.85. Specific information can be found in the MRC-IEU(https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). Pooled data for 5 allergic diseases were taken from genetic data extracted from the European Bioinformatics Institute for asthma (56,087 cases and 428,511 controls), allergic rhinitis (27,415 cases and 457,183 controls), eczema (12,176 cases and 472,422 controls), and atopic dermatitis (22,474 cases and 796,661 controls) and Genetic data on chronic cough (15,367 cases and 97,216 controls) extracted from the UK Biobank. Cases were primarily based on participants’ self-reported physician diagnostic questionnaires. Controls were individuals who self-reported no relevant diagnostic records.[14,15] Within the MR framework, we employed uncorrelated instrumental SNPs as IVs for exposures (such as tea intake) in order to assess and verify their causal impacts on outcomes (specifically, 5 allergic conditions). In addition, the study did not place any restrictions on the year in which the data were obtained from the dataset. For IVs to be effectively utilized, standard MR analysis depends on 3 crucial modeling assumptions: IV must have a significant association with tea intake at the genome-wide level; IV must be unrelated to any potential confounding factors; and IV must influence the development of the 5 mentioned allergic diseases solely through its impact on tea-drinking habits (shown in Fig. 1). This study was exempted from the requirement of ethical review board approval due to the fact that the data used were publicly available and had been anonymized to ensure that individuals were not identifiable. 2.2. Genetic IVs

To fulfill the initial hypothesis of the MR analysis that IVs are closed linked to tea intake, we selected independent IVs with statistically significant correlations with tea intake at the genome-wide level (P < 5 × 10−8, linkage disequilibrium < 0. 001, genetic distance = 10,000 KB).[16] We then used R software (version 4.3.2) and the TwoSampleMR package to obtain 50 SNPs significantly associated with tea intake (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q633), which satisfy the 1st hypothesis for avoiding the potential confounding effects of genetic variation. We examined the PhenoScanner database (https://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk) to ascertain if the listed IVs were linked to potential confounding factors. In this study, we removed SNPs associated with smoking, alcohol consumption, eosinophil count, emotional factors and SNPs associated with other relevant confounders. Finally, we used the remaining SNPs in our study as IVs for tea drinking. Furthermore, we computed the F statistic (F = beta2/se2)[17] in order to prevent the incorporation of IVs influenced by weak IVs.[18]

2.3. Statistical analysis

Our primary approach for estimating causal effects through statistical analysis was the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method.[19] We analyze the results obtained by the IVW method, the weighted median method, and the MR-Egger method in a careful comparison. The IVW method typically produces robust estimates when more than half of the information is derived from valid IVs. In contrast, the MR-Egger method does not require all IVs to be valid. Therefore, if the results of the 3 models show consistency, their combined conclusions will be more convincing. To assess the strength of the findings, we conducted 2 extra sensitivity analyses: weighted mode[20] and simple mode.[21] Cochran Q-test was used to evaluate the diversity of the IVW model. Cochran Q-test of P < .05 would indicate the presence of heterogeneous.[22] Despite the presence of heterogeneity, it does not automatically mean that the IVW model is invalid. The MR-Egger technique permits nonzero intercepts and is utilized to identify directed pleiotropy. Furthermore, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess if a single SNP had a significant impact on the overall estimate. We use the MR-PRESSO method to detect anomalies and reject them when they occur. After removing outliers, we rerun the MR analysis. All analyses were done in the R software (version 4.3.2), using the “MendelianRandomization,”[23] “MRPRESSO,”[24] and “TwoSampleMR” software packages. The threshold for statistical significance was established at 0.05.

3. Results

After examining genetic variations linked to tea intake, we identified 50 SNPs meeting stringent criteria (P < 5E − 08, r2 < 0.001). All of these SNPs had F-statistics exceeding the standard threshold of 10, suggesting a minor instrumental bias in our MR investigation. We excluded SNPs linked to eosinophil levels, emotional variables, and their confounding factors while conducting the screening. Detailed data on all pertinent SNPs can be found in Tables S2 to S6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q633. IVW analysis findings indicated that consuming tea was linked to a higher likelihood of developing eczema (odds ratio [OR] 1.009; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.002–1.016; P = .016). The results of the MR-Egger (OR: 1.015; 95% CI: 0.999–1.031; P = .068) and weighted median model (OR: 1.012; 95% CI: 1.003–1.022; P = .013) further validated this finding. Additionally, it was discovered that consuming tea was associated with a decreased risk of atopic dermatitis (OR 0.638; 95% CI 0.456–0.895; P = .009). Furthermore, in this MR study, tea intake was not associated with asthma (OR: 0.969; 95% CI: 0.932–1.008; P = .115), allergic rhinitis (OR: 0.996; 95% CI: 0.979–1.014; P = .665) and chronic cough (OR: 0.979; 95% CI: 0.932–1.027; P = .381) were not causally related. 4. Discussion

This study demonstrates how drinking tea can impact 5 allergic conditions using a two-sample MR approach. By using an independent validation dataset and a diverse MR approach based on different modeling assumptions, we rigorously tested the results and demonstrated the robustness and credibility of the study findings. In addition, the two-way analysis precludes the possibility of reverse causation. This study fully utilizes the potential of genetic tools in MR analysis and benefits from the large sample size provided by GWAS. Our MR study reveals that tea intake increases the risk of eczema while it may have a protective effect against atopic dermatitis. We did not find a direct causal relationship between tea intake and asthma, allergic rhinitis, or chronic cough. Tea, as a natural herbal beverage, plays an important anti-allergy role in the prevention and treatment of allergies. The components found in the tea could influence how the body reacts to allergens by preventing the creation of allergen–IgE combinations, decreasing the presence of FcεRI, or stopping the attachment of allergen–IgE combinations to FcεRI through different methods. Moreover, tea has the ability to regulate the levels of Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg cytokines and suppress the production of Th2 cytokines, ultimately aiding in the relief of allergy symptoms.[25–27]

A cup of tea contains 35 to 55 mg of caffeine.[28] Alcohol and caffeine consumption by mothers during pregnancy were both significantly associated with elevated IgE concentrations, which in fact affects the regulation of fetal IgE production.[29] One study found that maternal caffeine intake during pregnancy may be positively associated with the risk of childhood disorders, including eczema.[30]

Several previous studies have shown that endogenous molecules released from tissues or cells by skin injuries, including scratching, stimulate the immune response in atopic dermatitis, leading to worsening of the lesions.[31–33] Whereas some proteases (e.g., kinin-releasing enzymes, trypsin-like enzymes, and endogenous/exogenous proteases) are thought to cause pruritus.[34,35] Theaflavin is the main active ingredient in black tea. It has been found that theaflavin monomer theaflavin-3,3’-digallate (TF3) significantly reduces the accumulation of lipid droplets in hepatocytes and directly binds to and inhibits plasma kinin-releasing enzyme (PK) activation.[36] Tea is rich in flavonoids with potent antioxidant, cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory activities. Flavonoids consist of a tricyclic structure (A, B, and C). The specific hydroxylation pattern of the B ring of flavonoids allows them to inhibit the release of histamine, trypsin-like enzymes, interleukin-6 and interleukin-8 from cells.[37]

Observational studies have revealed that tea has significant anti-allergic effects on asthma, food allergies, atopic dermatitis and allergic reactions.[38] However, these discoveries are not entirely consistent with our MR study. The difference could be due to observational studies not being able to completely eliminate the impact of remaining variables and potential reverse causation. Furthermore, observational studies may also have inaccuracies in assessing long-term tea intake. Just like retrospective case-control studies, recall bias is a significant concern that impacts the precision of findings.[39] Asthma and allergic rhinitis have a delayed onset characteristic.[40,41] Tea intake does not reach the threshold for inducing disease and may be insufficient to cause them. In addition, achieving a perfectly random distribution of habitual tea drinkers is challenging in practice due to factors like age, personal eating habits, and the prevalence of local tea customs.[42] All of the factors mentioned above may have influenced the conclusions of previous observational studies.

This study has several significant strengths. Initially, we employed a MR strategy to investigate, for the 1st instance, the cause-and-effect connection between tea intake and 5 prevalent allergic conditions, effectively avoiding the constraints of conventional observational research, like external factors, possible reverse causation, and inadequate sample size. Second, with the help of a large number of sample case data, we substantially increase the credibility of our findings. Furthermore, we performed various sensitivity analyses to verify that our research was not influenced by horizontal pleiotropy and heterogeneity, to validate the absence of violation of the assumption of independence of IVs in the analyses, and to identify and address potential outliers using the MR-PRESSO method, thereby enhancing the strength and reliability of our results. Our research is anticipated to enhance our comprehension of the connection between tea intake and allergic conditions, offering valuable guidance for managing the diet of individuals with allergies.

It is important to note that despite the several strengths of this study, there are some unavoidable limitations. The results primarily focus on individuals of European descent, potentially restricting their applicability to other ethnicities. Furthermore, given the limitations of the database information, we were not able to assess the potential impact of tea subclasses and disease-specific classifications on the study results, nor were we able to conduct a more detailed stratified analysis and consider other possible confounding factors. In addition, allergic diseases are influenced by interactions between genes and environmental exposures, along with epigenetic modifications like methylation and histone changes.[43,44] In addition to this, the diagnosis of disease is largely based on patient self-reports, which may be subject to recall bias, and relies on the initial diagnosis of the primary care physician. Like many chronic health conditions, allergic disease likely results from complex gene–environment interactions. The increase in the prevalence of allergic disease over the past few decades has occurred too rapidly to be accounted for by changes in the genome alone and is more likely to be the result of changes in environmental factors that are in some cases accompanied by epigenetic changes. For example, microbial exposure, indoor allergen exposure, and exposure to ambient air pollution may affect susceptibility to allergic disease.[45] Unfortunately, we were unable to assess these influences under the current MR analysis framework. Although MR analysis is a valid tool to assess the causal relationship between tea intake and allergic diseases, it is necessary to validate the results of this study with basic experimental data. Therefore, caution should be taken when interpreting our findings and a more comprehensive body of evidence should be synthesized when formulating relevant dietary recommendations.

5. Conclusion

This study reveals a correlation between tea intake and elevated risk of eczema, and indicates that tea intake has a significant protective effect against atopic dermatitis. Furthermore, the study findings indicated that consuming tea did not have a significant correlation with the occurrence of asthma, allergic rhinitis, or chronic cough. Our findings are expected to be useful for the dietary management of patients with allergic diseases. However, there are some limitations in our study, and therefore, further studies are still needed to explore and elucidate the biological mechanisms between tea intake and allergic diseases for better understanding and management of these diseases.
