Digital impression accuracy for bone-level and tissue-level implants using scan bodies of different heights
Authors: Jae-Hyun Lee, Ji-Su Jo, Su Young Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraoral scanning has gained acceptance for its accuracy comparable to conventional implant impressions. This study compared digital impression accuracy based on implant placement level and scan body height. Two mandibular partial edentulous models missing the left second premolar and first molar, were used: one with a tissue level implant and the other with a bone level implant. Models were divided into four groups: Group TS-S (bone level, short scan bodies), Group TS-R (bone level, Regular scan bodies), Group SS-S (tissue level, short scan bodies), and Group SS-R (tissue level, Regular scan bodies). Each group was scanned with a model scanner for reference STL files and two intraoral scanners (IOSs; Medit i700, Cerec Primescan) for experimental STL files. Deviation between experimental and reference STL files was measured. The Medit i700 demonstrated smaller scan discrepancies compared to the CEREC Primescan. For both intraoral scanners, the use of short scan bodies resulted in higher trueness than regular scan bodies. Tissue level implants exhibited greater trueness and precision than bone level implants (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that both the implant placement level and scan body height are factors that can influence the accuracy of digital impressions.Keywords: Dental implant, Digital impression, Intraoral scanner, Computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM), Scan bodySubject terms: Dentistry, Dental equipment, Dental materials, Prosthetic dentistry

Introduction

As the production of prosthetics using computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology advances in the dental field, its application in the fabrication of implant prostheses is becoming increasingly prevalent. Recently, many clinicians have begun utilizing intraoral scanners for taking impressions, revolutionizing the process and enhancing the accuracy of digital impressions. Intraoral scanning has many advantages: (1) reducing impression time and eliminating patient discomfort caused by impression materials1,2; (2) no need to disinfect the impression after removal from the mouth, thus avoiding potential volumetric changes in the impression due to the action of disinfectant agents; (3) digital images obtained through this process are not damaged and, if necessary, can be repeatedly reproduced regardless of time or place, enabling efficient model storage and research compared to existing plaster models2,3; and (4) the accuracy of digital impressions obtained with an intraoral scanner is similar to that of conventional impressions and clinically acceptable4–7.

For digital scanning of the implant, like impression coping used in traditional impressions, the scan body transmits the implant position in a digital image8. When the scan body is attached to the implant and the oral cavity is scanned, the obtained digital impression can backtrack the position of the scan body and produce a prosthesis based on the position of the implant. Various types of scan bodies have been introduced in terms of material, size, surface, shape, reusability, connection method, software-scanner compatibility, and price. Factors affecting the accuracy of digital impressions are known to include the shape and the material of the scan body, implant location, implant angle9. Therefore, it is important to select an appropriate scan body to obtain an accurate digital image of the implant location using in an intraoral scanner.

Although various studies have been conducted to assess the accuracy of digital impressions, the accuracy of digital impressions based on the height of scan bodies remains controversial. Several studies have suggested that a longer scan body leads to higher accuracy10–14 . On the other hand, other studies have found that shorter scan bodies can be more accurate15–17. Despite these findings, there is a lack of research on the accuracy of impressions according to the height of the scan body at different implant placement levels. Previous studies have largely assessed implant placement level and scan body height independently, which has restricted the understanding of their potential interaction on digital implant impression accuracy. The present study addressed this limitation by simultaneously evaluating both variables and analyzing their combined effects. Therefore, the aim was to compare the accuracy of digital impressions according to implant placement level and scan body height using two intraoral scanner systems. The null hypothesis was that there was no difference in the accuracy of impression depending on the implant placement level or the height of the scan body.

Materials and methods

Two partially edentulous acrylic resin dentoform with missing mandibular left second premolar and first molar were prepared. The gingiva was represented by silicone material with 2 mm thickness. Two internal connection type bone level implants (TS III 5.0×10.0 mm, Osstem Implant Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea) and two internal connection type tissue level implants (SS III 5.0×10.0 mm, Osstem Implant Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea) were placed in the mandibular left second premolar and first molar area in each of the two models. These implants were placed in ideal positions with the use of pre-fabricated surgical guide stent (One Guide, Osstem Implant Co. Ltd., Seoul, Korea). The insertion depth of bone level implants was set at 1 mm below the bone height, which was 3 mm subgingival based on the tissue level. The insertion depth of the tissue level implants was set at equigingival level. Scan bodies connecting to the two implants were of the following two types in the two models: regular scan bodies and short scan bodies (Fig. 1).

Experimental models were divided into four groups based on the level of implant placement and the height of the scan body (Table 1 and Fig. 2).

Each scan body was connected to the model with a 15 Ncm tightening torque using a torque wrench. The position of the groove was directed in the buccal position. Correct positioning and superimposition of the scan body were enabled by the buccal groove.

Reference scan data were obtained through the scanning process using a model scanner (MEDIT Identica T500; Medit Inc., Seoul, Korea) with an accuracy (ISO 12,836) of 7 μm. Reference scan data from each group were exported into the Standard Tessellation Language (STL) file format. Digital impressions of the four groups were recorded using two types of intralora scanners (IOSs, Medit i700, Medit Inc., seoul, Korea; Cerec Primescan, Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany).

Sample size calculation was conducted using G*Power version 3.1. The calculation was based on the results of a pilot study involving scan bodies, comparing four groups, as well as on previous studies with similar methodologies and objectives18–21. An anticipated mean difference of 50 µm and a within-group standard deviation of 25 µm were determined based on preliminary experiments. Assuming a medium effect size of 0.4, alpha of 5%, and a power of 95%, the required sample size was calculated for four independent groups. The result indicated a required sample size of 10 per group.

The scan was performed 10 times for each group, with 80 scans obtained: 40 scans using each IOSs. All digital impressions were recorded in the clinic. The color temperature of clinic lights was 3900 K, and the brightness was 400–500 lx. The distance between the actual scanner of the IOS and the scan body was kept constant at 2.5–5.0 mm. The occlusal plane of the model was kept parallel to the floor, and the IOS was kept parallel to occlusal and sagittal planes.

Calibrations were performed before each scan. Strategies used in the present study followed the manufacturer’s instructions. All scans started from the left second molar in one direction. Scan strategies used in the present study were as follows: the scan was started by capturing occlusal surfaces. Lingual surfaces of the entire arch were then scanned moving back to the starting point. The acquisition was completed by scanning buccal aspects. All scans started from the second molar and moved to the implant.

After completion of scanning, scan files were saved in STL formats to obtain experimental STL files. To assess the accuracy of each scan body, experimental STL files were superimposed over reference scan data using 3D metrology software (Geomagic Control X 2023.1, Geomagic, Rock Hill, SC, USA). Reference and experimental scan data were superimposed and aligned using the best-fit alignment algorithm.

Best-fit alignment is an automatic algorithm that iteratively translates and rotates the data group, to bring it as close as possible to the reference. Deviation of experimental scan data from reference data was analyzed. The software automatically calculated the root mean square (RMS), which was used for the deviation analysis to evaluate the trueness of impressions. Comparing RMS values is an appropriate method for comparing the deviation between two large sets of data.

The |Mean| is the average of absolute values of the average negative deviation and the average positive deviation. As the average deviation value in the program was calculated by adding positive and negative deviations, the absolute value of the average negative deviation was used for deviation analysis. In addition, the tolerance of deviation values in the program was set to 100 μm. The ratio of deviation values to be included in this tolerance was measured and documented as %. This value indicates how many deviation values are distributed within the tolerance. It is used to estimate precision.

Data were analyzed using statistical analysis software (SPSS Statistics 25.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate whether deviation values varied with the height of the scan body and the placement level. An independent t-test was performed to assess deviations according to the height of scan bodies, placement level, and scanner types. Post-hoc tests such as Duncan’s test were performed. The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

The within-tolerance ratio for short scan bodies was higher than that for regular scan bodies, although their difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.217). Consequently, the use of short scan bodies resulted in a higher trueness than regular scan bodies, while the precision according to the height of scan body did not show a statistically significant difference. |Mean| and RMS values for tissue level implants were significantly smaller than those for bone level implants (p < 0.01). In addition, the within-tolerance ratio of tissue level implants was significantly greater than that of the bone level implants (p < 0.05). Therefore, when a tissue level implant was used, both trueness and precision were superior to a bone level implant.

Discussion

This study measured the accuracy of digital impression according to implant placement level and height of the scan body. Findings of this study demonstrated that both the level of implant placement and the height of the scan body significantly impacted the accuracy of digital impressions. Among four groups evaluated, the group using short scan bodies with tissue level implants (Group SS-S) consistently showed the best results. Consequently, the null hypothesis that there was no difference in the accuracy of impression depending on the implant placement level, and the height of the scan body was rejected.

The present study extends the current understanding of digital implant impressions by simultaneously evaluating two critical variables—implant placement level and scan body height—and their combined effects on impression accuracy. While previous investigations have primarily examined these factors independently, the present findings indicate that they interact significantly, offering new insights that may inform clinical decision-making in implant dentistry.

In addition, the use of robust statistical methods (two-way ANOVA with post-hoc testing) enhanced the reliability and validity of the results. By incorporating both bone-level and tissue-level implants in conjunction with different scan body heights, the study design reproduced clinically relevant conditions. These methodological considerations distinguish this work from previous studies and highlight its contribution to strengthening the evidence base for digital implant impression accuracy.

Several studies have investigated differences in the accuracy of digital impressions depending on scan body height7,8,22–24. Alikhasi et al.24 have found that the accuracy of impressions decreases with larger implant angulation, particularly when longer scan bodies are used, suggesting that shorter scan bodies can help mitigate the negative impact of angulation on impression accuracy. Additionally, Mizumoto and Yilmaz8 have discussed that longer scan bodies could introduce leverage-related errors, especially when used at improper angles, highlighting the importance of accurate angulation to reduce errors and improve the reliability of digital impressions. Mühlemann et al.7 have also reported that shorter scan bodies result in more precise digital models. These studies aligned with our findings that short scan bodies were associated with increased accuracy compared to long scan bodies. These results demonstrated the critical factor of scan body length in achieving precise and accurate digital impressions for implant dentistry. Findings of our study indicate that short scan bodies are associated with increased an accuracy compared to long scan bodies. This may be because the reduced distance between the scan body and the implant fixture can minimize measurement errors and distortions. Longer scan bodies, on the other hand, increase the distance from the fixture, which could amplify potential inaccuracies and introduce leverage effects that could distort data.

Our study also observed differences based on the implant placement level. Tissue level implants showed a higher accuracy than bone level implants. One potential reason for the superior results observed in the group involving tissue level implants (Group SS) regarding implant placement level could be due to better accessibility and visibility during scanning. The quality of the intraoral scan data may vary depending on the accessibility of the IOS and the range of access25. Tissue level implants were placed at the equigingival level, making them easier to capture accurately with the scanner and reducing challenges associated with subgingival scanning. This improved accessibility likely enhanced the stability and consistency of the scan body connection, leading to more accurate digital impressions. The interaction between implant placement level and digital impression accuracy suggests that clinicians should aim for placement that can minimize biological interference. This approach not only enhances the accuracy of digital impressions, but also contributes to long-term success of implant-supported restorations.

The accuracy of digital impressions can be defined by precision and trueness (ISO 5725–1). Precision is described as deviation between recorded digital images when measured repeatedly26. Trueness is described as deviation between the actual model and the recorded digital image when measured in the same manner. For obtaining accurate impression, trueness in digital impressions is important18. Taking accurate impressions is a crucial factor for successful implant prostheses. Inaccurate registration of implant position will result in fabrication of an ill-fitting prosthesis, which can ultimately lead to biological and mechanical complications27.

Various devices have been utilized to assess the accuracy of digital impressions, each with distinct advantages depending on the study design and required resolution. An accurate reference scan can be obtained using industrial optical scanners or coordinate measuring machines (CMM), both of which are widely used in industrial applications due to their high accuracy and have been introduced in dentistry28,29. Although a CMM is known for its high accuracy in obtaining reference data29, a model scanner was selected in this study because of its proven trueness, precision, and widespread use in digital dentistry28. While CMM offers superior resolution and contact-based measurement that minimizes optical distortion, its application is often limited by high cost, complex operation, and incompatibility with typical dental workflows. Conversely, model scanners are specifically designed for scanning dental models, are more cost-effective, and are readily accessible in clinical and laboratory settings. Previous study comparing model scanners with industrial scanners has demonstrated that model scanners are suitable as reference data for evaluating the accuracy of intraoral scanners (IOSs)28. Therefore, the model scanner employed in the present study was considered appropriate for generating reliable reference data while maintaining clinical relevance and methodological practicality.

The overall difference between reference data and data obtained for each group was evaluated by calculating root mean square (RMS) values. The primary difference between our study and prior research lies in the simultaneous evaluation of both implant placement levels and scan body heights. In this study, RMS values ranged from 104.24 to 238.23 µm. Most results obtained with digital scans were within a clinically acceptable threshold19,23,25. However, deviations observed in our study were relatively high when evaluating them against other studies. Variations in scanner technology and calibration methods can influence accuracies of measurements. Differences in study protocols, including scanning techniques, might also contribute. Environmental conditions during scanning, such as humidity and lighting, could introduce additional variability. These factors collectively present the complexity of achieving precise digital impressions in clinical settings and indicate the need for standardized protocols and advanced scanning technologies to minimize discrepancies. Given that intraoral scanning is technique-sensitive and subject to various influencing factors, significant efforts should be made to minimize errors during the scanning process.

In this study, we observed significant differences in the accuracy of digital impressions based on the type of intraoral scanner used. The Medit i700 demonstrated higher trueness and precision than the Cerec Primescan, although the latter required less scanning time, which could be advantageous in clinical settings. Several studies have examined differences in accuracy depending on the type of intraoral scanner used. Giuliodori et al.30 have evaluated accuracies of six IOSs based on different scanning strategies and operator experience levels. Their findings demonstrated that the Medit i700 and Primescan IOS systems excelled in terms of trueness and precision compared to the other four scanners. Róth et al.20 have conducted a comprehensive comparison of 12 different IOSs based on objective parameters. Their study identified Primescan as the most accurate IOS among those evaluated. These results suggest that different clinical situations and research methodologies can lead to varying outcomes for scanner accuracy, indicating that scanner performance may vary depending on the specific context in which it is used.

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as an in vitro study conducted in a controlled laboratory setting, its findings might not fully represent conditions and challenges encountered in actual clinical practice. Clinical environments often introduce variables such as patient movement, saliva, and other factors that can affect the accuracy of digital impressions31. Moreover, the operator’s experience can influence the accuracy and outcome of digital impressions32–34. In our study, all digital impressions were taken by a single operator to minimize variability in the data. Although this consistency in operator was intended to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the impression-taking process throughout the study, the operator’s experience had approximately three years of clinical experience with digital impression techniques, which we consider to be an intermediate level of proficiency. To address this issue, it could be advantageous to develop standardized supports or scanning jigs that can stabilize implant models, ensuring consistent scanning angles and distances. Such standardization could mitigate the variability and enhance the overall accuracy of digital impressions. Additionally, in the present study, tissue-level implants were placed in parallel, whereas bone-level implants exhibited a slightly convergent configuration. Although minor deviations in angulation present, this discrepancy may have partly contributed to the greater accuracy observed in tissue-level implants. Therefore, the interpretation of our findings should consider the methodological bias related to implant angulation.

Within these limitations, our study provides significant insights into the impact of implant placement level and scan body height on the accuracy of digital impressions. Results of this study highlight the importance of selecting an appropriate scan body and optimizing the placement level to achieve superior accuracy in digital dental procedures. This research emphasizes the potential of digital impression techniques to enhance clinical outcomes, improve patient comfort, and streamline dental workflows. By understanding these factors, clinicians can precisely determine the optimal scan body height, select an appropriate implant placement level, and choose the most effective scanning techniques for each specific clinical situation. This careful approach will lead to highly precise and reliable implant-supported restorations, minimizing the risk of prosthetic misfits and associated complications.

Conclusions drawn from this research demonstrate the importance of selecting appropriate scan body lengths and implant placement levels based on specific clinical conditions and technological capabilities of the scanning equipment used18,35. Additionally, our findings collectively emphasize advancements and variability among IOS technologies available in the dental field. Clinicians and researchers should consider these differences when selecting an IOS for clinical practice, aiming for systems that can offer both high trueness and precision to enhance quality of intraoral scanning and patient outcomes. Further studies could explore specific factors influencing the accuracy of intraoral scanners (IOS) and investigate strategies to optimize their performance in various clinical situations. In addition, additional research is needed to evaluate how different scan body materials affect scanning accuracy.
