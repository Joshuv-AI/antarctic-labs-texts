Theiler's murine encephalomyelitis virus as the infectious agent for a virally induced mouse model of Parkinson's disease
Authors: Tae Wook Kang, Rahul Srinivasan, Candice Brinkmeyer-Langford, C. Jane Welsh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parkinson's disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder with a prevalence of over ten million patients worldwide. The etiology of PD remains unclear but neuroinflammation specifically associated with viral infection has risen as a possible contributor to the disease. Classical animal models of PD reproduce certain pathophysiological outcomes such as the degeneration of dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), but these models are limited by the need to inject harmful neurotoxins to induce disease-like symptoms. We present a virally induced neuroinflammatory model of PD in C57BL/6J mice using a naturally occurring pathogen, Theiler's murine encephalomyelitis virus (TMEV), as the infectious agent. This model offers a tool for advancing our understanding of PD pathogenesis and potential treatment options.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is one of the most common neurodegenerative disorders, characterized by loss of dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), which results in a constellation of non-motor and motor symptoms that significantly impact patient quality of life (Thomas and Beal, 2007). PD prevalence is projected to rise with the aging global population, which may further increase the burden associated with disease management and treatment (Dorsey et al., 2007; Li et al., 2025; Rocca, 2018; Willis et al., 2022). Despite its increasing prevalence, the etiology of PD remains unclear; however, recent findings suggest that neuroinflammation may play a critical role (Pajares et al., 2020). Current animal models for PD primarily rely on neurotoxins or genetic mutations to induce the loss of SNc DA neurons but few models address on neuroinflammation as a primary causal factor (Swanberg, 2018). The multiple-hit hypothesis of PD suggests that a combination of genetic predispositions and environmental factors may be required to initiate disease pathology (Cabezudo et al., 2020). Among many environmental exposure risk factors, viral infections have been recognized as potential contributors to the development of PD (Leta et al., 2022). In fact, several viruses including influenza, dengue, herpes simplex, hepatitis E, hepatitis C and Epstein-Barr have been associated with neurological diseases (Bopeththa and Ralapanawa, 2017; Ghosh et al., 2021; Guan et al., 2012; Jang et al., 2009; Takahashi and Yamada, 1999; Wang et al., 2020). However, there are currently no well-established models in which an infection-centered mechanism leads to the progressive loss of SNc DA neurons. Based on this evidence, a virus-based model serves as a valuable tool to further advance our understanding of PD pathogenesis.

Theiler's Murine Encephalomyelitis Virus (TMEV) is a natural pathogen of mice that causes various neurological diseases in susceptible strains (Gerhauser et al., 2019; Lipton, 1975; Theiler, 1934). Historically, TMEV has been used extensively as a model for studying multiple sclerosis (MS) and epilepsy (Dal Canto et al., 1996; Stewart et al., 2010) Notably, TMEV has been shown to specifically infect and destroy DA neurons in the SNc (Oliver et al., 1997). However, no assessment of motor function has been performed to confirm whether the destruction of DA neurons due to TMEV leads to altered motor symptoms observed in classical neurotoxin-induced PD animal models such as the 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) or 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP) models. Adenovirus associated models involving α-synuclein (AAV-Syn) have been used to model PD, and a previous study identified that adenovirus-induced reactive astrogliosis exacerbates PD pathology but investigation into the role of direct viral infection by a neurotropic virus and subsequent neuroinflammation has been limited (An et al., 2021; Kirik et al., 2002). We hypothesized that specific infection of SNc DA neurons by TMEV would lead to persistent depletion of the nigrostriatal DA pathway to induce PD-like motor deficiencies observed in the classic PD models. We propose that TMEV induced DA neuron depletion in the SNc represents a promising virus-based animal model to advance our understanding of PD pathogenesis.

Methods

Animals

All animal care protocols were in accordance with the NIH Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (AUP, 2022-0252 and 2023-0066). C57BL/6J mice were selected for this study as they have a marked neuroinflammatory response following infection with TMEV (Bijalwan et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2010). Adult (8-12-week-old) male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Texas A&M Institute for Genomic Medicine. The mice were maintained in an AAALAC accredited facility under a 14-h light and 10-h dark cycle with food and water ad libitum.

Experimental design

We performed two sets of experiments to test our hypothesis regarding the validity of a TMEV induced animal model of PD. Experiment A was performed to confirm that TMEV is able to infect DA neurons in the SNc at the acute infection time point of 1-week post-infection and to confirm the loss of DA neurons in the SNc at 1-month post-infection timepoint (N = 1 at the 1-week post-infection timepoint, N = 5 TMEV group, N = 3 control group at the 4-weeks post-infection timepoint). N = 1 for the 1-week post-infection timepoint was for preliminary qualitative confirmation of viral infection of the DA neurons via our surgical method as DA neuron infection using TMEV was shown in a previous study (Oliver et al., 1997). Experiment B was conducted to determine if TMEV induced DA neuron loss in the SNc leads to motor deficiencies associated with PD-like symptoms (N = 13 TMEV group, N = 14 control group). Unilateral injection of TMEV into the SNc

All mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane for induction and maintained on 2-3% isoflurane throughout the procedure. Once anesthetized, fur over the surgical site was removed using clippers and the mice were placed in a stereotaxic frame equipped with a heating pad to regulate body temperature. The surgical area was sanitized with povidone-iodine followed by 70% ethanol and ophthalmic ointment was applied to the eyes to prevent corneal drying. Approximately 1400 plaque forming units (PFU) of TMEV (American Type Culture Collection [ATCC] VR 995, Manassas, VA) in 0.5 μl of Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) were injected into the SNc at a flow rate of 0.25 μl/min. The stereotaxic coordinates for the injection were 3.0 mm posterior to bregma, 1.5 mm lateral to the midline and 4.2 mm ventral to the pial surface. Control mice received the same surgery but were injected with 0.5 μl of DMEM only. For 2 weeks following surgery, mice were observed and weighed daily to monitor general health and from 3 weeks post-surgery they were observed weekly. Mice that lost more than 20% of their presurgical body weight were euthanized and excluded from the study.

Behavioral testing

Pole test

Mice were placed head upwards on top of a vertical wooden pole with a smooth surface, 50 cm in length and 1 cm in diameter. The time taken for the mouse to turn and face downwards just before starting its descent was recorded as time-to-turn (TTurn). The time from when the mouse started descending until it was completely off the pole was recorded as time-to-descend (TDescend). The maximum duration for each measurement was 60 s, and if the mouse was unable to turn or fell off the pole, the time was recorded as 60 s, indicating maximal severity. This protocol was slightly modified from previous pole test experiments (Matsuura et al., 1997). All mice were assessed three times per session. The mice received habituation sessions which were identical to the testing procedures one day prior to each assessment date.

Apomorphine induced rotations

The apomorphine-induced rotations test was conducted as previously described (Pandey et al., 2025; Zarate et al., 2021, 2025). Briefly, each mouse was placed in a 5-gallon circular bucket (33 cm in diameter) and allowed to acclimate for 15 min prior to testing. After the acclimation period, apomorphine (0.5 mg/kg in 0.9% saline; Sigma, Cat #1041008) was injected intraperitoneally (i.p.), and immediately afterwards, rotational behavior was recorded for 15 min via video using a ceiling-mounted camera. The total number of contralateral rotations made during this 15-min test was counted using EthoVision XT (Noldus, Leesburg, VA, RRID). All arena sizes and detection settings were kept consistent throughout all timepoints.

DigiGait

DigiGait (Mouse Specifics, Boston, MA, USA) is a transparent-belt treadmill system equipped with a camera to record the footprints of rodents. These recordings allow for quantitative assessment of gait patterns. 48 gait parameters were evaluated, and the experiments were conducted as previously described with a slight modification to the speed of the treadmill (Eldridge et al., 2020; Karmakar et al., 2023). Briefly, mice were placed on the DigiGait treadmill and allowed to walk at a speed of 12 cm/s for a minimum of six consecutive steps per limb. Measurements for each limb were assessed independently using DigiGait analysis software. Mice that were unable to perform the test due to inability to walk on the treadmill were excluded from the analysis.

Histology

Tissue collection

Mice were euthanized at 20 weeks post-surgery via i.p. injection of a lethal dose of Beuthanasia 150 mg/kg. After euthanasia, mice were transcardially perfused through the left ventricle with 10 mL of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 10% formalin in PBS. Following perfusion, a necropsy was performed on each mouse, and brains were collected and fixed in 10% formalin for at least 48 h. After fixation, brains were cryoprotected in a 30% sucrose solution in PBS. Brains were then sectioned coronally at 40 μm using a cryostat (Model # CM1950, Leica Microsystems). Sections were stored in 0.01% sodium azide (Sigma, Cat #S2002) at 4 °C until immunostaining.

Quantification of DA neuron loss in SNc

SNc DA neuron degeneration was assessed by comparing the fluorescent intensities from mouse midbrain sections, as previously described (Zarate et al., 2021). Briefly, six midbrain sections were selected at 120 μm intervals for immunostaining. Following three washes in PBS, sections were permeabilized and blocked with 0.5% Triton X-100 and 10% normal goat serum (NGS) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). To evaluate the effect of surgery on tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive DA neurons, sections were co-labeled with rabbit anti-TMEV (1:500; in-house) and chicken anti-TH primary antibody (1:1500, Abcam, Cat # ab76442) overnight at 4 °C, followed by goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000, Abcam, Cat # ab150077) and goat anti-chicken Alexa Fluor 594 secondary antibody (1:2000, Abcam, Cat # ab150176) (4-week timepoint) or goat anti-chicken Alex Fluor 405 (1:2000, Abcam, Cat # ab175675) (1-week timepoint) for 1 h at RT. The antibody incubation buffer was composed of 0.05% Triton X-100 and 1% NGS in PBS.

An Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope equipped with LED excitation at 405 and 488 nm was used for imaging TMEV infected TH positive neurons in the SNc at the 1-week timepoint at 20x magnification. All brain sections at the 4-week timepoint were scanned at 20x magnification using an Olympus VS120 Virtual Slide Scanning System, and images were analyzed using Fiji ImageJ software. Of the six immunostained sections, three representative sections in which the SNc and VTA could be visually separated were selected for analysis. Individual regions of interests (ROI) were manually drawn around the injected and uninjected SNc. Fluorescent intensities were obtained by thresholding to select TH-positive fibers and cell bodies for each ROI. Fluorescent intensities from the three midbrain sections per mouse (injected and uninjected SNc) were collected, and the ratios of the injected to the uninjected sides were compared.

Statistics

All data were tested for normality prior to conducting statistical testing. To investigate the behavioral differences in the pole test between the TMEV infected group and the control group, the three TTurn and TDescend measurements collected at each timepoint were averaged and two-way repeated measures ANOVA was performed to compare the effect of viral infection and timepoint post-infection on TTurn and TDescend. Tukey's post hoc tests were performed for each timepoint comparing the differences between the control and TMEV infected groups. For the apomorphine induced rotations test, the effect of viral infection and timepoint post-infection on the number of contralateral rotations was examined by performing a two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Tukey's post hoc tests were performed for each timepoint comparing the differences between the control and TMEV infected groups. Outliers were detected using the Robust Regression and Outlier Removal (ROUT) method with a Q of 1% and were removed before performing the ANOVA and post hoc tests. To identify differences between the groups in the DigiGait analysis, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc tests for each timepoint were performed. To assess the degree of DA neuron loss in the SNc, a two-sample t-test was performed to compare the fluorescent intensities between the injected and uninjected sides of the brain. Data were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05 and all data in graphs are presented as means ± S.E.M. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.2 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Graphical depictions of experiments in Fig. 1, Fig. 3, Fig. 4A were created using Biorender.com with publication rights provided through the license of Texas A&M University.

Apomorphine induced contralateral rotations increased following unilateral TMEV injection

The apomorphine-induced rotations test is commonly used to assess the extent of unilateral damage to the nigrostriatal dopamine system (Bjorklund and Dunnett, 2019). The DA receptors of post synaptic neurons in unilateral lesions induced by neurotoxins such as 6-OHDA become hypersensitive to DA (Ungerstedt, 1971). Apomorphine, a DA receptor agonist, acts post-synaptically to induce contralateral rotation towards the opposite side of the lesion due to hyperstimulation of the supersensitive DA receptors (Fig. 3A). In the current study, mice unilaterally injected with TMEV showed a significant increase in the number of apomorphine-induced contralateral rotations (Fig. 3B). A two-way ANOVA was conducted to analyze the effect of TMEV injection and time post-injection on the number of contralateral rotations following apomorphine administration. Main effects analysis showed that TMEV injection had a significant impact on the increased number of rotations (F(1, 25) = 17.03, p = 0.0004). The effect of time post-injection was also significant (F(2.730, 68.25) = 8.139, p = 0.0002). The effect of the interaction between time post-injection and TMEV injection was significant as well F(6, 150) = 6.993, p < 0.0001). A post-hoc Tukey test revealed that the number of contralateral rotations in the TMEV injected group was significantly increased at all timepoints following TMEV injection compared to the control group: 1-week (p = 0.0255), 4-weeks (p = 0.0027), 8-weeks (p = 0.0222), 12-weeks (p = 0.0024), 16-weeks (p = 0.0027), 20-weeks (p = 0.0006). This significant difference persisted until the end of study at 20-weeks. At baseline, prior to surgeries, there was no significant difference between the number of rotations in the TMEV mice and control mice.

Maximal rate of change of paw area during the propulsion phase of walking was decreased in TMEV injected mice

Out of 48 gait parameters analyzed using the DigiGait system, the maximal rate of change in paw area in contact with the treadmill belt during the propulsion phase (MINdAdT) for the left forepaw differed significantly between TMEV injected mice and control mice. MINdAdT assesses how quickly the limb is unloaded from the treadmill during the later stance phase, serving as an indicator of how quickly and effectively the animal generates propulsive force to transition into the next step. Starting from the 1-week and up to the 20-week timepoint, MINdAdT of the left forelimb (MINdAdT LF) was consistently decreased in TMEV injected mice compared to those in the control group (Fig. 4B). A two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed that TMEV injection and time post-injection both significantly impacted the MINdAdT LF following TMEV injection (F(1, 20) = 4.592, p = 0.0446; F(3.016, 60.32) = 5.355, p = 0.0024). A post hoc Tukey test showed that the decrease in the TMEV injected group was significant at the 12-week timepoint (p = 0.0005).

Discussion

The most widely used animal models of PD are toxin-based models which involve the injection of chemicals such as 6-OHDA and MPTP, inducing loss of DA neurons in the SNc (Duty and Jenner, 2011; Ungerstedt and Arbuthnott, 1970). These neurotoxins are selective to catecholaminergic DA neurons inducing cell death through mitochondrial oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Glinka et al., 1997). These classic animal models have been useful tools for improving understanding of PD pathophysiology and have helped in testing new drug therapies (Cui et al., 2024). However, while neurotoxin-based models replicate the clinical aspect of DA neuron loss in the SNc, they may not be the best representation of disease development in humans since humans are rarely exposed to high levels of neurotoxins. PD is a complex disease with an unknown etiology, but a combination of genetic and environmental risk factors is thought to be involved in the development of disease (Veldman et al., 1998). Among possible risk factors, viruses have emerged as suspected contributors and recent evidence has linked influenza, herpes simplex and hepatitis virus infection as risk factors for PD (Wang et al., 2020). Based on this evidence, we envisioned that a virally induced disease model could be a useful tool to further our understanding of PD pathogenesis.

There is well established evidence that a single virus can cause multiple distinct diseases depending on the host genetic background, immune status or tissue tropism. For example, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a disease mainly associated with infection mononucleosis, has also been associated with lymphoma as well as multiple sclerosis. Similarly, SARS-CoV-2, which causes the mainly respiratory disease COVID-19, can also manifest in cardiovascular and neurological disease (Bjornevik et al., 2022; Gupta et al., 2020; Young and Rickinson, 2004). TMEV, a mouse specific virus, has traditionally been used to model MS and epilepsy (Dal Canto et al., 1995; Oleszak et al., 2004; Stewart et al., 2010). Interestingly, the genetic background of the infected mouse determines the resulting neurological disease symptoms (Brinkmeyer-Langford et al., 2017; Kang et al., 2025). Recently, in a specific strain of Collaborative Cross (CC) mouse, TMEV infection has been shown to replicate symptoms of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) (Lawley et al., 2025). These studies provide evidence that TMEV can be used to model multiple diseases based on the genetic background of the infected host. In addition, a previous study has identified that TMEV is able to infect DA neurons in the SNc (Oliver et al., 1997). However, prior to the current study, further investigation into behavioral and pathological symptoms due to infection has not been conducted for TMEV infection of DA neurons. In this current study we provide evidence that TMEV viral infection in mice can serve as a viable virally induced model of PD.

The pole test is a commonly used behavioral assay to measure motor coordination in PD model mice (Glajch et al., 2012; Matsuura et al., 1997). Previous studies have observed that depletion of DA neurons in the SNc in PD mouse models led to increased times for mice performing the pole test, indicative of bradykinesia, a common symptom observed in PD patients (Glajch et al., 2012; Matsuura et al., 1997). We hypothesized that if TMEV successfully infects and degenerates DA neurons in the SNc, the levels of DA in the striatum would be decreased, leading to increased times on the pole seen in the experiments of classical PD models. The results of this experiment revealed a significant increase in TDescend and a trend toward significance in TTurn due to the effect of TMEV injection. Moreover, TTurn was increased in TMEV injected mice at all timepoints excluding week-8, while TDescend was increased at all timepoints (Fig. 2). The effects of motor coordination loss due to TMEV injection persisted until the end of the study at 20 weeks post-injection. This indicates that the motor coordination loss due to the effects of DA loss is replicated by TMEV injection in our model and these effects continue to be observed chronically following the initial injection of the virus.

The apomorphine induced rotations test enables the visualization and quantification of the extent of unilateral nigrostriatal damage due to loss of DA neurons (Bjorklund and Dunnett, 2019). It has been used extensively in the 6-OHDA and MPTP unilateral lesion models which selectively destroy dopaminergic neurons in the nigrostriatal pathway which produces dopamine depletion on the lesioned side of the brain (Przedborski et al., 2000; Ungerstedt and Arbuthnott, 1970). A decrease in DA input induces postsynaptic DA receptor hypersensitivity to DA on the lesioned side of the brain. When apomorphine, a DA receptor agonist and treatment drug for PD, is administered, activation of the hypersensitive striatum produces contralateral rotations toward the opposite side of the lesion (Schwarting and Huston, 1996). In our experiments, we observed a significantly increased number of contralateral rotations in the TMEV injected group at all measured timepoints (Fig. 3B). The effect of TMEV injection on the number of rotations was significant indicating that viral infection led to the depletion of DA in the injected site due to the loss of DA neurons. These behaviors are comparable and similar to previous classical unilateral lesion models. The continued increase in contralateral rotations in the TMEV injected group until the 20-week timepoint indicated that the effects of viral infection produced a prolonged degeneration of DA SNc neurons similar to the symptoms observed in PD patients.

Gait abnormality is also a common clinical feature of PD patients (Kalia and Lang, 2015). For example, in humans and in mouse models, Parkinsonian gait includes changes in stride length and cadence (Jankovic, 2008; MacKay-Lyons, 1998; Nieuwboer et al., 2001; Salarian et al., 2004). The DigiGait parameter MINdAdT is a measure of how quickly the limb is unloaded from the treadmill during a gait cycle. A typical gait cycle consists of a stance phase, where the foot is on the ground, followed by the swing phase, when the foot is in the air. Our results indicated that the left forelimb of TMEV infected mice was more quickly unloaded from the treadmill during the stance to swing phase transition compared to the control mice with a significant difference observed at the 12-week timepoint (p = 0.0005). This indicated that the TMEV injected mice were unable to maintain normal strength in the left forelimb leading to quickly shifting weight off that limb during the gait cycle, compared to the control mice. Since the right brain controls movement of the left side of the body (Ackerman, 1992), the unilateral degeneration of DA neurons in the SNc in our experiment is expected to produce weakness in the left side of the body, which is implicated in the gait differences observed in this study.

In addition to investigating the behavioral changes due to unilateral viral infection, our experiments tested for confirmation that our surgical methods would be able to replicate the specific infection of DA neurons in the SNc as demonstrated in the previous studies performed by Oliver et al. (1997). Our results confirmed that TMEV infected the DA neurons of the SNc specifically at the injection site as indicated by the colocalization of TMEV in TH positive DA neurons (Fig. 5). At 4-weeks post infection, our histological results also confirmed that TMEV caused TH positive DA neuron degeneration unilaterally on the TMEV injected side of the SNc (Fig. 6). The immunofluorescence intensity ratios of TH positive DA neurons in the uninjected/injected sides of the SNc showed significant decrease in the intensity of DA neurons in the SNc of TMEV injected mice while the control treated mice did not show a decrease in intensity (Fig. 6C). These results indicate that the injection of TMEV led to the loss of DA neurons in the injected side similar to other established models of PD.

Our study replicates the specific infection of TH positive DA neurons shown in a previous study and demonstrates that the loss of DA neurons leads to behaviors that mimic the effects of DA depletion shown in toxin-based PD models. TMEV is known to produce inflammation associated with neuropathology involving the interaction of viral infection and host immune responses (Tsunoda and Fujinami, 2010). While toxin-based models replicate aspects of PD, the neuroinflammatory responses, especially to environmental triggers such as viral infection remain largely unexplored. The availability of a viral model of PD using TMEV allows for further investigation into how responses to viral infection may contribute to PD disease processes. The neuroinflammatory environment and glial responses were not examined in this study and remains to be evaluated in future studies. Although the mechanism by which TMEV enters cells is not well defined, it is a well-documented neurotropic virus that infects and replicates in neurons during the acute phase of infection, followed by the infection of glial cells in the chronic phase (Tomatis et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2013). The receptor for TMEV entry into cells is not yet known, but it has been shown that TMEV attaches to cell-surface glycoconjugates such as sialic acid (Tsunoda et al., 2009). Sialic acids are present in the DA neurons of the SNc, and therefore, TMEV may use sialic acid as a receptor to infect DA neurons (Schnaar et al., 2014; Seyfried et al., 2018). A similar mechanism may also explain how viral infections directly damage DA neurons in humans. However, in PD, neuronal death mainly occurs via apoptosis and can be mediated by inflammatory responses of the immune system (Erekat, 2018; Pedrao et al., 2024). TMEV infects neurons lytically but also induces apoptosis via the results of immune system activation (Anderson et al., 2000). TMEV infection in our study may induce apoptosis of DA neurons, mirroring pathological neuronal apoptosis in PD patients. Further investigation of the mechanism of TMEV induced DA neuron loss in future studies could provide insight into viral contributions to pathological features of PD.

The C57Bl/6 strain of mice that was used for the experiments is considered resistant to TMEV induced demyelination and is able to clear the virus within a few weeks post-infection (Tsunoda et al., 2016). In our experiments we hypothesized that damage to the nigrostriatal system causing DA neuron loss would occur during the acute phase of infection (1-2 weeks post infection). However, the degeneration of DA neurons persisted into later timepoints, allowing assessment of PD-related motor deficiencies and histological evaluation at chronic timepoints. Consistent with prior studies, the C57Bl/6 mice used in this study cleared the virus within four weeks, suggesting that the motor deficits and histological findings of later time points were caused by lasting damage from the initial viral infection rather than persistent effects of the viral infection.

TMEV infection robustly activates microglia and astrocytes, producing a chronic inflammatory response characterized by cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), and complement activation (Olson et al., 2001; Palma et al., 2003). Activated microglia and macrophages generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), nitric oxide (NO), and pro-inflammatory mediators, which can produce bystander neuronal injury or facilitate immune-mediated attacks on infected neurons. Astrocytes reinforce inflammation through chemokine release and modulation of the blood–brain barrier (BBB). We hypothesize that these glial responses contribute to the observed effects seen in this model and future studies should investigate these glial and immune contributions in detail. These processes mirror several pathways implicated in PD, in which sustained microglial activation and astrocytic dysfunction amplify degeneration of substantia nigra dopaminergic neurons (Hirsch and Hunot, 2009; Lee et al., 2009; Tansey and Goldberg, 2010).

Limitations and future directions

The results of this study show that TMEV infection of the SNc is a promising virally induced model of PD in mice and can be a useful tool to explore new mechanisms associated with disease pathogenesis. However, since this is a pilot study, the results are not without limitations and future studies should aim to further investigate additional features of the model. A primary goal in future studies should be to evaluate disease mechanisms specifically associated with neuroinflammation. Since glial responses are closely associated with PD and TMEV induced disease pathogenesis, microglial and astrocytic activity should be examined histologically. Another focus should be to evaluate cytokines associated with PD such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 to investigate systemic immune mediated responses. The current study also shows robust TH loss at acute timepoints and related behavioral deficits persisting into chronic timepoints, but the model would be strengthened by the addition of histological evaluation of TH loss at later timepoints as well. Additional DA neuronal markers such as dopamine transporter (DAT), vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) and paired-like homeodomain transcription factor 3 (PITX3) should also be studied to confirm DA identity and loss. Other mechanistic studies that would strengthen the model would be to include an inactivated virus control group for comparison, to conduct apoptosis assays to evaluate apoptosis, and to measure DA content in the striatum.

The behavioral studies in this investigation show marked persistence of motor deficits in the TMEV infected mice until the end of the experiment. However, while the apomorphine induced rotations test showed clear increases in contralateral rotations at all timepoints, the results of the pole test were not as significant throughout all the timepoints. Since the tested model was a unilateral lesion model, the apomorphine test showed a higher sensitivity based on its mechanism of action compared to the other testing. PD has chronic and bilateral disease progression so in future testing, bilateral infection with TMEV should also be considered to evaluate the behavioral and histological effects of bilateral DA neuron degeneration. The pole test in particular was challenging for mice to perform even with habituation sessions, and we believe that the sensitivity of the pole test assay could be strengthened by modification of the apparatus to a pole with a rough surface allowing for improved testing of the subjects.

Another limitation is that TMEV is a naturally occurring disease in mice, with no known effect on humans. Therefore, the virus will not exactly mimic a viral infection that is found in humans. However, since it is a neurotropic virus, it can be a useful tool to provide insight into virally associated mechanisms, specifically with regard to how neuroinflammation can trigger PD-like disease. After the establishment of a viral model using TMEV, future studies could lead to development of a model using a human relevant virus.

Lastly, the model has also only been tested in C57BL/6 mice which are considered relatively resistant to Theiler's virus induced demyelinating disease due to rapid clearance of virus (Jin et al., 2007). However, they have been shown to develop epilepsy following TMEV infection (Stewart et al., 2010). Previous research has shown that TMEV can produce differing neurological outcomes based on the strain of mouse infected (Brinkmeyer-Langford et al., 2017). Future studies should also consider using additional strains of mice to replicate the model.

Conclusion

Overall, with this study we characterized a viral infection-based model to study the complex nature of PD pathophysiology. Although the proposed model is subject to limitations, the currently available models of PD also reproduce certain physiological and behavioral hallmarks of PD, and the TMEV infection model provides a naturally occurring disease model based on viral infection. In future studies we will perform a direct comparison of our model with a classical model of PD such as the unilateral 6-OHDA lesion model and to assess biomarkers associated with PD. We will also evaluate how the systemic inflammatory response to viral infection influences pathophysiology. We anticipate that our model can serve as a novel tool to unravel the complex etiology of PD.
