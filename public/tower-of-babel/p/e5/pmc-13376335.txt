Spinal Cord Schistosomiasis: A Rare and Severe Presentation of a Common Disease—A Case Report and Review of Literature
Authors: Elham Sany Shemsu, Thomas Asfaw Atnafu, Eskedar Ferdu Azerefegne, Ilili Amin Aliye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSpinal cord schistosomiasis (SCS) is a rare but potentially disabling manifestation of Schistosoma mansoni infection caused by ectopic deposition of parasite eggs within the spinal cord via Batson's vertebral venous plexus, resulting in granulomatous inflammation and neurological injury. Early diagnosis is challenging because the clinical presentation is often nonspecific and mimics other spinal cord disorders, particularly in resource‐limited settings. We report the case of a 12‐year‐old Ethiopian boy who presented with progressive lower back and radicular pain, lower limb paresthesia, and intermittent low‐grade fever without objective motor weakness, sensory deficits, or bladder dysfunction. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated intramedullary T2‐weighted hyperintensity and swelling involving the conus medullaris, while positive schistosomal serology supported the diagnosis. Prompt treatment with praziquantel and corticosteroids resulted in complete clinical recovery without progression to neurological deficits or the need for surgical intervention. We also review the literature on SCS in endemic regions, highlighting the challenges of early diagnosis, current management strategies, and factors influencing neurological outcomes. This case demonstrates that maintaining a high index of suspicion and combining characteristic MRI findings with serological testing can facilitate early diagnosis and timely medical treatment before irreversible neurological damage occurs, leading to excellent clinical outcomes.Keywords: experience from endemic region, neuroschistosomiasis, schistosomiasis diagnosis, schistosomiasis treatment, spinal cord schistosomiasis

Introduction

Spinal cord schistosomiasis (SCS) is an uncommon but severe complication of schistosomiasis, a parasitic disease caused by blood flukes of the genus Schistosoma. Schistosomiasis affects more than 200 million people worldwide, predominantly in sub‐Saharan Africa, with Schistosoma mansoni being the species most associated with SCS [1, 2]. While schistosomiasis primarily involves hepatosplenic and genitourinary systems, CNS involvement is rare, occurring in 1%–4% of symptomatic cases [1]. Among CNS manifestations, the spinal cord is the most frequently affected site, leading to acute or subacute myelopathy. It occurs due to ectopic deposition of the S. mansoni eggs in the spinal cord by traveling through Batson's vertebral venous plexus and causes granulomatous inflammation and spinal cord damage. Notably, the adult worms never invade the CNS and stay in the mesenteric venous plexus [3].

Clinical presentation varies but typically includes lower back pain, lower limb weakness, sensory disturbances, and bladder or bowel dysfunction [4]. In endemic areas like Ethiopia and Egypt, delayed presentation and misdiagnosis as spinal cord tumors are common, often resulting in unnecessary surgical interventions and poor outcomes [5, 6]. Early diagnosis is crucial as timely treatment with praziquantel and corticosteroids significantly improves prognosis [7, 8]. In this report, we describe an early‐diagnosed case of SCS in a 12‐year‐old boy from Ethiopia, highlighting clinical features, diagnostic approach, and successful outcome, followed by a literature review of diagnostic and management challenges.

Case Presentation

A 12‐year‐old male, previously healthy, presented to our hospital with a 1‐month history of progressive and intermittent lower back pain. The pain initially occurred while walking to school, was dull and aching in nature, radiated to the lower extremities, and worsened with running and playing while improving with rest and lying on his side. A week later, he developed tingling sensations in his lower extremities and buttocks but did not experience bowel or bladder dysfunction or lower limb weakness. Two weeks after the back pain, he developed low‐grade intermittent fever.

He initially sought care at a local health center, where he received unspecified intramuscular injections once daily and paracetamol twice daily for 5 days. However, his symptoms persisted and progressively worsened, culminating in excruciating lower back pain. He denied night sweats, cough, or contact with individuals with chronic cough or known pulmonary tuberculosis. There was no history of urinary frequency, urgency, dysuria, or urinary retention. He had no history of heavy weightlifting, falls, back trauma, or strenuous physical activity. He had not undergone any prior surgical procedures.

The patient reported frequent swimming in nearby rivers but did not recall post‐swim itching or fever. His diet consisted of cooked meat (beef, ox, and goat), with no history of consuming raw meat or pork. He resided in a malaria‐endemic region but had no prior history of acute malaria. He had not taken any prescribed or over‐the‐counter medications and had no known drug or food allergies. He had no history of childhood illnesses and had completed age‐appropriate immunizations.

On presentation, his vital signs were within normal limits. His weight was 34 kg, height was 146 cm, mid‐upper arm circumference (MUAC) was 15 cm, and body mass index (BMI) was 16, with a Z‐score between 0 and −2. There was no hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, or lymphadenopathy. Neurological examination revealed intact motor power in all extremities: motor grade (5/5), normal reflexes (2/4), normal tone, and no evidence of muscle atrophy, fasciculations, or sensory deficits.

Investigations and Treatment

Baseline laboratory investigations (Table 1) revealed leukocytosis with eosinophilia and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Figure 1) demonstrated a 1.5 × 4.5 cm T1‐isointense and T2‐hyperintense lesion at the conus medullaris. Post‐contrast imaging revealed intense heterogeneous enhancement (Figure 1C). A short segment of spinal cord abnormality with T2 prolongation at the T10–T11 level was observed, which did not enhance on post‐contrast imaging (Figure 1). Given the patient's endemic exposure, eosinophilia, and MRI findings suggestive of SCS, S. mansoni antibodies were tested and found to be elevated.

The patient was diagnosed with SCS and was started on oral prednisolone (15 mg twice daily, 1 mg/kg/day) for 15 days, followed by a tapering regimen. Additionally, he received oral praziquantel (680 mg twice daily, 40 mg/kg/day) for 5 days.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

Following the initiation of treatment, the patient's back pain, radicular pain, and paresthesia improved significantly. Upon reevaluation 2 months later, they have completely resolved, his eosinophil count had normalized, and repeat imaging demonstrated complete resolution of conus medullaris lesion and cord expansion with subtle residual focal T2 hyperintensity (Figure 2).

Discussion and Literature Review

This case underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis and treatment in SCS. The patient's history of freshwater exposure, presence of eosinophilia, and characteristic MRI findings, together with elevated Schistosoma antibodies, facilitated a noninvasive diagnosis and timely initiation of therapy. The favorable clinical outcome in this case contrasts sharply with many reports from endemic regions where delayed recognition frequently results in irreversible neurological deficits [1, 7, 8].

The pathogenesis of SCS differs fundamentally from direct parasitic invasion of the central nervous system. Adult Schistosoma worms remain confined to the mesenteric venous plexus and do not migrate into the spinal cord. Instead, SCS results from the ectopic embolization of Schistosoma eggs through the valveless Batson's vertebral venous plexus, which provides communication between the pelvic and mesenteric venous systems and the spinal venous circulation. Once deposited within the spinal cord or surrounding tissues, the eggs secrete highly antigenic products that elicit a vigorous host immune response characterized by eosinophilic infiltration, granuloma formation, edema, and inflammatory demyelination. This granulomatous inflammation can produce spinal cord enlargement and compressive effects, resulting in acute or subacute myelopathy manifested by lower back pain, motor weakness, sensory disturbances, and bladder or bowel dysfunction. The conus medullaris and lower thoracic spinal cord are most frequently affected, likely because of their venous drainage pattern. Prompt administration of praziquantel eliminates the adult worms, thereby preventing further egg deposition, while corticosteroids suppress the inflammatory response surrounding existing eggs, reducing edema and limiting irreversible neurological damage [1, 9, 10].

SCS remains a rare yet devastating complication of schistosomiasis. Misdiagnosis as spinal cord tumors is common, leading to unnecessary surgical interventions and delays in appropriate therapy. Maintaining a high index of suspicion for SCS in patients from endemic regions who present with myelopathy and eosinophilia is essential. Early use of MRI and serological testing can enable prompt diagnosis and effective treatment, preventing long‐term disability.

MRI is the imaging modality of choice for SCS, typically showing intramedullary lesions with T2 hyperintensity and post‐contrast enhancement, especially at the conus medullaris or thoracic cord, as observed in our patient. MRI also reveals cord enlargement and heterogeneous contrast enhancement on T1‐weighted sequences. However, the similarity of these findings to neoplasms such as ependymomas or astrocytomas often leads to misdiagnosis. Consequently, invasive procedures such as laminectomy or spinal cord biopsy have been reported in numerous cases, particularly in Ethiopia and Egypt, resulting in treatment delays and poor outcomes [1, 4, 5, 11].

Diagnostic Challenges in Endemic Areas

Diagnosing SCS in endemic regions is complicated by its nonspecific clinical presentation and limited clinical awareness among healthcare providers [1, 3]. Although histopathological demonstration of Schistosoma eggs in spinal cord tissue remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, obtaining tissue requires invasive neurosurgical biopsy, which carries a substantial risk of additional neurological injury and is therefore rarely performed in routine clinical practice. Instead, SCS should be strongly suspected in patients from endemic areas who present with acute or subacute lower back pain, sensory disturbances, weakness, or bladder dysfunction, particularly when associated with eosinophilia and a history of freshwater exposure [1, 3]. Diagnostic confidence can be achieved through a combination of compatible clinical findings, identification of Schistosoma eggs in stool, urine, or rectal biopsy specimens, positive serological tests for Schistosoma antibodies, and characteristic MRI findings consistent with SCS. In endemic settings, this multidisciplinary, noninvasive diagnostic approach often allows timely initiation of therapy without the need for histopathological confirmation [1, 3].

Delayed Presentation and Prognostic Implications

Delayed diagnosis in endemic regions is a major contributor to poor prognosis. Many patients present late, often with irreversible neurological sequelae such as paraplegia, bladder dysfunction, or chronic pain [4, 12]. Misinterpretation of SCS as spinal tumors or other myelopathies prolongs the diagnostic process and worsens outcomes [12]. For instance, a retrospective study in Egypt revealed that nearly 50% of patients experienced residual neurological deficits despite treatment, underscoring the consequences of diagnostic delay [4].

Early Diagnosis and Treatment

Early recognition and prompt treatment markedly improve outcomes in SCS. Standard therapy includes corticosteroids to reduce granulomatous inflammation and praziquantel to eliminate adult worms and prevent further egg deposition. Patients treated early often achieve full neurological recovery and avoid long‐term disability. A sub‐Saharan African cohort demonstrated favorable outcomes in over 80% of patients receiving early therapy, while a Sudanese series of five patients reported significant neurological improvement in all cases following timely intervention [7, 8, 13].

Although WHO recommends a single‐day praziquantel regimen (40–60 mg/kg) for uncomplicated schistosomiasis, no standardized regimen exists for neuroschistosomiasis. Published reviews report treatment courses ranging from 1 to 14 days, with several experts recommending 50–60 mg/kg/day for 3–5 days in SCS. The rationale being CNS involvement by the eggs can suggest a heavy helminth load in the body, requiring prolonged treatment for eradication. Therefore, a 5‐day course was selected in our patient because of central nervous system involvement and the absence of universally accepted treatment recommendations [3, 14].

Although prolonged corticosteroid therapy with gradual tapering over several weeks or months has been advocated in patients with severe neuroschistosomiasis to suppress persistent granulomatous inflammation, the optimal duration of corticosteroid therapy remains undefined. In the present case, the patient presented early in the disease course with severe radicular pain and subjective paresthesia but without objective motor weakness, sensory deficits, sphincter dysfunction, or other signs of established myelopathy. Following prompt treatment with praziquantel and corticosteroids, he experienced rapid clinical improvement. Therefore, corticosteroids were tapered after 15 days to minimize the potential adverse effects of prolonged corticosteroid exposure while maintaining close clinical follow‐up. This management approach was individualized according to the patient's mild neurological involvement and favorable response to therapy [15].

This case report of a 12‐year‐old Ethiopian boy illustrates the positive impact of early suspicion, noninvasive diagnosis, and prompt initiation of medical therapy in SCS. The patient achieved complete recovery without the need for surgical intervention, highlighting the importance of awareness, especially in resource‐limited settings.

Conclusion

SCS is a rare but potentially disabling manifestation of S. mansoni infection caused by ectopic deposition of parasite eggs within the spinal cord, leading to granulomatous inflammation and neurological injury. Because its presentation often mimics other spinal cord disorders, diagnosis is particularly challenging in resource‐limited settings where awareness and access to advanced diagnostic modalities may be limited. This case demonstrates that maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients from endemic regions presenting with acute or subacute lower back pain and radicular symptoms, together with characteristic MRI findings and supportive serological evidence, can facilitate early noninvasive diagnosis and timely initiation of praziquantel and corticosteroid therapy. Early recognition before the development of objective neurological deficits may prevent irreversible disability, avoid unnecessary surgical intervention, and result in excellent clinical outcomes. Greater clinician awareness and improved access to appropriate diagnostic tools are essential to reduce the burden of this preventable neurological complication in endemic regions.
