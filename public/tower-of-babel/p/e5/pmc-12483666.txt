Research note: Individual variability in early-life play and social behaviors of broiler chicks
Authors: Alexandra Jackson, Denise Landers, Joseph Purswell, Bethany Baker-Cook
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlay and social behaviors are important in behavioral development as they support neurodevelopment, social bonding, and skills development. The individual differences in performance of these behaviors in poultry are currently unknown. This study assessed individual variability of play and social behaviors in broiler chicks across the first week of life. Seventy-five chicks (25 birds/pen) were randomly placed in three pens. Continuous focal sampling of nine birds (3 chicks/pen; labeled A-I) was performed from D 1-7. The play behaviors assessed were sparring, frolicking, and worm-running, and allopreening was used to assess social behavior. Play initiation was calculated as the number of times a chick initiated play divided by its total play frequency. The duration Bird A performed sparring was greater than Birds B, E, F, and H. Bird C performed a greater amount of frolicking than Bird E. Birds D and I spent more time performing worm-running than Bird G. For play duration tertiles, top-ranked birds for sparring were A, C, and I while bottom-ranked birds were B, E, and H. For frolicking, Birds C, D, and I were top-ranked and Birds B, E, and H were bottom. For the duration of worm-running, top ranks included Birds B, D, and I and the bottom ranks were Birds A, E, and H. For allopreening, Birds A, F, and H were ranked top and Birds B, E, and G were ranked bottom for duration. Bird C had significantly higher odds to initiate play (Odds = 4.06; P = 0.034), and Bird I tended to initiate play behaviors more often (Odds = 3.25; P = 0.07). The duration of sparring strongly positively correlated with frolicking (r = 0.78; P < 0.01) and worm-running (r = 0.83; P < 0.01) and frolicking strongly positively correlated with worm-running (r = 0.80; P < 0.01). Overall, individual differences in play behaviors performance were found in the first week of life. Chicks engaging in one type of play not only performed more play behaviors overall but also showed greater propensity to initiate play.Keywords: Sparring, Frolicking, Allopreening, Worm-running, Play initiation

Introduction

Play and social behaviors are important components of early behavioral development in poultry. These behaviors support neurodevelopment, facilitate environmental engagement, help form social bonds, and assist in building skills important for stages later in life (Burghardt, 2005; Blake and McCoy, 2015). The early life of poultry is marked by numerous physiological and behavioral changes (Appleby et al., 2004). After broiler chicks are placed on farm, they undergo significant changes as they transition from relying solely on innate behaviors to utilizing more adaptive responses shaped by their experiences (Appleby, 2004). During this period, the expression of behavior becomes more robust in terms of duration and diversity (Jackson, 2025). While there is some understanding of the ontogeny of play and social behaviors in poultry, research on the variation of its evolution in individual birds remains limited (Dawson and Siegel, 1967; Jackson, 2025).

Play is a behavior frequently observed in animals during their juvenile stage (Burghardt, 2005). While the adaptive function of play is not yet fully understood, it is believed to prepare animals for necessary behaviors later in life and to help support the growth of motor, social, and cognitive skills (Burghardt, 2005). Play typically occurs only when there are no perceived threats to life, significant physiological needs, or pain (Burghardt, 2005). Therefore, the presence of play indicates relatively positive animal well-being. Furthermore, since play is a self-rewarding behavior, the ability to perform the behavior can improve the well-being of birds (Burghardt, 2005). Play is typically categorized into three types: social, locomotory, and object (Appleby, 2004; Burghardt, 2005; Jackson, 2025). In young poultry, this includes behaviors such as sparring, frolicking, and worm-running (Dawson and Siegel, 1967). Play sparring in chicks resembles adult fighting with stand-offs and breast-to-breast contact, but occurs with less intensity and lower risk of injury. Frolicking is play behavior defined as a bird running while flapping its wings without any clear purpose or function other than to perform the behavior. Worm-running occurs when a bird holds an object in its beak and runs back and forth between its conspecifics, prompting the other chicks to chase.

Poultry are inherently social animals, with their social behaviors evolving as they mature and flock hierarchies form (Appleby, 2004). During the early stages of development, chicks may engage in social behaviors such as allopreening, which helps them become familiar with their conspecifics and fosters social bonding (Vallortigara and Andrew, 1994; Appleby, 2004). These initial interactions are important for building social skills, learning through social facilitation, and establishing a hierarchy within the group later in life (Appleby, 2004). During the first couple weeks of life, chicks begin to exhibit more independent behaviors, which allows them to explore their environment and develop bonds with their conspecifics (Vallortigara and Andrew, 1994). Over time, social behaviors will become more aggressive as hierarchies begin to form, and birds acquire a more dominant or submissive status (Appleby, 2004).

While there is growing interest in understanding individual differences in animal behavior, there are few studies that have investigated these differences in livestock and poultry compared to wild animals, especially in terms of interpreting and reporting those differences solely at animal level (Brown et al., 2015). Poultry behavior is influenced by multiple factors such as diurnal rhythm, environment, and social interaction (Appleby et al., 2004). As birds mature, their range of behaviors evolve to support their survival and well-being. Consequently, the numerous factors influencing poultry behaviors may result in non-homogeneous behavioral development among individuals (Dingemanse and Wolf, 2013). Another factor contributing to individual differences in poultry behavior is the presence of inherent traits such as temperament, personality, and preferences (Brown et al., 2015; Achterberg et al., 2023). Some birds may exhibit more exploratory, playful, and socially active behaviors, while others may be more reserved, cautious, or avoidant. In addition to these individual traits, the social interactions and relationships between conspecifics can influence individual behavioral patterns (Achterberg et al., 2023).

One area in which individual differences maybe present is in play initiation. Although understudied in farm animals, previous research on wild animals has found differences in individual play initiation, with older individuals and larger being initiators of play more often (Burghardt, 2005). However, this finding may not directly apply to commercial poultry settings, where birds are typically the same age. Individual differences in play behavior have been observed in juvenile animals of some livestock species, such as pigs, but have yet to be examined in young poultry (Brown et al., 2015). The willingness of individuals to initiate play might depend on the specific components that make up the type of play. For example, sparring involves a social component, while object play contains an explorative aspect. Based on personal observation, certain chicks actively initiate play more, while others position themselves to invite or facilitate play. For example, before a play bout of worm-running, multiple birds would gather around the object but typically only one or two would peck at and take it (Jackson, personal observation). Some birds appeared to prefer chasing and participating rather than taking the object themselves and leading. Other hypotheses propose that individual differences in play behavior could be influenced by emotional contagion from conspecifics or by homeostatic processes, where birds adjust their playfulness in an inverse response to the level of play exhibited by nearby conspecifics (Achterberg et al., 2023).

To the author’s knowledge, there is currently little scientific data regarding early play and social behavior or the individual differences in chick performance of these behaviors. Thus, the objective of this explorative study was to assess the individual variability of play and social behaviors among broiler chicks during their first week of life, with a specific focus on the duration of these behaviors and the frequency of play initiation. Additionally, the study aimed to investigate the relationships between the type of play and social behaviors to gain a deeper understanding of respective behavioral patterns in early chick development.

Materials and methods

Birds and housing

A total of seventy-five Ross 308 mixed-sex chicks were obtained from a commercial hatchery. Chicks were randomly distributed between three pens (25 birds per pen), at a stocking density of 1ft2/bird (0.09m2/bird) with a potential estimated final stocking density of 32kg/m2 (if birds had been grown out to 42 days). Each pen contained fresh pine shaving bedding. Throughout the trial, chicks were provided feed and water ad libitum. Chicks were provided with a standard commercial starter diet formulated based on breeder nutrition specifications for broilers which was delivered via a hanging pan feeder and a supplemental feeder. Chicks were provided water via nipple drinkers and a supplemental drinker. At day 3, the supplemental drinker was removed. During the 7-day long study, the photoperiod started at 23L:1D for day 0-2, then was gradually decreased 1 hour per day, until 20L:4D from day 5 onwards. The light intensity was 40 lux until day 3, then lowered to 30 lux on day 4, and 10 lux from day 5 to 7. At placement, temperature was set to 30°C then gradually decreased to 27°C, with relative humidity at 60-70 %. Human contact with the chicks was restricted to brief daily checks to avoid any significant impact to chick behavior. The study was conducted with the approval of Auburn University's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Data collection

Behavioral observations were performed through continuous focal sampling of nine focal chicks (three birds/pen) from day 1 to 7, with day 1 considered as the day after placement. Chicks were randomly selected and marked with a distinct color and shape to facilitate their identification. Video recording was done via an infrared camera and NVR system (Lorex Technology Inc, Markham, Canada) installed above each pen facilitating view of all birds. Behaviors were manually coded in the Observer XT v17 software (Noldus Information Technology, Wageningen, the Netherlands). The behaviors recorded in this study included and were defined as the following (Jackson, 2025): Sparring - simulated fighting between conspecifics with stand-offs, breast to breast contact, and wing-flapping without significant aggression; Frolicking -spontaneous, rapid movements by chick involving running, jumping, and wing-flapping, without a clear purpose; Worm-running - chick carrying an object in their beak while running back and forth across the pen while being chased by conspecifics; Allopreening - chick preening a conspecific. The video recordings of chick behavior were coded between two observers, with an interobserver reliability (Cohen’s Kappa score) of >90 %. Behaviors were analyzed for duration and initiation frequency. Themeasurement types utlized have been previously employed in other play and social variation studies, such as Thompson (1996). Behavior total duration was measured as the total average time individual broiler chicks spent performing bouts of behaviors from days 1 to 7 of age. The relative frequency of play initiation was calculated by dividing the number of times each chick initiated a play bout by their total play frequency.

Statistical analysis

The distribution of duration data was tested for normality and (log + 1) transformed when needed. A one-way ANOVA was conducted using the GLIMMIX procedure with Gaussian distribution to evaluate the impact of individual bird and pen on behavior duration, with means compared using the Tukey-Kramer test. Tertile ranks were assigned to chicks based on the duration of play behavior, with a separate rank for each behavior, to help identify individual patterns and trends in behavioral performance (Top Tertile = >66 %, Mid Tertile = 33–66 %, Bottom Tertile = <33 %). To assess individual differences of bird play initiation, the data was transformed into binary values, assigning “1″ to relative frequencies above the median and “0″ to those below the median. This transformed data was then analyzed using logistic regression with a logit function, with the bird exhibiting the closest overall average of play initiation designated as the reference. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was performed via Proc Corr to examine potential relationships between the durations of behaviors. All statistical analysis was carried out using SAS 9.4 (2013) and significance for the tests was established at a threshold of P ≤ 0.05.

Results and discussion

The average duration of play behavior performance differed between all individuals, but no difference was found for allopreening (Table 1). Bird A spent more time sparring, than Birds B, E, F, and H (P < 0.01). Whilst, Birds D and I spent more time engaged in worm-running than Bird G (P < 0.01). In duration of time spent engaged in frolicking, Bird C performed the play behavior at a greater amount than Bird E (P = 0.01). Pen was found to have no effect on the duration of the assessed behaviors. These results show that within the first week of life individual differences can be found in the length of time chicks spend performing play behaviors. Although only minimal differences in duration were found in our analysis, additional differences may have been obscured by day-to-day intra-variation among the birds, particularly as they were developing their behaviors in response to the environment, locomotive ability, and their conspecifics which occurs during this early stage of life (Appleby et al., 2004). A study conducted over a longer period with a larger sample size may be able to better detect bird individual differences. This could be particularly important for detecting variations in social behaviors, which were not observed in our study but are known to emerge in birds as part of the natural social hierarchy that develops in poultry during later stages of life (Appleby, 2004).

For play duration tertile ranks (Table 1), birds in the top rank for sparring were A, C, and I, in the mid rank were D, F, and G, and in the bottom rank were B, E, and H. For frolicking duration, birds in the top rank included C, D, and I, in the mid rank were A, F, and G, and in the bottom rank were B, E, and H. For the duration of worm-running, birds in the top rank were B, D, and I, in the mid rank were C, F, and G, and in the bottom rank were A, E, and H. Finally, for allopreening duration, birds in the top rank included A, F, and H, in the mid rank were C, D, and I, and in the bottom rank were B, E, and G. These rankings show that Bird I was consistently included among the top ranked birds for performance duration in all play behaviors, whilst Bird E was in the bottom rank for all play behaviors. Interestingly, Bird E and Bird B, who was in bottom for sparring and frolicking, also were in the bottom rank for allopreening. The only other birds that ranked in the top tertile for multiple play behaviors were Bird C, which exhibited top rankings in both frolicking and sparring, and Bird D, which ranked top in frolicking and worm-running. Some chicks consistently ranked top and mid more in their duration of play than others. In contrast, certain birds have a stronger preference for one type of play, demonstrating greater motivation to perform that behavior. As seen with Bird A, who has the highest duration for sparing, but ranks mid and low for the other play behaviors. This could be indicative that Bird A, is only highly motivated to perform sparring behavior. Although the authors acknowledge that the rank results are limited in their significance alone, they present potential patterns that serve to support and improve the interpretation of the other statistical analyses conducted in this study.

For the differences in play initiation, Bird F exhibited the closest average relative frequency of initiating play behavior among the group of observed chicks and was designated as the reference bird for comparison (Table 2). The only bird that showed significantly higher odds of initiating play behaviors at a greater rate than the average was Bird C (Odds = 4.06; P = 0.034). Bird I tended to initiate play behaviors more often than average (Odds = 3.25; P = 0.07). Both bird A and D show increased odds of initiating play but did not reach statistical significance at the 0.05 level (Odds = 2.64, 2.64; P = 0.13, 0.13, respectively). Furthermore, while not statistically significant, Bird E demonstrated the lowest odds of initiating play, with nearly half the odds compared to average (Odds = 0.51; P = 0.32). By comparing these results to the duration and ranks of play behaviors, we can observe that birds with relatively greater play durations and higher consistent ranks were also potentially more likely to initiate play. In contrast, birds with lower play durations and ranks showed a reduced likelihood of initiating play. This implies that some individuals may have a stronger inclination towards play than others, leading them to initiate play more frequently. The birds that exhibited average initiation rates and durations may be less motivated to initiate play, but were more likely to join in or perform the behavior when observing other chicks engaging in play. Although it was not measured in this study, underlying social dynamics may result in certain birds being selected as playmates by their conspecifics more frequently. Preference for specific partners during play has been observed in mammals (Thompson, 1996). Preference for specific play patterns warrants further study in poultry, as research has found that chicks recognize and show preference for conspecifics at an early age (Vallortigara and Andrew, 1994; Porter et al., 2006). Play behavior and its performance in social contexts are complex, and more research is needed on how bird personality and social dynamics may impact its expression.

The time birds spent sparring showed a strong positive correlation with both frolicking (r = 0.78; P < 0.01) and worm-running (r = 0.83; P < 0.01). Additionally, the duration individual chicks spent frolicking was strongly positively correlated with worm-running (r = 0.80; P < 0.01). The duration of allopreening from days 1 to 7 did not present a significant correlation with the duration of sparring (r = −0.14; P = 0.29), frolicking (r = −0.08; P = 0.51), worm-running (r = −0.17; P = 0.18). One notable pattern in the duration correlation results was that all play behaviors showed a strong positive correlation with each other, suggesting that chicks displaying higher levels of one type of play behavior were likely to engage relatively more in play overall. This observation is supported by both the duration analysis and assigned ranks, which showed that birds with relatively high levels of one play behavior tended to maintain this pattern across other types of play.

While variations in play initiation and duration may be attributed to individual preference, other aspects of personality may also have an impact. This includes bird temperament and adaptability. While some birds may be more explorative and socially engaging, others might be more cautious or avoidant. It is also important to acknowledge that differences in social dynamics, if present, could have also influenced these behaviors in ways that are not possible to be fully accounted for in this study. To assess whether personality, social dynamics, or other factors contributed to the individual differences in play behavior, assessments would need to be conducted over the lifespan to determine if the pattern remains consistent. Previous studies assessing play behavior have indicated that play performance tends to increase during early-life, followed by a gradual decline as birds mature (Dawson and Siegel, 1967; Jackson, 2025). Although these findings provide preliminary insights, further research building upon the patterns reported herein may contribute to enhancing the well-being of birds. By learning the normal individual variation and creating conditions conducive to play, we can help facilitate the performance of this self-rewarding behavior (Burghardt, 2005). The lack of individual differences or significant correlations observed in allopreening may be attributed to the age of the birds, as their conspecific familiarization and social bonds are still developing and vary day-to-day, increasing the difficulty to identify patterns (Vallortigara and Andrew, 1994). In this study, allopreening was utilized as a proxy to measure individual variation in social behavior in young poultry. This approach was selected as physical interaction via preening or pecking between conspecifics is prevalent in poultry social behavior research and aligned with the design of this observational study (Porter et al., 2006). To better recognize individual differences or behavioral relationships, it may be necessary to analyze additional behaviors with social components over a longer period of time. Furthermore, while this study examined individual differences among chicks raised in a mixed-sex group, more insight could be gained by analyzing these behaviors within a straight-run separate placement of sexes.

In conclusion, the results of this study suggest that individual differences may be present in the expression of play behaviors during the early stages of life in birds. Specifically, chicks who engaged more in one type of play behavior tended to engage more overall across all play behaviors. Additionally, chicks that spent more time performing play behaviors were also more likely to initiate them. While the authors acknowledge the limitations of this study’s restricted sample size, the patterns observed offer preliminary support for individual differences in early play behavior. These findings can guide future investigations aimed at improving our understanding of the ontogeny of broiler behavior, its long-term impact, and bird behavioral needs, ultimately contributing to greater poultry well-being. Further research is needed to investigate how underlying factors, such as development, social dynamics, initial habituation to the environment after placement, the role of age, sex, and personality, might contribute to individual difference in play behavior and initiation.
