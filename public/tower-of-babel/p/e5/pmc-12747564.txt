Psychiatric Risk Factors for Progression From Mild Cognitive Impairment to Alzheimer’s Disease: A Systematic Review
Authors: Melissa A. Meynadasy, Natalie Sachs-Ericsson, Sarah D. Cushing, Julia L. Sheffler, Mohamed Kabbaj, Aaron Wilber
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), the intermediate stage between healthy aging and dementia, is a window for identification of risk factors for progression to Alzheimer’s Disease (AD). Rodents modeling aspects of AD-related pathophysiology are useful for examining mechanisms underlying AD risk factors. We provide a systematic update of human literature on psychiatric risk factors for progression from MCI to AD and use these findings to motivate a targeted review of related rodent literature. We searched databases to identify human studies published since a previous systematic review. We included articles if longitudinal, assessed MCI at baseline and AD at follow-up, and reported on risk factors for progression of MCI to AD. We categorized articles by risk factor type and included those examining psychiatric factors. Results were synthesized based on psychopathology examined and methods used. Relevant rodent literature was reviewed and incorporated. We found seventeen papers examining psychiatric risk factors for MCI progression to AD; we found cross species support for the role of depression as an important risk factor. We discuss hypotheses to explain the role of depression and suggest investigating transdiagnostic factors related to depression and AD (e.g., sleep, stress) that lend themselves to investigation across species.

In the United States, 10.9% of people aged 65+ have Alzheimer’s Disease (AD), the most common form of dementia (i.e., significant decline in cognitive abilities, impacting daily functioning),1,2 AD pathology (amyloid β (Aβ) aggregation, & phosphorylated tau accumulation)3 appears early, before cognitive symptoms emerge.4 Thus, preventative measures are needed for slowing or stopping disease progression. Psychiatric problems (i.e., mental health disorders affecting an individual’s emotional and functional wellbeing) are modifiable factors that could provide a pathway for altering disease progression and improving quality of life. A substantial body of research has used animal models to understand AD including psychiatrically related risk factors (stress, coping behavior, etc.) that may enhance the rate of pathology accumulation or other aspects of disease progression. However, comprehensive synthesis of the human and animal literature on this topic is needed. This systematic review will focus on psychiatric factors and their relationship to AD progression with the goal of revealing areas that could benefit from further attention.

MILD COGNITIVE IMPAIRMENT AND WHY IT IS IMPORTANT

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) represents an intermediate stage between healthy aging and dementia.5 AD prevalence increases with age.1 Across studies, among individuals aged 65+, prevalence of MCI ranges from 15% to 25%.6 Those with MCI have higher dementia risk than the general population.7 Annual MCI to AD conversion rates range from 6.8% in community to 8.1% in clinical studies.8 Individuals diagnosed with amnestic MCI (aMCI; cognitive decline appearing in memory domain) have higher conversion rates to AD (i.e., 19.1% in community & 37.2% in clinic samples) compared to nonamnestic MCI (naMCI; cognitive decline appearing in nonmemory domains; ~11% in community/clinic samples).9 Thus, type of MCI is important. MCI always precedes AD, but not all individuals with MCI will progress to AD.10 MCI is a critical point for identifying risk factors for the progression to AD11 and furthering knowledge in the development of interventions to slow/prevent progression. Once risk factors are identified in humans, nonhuman animal studies can provide mechanisms and guide treatments that are likely to produce the greatest benefit in humans.

MODELING RISK FACTORS FOR AD IN RODENTS

Rodent model approaches have furthered our understanding of AD by modeling aspects of human AD. Model types include those manipulating Aβ, tau, or both and use a variety of methods (transgenic, knock-in, knock-out).12 These approaches have increased our understanding of the impact of tau and Aβ aggregation on a variety of biological processes and helped develop approaches for removing Aβ and tau. However, while the reason is unclear, rodent studies have been less successful as preclinical models for development of pharmaceuticals that successfully target cognition in humans.13

Rodent models have also been useful for investigating mechanisms underlying risk factors for AD. Research has examined the intersection between metabolic risk factors such as type II diabetes, and the impact of stress on the development of AD.14,15 Studies in these areas examine the risk factor in question by introducing it to a rodent modeling aspects of AD and assessing the impact on pathology, cognition and other measures with versus without the risk factor. This approach may be useful for investigating the mechanistic relationship between psychiatric risk factors and AD.16,17

While we cannot directly determine whether an animal is experiencing a psychiatric problem such as depression or anxiety, various behavioral protocols have been developed to assess specific aspects associated with these conditions. For example, researchers commonly employ standardized tests to evaluate behaviors in rodents often described as “anxiety-like” or “depression-like.” Depression-like behaviors are commonly evaluated using the forced swim test (FST), tail suspension test (TST), and sucrose preference test, which are intended to capture constructs such as behavioral despair and anhedonia. Anxiety-related behaviors are typically assessed using the elevated plus maze, open field test, light-dark box, and novelty-suppressed feeding test, which measure avoidance of open or brightly lit areas and the reluctance to feed in a stressful environment. However, caution must be taken in generalizing from clinical terms like “anxiety” and “depression” in the context of rodent behavior.. These terms stem from humans’ subjective experiences (e.g., persistent sadness, excessive worry) which cannot be directly measured in rodents. Applying such labels risks anthropomorphizing observed behaviors and oversimplifying the complex biological and psychological processes involved. A more scientifically rigorous approach involves focusing on behavioral and psychological constructs that are measurable across species (see18,19 for further discussion as it relates to rodent models of AD). Examples include motivation, coping strategies, risk-taking behaviors, cognitive deficits, stress responses, anhedonia, and sleep patterns.20 Indeed, researchers have begun to examine these behaviors in rodents modeling aspects of AD pathophysiology to model neuropsychiatric symptoms commonly seen in AD.21

PSYCHIATRIC PROBLEMS AND RISK FOR PROGRESSION TO AD

Previous human research has demonstrated a link between psychiatric problems (e.g., depression) and risk of progression from MCI to dementia.22,23,10,24,25 However, due to the nature of this work, these studies were not able to examine the specific mechanisms driving progression to AD. For example, epidemiological studies have only recently begun to include blood biomarkers to inform the assessment of neurobiological mechanisms. Li et al.26 conducted a broad systematic review and meta-analysis assessing risk factors for progression from MCI to AD. They found current or recent depressive symptoms at baseline, but not symptoms of apathy or anxiety, were associated with high risk of progression to AD. Two recent reviews have demonstrated a relationship between apathy25 and anxiety22 and progression from MCI to dementia. However, it should be noted that van Dalen et al.25 assessed the relationship between apathy and progression from MCI to dementia, rather than AD specifically. Further, although Li & Li22 found anxiety to be a risk factor for progression from MCI to dementia, this result was not sustained when examining risk for progression to AD, specifically. The current review aims to provide a cross-species perspective on psychiatric factors associated with risk of progression from MCI to AD and provides a systematic and comprehensive update of the human literature on this topic.

SYSTEMATIC SEARCH METHOD

We conducted a systematic search to identify human studies published since 2016 examining risk factors for progression of MCI to dementia due to AD. All human research in this review utilized the following MCI criteria: 1) complaint about cognition from the patient/informant, 2) impairment in 1 or more cognitive domains, 3) intact activities of daily living (ADLs) and potential mild impairment in instrumental ADLs but maintained independence, 4) not demented.27,2,28. To initially capture the broadest possible search, we modeled search terms after the broad systematic review completed by Li et al.26. Search terms included “mild cognitive impairment,” “Alzheimer’s Disease,” and “risk factor,” or some variation based on the database (see Table 1). We limited to 2016 and after because of the broad systematic review that covers the literature prior to 2016.26 The following databases were searched in March 2021, January 2023, and January 2025: Pubmed, APA PsychInfo, Embase, and EBSCO with time restrictions of 2016–2021, 2021–2023, and 2023–2025, respectively. Using the same criteria, Google Scholar was searched with the additional restriction to articles that cited the 2016 review.26 Articles were included if longitudinal (i.e., to appropriately assess risk of progression), assessed MCI at baseline using the criteria outlined above and AD-type dementia at follow-up, and reported on risk factors for progression of MCI to AD. Reviews, meta-analyses, commentaries, letters to editors, conference abstracts, case studies, theses/dissertations, study design papers, animal studies (none assessed psychiatric risk factors for progression from MCI to AD), and intervention studies were excluded. Articles were initially screened by MM, DC, or JS by title and subsequently screened by MM by abstract and then categorized by risk factor type. Only psychiatric risk factor studies were included in the final sample (Figs. 1, 2, and 3). Included articles were independently reviewed by the first author and relevant data (e.g., sample characteristics, measures used, psychopathology variables, main findings) were extracted by reading the article. Results were synthesized based on main findings, psychopathology variables, and methods used. Themes were identified initially by consensus among the reviewers and eventually by consensus among all authors of the manuscript. See Table 2 for a summary of the resultant articles (n = 17).

SYSTEMATIC SEARCH RESULTS

Depressive Symptoms at Baseline and Conversion From MCI to AD

Consistent with previous reports, greater depressive symptoms at baseline were associated with higher rates of conversion from MCI to AD. Defrancesco et al.31 and Kida et al.36 utilized the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS)45,46 to assess whether baseline depressive symptoms predicted conversion to AD from samples of 260 geriatric patients with MCI from a memory clinic and 276 Japanese older adults with MCI from the Tone Project47 respectively. Defrancesco et al.31 found higher baseline depressive symptoms predicted conversion to AD in both multiple logistic regression (i.e., controlling for age, gender, neuropsychological performance, medication, APOE status, health factors) and survival analysis. Kida et al.36 found participants with MCI who had GDS scores of 5 or higher (i.e., cut point between no depression and suggestion of depression45) had ~2x the likelihood of conversion to AD than nondepressed (i.e., GDS < 5) participants with MCI. This association was only significant for those with aMCI and not naMCI. The authors suggest this finding may indicate higher probability of hippocampal degeneration in comorbid aMCI and depression.36 Similarly, Han et al.34 found GDS score to be a significant predictor of conversion from MCI to moderate and severe cognitive impairment (MSCI; an intermediate stage between MCI and AD), and conversion from MSCI to AD. Further, using a Bayesian networks model, they found depression (as measured by GDS) to have a direct effect on cognitive status.

Using the National Alzheimer’s Coordinating Center (NACC)’s Uniform Data Set (UDS), Gallagher et al. identified older adults (N = 1,956) with past/present depression and MCI at baseline.33 Depression in the last 2 years was independently associated with increased risk of conversion to AD compared to remote depression (prior to past 2 years; 10% higher risk). This supports the hypothesis that depression could be a prodrome of dementia,48 and suggests that treatment of depression may decrease risk of developing AD.

History of Depression, Genetic Risk for Depression, and Conversion From MCI to AD

Consistent with the above evidence supporting the role of ongoing depressive symptoms as a risk factor for progression from MCI to AD, earlier studies found history of depression increases risk, particularly if recurrent.49,50 Ownby et al.50 conducted a meta-analysis which revealed history of depression was related to greater likelihood of an AD diagnosis later in life. They found a significant and positive relationship between longer length of time between diagnoses of depression and AD and risk for developing AD, suggesting depression may be a true risk factor for AD rather than a prodrome. In contrast, a recent review and meta-analysis found associations between depression and dementia (type not specified) were stronger among studies with shorter follow-up periods.51 Relatedly, Bartels et al.29 found no effect of past depression on risk of conversion in a sample of 755 currently nondepressed individuals with MCI from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort. Taken together, these findings suggest past depression may be a general risk factor for developing dementia and AD, but whether someone has a history of depression may not affect risk for conversion from MCI to AD.

While past depression may not play a role in conversion from MCI to AD, genetic risk for depression might. Xu et al.42 investigated the predictive value of polygenic risk scores of major depressive disorder (MDD)-specific genetic variants (PRSsMDD) on conversion from aMCI to AD in a sample of nondepressed older adults from the first stage of the ADNI cohort. They found PRSsMDD significantly predicted conversion to AD, indicating MDD-specific genetic variance contributes independently from AD-related genetic variance to conversion from aMCI to AD. Relatedly, Li et al.52 examined whether genetic risk for neuroticism (N-PRS), a risk factor and endophenotype for depression, predicts conversion from aMCI to AD using the first stage of the ADNI cohort. They found N-PRS predicted conversion from aMCI to AD over 1-year follow up, but not 10-year follow up while controlling for PRSs for depression, global cognition, and anxiety, all of which did not significantly predict progression from aMCI to AD over 1-year period. Findings were replicated in a second dataset (UK Biobank). Alternatively, another study examining the effect of PRS for depression on progression from MCI to AD in a sample of older adults from the ADNI cohort38 found no association between PRS for depression and conversion from MCI to AD within 3 or 5 years. Discrepancies in findings may be related to methodological differences such as follow-up times or discovery samples for developing PRSs.

SSRIs May Protect Against Conversion From MCI to AD

Antidepressants may decrease conversion from MCI to AD. Whereas depressive symptoms at baseline were associated with increased likelihood of conversion from MCI to AD, Defrancesco et al.31 found use of antidepressants was associated with significantly reduced conversion to AD. Gallagher et al.,33 however did not find a protective effect of antidepressants on conversion from MCI to AD. This discrepancy may be explained by missing information regarding the type of antidepressants participants were treated with. For example, Bartels et al.29 assessed the impact of Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) on progression to AD in a sample of 223 currently nondepressed older adults with MCI and history of depression from the ADNI cohort. They found lower progression for those treated with SSRIs compared to other antidepressants. Treatment with SSRIs for greater than 4 years was associated with delayed progression to AD by 3 years compared to those treated with SSRIs for 4õr fewer years, those treated with other antidepressants, and those not treated. This suggests SSRIs may be effective at preventing progression to AD; however, since SSRIs are a first line treatment, it is possible those treated with antidepressants other than SSRIs had treatment resistant depression or other comorbidities relevant to risk of progression. Epidemiological research often does not afford the opportunity to assess the potential effects of dosage, treatment response, or adherence, factors which may aid in the interpretation of these findings. Findings are consistent with a recent review53 suggesting benefits of SSRIs on pathophysiological biomarkers of AD (amyloid burden, tau deposits, neurogenesis) in animal models, and protective effects of most SSRIs against onset of AD in human participants with history of depression. Further investigation is warranted as protection from conversion to AD by some but not other antidepressants is critical information.

In contrast, another recent systematic review and meta-analysis54 found antidepressant usage was associated with higher risk for dementia and MCI in depressed individuals. However, Chan et al.54 assessed risk for MCI and dementia in general and not specifically conversion from MCI to AD. Whereas they reported higher risk of dementia in those taking antidepressants without a diagnosis of depression this finding did not reach statistical significance despite a very large sample size (446,267 total participants; 25,785 diagnosed with dementia), calling into question whether the risk associated with antidepressants was due to antidepressants or underlying depression/psychopathology.

Neuropsychiatric Symptoms in MCI as Risk Factors for Progression to AD

Several studies have used the Neuropsychiatric Inventory Questionnaire (NPI-Q),55 a measure developed for assessing neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) in dementia, to assess the impact of NPS on risk for conversion from MCI to AD. NPS refer to psychological (e.g., apathy) and behavioral symptoms (e.g., disinhibition) that commonly develop across the AD spectrum.56,10 These symptoms can develop in individuals with or without prior psychiatric diagnoses.

Several studies found affective symptoms in older adults with MCI were related to higher risk for conversion to AD,35,40,41,43,37 Sacuiu et al.41 investigated AD incidence in a sample of 94 older adults from the ADNI cohort with MCI and chronic depressive symptoms versus those without depressive symptoms. They defined chronic depressive symptoms as at least 1 of 3 depression-related items on the NPI-Q (depression/dysphoria, apathy/indifference, appetite/eating disturbances) endorsed at each of the available assessments. Conversion to AD was more common in the chronic depressive symptom group compared to the no depressive symptoms group (62.1% vs. 33.3%). Chronic depressive symptoms were associated with 60% shorter conversion times to AD compared to those without depressive symptoms. This relationship was independent of cortical atrophy rates in temporal regions, but not frontal regions, perhaps indicating co-occurring frontosubcortical pathology.

Whereas Sacuiu et al.41 combined symptoms of depression/dysphoria, apathy/indifference, and appetite/eating disturbances to measure depressive symptoms, other studies examined these symptoms (and others) separately from 1 another. Jang et al.35,* found symptoms of depression, anxiety, irritability, agitation, and changes in appetite predicted progression to AD in a sample of 617 older adults with MCI from the ADNI cohort. Similarly, Liampas et al.37 found symptoms of anxiety, apathy, agitation, and irritability in addition to disinhibition, motor disturbance, appetite disturbance, and hallucinations were significantly related to progression to AD in a sample of older adults with MCI from the NACC UDS. Ruthirakuhan et al.40 found apathy alone significantly predicted risk of conversion from MCI to AD compared to no NPS in a sample of 4,932 older adults with MCI at baseline from the NACC UDS. Depression alone was not a significant predictor compared to no NPS; however, older adults with both apathy and depression had the greatest risk for conversion to AD. Given reduced volume of the putamen found in AD,57 these findings are consistent with work demonstrating smaller putamen volumes in older adults with melancholic (i.e., characterized by anhedonia) compared to nonmelancholic depression.58

They are also consistent with conclusions made by van Dalen et al.25 that apathy deserves more attention as a relevant marker of increased risk for AD. Consistent with this view, Premnath et al.59 found that tau burden in parietal and temporal brain regions affected in AD (supramarginal gyrus, entorhinal cortex) were associated with increased apathy over time in older adults with MCI and AD. In contrast, Yang et al.43 found only depression was predictive of conversion to AD in a community sample of 465 Chinese older adults with MCI.

Two studies assessing neuropsychiatric symptoms with the NPI-Q did not find affective symptoms to predict conversion from MCI to AD. Yokoi et al.44 found only symptoms of abnormal perception and thought content (i.e., delusions & hallucinations) were significantly higher in converters from MCI to AD compared to nonconverters in a sample of 234 patients with MCI from the Japanese version of the ADNI cohort. There were no significant differences in affective symptoms between converters and nonconverters. It should be noted, however, that exclusionary criteria for this study included presence of a mood disorder episode within the past year. Similarly, Dietlin et al.32 found agitation/aggression, delusions, and aberrant motor behaviors increased risk of progression to AD during a 4 year follow up period in a sample of 96 older adults with MCI from the Research Of BiomarkerS in Alzheimer’s diseaSe study, but no significant risk for symptoms of depression, anxiety, or apathy. Thus, not all studies have found affective symptoms are significant risk factors and more work is needed to examine psychotic symptoms as risk factors for this progression.

Finally, 2 studies used different approaches by performing latent class modeling to identify groups of older adults with MCI exhibiting constellations of neuropsychiatric symptoms (i.e., irritability, apathy, anxiety/depression, asymptomatic)39 or neuropsychiatric symptom trajectories (i.e., stable, worsened, improved).30 David et al.30 found the worsened class were significantly more likely to be diagnosed with AD than both the improved class and the stable class, and no difference between the improved and stable classes. Roberto et al.39 found the apathy and anxiety/depression classes were more likely to convert to AD compared to other dementias but had lower conversion rates compared to those in the asymptomatic class, which is inconsistent with many of the findings described above. Another study using a similar latent class modeling method identified 3 neuropsychiatric symptom classes. (severe, affective, asymptomatic,60 in older adults with MCI from the NACC UDS. Similar to David et al.,30 Forrester et al.60 found severe and affective symptom classes to have significantly higher risk for conversion from MCI to dementia compared to the asymptomatic class (severe class demonstrating the highest risk). Although this study examined progression from MCI to dementia broadly, authors note that conversion rates were consistent for AD, specifically.

Taken altogether, and consistent with Li et al.,26 findings that suggest depressive symptoms are an important predictor of conversion from MCI to AD and should be considered when assessing for early-stage or prodromal AD, although there are some contradictory findings.26 Unlike the previous systematic review,26 the current review revealed some evidence for anxiety and psychotic symptoms as potential risk factors for conversion from MCI to AD. However, more research is needed to determine the importance of anxiety and psychotic symptoms in prediction of conversion from MCI to AD.

STUDYING PSYCHIATRIC FACTORS RELATED TO RISK FOR AD USING ANIMAL MODELS

Three recent reviews examined evidence from animal literature which informs the relationship between psychiatric symptoms and AD pathology. Based on prior literature, Nyarko et al.16 hypothesize that depression-related neurochemical changes are associated with abnormal APP processing that could contribute to changes at the cellular level, ultimately impairing cognition. The review by Nyarko et al.16 found that multiple mouse models of AD (3xTg-AD, TgCRND8) exhibit impaired coping strategies - a feature of depression in humans – in animal tests of despair (e.g., TST, FST)†, which are thought to model learned helplessness.61 Nyarko et al.16 also present evidence for a shared role of the monoaminergic system in both learned helplessness and AD. For example, both 3xTg-AD (vs. wild-type littermates) and TgCRND8 (vs. non-Tg littermates) mouse models display decreased noradrenaline and/or serotonin functioning related to behavioral despair noted above. Relatedly, Kosel et al.21 find that depressive-like behaviors (impaired coping strategies in tests of despair) in transgenic mice are related to severity of AD-related pathology. Finally, very recent work suggests that APP/PS1 mice benefit from targeting of both seretonin and NMDA receptors suggesting combinatorial targeting of mood and cognition provides greater benefit to cognition62. Thus, in humans, the association between depression and risk for converting from MCI to AD may be related to overlapping underlying pathology‡ and further supports potential protective effects of SSRIs against conversion from MCI to AD given disrupted noradrenaline and/or serotonin functioning.

Pentowski et al.17 examined the current literature regarding animal models of risk-taking behaviors (Pentowski refers to it as anxiety–like behaviors), assessed in the elevated plus maze, open field and light dark boxes and concluded that the work completed thus far has produced inconsistent results due to use of different models, testing models at different disease stages, and using different behavioral tests across studies, findings consistent with an additional review by Kosel et al.21. Our systematic search did not reveal any articles since 2016 examining anxiety as a risk factor for conversion from MCI to AD in humans aside from those examining NPS more broadly.32,35,43,44,37 Of those, only Jang et al.35 and Liampas37 found the anxiety symptom from the NPI-Q significantly predicted conversion from MCI to AD. Based on evidence that AD pathology disrupts hippocampal theta/gamma oscillations in transgenic rat and mouse models, Pentowski et al.17 suggest investigation of the relationship between oscillatory dysfunction and risk taking behaviors in AD and their dynamic interactions across hippocampal circuits in the ventral pole. Like the relationship between depressive symptoms and AD, use of animal models to examine mechanistic relationships between risk taking and fear responses (e.g., time spent in closed arms of the elevated plus maze, freezing behavior) and AD at the cellular, molecular, and systems levels could ultimately reveal a role for anxiety as a risk factor for progression to AD in humans. Indeed, Li et al.63 present evidence for a link between pathological hallmarks of AD and trait anxiety. Targeting behaviors or features that are common across psychiatric problems may also allow for examination across species. Both Nyarko et al.16 and Pentowski et al.18 discuss the roles of sleep and stress in AD from an animal perspective. In humans sleep disruption is a core feature of depression and anxiety as well as AD.64,65,66 Similarly, several mouse models of AD (e. g., 3xTg-AD, APP23, APPswe/PS1δE9, PDAPP, 5xFAD, Tg2576, APPNL-DG-F) exhibit changes in sleep architecture compared to wild type controls.67,68,69,70,71,72 For example, the TgCRND8 mouse model of AD has been shown to spend less time in both rapid eye movement (REM) and non-REM sleep compared to their wild-type litter mates, a sleep pattern that is partially rescued by a selective alpha1-noradrenergic receptor antagonist, further supporting the role of the monoaminergic system in progression of AD.73

Similarly, stress has been delineated as an important factor in both psychopathology and AD pathology.74,75,76 Solas et al.77 examined the effects of a rat model of early life stress (maternal separation for 3h/day during the first 3 postnatal weeks) on behavior and AD-related pathology. They found longer immobility during the FST and poorer performance on multiple cognitive tasks (Morris water maze, object recognition test) in rats that experienced maternal separation compared to controls. These differences were present at 3 and 18 months. At 18 months, maternally separated rats had increased hippocampal Aβ. Baglietto-Vargas et al.14 modeled the effects of “modern life-like” stress (5h multimodal stress) in the 3xTg-AD mouse to examine the effect on pathogenesis and synaptic plasticity. They found stress reduced the number of synapse-bearing dendritic spines and increased Aβ levels in these mice. This work demonstrates how modeling a transdiagnostic factor like stress could inform the relationship between psychiatric risk factors and pathogenesis related to AD.

DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The current review reveals a need for work examining psychiatric problems other than depression as risk factors for progression from MCI to AD and consistent with the 2016 systematic review,26 provides further support for the role of depression as an important risk factor for this progression across both human and animal literatures. Unlike the previous systematic review,1 the present review provides some evidence for anxiety and psychotic symptoms as potential risk factors for conversion from MCI to AD, areas that would benefit from further research (see Fig. 4 for graphical summary). A key question remains as to what role(s) depression plays in the progression of MCI to AD. It is unclear whether the relationship between depressive symptoms and AD is because depression is a prodrome of AD, depression is a risk factor for AD, a third variable causes both conditions, or some combination of these hypotheses.51 Disruptions in the monoaminergic system may be a shared underlying mechanism for both depression and AD.16 This suggests shared underlying pathology may produce a prodrome of AD exhibited as depressive symptoms; or the presence of some third factor may contribute to the development of both conditions (e.g., serotonin changes impair sleep and lead to depression). Consistent with this hypothesis, the current review found evidence for protective effects of antidepressant usage42,31 and SSRIs in particular29,53 on progression from MCI to AD in both human and animal studies. These findings hold promise for SSRIs as early preventative treatment for AD in those with depression, although further cross-species research is warranted.

Compared to depression and some of its components that we can measure in animal models, there is less consistent evidence across human and animal literatures for the role of anxiety as a risk factor for progression from MCI to AD and minimal evidence for other psychiatric problems. More work in these areas is needed to elucidate these potential risk factors. Finally, our diagnostic criteria for psychiatric problems are formed based on symptomatology exhibited through human experiences. Thus, modeling a psychiatric disorder such as depression in an animal proves quite difficult. Examining transdiagnostic and cross-species factors (e.g., stress, sleep) provides more direct comparisons and allows generation of more specific research questions, use of particular methods (e.g., use of biomarkers in human research), and further mechanistic understanding to improve treatment and prevention strategies. This construct-based perspective aligns with the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) framework developed by the National Institute of Mental Health, which promotes a dimensional, mechanism-oriented understanding of mental functions beyond traditional diagnostic categories. For example, SSRIs could be administered to rodent models of AD pathology to examine the impact on sleep and pathophysiology. SSRIs could be applied early, when sleep depth (delta power) is reduced (potential biomarker associated with early amyloid aggregation in rodents),78 and before severe sleep disturbances develop (fragmented sleep), and applied alone versus in combination with drugs appropriate for facilitating sleep in older adults (dual orexin receptor antagonists).79 Effective approaches identified in rodent models could be tested in humans using EEG to identify the onset of reduced sleep depth and administering sleep medication and/or SSRIs to assess efficacy in preventing or delaying progression to AD.
