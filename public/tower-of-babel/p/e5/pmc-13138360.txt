Utility of Far‐Field Potentials as a Biomarker of Neurodegeneration in Spinal Muscular Atrophy
Authors: Aicee Dawn Calma, Nathan Pavey, Cláudia Santos Silva, Yukiko Tsuji, Amirul Ab Ghapar, Katrina Morris, Matthew C Kiernan, Michelle A Farrar, Parvathi Menon, Steve Vucic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsFar field potentials (FFP) have been proposed as a reliable neurophysiological prognostic biomarker in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). This study evaluated the utility of ulnar nerve FFP as a robust research biomarker of lower motor neuron degeneration in spinal muscular atrophy (SMA).MethodsPeripheral neurophysiological assessments were performed in 13 participants with SMA, 19 with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and 19 healthy controls. The ulnar nerve was stimulated at the wrist, and motor responses were recorded over the abductor digiti minimi (ADM) muscle. Recorded measures included compound muscle action potential (CMAP), FFP and near‐field potential (NFP) amplitudes, and motor unit number index (MUNIX).ResultsThe FFP amplitude was significantly lower in SMA participants compared to healthy volunteers (p < 0.001), but comparable to ALS (p = 0.11). The FFP amplitude showed strong correlations with the Revised Upper Limb Module (RULM) (ρ = 0.92), ALS Functional Rating Score‐Revised (ρ = 0.85), upper limb MRC score (ρ = 0.89), CMAP amplitude (ρ = 0.97), NFP amplitude (ρ = 0.88), and MUNIX values (ρ = 0.84), all of which were highly statistically significant. Multiple linear regression indicated that FFP amplitude was an independent predictor of RULM (p < 0.001).DiscussionFFP amplitude appears to be a promising neurophysiological biomarker for SMA, with potential utility for monitoring disease progression, particularly in a clinical trial setting.Keywords: CMAP, far‐field potential, near‐field potential, spinal muscular atrophy

Introduction

Neurophysiological measures are important biomarkers in spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), providing information on changes in the composition of motor units [1, 2], which is of particular importance for assessing disease progression. The compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude is one of the most accessible measures for assessing lower motor neuron dysfunction. It reflects both denervation and collateral reinnervation. It does not, however, always provide an accurate representation of axonal loss, nor indeed localize the site where degeneration occurred. Separately, CMAP amplitude is highly dependent on electrode placement, which can affect reproducibility [3, 4, 5, 6]. Moreover, in chronic denervation, collateral sprouting may maintain CMAP amplitudes despite substantial motor unit loss, as surviving axons reinnervate muscle fibers previously supplied by degenerated motor neurons [7].

To address these limitations, more refined neurophysiological biomarkers exploring motor unit number estimation (MUNE) have been developed and applied in SMA, including MScanFit‐MUNE and motor unit number index (MUNIX) techniques [8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. These techniques have yielded measures that have shown promise as sensitive indicators of motor unit loss and may also serve as potential biomarkers of treatment response. Some MUNE techniques, such as MScanFit‐MUNE, require specialized equipment and technical skills, which can be challenging in SMA populations.

Far‐field potentials (FFPs) are neurophysiological responses [4, 13, 14] that have emerged as promising diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers of lower motor neuron dysfunction in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) [6, 13, 15]. The FFP is a triphasic signal (N1–P1–N2) occurring after ulnar nerve stimulation that predominantly reflects electrical activity from intrinsic hand muscles [4], particularly the interossei [4, 14, 16, 17]. Given that FFPs originate remotely from active and reference electrodes [4], they are less influenced by electrode placement, in contrast to conventional CMAP and near‐field potential amplitudes that reflect activity of hypothenar muscles.

Consequently, FFP may represent a more consistent and robust measure of lower motor neuron integrity in neuromuscular disorders [4]. This feature is particularly appealing in SMA populations, who tend to be younger and may be less tolerant of extensive neurophysiological testing. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of FFP amplitude in SMA, with a focus on its utility in clinical assessments in predicting upper limb function.

Methods

Subjects

In this cross‐sectional study, we analyzed data from prospectively recruited adult participants with genetically confirmed SMA. All SMA patients were recruited from the multidisciplinary Neuromuscular Clinic (Concord Hospital), Sydney. Inclusion criteria for SMA patients included: (i) older than 18 years of age; (ii) genetic diagnosis of SMA; (iii) CMAP amplitude > 0.5 mV. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis patients were recruited from the multidisciplinary ALS clinic (Brain and Nerve Research Centre, Concord Hospital). Inclusion criteria were: (i) ALS by Gold coast criteria [18], (ii) CMAP amplitude > 0.5 mV. Healthy volunteers were recruited from patients' relatives and from staff at the Brain and Nerve Research Centre, and were required to have no neurological or related medical conditions that could affect the peripheral nervous system. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki prior to enrolment. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee.

Clinical Assessment

A comprehensive assessment was conducted in SMA participants, including a detailed clinical examination and neurophysiological evaluation. Clinical assessments included the Revised Upper Limb Module (RULM, 19 items, maximum 37 points) [19], the repeated nine‐hole peg test (rNHPT), and the Hammersmith Functional Motor Scale—Expanded (HFMSE, 33 items, maximum 66 points) [20]. The RULM incorporates tasks assessing strength, movement range, and fine motor control of the shoulders, elbows, wrists, and hands [19, 21]. The rNHPT is a measure of fatigability of arm and hand function in SMA participants [22]. The HFMSE assesses functional motor abilities in people with SMA who can sit or walk [20].

Muscle strength was measured using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scoring system, evaluating the upper limbs bilaterally (shoulder abduction, elbow flexion and extension, wrist extension, finger abduction, and thumb abduction) for a maximum score of 60, and lower limbs bilaterally (hip flexion, knee extension, and ankle dorsiflexion) for a maximum score of 30. The combined total MRC score varies from 0 (complete paralysis) to 90 (normal strength). Site of disease onset and disease duration (months) were recorded in ALS and SMA participants, as was the ALS functional rating scale‐revised (ALSFRS‐R) score [23] in ALS and SMA.

Neurophysiology Assessments

Far‐field potentials were recorded using standard techniques, with the E1 electrode positioned over the ulnar styloid and E2 electrode placed over the distal fifth phalanx, in accordance with previously established methodology [15]. Baseline‐to‐positive peak FFP amplitudes were recorded from the abductor digiti minimi (ADM) [4]. Near field potentials (NFP) were obtained with the E1 electrode on the ADM muscle belly and E2 electrode on the ulnar styloid process. Conventional baseline‐to‐peak CMAP amplitudes were recorded by placing the E1 electrode over the ADM muscle belly while the E2 electrode was positioned over the fifth distal phalanx (Figure S1). The ulnar nerve was stimulated at the wrist, resulting in a triphasic FFP as well as biphasic NFP and CMAP curves (Figure S1).

Motor unit number index studies were performed on the ulnar nerve with recordings from the ADM muscle following established methodology [24, 25, 26]. Surface electromyography interference pattern (SIP) was recorded across five different contraction levels. A minimum of 20 SIPs was collected while maintaining a stable contraction. Unstable or brief SIPs (< 300 ms) were excluded. Only signals with SIP area > 20 mV ms, SIP area/CMAP area > 1, and ideal case motor unit count (ICMUC) < 100 were included for MUNIX calculation, as previously recommended [24]. All measurements were recorded from the less affected hand using a Natus electrodiagnostic machine (Version 22.1.0.151, Natus Neurology Inc., Middleton, WI, USA).

The split hand index and neurophysiological index (NI) were calculated using previously reported formulas [27, 28]. For calculation of the split hand index, CMAPs were recorded from the abductor pollicis brevis following median nerve stimulation at the wrist, and from the first dorsal interosseous and ADM following ulnar nerve stimulation at the wrist. The NI was calculated by stimulating the ulnar nerve at the wrist and recording motor responses from the ADM. CMAP amplitude and distal motor latency were measured. Ten F‐wave responses were obtained to calculate F‐wave frequency.

Statistical Analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For variables that followed a normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t‐test, while differences among three or more groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted using Bonferroni correction. For non‐parametric data, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. Associations between variables were examined using Pearson's correlation coefficient or Spearman's rho, depending on data distribution. Multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the relationship between FFP and the clinical and neurophysiological measures in SMA participants. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non‐normally distributed data are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). A p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Patient Demographics

In total, 13 SMA participants were recruited and their results compared to 19 ALS and 19 healthy control participants. When compared to ALS, SMA participants were significantly younger (p < 0.001) and exhibited longer disease duration (p < 0.001) (Table 1). The median age of the healthy participants was 40 (30–46) years. In the ALS cohort, 14 (74%) had limb onset and 5 (26%) had bulbar onset disease.

The SMA cohort was clinically and therapeutically heterogenous. Detailed information on functional status and treatment of the 13 SMA participants is in Table S1. Most SMA participants were classified as Type 3 SMA. At the time of the assessment, four were ambulant, six were sitters, and three were non‐sitters. Five participants underwent HFMSE assessment, as the rest of the participants were excluded due to severe contractures and scoliosis that would confound the scoring of HFMSE. Of those who had HFMSE testing, the participants exhibited mild impairment (Table 1). Upper limb dysfunction was moderate across the entire group with a moderate functional impairment indicated by the ALSFRS‐R score (Table 1). Treatment ranged from single therapies (nusinersen or risdiplam) to transitions and periods without treatment, with a median duration of 33.6 months among treated participants.

Neurophysiological Parameters

One‐way ANOVA revealed a significant difference in FFP and NFP baseline‐to‐peak amplitudes between the three groups (FFP: F (2,48) = 9.65; NFP: F (2,48) = 24.92; p < 0.001, Table 2). Post hoc tests indicated that SMA participants had lower FFP amplitude than controls (p < 0.001) but not different from ALS (Figure 1A). Effect size was large (η 2 = 0.29), showing that diagnostic category accounted for a considerable proportion of variance in FFP amplitude. NFP amplitude was also lower in SMA versus controls (p < 0.001), with a larger effect size (η 2 = 0.51).

A significant difference in baseline‐to‐peak CMAP amplitude was also evident among the groups (F (2,48) = 27.75, p < 0.001). Post hoc Bonferroni comparisons showed that SMA participants had significantly lower CMAP amplitudes compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001) and ALS participants (p = 0.003). Furthermore, the CMAP amplitude was significantly reduced in ALS compared to healthy controls (p < 0.001, Table 2, Figure 1C).

One‐way ANOVA showed that the MUNIX and the split hand index values differed significantly across groups (MUNIX: F (2,42) = 8.75, p < 0.001; split‐hand index: F (2,48) = 19.07, p < 0.001). MUNIX was lower in SMA (p < 0.001) and ALS (p = 0.043) participants compared to healthy controls (Table 2). There were no significant differences in MUNIX values between SMA and ALS cohorts (p = 0.19). The split‐hand index was significantly smaller in SMA (p < 0.001) and ALS (p < 0.001) participants when compared to healthy controls, but not between SMA and ALS participants (p = 1.0). The neurophysiological index (p = 0.33) did not differ significantly between the SMA and ALS cohorts (Table 2).

Correlation With Clinical and Neurophysiological Biomarkers

For the SMA participants, the FFP amplitude correlated strongly with the total RULM score, total ALSFRS‐R score, ALSFRS‐Rfine motor subscore, and upper limb MRC score (Table 3). In contrast, FFP amplitude did not significantly correlate with the HFMSE score, although the number of SMA participants that underwent HFMSE assessment was small (n = 5).

At a neurophysiological level, FFP amplitude significantly correlated with the conventional CMAP amplitude, NFP amplitude, and MUNIX values. Age of participants at assessment was not significantly correlated with the FFP amplitude (p = 0.16), NFP amplitude (p = 0.35), or CMAP amplitude (p = 0.13).

Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify which of the correlated measures were independently associated with FFP amplitude in the SMA cohort (Table 4). The FFP amplitude was a strong significant independent predictor of the RULM score (Figure 2), while the CMAP and NFP amplitudes were not significant independent predictors. Separately, the RULM score was found to be a significant predictor of both the FFP and CMAP amplitudes, with a higher regression coefficient for FFP amplitude. The CMAP amplitude was an independent predictor of FFP amplitude, while the NFP amplitude and MUNIX were not significant independent predictors.

While the ALSFRS‐R total and upper limb MRC subscore exhibited significant bivariate correlation with the FFP amplitude, they were not independent predictors in the multiple linear regression model (ALSFRS‐R, p = 0.97, upper limb MRC score p = 0.51), suggesting that their effects are best explained by shared variance with other predictors.

Discussion

The present study demonstrated a significant reduction in FFP amplitude in SMA participants compared with healthy controls, similar to the reductions observed for CMAP amplitude and MUNIX values. This reduction was closely associated with clinical measures of functional decline, with the RULM score emerging as an independent predictor of FFP amplitude. Furthermore, FFP amplitude correlated with other indicators of lower motor neuron dysfunction, including CMAP amplitude, MUNIX, and motor unit number estimates, supporting its potential as a surrogate biomarker of disease progression in SMA. Notably, CMAP amplitude was also an independent predictor of FFP amplitude, reinforcing its value as an outcome measure.

The prognostic utility of FFP amplitude was recently established in ALS [6, 15]. In the present study, the reduction in FFP amplitude among SMA participants was comparable to that observed in the ALS cohort, suggesting a similar extent of functional lower motor neuron involvement across the two conditions. The statistically significant bivariate correlations between FFP amplitude and measures of clinical function, including the RULM, ALSFRS‐R, ALSFRS‐Rfine motor subscore, and upper limb strength, support its potential role as a marker of disease progression in SMA.

The FFP amplitude independently predicted the RULM score, highlighting its potential as a neurophysiological marker of upper limb function in SMA. The RULM score is a standardized measure of upper limb motor function but can be limited by floor effect [21]. Notably, three SMA participants in this study scored zero on the RULM scale yet still had recordable FFPs. Although both FFP and CMAP amplitudes were independently associated with RULM score, FFP retained a stronger independent association on multivariable modeling, supporting its potential complementary role in assessing motor function in SMA (Table 4). Moreover, the observation that FFP amplitude independently predicted RULM score, whereas CMAP and NFP amplitudes did not, suggests that far‐field responses capture broader aspects of corticospinal and motor unit integrity beyond those reflected in conventional peripheral measures. The strong association between FFP amplitude and functional status highlights its potential as a sensitive biomarker of motor function in SMA.

A potential advantage of FFP recordings relates to their practicality and accessibility as a neurophysiological tool in clinical practice, particularly in the pediatric setting. Far‐field potentials can be obtained using standard EMG equipment, in contrast to more complex techniques such as MUNIX, which require dedicated software and advanced expertise. It should also be stressed that FFP acquisition does not require greater stimulation intensity or duration than conventional CMAP recording. While FFP is unlikely to substantially alter patient tolerance to testing, its potential value lies in the complementary physiological information it may provide.

A potential limitation of this study is the relatively small number of SMA participants. In slowly progressive neurological disorders, collateral reinnervation may mask the extent of motor unit loss, potentially limiting the utility of both FFP and CMAP amplitude. While FFP reflects the summated contribution of functioning motor units, and reductions in FFP amplitude may capture early or subtle motor unit dysfunction not fully compensated by collateral reinnervation, the cross‐sectional design of the present study does not permit assessment of this possibility. Longitudinal studies are required to determine whether FFP is more sensitive to change over time compared with CMAP in this context. Finally, although previous studies in ALS cohorts have shown excellent intra and inter‐rater reliability for FFPs [6], a dedicated longitudinal test–retest study in an SMA cohort is required to confirm reproducibility in this population and to compare with other neurophysiological measures.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated a significant reduction in FFP amplitude among participants with SMA compared to healthy controls, showing its potential as a complementary neurophysiological marker of lower motor neuron dysfunction in SMA. The FFP amplitude was an independent predictor of the RULM score, highlighting its potential as a surrogate prognostic biomarker in SMA. Incorporating FFP amplitude measurements into clinical assessments and trials may enhance disease monitoring and provide a reliable marker for tracking disease progression.
