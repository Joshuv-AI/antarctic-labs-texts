Early repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation for preventing chronic postoperative pain in older adults: a randomized clinical sub-study
Authors: Wei-Wei Zhou, Zhi Yang, Can Zhou, Qiao Qiao, Yong-Hua Zhang, Jia Hu, Ya-Nan Gao, Ling-er Chen, Wei-Wei Yang, Yi-Bin Qin, Chao-Chao Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) is particularly relevant in thoracoscopic lung cancer surgery, a procedure predominantly performed in older adults, yet it remains a major clinical challenge with few effective preventive strategies. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has demonstrated analgesic potential and may offer a novel approach for CPSP prevention. This study investigated whether early postoperative rTMS could reduce CPSP in older patients undergoing thoracoscopic surgery and explored its potential mechanisms.MethodsIn this randomized controlled trial, 286 patients were screened and 230 undergoing thoracoscopic surgery were randomized to receive either active or sham rTMS targeting the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (10 Hz, 100% resting motor threshold, 2000 pulses per session) immediately after extubation in the PACU. A total of 198 patients completed the 3-month follow-up, during which clinical and biochemical outcomes were assessed by blinded evaluators.ResultsIn the modified intention-to-treat analysis, the incidence of CPSP was significantly lower in the active rTMS group than in the sham group (24.3% vs 43.5%; RR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.39–0.80; P = 0.002). In addition to reducing CPSP, active rTMS resulted in significant improvements in anxiety (26.0 [IQR, 25.0–26.0] vs 29.0 [IQR, 27.5–30.0]; P < 0.001) and depression scores (26.0 [IQR, 25.0–26.0] vs 29.0 [IQR, 27.5–30.0]; P < 0.001) at 3 months. Additionally, serum CXCL10 levels—an inflammation-related biomarker associated with chronic pain—were significantly lower in the active rTMS group (68.9 [48.1–85.7] vs 82.6 [67.3–105.5] ng/mL; P = 0.018). Exploratory analyses further demonstrated that CXCL10 had good predictive accuracy for CPSP (AUC = 0.90; cutoff = 90.5 pg/mL).ConclusionsEarly postoperative rTMS targeting the DLPFC effectively reduces the development of CPSP after thoracoscopic surgery and may be associated with CXCL10-related inflammatory processes. Background

Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. For early-stage disease, surgical resection is the most effective and potentially curative treatment [2]. In recent years, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) has become the standard approach due to its minimally invasive nature, reduced surgical trauma, and faster recovery [3, 4]. However, chronic postoperative pain (CPSP) has emerged as a common and burdensome complication following thoracic surgery, with incidence reaching up to 60%—the highest among all surgical types [5–7]. Reported CPSP incidence after VATS varies widely, in part because studies use different NRS thresholds (e.g., NRS ≥ 1 vs ≥ 3) and follow-up definitions; estimates range from ~ 5 to 44% after VATS [6] and may reach ~ 57% after thoracotomy [8]. CPSP not only causes persistent physical discomfort but is also associated with increased risks of postoperative anxiety, depression, and other psychological disorders, leading to reduced quality of life and substantial healthcare burden [9]. These challenges underscore the need for effective strategies to prevent CPSP and improve long-term outcomes in patients undergoing lung cancer surgery.

CPSP is defined as pain persisting for more than 3 months after surgery, localized to the surgical site or adjacent area, without other identifiable causes [10]. It is not only common but also notoriously difficult to manage, often lasting for months or years despite standard treatments. The pathogenesis of CPSP involves a complex interplay among peripheral nerve injury, neuroinflammation, central sensitization, and psychological factors such as perioperative anxiety and depression [11, 12]. Although multimodal analgesia, regional nerve blocks, acupuncture, and perioperative psychological interventions have been proposed, their clinical efficacy remains inconsistent, and most do not adequately address central mechanisms or affective comorbidities [13–16]. This has led to increasing interest in individualized, neuromodulation-based, non-pharmacological approaches for CPSP prevention.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a noninvasive technique that modulates cortical excitability via electromagnetic induction [17]. High-frequency rTMS targeting the primary motor cortex (M1) has shown efficacy in relieving neuropathic pain [18]. While M1 is conventionally used for pain modulation, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has been extensively studied in mood and cognitive disorders [17, 19]. Growing evidence suggests that affective disturbances, such as anxiety and depression, are both contributors to and consequences of chronic pain, reflecting a bidirectional and comorbid relationship [20, 21]. Our previous clinical study demonstrated that rTMS targeting the left DLPFC significantly improved postoperative anxiety and depressive symptoms [17], supporting the premise that DLPFC neuromodulation may strengthen postoperative emotional resilience and potentially influence pain trajectories. Consistent with this framework, postoperative stimulation of the DLPFC has also been evaluated for perioperative analgesia. Randomized sham-controlled studies reported that a single session of postoperative left prefrontal rTMS reduced patient-controlled opioid consumption and produced clinically meaningful short-term analgesic effects, with subsequent replication [22, 23]. However, the available evidence has predominantly focused on acute pain and opioid outcomes, and whether early postoperative DLPFC rTMS can prevent chronic postsurgical pain (CPSP) remains insufficiently established. Beyond network-level modulation, accumulating evidence indicates that neuroinflammatory processes contribute to pain chronification and the transition from acute to chronic pain [24, 25]. Among the inflammatory mediators, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) has been identified as a key factor promoting microglial activation, neuronal sensitization, and central pain maintenance [26]. Previous studies have shown that CXCL10 is closely associated with the development of chronic pain, with early elevations observed in both animal models and patients, suggesting its potential role as a biomarker for chronic neuropathic pain [26–28]. Elevated CXCL10 levels have been reported in both neuropathic and inflammatory pain models, linking peripheral injury to sustained central sensitization [28].

Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether early postoperative DLPFC rTMS could reduce CPSP after thoracoscopic surgery and whether it modulates neuroinflammatory processes, with serum CXCL10 included as an exploratory biomarker.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was conducted as a predefined sub-study within a larger registered randomized controlled trial carried out from May 3, 2024, to February 13, 2025, in the Departments of Anesthesiology and Thoracic Surgery at the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University. Follow-up of the final enrolled patient was completed on May 13, 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University and was registered at the Clinical Trials Center on May 1, 2024 (identifier: NCT06392919). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives before enrollment. The trial was conducted in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines.

Study procedures and data collection

Participants were identified and screened using the Hospital Information System of the Department of Thoracic Surgery, which provided access to patients’ electronic medical records. After obtaining written informed consent and collecting baseline demographic and clinical data, eligible patients were randomly assigned to either the active rTMS group or the sham rTMS group. Group allocation was performed by an anesthetic nurse not involved in the intervention or outcome assessment, ensuring allocation concealment. Both patients and outcome assessors were blinded to group assignment.

The rTMS intervention was administered using a water-cooled figure-of-eight coil connected to a transcranial magnetic stimulator (Xiangyu Medical, China). Patients were placed in a semi-recumbent position. Cortical targeting was performed using a positioning cap based on the international 10–20 EEG system. The coil was positioned tangential to the scalp over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC; F3 location), with the handle oriented 45° posterior-laterally to the midline. Electromyographic electrodes were applied to the right abductor pollicis brevis (APB) muscle for determination of the resting motor threshold (RMT), which was defined as the minimum intensity required to elicit at least five motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) ≥ 50 μV in ten consecutive trials at rest [29]. The RMT was measured before anesthesia induction. All stimulations were performed by the same experienced operator to ensure consistent coil positioning and stimulation parameters across participants.

rTMS was delivered after extubation when patients were fully awake and able to follow verbal commands (Ramsay sedation score ≤ 2). Hemodynamics were continuously monitored, and stimulation was paused if blood pressure or heart rate exceeded 20% of baseline. In the active rTMS group, stimulation consisted of a 20-min session at 10 Hz with an intensity of 100% RMT. A total of 2000 pulses were delivered per session (40 trains of 50 pulses each, with 25-s inter-train intervals). Sham stimulation was performed using the same coil positioned at the same scalp site but rotated 90° away from the skull, producing identical sound and tactile sensations without cortical activation. All participants were closely monitored during the procedure, and stimulation was immediately discontinued if intolerable discomfort occurred. The rTMS protocol adhered to established safety guidelines [30]; minor transient symptoms were not systematically recorded.

CXCL10 measurement and variables

To explore whether early postoperative rTMS modulates neuroinflammatory signaling, serum CXCL10 was measured as an exploratory biomarker. Immediately after the rTMS/sham session, 5 mL of central venous blood was collected, centrifuged, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Serum CXCL10 concentrations were quantified using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Owing to cost constraints, 30 samples per group were randomly selected from all available specimens prior to unblinding. Perioperative variables were also recorded, including surgical type and duration, anesthesia duration, intraoperative blood loss, urine output, fluid administration, and intraoperative opioid consumption. In addition, postoperative cancer-directed treatments (adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy) and vital status were additionally ascertained via telephone follow-up after peer review, using a standardized questionnaire. When participants were deceased, information was obtained from family members/caregivers.

Participants

Given the higher prevalence of lung cancer surgery among older adults, patients aged 60 years or older with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification of ≤ III who were scheduled to undergo thoracoscopic surgery were considered eligible for inclusion in this study. A total of 286 patients were screened. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a prior history of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) or open thoracic surgery; (2) a history of acute or chronic pain conditions requiring regular analgesic treatment; (3) diagnosed neurological or psychiatric disorders; (4) brain or scalp injuries; (5) substance abuse (alcohol or drugs); (6) visual or hearing impairments, or significant communication difficulties; (7) presence of metal implants, skin lesions, or known allergies to surface electrodes; (8) severe cardiovascular disease, or significant hepatic or renal dysfunction.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the active rTMS group or the sham rTMS group using an online centralized randomization system. The randomization sequence was generated using computer-based random numbers. Clinical management and data collection were performed in a sequentially numbered manner and supervised by healthcare personnel who were not involved in the study. Allocation concealment was ensured using opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened by the investigators upon the patient’s arrival in the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for rTMS administration. The investigators responsible for outcome assessment, as well as those involved in data collection and processing, remained blinded to the group assignments, along with the surgeons, anesthesiologists, nurses, and patients.

Anesthesia

Upon arrival in the operating room, standard monitoring was initiated, including electrocardiography (ECG), pulse oximetry (SpO2), noninvasive blood pressure (NIBP), and bispectral index (BIS) monitoring. Invasive arterial blood pressure was monitored via radial artery catheterization under local anesthesia. Central venous access was established for the administration of anesthetic agents and intraoperative fluid management.

General anesthesia was induced with intravenous etomidate (0.3–0.4 mg/kg), sufentanil (0.5 μg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.2–0.3 mg/kg). Once the BIS value fell below 60, a double-lumen endobronchial tube was inserted orally under direct visualization. Mechanical ventilation was initiated after confirmation of correct tube placement using auscultation and fiberoptic bronchoscopy.

During the non-one-lung ventilation (non-OLV) period, volume-controlled ventilation was applied with a tidal volume (VT) of 6–8 mL/kg, respiratory rate of 10–12 breaths/min, an inspiratory-to-expiratory (I:E) ratio of 1:2, FiO2 of 100%, and oxygen flow of 2 L/min. During OLV, VT was set to 5–6 mL/kg, respiratory rate to 12–16 breaths/min, and the I:E ratio was maintained at 1:2. FiO2 was kept at 100% with an oxygen flow of 2 L/min. End-tidal carbon dioxide (PETCO2) was maintained between 35 and 45 mmHg, and SpO2 was kept at ≥ 90%. To optimize oxygenation and minimize ventilator-induced lung injury, PEEP was individualized through titration based on the lowest driving pressure. PEEP was increased in 2 cm H2O increments from 2 to 10 cm H2O, with each level maintained for ten respiratory cycles. Driving pressure was calculated as the difference between plateau pressure (Pplat) and PEEP and was recorded during the final cycle at each level. The PEEP corresponding to the lowest driving pressure was selected as the optimal value.

Anesthesia was maintained with intravenous propofol (3–4 mg/kg·h) and remifentanil (0.1–0.2 μg/kg·min), supplemented with inhaled sevoflurane. BIS was targeted at 40–60 and was maintained primarily by titrating the end-tidal sevoflurane concentration (approximately 0.8–1.0%). Propofol and/or remifentanil were adjusted as needed in response to surgical stimulation and hemodynamic indicators of nociception (e.g., changes in heart rate and/or mean arterial pressure relative to baseline). The Analgesia Nociception Index (ANI) was not used in this study. Hemodynamic fluctuations were actively managed. Hypotension, defined as a systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or > 30% decrease from baseline, was treated with intravenous norepinephrine (50 μg). Hypertension, defined as a systolic blood pressure > 150 mmHg or > 20% increase from baseline, was managed with intravenous urapidil (12.5–25 mg). Bradycardia (heart rate < 50 beats per minute) was treated with atropine (0.25–0.5 mg), while tachycardia (heart rate > 100 beats per minute) was managed with esmolol (1 mg/kg). Intraoperative core body temperature was maintained above 36.5 °C using a medical warming blanket. Intravenous tramadol (50 mg) was administered 30 min before the end of surgery for preventive analgesia. At the conclusion of surgery, all anesthetic agents were discontinued. Airway secretions were suctioned, and lungs were manually inflated to ensure full re-expansion. The tracheal tube was removed after the patient regained consciousness and adequate spontaneous respiration. Patients were subsequently transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). Postoperative analgesia was provided using a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) device. The analgesic solution consisted of sufentanil (3 μg/kg), tropisetron (8.96 mg), and normal saline to a total volume of 100 mL. The PCIA settings included a bolus dose of 0.5 mL per demand, a lockout interval of 15 min, a background infusion rate of 2 mL/h, and a maximum limit of 6 mL/h.

Outcomes and measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of CPSP at 3 months after surgery. CPSP was defined as continuous or intermittent pain located at the surgical site or adjacent areas, persisting for at least 3 months postoperatively, and not attributable to other causes [31]. Pain intensity was assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS). Patients were classified as having CPSP if they reported persistent pain with an NRS score ≥ 1 at 3 months postoperatively. This definition was chosen to maximize sensitivity for detecting persistent pain after thoracic surgery (including VATS), as even mild pain can limit shoulder mobility and respiratory recovery. Similar low-threshold definitions have been applied in previous studies [32].

Secondary outcomes included NRS scores at rest and during activity at 24 and 48 h after surgery; total consumption of analgesic drugs administered via patient-controlled analgesia; time to first ambulation; time to chest tube and negative-pressure drainage tube removal; and length of hospital stay. NRS scores at rest and during activity were also reassessed at 3 months postoperatively to evaluate persistent pain intensity. In addition, anxiety and depression levels were evaluated at 3 months using the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) and the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS), respectively. The SAS and SDS are 20-item self-report questionnaires rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1–4), with several reverse-scored items. Total raw scores range from 20 to 80 (reported as raw totals without index-score conversion), with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity [33]. Given that serum CXCL10 has been suggested as a potential biomarker for predicting the development of chronic pain [27], serum CXCL10 levels were also measured in the PACU following rTMS intervention and included as one of the secondary outcomes.

Sample size and statistical analysis

The sample size was determined a priori using PASS 15.0. Based on the literature [34], the incidence of CPSP in 3 months after surgery is about 47%. At the time the protocol was finalized, thoracic/VATS datasets reporting 3-month CPSP using an NRS ≥ 1 definition were limited; therefore, we used a study employing a comparable low-threshold definition to inform the assumed control-event rate. Although this manuscript presents a sub-study from the parent randomized trial cohort, the sample size was determined a priori based on the CPSP endpoint. We hypothesized that the incidence of CPSP would decrease from 47% in the control group to 27% in the intervention group. We chose a study power of 0.80 and a significance level of 0.05, using a two-sided significance level to indicate a significant difference in CPSP incidence. We then concluded that 91 patients were needed in each group. The sample size was increased to 115 per group to allow for a 20% loss to follow-up.

The primary analysis was performed in the ITT population, with a per-protocol (PP) sensitivity analysis. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations under a missing-at-random assumption (20 imputations). The imputation model included treatment group, the outcome variable, and prespecified baseline/perioperative variables associated with CPSP and/or follow-up completion (e.g., age, sex, surgical method, early postoperative pain scores, and baseline SAS/SDS), using models appropriate to data type; diagnostics showed no instability. Estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to assess normality for continuous variables. Normally distributed data were presented as means (SDs) and compared using an independent-sample, two-tailed t-test. Non-normally distributed data were reported as medians (IQRs) and analyzed via the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were evaluated using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to determine the effect of rTMS on CPSP prevention. Since the primary endpoint was a binary variable, the chi-square test was used for its analysis. To assess the predictive value of CXCL10 for CPSP, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was also performed. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated to evaluate the diagnostic performance. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp). A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Primary outcome

In the intention-to-treat population, the incidence of CPSP was significantly lower in the active rTMS group (28 of 115 [24.3%]) compared with the sham rTMS group (50 of 115 [43.5%]; relative risk, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.39–0.80; Pearson χ2 = 9.39, P = 0.002). The overall incidence of CPSP among all randomized patients was 33.9% (78 of 230).

Secondary outcomes

Clinical postoperative outcomes are summarized in Table 2. In terms of acute postoperative pain, no difference was observed between groups in resting NRS scores at 24 h (χ2 = 4.61, P = 0.203). However, the active rTMS group reported significantly lower NRS scores during activity (e.g., coughing) at 24 h (χ2 = 20.86, P < 0.001), indicating a shift toward lower pain score distributions. Consistent with this, a higher proportion of patients reported low pain (activity NRS ≤ 3) at 24 h in the active group than in the sham group (93/115 [80.9%] vs 65/115 [56.5%]; risk difference, 24.3%; 95% CI, 12.8–35.9). At 48 h, both resting and activity NRS score distributions favored the active rTMS group (resting: χ2 = 23.47, P < 0.001; activity: χ2 = 19.58, P < 0.001). In addition, patients in the active rTMS group required a lower volume of analgesics within the first 48 h postoperatively (56.0 [IQR, 49.0–62.0] mL vs 64.0 [IQR, 53.0–71.0] mL; Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.001, Table 2).

Regarding recovery outcomes, no significant differences were observed between groups in initial ambulation time, chest tube removal time, or duration of hospitalization. However, the active rTMS group showed a slightly shorter negative pressure tube removal time compared with the sham group (4.00 [IQR, 3.00–5.00] days vs 4.00 [IQR, 4.00–5.00] days; Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.047, Table 2).

At 3 months postoperatively, patients in the active rTMS group reported significantly lower NRS pain scores compared with the sham group (resting: χ2 = 66.73, P < 0.001; activity: χ2 = 64.66, P < 0.001). Furthermore, depression scores (26.0 [IQR, 25.0–26.0] vs 29.0 [IQR, 27.5–30.0]; Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.001, Table 2) and anxiety scores (26.0 [IQR, 25.0–27.0] vs 29.0 [IQR, 28.0–30.0]; Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.001, Table 2) were also significantly lower in the active rTMS group. Postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy were comparable between groups (see Additional file 1: Table S3).

Hematological and exploratory analysis

As shown in Fig. 3A and B, patients in the active rTMS group exhibited significantly lower CXCL10 levels compared with the sham group (68.9 [IQR, 48.1–85.7] ng/mL vs 82.6 [IQR, 67.3–105.5] pg/mL; Mann–Whitney U test, P = 0.018). Furthermore, ROC curve analysis demonstrated that CXCL10 had good predictive accuracy for CPSP, with a cutoff value of 90.5 ng/mL, an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI, 0.83–0.99), sensitivity of 75%, and specificity of 95% (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that early postoperative rTMS significantly reduced the incidence of CPSP in older adults undergoing thoracoscopic surgery. In an exploratory subsample, post-session CXCL10 levels were lower with active rTMS and showed good discrimination for CPSP status at 3 months. Because CXCL10 was measured after the intervention and only in a subsample, these findings are hypothesis-generating and require validation. If validated, CXCL10 may help identify patients at higher risk for CPSP and inform future preventive approaches.

CPSP remains one of the most challenging complications following thoracic surgery, with reported incidence rates ranging from 30 to 60%, which are among the highest of all surgical procedures [5]. Despite advances in surgical techniques, including video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) that reduces surgical trauma and accelerates recovery [35], the burden of CPSP remains substantial. Current preventive approaches [36], such as multimodal analgesia, perioperative regional nerve blocks [37], and pharmacological agents including gabapentin, ketamine, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [36, 38, 39], have shown inconsistent results. Many strategies are limited by side effects, insufficient long-term efficacy, or inability to address central mechanisms that underlie pain persistence.

The application of neuromodulation, particularly rTMS, has attracted increasing interest in chronic pain research. High-frequency rTMS delivered over the primary motor cortex (M1) has been reported to relieve neuropathic pain syndromes [39, 40], including post-stroke pain and peripheral neuropathy. However, most of these studies focused on treatment of established chronic pain rather than prevention. In the perioperative setting, only a few trials have evaluated postoperative DLPFC rTMS and reported reduced acute pain and opioid consumption, including the sham-controlled studies by Borckardt and colleagues [22, 23]. Consistent with these findings, we observed lower acute pain scores at 24 and 48 h in the active group. However, evidence that perioperative rTMS prevents CPSP has been limited. Our findings extend this literature by demonstrating, for the first time to our knowledge, that early postoperative rTMS targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) can significantly lower CPSP incidence in a high-risk surgical population. This provides novel evidence supporting the preventive use of rTMS in the perioperative setting.

In this study, we specifically chose the DLPFC rather than M1 as the stimulation site for two main reasons. First, stimulation of M1 for neuropathic pain treatment has been reported previously [41], whereas evidence for DLPFC stimulation remains limited. Second, both prior literature [42] and our earlier clinical studies [17] indicated that targeting the DLPFC effectively reduces postoperative anxiety and depression. Given that anxiety and depression are recognized contributors to CPSP development [43], our findings show that DLPFC rTMS was associated with both a lower CPSP incidence and lower SAS/SDS scores at 3 months. Because SAS/SDS were not measured at an earlier postoperative time point before CPSP assessment, we could not determine whether mood changes mediate the effect of rTMS on CPSP. Future trials should include early postoperative affective assessments to enable temporally ordered mediation analyses and mechanistic inference.

The mechanisms underlying the preventive effects of rTMS on CPSP are likely multifactorial. The DLPFC plays a central role in cognitive–affective regulation of pain [44]. Functional imaging studies have shown that the DLPFC is interconnected with limbic and subcortical regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, and periaqueductal gray [45], which are key nodes in descending pain modulatory circuits [46]. By modulating cortical excitability in the DLPFC, rTMS may enhance top-down inhibitory control of nociceptive processing and reduce central sensitization, a critical mechanism in the transition from acute to chronic pain [47]. Another possible mechanism involves immunological and inflammatory pathways [12]. CXCL10, also known as interferon gamma–induced protein 10 (IP-10), is a chemokine involved in T-cell recruitment and microglial activation [48]. Preclinical studies have shown that CXCL10 is upregulated following peripheral nerve injury and contributes to the development of neuropathic pain by enhancing excitatory synaptic transmission and promoting neuroinflammation [27, 49]. In our trial, active rTMS was associated with lower serum CXCL10 concentrations than sham stimulation in an exploratory subsample, and CXCL10 demonstrated good discrimination for 3-month CPSP status in ROC analysis within this subset. These observations are hypothesis-generating and are consistent with the possibility that perioperative rTMS may influence immune–inflammatory signaling; however, given the post-intervention timing of CXCL10 measurement and the cost-limited subsample, causal pathway inferences cannot be made and the biomarker findings require validation. It is also worth noting that rTMS has been shown in prior studies to increase brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels [17], promote synaptic plasticity [50], and facilitate functional reorganization of cortical circuits [51]. These neuroplastic changes may contribute to both improved emotional regulation and enhanced pain modulation [52]. Although our study did not directly measure BDNF, future investigations integrating neurotrophic and inflammatory biomarkers may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying rTMS effects on CPSP.

The clinical implications of our findings are substantial. First, rTMS represents a safe, well-tolerated, and non-pharmacological intervention that can be feasibly incorporated into perioperative care. Unlike systemic pharmacological agents, rTMS does not carry risks of respiratory depression, gastrointestinal side effects, or drug interactions, making it particularly attractive for older surgical patients who often have multiple comorbidities. The ability of rTMS to reduce both pain [18] and negative affective symptoms [53] may further enhance postoperative recovery and quality of life. Second, our results support the role of CXCL10 as a potential biomarker for CPSP risk stratification. Identifying patients at higher risk could enable targeted preventive interventions, including early rTMS or other neuro-modulatory strategies, thereby personalizing perioperative pain management. The high predictive accuracy observed in our ROC analysis suggests that CXCL10 measurement may be clinically useful, although validation in larger and more diverse cohorts will be necessary. Third, these findings contribute to the broader paradigm shift toward multimodal, mechanism-based approaches to perioperative care. By combining neuromodulation with pharmacological and regional analgesic strategies, it may be possible to achieve synergistic effects in preventing CPSP. Integration of rTMS into enhanced recovery after surgery protocols could represent a promising direction for future clinical practice.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, analgesic use after hospital discharge was not systematically recorded, which may have introduced residual confounding in long-term pain outcomes. Postoperative adjuvant therapy was retrospectively obtained via telephone follow-up (or caregiver report when applicable) rather than detailed oncology records; therefore, misclassification is possible and specific chemotherapy regimens could not be evaluated. Nonetheless, no participant received preoperative therapy and postoperative treatment rates were balanced between groups, reducing the likelihood of differential confounding. Second, this work represents a predefined sub-study of a larger registered randomized trial, focusing specifically on thoracic surgery patients. Although the sample size was adequate to detect differences in CPSP incidence, the subset of patients included in the hematological analysis was relatively small due to cost constraints. Consequently, the biomarker results should be interpreted as exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts. Third, neuro-navigation was not used for DLPFC targeting; instead, the F3 site of the 10–20 EEG system was applied, which may have introduced minor variability in coil positioning. The interval from surgery to rTMS initiation and adverse events were not systematically recorded, limiting evaluation of timing effects and tolerability. Future studies should incorporate neuro-navigation, prespecified peri-intervention timestamps, and structured adverse-event collection. Fourth, although patients with preoperative pain requiring analgesics were excluded, transient mild discomfort not requiring medication was not systematically recorded. In addition, CPSP was defined using an NRS ≥ 1 threshold to enhance sensitivity; this differs from more stringent definitions (e.g., ICD-11–aligned thresholds such as NRS ≥ 3) and may limit comparability across studies. Fifth, follow-up was limited to 3 months, and it remains uncertain whether the protective effects of rTMS persist over longer periods. Extended follow-up is needed to assess the durability of pain reduction and its impact on long-term quality of life. Sixth, we did not quantify cumulative intraoperative opioid/adjunct analgesic doses (e.g., total remifentanil), although anesthesia duration was comparable and opioid titration followed standardized clinical parameters. Future studies should prospectively capture cumulative perioperative analgesic exposure. Finally, although we targeted the DLPFC based on its established role in affective regulation, other cortical targets such as the primary motor cortex may also influence CPSP risk. Future comparative studies exploring different stimulation sites, frequencies, and protocols are warranted to optimize perioperative neuromodulation strategies.

Conclusions

Early postoperative rTMS reduced the incidence of CPSP in older adults undergoing thoracoscopic surgery. In an exploratory biomarker subsample, post-session CXCL10 levels were lower in the active rTMS group and demonstrated good discrimination for CPSP status at 3 months. Overall, rTMS may represent a promising non-pharmacological strategy for perioperative CPSP prevention.
