Spatiotemporal photon distribution control on active sites enables bio-inspired methane-to-methanol conversion
Authors: Yi Li, Yuehan Cao, Chunqiu Han, Kaibo Zheng, Shaowen Cao, Jie Meng, Ying Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDirect catalytic conversion of methane to methanol offers a pathway for transforming a potent greenhouse gas into a portable clean liquid fuel, thereby mitigating carbon emissions and supporting sustainable energy. However, this process faces challenges from thermodynamically favorable methanol overoxidation. Here, we show that spatiotemporal regulation of photogenerated charge carriers on engineered catalytic sites enables a bio-inspired ordered two-step photocatalytic process that imitates methane monooxygenase. In a platinum-loaded cadmium sulfide photocatalyst, unsaturated sulfur sites modulate hole migration while platinum sites modulate electron migration, ensuring their concurrent surface arrival within picoseconds and prolonged localization. This dynamics temporarily anchors methane at hole-enriched sulfur sites while hydroxyl radical generation occurs at electron-rich platinum sites, decoupling hydroxyl radical formation from methane dehydrogenation to suppress overoxidation. The approach achieves methane-to-methanol conversion with selectivity of 83.5%, offering a bio-inspired solar-driven strategy for C1 valorization.Subject terms: Artificial photosynthesis, Photocatalysis

Introduction

The depletion of crude oil reserve has driven catalytic methane conversion into value-added chemicals as a sustainable alternative to petroleum-based processes. Conventional approaches for producing syngas or hydrocarbons require high temperatures (600–1100 °C), facing three key limitations: catalyst sintering, poor selectivity, and high energy demands1–3. These challenges have intensified researches into mild-condition methane (CH4) oxidation to methanol (CH3OH), which is regarded as a strategic C1 oxygenate-offering and energy-efficient production of liquid platform chemicals2,4–7. Photocatalytic direct conversion of CH4 to CH3OH achieves efficient C-H bond activation under mild conditions, utilizing light-driven charge transfer to selectively oxidize methane at tailored catalytic sites. However, thermodynamic limitations inherently drive CH4 molecules toward overoxidation pathways, predominantly yielding fully oxidized carbon dioxide (CO2) as the terminal product8. This fundamental challenge necessitates the development of advanced catalytic architectures with precisely engineered active sites to kinetically steer CH4 activation trajectories toward partial oxidation while suppressing deep oxidation processes.

In recent works, simulating the active center of methane monooxygenase (MMO) for catalysts designs have made progress in modulating CH4 to CH3OH9–15. Not just the precisely constructing biomimetic active sites over photocatalysts, we are also inspired with the sequence of crucial steps, which requires the additional focus on temporal modulations. It is noted that enzymatic process occurs in two sequential steps, which is as illustrated in Fig. 1a. Firstly, electron and molecular oxygen (O2) are coordinated on active site to generate the reactive oxygen species (ROS). After that, CH4 is captured and one of the hydrogen atoms is abstracted by ROS (CH4 dehydrogenation), forming a methyl radical (·CH3) intermediate that subsequently yields CH3OH. Such a strictly ordered process is conceptualized in photocatalysis like: simulating the enzymatic oxygen activation process through light-induced electron transfer that combines with O2 to generate ROS; then replicating the enzymatic hydrogen extraction via ROS-mediated C-H bond dissociation in CH4 to form CH3OH.

However, translating these blueprints into functional photocatalytic platforms confronts substantial implementation barriers. This limitation stems from fundamental differences in reaction sequences between biological and artificial catalytic systems. In enzymatic catalysis mediated by MMO, CH4 adsorption strictly occurs after oxygen activation process, ensuring one hydrogen abstraction process that prevents overoxidation. Conversely, it is common that engineered photocatalysts employ photoinduced-electron-enriched active centers to facilitate efficient O2-to-ROS conversion (O2 + e− → ·O2−, ·O2− + H+ → ·OOH, ·OOH + H+ + e− → ·OH)16–18. But it is possible that these active sites simultaneously adsorb both O2 and CH4 molecules during illumination. This concurrent adsorption creates two reaction pathways of ROS-mediated hydrogen abstraction from CH4 and direct electron transfer attacks on adsorbed CH4. The parallel activation mechanisms lead to non-selective extraction of multiple hydrogen atoms, promoting the generation of overoxidation products of CO2 rather than controlled single-step oxidation to CH3OH. This fundamental mismatch in reaction choreography highlights the critical need for spatially/temporally decoupled active sites in artificial photocatalytic systems to replicate the precision of enzymatic catalysis.

To address these challenges, we propose a kinetic modulation strategy through spatiotemporal charge separation engineering on catalyst surfaces. Our conceptual framework (Fig. 1b) involves constructing an active site rich in photogenerated electron adjacent to the original active site that rich in photogenerated hole. By utilizing this hole-rich site to preferentially capture CH4 molecules under light illumination, thereby delaying the adsorption process of CH4 toward active site rich in photogenerated electron. However, implementation faces fundamental kinetic barriers due to the disparate charge carrier dynamics—photogenerated electron typically migrate faster than hole in conventional semiconductors19–21. This temporal mismatch disrupts the desired reaction sequence, allowing premature CH4 adsorption at electron-rich sites rather than at hole-rich sites. Therefore, to realize this concept, it is necessary to develop effective strategies enabling photogenerated electron and hole to migrate to adjacent active sites within the same timescale.

Herein, we carried out investigations based on typical cadmium sulfide (CdS) to achieve the similar trapping of photogenerated electron and hole on ultrafast timescale. Specifically, the migration of hole to the surface is accelerated by utilizing unsaturated sulfur (~10.6 ps)22, addressing the issue of slow hole migration due to their large effective mass. Subsequently, platinum (Pt)23,24 enables the migration of electron to the surface within ~3.3 ps, achieving a temporal match between electron and hole migration. Then, the Pt-S bond forms independent localized sites for electron and hole, spatially separating them. As a result, electron-rich and hole-rich sites can stably exist over a longer timescale, allowing CH4 and O2 to interact with hole and electron, respectively, which significantly enhances the activity and selectivity of CH3OH production.

Results

Photocatalyst preparation

Based on a typical hydrothermal method (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2), unsaturated-sulfur-rich cadmium sulfide (CdS) is prepared as substrate, and platinum (Pt) is loaded using impregnation method (1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS). X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) are indicative of the successful preparation of CdS substrate. The primary diffraction peaks at around 24.9°, 26.5° and 28.2° in XRD (Supplementary Fig. 1b) can be indexed to (100), (002) and (101) of hexagonal CdS (PDF#65-3414), which are relatively sharp and narrow. The measurable lattice fringes in HRTEM (Supplementary Fig. 3b) include typical (100) and (011) of CdS. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) images (Supplementary Fig. 3c) demonstrate the polycrystalline property of substrate materials, where the obvious diffraction rings all correspond to hexagonal CdS crystal. Moreover, TEM reflects a typical particle shape of as-prepared CdS and the average size is less than 100 nm (Supplementary Fig. 3a). This result is consistent with the crystal average thickness deduced from Scherrer Formula (Supplementary Table 1). After Pt impregnation, a negligible impact on the crystal structure and micro-morphology of substrates can be noticed. XRD and TEM of 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS are basically same with CdS (Supplementary Fig. 3d-i). Furthermore, the electronic state and coordination environment is validated through X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption fine spectroscopy (XAFS). In XPS Cd 3d fine spectra of CdS (Supplementary Fig. 4a), the doublet peaks (3d5/2 at 404.7 eV, 3d3/2 at 411.5 eV) are ascribed to Cd2+ in crystal20. As to S 2p fine spectra (Supplementary Fig. 5a), the dominating signals (2p3/2 at ~161.1 eV, 2p1/2 at ~162.3 eV) originate from lattice S2-, while the minor ones at ~162.4 (2p3/2) and ~163.6 eV (2p1/2) are ascribed to bridging disulfide (S22-)9,25, demonstrating the formation of surface unsaturated sulfur. Worth noting that a positive shift of Cd 3d and S 2p binding energy emerges after the impregnation of Pt, which results from the surface reconfiguration over 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS. In terms of Pt 4f (Supplementary Fig. 4b), it is noticed that doublet subpeaks appear to be present in the spectra of 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS, and their spin-orbital splits are 3.3 eV according to the deconvolution analysis. Compared with standard Pt0 state (4f7/2 at ~70.6 eV, 4f5/2 at ~73.9 eV), the binding energy of dominating doublets are positively shifted, indicating that Pt is in an oxidation state (Ptδ+)26–28. Moreover, as Cl signals were not detected in XPS (the lower limit of detection is 0.10%), it is disclosed that chloroplatinic acid precursor residues on materials surface have been basically removed (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Importantly, electron paramagnetic spectroscopy (EPR) revealed that the signals for unpaired electron over CdS decreased with the loading of Pt, indicating Pt could be anchored on unsaturated sulfur (Supplementary Fig. 7). Additional demonstrations on Pt coordination are provided by analyzing X-ray absorption fine structure consisting of X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS). Figure 2a displays the normalized Pt L3-edge absorption spectra, which are calibrated referenced on XANES first derivative spectra. 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS exhibit almost identical XANES of which the white-line peak intensity is between Pt foil and PtO2. This phenomenon manifests that Pt species in 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS are both in an average electron efficient state and it rises from Pt0 to Ptδ+(0 < δ < 4)29. Pt L3-edge EXAFS is presented in k-space and R-space. EXAFS in k-space (Supplementary Fig. 8) still exhibits distinguished discrepancy between samples and references. But in R-space (Fig. 2b), exclusive Pt-S bonds are observed in the first shell of 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS, while the contribution of Pt-O and Pt-Pt is absent. Taking these phenomena into consideration, it is reasonable to speculate that Pt is anchored on CdS through coordinating with unsaturated sulfur.

Accordingly, a possible configuration for Pt is constructed (model structures are provided in Supplementary data 1) and the corresponding EXAFS fitting is carried out (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2). In the first shell of 1%Pt/CdS, Pt is coordinated with S atom ( ~ 2.33 Å) and average coordination number (N) is determined to be ~2.58. The contributions in the second shell originate from Pt-Cd (N = 1.16) coordination of which the radial distance is ~2.78 Å. The appearance of Pt-Cd rather than Pt-Pt coordination indicates that Pt species exhibits high dispersity and Pt clusters or particles are not formed. As to 2%Pt/CdS, Pt-S bond in the first shell is still found and the coordination is close to 1%Pt/CdS, but the Pt-Cd contribution can hardly be observed (Supplementary Table 3). The wavelet transformation (WT) is adopted to combine k and R space together and unveil Pt coordination environment. As displayed in Fig. 2d-g, Pt-S bond makes dominated contributions to the ultimate results for 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS, while Pt-Cd contribution can only be found in the former. CO adsorption-desorption experiments for Pt configuration revelation are recorded (Fig. 2h, i). In contrast to CdS (Supplementary Fig. 9), the overlapped vibrations signals can be observed over 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS when CO have been adsorbed until saturation. After continuous purging with inert gas, weak CO adsorption signals retained at 2110–2104 cm−1, corresponding to the adsorbed CO on Ptδ+. Compared with typical signals ( ~ 2084 cm−1), the wavenumber was increased30–32. It is speculated that the anchored Pt over CdS could have strong affinity with CO. Importantly, no band at ~1950 cm−1 was found, demonstrating there was no any bridge-adsorbed CO between Pt clusters and the support. High-angle annular dark field-scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) revealed the sporadic Pt atoms on the lattice of 1%Pt/CdS due to the contrast difference (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Same phenomenon was also detected over 2%Pt/CdS, while the density of Pt atoms was increased (Supplementary Fig. 10b). The abovementioned results support that the highly dispersed Pt-S configuration (Fig. 2j, Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Data 1) is formed over 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS, even if there is minor difference in the coordination environment. Regarding these differences, this work ascribes it to the variation of Pt amount. According to inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), the accurate Pt amount over 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS are determined to be 0.98 wt% and 1.99 wt%. It is speculated that with the increasing of Pt amount, the coordination environment becomes homogeneous and regular.

Taking the micro-configuration characterizations into account, it can be concluded that CdS surface which is rich in unsaturated sulfur site was successfully constructed. The loading of Pt led to the formation of additional Pt sites on CdS surfaces. The Pt sites were identified to exist in an oxidation state of δ+. As the accurate amount of Pt in sample is pretty close to the theoretical value, 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS are then marked as Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS for the following works.

Excited-state dynamics and charge migration

To investigate the effects of unsaturated sulfur and Pt sites on the migration processes of photogenerated electron and hole, the light absorption capacity and charge carrier dynamics were systematically analyzed. The steady-state absorption of prepared sample was revealed through ultraviolet-visible diffuse and reflectance spectroscopy (UV-vis DRS). The dominating absorption peaks at around 500 nm correspond to the band edge electron transition in CdS, which are prominent over three samples (In Supplementary Fig. 12). Importantly, through analyzing the band structure based on XPS valence band spectra and steady-state absorption edge revelation (Supplementary Fig. 13), it is found that the band edge position of CdS, Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS is similar with each other. This result indicates that there is limited discrepancy in the energy of excited carriers and more attention should be given to excited-state dynamics. To detail the charge excitation of samples, femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) was adopted for excited-state dynamics studying. TAS probing region is from 450 to 700 nm, and 400 nm pump light is used to excite the samples. The pump laser pulse fluency is modulated to a low level ( ~ 5 μJ·pulse−1·cm−2) so that there is exclusively single exciton dynamics reflected in TAS and the possible Auger recombination is not considered (Supplementary Fig. 14). Exhibited in Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 15a, TAS of CdS are mainly featured with two typical signals: an increased absorption at ~420 nm induced by the excited-state absorption (ESA) in the presence of 1S excitons, and a ground-state bleach (GSB) at ~505 nm resulting from the state filling at the conduction band (CB) edge. The weak tail bleach at ~600 nm is ascribed to the interband states induced by surface unsaturated sulfur33–35. To determine the contribution of excited electron and hole to the tail bleach, electron trapping agent p-benzoquinone (BQ)36,37 was used to quench the signal. In Supplementary Fig. 16a, as BQ is introduced, kinetic decay curves at 600 nm keep almost identical, illustrating that the tail bleach is only contributed by hole. Noting that with the growing of Pt amount, additional steady-state absorption from 550 to 800 nm is increasing over Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS while they could possibly generate TAS signal. However, 600 nm bleach signals over three samples (Supplementary Fig. 16b and Fig. 16c) are almost identical and they are all not influenced by BQ. These phenomena illustrate that tail bleach at 600 nm in Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS are still contributed by CdS. Importantly, the tail bleach at 600 nm can serve as an indicator to illustrate the hole-related processes.

The excited state dynamics reflected in TAS is visualized through singular value decomposition (SVD) global fitting, and the results (Supplementary Table 4) are attached with the contour plots. SVD global fitting is a unified separable nonlinear model which can describe all measurements collected over multiple independent variables38. The carrier dynamics of CdS can be fitted with four bleach recovery time constants. The fastest one (τ0, 3.5 ps) is ascribed to the exciton cooling, where there is only a bleach at 480 nm. For the second constant (τ1, 13.4 ps), the tail bleach at 600 nm appears accompanying with GSB at 505 nm, illustrating that a hole-transfer process from valence band (VB) edge to interband state is revealed. τ2 at 397.0 ps is only featured with GSB, and tail bleach has been completely removed. It demonstrates that the hole on interband state is further trapped, and τ2 should be ascribed to the intrinsic recombination of CdS. Inf includes the photodynamic processes of which the lifespan exceeds the TAS time-window. As to Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS, the intrinsic excited dynamics of CdS were both maintained but the average lifespan has been reduced. This phenomenon indicates that the exotic noble metal can facilitate the carrier relaxation39. The distinguished variations are detected over time-components number as there is an additional recovery time-component (τef) of which the timescale is only slower than τ0. τef consists of ESA and GSB, and there is no any tail bleach. It is thus reckoned that τef should be associated with electron transfer from CdS CB edge to Pt states. To demonstrate this speculation, GSB signal decay at ~505 nm is also analyzed using BQ as trapping agent. In Supplementary Fig. 17, GSB decay of CdS is remarkably accelerated when BQ is introduced, demonstrating excited electron of CdS can be trapped by BQ. As to Pt1/CdS, GSB decay is slightly enhanced because a part of electron is simultaneously transferred to Pt. While for Pt2/CdS, excessive Pt attracts most of electron, and the influence of BQ on GSB decay is negligible.

To well display the variations in photodynamics, GSB signal decay of three samples are presented in Fig. 4a. For Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS, kinetic decay can be initially observed at ~2 ps due to the electron transfer (τet) induced by Pt, while for CdS, the prominent decay is found at ~10 ps because of the hole transfer (τ1). The overall trend matches with the global fitting results above. The kinetic studying is also supported by time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) and steady-state photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy which can explore the involved carrier recombination. Limited by instrument response function (Fig. 4b), recombination process observed in TAS are all included in the fastest time-component (Supplementary Table 5) of TRPL and it is almost identical over CdS, Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS (τ1, ~ 2.0 ns). But as Pt has noticeably accelerated the carrier relaxation, the lifetime of τ2 and τ3 shrink with the increasing of Pt amount. The average photoluminescence lifetime (τave) was 258.6 ns, 62.9 ns and 49.1 ns for CdS, Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS, respectively. Worth noting that the faster carrier recombination in Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS was also accompanied with a steady-state PL deterioration (Supplementary Fig. 18), indicating that a non-radiative channel is simultaneously formed, which is strongly related with the process of electron-transfer to Pt. Excited-dynamics reveals the contribution of Pt and unsaturated sulfur to electron and hole trapping in similar ultrafast timescale. It is also reasonable to speculate that excited electron relaxation is promoted by transferring to Pt states, while hole is simultaneously trapped on unsaturated sulfur sites (Fig. 4c).

But surface reaction occurs beyond the window of time-resolved spectroscopies, to demonstrate long lifespan of those trapped carriers, the carrier distribution on photocatalyst surface is further investigated through in-situ XPS. In-situ XPS in dark field is collected as reference while the data in light field is recorded after 5 min irradiation (Fig. 4d-i and Supplementary Fig. 19). Scrutinizing the fine spectra of CdS, it is clear to notice that the element binding energy is slightly increased under light irradiation, illustrating the hole-aggregation on photocatalyst surface. This result unveils the long-lived hole over CdS, and it demonstrates that the trapped excited hole can be further localized on unsaturated sulfur. As to Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS, the binding energy shift is not consistent with each other, and it changes from positive to negative with the growing of Pt amount. The electron-aggregation dominating the surface manifests that Pt can also localize electron for a long timescale. Transient photocurrent response provides additional evidences to this speculation. Under visible light irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 20), an increased positive photocurrent can be detected over CdS as the unbound electron is released into the circuit with applied bias, and hole is localized on unsaturated sulfur. Noticeably, photocurrent intensity over Pt1/CdS is sharply decreased and for Pt2/CdS there is almost no detected photocurrent. Incident monochromatic photon-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) profiles of CdS, Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS all aligns with their steady-state absorption curve (Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Fig. 22), but the highest IPCE are still observed over CdS (Supplementary Table 6). These phenomenon can be associated with the strong electron localization on Pt, which hinders the electron transfer from working electrode to the counter electrode40. Here, integrating the charge carrier dynamics in different timescale together, the function of Pt and unsaturated sulfur in photocatalysis is clearly disclosed, which can separately trap electron and hole within picoseconds, while simultaneously localize them for surface photocatalytic reactions.

Photocatalytic evaluation and mechanism revelation

Given the characteristics of as-prepared samples, an evaluation on photocatalytic CH4 selective oxidation was subsequently carried out based on a top-irradiation sealed glass reactor (Supplementary Fig. 23). To commence with, a contrast experiment was conducted to determine the optimized photocatalyst amounts for current system. In Fig. 5a, the gradient-increasing CH3OH production rate is observed when there is less than 10 mg photocatalysts. But when photocatalyst amount exceeds 10 mg, the average CH3OH production rate rapidly decreases, because the superfluous photocatalyst can hardly participate in target reactions41–44. Based on the determined photocatalyst amount, the evaluation experiments were subsequently carried out. Figure 5b and 5c display the statistic amount of CH3OH and other C1 oxygenates during the irradiation period. A prominent CH3OH production rate is detected over Pt1/CdS (12.72 μmol g−1 min−1), which is remarkably better than CdS (5.07 μmol g−1 min−1) and Pt2/CdS (7.88 μmol g−1 min−1). Peroxides in current system consist of HCHO and CO2, and their distributions in products vary with CH3OH amount: the higher CH3OH production rate, the smaller peroxides proportion in products (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 7). The C2 hydrocarbons (including C2H6, C2H4 and C2H5OH) was also scrutinized using Pt1/CdS as photocatalyst, while no any products were found, indicating the products mainly consist of C1 hydrocarbons. On this basis, supplementary contrast group Pt0.5/CdS and Pt1.5/CdS were also prepared to demonstrate the performance of Pt1/CdS. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 24, the overall performance of Pt0.5/CdS and Pt1.5/CdS are still inferior to the marked Pt1/CdS, indicating the scare or excessive Pt sites can hardly maximize the synergy of Pt and unsaturated sulfur in photocatalysis. To further complement the evaluation process, additional tests were carried out by modulating the necessary parameters, and the photocatalyst was Pt1/CdS. In Fig. 5e, the removal of O2 seriously deteriorated the overall performance, while when CH4 and light were separately removed, there was no CH3OH detected in the system. Moreover, it was noticed that the reaction temperature contributes to ultimate performance, because in contrast to optimized conditions, lower temperature (40 °C) leads to lower CH3OH amount. As to the durability of photocatalysts, cycle experiments were carried out. Similar CH3OH production rate and products selectivity were observed in the first three rounds, indicating its photocatalytic stability in 180 min (Fig.5f and Supplementary Fig. 25). Moreover, to evaluate the structural stability of photocatalyst, XRD and XPS of used Pt1/CdS was scrutinized. The typical diffraction peaks of used samples in XRD can match with fresh ones while no additional peaks appeared, illustrating the crystal stability of CdS, Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS (Supplementary Fig. 26). Cd 3d and S 2p fine spectra of used Pt1/CdS are almost identical with the fresh one (Supplementary Fig. 27d and Supplementary Fig. 27e) while no lattice oxygen (Supplementary Fig. 27f) and SO42− (Supplementary Fig. 28c) signal appeared, excluding the photocorrosion of used Pt1/CdS. Besides, in Pt 4f fine spectra of used Pt1/CdS (Supplementary Fig. 28d), Ptσ+ was still detected after XPS fitting accompanied with no any reduced metallic Pt0. This phenomenon further demonstrates the stability of Pt-S configuration over Pt1/CdS. Ultimately, to present the performance of current system from a holistic perspective, turnover number (TON, ~15.19) and turnover frequency (TOF, ~0.25 min−1) over best Pt1/CdS are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 29, while apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) at 395 nm was calculated. AQE at 395 nm is ~1.00% for CH4 to CH3OH conversion (Supplementary Fig. 30, Supplementary Fig. 31 and Supplementary Table 8). Moreover, the activity of Pt1/CdS has been compared with analogous systems in Supplementary Table 11 and it is competitive in normal pressure system.

The experimental data above indicate that when utilizing unsaturated sulfur sites solely to accelerate hole transfer and create hole-localized centers, CH4 activation occurs but leads to overoxidation, resulting in reduced CH3OH selectivity. Similarly, excessive introduction of Pt sites creates electron-rich centers on surfaces, yet still triggers overoxidation. In contrast, the Pt1/CdS system combines electron-enriched Pt sites and hole-localized unsaturated sulfur sites. The formation of Pt-S bonds enables long-term stabilization of photogenerated electron and hole at their respective active centers. This synergistic configuration not only achieves efficient CH4 activation but also directs its selective conversion to CH3OH while suppressing overoxidation pathways.

To probe the underlying mechanisms, the CH4 conversion processes on various sample surfaces are systematically analyzed through in-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier-transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) as Fig. 6a-c and Supplementary Fig. 32a-e display. For CdS, during the adsorption process, O-H bond bending vibration45 in H2O ( ~ 1640 cm−1) and C-H bond stretching vibration46 in CH4 ( ~ 1340 cm−1) are enhanced, demonstrating that reactants are adsorbed on photocatalyst surface. A new peak at 1013 cm−1 is assigned to H-C-S vibration of S-*CH4 configuration47,48, proving that the unsaturated sulfur is the adsorption site of CH4. Under light irradiation, there are new absorption bands assigned to *HCHO (1170–1132 cm−1)49,50, *CH2OH ( ~ 1036 cm−1)9 and CO2− ( ~ 1245 cm−1 *O-C-O)51 appearing. This indicates the following CH4 conversion pathway on CdS surface.

To understand why the structurally different sites can effectively inhibit the overoxidation of CH4, the mechanism of action of the active sites on different surfaces has been analyzed. For CdS, due to the fact that the sites on the surface are mostly unsaturated sulfur sites that enrich photogenerated holes under light irradiation, these sites can not only effectively capture CH4 to promote the activation of its C-H bond, but also convert H2O into hydroxyl radicals (·OH, OH− + h+ → ·OH). To confirm the above conclusion, the contrast experiment of CH4 conversion in anerobic and moist atmosphere (H2O + CH4) under light irradiation was carried out. As shown in the Supplementary Fig. 33, after CH4 and H2O adsorption equilibrium was reached, the peaks of typical H-C-S vibration, adsorbed H2O and CH4 appeared. With constant light irradiation, the intensity of these characteristic peaks decayed while *CH2OH rose (*CH4 → *CH2OH), indicating that CH4 overoxidation can also be promoted. More importantly, when CH4 is bubbled into aerobic water to imitate photocatalytic CH4 conversion (Supplementary Fig. 34a), ·OH intensity dramatically decreased, which demonstrates that the unsaturated sulfurs sites to generate ·OH have been occupied by adsorbed CH4. The above data indicate that on the surface of CdS, the constructed unsaturated sulfur sites that enrich photogenerated holes are not only the adsorption and activation sites of CH4, but also the sites for H2O oxidation to generate ·OH. Since the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the adsorption of CH4 both occur through the same site, it causes CH4 to be prematurely attracted and captured by the photogenerated holes at the site, resulting in that CH4 was consecutively attacked by ROS, leading to the occurrence of overoxidation reactions.

For Pt2/CdS of which surface is predominantly composed of electron-enriched Pt sites under illumination, CH4 activation and ROS could also be carried out on one site. Revealed by in-situ EPR (Supplementary Fig. 34b), ·OH signals were still deteriorated when CH4 was bubbled into aerobic water. Moreover, in-situ DRIFTS unveiled the decreased peak assigned to H-C-S vibration on Pt2/CdS surface in Fig. 6b, ruling out unsaturated sulfur as primary CH4 adsorption sites (unlike CdS). Thus, the contrast experiments of ROS detection in aerobic CH3OH were additionally conducted (Supplementary Fig. 35). CH3OH is the trapping agents for ·OH so that ·OOH signals can be triggered. In contrast to CdS, ·OOH signals have remarkably declined as the rapid electron-injection over Pt2/CdS promotes ·OOH conversion to ·OH (O2 + e− → ·O2−, · O2− + H+ → ·OOH, ·OOH + H+ + e− → ·OH). Pt sites, based on above results, are identified as the ·OH generation sites over Pt2/CdS, which can simultaneously facilitate CH4 activation via the pure electrophilic activation process.

For Pt1/CdS, which possesses both electron-enriched Pt sites and hole-enriched unsaturated sulfur sites on its surface, the prerequisites for mimicking the stepwise reaction mechanism of enzyme are established. Specifically, the electron-rich Pt sites activate O2 to generate ROS, while the hole-localized unsaturated sulfur sites temporarily capture CH4. Subsequently, ROS attack the CH4 adsorbed on the unsaturated sulfur sites, enabling single hydrogen atom extraction from CH4 to form CH3OH. To validate this hypothesis, CH4 adsorption behavior was first investigated. As shown in the Fig. 6c, the H-C-S vibration characteristic peak still emerged on Pt1/CdS surface during adsorption, confirming that unsaturated sulfur sites serve as CH4 adsorption centers. Notably, under light irradiation, the attenuation rate of this signal was slower compared to that observed on CdS, indicating that while photogenerated holes effectively capture CH4 molecules, they do not accelerate C-H bond cleavage as aggressively as in CdS. Necessarily, in-situ EPR analysis (Supplementary Fig. 34c) implied that ·OH generation over Pt1/CdS can hardly be influenced with CH4 adsorption, indicating that there are separated sites for CH4 activation and ·OH generation. Furthermore, Pt1/CdS can also give impetus to ·OOH conversion to ·OH in aerobic CH3OH because Pt sites promote electron-injection for ·OH generation (Supplementary Fig. 35). Computational simulations for static adsorption corroborated the findings above, demonstrating preferential adsorption of CH4 at unsaturated sulfur sites. The geometry optimized configuration Pt1/CdS (Fig. 6d, e) consists of typical unsaturated sulfur sites and Pt sites, but CH4 adsorption energy (Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Data 1) on the former site is more negative (−0.19 eV) than the latter site (−0.098 eV). This spatially segregated adsorption ensures that ROS generation (at Pt sites) is not overlapped with CH4 activation (at unsaturated sulfur sites), establishing a stepwise reaction pathway. The temporal and spatial decoupling of these processes allows ROS to dominate the selective extraction of a single hydrogen atom from CH4, steering the reaction toward CH3OH formation while suppressing overoxidation. Ultimately, surface area analysis (Supplementary Fig. 36 and Supplementary Table 10) was also carried out, and the similar surface area among three samples indicated that the total active sites of CdS are not influenced by Pt sites construction, but the charge carrier dynamics induce a more productive conversion process.

Discussion

In this work, a new strategy for achieving the conversion of CH4 to CH3OH based on the spatiotemporal modulation of photogenerated charge carriers is proposed. Through combined XAFS, TAS, and in-situ XPS, etc., it is found that when unsaturated sulfur sites and Pt sites are constructed on the surface of CdS, they can regulate the migration process of hole and electron, respectively. More importantly, the simultaneous construction of two sites can form a local photogenerated hole and electron active center with a long lifetime under light irradiation through the Pt-S bond. Among them, Pt enriched with electron acts as the active center for ·OH generation, while unsaturated sulfur enriched with hole can temporarily adsorb CH4. Benefitting from it, a strictly stepwise two-step catalytic process similar to natural enzyme occurs on Pt1/CdS surface, which can be proved by in-situ spectroscopy. Consequently, the CH3OH production rate over Pt1/CdS reaches 12.72 μmol g-1 min-1 with a selectivity of 83.5%.

Method

Chemicals

Chloroplatinic acid (H2PtCl6·6H2O, ≥99.9%), cadmium chloride (CdCl2·2.5H2O, ≥98%), p-benzoquinone (C6H4O2, ≥99.9%) and sodium sulfite (Na2SO3, ≥98%) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Sodium sulfide (Na2S·9H2O, ≥98%) and hydrochloric acid ( ≥ 37%) were provided by a local chemical agent company (Chron Chemicals, Chengdu, China). The reagents used for materials synthesis and related experiments were all in analytical grade without further purification.

Characterizations

Crystal structure of as-prepared photocatalyst was researched through X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical X’Pert) which was operated with Cu Kα radiation. The morphology and microstructure characterizations were conducted through high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM). HRTEM was recorded on a Tecnai G2 F30 transmission electron microscope. Pt element distribution in samples was investigated through Agilent 5110 inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES). A Thermo-Scientific Kα spectrometer was used to record X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) of samples to investigate electronic state of element. The coordination environment of noble metal elements was explored by recording X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS). X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) at Pt L3-edge were collected at Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility BL11B station. XAFS data of standard Pt foil and PtO2 were shared by the group of Nano-Catalysis Group at Sum Yat-sen University and Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility. k3-weighted EXAFS was wavelet-transformed based on the Continuous Cauchy Wavelet Transform (CCWT) method53,54. XAS data analysis and fitting were all processed using Athena, Artemis, and Hephaestus software55. Steady-state absorption spectroscopy and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy were collected to investigate the optical properties of samples. The former was recorded on a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer equipped with an integrating sphere (BaSO4 was the reference standard), and the latter was obtained on a Hitachi F-7000 fluorescence spectrophotometer. In-situ electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra are recorded by a JES FA200 electron spin resonance spectrometer, and 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) served as trapping agents. EPR for crystal defect characterizations was recorded on a CIQTEK EPR200 electron spin resonance spectrometer. To detect the element binding energy shift, XPS in dark and light fields was then collected on an AXIS Supra (Shimadzu, Japan) spectrometer with Al Kα source (1486.6 eV), and 365 nm monochromatic light was used to provide excitation light. Before the XPS collection in dark field, sample film was fixed on a sample stage using conducting resin while the XPS chamber was vacuumized. Then the sample was irradiated under monochromatic light for 5 min, and XPS in light field began to be collected. When XPS in light field was collecting, monochromatic light was constantly irradiated. Given that in vacuum environment, the binding energy shifts of in-situ XPS can only be induced by surface charge carrier localization, the energy calibration is referenced on adsorbed carbon species (C 1 s at 284.8 eV).

Photocatalyst preparation

CdS preparation

A conventional hydrothermal method is adopted to synthesize CdS. Specifically, CdCl2·2.5H2O (4 mmol) is dissolved in 20 mL hydrochloric acid aqueous solution (19.5 mL deionized water and 0.5 mL hydrochloric acid) to prepare solution A. Then, Na2S solution is prepared by dissolving 8 mmol Na2S · 9H2O in 20 mL deionized water, and it is marked as solution B. Solution B is dropped into solution A slowly with constant magnetic stirring. An orange-yellow suspension is obtained with the mixing of two solution, and the suspension was immediately transferred to a 50 mL polytetrafluoroethylene autoclave equipped with Teflon-lined stainless steel. Furthermore, the sealed autoclave was maintained at 140 °C for 18 h to promote the secondary growth of crystal. When the autoclave was cooled to room temperature, the suspension in autoclave was centrifugated and washed using deionized water and alcohol. After being dry in an oven overnight, CdS powder is obtained.

Pt-loaded CdS preparation

This work prepares Pt-loaded CdS through an impregnation method. Firstly, 100 mg CdS powder was dispersed in 30 mL deionized through ultrasonic treatment. Following the adding of an amount of chloroplatinic acid aqueous solution, the suspension was maintained at 70 °C for 6 h with constant magnetic stirring. Pt-loaded CdS is obtained after centrifugation, washing and vacuum drying. Supplementary Fig. 2 displays the image of as-prepared samples. Based on the theoretical amount of Pt, Pt-loaded CdS is marked as X%Pt/CdS, and X is calculated based on Eq. 1. The accurate amount of Pt element in X%Pt/CdS is determined through inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES). According to the results of ICP-OES, 1%Pt/CdS and 2%Pt/CdS is also written as Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS. Same procedures are also adopted to prepared 0.5%Pt/CdS and 1.5%Pt/CdS, and they are marked as Pt0.5/CdS and Pt1.5/CdS.

Excited-state dynamics revelation

Excited-state dynamics of sample was deduced from transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) spectroscopy. TAS was recorded on an Ultrafast System Helios-TA spectroscopy system of which 800 nm pulse laser was produced by a Spectra-Physics Solstice Ace laser device. The pulse width of the laser was ~140 fs and the corresponding repetition rate was 1000 Hz. 800 nm laser pulse was divided into two beams which are separately used as pump light and probe light. The wavelength of pump light was conversed to 400 nm as 400 nm pump light can well excite sample based on their steady-state absorption. The corresponding pump light power ranged from 70 to 500 μW. Probe light was also modulated and it consisted of continuous white light (450–700 nm). To prepare the samples suspension, 4 mg CdS or Ptx/CdS powder was dispersed in 10 mL ethyl alcohol with constant ultrasonic treatment. The suspension was transferred to a glass cuvette which can allow the transmission of both pump light and probe light. TAS signals were collected when pump light and probe light beams were overlapped. To reveal the contribution of trapping agents to photodynamics, p-benzoquinone (BQ) was added into sample suspension. BQ is a type of organic molecule where there are two symmetrical ketone functional groups in their structure, so that BQ exhibit oxidizing properties and it can actively attract photogenerated electron, simultaneously facilitating excited carrier relaxation in TAS. The related experiments were based on the following procedures. BQ was dissolved in ethyl alcohol to prepare 0.5 M electron-trapping agent solution (solution X). 100 μL solution X was mixed with 200 μL sample suspension with constant stirring so that BQ can be fully adsorbed. The TAS signals of prepared suspension were then recorded. Excited-state dynamics in this work are fitted with SVD global fitting. Particularly, SVD involves eliminating the less important parts in dataset and producing an accurate approximation data for which sufficient exponential decays and their amplitudes will be adopted to fit. Then the feature and timescale of specific dynamic processes can be assigned. TRPL measurements were carried out based on a time-correlated single photon counting setup (TCSPC). The associated pulsed diode laser is triggered at 5 MHz and 438 nm photon excited the sample dispersed in alcohol.

Photoelectrochemical evaluation

Photoelectrochemical characterizations were conducted using a Chenhua Instrument (Shanghai, China) electrochemical station equipped with a three-electrode system. Pt wire and Hg/HgO electrode worked as counter electrode and reference electrode, respectively. To prepare working electrode, a doctor-blade coating technique was adopted to make a photocatalyst film on 2.0 × 1.5 cm2 indium tin oxide (ITO) glass (the size of photocatalyst film is 1.0 × 1.0 cm2). To get the film stable, working electrode was annealed at 160 °C for 2 h before photoelectrochemical evaluations. 0.6 M Na2SO3 solution worked as electrolyte and a Merry Change XS500 lamp provided light source. To record transient photocurrent response, Pt wire, Hg/HgO electrode and working electrode were firstly integrated and immersed in 50 mL 0.6 M Na2SO3 solution electrolyte. The electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 3.7812 g Na2SO3 into 50 mL deionized water, and it was stored in sealed reactor. The pH value of electrolyte was determined to be 10.8 ± 0.1 at room temperature. The open circuit potential (OCP) of working electrode was observed until the value got relatively stable (the sensitivity was set to 10−4 V). Then an applied potential which equal to OCP was set between working electrode and Pt electrode. With 50 s interval time, an artificial light was irradiated on working electrode and photocurrent was recorded. The photocurrent cycles were lasted for more than 20 rounds. Corresponding OCP over CdS, Pt1/CdS and Pt2/CdS are 1.14 V, 0.60 V and 0.62 V (reference to RHE). Incident monochromatic photon-electron conversion efficiency (IPCE) measurements was carried out in same conditions but the applied potential was set 1.13 V (corresponding to the onset potential of linear sweep voltammetry, RHE). To provide monochromatic light, a filter was installed on lamp to bypass photons (450 nm, 500 nm, 600 nm and 700 nm). See more experimental details in Supplementary Information for IPCE measures. The voltage is not iR corrected for all photoelectrochemical tests.

Photocatalytic evaluation

Methane (CH4) photooxidation evaluation was conducted in a 400 mL PQ-256 glass reactor (Beijing Perfect Light Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) with a quartz window for light irradiation. The reactor was sealed after the photocatalyst suspension (10 mg sample was dispersed into 50 mL deionized water) was poured into the reactor. The simulation sunlight was produced by a 300 W CEAULIGHT Xe lamp (CEL-HXF300, central light intensity is set ~600 mW·cm−2, λ > 420 nm). The temperature of reaction system was maintained at 60 °C using a water-cooling bath. Before photocatalytic tests, the sealed glass reactor was ventilated using diluted oxygen gas (10 vol% in N2 gas) to exhaust the air. The ventilation process lasted for approximately 30 min. Furthermore, 5 mL CH4 gas (99.99%) was injected into the reactor before the irradiation got started. Every 20 min, 1 mL gas in the reactor was extracted for gas components analysis through the gas chromatographs (Shimadzu GC-2010 and Agilent Technologies 7890B). To quantify the amount of HCHO, a reported colorimetric method is adopted to determine the target substances in aqueous solution56. HCHO owns electrophilic properties and it can react with pentane-2,4-dione to form colored substances. The specific experimental procedures of colorimetric method include: Firstly, colorimetric reagent solution was prepared through dissolving 15 g ammonium acetate, 0.3 mL of acetic acid and 0.2 mL pentane-2,4-dione in 95.5 mL deionized water. Secondly, 0.5 mL liquid in photocatalytic reactors and 0.5 mL of colorimetric reagent solution was dissolved in 2.0 mL deionized water, and then maintained at 80 °C for 10 min with water bath. Furthermore, the ultra-violet and visible absorption spectroscopy of the mixed liquid was measure. The absorbance at 412 nm is proportional to the concentration of HCHO in the liquid. Photocatalytic cycle experiments were based on the following procedures. After 60 min photocatalytic reaction, the photocatalyst dispersed in suspension was centrifugated and collected. In-situ DRIFTS measurements

In-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (In-situ DRIFTS) measurements were carried out to explore possible surface reaction steps, which were conducted based on a Bruker infrared spectrometer (Tensor II) equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled mercury-cadmium-telluride (MCT) detector. A Harrick Praying Mantis three-window diffuse-reflectance cell was installed to load samples. The silicon oxide window was used for light irradiation, while two measurement windows were made of ZnSe. In specific, sample was placed into the cell chamber and heated at 100 °C to remove adsorbed substances. Then the constant gas flow consisting of reactants (CH4, O2 and H2O) was injected into the chamber for reactants adsorption. Immersed in the atmosphere for more than 20 min, the corresponding in-situ DRIFTS intensity became stable and there was no more obvious growing. The samples were subsequently illuminated by artificial light. Moreover, to collect CO desorption in-situ DRIFTS, samples was also placed in the cell chamber and continuous CO flow was injected into the chamber. The intensity of CO characteristic peaks in the region from 2300 to 1900 cm-1 were intentionally focused. Finally, CO flow was aborted and continuous Ar flow was injected to drive CO desorption.

Computational details

The computational calculations were implemented based on the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP). The interaction between ion cores and electrons were described using the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. The electron exchange-correlation was carried out through generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional. The k-point was set to be 2 × 2 × 1 and cutoff energy was 400 eV. To guarantee the energy convergence, the configurations of the convergence threshold were set to be namely 1 × 10−5 eV per atom for the maximum energy change, 0.02 eV/Å for maximum force. The lattice constants of CdS crystal used in this work are a = b = 4.135 Å and c = 6.749 Å. The configurations concerning CH4 adsorbed on unsaturated sulfur site and Pt site are marked as Pt1/CdS (S) + CH4 and Pt1/CdS (Pt) + CH4, respectively.
