Clinicopathological Characteristics of Atypical Polypoid Adenomyoma of the Uterus in Association With Endometrial Atypical Hyperplasia and Endometrioid Carcinoma
Authors: TAEYEONG KIM, YOON AH CHO, HYUN-SOO KIM
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background/AimAtypical polypoid adenomyoma (APA) is an uncommon biphasic uterine tumor characterized by irregularly shaped, atypical endometrial glands haphazardly distributed within a fibromuscular stroma. APA is frequently associated with atypical endometrial hyperplasia/endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia (AH/EIN) and endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EC). This study aimed to characterize the clinicopathological features and outcomes of APA concomitant with AH/EIN and EC.Patients and MethodsNineteen cases of APA coexisting with AH/EIN or EC were retrospectively analyzed. Clinical presentation, pathological findings, treatment modalities, and outcomes were reviewed.ResultsMost patients (78.9%) were of reproductive age (26-45 years). The most common presenting symptom was abnormal uterine bleeding (47.4%), while 26.3% were asymptomatic. EC and AH/EIN were identified in 14 (73.7%) and five (26.3%) patients, respectively. Compared with AH/EIN, EC more often demonstrated cribriform architecture, papillary intraluminal growth, and stromal desmoplasia. Six of the 14 patients with EC underwent hysterectomy and remained recurrence-free. Of the eight patients managed with progestin therapy, four had persistent EC or AH/EIN on follow up curettage at three months. Similarly, one of the five patients with AH/EIN treated conservatively showed persistent disease after three months of progestin therapy. Nevertheless, all patients were disease-free at the last follow-up.ConclusionIn APA, meticulous pathological evaluation is essential to detect concurrent AH/EIN or EC. Although conservative management may be appropriate for selected patients, close surveillance is necessary to monitor for persistence or recurrence. Hysterectomy remains a definitive and reliable therapeutic option.

Introduction

Atypical polypoid adenomyoma (APA) is a rare biphasic uterine tumor composed of atypical endometrial glands embedded within a fibromuscular stroma. It predominantly affects premenopausal women and typically presents as an exophytic intrauterine mass (1-3). Due to its resemblance to endometrial polyps or submucosal leiomyomas on imaging and gross examination, histological evaluation is essential for accurate diagnosis (4-6).

Owing to its rarity, a standardized approach for managing APA has not been established, with both conservative and surgical strategies being employed. A recent systematic review suggested that fertility-sparing treatment may be appropriate for women of reproductive age who wish to preserve future fertility (7). The authors recommended hysteroscopic resection as the preferred option, as it preserves fertility, reduces recurrence risk, and enables histological assessment of the surrounding non-polypoid endometrium to detect coexisting endometrial hyperplasia/endometrioid intraepithelial neoplasia (AH/EIN) or endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EC). Similarly, hysteroscopic resection followed by surveillance endo-metrial biopsy is considered the most effective and safe treatment approach (8-10).

However, some patients treated with hysteroscopic resection alone experience high rates of recurrence and progression to AH/EIN or EC (11-14). Although historically regarded as benign, APA has demonstrated potential for malignant transformation and recurrence, supporting the hypothesis that it may represent a premalignant lesion (15,16). Nevertheless, the precise relationship between APA and AH/EIN or EC remains unclear (17), and the histological criteria distinguishing APA from these neoplastic entities have not been clearly defined. Consequently, the biological behavior and oncological implications of APA remain poorly understood.

In this study, we aimed to characterize the clinico-pathological features of uterine APA coexisting with AH/EIN and EC. Additionally, we evaluated patient outcomes to provide insights into appropriate diagnostic and management strategies.

Patients and Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (2025-07-037). Owing to its retrospective nature, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Using institutional databases, we identified 19 patients diagnosed with APA-associated AH/EIN or EC between January 2015 and March 2022. These cases were retrospectively analyzed as a consecutive cohort treated at our institution.

Clinical information was extracted from electronic medical records and included the following parameters: age at diagnosis, clinical presentation, imaging findings, size of the endometrial mass, initial diagnostic procedure, pathological diagnosis, initial treatment, presence of myometrial invasion in hysterectomy specimens, findings of follow-up curettage after progestin therapy, current status, and overall survival.

All available hematoxylin and eosin-stained slides from the resected specimens were independently reviewed by two board-certified gynecologic pathologists (T.K. and H.-S.K.). Pathological diagnoses were made in accordance with the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Female Genital Tumors (1). In cases of diagnostic discrepancies, a final diagnosis was reached by consensus review. The following pathological parameters were evaluated based on pathology reports and slide reviews: presence of AH/EIN or EC associated with APA; degree of nuclear atypia in glandular epithelial cells; glandular architecture and arrangement, including irregular or angulated contours, haphazard distribution, confluent proliferation, cribriform pattern, intraluminal papillary growth, and non-morular solid growth; presence and proportion of squamous morules; degree of nuclear atypia in stromal cells; and stromal changes, such as inflammation, desmoplasia, edema, and myxoid degeneration. The presence of AH/EIN or EC in non-APA endometrial tissue fragments was also assessed.

Results

The clinical characteristics of the 19 patients with APA are summarized in Table I. Patient ages ranged from 26 to 67 years (median, 39 years; mean=40.1 years), with 15 patients (78.9%) aged ≤45 years. The most common presenting symptom was vaginal bleeding (9/19, 47.4%), followed by dysmenorrhea (3/19, 15.8%) and menorrhagia (3/19, 15.8%). In five patients (26.3%), endometrial masses were incidentally detected during routine pelvic ultrasonography. Imaging studies revealed endometrial masses in all but one patient (18/19, 94.7%), among whom six (6/18, 33.3%) had polypoid lesions. In three patients (15.8%), the lesions appeared as endophytic or myometrial masses. Most APAs (18/19, 94.7%) presented as solitary lesions within the endometrial cavity; however, one patient (case 16) presented with multiple polypoid masses. The largest dimension of APA was reported in 14 cases (73.7%), ranging from 0.5 to 6 cm (mean=2.6 cm; median=2.4 cm). The initial diagnostic procedures included endometrial curettage (7/19, 36.8%), polypectomy (6/19, 31.6%), and myomectomy (5/19, 26.3%), the latter performed under the presumption of a submucosal leiomyoma.

Among the 19 patients, EC and AH/EIN were diagnosed in 14 (73.7%) and five (26.3%) cases, respectively. Notably, in one case (case 6), initial endometrial curettage revealed AH/EIN involving APA, but repeat curettage one month later showed no residual APA; instead, EC was identified in the non-APA endometrial tissue. The extent of AH/EIN and EC varied among cases, and multiple distinct foci were often observed. In three cases with limited complex glandular proliferation, the initial diagnoses of APA with suspected microscopic foci of carcinoma (n=2) or AH/EIN (n=1) were revised, after consensus review, to EC (n=2) and AH/EIN (n=1). All ECs were classified as grade 1 according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics grading system (1).

Treatment and follow-up data are detailed in Table I. All 19 patients are currently alive without evidence of disease at the last follow-up, with a mean overall survival of 21.4 months. Among the 14 patients with EC involving APA, six underwent total hysterectomy - five following pathological confirmation and one (case 7) based on imaging findings prior to pathological diagnosis. No patients exhibited myometrial invasion or disease recurrence. Overall survival for these patients ranged from 5 to 41 months (mean=22.8 months). The remaining eight patients received high-dose progestin therapy. Of these, four showed no residual disease on follow-up curettage and continued progestin treatment. The other four had persistent EC (n=3) or AH/EIN (n=1) detected on curettage performed at two months (n=1) or three months (n=3) after treatment initiation. One patient (case 8) subsequently underwent hysterectomy, while the remaining three continued progestin therapy and achieved no residual disease on repeat curettage at six months. All eight patients treated with high-dose progestin remained alive without recurrence, with an overall survival ranging from 4 to 54 months (mean=25.8 months). Among the five patients with AH/EIN, one (case 16) showed persistent disease at the three-month follow-up curettage, but no recurrence was observed on subsequent biopsies at six months and beyond.

The histological features of APA are shown in Figure 1. APA exhibits a biphasic proliferation of epithelial and mesenchymal components, consisting of endometrioid glands with variable architectural and cytologic atypia, squamous morules, and a fibromuscular stroma. The glandular component demonstrated irregular, angulated contours with a haphazard arrangement (Figure 1A). The squamous morules varied in size and were found to partially or completely fill, or project into, the glandular lumen (Figure 1B). The glandular epithelial cells generally showed minimal to mild cytological atypia (Figure 1C). The stromal component was composed of bland spindle cells arranged in fascicles (Figure 1D). Mild to moderate stromal inflammation and desmoplastic reactions were present in some cases but were focal and limited. Several cases showed glandular crowding and an increased gland-to-stroma ratio suggestive of endometrial hyperplasia (Figure 1E); however, overt back-to-back glandular arrangement, cribriform architecture, or moderate-to-severe cytologic atypia were absent (Figure 1F).

The histological features of AH/EIN and EC associated with APA are shown in Figure 2. AH/EIN was characterized by prominent glandular crowding (gland-to-stroma ratio >1), complex architecture, and cytological atypia distinct from the adjacent non-atypical glandular epithelium (Figure 2A). AH/EIN involvement was typically limited to scattered microscopic foci. EC exhibited greater architectural complexity, including intraglandular papillary growth and back-to-back glands (Figure 2B). In some cases, EC included non-APA endometrial tissue fragments. Cytological atypia in EC was clearly distinguishable from that in benign endometrial glands (Figure 2C). Cribriform architecture was observed in cases of coexisting EC (Figure 2D). Squamous morules, predominantly non-keratinizing, were present throughout APA, AH/EIN, and EC. Focal stromal inflammation (Figure 2E) and desmoplasia were also observed in some cases.

The frequencies of histological parameters in APA and associated lesions are summarized in Table II. Irregular angulated glands with haphazard distribution were consistently present in the APA regions. Confluent glandular proliferation (focal or diffuse) was observed in all cases. Squamous morules were also present in all cases, comprising 5-60% of the tumor area. Compared to AH/EIN-associated cases, EC-associated cases more frequently exhibited cribriform architecture (6/12, 50%), intraluminal papillary growth (5/12, 41.7%), and stromal desmoplasia (2/12, 16.7%). No non-morular solid epithelial growth was observed. Stromal inflammation was generally patchy and minimal (12/16, 75.0%), with moderate lymphocytic infiltrates in two cases (2/16, 12.5%). Stromal desmoplasia was identified in two EC-associated cases and was detectable only at intermediate to high magnification. Additional stromal changes included focal myxoid stroma in two EC-associated APA cases (2/12, 16.7%) and stromal edema in two AH/EIN-associated APA cases (2/4, 50.0%). Among the 16 patients with evaluable non-APA endometrial tissue, 10 contained adequate tissue; of these, four revealed EC and two showed AH/EIN in areas distinct from APA.

Discussion

We characterized the clinicopathological features and post-treatment outcomes of APA, with particular emphasis on its concurrent association with AH/EIN and EC. Most patients were ≤45 years (median=39 years) and most commonly presented with vaginal bleeding or dysmenorrhea, typically associated with a solitary polypoid mass. These findings are consistent with the well-established clinical profile of APA. In a large retrospective study, the mean age of patients with APA was 39.3 years, and abnormal uterine bleeding was reported in 70.9% of cases (18). In many cases, APA was initially misinterpreted as a submucosal leiomyoma or endometrial polyp on ultrasonography or hysteroscopy, leading to procedures such as myomectomies or polypectomies, which are frequently encountered among these patients (18,19). Both the clinical presentation and gross morphology of APA are nonspecific, underscoring the importance of histological confirmation for accurate diagnosis. Notably, a subset of our patients exhibited atypical presentations, including relatively large APA lesions (2.3-4 cm) identified incidentally on curettage specimens, or masses with intramural or endophytic growth patterns rather than the typical intracavitary polypoid morphology. These findings highlight the importance of considering APA in the differential diagnosis, even when imaging or hysteroscopic findings are not characteristic. Of particular clinical relevance, distinguishing APA from well-differentiated EC can be challenging, especially when only limited biopsy material is available. Furthermore, neoplastic foci were frequently identified both within the APA and in the adjacent non-APA endometrium, emphasizing the need for thorough sampling of the background endometrium, even after complete excision of the polypoid lesion. In one patient, initial endometrial curettage revealed AH/EIN involving the APA, whereas a second curettage performed one month later revealed EC in the non-APA endometrial tissue. As sampling of non-APA endometrial fragments is common in specimens containing APA, meticulous histological evaluation of these areas is essential to identify coexisting AH/EIN or EC, which directly affects both clinical diagnosis and management.

Recommendations for the management of APA accompanied by AH/EIN or EC are not yet well established. In a quantitative systematic review, recurrence and malignant progression rates among conservatively managed women were reported to be 28.9% and 16.6%, respectively (8). A separate meta-analysis of 296 cases demonstrated that complete hysteroscopic resection significantly reduced the recurrence rate compared with curettage and lowered the five-year incidence of newly diagnosed carcinomas (7). In contrast, neither this study nor an additional systematic review (20) demonstrated a consistent benefit of progestin therapy alone, likely due to small sample sizes and heterogeneity among studies. In the present study, among 14 patients with EC, none of the six who underwent hysterectomy experienced recurrence, supporting surgical management as an appropriate and effective approach in such cases (21). Conversely, among the eight patients managed conservatively with high-dose progestin therapy, four exhibited persistent EC or AH/EIN on follow-up curettage at three months. These findings suggest that fertility-preserving treatments may carry a higher risk of residual or recurrent disease in patients with APA (21), thereby underscoring the need for close clinical monitoring and repeated histological evaluation. Nonetheless, all four patients continued progestin therapy, and no residual tumor was identified at six months, indicating that conservative treatment can remain a viable option in carefully selected patients - particularly when hysteroscopic resection or polypectomy is complete, progestin therapy is well tolerated, and close endometrial surveillance is maintained. Ultimately, when fertility preservation is no longer desired or oncological safety cannot be adequately ensured, hysterectomy remains the most definitive and reliable therapeutic approach.

Several studies have investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying endometrioid carcinogenesis arising from uterine APA. Ota et al. (22) reported MutL homolog 1 (MLH1) promoter hypermethylation accompanied by the loss of MLH1 protein expression in two of six APA cases. Notably, none of the APA cases exhibited microsatellite instability (MSI), which is consistent with previous observations that AH/EIN with coexisting EC showed focal loss of MLH1 immunoreactivity in the absence of an MSI phenotype (23). MLH1 promoter hypermethylation is a well-established early event in both AH/EIN and EC, often preceding the development of overt MSI (23). The presence of MLH1 promoter hypermethylation without MSI in a subset of APA cases, along with similar findings in AH/EIN and EC, suggests that defective mismatch repair (MMR) may represent an early molecular alteration in the progression from APA to AH/EIN or EC. These findings support the hypothesis that APA may function as precursor lesions in the MMR-deficient pathway of endometrioid tumorigenesis. In contrast, a comprehensive immunohistochemical and molecular analysis of 21 APA cases by Němejcová et al. (24) revealed no loss of MMR protein expression. Instead, frequent alterations were identified in β-catenin protein expression and mutations in phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) - genes commonly mutated in EC. These results argue against a major role for MMR deficiency in the premalignant or malignant transformation of APA. Accordingly, further large-scale studies are warranted to clarify the role of MMR deficiency and MSI in APA and their potential progression to AH/EIN and EC. Alterations in the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway have also been proposed as potential molecular links among APA, AH/EIN, and EC (25,26). Supporting this hypothesis, elevated expression of phosphorylated mTOR was observed in 17 of 19 APA cases (24), suggesting that activation of the mTOR pathway in APA may contribute to the development of AH/EIN and EC. However, further research is required to elucidate the precise significance of mTOR signaling in endometrial neoplastic lesions. Additionally, Němejcová et al. (24) reported increased expression of hepatocyte nuclear factor 1β (HNF1β) and glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1) in the glandular component of a small subset of APA cases. While these findings may indicate a potential molecular association between APA, AH/EIN, and EC, it is important to note that up-regulation of HNF1β and GLUT1 is nonspecific and has been observed in various endometrial pathologies (27-30).

Longacre et al. first proposed the term “APA of low malignant potential” to describe lesions exhibiting markedly complex glandular architecture (14). Subsequent studies have supported the clinicopathological relevance of this concept. In a systematic review, APA was found to be associated with AH and EIN in 5.5% and 5.9% of cases, respectively (8). A more recent study reported concurrent AH/EIN in 18.2% and EC in 10.8% of APA cases, with a recurrence rate of 20% and a malignant transformation rate of 9.1% (18). These findings suggest that APA may coexist with or precede AH/EIN or EC, and that recurrence and malignant transformation are not uncommon, supporting its potential for malignancy. Furthermore, squamous morules, frequently observed in APA, have been associated with CTNNB1 (β-catenin) mutations (31). These mutations result in nuclear accumulation of β-catenin and activation of the Wnt signaling pathway. Through this mechanism, CTNNB1 mutations have been implicated in the progression of various malignancies, including EC (32). Such molecular findings lend additional support to the concept of APA as lesions with low malignant potential. Further research integrating histopathological, immunophenotypic, and molecular data is warranted to better define the biological behavior and prognostic significance of APA. A more precise determination of its malignant potential is needed, and the adoption of a diagnostic designation such as “APA of low malignant potential” may be justified in light of the accumulating histopathological and molecular evidence.

Histologically, the glandular component of APA consists of endometrioid-type glands exhibiting abnormal architectural complexity and varying degrees of cytological atypia (16), which can lead to confusion with myoinvasive endometrial carcinoma. Longacre et al. (14) reported that the glandular complexity of APA was indistinguishable from that of well-differentiated EC in 25 of 55 cases. This distinction is particularly challenging in curettage specimens, where tissue fragmentation may obscure key diagnostic features (19). Squamous morules, considered a hallmark of APA, are commonly observed and may assist in distinguishing APA from other uncommon benign endometrial lesions. However, morular metaplasia can mimic solid growth patterns and potentially confound interpretations. Several histological features can aid in differentiating APA from well-differentiated EC, particularly in fragmented or diagnostically challenging specimens. APA typically demonstrates architecturally complex but non-infiltrative glands embedded within a prominent myofibromatous stroma. In contrast, well-differentiated EC often exhibits confluent proliferation of tightly packed glands with minimal intervening stroma and may show stromal or myometrial invasion. Squamous morules are more frequently and prominently observed in APA than in EC. Cytologic atypia in APA is usually mild to moderate, with relatively bland nuclei and preserved nuclear polarity, which contrasts with the more pronounced nuclear atypia, loss of polarity, and conspicuous nucleoli characteristic of EC. Mitotic activity is typically low in APA, whereas EC often demonstrates a higher proliferative index. Tumor-type necrosis, commonly observed in EC, is typically absent in APA. Grossly, APA usually presents as a well-circumscribed polypoid lesion without true myometrial infiltration. In contrast, EC more often displays an irregular, infiltrative growth pattern with destructive stromal invasion and, although less common, a desmoplastic stromal reaction. In this study, cribriform architecture, intraluminal papillary growth, and stromal desmoplasia were observed exclusively in EC-associated APA. However, the diagnostic reliability of these features in distinguishing EC from AH/EIN in routine practice remains uncertain. Importantly, regardless of their association with APA, differentiating EC from AH/EIN remains both subjective and challenging. Therefore, to improve diagnostic accuracy and reproducibility, it is essential to rigorously apply standardized diagnostic criteria and seek peer consultation or consensus review in difficult cases.

Study limitations. As a single-institution study, it benefits from the application of consistent diagnostic criteria and a comprehensive histologic review. However, the patient cohort may be subject to selection bias due to referral patterns and limited access to tertiary care centers. While some patients showed no evidence of residual disease during long-term follow-up, extended observation may be necessary for those with relatively short follow-up durations. Future multi-institutional studies with larger patient cohorts and longer follow-up periods are warranted to validate our findings and better assess long-term clinical outcomes. Although a comprehensive ancillary analysis comparing the immunophenotypic and mutational profiles of APA and EC is beyond the scope of this study, we believe that documenting the frequency of AH/EIN and EC in association with the rare APA entity, along with an evaluation of treatment outcomes, represents a meaningful contribution to the literature.

Conclusion

This study highlights the diverse clinicopathological spectrum of APA, with particular emphasis on its frequent association with AH/EIN and EC. Histologically, features such as cribriform architecture, intraluminal papillary growth, and stromal desmoplasia may support the diagnosis of EC; however, these findings are not definitive in isolation and must be interpreted within the broader histopathological context. Our findings underscore the importance of meticulous histological evaluation of both APA lesions and the adjacent non-APA endometrium to identify coexisting AH/EIN or EC. In our cohort, hysterectomy resulted in disease-free outcomes in all patients with surgically treated EC. In contrast, a subset of patients managed conservatively with high-dose progestin therapy demonstrated persistent disease during follow-up, highlighting the potential risk of residual or recurrent lesions in conservatively treated individuals. These observations reinforce the need for accurate diagnosis, careful patient selection, and close clinical and pathological follow-up to optimize outcomes in patients with APA and its associated lesions.
