LncRNA FTX/OCT4/miR-122-5p orchestrates self-renewal and lineage commitment of human dental pulp stem cells by directly targeting FOXO3
Authors: Kai Zeng, Lijing Wu, Weini Xin, Chen Cui, Sui Mai, Hong Hong, Xi Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs) possess multipotent properties and play a pivotal role in tissue regeneration. Elucidating the molecular mechanisms governing hDPSC pluripotency is essential for advancing their clinical application. We previously demonstrated that long noncoding RNA five prime to Xist (lncRNA FTX) suppresses the proliferation and multilineage differentiation capacity of hDPSCs by inhibiting octamer-binding transcription factor 4 (OCT4). This study aims to explore the molecular mechanisms by which FTX regulates hDPSC proliferation/differentiation via downstream miRNAs and their biological implications.MethodsSmall RNA sequencing was utilized to identify differentially expressed miRNAs between the FTX-overexpressing and vector hDPSCs. Bioinformatic analysis, luciferase assay, and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) were employed to elucidate the regulatory network of the FTX/OCT4/miR-122-5p axis. Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), western blotting, alizarin red staining, and oil red O staining were performed to validate the functional roles of miR-122-5p and forkhead box O3 (FOXO3) in FTX-mediated proliferation and differentiation potential in hDPSCs. Furthermore, the effect of FTX/miR-122-5p on dentin formation was assessed using a subcutaneous implantation model.ResultsSequencing revealed 115 differentially expressed miRNAs (26 upregulated and 89 downregulated; fold change ≥ 1.5, P < 0.05). miR-122-5p displayed a significant decrease in expression in FTX-overexpressing hDPSCs. Overexpression of miR-122-5p partially alleviated FTX’s inhibitory effect on hDPSC proliferation and differentiation. Cotransfection experiments demonstrated that FTX overexpression impaired the pluripotency of hDPSCs in part through miR-122-5p-mediated regulation of FOXO3 in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, FTX specifically suppressed OCT4 expression, which led to the transcriptional inactivation of miR-122-5p. Furthermore, upregulation of FTX resulted in enhanced expression levels of FOXO3, an effect that could be counteracted by miR-122-5p.ConclusionsLncRNA FTX overexpression exerts a suppressive effect on the transcriptional expression of miR-122-5p, which subsequently activates FOXO3 and thereby impedes cell proliferation as well as multilineage differentiation capacity of hDPSCs. Moreover, a novel lncRNA FTX-OCT4-miR-122-5p interaction pathway was discovered. These findings shed light on two novel regulatory mechanisms – lncRNA FTX/OCT4/miR-122-5p regulation and lncRNA FTX/miR-122-5p/FOXO3 regulation – that unravel crucial signaling pathways governing the pluripotency of hDPSCs, potentially offering promising RNA-based therapeutic strategies and gene-editing targets for dentin regeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04685-9.Keywords: LncRNA FTX/OCT4/miR-122-5p, LncRNA FTX/miR-122-5p/FOXO3 , Dental pulp stem cell, Pluripotency

Background

Human dental pulp stem cells (hDPSCs), which originate from neural crest cells and reside in the microvasculature region of the dental pulp, exhibit self-renewal capabilities and have the potential to differentiate into various cell types, including osteoblasts, odontoblasts, adipocytes, and neural cells [1]. Compared with other mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) sourced from bone marrow, umbilical cord blood, adipose tissue, and peripheral blood, DPSCs have the advantages of easy obtainability, limited ethical concerns, and superior therapeutic effects on neuroprotective treatment [2], bone repair [3], liver regeneration [4], etc., making them promising sources for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, particularly for dental pulp repair. Elucidating the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying the determination of the cell fate of hDPSCs is imperative for further exploration.

The proliferation and differentiation processes of stem cells are orchestrated by a spectrum of signaling events, including lncRNAs [5], growth factors [6], inflammatory factors [7], and microRNAs [8]. LncRNAs are a class of noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs) that are generally longer than 200 nucleotides, transcribed by RNA polymerase II (Pol II), and typically lack protein-coding potential [9]. An expanding corpus of literature supports that lncRNAs play key roles in biological processes including stem cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation through diverse molecular mechanisms, such as transcriptional regulation, post-transcriptional processing, chromatin remodeling, and modulation of protein activity [10–12]. Long non-coding RNA five prime to Xist (lncRNA FTX), a 2356-bp transcript encoded by a conserved sequence of the X chromosome inactivation center [13, 14], has been demonstrated to be an essential regulator of the proliferation and metastasis of different cancer cells [15–17] and is closely associated with apoptosis in adult cells [18, 19] and tissue injury repair processes [20, 21]. Our previous study revealed that lncRNA FTX exerts a significant inhibitory effect on the proliferation and multilineage differentiation potential of hDPSCs, and participated in a negative feedback loop with the core transcription factor (TF) OCT4 to finely regulate the pluripotency homeostasis of hDPSCs [22], suggesting that it is a potential target for optimizing dental-derived stem cell sources in regenerative endodontics. Nonetheless, the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying its regulatory function have yet to be fully elucidated.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs/miRs), which are a class of highly conserved small non-coding RNAs with a length of approximately 20–24 nucleotides, play crucial roles in determining the fate of stem cells [23, 24] and hold significant potential as a therapeutic option in regenerative dentistry [25]. As important regulatory molecules, miRNAs engage in complex interactions with lncRNAs and TFs, thereby influencing gene regulatory networks associated with physiological processes [26, 27] and human diseases [28, 29]. Numerous studies have established that FTX functions as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by sponging various miRNAs in both cancerous and non-cancerous human cells [30–33]. Additionally, the lncRNA-miRNA axis has emerged as a pivotal regulatory mechanism significantly impacting the differentiation and functional properties of hDPSCs [34, 35]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that miRNAs may occupy a central role within the molecular network of lncRNA FTX in regulating hDPSC pluripotency. A deeper understanding of lncRNA FTX, its functions, and its interactions with miRNAs may facilitate the identification of superior gene-editing targets, thereby enhancing the safety and specificity of RNA-based therapies for dental pulp regeneration. In this study, small RNA sequencing was employed to identify key miRNAs involved in the regulation of hDPSC pluripotency mediated by FTX. Furthermore, loss-of-function and gain-of-function approaches were utilized to ascertain the regulatory effects and corresponding mechanisms of lncRNA FTX, candidate miRNAs, and their target genes. Finally, a heterotopic dentin formation assay was conducted to confirm the role of lncRNA FTX in the odontoblastic differentiation of hDPSCs in vivo.

Methods

Isolation and culture of hDPSCs

The hDPSCs utilized in this study were freshly isolated from caries-free, intact premolars extracted for orthodontic treatment or third molars of patients aged 18–25 years at the Hospital of Stomatology, Sun Yat-sen University. Prior to their inclusion, informed consent was obtained from all donors. Experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Review Board (Approval No.: KQEC-2022021-01). Pulp tissues were meticulously dissected and minced into approximately 1 mm2 fragments, followed by enzymatic digestion with a cocktail of 3 mg/mL type I collagenase (Sigma, USA) and 4 mg/mL dispase (Sigma, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C. The resulting cellular suspension was then cultured in Minimum Essential Medium, Alpha Modification (αMEM, Gibco, USA), supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37℃ in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells from passages three to five were used for subsequent experiments.

Flow cytometry assay

hDPSCs at passage 3 were identified by their surface antigens using a flow cytometry method. The cells were first trypsinized and subsequently incubated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Servicebio, China) with fluorescein-conjugated monoclonal antibodies specific for CD29-PE (Invitrogen, USA), CD44-APC-Cy7 (Tonbo Biosciences, USA), CD73-FITC (Invitrogen, USA), CD90-APC (Invitrogen, USA), CD105-PE (Invitrogen, USA), CD34-FITC (Invitrogen, USA) and CD45-PE-Cy7 (Invitrogen, USA) for 30 min, adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following incubation, flow cytometry analysis was performed using a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Inc., USA).

Cell transfection

The cDNA encoding lncRNA FTX was sequentially amplified and cloned into a lentiviral shuttle vector pHBLV-CMV-MCS-3FLAG-EF1-ZsGreen-T2A-PURO by Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China), which was subsequently used to generate a lncRNA FTX-overexpressing recombinant lentivirus (FTX) in 293T cells. A corresponding control lentivirus, FTX overexpression negative control (FNC), was also constructed. For transfection into hDPSCs, the cells were exposed to a mixture of 2 mL αMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 4 µg/mL polybrene (Hanbio, China) for 24 h, along with the respective lentiviruses. Following a week of culture in the presence of 1 µg/mL puromycin (Hanbio, China), the overexpression efficiency was assessed using qRT-PCR.

To regulate miR-122-5p expression in hDPSCs, the miR-122-5p mimic (Product Number: miR10000421-1-5), mimic negative control (mimic NC) (Product Number: miR1N0000002-1-5), miR-122-5p inhibitor (Product Number: miR20000421-1-5) and inhibitor negative control (inhibitor NC) (Product Number: miR2N0000002-1-5) were custom-designed and synthesized by RiboBio Inc. (China). To manipulate FOXO3 levels, small interfering RNAs targeting FOXO3 (sense, 5′-GCUGUCUCCAUGGACAAUATT-3′; antisense, 5′- UAUUGUCCAUGGAGACAGCTT-3′) along with a negative control (NC; sense, 5′- UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′; antisense, 5′- ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′) were procured from Genepharma Inc. (China). Prior to transfection, hDPSCs were seeded in six-well plates and allowed to grow for 24 h. The transfection process involved the addition of Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, USA) along with 50 nM miR-122-5p mimic, 50 nM mimic NC, 100 nM miR-122-5p inhibitor, 100 nM inhibitor NC, 50 nM FOXO3 siRNA or 50 nM NC, as per the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection for a duration of 6 to 8 h, the culture medium was replaced by 2 mL αMEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

Small RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA samples from 3 patients per group were pooled into one sample for RNA-sequencing. The quantity and quality of the extracted RNA were assessed via a NanoDrop ND-2000 (Nanodrop Technologies, USA). Small RNA sequencing was subsequently performed by RiboBio Company (China) to identify the differentially expressed miRNAs in hDPSCs between the lncRNA FTX-overexpressing group and the corresponding vector group. Differentially expressed miRNAs were identified based on fold change (|FC| >1.5) and statistical significance P < 0.05.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

hDPSCs were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells per well. Following incubation for 1, 3, 5 and 7 days, each well was supplemented with 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) and then cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was subsequently accurately determined via a Bio-Rad microplate reader (USA).

Odontoblastic differentiation induction of hDPSCs

hDPSCs were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. Once the cells reached 70% confluence, the culture medium was exchanged with odontoblastic differentiation medium containing αMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate (Sigma, USA),10− 7 mol/L dexamethasone (Sigma, USA), and 50 mg/mL ascorbic acid (Sigma, USA) for 14 days. The medium was replenished every 3 days to ensure optimal conditions.

For alizarin red staining, fixed hDPSCs were treated with alizarin red solution (Cyagen Biosciences, China) for 3–5 min and visualized via an inverted microscope (Axiovert, Zeiss, Germany). For quantification, mineral nodules were dissolved in 10% cetylpyridinium chloride solution (Sigma, USA) for 30 min and the resulting absorbance at 562 nm was measured.

Adipogenic differentiation induction of hDPSCs

To induce adipogenic differentiation, hDPSCs were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. When hDPSCs reached 90% confluence, the culture medium was exchanged with adipogenic induction medium composed of αMEM enriched with 0.5 mM 3-isobatyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX; Sigma, USA), 1 mM dexamethasone, 10 mg/mL insulin (GIBCO-BRL, Life Technologies, USA), 100 mM indomethacin (Sigma, USA), and 15% FBS for 3 days, followed by αMEM supplemented with 10 mg/mL insulin and 15% FBS for 1 day. hDPSCs were cultured in adipogenic differentiation medium for a total of 21 days.

For oil red O staining, the fixed hDPSCs were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with oil red O solution (Cyagen Biosciences, China) for thirty minutes. Lipid droplets were visualized under an inverted microscope (Axiovert; Zeiss, Germany).

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was efficiently extracted from cultured cells utilizing the RNAzol reagent (GeneCopoeia, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription of both lncRNAs and mRNAs was performed via the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). qRT-PCR was carried out via a LightCycler®480 SYBR Green I Master Kit (Roche, Switzerland) to measure RNA expression levels. For miRNAs detection, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis and qPCR were performed via the All-in-One™ miRNA qRT-PCR Detection Kit (GeneCopoeia, USA). Primers targeting miR-122-5p (HmiRQP0056), miR-17-5p (HmiRQP0230), miR-335-5p (HmiRQP0421), miR-140-5p (HmiRQP0181) and miR-143-3p (HmiRQP0188) were purchased from GeneCopoeia Inc. (USA). Primer sequences used for amplification are listed in Table 1. β-actin served as an internal control for lncRNAs and mRNAs normalization, and U6 was used as a reference gene for miRNAs normalization. Relative gene expression was calculated employing the 2− ΔΔCq method.

Western blot analysis

Total proteins were extracted from cells via radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) on ice for 30 min. Following centrifugation at 4 °C and 14,000 g for 20 min, the clarified supernatant was collected. The concentration of total protein was subsequently measured via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of proteins from various groups were separated by 4–20% Bis-Tris SDS-PAGE gels (Invitrogen, USA) and subsequently transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After being blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Amresco, USA), the membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with the following diluted primary antibodies: OCN (1:1000, Affinity, China), OPN (1:1000, Santa Cruz, USA), DSPP (1:1000, ZENBIO, China), LPL (1:1000, Santa Cruz, USA), PPARγ-2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), OCT4 (1:1000, Abcam, UK), GAPDH (1:1000, EMAR, China), Vinculin (1:100, Santa Cruz, USA), FOXO3 (1:1000, Affinity, China), β-actin (1:1000, EMAR, China). After washing with Tris-buffered saline-Tween 20 (TBST, CWBio, China) three times, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies specific for rabbit (1:5000, EMAR, China) or mouse (1:5000, EMAR, China) for one hour at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized via the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore Corporation, USA). ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) was employed to quantify the intensities of the protein bands.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The pmirGLO luciferase reporter vectors, harboring either the 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR) sequence of FTX or FOXO3, alongside their respective mutant variants, were synthesized by HC-Bio (China). The pmirGLO vectors (comprising wild-type fragments or mutated fragments) were subsequently cotransfected into 293T cells with the miR-122-5p mimic or mimic NC via Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was measured via the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) and normalized against Renilla luciferase activity according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) with quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed via ChIP kits (HC-ChIP, HC-Bio, China). Briefly, hDPSCs were treated with 37% formaldehyde for 10 min and then exposed to 1.375 M glycine. Ultrasonic sonication was then employed to fragment the chromatin into 200–600 bp fragments. The cell lysates were incubated with an anti-OCT4 antibody (Abcam, UK) to immunoprecipitate the DNA-protein complexes. Normal rabbit IgG was used as a control. The eluted DNA was then quantified by qPCR using the primers listed in Table 2.

In vivo heterotopic dentin formation assay

All animal care and experimental procedures were rigorously reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No.: SYSU-IACUC-2021-000266), ensuring compliance with ethical standards and established guidelines. Six-week-old BALB/c nude mice, sourced from the Laboratory Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University, were utilized in this study. The hDPSCs (5 × 106 cells/mL) were seeded onto hydroxyapatite/tricalcium phosphate scaffolds (HA/TCP, Engineering Research Centre in Biomaterials, Sichuan University, China), which were subsequently divided into four distinct experimental groups: the FTX + mimic group, the FTX + mimic NC group, the FNC + mimic group, and the FNC + mimic NC group. After one week of culture in an odontoblastic differentiation medium, the hDPSC-laden scaffolds were implanted subcutaneously into the backs of nude mice. The mice were euthanized, and the samples were harvested eight weeks after implantation. To observe cell adhesion, the scaffolds loaded with hDPSCs were meticulously examined under a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi, Japan).

Masson’s trichrome staining

After eight weeks, the implants were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China), and subjected to a two-week decalcification process using 10% edetic acid (pH 7.4). Following decalcification, the transplants were embedded in paraffin, sliced into 3 μm thick sections, and subjected to Masson’s trichrome staining (Servicebio, China). Briefly, the tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated with xylene and graded concentrations of ethanol. The slides were then mordanted in potassium dichromate for 30 min. Next, the sections were stained with Weigert’s hematoxylin for one minute followed by Ponceau red liquid dye acid complex staining for six minutes; and then differentiated with a phosphomolybdic acid solution for one minute. Finally, the tissue was meticulously stained with aniline blue liquid and acetic acid for one minute. The slides were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and dehydrated before being observed with a microscope (ZEISS, Germany).

Immunofluorescence analysis

For immunofluorescence staining, the tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated according to the same protocol employed for Masson’s trichrome staining. Following washing with PBS, the sections were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, China) in PBS for 10 min to facilitate antibody penetration. Subsequently, blocking was achieved with 10% goat serum (Bosterbio, USA) in PBS for 30 min to minimize nonspecific binding. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specifically targeting DSPP (1:500, Servicebio, China) or FOXO3 (1:200, Proteintech, USA). Following this primary antibody incubation, the sections were subjected to incubation with cyanine 3-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100, Servicebio, China) for one hour at room temperature. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:100, Servicebio, China) for five minutes. Fluorescence images were captured via a fluorescence microscope (Olympus FV300, Japan), and the mean fluorescence intensity of the immunofluorescence-stained sections was quantitatively analyzed via ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as the means ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three individual experiments, and statistical evaluations were conducted via SPSS 26.0 statistical software (IBM Corp, USA). The distribution of the data was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test and Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Data obeying normal distribution and homogeneity of variance between two groups were compared via an unpaired Student’s t-test. For multiple comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of hDPSCs

The hDPSCs were successfully isolated and characterized by a spindle-like morphology (Fig. 1A-D). The flow cytometry results revealed that the isolated hDPSCs were positive for the MSC surface markers CD29 (98.49%), CD44 (98.46%), CD73 (95.75%), CD90 (98.96%), and CD105 (99.43%) but negative for the hematopoietic marker CD34 (0.43%) and CD45 (2.69%) (Fig. 1E). CCK-8 assays indicated the robust self-renewal capacity of hDPSCs (Fig. 1F). Moreover, successful induction confirmed the multilineage differentiation potential of hDPSCs into osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic lineages (Fig. 1G-I).

Small RNA sequencing analyses of miRNAs in FTX-overexpressing hDPSCs

To identify the specific miRNA implicated in the functional role of lncRNA FTX, small RNA high-throughput sequencing was utilized to compare the expression patterns of miRNAs between FTX-overexpressing and control hDPSCs (Fig. S1). A total of 115 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified between the FTX and FNC groups, with 26 upregulated and 89 downregulated (Fig. 2A, B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis were utilized to assess the functional roles of these differentially expressed miRNAs-related mRNAs in FTX-overexpressing hDPSCs (Fig. S2). GO analysis revealed that the coexpressed mRNAs associated with these differentially expressed miRNAs primarily participated in biological processes such as cell proliferation, cell cycle, cell differentiation, and signal transduction (Fig. S2A-C). KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the coexpressed mRNAs related to these differentially expressed miRNAs were enriched in various pathways, including FOXO signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, cell senescence, and regulation of pluripotency (Fig. S2D).

To verify the sequencing results, five miRNAs (miR-122-5p, miR-17-5p, miR-335-5p, miR-140-5p, and miR-143-3p), which may participate in FOXO signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway according to bioinformatics analysis and were proved to be involved in the regulation of stem cell pluripotency, were selected and analyzed via qRT-PCR (Fig. 2C, D). The results confirmed consistency with the sequencing data (Fig. 2D). Additionally, miR-122-5p was significantly downregulated in FTX-overexpressing groups compared to miR-17-5p and miR-335-5p (Fig. 2D, E). Notably, miR-122-5p has been functionally implicated in directing lineage commitment of mesenchymal stem cells by modulating downstream signals [36, 37]. The alignment of our differential expression profile with published evidence strongly supports miR-122-5p as a key mediator of DPSC proliferation and differentiation dynamics. Based on this rationale, we focused on miR-122-5p for further investigation.

miR-122-5p promotes the proliferation and multidirectional differentiation capabilities of hDPSCs

To investigate the biological function of miR-122-5p, hDPSCs were transfected with the miR-122-5p mimic or miR-122-5p inhibitor (Fig. 3A, B). The proliferation of hDPSCs was significantly greater in the miR-122-5p overexpression group than in the mimic NC group, as demonstrated by the results of the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 3C). Moreover, upregulation of miR-122-5p led to a significant increase in the expression levels of the odontoblastic markers OCN, OPN and DSPP (Fig. 3E-G). The expression of the adipogenic markers LPL and PPARγ-2 in the group with miR-122-5p overexpression was also markedly elevated (Fig. 3M-O). Furthermore, compared with those in the mimic NC group, hDPSCs overexpressing miR-122-5p presented an increased number of mineral nodules or lipid droplets (Fig. 3H, P). In contrast, inhibition of miR-122-5p compromised the proliferation and multilineage differentiation capacity of hDPSCs, as evidenced by reduced cell proliferation rates, diminished formation of mineralized nodules and lipid droplets, as well as downregulated expression levels of odontoblastic and adipogenic marker genes (Fig. 3D, I-L, Q-T). Collectively, these findings suggest that miR‑122‑5p enhances both the proliferation and odontoblastic/adipogenic differentiation potential of hDPSCs.

LncRNA FTX inhibits the proliferation and differentiation of hDPSCs by downregulating miR-122-5p expression

To further ascertain the impact of FTX on the cellular functions of hDPSCs through the regulation of miR-122-5p expression, miR-122-5p mimic was cotransfected into FTX-overexpressing hDPSCs. The results of the CCK-8 assay showed a significant decrease in proliferation activity in FTX-overexpressing hDPSCs, which was partially alleviated by cotransfection with the miR-122-5p mimic (Fig. 4A). The qRT-PCR and western blot assays revealed that the overexpression of FTX significantly downregulated the mRNA and protein expression levels of the odontoblastic markers OCN, OPN, and DSPP, as well as the adipogenic markers LPL and PPARγ-2. However, the expression of these markers was restored upon transfection with the miR-122-5p mimic (Fig. 4D-F, H-J). Alizarin red staining and oil red O staining confirmed that the miR-122-5p mimic also relieved the inhibitory effect exerted by lncRNA FTX on mineralized nodule formation and lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 4B, C, G). These results indicate that the suppressive effects of lncRNA FTX on the proliferation and odontoblastic/adipogenic differentiation of hDPSCs can be rescued by miR-122-5p.

LncRNA FTX transcriptionally suppresses miR-122-5p expression in hDPSCs

Numerous studies support the role of lncRNAs as ceRNAs, which competitively bind to miRNAs and consequently impact their expression [38, 39]. Therefore, we investigated whether FTX regulates miR-122-5p expression via this ceRNA mechanism. The potential binding sites between FTX and miR-122-5p were predicted (Fig. 5A). FTX luciferase vectors harboring either wild-type (FTX-wt) or mutated (FTX-mut) miR-122-5p binding sites were then cotransfected with either the miR-122-5p mimic or mimic NC into 293T cells. The results revealed that neither the transfection of miR‑122‑5p mimic nor negative control had a significant effect on the relative luciferase activity associated with both the wild-type and mutant forms of FTX constructs (Fig. 5B), suggesting an alternate mechanism for the FTX-mediated downregulation of miR‑122‑5p expression.

To further clarify the precise step in miR-122-5p biogenesis regulated by FTX, we detected the expression levels of miR-122-5p precursors (pri-miR-122-5p and pre-miR-122-5p) in FTX-overexpressing hDPSCs compared with those in control cells. The qRT-PCR results showed a significant reduction in pri-miR-122-5p and pre-miR-122-5p expression upon FTX overexpression (Fig. 5C), suggesting that FTX transcriptionally downregulates miR-122-5p expression. Given previous reports implicating lncRNAs in gene regulation through interactions with TFs [40], PROMO and the JASPAR algorithm were employed to predict potential TFs that could interact with the promoter region of miR-122-5p. Notably, among these predicted TFs, OCT4 was of particular interest due to its well-established critical role in governing stem cell pluripotency and its repression by FTX overexpression in hDPSCs as reported in our previous study [22] and in the supplementary material (Fig. S3). Therefore, we investigated whether OCT4 regulates the expression of miR-122-5p and explored the underlying mechanism involved. The predicted binding sites (rank 1 to 2) between OCT4 and the promoter region of miR-122-5p are shown in Fig. 5D and were validated via ChIP-qPCR assay. The data indicated the enrichment of immunoprecipitates from anti-OCT4 antibodies at predicted binding site 1, site 2, as well as site 1and 2 within the DNA fragments of miR‑122‑5p promoter compared with the isotype antibody control (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, lentiviral-mediated silencing of OCT4 expression was conducted in hDPSCs (Fig. S4). The qRT-PCR assay results revealed significantly reduced expression levels of pri-miR-122-5p, pre-miR-122-5p, and miR-122-5p upon OCT4 repression (Fig. 5F). These data indicate that lncRNA FTX downregulates the expression of OCT4, which subsequently binds to the miR-122-5p promoter region, thereby inhibiting the transcriptional activity of miR-122-5p.

FTX positively modulates FOXO3 expression through miR-122-5p

Having established that FTX could negatively regulate miR-122-5p expression, we further investigated the downstream mechanisms by which FTX/miR-122-5p influences the pluripotency of hDPSCs. Utilizing miRAD, miRWalk, TargetScan, and miRTarBase databases, sixty-nine potential target genes for miR-122-5p were collectively predicted (Fig. 6A, B). Among these genes, FOXO3 drew our attention as a key component of the FOXO signaling pathway, which plays crucial roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, survival, senescence, DNA damage repair, and cell cycle control [41]. To confirm the direct targeting of FOXO3 by miR-122-5p, luciferase reporter detection was conducted in 293T cells cotransfected with miR-122-5p mimic and wild-type (wt) or mutant (mut) FOXO3 luciferase reporter plasmids. The data indicated specific inhibition of luciferase activity by miR-122-5p only in cells containing the wild-type FOXO3 3′UTR with no effect on the activity of cells containing the mutated FOXO3 3′UTR (Fig. 6C), suggesting that FOXO3 is indeed a potential target of miR-122-5p. Additionally, FOXO3 expression was significantly downregulated by miR-122-5p mimic but upregulated by miR-122-5p inhibitor (Fig. 6D-I). Furthermore, qRT-PCR analysis also revealed that overexpression of FTX upregulated both the transcript and protein levels of FOXO3, but this increase was attenuated by transfection with the miR‑122‑5p mimic (Fig. 6J-L). In summary, our findings confirm that FOXO3 is targeted by miR-122-5p and demonstrate how FTX positively regulates its expression in hDPSCs.

Repression of FOXO3 enhances the proliferation and differentiation capacities of hDPSCs

After confirming that FOXO3 is a direct target of miR-122-5p, we sought to gain further insights into the role of FOXO3 in the proliferation and differentiation of hDPSCs. To achieve this goal, a small interfering RNA (siRNA) was designed to effectively silence its expression. As depicted in Fig. 7A-C, transfection with siRNA significantly decreased FOXO3 expression. The CCK-8 assay results revealed a remarkable increase in cell growth following FOXO3 silencing (Fig. 7D). Additionally, results of alizarin red staining, qRT-PCR analysis, and western blot analysis demonstrated that FOXO3 knockdown significantly enhanced the odontoblastic differentiation ability of hDPSCs (Fig. 7E-I). Furthermore, silencing of FOXO3 significantly augmented the capacity for adipogenic differentiation in hDPSCs, as evidenced by oil red O staining along with qRT-PCR and western blot assays (Fig. 7J-M). These results strongly suggest that the downregulation of FOXO3 promotes both the proliferation and differentiation processes of hDPSCs.

Silencing FOXO3 counteracts the inhibitory effect of FTX on the proliferation and differentiation of hDPSCs

Rescue assays were subsequently performed to verify the involvement of FOXO3 in the FTX-mediated regulation of proliferation and differentiation in hDPSCs. Cotransfection of the FTX-overexpressing lentivirus and FOXO3 siRNA was conducted in hDPSCs. The CCK-8 assay results showed that cotransfection with FOXO3 siRNA partially alleviated the inhibitory effect of FTX on cell proliferation (Fig. 8A). Furthermore, the data demonstrated that FOXO3 knockdown rescued the suppressive effects exerted by FTX on the mRNA and protein levels of odontoblastic and adipogenic markers in hDPSCs (Fig. 8D-F, H-J). Additionally, the inhibitory effects of FTX on mineralized nodule formation and lipid droplet accumulation in hDPSCs were significantly reversed by FOXO3 siRNA (Fig. 8B, C, G). Taken together, our data suggest that lncRNA FTX inhibits the proliferation and differentiation of hDPSCs partly by impairing the miR-122-5p-dependent downregulation of FOXO3.

LncRNA FTX/miR-122-5p/FOXO3 axis regulates in vivo heterotopic dentin formation of hDPSCs

The aforementioned results demonstrate that lncRNA FTX/miR-122-5p/FOXO3 axis participates in regulating the self-renewal and multilineage differentiation potential of hDPSCs in vitro. Considering the crucial role of DPSC pluripotency, particularly their odontoblastic differentiation capability, for dental pulp repair [42] and tissue engineering [43], we further explored the effects of lncRNA FTX and miR-122-5p on odontoblastic differentiation of hDPSCs in vivo. Specifically, hDPSCs transfected with lncRNA FTX alone or in combination with miR-122-5p mimic or mimic NC were implanted onto HA/TCP scaffolds (Fig. 9A, B) and subcutaneously transplanted into the backs of nude mice. Eight weeks after scaffold transplantation, the percentage of blue-colored collagen deposits was lower in FTX-overexpressing group than in the control group but was restored by cotransfection with the miR-122-5p mimic (Fig. 9C). Moreover, the immunofluorescence staining revealed that FTX inhibited DSPP expression, whereas cotransfection of miR-122-5p mimic attenuated this inhibition (Fig. 9D). Meanwhile, consistent with our in vitro findings, immunofluorescence staining confirmed that FOXO3 expression in hDPSCs from the FTX + mimic NC group was greater than that in hDPSCs from the FNC + mimic NC group; however, this upregulation was abolished by the miR-122-5p mimic in vivo (Fig. 9E). Overall, these data suggest that FTX negatively regulates the odontoblastic differentiation of hDPSCs by modulating miR-122-5p, and identify FOXO3 as a downstream effector of the FTX/ miR-122-5p pathway in vivo.

Discussion

DPSCs are characterized by their high accessibility, robust proliferative capacity, and multilineage differentiation potential, making them an ideal cell source for dentin-pulp regeneration [44] as well as other regenerative therapies [45]. Although there is growing interest in DPSCs within the field of regenerative dentistry, the mechanisms governing their proliferation and lineage-specific differentiation are intricate and remain only partially understood, posing a significant challenge to their clinical translation. The precise genetic manipulation of key regulatory genes has emerged as a revolutionary strategy to potentiate cellular viability and enhance differentiation fidelity in dentin-pulp regeneration, as extensively demonstrated in various preclinical studies [43, 46]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms involved in modulating the status of DPSCs can contribute to the development of novel and efficient strategies for reprogramming these cells specifically for dentin-pulp regeneration.

LncRNA FTX, recognized as a regulator of X chromosome inactivation [47], has previously been implicated in the apoptosis of neurons [30], pancreatic cancer cells [48], and endometrial stromal cells (ESCs) [49]. It also plays crucial roles in spinal cord injury (SCI) repair [50], stroke [21] and ischemia-reperfusion injury [51]. Our previous study demonstrated that overexpression of FTX significantly suppressed both the proliferation and multidirectional differentiation capabilities of hDPSCs, indicating its potential to influence the fate of DPSCs and suggesting its utility as a promising target for molecular therapeutic strategies in regenerative endodontics. Recent publications have highlighted the role of the lncRNA FTX as a ceRNA that regulates cell proliferation and apoptosis [30, 33]. To verify our hypothesis that FTX exerts its regulatory function through miRNAs in hDPSCs, small RNA sequencing analysis was utilized to identify the differentially expressed miRNAs in FTX-overexpressing and vector hDPSCs. A total of 115 differentially expressed miRNAs were identified. Five miRNAs were selected for validation, and the qRT-PCR results supported the sequencing data. Among these candidates, the most intriguing miRNA appeared to be miR-122-5p due to its significant downregulation in FTX-overexpressing hDPSCs and its association with genes involved in the pathological and physiological processes of stem cells, as indicated by GO and KEGG analyses.

miR-122-5p, a liver-specific miRNA, plays a crucial role in maintaining normal liver function and is implicated in the pathogenesis of various malignant diseases [52]. Additionally, it accelerates wound healing by increasing capillary density in perilesional skin tissues [53]. Previous research has demonstrated that miR-122-5p may promote the chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs and support cartilage tissue regeneration [36]. Similarly, our findings indicated that overexpression of miR-122-5p enhances the proliferation as well as odontoblastic and adipogenic differentiation capabilities of hDPSCs, while inhibition of miR-122-5p showed an opposite trend. Functional experiments further revealed that miR-122-5p counteracts the inhibitory effects of FTX on proliferation and differentiation. To our knowledge, this study represents the first investigation into the regulatory role of the FTX/miR-122-5p axis in stem cell pluripotency and differentiation.

We subsequently investigated the potential mechanism through which FTX regulates miR-122-5p. LncRNAs exert their influence on miRNA expression through diverse mechanisms, including acting as ceRNAs, participating in chromatin remodeling complexes, serving as a source of miRNA biogenesis, and interacting with specific TFs [54]. Increasing evidence suggests that lncRNAs can negatively regulate miRNAs in hDPSCs by competing with mRNAs for binding sites [35]. In the present study, bioinformatics analysis via online prediction databases indicated potential binding between miR-122-5p and two sites within the FTX sequence. However, dual-luciferase activity assays failed to confirm the interaction between FTX and miR-122-5p, suggesting the presence of an alternative mechanism beyond the ceRNA interaction.

Mature functional miRNAs are generated through a tightly regulated series of events in which primary miRNAs (pri-miRNAs) are transcribed and cleaved by the Drosha enzyme complex into hairpin precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs) in the nucleus. These pre-miRNAs are then exported to the cytoplasm where they undergo processing by the Dicer complex to yield mature double-stranded miRNA duplexes [55]. To elucidate the stage at which FTX negatively regulates miR‑122‑5p expression, we quantitatively assessed miR-122-5p expression at each synthesis stage. Our data demonstrated that FTX suppressed both pri-miR-122-5p and pre-miR-122-5p expression, suggesting that its regulatory effect on miR‑122‑5p occurs at the transcriptional level. LncRNAs have been reported to regulate gene expression through interactions with RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) [56], including TFs that bind to enhancers and promoters, thereby facilitating the assembly of the transcription machinery for initiation [57–59]. Therefore, we utilized the online TF prediction tools JASPAR and PROMO to identify potential TFs that bind to the promoter region of miR-122-5p. Among these predicted TFs, OCT4, which is widely recognized as a pivotal regulator of stem cell pluripotency, was identified. Our previous study demonstrated the importance of OCT4 in the proliferation and differentiation of hDPSCs, and uncovered a negative feedback relationship between FTX and OCT4 [22, 60], indicating that OCT4 may function as a key modulator within the regulatory network of FTX. ChIP-qPCR confirmed the enrichment of OCT4 at the predicted binding sites of the miR-122-5p promoter compared with that of the isotype control antibody. Correspondingly, the knockdown of OCT4 resulted in a significant reduction in the expression levels of pri-miR-122-5p, pre-miR-122-5p, and mature miR-122-5p. Taken together with our previous study, these findings suggest that FTX exerted negative regulation on OCT4 expression, which in turn binds to the promoter region of miR‑122‑5p, ultimately resulting in miR-122-5p downregulation. The FTX/OCT4/miR-122-5p network expands our understanding of the intricate interplay between lncRNAs, TFs, and miRNAs in governing the self-renewal and differentiation processes of hDPSCs.

MiRNAs have been demonstrated to exert intricate biological effects by inhibiting mRNA translation or promoting mRNA degradation through binding to the 3′-UTR of target genes [61]. To elucidate the underlying mechanism of miR-122-5p, databases were employed for predicting its putative mRNA targets. Sixty-nine candidate target genes, including FOXO3, were identified as harboring potential binding sites in their 3′-UTR regions for miR-122-5p. To the best of our knowledge, the precise role of FOXO3 within DPSCs has not been previously elucidated. However, previous study has shown that FOXO3, a member of the TF family in the FOXO signaling pathway, plays a crucial role in biological processes including aging, energy metabolism, apoptosis, cell cycle regulation, and stem cell maintenance [41]. FOXO signaling pathway was found to be enriched among the target genes of differentially expressed miRNAs between the FTX-overexpressing group and the vector group based on the KEGG analysis (Fig. 2C & Fig. S2D), indicating that FOXO3 may be a downstream target of miR-122-5p. Transfection of miR-122-5p mimic mitigated luciferase reporter activation with the FOXO3 3′-UTR. Moreover, both the transcript and protein levels of FOXO3 were increased by FTX; this effect was reversed by miR-122-5p overexpression. Therefore, we concluded that FOXO3 is a bona fide target gene regulated by FTX/miR-122-5p. It has been reported that FOXO3 is an essential factor that controls the adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by regulating the PPAR-γ and Notch signaling pathways, respectively [62]. Our study found that downregulation of FOXO3 attenuates the effects of FTX on the proliferation and differentiation of hDPSCs, indicating that FTX suppresses the pluripotency of hDPSCs by regulating the miR-122-5p/FOXO3 cascade. It is widely recognized that FOXO3 functions as a transcriptional activator or repressor to regulate the expression of target genes involved in modulating the differentiation and self-renewal capacity of MSCs, such as OCN, ALP, Runx2, and PPARγ, through direct DNA binding or as a component of protein complexes [63, 64]. In addition, FOXO3 has been identified as one of the key transcription factors that governs the expression of antioxidant gene SOD2 and autophagy-related gene LC3B, both of which are closely associated with the differentiation process of MSCs [65, 66]. Collectively, these findings suggest that FOXO3 may play a critical role in the multidirectional differentiation of hDPSCs through transcriptionally regulating the expression of differentiation-related genes and other genes essential to the cell differentiation program. However, the effect of FOXO3 on MSC differentiation appears to be controversial. One study indicated that FOXO3 activation promotes osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs by upregulating of autophagy-related factors [67]. The reasons for these contrasting findings remain unclear but may involve variations in cell types, stages of cell differentiation, or specific contexts/diseases [64].

The primary objective of regenerative endodontics is to restore normal pulp function in inflamed or necrotic teeth, thereby achieving genuine regeneration of dental pulp-like tissue [68]. A key process in this restoration is the formation of reparative dentin-like tissue, the major mineralized component of teeth, which is essential for re-establishing the functional integrity of the dentin–pulp complex. In our study, Masson trichrome staining results showed that the FTX + mimic NC group presented less collagen fiber deposition compared with the FNC + mimic NC group. Notably, miR-122-5p restoration reversed the inhibitory effect of FTX on the percentage of blue-colored collagen, indicating a regulatory role of FTX/miR-122-5p in dentin collagen formation. Dentin sialophosphoprotein (DSPP), a noncollagenous extracellular matrix protein secreted by odontoblasts, serves as a marker for the differentiation of DPSCs into odontoblasts [69]. We observed that co-overexpression of miR-122-5p and FTX significantly attenuated the suppressive effect of FTX alone on DSPP expression, underscoring the essential role of FTX/miR-122-5p in odontoblast differentiation of DPSCs and subsequent secretion of the dentin matrix.

To further clarify the potential mechanisms involved in the effect of FTX/miR-122-5p on dentin regeneration, we examined FOXO3 protein expression in vivo. Consistent with our in vitro findings, FTX overexpression significantly upregulated FOXO3 protein levels, whereas the miR-122-5p mimic exerted the opposite effect. Given the critical role of RNA in numerous cellular processes such as cellular activities and differentiation, harnessing its regulatory potential on core cellular genes opens up exciting avenues for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies. Indeed, several RNA-based therapeutics-encompassing ncRNA-targeted interventions, mRNA replacement, RNA interference (RNAi) therapies, and RNA-based vaccines-have already been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to provide long-lasting protection against a wide range of diseases [70]. Our study reveals, for the first time, that the FTX/ miR-122-5p/FOXO3 axis serves as a potent regulator in determining the fate of hDPSCs. This discovery presents novel molecular candidates for dentin-pulp complex regeneration and suggests that RNA-based therapeutic approaches targeting this axis may offer a promising alternative for dental pulp repair.

Nevertheless, it is important to situate these findings within the clinical context of dental pulpitis, a prevalent oral inflammatory disease characterized by persistent inflammation that often culminates in pulp degeneration and necrosis [71]. The injured pulp tissue produces a variety of proinflammatory mediators and elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which collectively impair the odontogenic potential of DPSCs and compromise their intrinsic reparative capacity [72]. Gene-editing technologies that modulate functional gene expression have shown promise in enhancing the differentiation of DPSCs while counteracting the detrimental effects of the inflammatory microenvironment [73]. Despite these advances, the development of effective and clinically feasible strategies to regulate gene expression in DPSCs remains a significant challenge. A recent study by Osorio et al. demonstrated that tideglusib-doped nanoparticles stabilize the cytoskeleton and enhance osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of DPSCs, effectively mitigating LPS-induced impairment [74]. Building on this concept, polymeric nanoparticles engineered to deliver small molecules targeting the FTX/miR-122-5p/FOXO3 axis may represent a promising approach to enhance the therapeutic application of this signaling axis in dental pulp repair and regeneration under inflammatory conditions.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The precise mechanisms by which FOXO3 regulates the self-renewal and differentiation of DPSCs remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation. Additionally, other clinical relevant microenvironments, such as inflammation and hypoxia, were not addressed in our study. As a pivotal stress-responsive determinant of MSC fate, FOXO3 integrates complex transcriptional circuits that orchestrate metabolic reprogramming, autophagy, and oxidative stress resistance [63, 75, 76]. This suggests that the FTX/miR-122-5p axis may also modulate DPSC differentiation trajectories under inflammatory conditions by influencing FOXO3-mediated mechanisms. Future studies employing clinically relevant in situ models (e.g., rats, beagle dogs, miniature pigs) are imperative to accelerate clinical translation of RNA-based therapies targeting FTX, miR-122-5p, or FOXO3. Furthermore, while certain differentially expressed miRNAs, such as miR-143-3p and miR-140-5p, have been functionally validated to attenuate the odontogenic differentiation capacity of hDPSCs [35, 77], other candidates, including miR-17-5p and miR-335-5p, which have been implicated in the osteogenic lineage commitment of other stem cells [78, 79], remain to be systematically investigated for their context-specific roles in hDPSC fate determination.

Conclusion

In summary, our results demonstrate that lncRNA FTX exerts a suppressive effect on the proliferation and multilineage differentiation capacity of hDPSCs by modulating the miR-122-5p/FOXO3 axis. Mechanistically, lncRNA FTX downregulates OCT4 expression and disrupts its binding to the miR-122-5p promoter region, thereby impeding miR-122-5p transcriptional activity and attenuating its inhibitory effect on FOXO3 (Fig. 10). This newly identified FTX/OCT4/miR-122-5p axis may serve as a potential therapeutic target for maintaining stem cell characteristics. In addition, our findings elucidate the regulatory role of FTX/miR-122-5p/FOXO3 in tissue formation for regenerative medicine, suggesting promising RNA-based gene-editing targets for dentin regeneration.
